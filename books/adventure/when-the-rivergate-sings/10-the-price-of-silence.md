## 10. The Price of Silence

The lockhouse smelled of rust and river rot, and by the time the storm-light faded from the broken windows, it had also come to smell of woodsmoke, feverfew, and Priya Chandrasekar's blood.

Mara had done what she could with the wound—a gash along the ribs where a snapped cable had opened her like a letter—but field medicine on three hours' sleep and half a med kit was an act of faith more than skill. Now Priya lay propped against the old lock operator's chair, which someone had dragged to the hearth forty years ago and never moved again, wrapped in Tam's dry shirt over her own soaked clothes, watching the fire with the flat patience of someone who had decided not to die today and was holding herself to it.

Tam sat across from her, the oilskin packet in his lap. He hadn't let go of it since the rapids. Mara had watched him hold it through the wreck, through the swim, through hauling Priya up the lockhouse stairs—one arm around the packet like it was a child, the other doing the work of two.

Priya noticed too. Her eyes went to the packet, then to Mara, and something shifted in her face—the look of a woman deciding how much truth a room could hold.

"You're Lockridge," she said. Not a question.

"Neither of us uses the name," Mara said.

"That's not the same as not being one." Priya coughed, winced, kept going. "I ran your face through the expedition files the night you two showed up at our camp perimeter. Mara Vale, ranger liaison, Voss survey contract. Biometrics flagged a ninety-one percent match to a sealed juvenile record out of Corvane County. I didn't say anything because it didn't matter. Then." She looked at the fire. "It might matter now."

The rain worked at the roof. Somewhere below, the river ground itself against the gorge walls, patient as a whetstone.

"Then tell us," Mara said. "What happened at the camp. Who cut the tents."

Priya was quiet long enough that Tam leaned forward. "Eleven people went missing," he said. "No bodies. Voss doesn't take prisoners for free."

"No," Priya said. "Voss didn't have to take anyone. We walked onto her barge ourselves." She turned her head against the chair back and looked at Mara directly. "Dane Okafor. Expedition lead. Twenty-two years running river surveys, medals, the whole résumé. He signed us over."

The word landed in the room like a dropped tool.

"Signed us over," Mara repeated.

"There was a night op, tide four of the survey. Dane took the tender out alone—said he was checking current gauges. He came back at 0300 with a Voss fleet jacket folded under his arm and wouldn't look at any of us. By morning we had new orders: abandon the wreck site, pull all sonar archives, stage at the mouth for 'contract renegotiation.' Two of my dive team refused. They were gone before breakfast—not dead. Just gone. Rerouted, Dane said. Reassigned." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "He'd traded the site. The manifest location, the survey data, the coordinates—all of it—to Voss. And in exchange, Voss made calls. His brother's debt got cleared. His daughter's school placement got fast-tracked into a district she'd been waitlisted on for two years. Small things. Cheap things. Things that cost Voss nothing and cost Dane everything he'd spent his life believing about himself."

Tam's jaw worked. "He gave up eleven people for a school placement?"

"He gave up eleven people because he was told no one would be hurt." Priya's eyes were dry and very bright. "That's the part you need to understand. Nobody sells their crew to a butcher. They sell them to a negotiator. Voss sat across from him in a clean cabin with tea and told him this was a labor dispute resolution, that the families downstream had retained her firm to quietly retire a historical liability, that everyone would be compensated and relocated and the story would simply—end. And Dane, who has spent twenty years watching companies do exactly that to exactly this kind of story, believed her. Because believing her meant his hands stayed clean. Because the alternative was admitting he'd already taken the deal the moment he heard his daughter's name."

The fire popped. Mara found she was gripping her own knee hard enough to ache.

"She showed him the ledger," Tam said quietly.

"What?"

"The ledgers." He patted the oilskin packet without opening it. "Grandfather's books. I've read every page twice. There are letters in the back—correspondence between Aldous Lockridge and a salvage broker in 1912, after the Meridian went down. Payments. Hush schedules. And there's a page where the handwriting changes mid-entry, where Aldous writes *the men are at rest and the valley is at peace* right above a column of figures, and you can see—he believed it. You can see the moment he talked himself into it. He didn't start as a monster. He started as a man who was offered tea in a clean cabin."

Priya nodded slowly. "That's how it always works. That's why it keeps working."

Mara stood. She hadn't decided to; her body just did it, carrying her to the broken window where the gorge fell away into blue-black dark and the rain stitched silver lines down the stone. Ten years she had built herself into a person no system could find, no ledger could hold, no family could spend. No record. No name. No debts. She had thought that made her clean.

"Dane's not evil," she said, to the window or to herself. "Grandfather wasn't evil. Voss isn't even evil, probably. She thinks she's—" She stopped.

"A steward," Priya said behind her. "She told us that. First day aboard the *Coriolis*, she gave this speech about how history is a load-bearing wall and some things are better left inside the structure. How the valley runs on the Lockridge endowment, how six towns drink from aquifers the family paid to protect, how digging up 1911 would burn down the lives of ten thousand people who never hurt anyone. She believes silence is a public service." A pause. "She almost had me. I want you to know that. Day three, I almost agreed with her."

Mara turned. "What stopped you?"

"The bones." Priya said it simply. "I dove the hull. Before Dane sold us, before Voss's divers came—I was the first one in the hold in a hundred and twelve years. There were nineteen skeletons, and they weren't arranged like a sinking. They were arranged like a locked door. Men who died pushing at something that would not open." She looked at her bandaged ribs as if the water were still on her. "You can't argue someone out of a thing like that. It argues for itself."

* * *

They ate what was left of the supplies—half a tin of smoked fish, river water boiled in a dented kettle Tam had found hanging in the lockhouse, a handful of Wren-bought cattail root Mara had carried since Cutter Bend without knowing why. Priya ate like a surveyor, methodical, cataloguing, and when she finished she said, "Ask me. You've both been circling it since the fire."

Mara set down her cup. "How many tides did we lose?"

"Two, if we leave at slack water tomorrow. Three if we wait for the weather window Wren promised." Priya's gaze sharpened. "But that's not the question you're afraid of."

"No," Mara admitted. "It isn't."

She looked at Tam. He knew. She could see him knowing—the way his thumb pressed flat against the oilskin packet, guarding it, the way he'd guarded it since the reeds, since before, maybe since the day their father erased him from the family and he'd spent twenty years stealing pieces of it back.

"Everyone serves power," Mara said slowly. "That's what tonight is, isn't it? Dane served it. Grandfather served it. Voss serves it and calls it duty. Even the families downstream—ten thousand people drinking Lockridge water, sending their kids to Lockridge schools—they serve it without ever signing anything." She heard her own voice go flat and ranger-cold, the voice she used on reports. "So tell me why we're different. Tell me why two siblings and a wounded diver with a stolen ledger are going to do what a hundred years of decent, reasonable people couldn't."

"Because we're not decent," Tam said.

The words came out of him rough, unpolished, like he'd been carrying them a long way.

"Look at us. I'm a thief. You've spent a decade making yourself unfindable—that's not virtue, Mara, that's desertion with better gear. Priya got her crew sold and lived. None of us came here clean. Dane wanted to stay clean and it broke him. Grandfather wanted to believe he was building something and it drowned nineteen men." He stood, and the firelight caught the scar along his jaw that Mara still hadn't asked about. "The difference isn't that we're good. The difference is that we already know exactly what we are. So when the offer comes—and it will come, Voss doesn't waste breath on people who can't be bought—we'll recognize it. We'll know the taste of the tea in the clean cabin. And we can refuse it *knowing*, instead of refusing it and telling ourselves later we were heroes."

Priya laughed once, short and real, and immediately regretted it against her ribs. "God. You two argue like family."

"We are family," they said together, and then glared at each other, and Priya laughed again despite everything.

Mara crouched by the fire. "The ledgers. Show me the page."

Tam hesitated—she saw the hesitation, the thief's reflex, the twenty-year-old wound of a boy whose things were never safe—and then he untied the oilskin and laid the ledger open on the floor between them, turning pages until he found it. The handwriting change. *The men are at rest and the valley is at peace.* Below it, columns of payments to widows who were never told why they were widows. A signature at the bottom that matched the one on the scuttling order Tam had described, the one waiting in a copper case at the bottom of a flooded quarry.

Aldous Lockridge's hand. Steady. Reasonable. The hand of a man who had been offered tea.

Mara touched the ink and felt nothing—no horror, no absolution. Just paper. Just proof that the distance between a good man and a drowned crew was one comfortable conversation long.

"I used to think inheritance was money," she said. "Land. The name. Something you either claimed or burned." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "It's not. It's a decision that gets made for you unless you make it yourself. Dane let Voss decide. Grandfather let the fortune decide. Every year the valley lets the silence decide." She looked up at the other two, at the firelight and the rain and the black throat of the gorge beyond. "Tomorrow we stop letting anything decide for us."

Priya pushed herself upright against the chair, gray-faced but steady. "Then we need a route past the second gorge, and we need it before the monsoon eats the charts. Wren mentioned a cable tram."

Tam's head came up. Something moved through his face—recognition, memory, a boyhood surfacing after twenty years underwater. "The Cutter Bend tramline," he said. "I rode it. When I was nine. Dad took me upriver the summer before—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I know how it works. I know where the anchor housings are. If the cable's intact, I can have it running."

"Nothing on this river stays intact," Mara said.

"Then I'll fix it." He was already pulling the ledger back into its oilskin, already moving, and for the first time since the reeds he looked less like a thief and more like a man coming home to something. "I grew up on this river too, Mara. Whatever else they took from us—they couldn't take that."

Outside, the rain thickened toward the true monsoon, and far upstream, in the drowned quarry pool, a copper case waited with a signature inside it, and somewhere below them both, on a barge riding at anchor, Ilsa Voss waited too—with tea, no doubt, and a clean cabin, and all the time in the world.

Three tides left. Mara banked the fire and began counting them down.
