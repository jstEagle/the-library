## 11. Tide Three

The second gorge announced itself before they saw it—a low, grinding roar that came up through the hull of the borrowed skiff and settled into Mara's back teeth like a warning. The river narrowed ahead into a slot of white water between walls of black basalt, and above it, strung across the gap like a spider's forgotten web, hung the cable tram.

Tam stood in the bow with one hand shading his eyes. "Still there," he said, and there was something almost boyish in it, something that didn't belong on the face of a man who'd spent twenty years being nobody's son. "I told you it would be."

"You told me you *hoped* it would be."

"Hoping is how Lockridges pray." He glanced back at her, and for a moment she thought he might smile. He didn't. The last two days had burned the smiling out of both of them—the wrecked skiff, the rusted lockhouse, Priya's ankle swollen to twice its size and her insistence that she could still walk, which was a lie they had all agreed not to call a lie. "My mother used to bring me up here. Before."

Mara said nothing. *Before* was doing a lot of work in that sentence, and neither of them had the strength to unpack it. She brought the skiff alongside the crumbling concrete apron of the old portage station and let the current swing them in. Priya climbed out without being asked, jaw set, favoring her left leg so obviously that it circled back around to looking deliberate.

The tram station was a ruin of rust and bird droppings: a concrete blockhouse with its roof half caved, a winch house grown over with creeper vine, and the cable itself running out over the gorge in a long sagging arc toward the far bank, maybe three hundred meters of open air below it. The gondola—little more than an iron cage with a plank floor—hung at the near tower, swaying gently in the wind that funneled up from the rapids.

"It'll hold our weight?" Priya asked.

"It held ore barges' worth of gear for forty years," Tam said. "It held my mother and me and a season's worth of smoked fish when I was nine." He was already moving toward the winch house, hands finding the machinery the way a blind man finds the walls of his own house. "It's been sitting, though. Brake bands will be seized. Cable's probably fine—galvanized steel doesn't care about weather much—but the sheaves will be gummed with grit, and if the brake doesn't release clean we'll be halfway across the gorge with no way to stop or go."

"And if the cable's *not* fine?"

"Then we'll find out in an educational way."

Priya looked at Mara. Mara looked at the cable, following it out over the void, reading it the way she read sign on the salt flats—not the thing itself but what the thing said about everything that had touched it. No kinks visible from here. No birds nesting on the far tower, which meant it hadn't gone slack enough to sag into reach. The wind was steady out of the south, which meant the monsoon was still offshore, still counting down.

Three tides. Odile's charts lived behind her eyes now like scripture she couldn't unread. Tide one had passed while they sheltered in the lockhouse. Tide two would pass tonight. After tide six, the gorge became a drowning machine, and everything upstream of it—including them, including whatever Voss hadn't yet destroyed—would be sealed under brown water until autumn.

"We fix it," Mara said. "All of it. We don't ride anything we haven't tested."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and Mara learned something about her brother that no ledger could have told her: Tam Lockridge was good with his hands.

Not competent—good. He talked to the winch the way some men talk to horses, low and constant, coaxing the seized brake band free with penetrating oil he'd been hoarding in his pack since Rivergate, working the adjustment screw a quarter turn at a time while Priya held the light and narrated the corrosion like a sportscaster. When the band finally broke loose with a shriek that scattered birds off the far cliffs, Tam laughed—a short, startled bark, as if the sound had escaped custody—and Mara felt something in her chest shift half an inch, like ice going out on a river.

She knew this work. That was the trouble. She knew the patience of it, the rhythm of problem and tool and problem again, and watching him do it was like watching a stranger wear her own coat. Ten years in the desert had taught her that competence was the only inheritance worth keeping. Apparently it ran in the family, along with the guilt and the silence.

"You grew up on this river," she said, handing him the next wrench before he asked for it. It was the first time she'd offered him anything without being asked, and they both noticed.

"First eleven years." He fit the wrench to a sheave bolt and leaned into it. "Mother never talked about why we left. Just that we did, fast, and that your father's name was a bad word in certain houses, and that the tram was ours in every way that mattered because nobody else wanted it." A pause. "She used to say the river keeps better records than any family does. I didn't understand her until this week."

"The ledgers."

"The ledgers." He wiped his hands on his thighs, leaving rust-colored streaks. "You've seen what's in them. Grandfather's handwriting gets shakier after 1911. Not from age—from knowing. Every page after the scuttling order is a man trying to write his way out of a room he built himself."

Mara turned away and busied herself with the gondola, checking the gate latch, the floor planks, testing each one with her full weight. Below her feet, through gaps in the iron, the gorge dropped away into spray and noise. Somewhere down there, at the bottom of the flooded quarry pool, her grandfather's signature sat in a copper case on top of a hold full of bones. Two days' travel. One tide after next.

"Tide's turning soon," Priya called from the blockhouse, where she'd propped herself against the doorframe with Odile's chart spread over her knee. "We should eat. And somebody should say out loud what we're all thinking, which is that Voss's fleet isn't behind us anymore."

Mara went still. "What do you mean?"

"I mean I watched the water all morning while you two played mechanic. No wakes. No engine noise. For a fleet that size, that's not falling behind—that's taking a different route." Priya tapped the chart. "There's a haul road on the north bank. Old logging grade. Wide enough for barges on trailers. If Ilsa Voss knows this river half as well as she pretends to, she's not chasing us through the gorges. She's driving around them and waiting where we have to come out."

Tam straightened up slowly. "The lower pool. Where the Meridian sits."

"Where the manifest sits," Priya said. "Where her divers are probably already working."

The wind shifted, coming up the gorge colder than it had been an hour ago, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet anywhere in the world. Mara looked at the cable stretching away over the void and understood that the race had changed shape. They weren't running from Voss anymore. They were running to a place Voss had already reached.

"Then we don't sleep tonight," she said. "Fix the far station. Test the tram empty. Cross at first light."

* * *

The test run went wrong in exactly the way tests are supposed to prevent.

They loaded the gondola with their remaining gear—double the weight of any single person, triple the weight of Priya—and Tam cranked it out over the gorge while Mara walked the near tower, hand on the cable, feeling for vibration. Forty meters out, fifty, the cage swinging in the crosswind with the slow patience of a pendulum. At sixty meters the far sheave, the one Tam hadn't been able to reach, began to squeal—a thin metal note that rose in pitch until Priya, wincing, said, "That sounds expensive."

Then the gondola stopped.

Not smoothly. It stopped the way a heart stops, all at once, and the cable above it hummed a new note, tighter, and Tam threw himself onto the brake lever to keep the whole system from feeding the cage out into slack and letting gravity finish the argument.

"Sheave bearing," he called back, voice flat with effort. "Seized solid. Cable's dragging over it instead of rolling. If I feed more cable it'll saw through by the far tower."

"So bring it back."

"If I bring it back fast, same thing. It has to come back slow, and it has to come back balanced, and I can't leave this brake." His knuckles were white on the lever. Sixty meters of air between him and his sister's entire equipment load, and every meter of retrieval had to pass through his hands.

Mara looked at the cage hanging out there in the wind, at the gorge below it, at her brother's face across the distance—drawn, sweating, absolutely concentrated—and made the decision the way she made all decisions, quickly and without ceremony.

"I'm going out on the cable."

"No," said Tam and Priya, in unison, in two entirely different tones.

"The bearing's at the far sheave housing. Somebody has to oil it or knock it loose, and the only way to it is the catwalk." There was a maintenance catwalk along the top of the cable, a rope-and-plank affair laid down by men in 1913 who had expected to be replaced by machines and never were. "I weigh less than the gear. I've walked worse."

"You fell off a *dune* last year," Priya said. "Odile told me. You broke three ribs walking downhill."

"That was a sandstorm. This is a Tuesday."

"Mara." Tam's voice cracked across the gorge, and something in it stopped her with one boot on the lowest catwalk plank. He was still braced on the brake, sixty meters away, shouting across open air, and his face had gone young and terrified in a way that had nothing to do with the mechanics. "If that cable goes while you're on it—"

"Then it goes, and you'll know I died fixing something instead of hiding from it, which is more than Grandfather managed." She said it before she could soften it, and immediately wished she hadn't, because his face got worse. She took a breath. The wind took hers away and gave her spray instead. "Tam. I'm coming back. Both of us are. That's the whole plan. Everybody comes back."

He held her eyes across the sixty meters for a long moment. Then he nodded once, jerkily, and said, "Slow steps. Hands on the handrope. If the wind gusts past what you can lean into, you flatten and wait it out. Don't fight it. Wait it."

"You sound like a ranger."

"You sound like my sister," he said, and neither of them had time to flinch from it, because she was already climbing.

* * *

The catwalk was forty centimeters of rotten plank suspended over a hundred meters of roaring nothing, and Mara loved it instantly, the way she loved all narrow places: honestly, completely, without apology. Out here there was no name to carry, no family to answer for, no grandfather's ghost in the ledgers. There was only the next plank, and the handrope, and the wind pushing at her shoulders like a large impatient animal.

Halfway out, the wind gusted, hard, from the south—the monsoon breathing on the back of her neck even now, even here—and she flattened against the cable as Tam had told her to, cheek against cold galvanized steel, and felt the whole span sing beneath her like a struck string. Below her, the river boiled white over rocks that had drowned better people than her. Above her, sixty meters back, Tam's hands were on the brake, holding her world steady, and he had not let go once, not for a second, not to wipe the sweat out of his eyes.

That was the thought that undid her, out there over the void. Not the height. Not the danger. The fact that someone was holding the brake.

She reached the far sheave housing, found the bearing packed with a decade of grit and wasp nests, and spent twenty minutes with a screwdriver and the last of the penetrating oil, talking to it the way Tam talked to machines, until the sheave spun free and silent. The gondola came the rest of the way across smooth as a drawer closing. When she stepped off the catwalk onto solid rock, her legs were shaking, and Tam was there, and he grabbed her arm—hard, too hard, the grip of a man who had spent sixty meters imagining the alternative—and then let go just as fast, embarrassed, and said, "Bearing's fixed, then."

"Bearing's fixed."

"Good." He cleared his throat. "Your hands are bleeding."

"Rope burns. They'll close." She flexed her fingers, watching him watch them. "You held the brake the whole time."

"Somebody had to."

"You didn't let go. Even when it would've been easier."

He looked out at the gorge rather than at her. The evening light was coming up gold off the water, and the first true raindrops of the leading edge of the monsoon spotted the rock around them, fat and warm and few. "Eleven years old," he said quietly, "I sat right there on that tower and watched a raft go through the gorge with a family on it, and one of them waved at me, and then the river took them under the ledge and they didn't come up. All of them. Mother held me and said, *the river forgives nothing, remember that.* And I decided the way to survive a thing that forgives nothing was to need nothing—to hold nothing, want nothing, owe nothing." He laughed, short and hollow. "Took me twenty years to figure out that needing nothing is just drowning slower."

Mara said nothing. The rain thickened by degrees, patient as everything else on this river.

"I held the brake," Tam said, "because you're the only thing in twenty years I've needed, and I found out about it four days ago, and I hate it, and I'd do it again." He finally looked at her. "That's all. That's the whole speech. Don't make it weird."

"It's already weird," Mara said. "We're standing in the rain on a cliff over a mass grave arguing about feelings. Weird is the water we're swimming in." She held out her rope-burned hand, palm up, an offer and a dare at once. "Come on. Tide's turning. We've got a crossing to make and a pool to reach before Voss's divers pull up anything else."

Tam looked at her hand. Then he took it—carefully, minding the raw skin, the way you handle something you've only just admitted matters—and shook it once, formally, absurdly, like a treaty between small nations.

"Crew of the tram," he said.

"Crew of the tram."

Behind them, Priya was already loading the gondola, and calling out in a voice pitched to carry over the rain: "If you two are done bonding, some of us would like to not spend tide four hanging from a cable!"

* * *

They crossed at dusk, one at a time, Priya first because she argued least about going first, then the gear, then Tam, then Mara last, riding the little iron cage out over the blackening gorge with the rain streaming through the mesh and the river snarling below. From the middle of the span she could see downriver, through a tear in the weather, and what she saw stopped her breath.

Far off, at the edge of sight where the gorge opened into the lower pool, lights. Not campfires—work lights, white and industrial, strung along a shoreline, and beside them, dark against the darker water, the low silhouette of a gunboat riding at anchor.

Voss was already there. Had been there, maybe, since before the lockhouse, before the rapids, patient as sediment.

Mara rode the rest of the way in silence, and when her boots hit the far bank she found Tam and Priya waiting, both of them looking at the distant lights too, both of them having already done the arithmetic.

"One tide to get there," Priya said. "One tide to do whatever we're going to do. One tide of margin, if nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing ever goes wrong," Mara said, "except always," and shouldered her pack, and started down the trail toward the lights.
