## 12. Ambush at the Narrows

The narrows announced themselves an hour before they arrived, the way bad news does.

First the walls began to lean in. The gorge, which had opened all morning into slabs of sun-baked sandstone wide enough to hold a farmstead, drew itself shut like a fist. The river had no choice but to quicken. Mara felt it under her hands on the tiller—the skiff's wake going ragged, the current grabbing the hull and pulling with the impatient strength of something that had somewhere to be.

Then came the sound. The water ahead made a low, continuous roar, not the white noise of rapids but something deeper, resonant, as if the river were singing through its own throat. Priya, propped against the gunwale with her splinted arm cradled to her chest, tilted her head toward it.

"That's the echo," she said. "The survey team called it the organ pipe. The walls are close enough that the river plays them."

"Close enough that there's nowhere to hide," Mara said.

"Close enough that you can hear a gunboat coming from two miles off." Priya managed a thin smile. "It cuts both ways."

Tam stood amidships, one hand on the cargo rail, watching the walls slide past. He'd barely spoken since dawn. Since last night—since Mara had stood in the dark behind the lockhouse door and heard him offer their grandfather's ledgers to Ilsa Voss over a stolen radio frequency, offering to trade away the truth of 1911 for his sister's life. They hadn't finished that conversation. Mara suspected they never would; some conversations you survive rather than conclude.

"The chart shows the narrows running four miles," Tam said now. "Single channel, no islands, no slack water after the first half mile. If Voss wants us, that's where she'll want us."

"She's been shadowing us since Cutter Bend," Mara said. "Letting Wren's route do her work for her. Funneling us."

"You could have taken the east fork," Tam said quietly. "Wren said it was longer but quieter."

"Wren said the east fork was a rumor." Mara kept her eyes on the water. "And we don't have time for rumors. One tide left after this one. We run the straight line."

Priya looked between them—at Mara's set jaw, at Tam's careful silence—and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary. She'd been doing a lot of that lately: saying nothing, watching the siblings circle each other like weather fronts deciding whether to merge or cancel out.

Mara brought the skiff around a bend and the narrows took them.

* * *

The walls rose sheer on both sides, sixty feet of striped stone so close together that in places the skiff's outriggers seemed about to kiss the rock. The light went greenish, filtered through the gap overhead, and the temperature dropped ten degrees as if they'd slipped underwater themselves. The river ran fast here but smooth—a brown-green ribbon pulled taut—and the organ-pipe hum filled everything, vibrating in Mara's back teeth, in the hull, in the water itself.

She'd spent a decade learning how to be unseen. It was a strange skill to bring to a river: how to move at the edge of vision, how to read terrain for the places eyes wouldn't go, how to let distance do your hiding for you. On the salt flats she'd been a smudge of heat. In the desert towns she'd been nobody, a woman paying cash, sleeping rough, leaving no signature anywhere except the faint wear of her boots. She had erased herself so thoroughly that when Odile had finally spoken her name aloud at the ranger station, it had sounded like a stranger's.

But rivers didn't work that way. A river was a corridor. A river funneled everything—water, boats, people, history—down a single throat. There was no edge-of-vision on open water. For the first time in miles, Mara understood exactly how visible they were: three figures in a twelve-foot skiff, framed by stone, moving at the speed the current allowed and no faster.

"There," Tam said.

He didn't point. He didn't need to. Ahead, where the channel bent left around a buttress of darker rock, a shape detached itself from the shadow of the far wall. Long, low, diesel-dark: Voss's gunboat, riding at anchor with engines idling, positioned precisely where the current would carry anything floating helplessly past its guns. Behind it, wedged into a shelf of rock, sat one of the salvage barges, crane folded, deck crowded with men.

They'd known. Of course they'd known. Wren's storm-proof route was old knowledge, and old knowledge belonged to everyone who'd cared to collect it—including whoever had sold charts to the Voss fleet thirty years ago.

"They're not even trying to hide," Priya said.

"Why would she?" Tam's voice was flat. "This is the polite version. This is her showing us the door before she has to put us through it."

The gunboat's engine note changed. Not advancing—not yet—but waking up, the idle deepening into readiness. Through the organ-pipe acoustics the sound arrived doubled and distorted, bouncing off the walls until it seemed to come from everywhere.

Mara did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four miles of single-channel water. Current pushing them forward whether she liked it or not. A gunboat athwart their path with a mounted autocannon and, judging by the silhouettes on deck, a dozen armed crew. No side channels on any chart. No islands. No slack water.

No way to be invisible.

Her decade of erasure rose up in her like bile. All those years of perfect absence, and it amounted to nothing here, because here there was nowhere else to *be*. The desert had rewarded her skills. The river punished them. And the people who'd paid for those skills with their trust—Odile, who'd covered her resignation; Priya, bleeding in the bottom of a stolen skiff; Tam, who'd tried to sell the truth to save her—were all of them aboard, all of them exposed, all of them in the path of what was coming because Mara Lockridge had decided she was the only one who knew how to move unseen.

The gunboat's bow swung a few degrees, squaring up to the channel.

"Hold on," Mara said, and turned the skiff toward the near wall.

* * *

"What are you doing?" Tam's voice cracked upward. "That's a wall."

"That's a wall *here*." Mara throttled down, fighting the current, holding the skiff in an eddy behind the buttress where the water curled back on itself. Twenty feet away, the stone face of the gorge dropped straight into the river—but twenty feet farther along, the rock was wrong. She'd seen it on the approach and hadn't let herself believe it: a seam in the sandstone, a horizontal band of lighter color, and above it, half-hidden by a curtain of hanging vine and flood debris caught at the high-water line, a darkness that wasn't shadow.

A slot. Maybe eight feet wide. Disappearing back into the rock.

"Flood channel," Priya breathed. She'd seen it too—of course she had; she'd spent three weeks surveying this exact stretch. "Old riverbed. When the monsoon comes, the main channel rises and part of the flow diverts through there—it's on the oldest surveys, pre-dam. Everyone stopped charting it once the quarry locks went in." Her voice picked up speed, the surveyor's excitement overriding the fear. "It won't be dry. It'll be running. But it should reconnect downstream, below the narrows, if the reports—"

"If," Tam said.

"It's eight feet wide," Mara said. "Maybe less at the entrance. The skiff is twelve."

Silence, except the organ-pipe hum and the growing growl of the gunboat.

"We can't fit," Tam said.

"We don't need to fit comfortably." Mara was already moving, throwing gear to the centerline, lashing the supply crates, stripping everything that protruded. "We need to fit once. At speed. With the current doing the steering."

"And if the current steers us into the rock instead?"

"Then it'll be fast," Mara said, and hated herself for the answer, and hated more that Tam almost smiled at it.

The gunboat's engine rose another register. Somewhere behind the buttress, a loudhailer crackled to life, and Ilsa Voss's voice rolled down the narrows, doubled and tripled by the stone, arriving from every direction at once like a god making an announcement:

"*Skiff at the bend. You are in a restricted salvage zone. Cut your engine and drift alongside or we will disable your vessel.*"

A pause, engineered, professional. Then, softer, almost kind, and somehow worse:

"*Mara. You know how this ends. Don't make these people pay for your choices.*"

Mara's hands went still on the tiller.

*Your choices.* As if Priya had been conscripted. As if Tam had been dragged aboard in chains. As if the expedition hadn't been bought and broken by Voss's money long before Mara ever reached the river. Voss spoke in the language of responsibility the way her grandfather's generation must have spoken it—laying every cost at the feet of whoever was least able to refuse it.

Priya moved. That was the thing Mara would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest like a swallowed stone for days afterward: Priya moved *first*, and without being asked.

She'd been rummaging in the forward locker since Voss's first transmission—Mara had assumed she was securing gear—and now she came up with the expedition's flare kit, three pencil flares and a signal cartridge, held in her good hand.

"How much of their attention do you need?" Priya asked.

"Priya—" Tam started.

"Thirty seconds?" She was already bracing herself against the gunwale, arm cocked. "Forty?"

"Priya, wait—"

"*Now* would be good," Mara said, because the gunboat was moving, its bow wave unrolling white across the smooth brown water, and there was no more time for anyone to decide anything safely.

Priya fired the signal cartridge high and wide, up over the buttress, out into the open sky above the narrows. It burst red, then white, a double star of burning magnesium that reflected off the wet stone and filled the gorge with stuttering crimson light. Every face on the gunboat's deck turned up toward it. Every barrel lifted. In the flare-light the crew became a frieze—frozen, gawking, beautiful in the way disaster is beautiful—and in that frozen second Mara opened the throttle and drove the skiff at the wall.

* * *

Later, Mara would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory refused the job. What remained were fragments, each one complete and terrible:

The rock face rushing at her, vines whipping across the windshield—

The slot appearing exactly where she'd aimed, black water inside it moving the *wrong direction*, upstream-then-down, confused and churning—

The sound of the port outrigger hitting stone, a crack like the world's largest knuckle rapping a door—

Tam's arm locking around her waist, hauling her down as a branch—a whole small tree, flood-carried and jammed in the slot's throat—raked over the boat, taking the radio antenna and half the rail with it—

Darkness. Green-black, total, the roar of the narrows cut off as if a door had closed, replaced by the different, hollower roar of water in a stone throat—

Light again, sudden, gray, rain beginning to spot the water around them, and the channel widening, unwidening, splitting around buried boulders that showed as boils of foam—

And Priya, sitting down hard in the bilge water, saying, in a small, precise voice entirely unlike her own: "I've been hit."

* * *

There was no time to stop. The channel was carrying them at walking pace, then jogging pace, then faster, and stopping meant broaching, and broaching meant swamping. So Mara shouted for Tam to keep the boat pointed while she crawled aft, and found Priya pressed against the transom with her good hand clamped high on her left shoulder, blood threading out between her fingers and diluting pink in the bilge.

Not a bullet. Later, in the calm of the sea cave, Priya would insist on the distinction, almost offended by the imprecision of everyone's memory: not a bullet, a splinter—one of the rail stanchions, snapped by the falling tree, driven sideways into the meat of her shoulder just below the collarbone. Three inches of hardwood, sharp as a chisel.

In the moment, in the channel, it might as well have been shrapnel. There was blood, and there was Priya's face gone the color of the stone around them, and there was the fact that Mara had no kit within reach, no dry bandage, nothing but the shirt off her own back, which she tore into strips one-handed while steering with her knees against the tiller bar.

"I'm fine," Priya said, which was the voice people used when they were establishing, out loud, a fact they needed to exist. "It's shallow. It's—I'm fine. Keep the boat moving."

"You're white."

"I'm always white. I'm a surveyor. We live underground." A breath that wanted to be a laugh and didn't make it. "Keep the boat moving, Lockridge."

So Mara kept the boat moving, and bound the shoulder as well as tearing cloth and adrenaline allowed, and watched the blood soak through anyway, and understood—with a clarity that felt like the splinter's own cold point—that this was the bill. This was what her invisibility cost when other people carried it for her. Ten years she'd spent ensuring that no one could ever be hurt on her account, because no one would ever be close enough. One week on the river, and the ledger had already opened: Odile lying to command for her. Tam trading truth for her. Priya bleeding in the bilge for her.

Voss's voice came back to her, doubled off the canyon walls. *Don't make these people pay for your choices.*

The worst part—the part Mara couldn't say, couldn't even fully think—was that Voss had been right about the shape of it, if not the substance. People *were* paying. The question Mara had to carry out of the narrows was whether the payment bought anything worth its price.

The channel bent, widened, and gave them back to the river.

* * *

They came out below the narrows into a country transformed. Rain fell in earnest now, fat monsoon drops that dimpled the brown water and blurred the gorge walls into watercolor. The main channel ran high and angry, and their little flood-channel stream joined it through a fan of gravel shallows, the skiff grinding briefly over stones before the current took them and they were part of the river again, indistinguishable, anonymous—one more piece of debris riding the rising water.

Behind them, muffled by rain and distance and the turning of the gorge, the gunboat's engine worked at the bend where they'd vanished. They could hear it probing, throttling up and down, and once, faintly, the crunch of hull on rock as someone tried the slot and thought better of it. Voss would find the channel eventually—she'd find it on the old surveys, or she'd simply wait, because waiting was her gift. But she'd lost the position, and the position had been everything.

Tam eased off the tiller and Mara took stock aloud, because saying it made it real: "Skiff's cracked but sound. Half our water's gone. Radio's dead. Food's wet but edible." She looked aft. "And Priya needs a doctor."

"I need a *stitch*," Priya corrected, gray-lipped, stubborn, one hand still keeping pressure on the wad of shirt. "There's a difference. Doctors are for people with time."

"Do we have time?"

Priya considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, even while bleeding. "One tide," she said. "Maybe. After that the gorge closes and nobody gets through—not us, not Voss, not the manifest, not the truth. Whatever happens at that wreck happens in the next day, or it happens never."

Rain drummed on the hull. The river ran high and fast, carrying them whether they willed it or not, and somewhere ahead, through the gray curtains of the coming storm, the flooded quarry pool waited with its bones and its copper case and its signature in Mara's family name.

Tam crouched beside Priya, checking the binding, and when he spoke his voice was low, meant for Mara alone beneath the rain-noise.

"She chose that," he said. "The flare. You didn't ask her. I didn't ask her. She saw the math and she acted. That's not what Voss thinks it is—that's not you spending people." His jaw worked. "That's what a crew looks like."

Mara wanted to believe him. She looked at Priya—pale, soaked, already giving Tam instructions for a better knot—and felt the guilt and the gratitude arrive together, inseparable, like the twin stars of the flare.

Ahead, the river bent toward a wall of black cloud, and out of the cloud, faint but unmistakable, came a sound none of them expected: bells. Old iron bells, ringing from somewhere up the left-hand bluff, tolling slow and steady against the rising wind—as if the river itself were counting down what remained.

Priya lifted her head. Rain ran off her chin.

"Cutter Bend's behind us," she said. "There's nothing on my charts that rings bells."

Mara turned the skiff toward the sound.
