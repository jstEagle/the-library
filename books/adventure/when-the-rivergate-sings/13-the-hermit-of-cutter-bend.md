## 13. The Hermit of Cutter Bend

The storm found them before Cutter Bend did.

It came down the gorge as a gray wall, eating the light in mouthfuls, and Mara ran the skiff hard against the current with Priya's arm hooked through hers and Tam bailing with a dented kettle because the bilge pump had died two rapids ago. Rain hit the river like gravel thrown from a great height. The cliffs on either side had narrowed to black ribs, and somewhere behind them—too far to see, too close to forget—the Voss fleet's running lights crawled along the channel like a procession of patient predators.

"There." Priya's voice was a rasp; she'd been favoring her bandaged side since the narrows, and her face had gone the color of wet ash. She pointed left, where the cliff face split around a stand of drowned willows. "Cutter Bend. Wren's smoke."

Mara saw it then—a thread of woodsmoke rising crooked from the leeward side of the bend, bent flat by wind but stubbornly alive. Someone up there was burning green wood, which meant they wanted to be seen by people who knew what green-wood smoke meant, and invisible to everyone else.

"Who is this person?" Tam shouted over the rain.

"Nobody knows," Mara said. "That's the point of them."

She swung the skiff into the eddy behind the willows and the current let go of them all at once, dropping them into a pocket of dead water as quiet as held breath. The transition was so sudden that Tam sat down hard in the bilgewater and laughed once, a cracked sound, like he'd forgotten how.

They dragged the skiff up a shelf of slick stone and climbed. The path—if it could be called that—was a goat track stitched into the cliffside, marked every few yards with river stones stacked in cairns no taller than a fist. Priya counted them under her breath. "These are old," she said. "Older than the locks. Somebody's been maintaining this since before the survey maps."

"Somebody who didn't want it on survey maps," Mara said.

The hermitage appeared out of the rain all at once: a low house built into an overhang of rock, roofed with sheet tin weighted by stones, walls of driftwood and salvaged lockhouse planking chinked with river clay. Behind it, terraced into the slope, were gardens gone wild and orderly at once—bean vines climbing fish-drying racks, a rain barrel system fed by tin channels, a smokehouse made from an overturned lifeboat. And beyond the garden, half-hidden by wind-bent pines—

Graves.

Dozens of them. Small markers of river slate, each one carved by hand, each one bearing a name or, where names had been lost, a single word: *unknown, river-taken, 1911*. They marched up the hillside in rows that followed the contour of the land, tended, weeded, each marker scrubbed clean of moss.

Priya went very still beside Mara. "This is where they brought them," she whispered. "The ones who washed downstream. This whole stretch of river—" Her voice caught. "My grandmother used to say the bend was cursed. It wasn't cursed. It was *kept*."

The door opened before anyone knocked.

* * *

Wren was small, weathered, and ageless in the way of people who have stopped performing their age for anyone. Their hair was iron-gray and cut with a knife, their hands scarred from rope and carving knife both, and they looked at the three soaked strangers on their step with the unhurried assessment of someone who has buried enough bodies to know exactly how much trouble living ones can be.

"You're bleeding," Wren said—not to Mara, not to Tam, but to Priya. "Through the bandage. Inside. Boots off."

It wasn't a request. It also wasn't unkind. There was a difference, Mara thought, between commands given to control and commands given to care, and she'd spent ten years learning to tell them apart.

Inside, the single room was warm and dense with the smell of woodsmoke, dried fish, medicinal herbs hanging in bundles from the rafters, and lamp oil. Shelves lined every wall, and most of them held books—waterlogged-looking ledgers, river charts annotated in three different inks, novels swollen fat from humidity, and journals. Dozens of journals, spines dated going back decades. On the largest shelf, set apart, sat a row of much older volumes wrapped in oilcloth, their spines cracked with age.

"The 1911 records," Priya breathed.

"Grandmother's grandmother kept them," Wren said, kneeling by the stove and pulling Priya's boot off with brisk, gentle hands. "She was nine when the Meridian went down. Her father worked the docks. He helped pull bodies out of the water for six days." They glanced up, eyes flat and dark as river stones. "He never spoke of it again except to write. The family kept writing after him. Somebody has to remember what everyone else agreed to forget. Sit still, this is going to hurt."

It hurt. Priya took it without a sound, which told Mara more about the last two days than any account would have. Wren cleaned the wound—a shallow gouge from the narrows, angry red at the edges—packed it with a poultice that smelled of pine and something bitter, and rewrapped it with linen boiled soft.

"Fever's coming," Wren said. "Not bad yet. You'll hate the next two days and keep your leg. Eat, drink this, sleep. In that order."

"What is it?" Tam asked, eyeing the cup of dark tea.

"Willow bark, yarrow, and river water boiled twice. It tastes like punishment and works like mercy." Wren pushed the cup into his hand. "Drink."

Then Wren turned to Mara, and the assessment changed. Sharpened.

"Lockridge," Wren said. Not a question.

Mara felt Tam go rigid beside her. "How—"

"You have his jaw." Wren nodded at Tam without looking away from Mara. "And you have the walk. I saw a Lockridge walk like that once, forty years ago, when the company men came upriver to buy silence from the families. Chin down, shoulders square, like the ground owed them something and they'd come to collect." They rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the oilcloth-wrapped books. "I was a child then. My grandmother spat at their boots. They paid her anyway. Everyone took the money eventually. That's how it works—that's how it *worked*. Silence isn't stolen. It's purchased, year after year, generation after generation, until nobody remembers there was ever anything to say."

"We're not here for that," Mara said.

"No," Wren agreed. "You're here because you're running from Voss's fleet, and you think I'm a harbor." They unwrapped the oldest book, and even in the lamplight the pages inside looked soft as cloth. "I am. But harbors charge. Sit down, Lockridge. Tell me what you found in the quarry pool, and tell me true, because I'll know if you don't."

* * *

Mara told it. The hull intact under sixty years of silt. The bones in the hold—more than the official count, so many more. The copper case. The manifest. And the scuttling order bearing her grandfather's signature, inked neat and confident above the names of thirty-one striking dockworkers who had boarded that barge expecting a negotiation and gotten a flooded hold instead.

Wren listened without moving. When Mara finished, the hermit stood, walked to the window, and watched the rain hammer the river for a long time.

"My grandmother wrote that the night the Meridian sank, the town bells rang wrong," Wren said finally. "Celebration bells. The Lockridge yards had just landed the biggest contract in valley history, and Rivergate rang its bells while thirty-one families sat up waiting for men who were already dead." They turned back. "Two days later, the company came with money and a story: accident, storm, tragic loss. The story fit what people needed. People need things to fit. That's the whole machinery of it. Not evil—just *fitting*."

"And you stayed," Tam said quietly. "Out here. Keeping graves."

"Somebody stayed. Then somebody else. We're the fourth generation of nobodies." Wren's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You want to know the joke, Lockridge? Your family paid us to forget. Every year, an envelope. Quiet money, routed through three companies so it couldn't be traced. My grandmother used it to buy the land. This land. The bend, the graves, the hillside. So the envelope bought the one thing it was meant to prevent." They tapped the old journal. "We remembered *better* because of it."

Mara stared at them. Something was rearranging itself in her chest, some load-bearing wall she hadn't known was there shifting on its foundation. Ten years she'd spent erasing herself—scrubbing her name from registries, burning photographs, walking out of her own history like a thief leaving a clean room. She'd thought renunciation was the only honest inheritance. Walk away, owe nothing, be no one.

But Wren's family had taken the enemy's money and built a fortress of memory with it.

"You didn't burn it," she said. "Any of it. You took what they gave you and you—"

"Reclaimed it." Wren shrugged. "Renouncing is easy. Any coward can walk into the desert and disappear—I've read about desert rangers who manage it beautifully." One gray eyebrow lifted, and Mara felt seen straight through, the way only strangers and rivers ever managed. "Staying is harder. Turning what they built into something that testifies instead of conceals—that's the work. That's the *only* work, in the end. Everything else is weather."

* * *

They ate smoked fish and beans and bitter greens from the terrace garden, and Priya slept by the stove with her fever climbing and falling like tide. Tam sat up late with Wren over the old journals, reading aloud in a low voice, translating the faded script, and Mara watched her half-brother—the boy erased from her life twenty years ago, the man who'd stolen ledgers from Voss to prove what their grandfather had done—and saw him weep without noise at a list of names written in a nine-year-old's careful hand.

Near midnight, Wren spread a chart across the table and weighed its corners with stones.

"Now. You want the storm-proof route, so listen, because I'll say it once." Their finger traced the river below Cutter Bend. "Voss knows the main channel. Voss knows the first and second gorges. What Voss does not know—" the finger moved to a thread-thin line branching off above the bend "—is the old log-flume cut. Dug in 1904, abandoned 1919. It runs behind Sawtooth Island, drops through the old sluice gates, and rejoins the river four miles past the third gorge. It's narrow, it's overgrown, and it's suicide in anything bigger than your skiff. But it's roofed by overhang most of the way, it can't be blockaded, and it comes out *behind* the fleet's anchorage."

"Can it be run at night?" Mara asked.

"In monsoon flood? No." Wren looked at her steadily. "In monsoon flood it runs itself. The sluice gates are rusted open—have been for years. The flume will be a river tomorrow, and it flows downhill the whole way. You won't row it. You'll *ride* it. Pray the whole time, if you pray. If you don't—" a glance at Tam, "—hold onto your brother."

Tam's ears went red. Mara said nothing, and Wren's almost-smile said they'd noticed everything.

"One more thing." Wren rose and went to the corner of the room where a bundle hung from a rafter, wrapped in waxed canvas. They lowered it and opened it on the table: a lantern, old-fashioned, brass, its glass panes thick and clouded—but behind the glass, a mechanism Mara didn't recognize, a shutter arrangement like a camera's eye. "Lock light. From the second gorge station, before the grid went automatic. My grandfather pulled it out of the scrap when they decommissioned it. It flashes a pattern—old signal code, pre-radio. River people knew it the way you desert folk know star paths." Wren pressed it into Mara's hands. It was heavier than it looked, warm from the fire. "Three long, two short means *safe passage, kin ahead*. Every lock keeper from here to the delta learned it before they learned to read. Most of them are dead now. But not all. And the ones who aren't still watch the water at night."

"Why give us this?" Mara asked. "You don't know us."

"I know what you're carrying." Wren nodded toward the waterproof satchel where the manifest rode against Mara's ribs. "Thirty-one people have been waiting sixty years for somebody to carry that downriver. My family kept the waiting. You do the delivering." They closed Mara's fingers over the lantern's cold brass handle. "That's the blessing. That's the whole liturgy. Now sleep. The flume won't wait and neither will the storm."

* * *

Mara woke before dawn to the sound of the river changed—deeper, hungrier, the monsoon's first real volume coming down from the high country. Priya's fever had broken in the night; she was sitting up, gray but clear-eyed, eating fish with the concentration of the genuinely starving. Tam stood at the window watching the rain.

Wren walked them down to the water in the last of the dark. At the skiff, the hermit stopped, and for a moment seemed uncertain—an odd thing on that composed, weathered face.

"My grandmother wrote one more thing," Wren said. "The last page of her last journal. She said: *someday a Lockridge will come up this path, and when they do, ask them which they choose—to be the name, or to be the answer.*" They looked at Mara. "I've wondered my whole life what that meant. Watching you, I think I finally understand. Your family spent sixty years being the name. Being the name means the story stays whatever you decide it stays." A nod at the satchel. "Being the answer means the story belongs to everyone it happened to."

Mara's throat was tight. "I don't know which I am yet."

"Good," Wren said. "Certainty is just another kind of silence." They stepped back from the skiff and raised one scarred hand, palm out—the old river gesture, Mara realized, the one carved into the grave markers up the hill. *Safe passage. Kin ahead.* "Go. Ride the flume. And Lockridge—when you get to the quarry, if it comes to choosing—"

"It will," Priya said quietly.

"—then remember the bells," Wren finished. "Rivergate rang celebration bells while the hold filled with water. Whatever you do down there, make sure the next bells that ring are ringing for *them*."

Mara pushed off into the brown, roaring current. Behind her, on the bank, the hermit's silhouette stood motionless in the rain, hand still raised, growing smaller and then gone as the current took the bow and swung it toward the hidden mouth of the flume.

Ahead, through the rain, she could see it: a slot in the cliffside, bristling with dead saplings, pouring white water—and beyond it, far downstream, the faint crawling lights of the Voss fleet, anchored and waiting, believing they had every exit covered.

They didn't know about the flume. But as Mara took the tiller and Tam hauled the sail down tight, she saw something that stopped her breath: on the bluff above the flume's mouth, a light. Moving. Deliberate. Signaling.

Three long. Two short.

Someone up there already knew they were coming—and someone else, lower down, near the fleet's anchorage, was signaling back.
