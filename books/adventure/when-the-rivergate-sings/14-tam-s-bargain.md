## 14. Tam's Bargain

The hermit's camp at Cutter Bend went quiet the way a held breath goes quiet.

Wren had taken Priya up the slope to the grave terrace at dusk—Priya walking on a crutch of river cane, cursing softly at every second step, Wren answering with the patient cadence of someone who had spent decades talking to people who didn't want to talk back. Their voices faded into the sound of the water, which never faded at all. The river ran high and brown under the cutbank, carrying monsoon's first offerings downstream: branches, fence posts, a drowned lantern still burning in its cage of glass until the current swallowed it.

Mara sat by the fire and cleaned the rifle she'd carried for ten years without once needing it, and she listened to her brother lie to himself across the clearing.

He thought he was being subtle. He'd always thought that, even as a boy—the way he'd slide sweets off the pantry shelf with his sleeve over his hand, certain no one could see what everyone saw. Now he was doing it with the expedition's shortwave set, the one they'd salvaged from the silent camp, propped against a driftwood log with its antenna strung up through the willows where it wouldn't read as an antenna unless you knew what string looked like when it was doing work.

She'd known since sundown. She'd watched him check the fire's sightlines three times. Watched him wind the crank charger behind his back like a boy hiding a cigarette. Watched him wait until Wren's lantern climbed the slope, until Mara herself turned toward the fire with her back half-angled to him—and then she hadn't turned at all. She'd let him believe it.

Because some part of her wanted to know what he would do when he thought no one from his blood was watching.

* * *

The radio crackled alive at full dark. Low power—he kept the gain down so the signal wouldn't carry past the bend, which meant it barely carried ten feet, which meant every word came through the night air to her as clearly as if he'd said them into her ear.

"Meridian actual, Meridian actual." A pause. His voice had gone flat and formal, the voice of a man reading from something he'd rehearsed while the others slept. "This is T. Lockridge. I know you're monitoring this band. You've been monitoring it since the mouth."

Static. Long enough that Mara's hands stilled on the rifle.

Then a woman's voice came through, calm as deep water. "Go ahead, Mr. Lockridge." Ilsa Voss. Mara had heard her exactly once, across four hundred yards of black river at the fleet anchorage, giving orders in a tone that made other people's orders sound like suggestions. It was worse hearing her small, compressed inside a speaker, intimate as a whisper.

"I have the ledgers," Tam said. "Grandfather's originals. Not copies. The scuttling order with his signature, the insurance correspondence, the payout schedules for the dockworkers' families who were paid to forget. Everything your backers sent you upriver to make disappear."

"And you're offering them to me."

"I'm offering a trade." A breath. "You take the ledgers. You burn them, sink them, whatever you were paid to do. In exchange, my sister walks. Off this river, out of this valley, tonight, before your boats move. She doesn't come near the wreck again. Neither do I. You get what you came for, and nobody else drowns."

Mara did not move. The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, Wren's lantern swung between trees like a slow yellow pulse.

Voss was quiet for a moment, and when she spoke again there was something almost gentle in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "You've thought about this."

"All day."

"You understand that I don't need to trade. I have two gunboats, forty men, and a storm coming that will close this gorge behind me. I can take the ledgers and leave all three of you for the river, and the river will be blamed, because the river is always blamed. That has been the arrangement in this valley for a hundred and fourteen years."

"I know."

"Then why am I talking to you?"

"Because you haven't done it yet." Tam's voice cracked and steadied itself. "You've had us in your sights twice—at the narrows, at the second gorge. Your divers reported our skiff running the channel before we ever reached it. If you wanted us dead, we'd be dead, and you'd tell your backers the ledgers burned with us. But you keep waiting. So either you're careful, or—" he exhaled— "or there's a line you don't cross, and I'm betting everything that there is."

Another silence. When Voss answered, the gentleness was gone, filed away like a tool returned to its case. "Midnight tomorrow. The gravel bar below the old lockhouse. One person brings the ledgers. If anyone else is visible within a mile of that bar, the trade is void and I stop being patient. Do we understand each other?"

"We understand."

"One more thing, Mr. Lockridge." A pause long enough that the static seemed to lean in. "Your grandfather made this same call in 1911. He also believed he was saving his family. Ask yourself, before midnight, whether you're making a bargain or repeating one."

The channel clicked dead.

Tam sat very still beside the radio, head bowed, the firelight moving on his face like water. Then he began, methodically, to pack the ledgers into an oilskin roll, and his hands were shaking, and he hid that too, badly, the way he'd hidden everything for twenty years.

Mara stood up out of the dark.

* * *

He didn't hear her until she was three steps away—she'd made sure of it, made her own silence a weapon one last time—and when he turned and found her standing there with the rifle slung and her face doing whatever it was doing, he went white to the lips.

"Mara—"

"How long?" Her voice came out low and even, which surprised her, because everything under it was screaming. "How long were you going to let me sleep first? Or were you going to leave a note?"

"It's not—"

"A note. Like Father used to leave." She heard it land, saw it hit, and couldn't stop. "You were going to trade our family's whole rotten inheritance to the woman who gutted an expedition, and you were going to do it while I slept, and then you were going to walk me off this river like a child being walked out of a burning house, and I wasn't supposed to get a vote. Was I. Was I supposed to get a vote, Tam?"

"She'll kill you." It burst out of him louder than he meant, loud enough to startle birds out of the willows. "You don't see it—you think because you got past her picket, because you run rapids like you were born in them, that you're fast enough. She's not trying to be fast. She's been patient this whole river, patient like ice forming, and the only reason we're alive is that she hasn't decided we're worth the mess. And you—" his voice broke sideways— "you're planning to open floodgates in a monsoon. On a quarry pool. With Priya already stitched together with fishing line and me not having been in moving water in twenty years. You're going to die, Mara. You're going to die beautifully and righteously and the ledgers will burn anyway, and I will be the last Lockridge standing in a valley that hates our name, holding nothing."

"So your answer is to give Voss exactly what she came for."

"My answer is to save my sister!"

The words rang off the cutbank and came back at them both. Below them the river kept on, indifferent, enormous.

Mara breathed. Once. Twice. She made herself look at him—really look, the way she read sign, the way she read anything true. Twenty years older than the boy who'd been erased from the family photographs, but the same hands, the same stubborn jaw, the same terrible habit of loving people by deciding things for them.

"That's what he did," she said, quieter now. "Father. When they cut you out. He decided it was safer for you somewhere else, and he decided I was better off not asking questions, and he decided Grandfather's name was worth more than the truth, and he never once—not once—asked either of us what we could live with." She stepped closer. "You're not trading the ledgers for me, Tam. You're trading me for the ledgers' absence. You're buying the same silence he bought. Just with better intentions."

Tam's eyes were wet and furious. "And what's your plan? Martyrdom with extra steps?"

"Yes!" She laughed, and it came out ragged. "Maybe! Maybe it is. But it's mine, Tam. That's the difference. Nobody assigned it to me. I chose the desert for ten years, I chose to erase myself, I chose every mile of that—I chose wrong sometimes, God knows, but I chose. And then I found Priya in a lockhouse and Wren on this hill and you in the reeds with a stolen ledger bag, and for the first time in my life I'm not alone, and you want to solve that by handing me over to be safe somewhere far away." She shook her head slowly. "I spent a decade learning how to disappear. Don't you dare make me practice."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The fire settled. Upstream, thunder muttered behind the gorge walls like something turning over in its sleep.

Tam sat down heavily on the driftwood log, the oilskin roll still clutched in both hands, knuckles white. "I was eight," he said finally, to the fire. "When they sent me away. I didn't understand it was about the money or the name or any of it. I just understood that Dad decided, and I went. And I spent twenty years thinking if I'd been better—quieter, easier—maybe he'd have decided differently." He looked up at her, and the fury had drained out of his face and left something younger underneath. "So when I decide things now, I tell myself it's different, because I'm deciding *for* someone instead of *against* them. But it's the same muscle, isn't it. Same fist."

"Yeah," Mara said. "It is."

They stood in that admission together, awkwardly, the way siblings stand in doorways after fights—too much history to hug, too much blood to walk away. Then Tam looked down at the oilskin roll and laughed, short and hollow.

"He warned me, you know. Voss. At the end. Said our grandfather made the same call in 1911." He turned the roll over in his hands. "A hundred years, and this family keeps reaching for the same lever."

"Then maybe it's time somebody broke the lever." Mara crouched across the fire from him, forearms on her knees. "Here's what happens now. We don't go to the gravel bar. We don't trade. And we don't pretend I'm walking away, because I'm not, and you're not either—you're coming to the wreck with me, and Priya, and we finish this the way we started it: together, badly, all of us voting."

"And Voss?"

"Voss expects a trade at midnight tomorrow." Mara felt the plan assembling itself in her the way weather assembled over the gorge—pressure first, then shape, then violence. "So that's exactly what she'll get. Someone walking onto that gravel bar carrying something wrapped in oilskin. She just won't have asked closely enough what's inside."

Tam stared at her. "What's inside?"

Mara smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and she was glad, suddenly, to have inherited something from this family after all. "Wren's storm flares. All of them. Wrapped in the map case, packed tight, wired to pop the moment anyone cuts the oilskin." She fed a branch to the fire. "If Ilsa Voss wants a bargain from a Lockridge, she can have one. Our family's always been generous with things that burn."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then, despite everything—despite the fear and the fight and the hundred years pressing down on both of them—he laughed too, a real one, startled out of him, and the sound went up through the willows like a bird released.

"You're insane," he said. "You're actually—Dad would have—"

"Dad isn't here." Mara rose, shouldering the rifle, and looked upstream toward the black wall of the gorge where the last tide waited, and beyond it the flooded quarry pool where her family's signature lay sealed in copper under a century of cold water. "That's rather the point."

Behind them, downslope, Wren's lantern came bobbing back through the trees with Priya limping beside it—and Priya stopped dead at the edge of the firelight, reading their faces the way rangers read sign, and said:

"What did I miss?"

Mara opened her mouth to answer—but the radio crackled first, unbidden, both their heads snapping toward it. Voss's voice, flat and cold, filling the little camp:

"Change of plans, Mr. Lockridge. My divers report movement at the quarry pool. Midnight is cancelled. We're coming to you now."
