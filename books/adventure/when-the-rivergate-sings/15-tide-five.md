## 15. Tide Five

The wind arrived before the rain did, the way bad news always does.

Mara felt it first as a pressure against her ears, a change in the river's voice—a new note threaded under the water's constant speech, high and thin and getting higher. She had spent ten years reading the desert's silences, and she had learned on this voyage to read the river's noise, and the noise was telling her the monsoon had crossed the coast and was coming inland like a debt collector.

"Hour, maybe two," Priya said. She sat in the bow with Wren's brass compass open on her knee and a hand-drawn chart weighted with a stone, though she barely looked at either. Her wounded shoulder was strapped tight, her face gray with pain she refused to name, but her eyes were clear. "The wind comes first. Then the front. Then the water comes up like something angry."

"Then we don't waste the hour," Mara said, and opened the throttle.

The skiff climbed the darkening river. Behind them, far back where the gorge walls swallowed everything, the running lights of Voss's fleet hung in the dusk like a string of patient lanterns—three boats, maybe four, holding a distance that never changed. Not chasing. Escorting. Ilsa Voss had stopped trying to catch them somewhere below the narrows, and that frightened Mara more than a chase would have. A hunter who stops hunting has either lost you or has decided the river will do the work. Voss did not lose people.

"She's waiting for the storm," Tam said from the stern. He'd been saying it for an hour, and Mara let him say it again because saying it was how he kept his hands steady. "She figures the gorge does her killing for free. We wreck, she salvages. We survive, she's waiting at the pool with more guns than we can count."

"Then we survive and deal with the guns," Mara said. "One problem at a time. That's how you stay alive out here."

"That's how *you* stay alive out here," Tam said. "I've been alive twenty years by doing the opposite."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. The wind took the smile's place, shoving against the skiff's flank as they rounded a bend and the last gorge opened before them.

Wren had described it at Cutter Bend in the low firelight, drawing it in spilled tea on the tabletop: a twelve-mile throat of granite where the river dropped ninety feet through a staircase of rapids the old lock-builders had named like curses. The Widow. The Teeth. The Long Sigh. And threaded through it, if you knew where to look, the lock lights—oil-burning beacons the river towns had maintained for a hundred years, long after the locks themselves had rusted shut, because the people of the river remembered what the charts forgot.

"They're not lit for you," Wren had said, pressing a stub of chalk into Priya's hand and marking the chart with quick, certain strokes. "They're lit for the dead, mostly. But the dead don't mind sharing."

Now, as full dark came down and the first fat drops of rain began to strike the river like thrown gravel, Mara saw the first one.

It stood on a shelf of rock forty feet above the water: a small iron cage around a flame, burning a steady gold against the black, exactly where Wren's chalk mark said it would be. Below it, invisible in the dark, the Widow waited—the first great drop, where the river funneled between two granite shoulders and fell into churning white that even now, in the rain-shadowed gloom, Mara could hear like distant applause.

"There," Priya said. "Bear left of the light. Not right. Wren was very clear. Right of the light is the Widow's tongue."

"Left of the light," Mara repeated, and steered left.

The skiff entered the gorge's throat and the world contracted to a corridor of black rock and white water and the gold pinpricks of lights appearing ahead like a sentence being spelled out one word at a time. Rain came harder, slanting, cold. The wind funneled down the gorge and doubled itself, screaming off the walls. Mara stood at the wheel with her whole body, legs braced, arms burning, reading the water by what little the lock lights gave her and by the river's sound—the deep-throated roar of a drop ahead, the flat hiss of a shoal, the hollow boom of water hitting stone.

The Widow took them at the second light.

The bow rose, hung, and the river simply left them—two full seconds of weightlessness in which Mara heard Tam swear and Priya shout a heading and her own heart hammering like a fist on a door—and then the hull slammed down into the trough and green water came over the gunwales and everything was noise and cold and motion. The engine screamed, caught water, screamed again. Mara hauled the wheel hard left as the current tried to set them against the right-hand wall, where the Widow's tongue—a fang of submerged granite—waited just under the surface with the patience of a hundred years.

"Light!" Priya's voice, raw against the wind. "Two points starboard—next light—*go*—"

Mara went. The skiff answered the way she had learned to make it answer, sliding sideways through the current, and the Widow's tongue hissed past close enough that Mara felt the spray of it breaking against the rock, close enough to touch, and then they were through, into the relative gentleness between rapids, all three of them gasping like surfacing divers.

"That," Tam said, "is the last time I complain about the tram."

"It is not," Priya said, and even in the dark Mara heard the ghost of a grin in it.

* * *

The Teeth came at the fourth light, and the Teeth were worse.

Not one drop but a brawling mile of them, the river broken into braids around drowned pillars, every channel a lie except one. Wren's chart showed it as a tangle of chalk lines with a single thread of truth running through, and Priya called it from the bow with her wounded arm pressed against her ribs and her good arm stabbing the dark: "Light—hard port—*now*—" and Mara threw the wheel and the skiff slewed between two pillars close enough to leave paint, and then "starboard—starboard—trust me—" and Mara trusted her, because there was nothing else left to do with the night, and the pillars scraped past on either side like the walls of a collapsing building.

Halfway through the Teeth, a light went out.

Mara saw it happen—one of the gold beacons ahead simply vanished, snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, either drowned by the rising river or blown out by the wind or dead of old age, and it didn't matter which. What mattered was that the sentence they were spelling lost a word, and the word had been *left*.

"The light's gone," Priya said, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone doing arithmetic with their life. "The light's gone and I don't—I can't see the channel—"

Mara cut the engine.

It was the hardest thing she had done in twelve miles of hard things—killing the engine in the middle of the Teeth, letting the current take the skiff broadside while the rain hammered down and the wind tried to fold them against the rock—but a drifting boat could be steered with the oars and the current's own logic, and a boat driven blind at speed through a braid of drowned pillars was already a wreck, just not officially yet.

"Oars," she said. "Tam. Oars. We drift and feel for it."

They drifted. The river carried them sideways through the dark, and Mara stood in the bow with a boathook, feeling—reading the water the way she had once read the salt flats, by touch and sound and the small telltale differences that separate a smooth surface from a lie. The current pulled left, then right, then left again. Under the hull, once, she felt the deep hum of water over a pillar's shoulder, close, and she pushed off it with the hook and they swung wide, and Priya breathed again.

Then Tam, hauling on the starboard oar, said, "There. Look. Up on the wall."

Mara looked. High on the left-hand gorge wall, where no light should be, a second flame flickered—small, unsteady, sheltered by someone's hand or some accident of the rock—and below it, faint in the rain, a shape: the ruined arch of the old Lock Four, and through the arch, the true channel, the thread of truth, running clean.

"Somebody relit it," Priya whispered. "Or lit a new one. That's not on Wren's chart."

"River people," Tam said. "They light them for the dead. Maybe tonight they lit one for the living."

Mara restarted the engine and ran the channel under the arch, and the Teeth spat them out the far side into the Long Sigh—a long, heaving mile of heavy water, no drops, no pillars, just the river gathering itself for the final pool—and it was there, in the Sigh, with the wind rising to a sustained scream and the rain no longer falling in drops but in sheets, that she looked back.

The lights of the fleet had entered the gorge.

* * *

"They're following us in," Tam said. He'd turned in the stern, and the running lights behind them were closer now, and moving. "In *this*. She's actually—"

"She's not following us," Mara said. The understanding arrived cold and complete, the way the worst understandings do. "Look at their spacing. They're not running the channel, they're holding the middle of the pool above the Widow. They'll anchor above the Teeth and wait out the worst of it. She's not chasing us, Tam. She's herding us. She wants us committed to the gorge in the storm's teeth so that whatever's at the end of it, we arrive broken—or we don't arrive."

"Then we don't give her broken," Priya said.

Mara turned forward again. The Long Sigh heaved under them, the skiff climbing and falling, climbing and falling, and ahead—barely visible through the rain, a pale seam of churning white where the gorge walls finally, after twelve miles, began to open—the last drop, and below it, if Wren's chart and Priya's memory and a hundred years of dead men's lights were all telling the truth, the flooded quarry pool, and the Meridian, and everything her family had drowned to keep.

One tide left. Wren had counted them on her fingers at Cutter Bend: the monsoon's leading edge would reach the upper valley on the next high tide, and after that the gorge would run with debris and the pool would rise and the wreck site would be unreachable for days—days in which Voss's divers could work in the storm's aftermath, undisturbed, finishing whatever her backers had paid a hundred years of silence to finish.

"We run the last drop," Mara said. "Then we're in the pool, and the storm covers us, and we do what we came to do before she can stop us."

"And if she's already there?" Tam asked. "If her divers are already in the water?"

"Then we're late," Mara said, "and we improvise. That's the whole family method, isn't it? Grandfather improvised. Father improvised. We're due for something better."

Tam didn't answer, but in the flash of the next lock light she saw his face, and it wasn't fear anymore. It was something harder and more useful than fear.

* * *

The last drop came at them out of the rain like a decision already made.

Priya called the line—"center, center, then hard left at the boil, the pool eddy catches you, *trust the eddy*"—and Mara centered, and the bow lifted, and the river dropped them ninety feet through a chute of white chaos with the wind trying to broach them sideways the whole way down, and Mara fought it with everything her arms had left, which by then was not much, and it was enough. The hull struck the pool's surface flat, plowed under a curtain of spray, and rose, wallowing, into the sudden strange calm of the quarry pool—a broad, black amphitheater of water ringed by drowned cliffs, the wind above them howling over the rim while down here the rain fell almost gently, as if the storm hadn't noticed this room yet.

They drifted, all three of them breathing like bellows, listening.

The pool was not empty. Somewhere out in the black, a generator throbbed. A work light burned on the far shore, hazed by rain. And on the water itself, low and close, a shape Mara's eyes took a full ten seconds to resolve: a dive barge, anchored mid-pool, floodlit from below, with a compressor hose running down into the water and a single diver's bubble-line trembling on the surface.

"They're diving," Priya whispered. "Right now. Tonight. She didn't wait for the storm at all."

"She waited for *us* to run the gorge," Mara said. The cold in her voice surprised her. "Now she doesn't have to."

The skiff drifted on the black water, three exhausted people in a shell of wood and rust, the storm gathering above the cliff rim like a held breath, and between them and the dive barge nothing but open water and the last of the dark.

Mara reached down and cut the engine to silence.
