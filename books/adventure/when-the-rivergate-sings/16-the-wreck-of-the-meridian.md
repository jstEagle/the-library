## 16. The Wreck of the Meridian

The quarry pool appeared the way Wren had promised it would: all at once, between one bend of the drowned road and the next, a circle of green-black water lying in the fist of the hills like something swallowed and never digested.

Mara brought the skiff in against the last of the wind, Priya calling depths from the bow with a lead line and a voice gone hoarse from shouting over the gorge. Tam sat amidships with the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and he didn't speak at all, which was how Mara knew they'd arrived. He had been talking since dawn—nervous, bright, filling the air the way their father used to fill a room—and now the air had emptied out of him entirely.

"That's it," Priya said, and let the lead line run slack. "Bottom's forty feet off the north shelf. Deeper in the middle. The old survey marked the hull at thirty-one."

"Thirty-one feet," Tam repeated, as if the number were a prayer or a sentence.

The rain had held off through the night, but the sky above the quarry rim was the color of wet slate, and the wind coming down the valley carried that particular mineral smell Mara had learned to dread on the desert's edge—the smell of a storm still deciding where to land. One tide left. Maybe less. The river behind them ran high and brown and impatient, and somewhere downriver, patient as tide itself, Voss's fleet was following.

They beached the skiff on the quarry's northern shelf, where a crumbling ramp of cut stone slid into the water—the old loading way, built when this pool had been a working pit and the Lockridge barges had come up the canal to fill their holds. Moss had taken the stone. Alders grew from cracks in the walls forty feet overhead. But the shape of the place remained: an amphitheater of industry, abandoned mid-sentence, its audience drowned.

Tam stood on the ramp and looked at the water for a long time.

"I used to swim here," he said finally. "When I was small. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "Nobody ever told me what was under us."

"Nobody told me either," Mara said. "And I grew up in the house that did the telling."

Priya had already begun unpacking the dive gear they'd salvaged from the wrecked skiff—one working regulator, one set of tanks half-full, a dive light with a cracked lens, and rope. Not enough for three. They hadn't discussed who would go down; some decisions make themselves, and everyone pretends afterward that they were debated.

"You shouldn't go alone," Priya said anyway, because she was the kind of person who said the true thing even when it was useless.

"The second tank's at maybe sixty percent," Mara said. "I'll take it as bailout, leave it on the line. If I'm not back in twenty minutes, don't come after me. Take the ledgers and Tam and get downriver."

"No," Tam said.

"It wasn't a question."

"Yes, it was. You just phrased it like a statement." He crouched by the gear, jaw tight. "You've been deciding things alone since I found you on the flats. Every mile upriver, you decide, we endure. Well, not this one. This one's mine too. Whatever's down there—it's my name on it same as yours."

Mara looked at him—at the man who had been a boy at a funeral twenty years ago, standing apart from the family the way she stood apart from everything now, and whom she had walked past without a word because walking past people was what Lockridges did to each other—and she heard her own voice say something she hadn't planned:

"Then stay on the line. Ten meters down, no further. If I signal twice, pull."

* * *

The water took her cold the way a hand takes a wrist.

Not gradually—there was no gentle thermocline, no warning. One moment she was breathing surface air in gray daylight, and the next the cold closed around her chest and squeezed, and her body wanted to gasp and she made it not gasp, made herself breathe slow and long the way the ranger manual taught for cold-water work, though the manual had never imagined this water, this dark, this weight of history pressing down from above.

She descended along the loading ramp, following the stone as it stepped down into blackness, her dive light cutting a cone through water thick with silt. Visibility was perhaps three meters. Four on a good sweep. The light turned the suspended sediment into a blizzard, and beyond the edge of the beam the pool simply ended—not darkness exactly, but absence, the kind of nothing she had spent ten years building around herself and had never once thought of as drowning.

Ten meters. She felt the tug of the safety line and knew Tam was there above her, holding his depth, holding his breath in every sense. Good. Keep holding it.

Fifteen meters. The ramp gave way to mud, and the mud gave way to timber.

She saw the bow first—or what she understood, a heartbeat later, must be the bow: a great curved lattice of iron and wood rising out of the silt like the ribcage of something enormous, furred with a century of algae, hung with dead branches that had sunk and lodged. The *Meridian*. She knew the name before her light found the faded letters, because she had known it her whole life without knowing she knew it—the way you know a scar's shape under your clothes. Her grandfather's first barge. The one the family histories called *lost with all hands in the flood of 1911*, the loss that had nearly ruined the young company, the tragedy that had made Elias Lockridge the careful, pious, beloved patriarch of Rivergate.

The hull lay on its starboard side, settled deep into the quarry floor, and it was intact.

That was the thing Tam had tried to tell her around the driftwood fire, the thing she had not let herself believe: intact. Not broken on the gorge rocks, not scattered, not dissolved into rumor. Whole. Preserved in cold, still, airless water while the world above forgot it. A sealed room at the bottom of a flooded pit, waiting ninety years for someone to open it.

Her light swept along the hull toward the hold, and the bones began.

At first she thought they were branches. Then roots. Then her mind offered her, desperately, the word *driftwood*, and she held onto that word the way a drowning woman holds a plank, right up until the light steadied and the plank became a forearm, and the forearm became an arm, and the arm became a man lying on his back in the silt with his coat rotted to ribbons and his hands folded—folded—over his chest, as if someone had arranged him, as if in the last black minutes someone aboard had gone down the line of the dying and composed them for a burial that never came.

There were more. She counted without wanting to. Seven visible in the light's reach, laid along the cargo deck where they had been working or sleeping or hiding when the sea-cocks opened. Dockworkers. Strikers. Men who had walked off the Lockridge wharves that autumn demanding wages that wouldn't shame a dog, and who had been invited aboard—she could see it now, could read it in the arrangement, in the lantern brackets still bolted to the beams, in the galley door hanging open—for a negotiation. For passage. For something. And then the water had come in from below, quiet as a decision, and the barge had settled here in the deepest hole the quarry offered, and the company had reported a tragic sinking in the gorge, and the valley had mourned, and the Lockridges had paid for a memorial wall in Rivergate with money earned by the killing.

Mara hung in the cold water above the dead and felt her whole inheritance rearrange itself around her like furniture in a dark room.

She had spent ten years erasing herself from records because she believed the name was a cage. She had told herself the Lockridge fortune was merely dull—timber and freight and interest compounding politely across generations—and that her exile was a matter of temperament, of preferring horizon to hedge. She had let herself believe that the worst thing her family had done was boring.

It was not boring. It was this. It was seven men folded neatly in the dark, and a copper case bolted inside the purser's strongroom that her grandfather had ordered sealed rather than raised, because the manifest inside listed not only cargo but passengers, and the scuttling order itself, signed in the firm's own hand, bore a signature she had traced in ink a thousand times on documents in the estate office without ever once asking what else that hand had written.

Elias Lockridge. Her grandfather. The man whose portrait hung in the stairwell, whose Bible sat on the hall table, whose rules she had rebelled against so cleanly and so comfortably, because rebelling against piety is easy. Rebelling against murder—that requires knowing.

Twice, she signaled on the line. Twice she felt the answering tug: *still here, still here.* Tam, holding his post. Their father's son doing what their father never did—staying.

The strongroom door was a small iron hatch aft of the hold, and it stood ajar, wedged by Voss's divers' work—fresh tool marks bright against the rust, a modern padlock sheared and hanging. They had been here. They had opened it. And they had left it, because Voss's orders were not to take the manifest but to destroy it, and destruction, apparently, required patience, or a dry deck, or permission from the families who were paying for silence.

Inside, on a steel shelf furred with corrosion, the copper case sat exactly where a careful man had placed it ninety years ago. Cylindrical. Sealed with wax and solder. Stamped with the Lockridge freight mark—a river wave beneath a mountain—which Mara had seen burned into crates her whole childhood and had never once wondered about.

She took it. It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it—the whole truth of Rivergate, the deaths of seven men and the lie of four generations, and it fit in one hand, and it was light as a tin of biscuits.

On the way up, her light swept the hold one last time, and she made herself do what no one had done for these men in ninety years: she looked at each of them. One wore a ring gone green with age. One was very young—younger than Tam. One had been laid with a folded cap under his head like a pillow, and Mara thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water entering her lungs, *someone loved him enough to try, in the last minutes, to make him comfortable,* and that someone had also drowned, and neither of them had ever been named, because naming them would have meant explaining them.

The surface came up to meet her, gray and loud with wind.

* * *

She broke the water beside the ramp and Tam hauled her in by the harness, and for a moment none of them spoke. Priya wrapped her in a blanket. Tam knelt in front of her, dripping, eyes wide, and Mara opened her hand and showed him the copper case, and watched her brother's face do what hers had done at fifteen meters down: collapse and rebuild itself into something harder.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's everything."

"It's everything," Mara said. Her voice came out scraped. "Grandfather's signature's inside. The scuttling order. The passenger list." She looked at the case sitting innocently on the stone between them, rain beginning to stipple the pool behind it. "Four generations of us have been living on top of a grave and calling it a foundation."

Priya crouched beside the case but didn't touch it. "Voss's divers opened the strongroom. Why didn't they take it?"

"Because she doesn't want it found," Tam said. "She wants it destroyed. There's a difference. Found, it can be argued about. Destroyed, it can only be denied." He laughed, short and bitter. "Our family's been denying it for ninety years. She's just the newest contractor."

"And when she comes," Priya said quietly, "and she will come, because Wren's route ends here and she knows this pool better than we do—when she comes, that case is the only leverage we have."

Mara picked it up. It was still almost weightless. She thought about the young one in the hold, the one younger than Tam, and about the folded cap, and about her own decade of clean, comfortable invisibility, purchased by never looking down.

"No," she said. "Leverage is what you trade away. This isn't leverage." She looked up at the quarry rim, at the storm-light gathering over the hills, at the river running high and brown toward the valley where four generations of names had kept this secret. "This is testimony. And testimony doesn't get traded."

Tam stared at her. "Then what do we do with it?"

Mara turned toward the head of the old loading ramp, where moss-grown stonework framed a square of deeper black—the quarry's original sluice channel, the floodgate that had once drained the pit, sealed shut since before any of them were born.

Above the hills, thunder cleared its throat.

"We wait for the storm," Mara said. "And then we let the river read it aloud."
