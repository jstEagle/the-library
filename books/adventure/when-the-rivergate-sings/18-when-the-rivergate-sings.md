## 18. When the Rivergate Sings

The storm arrived the way Mara had always imagined grief would come for her—not gradually, not with warning, but all at once, a wall of black water and wind that swallowed the gorge whole.

She stood at the quarry's rim with rain hammering her shoulders, watching the river below climb its banks in great brown surges. The flooded quarry pool lay beneath her like a held breath, dark and deep, cradling the bones of the *Meridian* and everything her family had paid to keep silent. Across the water, Voss's gunboat sat at anchor, its running lights smeared by rain, its boarding party still on the shore near the old pump house where they'd pinned Mara and Tam and Priya down an hour ago.

An hour. It felt like a year.

Tam crouched beside her behind a collapsed crane housing, his breath ragged, his hands—her grandfather's hands, their grandfather's hands—shaking as he cinched the last strap of the copper case to his chest rig. The manifest. She could feel it between them like a third person, dense and cold.

"The floodgates," he said again, because she'd said it first, ten minutes ago, and neither of them had stopped turning the idea over since. "You're sure."

"I'm sure the mechanism is there." Mara wiped rain from her eyes and looked down the quarry's eastern wall, where the 1911 engineers had cut a spillway into the rock—a channel meant to let the river flush the quarry after construction finished, sealed behind two iron gates the size of barn doors. Wren had told them about it at Cutter Bend, drawing it in spilled tea on the table: *My grandmother helped pour that concrete. They never unchained the gates. Never had reason to.* "I'm sure the chains are a hundred years old. And I'm sure the river is higher right now than it has been since before I was born."

"And if you open it," Priya said from her place against the crane housing, one arm pressed to the bandage on her side, her voice thin but level, "everything in that pool goes downriver. The wreck. The—" She stopped. Mara knew what word she wouldn't say. Bones. "Everything. Into Rivergate. Into the valley. In front of everyone."

"That's the point."

"It's also," Priya said, "a hundred years of water pressure and a storm surge hitting gates nobody has tested since 1912. If the channel is blocked, if the gates shear instead of swing, you'll be standing next to them when it happens."

Mara looked at her. Priya Chandrasekar, who had hidden in a rusted lockhouse with a broken wrist and a dead expedition around her, who had run flares through a hail of gunfire in the narrows, who had not once asked Mara why she'd spent a decade making herself into no one. Priya, who was bleeding through her bandage and still here.

"Then don't stand next to me," Mara said.

* * *

The plan came together the way plans did in storms—badly, quickly, out of desperation dressed up as strategy.

Voss wanted three things: the manifest, the ledgers, and silence. She had offered Mara the bargain an hour ago under a tarp strung between gun muzzles, and she had offered it honestly, which was somehow worse than offering it with a knife. *Your grandfather bought a hundred years of peace,* Voss had said, rain streaming off her chin, her voice carrying the flat patience of a woman who had done this arithmetic many times. *The families of this valley slept well because of what your family buried. You can be the one who keeps them sleeping. Or you can be the reason they wake up angry. Either way, I get paid.*

Mara had refused. Tam had nearly gotten them shot doing something reckless and half-brilliant with a flare gun. They'd scattered into the storm, and now Voss's people were spread along the quarry rim searching, patient as tide, waiting for the weather to do what bullets hadn't.

But Voss had made one mistake, and her name was Ilsa Voss: she believed the manifest was still in the pool. She didn't know Tam had already dived for it—that Mara had gone down into the cold black water while Tam ran the surface line, that her fingers had found the copper case wedged in the hold beneath a rib cage that had once been a man who loaded cargo for a living, that she had carried it up through forty feet of drowning dark with her family's signature burning a hole in her mind. Voss thought she had time. Voss thought the storm would pin them here until morning, when her divers could go down and make the pool give up its secret quietly.

Mara intended to make the pool give up everything else instead.

"Here's how it works," she said, huddled with Tam and Priya in the lee of the crane housing, shouting over the wind. "The gates are my job. The chains are old iron—I've got a cutting torch from the pump house, maybe twenty minutes' work if the rain lets me keep it lit." She looked at Tam. "While I'm doing that, Voss needs to believe the manifest is still down there. Her divers are rigged and ready on the gunboat. So we give her divers a reason to dive."

Tam's grin came slow and crooked, the same grin from the driftwood fire, from the cable tram, from every moment in the last five days when he'd chosen her over his own fear. "A light on the wreck site," he said. "Strobe. Underwater. Like somebody's down there cutting into the hold."

"You're going to swim down in this water and set a strobe?"

"I grew up on this river, Mara. This pool is the calmest water I'll have swum all week." He paused, and the grin faded into something older. "And if the gates open while I'm in the pool—"

"They won't. I'll signal. Three long flashes on the lamp before I cut the last chain. You'll have time to clear."

"You said that about the skiff in the first rapids."

"The skiff," Mara said, "did not have me operating it."

Priya laughed—a short, pained bark—and then pressed her hand harder against her side. "And me?"

Mara turned to her last piece of the plan, the one she hated most. "The gunboat. When the gates go, Voss will have exactly one thought: save her equipment, control the story, get her divers out of the water before the surge hits. Every hand on that boat will be looking at the quarry mouth. That's when someone needs to be aboard."

"No," Tam started.

"Somebody," Mara went on, "needs to take that wheel, get that boat moving downriver ahead of the flood, and put it where the whole valley can see it flying Voss colors with Voss divers' gear stacked on deck—next to whatever's floating out of that quarry. Radios, survey equipment, the works. Evidence doesn't mean anything unless it arrives somewhere public, and that boat is the most public thing on this river tonight."

"That's a two-person job," Priya said quietly. "Boarding, holding the wheel, running the boat in flood current. One person gets shot; the other drives."

"I know."

"So it's me and Tam," Priya said, and when Mara opened her mouth: "Mara. You're the only one who knows those gates. You're the only one Wren trusted with the mechanism. And you're the only one Voss will follow—if she sees you at the gates, she'll commit everyone to stopping you, and that pulls her crew off the boat. You're the bait whether you like it or not." A ghost of a smile. "Invisible girl. Be visible for five minutes. It's the loudest thing you'll ever do."

Mara looked between them—the brother she'd known for five days and hated for twenty years, the stranger who'd become the closest thing to crew she'd ever had—and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't fear.

"Three long flashes," she said. "Then run like the river itself."

* * *

The descent to the spillway took her twenty minutes alone. Twenty minutes of climbing down rusted rebar stirs in the spillway access shaft, rain turning to horizontal sheets, lightning showing her the gates below—two vast iron leaves furred with a century of silt and moss, chained at the center with links thick as her wrist.

The torch lit on the fourth try. Its blue flame hissed and guttered in the wet, and Mara fed it oxygen and steel and will, and began to cut.

The first chain link parted at the two-minute mark. The second took longer; the iron fought her, swollen with age, and sparks sprayed sideways into the deluge and died instantly, and somewhere above her she heard voices—Voss's searchers, working the rim, close enough that she killed the torch and flattened herself against the gate and counted to sixty with her heart slamming her ribs.

They moved on. She cut.

Link by link. The chain sagged. Behind the gates she could hear it now—not see, hear—the river pressing, a low groan through the iron like a whale song played through stone, like something enormous waking up. The water seeped through the seams in silver threads, and each thread hummed.

Her radio crackled. Tam's voice, shredded by static: "*—strobe's set—coming up—Voss launched her divers—they bit—*"

Good. Good. She cut faster.

Two links left when the floodlight hit her.

It came from the rim above—white, brutal, pinning her to the gate like a specimen. Through the glare she heard Voss's voice, amplified by a megaphone, almost conversational under the storm:

"Mara Lockridge. Step away from the gate."

Mara didn't step away. She lifted the torch so the flame stood between them, small and blue and absurd, and she looked up into the light until she found the silhouette behind it—Voss at the rim, raincoated, immovable.

"You're standing on a hundred-year-old dam foundation in a monsoon," Mara called back. "You should be more worried about the ground than the gate."

"I know what you're doing." No anger in it. That was the terrible thing about Ilsa Voss; there was never any anger, only assessment. "You think opening these gates makes you righteous. It makes you a vandal. Everything in that pool stays in that pool or comes out controlled, with names attached properly, with process—or it comes out as mud and rumor and thirty families tearing each other apart. I've seen what happens when history arrives without a chaperone. It kills people."

"So does silence!" The words tore out of her louder than the wind. "I spent ten years learning how to disappear because my family taught me that disappearing was safety! My grandfather drowned forty men and called it peace! You want to chaperone that?" She raised the torch. "The river's done being chaperoned."

"Last chance," Voss said. "Walk away, and you walk away rich. Your brother too. That's not a bribe, Lockridge. It's the same offer your grandfather accepted, adjusted for inflation."

And Mara—who had refused the offer an hour ago, who would refuse it a hundred times—understood suddenly, with total clarity, why Voss kept asking. Not because she expected yes. Because every no cost Mara something, and Voss was counting the cost, spending it down, waiting for the account to empty.

Mara smiled into the floodlight. "Three flashes," she said—to Voss, to herself, to the river—and cut the second-to-last link.

Gunfire cracked. Sparks off the iron inches from her face. She dropped flat, rolled behind the gate's hinge boss, and kept cutting blind, by feel, the torch screaming, the chain groaning—

One link left.

Above, shouts. A flare went up from the direction of the gunboat—red, then green. Priya and Tam. They were aboard or they were dead; the flare code Wren taught them couldn't tell her which.

She cut.

The last link parted, and for one full second nothing happened. The gates stood in their frame, free and unmoving, and Mara had time to think, *a hundred years of silt, oh God, they're welded shut, it was all for—*

Then the river spoke.

Not a groan. A voice. The gates shuddered, bowed inward like lungs filling, and burst—not swung, *burst*—and the quarry pool exhaled a century in a single roar. Water hit the spillway channel like a freight train derailing, black and thunderous, and the pool began to drop, and everything the pool had held began to rise.

Mara clung to the hinge boss as the surge sucked past her feet, dragging silt and debris and the smell of old wood and older iron. Below, in the churning torch-lit chaos, shapes came loose from the depths: planking from the *Meridian*'s hull, pale and splintered. A cargo winch, tumbling end over end. Diver's tanks—Voss's divers' tanks, abandoned mid-survey, spinning away downstream. And other things. Lighter things. Things that moved with a terrible human slowness, that the river carried gently, respectfully, out through the broken gates and into the flood and down toward Rivergate, where lanterns were already appearing along the levee, where people were coming out into the storm to watch their river run strange and high and full.

The rivergate was singing. A hundred years late, in a voice of storm and bone, but singing—every drowned man's testimony rolling downvalley in the flood, undeniable, unownable, free.

Mara pressed her forehead against the wet iron and laughed, or sobbed, or both, and let the sound tear out of her and join the roar.

* * *

The gunboat fought the current like a living thing.

Tam had the wheel. His hands remembered boats even after twenty years away from this river—wheel hard aport, throttle feathered against the surge, riding the flood's spine the way Wren had described riding it, the way no chart showed because no chart believed in it. Rain lashed the glass. Behind them, the quarry mouth glowed with Voss's floodlights, and the flood itself glowed brighter, carrying its cargo of wood and bone and truth down the gorge.

On the deck behind him, Priya knelt over the two crewmen they'd subdued—one out cold, one zip-tied to the rail with his own restraints—and then moved to the stern, where the divers' equipment racks stood bolted down: radios, survey gear, sample cases, the whole apparatus of controlled forgetting. She started unbolt-ing them and stacking them in plain view amidships, high and visible, a museum no one had consented to build.

"Contact astern!" Tam shouted. In the floodlight glow, Voss's second vessel—a workboat packed with boarding crew—was swinging out of the quarry mouth, chasing them down the flood.

"They won't catch us in this current," Priya yelled back, hauling a case up onto the stack.

"They don't need to catch us!" Tam threw a look over his shoulder, and his face in the binnacle light was wild and young and terrified and certain. "They need to stop us before Rivergate! Before witnesses! Priya—hold on!"

He put the wheel over and ran the gunboat straight down the throat of the flood.

The river took them. It should have destroyed them—it should have destroyed anything—but Tam flew it the way Mara read sign, instinctively, prayerfully, reading the water's grammar: the V of a submerged snag to port, the smooth tongue of current off the point, the place where the flood briefly slackened in the lee of the bluff. The workboat fell behind. The lights of Rivergate grew ahead, and the levee was lined now, hundreds of them, lanterns and phone lights and truck headlights, the whole town come out into the rain to watch the impossible river.

And floating down among them, gentle as leaves: planks. Timbers. And shapes that made the lanterns begin to shake.

The gunboat grounded itself on the town landing bar with a grinding shudder, and Tam killed the engine, and for a moment there was only rain and the river's roar and the silence of two hundred people staring at the boat flying Voss colors, stacked with Voss equipment, tied to a story the water was telling them plank by plank by bone.

Priya came and stood beside him at the rail. Her side was bleeding again; her hands were steady.

"They're going to want to know whose bones those are," she said quietly.

Tam watched the crowd, watched the lanterns, watched the first figures wade out into the shallows to lift a splintered hull plank from the water like something holy.

"Our family's," he said. "All of ours, one way or another." He gripped the rail. "Come on. We'd better be there when they ask."

Behind them, upriver, the flood roared on through the broken gates, and the quarry pool drained toward empty, and the *Meridian* rose out of hiding for the first time in a hundred years—unburied, unowned, and finally, finally heard.
