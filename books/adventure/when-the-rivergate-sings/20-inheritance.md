## 20. Inheritance

The valley assembly met in the old grain exchange at Rivergate, a hall built by the same hands that had built the *Meridian*—and, if Tam's ledgers were honest, paid for with the same blood. Rain still dripped from its eaves nine days after the monsoon broke, and the gutters sang a thin, constant note that Mara could not stop hearing.

She stood at the back of the hall while they set up. She had learned long ago how to stand in a room without being part of it: weight even on both feet, eyes down, presence folded small. A decade of practice. But today the trick kept failing her. People looked. They turned in their seats and looked, and whispered her name like it was something they'd found washed up on the bank.

The manifest lay on the clerk's table under glass, because half the valley still didn't believe it was real and the other half was afraid someone would steal it back into silence. The copper case sat open beside it, green-crusted, river-smelling even now. Mara had pried it out of the hold of the *Meridian* with her own hands, in water so cold her fingers had stopped reporting to her, and she could still feel the seam of it against her palm when she wasn't paying attention.

Priya found her at the back, moving carefully on the leg Voss's gunboat had nearly taken from her. The wound had closed to an angry pink seam; the limp would be permanent, the doctors said, and Priya had said good, something to remember them by.

"You're doing the thing," Priya said.

"What thing."

"The standing-in-the-corner thing. The Mara Lockridge is not actually here thing." She leaned on her crutch and studied her. "It doesn't work anymore. You're legible now. Get used to it."

"I liked being illegible."

"I know." Priya's voice gentled. "That's the part you have to grieve."

Tam came through the doors with Wren, both of them rain-damp, both of them carrying boxes—the ledgers, the affidavits from the sea cave survivors, the letters Wren's people had kept for a hundred and thirteen years without ever once saying aloud why. Tam caught Mara's eye across the room and gave her the small nod that had become their whole language: *here, still here.*

The assembly bell rang. The hall settled like water going flat.

* * *

They read the manifest aloud.

Not all of it—it ran forty pages, most of it freight tonnage and insurance figures—but the assembly's reader, an old schoolteacher named Halloway with a voice like gravel poured slowly, read every line that mattered. The charter of the Lockridge Barge Company, 1904. The labor contracts for the dockworkers' cooperative, 1909. And then the page everyone had come to hear:

*Requisition order, October 1911. One barge, Meridian, to be ballasted and sunk at Cutter Bend following loading of strike personnel. Disposition of cargo and personnel to be recorded as loss due to river conditions. Authorized: E. Lockridge. Countersigned: A. Voss, Marine Assurance.*

Halloway read it twice. The second time, he read slower, and the names of the thirty-one workers followed—names that matched, one for one, the graves Wren tended at Cutter Bend, names that had never appeared in any town record until this morning.

No one cheered. Mara had expected, without admitting she expected it, some kind of release—a roar, weeping, the catharsis of a secret finally out. Instead there was only a vast, held quiet, three hundred people breathing together, and somewhere in it a woman began to cry in the small, controlled way of someone who has been crying about the same thing for years and had simply run out of places to hide it.

Mara knew that sound. She had made it herself, once, alone in a ranger station with a corrupted transmission playing, when she understood that the world was arranged to let certain people drown on schedule.

The reading took two hours. When it ended, Halloway set the pages down and said, "So entered into the record of the valley assembly, this day, in perpetuity," and the quiet finally broke—not into cheering but into motion, people turning to each other, the low urgent hum of a town rewriting its own history in real time.

* * *

They came for her at midday.

Not a mob—she'd braced for that too, and been ashamed of bracing. A delegation. The assembly's steering committee, seven of them, led by a broad-shouldered woman named Ferro who ran the ferry co-op and had, Mara later learned, a great-grandmother on the manifest's list of the drowned.

"Mara Lockridge," Ferro said, in front of everyone, because privacy was over for her and Ferro clearly knew it. "The estate registry lists you as sole surviving principal heir. The Lockridge holdings—riverfront parcels, the quarry pool, the salvage rights, the company charters, the endowment accounts. It comes to more than anyone in this valley can count. Legally, it's yours."

"It was never mine," Mara said. "It was theirs." She nodded at the manifest under its glass. "Paid for in advance."

"Maybe." Ferro didn't flinch. "But the law is dumber than justice, and the law says it's yours. So the question isn't whether you own it. The question is what you do while you do."

Someone in the crowd called out, "Take the seat!" and someone else shushed them, and Mara felt the old reflex rise—the door-finding reflex, the exit-mapping reflex, ten years of making herself unfindable rising up like floodwater and asking her, reasonably, *why are you still in this room?*

She looked for her crew instead.

Tam stood near the windows with his arms crossed, and he didn't nod this time, didn't signal anything at all. This one was hers, and he knew it. Priya leaned on her crutch beside him, watching Mara with the level patience of someone who had spent weeks trapped in a rusted lockhouse waiting for rescue and had learned exactly how long patience could hold.

Wren was the one who moved. The old hermit came forward through the crowd—people parted for Wren the way water parts around a piling—and stopped at the edge of the committee's circle, hands folded, rain-stiff gray hair pushed back from a face lined like a riverbank.

"Miss Lockridge," Wren said, and the hall went quiet again, because Wren's voice carried the way church bells carry. "I've tended thirty-one graves at Cutter Bend for forty-one years. My grandmother helped dig them. Her mother helped dig them. Nobody ever asked us why we did it, and we never said, because saying it would have gotten us run off the river, and somebody had to stay to keep the names." Wren's eyes were dry and very steady. "You asked us why, the night you sheltered with me. First person in my lifetime to ask. So I'll tell you what inheritance looks like from where I've stood."

Wren turned, slow, and looked out at the hall—at the mill families, the dock crews, the salvage men who'd worked for Voss and now wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, the descendants scattered through the crowd who hadn't known they were descendants until this morning.

"Inheritance isn't the money," Wren said. "Money's just the shape the theft left behind. Inheritance is the keeping. Somebody keeps the truth or somebody keeps the silence, and either way they hand it to you, and either way you keep it. That family of yours kept silence for four generations. Kept it beautiful. Kept it funded." A ripple went through the hall, hard and cold. "And now the silence's keeper is standing right there, and the whole valley is watching to see which thing she keeps."

Ferro turned back to Mara. "The seat's yours if you want it. The name, the holdings, the chair on the valley board your grandfather bought in 1912 with Meridian insurance money. All of it. Or—" and here Ferro paused, and Mara saw that beneath the ferry captain's bluntness there was something almost like mercy, "—or you tell us what it should be instead, and we'll make the law catch up. We're good at that. We've had practice being lied to."

The hall waited.

Mara walked to the clerk's table. Her boots sounded enormous on the floorboards. Under the glass, the requisition order stared up at her, her grandfather's signature looping across the authorization line with the easy confidence of a man signing for a delivery of grain. *Authorized: E. Lockridge.* She had his handwriting. She'd known that since she was nine, practicing her letters, when her father had held her hand up next to a framed letter and said, *Look at that. Same hand. It's in the blood.*

She had spent twenty years trying to prove him wrong about everything else. She'd never once thought to question that.

"I don't want the seat," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I've spent ten years erasing myself from records. If I take that chair, I'll spend ten more learning to sit in it, and the valley can't wait ten years, and honestly—" a rough breath of almost-laughter went through the crowd at that, "—honestly, I'd be bad at it. I'm good at finding things. I'm good at rivers. I'm not good at rooms."

"But," she said, before relief could settle, "the estate isn't mine to refuse, either. Refusing it just leaves it sitting there, a locked house full of stolen money, waiting for the next Voss to pick the lock. So here's what I want done with it."

She laid it out plainly, the way she'd write a patrol report: the riverfront parcels and the quarry pool deeded to a trust, governed jointly by the assembly and the descendants of the thirty-one—Wren's people first among them, and Wren's face when she said it was the only thing in the hall Mara couldn't look at directly. The salvage rights revoked outright; no fleet would drag the gorge for profit again, and the *Meridian* itself would stay where it lay, undisturbed, a grave and not a site. The endowment accounts—decades of compounded interest on insurance fraud—split between the river towns' schools and ferries and the families, with the first year's disbursement earmarked for the expedition survivors and the search costs Command had refused to authorize.

"And the name?" Ferro asked. "The Lockridge name. You could bury it. Nobody'd argue."

Mara considered it. God, she considered it. Twenty years of wanting exactly that—one clean stroke, the name struck from every ledger, herself finally, fully erased.

"No," she said. "Burying it is just another kind of keeping. Let it stand in the record, attached to what it did. Let every kid in this valley learn it the way they learn the flood years. Some things shouldn't be forgotten comfortably."

She signed the transfer deed at the clerk's table with the same hand her grandfather had used, and this time the signature meant something different, and she felt the difference travel up her arm like current.

* * *

Afterward, the hall emptied slowly, the way water leaves a flooded field. People came to her in ones and twos—not to thank her, mostly, but to show her things. A pocket watch with a name engraved inside. A photograph of a man who had boarded the *Meridian* on a Tuesday and never come home. An old woman who pressed Mara's hand between both of hers and said only, "My grandfather. Thank you for saying his name out loud where I could hear it," and left before Mara could answer, which was a mercy, because Mara had nothing.

By evening the hall held only her crew and the smell of rain and old grain.

Tam dropped onto the bench beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was still new enough to feel like an event. "You know the ledgers list a second account," he said. "Off-book. Grandfather kept it separate from everything we just transferred. Money that never touched the company books at all."

Mara went still. "How much?"

"A lot. Enough to matter." He turned the ledger toward her, tapped a column of figures in faded ink. "And no legal claim on it. No heirs in the registry. It's ghost money, Mar. It belongs to nobody, which means it belongs to whoever finds it."

Priya, sprawled along the opposite bench with her leg propped on a crate, opened one eye. "There's a *but*. There's always a but. I've been on this crew long enough to hear the but coming."

"The account's not in Rivergate," Tam said. "The deposits trace to a bank in Salt Landing. Downriver. Past the delta, past everything we just spent six tides surviving." He looked at Mara, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the guilt he'd carried since the reeds, not the bargaining look from the night she'd overheard his radio call. Something lighter. Almost a grin. "The skiff needs rebuilding anyway. Wren already said he knows a route through the delta that isn't on any chart. And Priya says her doctors cleared her for sitting down, which is apparently all a skiff requires."

Mara looked at the ledger. At the ghost money, the last unclaimed piece of the theft, sitting downriver behind whatever locks and silences the Lockridges had built around it.

She thought about the desert, and how it had taught her that nothing out there answers. She thought about the grain exchange gutters singing, and the woman crying quietly in row twelve, and Wren's voice saying *somebody keeps the truth or somebody keeps the silence.*

"Well," she said, closing the ledger. "Somebody should go answer it."

Outside, the river ran high and loud through the heart of Rivergate, and for the first time in a hundred and thirteen years, it had nothing left to hide.
