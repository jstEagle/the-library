## 21. Crew of the Skiff

The skiff came home on a flatbed truck borrowed from the Rivergate ferry works, strapped down with ratchet straps that groaned at every pothole between the quarry and Wren's bend. Mara rode in the cab and watched it through the back window the whole way, the way you'd watch a wounded animal you weren't sure would survive the night.

It had no business surviving anything. The hull was stove in along the port chine where the first gorge rapids had thrown it against rock, the transom was cracked clean through from Voss's wake, the starboard gunwale had been peeled back like bark by something in the flooded side channel, and the outboard—Priya's outboard, technically, salvaged off a wrecked survey boat—was full of river silt and one very dead fish.

Wren took one look at it when they unloaded it onto the gravel above Cutter Bend and said, "That's not a boat. That's an apology."

"It got us here," Tam said.

"So did your legs," Wren said. "Doesn't mean I'd build a skiff out of them."

But Wren was already walking around it, hands behind their back, reading the damage the way Mara read sign—slowly, without touching, letting the thing tell its own story. Wren had lived on this stretch of river for longer than anyone could say, tending the graves of the 1911 dead and mending whatever the water gave back. Boats came to Wren the way lost hikers came to Mara: broken, and needing someone who didn't flinch at what they'd been through.

"Transom's the heart of it," Wren said finally. "Crack runs through both knees. We'll need new oak. There's a fallen elm upriver past the second bend, seasoned two years where it dropped. Good wood. It's been waiting."

Mara looked at the skiff. "Waiting for what?"

"For somebody to need it," Wren said, as if that were obvious, and went to find their tools.

* * *

They worked for nine days.

Not because the work needed nine days—a yard crew could have done it in three—but because none of them knew how to do anything quickly anymore. They had spent weeks moving at the speed of fear: running rapids at night, portaging in silence, diving cold water with their hearts hammering. Now there was no fleet behind them, no monsoon ahead, no clock made of tides. The river ran low and green-gold under the late-season sun, and every morning it seemed mildly surprised that anyone was still in a hurry about anything.

So they were slow. They split the elm into planks with wedges and a maul, and the wood came apart along its grain with a sound like a long exhale. Tam steamed the planks over a drum fire until they bent soft as ribbon, and it turned out he was good at it—patient in a way Mara had never seen him be, holding each plank against the hull frame until it stopped fighting and agreed to become part of the curve.

"You learned this somewhere," she said, watching him work.

"Rivergate boatyards. Summers, when I was fourteen." He didn't look up. "Before Dad decided I was more useful as a cautionary tale."

Mara let that sit. Twenty years of erasure sat between them—the half-brother scrubbed from photographs, from the family ledgers, from her own memory until he was just a name she wasn't allowed to say—and twenty years couldn't be steamed soft and bent into place in an afternoon. But he'd come back. He'd carried their grandfather's ledgers through a war zone of a river to put their family's signature on a massacre into the light. Whatever else Tam Lockridge was, he was not the man their father had decided he was.

"Hand me the clamp," she said, and that was as much forgiveness as either of them needed today.

* * *

Priya couldn't do heavy work—not yet, not with the wound in her side still stitched and angry—but she appointed herself quality control, which meant she sat on an overturned crate in the shade with her leg propped on a cooler and narrated everything they did wrong.

"That seam's proud," she'd call out. "You'll take water there."

"We'll caulk it," Tam said.

"You'll caulk it *badly*, is what you'll do, because you're rushing. You people rush. It's a desert-river thing. Everything's an emergency." She turned a page of the battered survey manual she'd recovered from the expedition camp, which she now treated as scripture. "In my family we built dhows. My uncle said a boat takes exactly as long as it takes, and if you argue with the boat, the boat wins."

"Your uncle sounds like Wren," Mara said.

"Everyone wise sounds like everyone else wise eventually," Priya said. "It's how you know it's wisdom and not just opinion."

She was better than she admitted. The color had come back to her face by the fourth day, and by the sixth she was down at the waterline testing caulking samples herself, pressing each cured bead with her thumb, frowning at them like a jeweler. On the seventh day she stood for a full hour without sitting down, and when Mara noticed, Priya caught her looking and said, "Don't make it a thing," and Mara didn't, and that was its own kind of care.

* * *

Wren taught them all something different.

To Tam, joinery: how to read grain, how to let wood move, how a fastening too rigid becomes a failure point. "River doesn't hold still," Wren said. "Neither does anything built to live on it. Build it loose enough to breathe."

To Priya, the old lock lights—though she already knew most of it; mostly Wren confirmed what she'd guessed and filled in the names of the men who'd kept the lights burning in 1911, some of whom lay in the graves up the hill.

To Mara, Wren taught nothing, or seemed to. Wren just talked while they worked, the way old people do, stories unspooling without apparent purpose—about the flood of '63, about a hermit before Wren who'd kept the same graves, about a dog that had once swum the gorge in high water and lived to be insufferable about it for twelve years. It took Mara most of the week to realize the stories had shapes, and the shapes were lessons: about patience, about repair, about the difference between fixing a thing and making it new.

On the eighth day, sanding the new transom smooth, Mara said, "Why do you stay here? Really. You could live anywhere."

Wren considered this with the seriousness they brought to everything. "Somebody has to remember where the dead are buried," they said. "And somebody has to be here when the living wash up." They blew sawdust off the transom. "Besides. Where would I go that the river isn't?"

* * *

On the ninth day they flipped the skiff, and it was a boat again.

Not the boat it had been—it was heavier in the stern, fairer in the lines, the new elm pale against the old weathered planking like a scar that hadn't tanned yet. Priya had painted a small name near the bow, unprompted, in careful white letters: **MERIDIAN'S ANSWER**. Nobody discussed it. Everybody understood it. The barge that had gone down in 1911 with the striking dockworkers aboard, scuttled on the order signed by a Lockridge hand—the barge whose bones had finally spoken, whose manifest had been read aloud to the whole valley—deserved an answer, and this battered little skiff was as good an answer as any.

They launched her at evening, when the light went long and amber across the water. Wren poured a measure of river over the bow—"she's already wet, but she doesn't know it yet"—and Priya ran the engine, and the rebuilt outboard caught on the third pull and settled into a steady, even idle that sounded, Mara thought, like breathing.

Tam sat in the bow. Priya held the tiller. Wren stayed on the bank, arms folded, wearing the expression of someone who has launched many boats and expects to launch many more.

"Well?" Tam called back. "Where to, Captain?"

He used the word lightly, but Mara heard the question underneath it, the real one, the one he'd been carrying since the quarry: *What happens to us now?*

Because the mission was over. The manifest had been read. The estate was in trust, the assembly had dispersed, Voss was negotiating her way into a smaller life, and the valley was busy arguing about markers and memorials and money, which was how valleys processed grief. There was nothing left to run toward. And four people who had become a crew under fire had to decide, now, in peacetime, whether they were still a crew—or whether they'd only ever been a temporary arrangement of necessity, the way strangers on a lifeboat are a crew.

Mara looked downriver.

She'd spent ten years going nowhere. Erasing herself from records, patrolling empty country, keeping her name out of every ledger and her face out of every photograph. She'd told herself it was discipline. She knew now it had been exile—the loneliness she'd chosen because choosing it felt safer than being chosen wrong. Her whole adult life had been a skiff with one person aboard, and she'd called that freedom.

Then she looked at the bow, where Tam was watching her with their father's eyes but none of their father's certainty. At Priya, who navigated by instinct and stubbornness and had bled in the narrows because Mara's plan required someone to stand in the open with flares. Back at the bank, where Wren stood among the graves they tended, keeper of a memory the whole valley had tried to drown.

Family chosen, not assigned. She hadn't known such a thing existed until six tides ago.

"Downriver," she said. "Past the mouth, out to the coast. There's a salvage registry office in Port Calloway that still has expedition records nobody's archived—families of the 1911 crew have a right to those manifests' supporting papers, and nobody's filed the claim." She shrugged. "After that, wherever the crew decides."

Priya grinned and swung the tiller. Tam laughed—actually laughed, a sound Mara was still learning to recognize—and the skiff came about and gathered way, the new elm flexing the way good wood does, breathing with the water instead of fighting it.

Behind them, Wren raised one hand, not waving so much as releasing them, the way you release a hawk.

The river took them. It sang under the hull, low and constant, the same song it had sung in 1911 and would sing long after every name on every marker had worn smooth—and for the first time in ten years, Mara Lockridge, who had spent a decade making sure no record anywhere contained her name, found she didn't mind being part of a crew of four, bound for Port Calloway, registered in no ledger but the ones that mattered:

Who was aboard. Who was trusted. Who was going together.

The current carried them toward the sea, and nobody asked it to hurry.
