## 1. The Map That Never Unfolds

The rain came into Harrow Bend sideways, the way it always did, driven up the valley by a wind that seemed to hold a grudge against the town. Jude Thornbury stood at the window of the Harrow Bend Hotel's second-floor corner room and watched it hammer the tin roofs below, silvering the streetlights, turning the coal smoke from the roundhouse into low gray banners that dragged along the ground. Somewhere under all that weather, four locomotives idled through the night on the ready track, breathing steam like animals too tired to sleep.

She had been in Harrow Bend for eleven days. It was, by her own strict accounting, nine days too many.

Jude did not stay anywhere longer than she had to. That was not a quirk or a superstition, whatever the conductors who knew her said when she wasn't listening. It was arithmetic. Every town held someone who might be looking, and every extra day multiplied the chance of being found. She kept her stays short, her accounts paid in cash, her name spelled differently on every register — Thornbury, Thornberry, Thorne, once memorably T. Burnham-Jude, which had been a mistake born of exhaustion and cheap wine. The map of closed lines lived folded inside her coat lining, drawn in her own hand over fifteen years of riding dead rails: every abandoned spur, every washed-out trestle, every line the companies had stricken from their public timetables but never quite erased from the earth. It was the most valuable thing she owned, and the only thing she would burn before letting anyone take it.

Below false ledger pages, that is. In her trunk, beneath three years of meticulously forged survey ledgers — drainage figures for culverts that didn't exist, gradient calculations for lines that had never been proposed — the map lay flat between sheets of oiled canvas, waiting. Anyone who went through her papers looking for the woman who testified against Aldous Crane would find a boring engineer with dull handwriting and no history worth arresting.

She turned from the window. The hotel room was clean in the way rooms are clean when nobody loves them: iron bedstead, washstand, a chair with one leg shimmed, a lamp turned down to a coin of light. Her instruments were laid out on the bed in their felt rolls — level, transit, chain, the good German calipers Vess had given her the year she turned twenty — because tomorrow morning she would sign a contract, and she wanted her hands to remember their work tonight.

The contract was why she'd stayed past her own limit. Nine days ago, a letter had reached her poste restante in Harrow Bend, forwarded twice, which meant someone had gone to trouble. *Survey required: Rivergate spur, twelve miles, mountain grade, closed 1898. Compensation generous. Report to county engineer's office.* No signature she recognized. No reason given for why a line dead for twenty years suddenly needed measuring.

Jude had read the letter four times in the hotel lobby, then walked out into the rain and stood under the awning of the feed store until she'd stopped hearing her own pulse. Because she knew the Rivergate spur. Of course she knew it. It was on her map — a thin pencil line running up the gorge east of Rivergate, annotated in her small cramped hand: *closed after rockslide. ask about the slide. nobody will talk.*

She had written that annotation seven years ago, standing on the platform at Rivergate while a station agent looked at her as if she'd asked him to describe his own funeral. Nobody would talk. She'd moved on, the way she always moved on, and the spur had stayed a pencil line and a question.

Now somebody wanted it surveyed, and they wanted *her* to do it, and Jude Thornbury had survived this long by believing that coincidence was just conspiracy moving slowly enough to look innocent.

* * *

The county engineer's office occupied the front room of a building that had once been a bank, judging by the vault door still set into the back wall, painted over so many times it looked like a gray egg. A clerk named Mr. Pellis received her at nine sharp, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular politeness of someone who has been told exactly how much he is allowed to say.

"Miss Thorne," he said, reading her latest alias off her card without blinking. "You come recommended."

"By whom?"

"That I'm not at liberty to say." He slid the contract across the desk. "Twelve miles of line, condition assessment, bridge and tunnel inventory. You'll have access to the old company records for right-of-way boundaries." He paused, and his eyes came up from the paper. "The compensation is generous because the work is difficult. The gorge road is gone. You'll go in by rail as far as Milepost Four, then walk."

"I've walked worse grades."

"I don't doubt it." Pellis blotted nothing; there was nothing yet to blot. His pen hovered. "There is one other matter. You will not be the only party interested in that corridor this season. There is a salvage concern — the Meridian Salvage and Recovery Company — operating under charter out of Rivergate harbor. If you encounter their crews, you are to complete your survey without interference, and you are to report any... irregularities you observe."

"What kind of irregularities?"

"The kind one observes," Pellis said, and signed the contract, and pushed it across the desk for her countersignature, and would say nothing else about anything at all.

Jude read the contract twice, all of it, including the clause that gave the county first claim on any findings and a smaller clause, tucked near the bottom, granting her expenses plus a bonus "for timely completion prior to seasonal closure of river navigation." Seasonal closure. She filed that phrase away where she filed everything, in the cold organized room behind her eyes where facts sat on shelves waiting to be needed.

She signed as J. Thorne. Her hand did not hesitate, because her hand never hesitated; hesitation was a smell, and dogs could be trained to it, and some men were dogs.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the railway reading room above the depot, pulling everything Harrow Bend had on the Rivergate division, and found almost nothing, which was itself a finding. Divisions that die natural deaths leave corpses everywhere — valuation maps, abandonment filings, scrap contracts, angry letters from towns that lost their depots. The Rivergate spur left a single sheet: a notice of emergency closure dated October 1898, citing "rockslide obstruction, main gorge segment, traffic suspended pending clearance," followed by a second notice, eight months later, quietly converting suspension to permanent closure. No clearance ever performed. No scrap contract. No abandonment filing through the state board, which should have been legally impossible.

A line doesn't just stop existing on paper. Someone had made it stop, and done it carefully, and the care was what frightened her.

She was copying the closure dates into her notebook when the stairs creaked behind her, and she knew before she turned that the man coming up them had been waiting for her to sit down.

He was ordinary in the deliberate way of men paid to be unremarkable: medium height, brown coat, a face you'd forget before he finished shaking your hand. He carried a newspaper under one arm and wore the expression of a fellow traveler killing an hour out of the rain.

"Mind if I sit? Place is empty as a church on market day."

"It's a public room," Jude said, and went back to her notes, writing nothing, feeling him settle two chairs away with the careful economy of a man who had chosen his seat for sightlines.

For a while he read his newspaper. Rain ticked against the tall windows. Down in the yard a switch engine shuffled cars with the patience of a beast sorting grain, and the sound filled the silence the way water fills a lock.

"You're the surveyor," he said at last. Not a question.

"I'm a surveyor. There's a difference."

"Rivergate spur." He folded the newspaper with crisp attention, though she noticed he hadn't opened it since sitting down. "That's a long walk for one woman with a transit."

"People keep saying that like it's a compliment."

He smiled as if she'd pleased him. "My employer has interests in the gorge. Freight interests. We understand a government team surveyed that same corridor recently — spring of last year, I believe. Seven men." He watched her over the folded paper. "You'd know something of that, perhaps. Their report was never delivered."

"I survey lines," Jude said. "I don't survey people."

"No. But people leave traces on lines. Camps. Caches. Instruments." He unfolded the newspaper fully now, and she saw it was three weeks old and had been read to death already. "Their gear was found stacked neatly, I'm told. Boots by the fire. Everything zeroed and stowed, like men stepping out for supper." His voice stayed pleasant, conversational, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "No tracks leaving camp. None. In mud you could cast a boot print in wax from."

Jude kept her eyes on her notebook. Inside her chest, something old and cold turned over, the way ice turns over in a river in late winter — silently, hugely, changing the whole current underneath.

Seven men. Neat camp. No tracks. She had heard none of this, and now she would never unknow it, and the stranger knew precisely what he was doing by telling her.

"Sounds like a story somebody tells to keep surveyors away," she said. "Cheaper than hiring men to scare them off."

"Could be." He rose, tucking the dead newspaper under his arm. At the top of the stairs he paused, and for one moment the pleasant blankness fell away like a mask slipping, and what was underneath was not menace exactly but *attention*, total and patient, a lighthouse beam passing over her once. "One more thing, Miss Thorne. Your last employer — the one who recommends you so highly. That would be Mr. Vess, wouldn't it? Marcus Vess, of the old Mountain Division?"

The rain filled the pause.

"I don't discuss clients," Jude said.

"Of course not. Neither does he." And he went down the stairs, unhurried, and out into the rain, and she heard the street door close with the soft finality of a book shutting.

* * *

She did not go back to the hotel. She walked, instead, down through the yard in the rain, past the coaling tower dripping black water, out along the ready track to where the four idle locomotives breathed their slow steam, and she let the rain run off her hat brim and thought.

Vess. Marcus Vess, who had pulled her out of Cordova Junction at nineteen with a fast horse and a forged bill of lading after she stood up in a courthouse and named Aldous Crane a thief and a murderer; Vess, who taught her the trade and taught her the rule — *never sleep twice in the same town, girl, because the men Crane owns don't forget faces, and neither do the ones he pays* — Vess, whose name was on the recommendation that brought her here, to a line that died wrong, in the same season a government team of seven men stepped out of existence leaving their boots neatly by the fire.

Either Vess had sent her into danger, or Vess had sent her toward truth, and after fifteen years she still could not tell those two things apart in him. Maybe he couldn't either.

She stood a long time among the sleeping engines, and the fear that lived in her — the fear she dressed up every morning as competence, as restlessness, as professional pride — spoke in its usual voice: *keep moving, don't sign anything real, be gone by Thursday.* And another voice, newer, colder, answered: *seven men, neat camp, no tracks, and a stranger who knows your teacher's name.*

Jude Thornbury took out her notebook, and under the closure dates she wrote three words in her small cramped hand:

*Ask about Vess.*

Then she closed the book, turned up her collar, and went back through the rain to pack, because win or lose, she was taking the contract, and the first train up the valley left at dawn — and somewhere ahead of her, six tides were already counting themselves down without her knowing it.
