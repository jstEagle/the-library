## 2. Six Tides

The river at Rivergate did not so much flow as argue with itself.

Jude heard it before she saw it—she'd been walking the last mile of the county road in the blue hour before dawn, pack straps biting her shoulders, and the sound came up out of the gorge like a crowd muttering behind a wall. Not the clean chatter of ordinary water over stones. This was deeper, older, a grinding complaint with weight behind it, the noise of a river carrying its winter grudges down from the high country. When she crested the bluff above the town and looked down, she understood: the water ran gray-green and muscular between walls of black rock, and riding on top of it, turning slowly as they went, were icebergs.

Not bergs, exactly—she corrected herself even as she thought it. Chunks. Calved pieces of the Serac Glacier, some no bigger than wagons, some the size of chapel roofs, drifting downriver with the patient indifference of things that had waited ten thousand years to travel. They ground against each other in the current, shearing off white scars, and the sound they made was the muttering she'd heard: stone-cold conversation, held at a volume just below language.

Below her, Rivergate clung to the south bank like something that had grown there rather than been built—a huddle of shingled roofs, a sawmill breathing smoke, a long timber wharf reaching out into the current, and beyond it the gorge walls narrowing upstream until the river vanished between them like a thread pulled through a needle's eye. That was where her spur went. Somewhere up that throat of rock, the old line climbed toward the mines, and somewhere along it, a government survey team had stopped existing.

She stood a moment longer than she needed to, letting the cold come up off the water and settle into her coat, because standing still was a luxury she rationed the way other people rationed money. Then she went down into the town to find the harbormaster.

* * *

The harbormaster's office occupied the landward end of the wharf, a room built mostly of windows and paperwork. Charts papered the walls in overlapping layers, some of them yellowed past reading, and every horizontal surface had acquired a sediment of brass instruments, tide tables, and coffee cups gone to rings. The man himself sat behind a desk scarred by a century of knives, boots, and cargo hooks—a broad, weathered figure with a beard going to iron-gray and hands like mooring line, thick and frayed at the ends.

His name, according to the board outside, was Oskar Vell. According to the look he gave Jude when she pushed open the door, he had already decided what she was: trouble arriving on schedule.

"Thornbury." He said it before she introduced herself, which told her the contract had traveled ahead of her, which told her something else she didn't like yet. "Railroad sent word you were coming. Survey work on the old spur." He didn't ask a question after it. He made it sound like a diagnosis.

"That's right." She set her papers on his desk—the commission, the county permit, the letter of authorization with its officious seals—and watched him read them without touching them. "I'll need to know about access up the gorge. The road, if there is one. The rail bed."

"There's a road." Vell leaned back until his chair complained. "Half of it's under scree most years. The rail bed's the better way, if your legs are honest." His eyes went down to her boots—railroad boots, worn at the ball of the foot from gravel ballast, laced twice around the ankle—and something in his face shifted a degree warmer, or maybe just less cold. "You've walked line before."

"I've walked nothing else for eleven years."

"Hm." He gathered the permits, squared their edges against the desk, and handed them back. "Then you'll want to know about the tides before you plan anything. Sit down. This is the part that kills people who don't listen."

Jude sat. It cost her something; sitting in offices was how people got found. But the room had two exits—the door and a window that opened onto the wharf planking—and she noted both the way she always did, automatically, the way other people checked for their keys.

Vell took down a chart from the wall, one newer than the rest, and spread it across the desk, weighting the corners with coffee cups. It showed the gorge in section: the river, the canyon walls, and upstream, drawn in confident ink, a pale mass labeled SERAC GLACIER with its toe terminating directly above the upper reach of the navigable channel.

"You came in on the coast road," he said. "You'll have seen the sea. You know tides." 

"Twice a day. Moon does the pulling."

"Out there, yes." He tapped the glacier's toe. "In here, we have a different arrangement. Six months of the year, that ice sits where it is, and the gorge runs free—well, free-ish. But when the calving season comes, the glacier starts shedding. Big pieces. Whole faces of it let go at once, and everything it sheds comes down this channel." He traced the river's course with one blunt finger. "For most of the season, the channel's choked. No boat goes up, no boat comes down, unless it wants to be rolled by a hundred tons of ice coming around a bend it can't see past. Except—" and here he paused, and Jude recognized the pause of a man who had explained this to fools before and buried at least one of them, "—except during the melt-tides. Twice a day, the sea pushes back up the gorge. The backing water lifts the ice. Floats it. Spreads it out along the banks instead of jamming it mid-channel. For a few hours at each high tide, the gorge opens. A boat can run it. Then the tide turns, the water drops, the ice settles and packs, and the door closes again."

"How long is the window?"

"Depends on the moon and the melt. Early season, three hours, maybe four, and rough ones. We're early season now." He looked at her over the chart. "And here's the part your railroad's accountants will care about. The calving gets worse as the melt builds. Each week, the windows get shorter and the ice gets meaner. Right now the gorge will open for six usable tides. Six runs up, six runs down, and then the season takes the channel away entirely until autumn. After that, the only way to the head of the gorge is over the mountains, and the mountains don't care about your schedule."

Jude was already doing the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one companion that had never lied to her. Her survey contract covered the lower twelve miles of the spur—enough, if she worked fast, to give the railroad its assessment. Twelve miles of dead track through a gorge accessible by water for six tides, or by a scree-choked road she could walk but couldn't haul instruments or camp gear along. Which meant the river. Which meant the tides.

Six tides. Three days, near enough, if she used every one of them. Go up on a rising tide, work the line while the water was high enough to keep her barge off the rocks, come down on the next. Two survey legs per tide if she pushed. No slack. No second chances—if she missed a window, she sat on a gravel bar watching the ice close the door behind her, and the contract died of exposure.

"I'll need a boat," she said. "A pilot who knows the channel."

"You'll need more than that." Vell began stacking his coffee cups with the absent precision of a man whose hands needed occupation. "You'll need to know that you're not the first person to do this math."

The words landed in the room and stayed there.

"The government team," Jude said carefully. "They went up three weeks ago."

"They did." Vell's face did something complicated—not quite grief, not quite guilt, settling finally on a kind of tired watchfulness. "Went up on the fourth tide of the season's opening. Had their own pilot, their own barge, all their instruments crated nice. Came down again on the sixth." He met her eyes. "Except they didn't. The barge came down empty. Pilot says he left them at the old mine landing to work the upper line, came back on schedule, and there wasn't a soul on the bank. No fire burning. No signal flag. Nothing."

"And the authorities?"

"Authorities sent a man. Man walked the line for two days and came back saying he found nothing worth finding." Vell said it flatly, and flatly was worse than any emphasis could have been. "That's the official version. I've lived here forty-one years, Miss Thornbury, and I can tell you the difference between nothing and nothing-worth-finding. This valley has both, and the government man brought back the second kind."

Jude turned her attention to the chart, mostly so she wouldn't have to let him see her face while her mind did what it always did with an unsolved problem—worried it like a dog with a bone, refusing to put it down. A team goes up. A team doesn't come down. A searcher finds nothing worth finding. And now a private railroad hires a stranger to survey the same closed line, in the same season, on the same six tides.

"Why now?" she asked. "The line's been shut nine years. Why does the railroad want it surveyed this spring, of all springs?"

"Salvage," Vell said. "Rails, ties, bridge iron. There's talk the state will reopen the spur if the grade proves sound. Your survey decides whether the iron comes out or the trains go back in." He shrugged, a big slow movement like a hill deciding something. "Money reasons. There are always money reasons."

But he said it the way a man says something he has been paid, in one currency or another, not to say more about.

* * *

She left the office by the window, out onto the wharf, because the door felt too much like a mouth.

Morning had arrived while they talked, and the harbor had woken with it. The wharf stretched a quarter mile along the bank, and along its length the working life of Rivergate was assembling itself—fishermen mending nets in coils of pale rope, stevedores rolling barrels, a crane swinging its arm over a hold like a patient heron. Woodsmoke and tar and the green smell of the river. In any other town, at any other time, Jude would have catalogued it all in passing and kept moving.

Instead she stopped, because at the far end of the wharf, three vessels were being loaded in a way that did not match anything else happening on the waterfront.

They were barges—sturdy, shallow-drafted, built for the gorge—and they were taking on cargo that had nothing to do with fish or lumber. She walked closer, unhurried, hands in her pockets, wearing the expression of a woman idly curious about other people's business, which was among the oldest disguises she owned.

Cutting gear. Long-handled torches stacked in racks. Crates stenciled with the mark of an explosives concern out of Corvane—she knew the stencil, had seen it on demolition jobs in three territories. Chain slings coiled like sleeping snakes. A portable derrick being bolted to the deck of the lead barge by men who worked with the economy of professionals. Nobody was laughing. On a working waterfront, loading crews joked, swore, sang, argued. These men loaded in silence, and the silence had a shape to it, the shape of men who had been told what they were doing mattered and what it was for.

She counted. Three barges, provisioned for longer than three days—water casks, canvas, cook rig. Crews of eight or ten each. And on a clipboard at the gangplank of the lead vessel, a manifest holder swung in the wind, and when the wind lifted the top sheet she caught, for two seconds, a header written in a fine commercial hand:

*Charter — Meridian Recovery & Salvage Co. — per contract, all recovered materials to be delivered to—*

The wind dropped. The sheet fell. She hadn't seen the name beneath the dash.

But she had seen enough. Salvage fleets did not carry dynamite to pull rails. Rails came out with torches and winches. Dynamite was for moving stone—for blasting, for collapsing, for making sure that something stayed exactly where you put it, forever.

Jude stood at the rail's end and let the numbers finish themselves, because they were going to whether she watched or not.

Six tides. The gorge opened six times before the ice sealed it. She had known that for twenty minutes, and she had already planned her survey around it, because that was what she did—she read constraints the way priests read scripture, and she built her movements inside them.

Someone else had read the same scripture. Someone had done the same math—six tides, one channel, one narrow season—and had signed a charter, hired three crews, bought explosives, and provisioned for a campaign. They hadn't come here because the spur was suddenly interesting after nine years. They had come because the window was open, and windows, in this valley, were the whole story. Whatever the government team had found—or failed to come back from—it lived up that gorge, and it would be unreachable by water for half a year once the ice closed. Anything anyone wanted to do about it had to be done now, in these six tides, or not at all.

Her own contract sat in her pocket like a coin someone had slipped her, and for the first time since Harrow Bend she wondered whose purse it had really come from, and why a railroad that had ignored a dead spur for nearly a decade had chosen this exact spring to hire a surveyor with her particular reputation for discretion.

The river muttered below the pilings, hauling its ice down toward the sea. Upstream, past the town's rooftops, the gorge walls narrowed to their dark thread, and somewhere beyond the bend the glacier waited, shedding its ancient weight one piece at a time, indifferent to schedules, contracts, and the plans of small warm creatures.

An invisible clock had started ticking. She could feel it now, under everything—the gulls crying, the crane groaning, the hammers of the shipwrights—a rhythm beneath the rhythm, counting down from six.

Behind her, on the lead barge, a man set down the crate he was carrying and looked directly at her across sixty feet of wharf. He didn't glare. He didn't wave. He simply looked, the way a man checks a gauge, and then he turned and spoke quietly to someone inside the deckhouse.

Jude adjusted her pack, turned up the street toward the chandlery to buy rope and lamp oil she would pretend she needed, and began, in the privacy of her own skull, to plan a survey she suspected she might not survive—and, more urgently, to wonder what was waiting at the old mine landing that three crews of silent men with dynamite had been hired to make sure no one ever saw.
