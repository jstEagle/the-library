## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The spur began where the map said it ended, which was the first thing Jude disliked about it.

She had walked closed lines before—dozens of them, from the salt flats to the timber country—and every dead railway had a way of announcing its death. Rust bloomed on the rails like lichen. Ties rotted soft as bread. Culverts collapsed into their own shadows, and the ballast sank in long slow swales where frost had worked at it for years. A closed line decayed honestly. It told you how long it had been dying.

This one didn't.

Jude stood at the mouth of the gorge with her surveyor's chain coiled over one shoulder and her theodolite case strapped to her back, and she looked at track that had been officially abandoned for eleven years and looked, instead, like a line that had been maintained by someone very careful who wanted no one to know.

The rails were oiled. Not greased for preservation the way a museum might do it—oiled the way an engineer oils a machine he intends to use again. The ties were sound. The drainage ditches along the east side ran clean, cut at proper grade, and someone had cleared the rockfall chutes of loose scree so recently that the swept stone lay in tidy windrows, sorted by size, the way Jude herself sorted it when she was preparing a bed for new rail.

Eleven years of abandonment did not look like this. Eleven years of abandonment looked like Harrow Bend's forgotten branch to the alkali works, all sag and rust and silence. This looked like a held breath.

She took out her notebook, licked her pencil stub, and wrote: *Spur condition inconsistent w/ closure date. Maintenance ongoing. By whom?*

The handwriting came out steadier than she felt. That was the trick of it, the thing Vess had taught her without ever meaning to: your hands could be afraid as long as they stayed busy. Fear lived in idle fingers. Put a tool in them and the fear had to wait its turn.

*Keep moving,* Vess used to say, tapping his temple with two fingers before every departure. *A body in motion is a hard target.* He'd been talking about rail barons and their hired men, about the years after she testified, but the advice had calcified into something larger, a whole philosophy of existence that Jude carried in her spine the way other people carried religion. She had not slept in the same town twice in nine years. She owned three changes of clothes and no photographs. Her map of closed lines—the real one, inked on oilcloth beneath false ledger pages—was the only possession she treated as irreplaceable, because it was not a record of places but of exits.

And now here she was, walking deliberately toward the center of whatever this valley was hiding, because a county clerk in Rivergate had signed a contract with her name on it, and because the money was good, and because—she admitted this only to the canyon walls, never to herself—the alternative to moving forward was standing still, and standing still meant being found.

The gorge narrowed as she followed the old right-of-way upstream. Granite walls rose on either side, streaked white with bird lime, and the river ran fast and green below the embankment, loud enough that she stopped hearing her own footsteps. The air changed temperature twice in a mile—cold pooling in the shade of the cliffs, then warming again where the sun found the trackbed. Somewhere above, ice creaked. She had heard it since dawn: a deep, structural groaning, like a house settling, except the house was a glacier and the settling was measured in tons.

Six tides. The harbormaster's arithmetic sat in her head like a splinter. The gorge would open for six tides when the calving season broke the ice dam, and then the river would run high enough to float salvage barges up to the wreck of the *Corvina*, the freighter that had gone down in the narrows with government cargo aboard. After the sixth tide, the ice would close again and the valley would seal itself for another year. Everything in this gorge operated on that clock—the river, the fleet, the vanished survey team, and now her.

She rounded a shoulder of rock where the line curved away from the river, and she smelled woodsmoke.

Not old smoke. Old smoke smells like ash and cold iron; it has no body to it. This smoke had body—resin and char, still warm, still breathing. Jude stopped, set down the theodolite case, and unslung the chain slowly, letting the links hang quiet against her leg. Her hand went to the hatchet on her belt, not drawing it, just confirming it was there.

The camp sat fifty yards ahead, on the flat beside the third bridge abutment, exactly where a competent field engineer would put it: out of the flood line, sheltered from the north wind by the abutment wall, close enough to water, far enough from the cliff face to be clear of rockfall. It was a good camp. It was, in fact, a textbook camp, laid out with a precision that made something in Jude's chest tighten—not fear yet, but the recognition of competence, and the unease that competent people always inspired in her, because competent people were the ones who left no mistakes to follow.

Two tents, ridge-poled, guyed taut. A fire ring of stacked granite with a cook grate across it. A drying line strung between two alders, empty. And gear—gear everywhere, arranged with a neatness that was worse than disorder would have been. Disorder meant panic. Panic meant running, or being taken, or some violence she could reconstruct from the wreckage of it. Neatness meant nothing she understood at all.

Jude circled the camp once, wide, reading it the way she read track. Then she went in.

The boots were the first thing. Two pairs of government-issue survey boots, hobnailed, broken in, stood side by side at the edge of the fire ring—toes toward the flames, the way men leave them when they mean to step back into them in the morning. The leather was dry. No dew had touched them overnight, though everything else in the camp wore a skin of moisture, the grass silvered, the tent canvas dark at the seams. As if the boots had been set there after the dew fell. Or as if they had been set there by hands that then never came back to claim them.

Inside the first tent: four bedrolls, rolled tight, straps buckled. Four packs, stowed upright against the tent wall in a row, weighted open at the flaps. Rations stacked inside—hardtack, tinned meat, bags of beans—counted, she saw at a glance, in the neat fives that quartermasters use. Untouched. The wax paper seals on the tins were unbroken. A water barrel stood full to within a hand's width of the brim.

In the second tent, the instruments.

That was where the dread arrived, arriving not as a jolt but as a slow cold pressure behind her sternum, because she knew instruments, and what she was looking at was wrong in a way that only an instrument man would understand.

A transit on its tripod, leveled. Not roughly leveled—leveled, the bubble centered in its vial to the hair, the locking screws backed off a quarter turn the way you leave them when you expect to resume work within the hour. A stadia rod, extended and locked, leaning against the tent pole. A barometer, its case open, the needle resting on a reading. An aneroid, same. A chronometer in its padded box, lid shut, wound—she lifted it to her ear and heard it ticking, ticking, ticking, hours past any reasonable interval, still keeping time for men who were not there to consult it.

Everything zeroed. Everything staged for a morning's work that had never begun—or had begun and been interrupted so completely, so instantly, that no one had thought to cover the transit against rain, and no rain had come, and the chronometer just kept ticking into the empty tent day after day.

Jude knelt beside the transit and did not touch it. Her breath fogged the cold air and drifted through the sight line of the telescope, and she watched it go and thought: *how long?*

The food said days, at least. Hardtack doesn't spoil, but the bread wrapped in cloth near the fire ring had gone stale-hard, and a tin of rendered fat left uncovered had grown a skin of dust. The fire ring held ash from more than one burn, layered, the top layer gray and cold and rained-on once. So: they had been here. They had lived here. They had cooked meals and mended gear and taken sightings, and then, on some morning or evening, they had stepped out of their boots—

No. Nobody steps out of their boots. Not in mountain country. You sleep in your boots in mountain country, because a man barefoot on scree is a dead man. Boots by the fire was a lowland habit, a garrison habit, a habit of men who expected no trouble and got none and then—

Then what?

She stood and turned a slow circle, and the engineer in her took over entirely, the part of her that could not abide a number that refused to balance, a load path that had no bearing at its end. Every structure tells you where its weight goes. Every event leaves tracks. That wasn't superstition; it was physics. Mass leaves evidence. Four men, two hundred and eighty pounds apiece with their gear, walking out of this camp in any direction, over ground this soft, would leave prints that lasted until the next hard rain. There had been no hard rain—the dust on the fat tin proved it, the undisturbed dew proved it.

So she looked for the prints.

She walked the perimeter in expanding spirals, eyes down, reading the ground the way she read ballast. Soft earth at the spring seep: boot prints, many, overlapping—all inbound patterns, converging on the camp from the direction of the trailhead, dated by their weathering to weeks ago. She found the print of a sledge runner. She found a latrine trench, used and filled, downwind of the tents, correct in every particular. She found the trampled work area around the instrument tent, the half-moon scuffs of men kneeling at a tripod.

And leaving the camp—in any direction, toward the river, the cliff, the track, the timberline—she found nothing.

Not few prints. Not faint prints. Nothing. The ground beyond the camp's work radius lay unbroken, dew-silvered, carrying only the thread-fine stitching of bird tracks and, higher up near the talus, the paired dots of a fox. Four men had walked into this camp and no mass had ever walked out.

Jude stood at the northern edge of the site with her notebook open and her pencil motionless, and she felt the dread change shape. It had been dread of danger, the animal kind, the kind that keeps you alive. What replaced it was worse. It was the dread of the unsolved equation, the dread that gets into engineers and surveyors and anyone whose mind is built to close loops: the data does not add up, therefore the data is incomplete, therefore there is a piece missing, therefore somewhere in this valley there exists a fact that four men found worth stepping out of their boots for.

She went back to the camp and searched it properly, turning professional now, methodical, because obsession was just attention with the safety off. She checked under the bedrolls: nothing but canvas and pine needles. She checked the ration stores, counting: sufficient for six more weeks. She checked the packs—maps inside, government issue, the spur surveyed in red ink to a point two miles upstream and then simply stopping, the linework ending mid-curve as if the pen had been lifted. She checked the fire ash and found, sifted fine with a stick, a button, brass, military, and a scrap of paper burned almost to nothing, one corner surviving with a fragment of typescript: *...junction...*

One word. One word out of a whole document, fed to a fire.

Jude sat back on her heels in the cold morning light with the scrap pinched between finger and thumb, and the glacier groaned overhead, long and grinding, and the river ran loud below, and the chronometer ticked on in its tent, keeping time for nobody.

They hadn't fled. Fleeing men take boots, weapons, food, instruments—fleeing men take *something*. They hadn't been taken by force; force leaves sign, and the ground around this camp was the most eloquent blank she had ever read. Which left the possibility she kept circling and refusing to land on: that they had gone somewhere deliberately, quietly, in stocking feet or bare feet or no feet at all, somewhere the ground couldn't follow. Down. Or in. Or through.

She looked at the abutment wall behind the camp—the third bridge abutment, massive granite blocks, and she had walked past it twice already because it was exactly what it appeared to be. Except that the drainage weep-holes in its face, which should have been crusted with mineral stain after eleven wet years, were clean. All of them. Scrubbed clean, or used often enough that nothing had time to grow.

And the lowest one, knee-high, half-hidden by fern, was wider than the others. Wider than a weep-hole needed to be. Wide enough, say, for a man to widen further with tools, over time, carefully, from the inside.

Jude crouched before it and put her palm against the cool dark of the opening, and felt, faint and rhythmic through the stone, a draft. Air moving. Air moving *outward*, steady as breath, from a space that had no business existing behind a bridge abutment on a line that had been dead for eleven years.

The engineer in her finally let the equation land. Four men, zero tracks, one sealed junction, one burned word: *junction*. They hadn't vanished off the face of the earth. They had gone under it.

She wrote three lines in her notebook, pressing hard enough to score the page beneath:

*Team entered subsurface feature at abutment 3. Deliberate. Equipped or resupplied from within.*

*Closure of this line was not abandonment. It was concealment.*

*Find what they found.*

Then she sat down among the neat, abandoned, impossible gear of four missing men, opened a tin of their untouched rations, and began, with cold and growing hunger, to plan how she would follow them into the dark.

* * *

By afternoon she had surveyed the abutment the way it deserved, and the abutment had lied to her beautifully, and she had caught it lying, and that was when the obsession stopped being a feeling and became a schedule.

The weep-hole was a throat, not a door. Behind its first six feet of rubble-choked crawl, the passage opened into a cut gallery—hand-cut, she judged from the tool marks, and old, older than the closure, older maybe than the railroad itself—running back into the mountain's root at a gentle descending grade. Her lamp showed timber sets at regular intervals, shored and sound, and dust on the floor marked with boot prints: many men, recent, all heading in. None coming out.

She stood at the threshold with the light trembling on the black air ahead and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since morning. Four men had gone down into this mountain weeks ago with six weeks of food topside, untouched. Either they had supplies below—and who supplies a hole in a mountain that officially contains nothing—or they had never planned to need them. Either way, the government team had not disappeared. They had descended on purpose, into a junction the railroad had sealed and scrubbed and paid eleven years of silence to keep forgotten.

And the last entry in the team's own field log, the one she'd found tucked in the transit case, read simply: *Day 19. Both galleries mapped. H. says stop writing it down.*

Both galleries. Stop writing it down.

Jude clicked the lamp shutter half-closed, until only a blade of light lay along the tunnel floor, and started down the grade, one hand on the cold timber, listening to the mountain breathe its slow outward breath around her. Above, faint through forty feet of granite, the glacier groaned again—the clock in the ice, ticking toward the first tide.

Whatever the fifth man had stopped writing down, it was below, in the dark, waiting to be read.
