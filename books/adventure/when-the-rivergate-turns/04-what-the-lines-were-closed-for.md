## 4. What the Lines Were Closed For

The county records office in Rivergate smelled of cold paper and lamp oil, the way all such rooms smell—like a place where time had been filed away and forgotten on purpose. Jude Thornbury stood at the counter with her surveyor's license held flat under her palm, watching the clerk's eyes travel from her face to the document and back again.

"Thornbury," he read aloud, as if tasting it. "You're the one surveying the old spur."

"I'm the one hired to survey the old spur," she said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" He was a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular patience of someone who had spent thirty years saying no to people who wanted things. "Most folks who come asking after that line aren't hired by anybody. They're just curious. Curiosity's free. Records cost a day's fee."

Jude set a silver dollar on the counter and let it sit there, gleaming. "I'm not curious. I'm thorough. There's a difference too."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but a relative of one. He took the coin and led her back through rows of shelving into a room where the county's dead paperwork went to be ignored: land grants, water rights, coroner's inquests, and the bound volumes of the Railroad Commission's annual reports, their spines cracked like old knuckles.

"The spur," he said, running a finger along the shelf. "Rivergate Branch, Milepost Eleven to Twenty-Three. Closed in—" He pulled a volume, blew dust from its top edge, and opened it with the care of a man handling something he didn't trust. "Here. Closure order, spring of '09. Cause of closure: rockslide, upper gorge section. Estimated repair cost exceeded line value. Line decommissioned by order of the Commission."

"That's it?"

"That's what's written." He set the book down in front of her. "Written and true are cousins, miss, not twins. You'll want the inquest records if you want the other twin. But I'll tell you now, you won't find much. Officially, nobody died on that line when it came down."

*Officially.* The word landed in Jude's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard the splash a long way off.

"And unofficially?"

The clerk looked at her for a long moment. Outside the window, Rivergate's harbor traffic clanked and shouted, gulls wheeling over the masts. Inside, the room was so quiet she could hear the paper breathing—or thought she could.

"Unofficially," he said, "I file what they give me to file. That's my whole job description. But I was a young man when that slide came down, and my uncle ran a supply wagon up the gorge road. He told me once—he'd been drinking, mind—that the wagons went up for three days carrying lime. Quicklime. Bags and bags of it." He straightened his cuffs, though they needed nothing. "You don't burn that much quicklime for a rockslide that killed nobody."

He left her alone with the volume then, and Jude sat down at the scarred oak table beneath the green-shaded lamp and began to read.

* * *

She worked the way she did everything—methodically, obsessively, the engineer's habit of refusing to leave a number unaccounted for. Cross-referencing the Commission report against the freight manifests, the manifests against the payroll ledgers, the payrolls against the coroner's docket. It was slow work, and the lamp burned lower, and somewhere outside the afternoon turned toward evening without her noticing.

The picture assembled itself the way a wreck assembles itself when you know where to look: not all at once, but piece by piece, each fragment confirming the last until the whole shape of the disaster stood up out of the paper and looked at her.

The Rivergate Branch had been built eleven years earlier to serve the mining camps above the gorge—copper, mostly, and some silver, enough to justify blasting a railbed into canyon wall that had never wanted one. For nine years it ran. Then, in the spring of '09, the slide came down across Milepost Seventeen, burying four hundred feet of track under forty feet of shattered granite, and the railroad did an extraordinary thing.

It stopped asking questions.

That was what struck her first, sitting there in the lamplight. Not lies—lies were common as gravel—but the *absence* of them. No petition to reopen. No engineering study. No protest filed by the mining companies whose freight suddenly had to go by wagon at triple the cost. The mining companies, whose ore was their lifeblood, had accepted the closure of their only rail line with the meekness of schoolchildren. She found the settlement agreements: each company paid an annuity, signed in the same month, by the same notary. Silence, purchased wholesale.

And the crews. She counted them. The slide had come down on a Tuesday morning, and the section gang had been working Milepost Sixteen through Eighteen that week—she found the work orders, still pinned to the ledger page. Fourteen men. Fourteen names. And in the coroner's docket, filed the same week: *no bodies recovered.*

Fourteen men buried or scattered under forty feet of rock, and not one body? Not one? Rockslides killed men, that was the nature of them, but they always gave something back. A boot. A hand. A hat. The mountain never swallowed fourteen men whole and clean.

Unless the mountain hadn't done it alone.

She turned back to the closure order itself and read the signature block for the fourth time. Commissioner's authorization, countersigned by the district superintendent. And below that, in the space reserved for the consulting engineer who certified the line as unsalvageable—one name, written in a hand she knew better than her own.

*E. Vess.*

The lamp hissed. Or maybe that was her own breath.

* * *

Vess had taught her the trade on a night train between two towns whose names she'd never learned, because he'd pulled her aboard mid-platform with a grip on her collar and a ticket in his fist, and neither of them had looked back.

She had been nineteen. She had testified—two days, a courthouse hot as a kiln, her voice cracking on the third hour—against Aldous Crane, the rail baron whose bridge inspectors had signed off on trestles they'd never climbed, whose books said the bolts were sound while the bolts were rust, and when the Harrow Creek trestle went down with a passenger train on it, someone had to say aloud what everyone already knew. She had said it. She had been the only one willing.

Crane's people had found her inside a week. She'd known they would; that was arithmetic too. What she hadn't known was that the man waiting outside her boarding house wouldn't be one of Crane's. He'd be a heavyset, gray-bearded engineer with sawdust in his cuff seams and a locomotive idling two streets over.

"You talked," Vess had said. Not a question.

"They'll kill me."

"Probably." He'd said it without cruelty, the way he said everything—like reporting a gauge reading. "Question is whether they get to do it before the story stops mattering. Right now you matter. In a month you're a footnote, and footnotes are easy to lose. So we're going to make you hard to find." He'd handed her a battered leather satchel, heavier than it looked. "Maps. Every closed line, abandoned spur, dead-end wye, and forgotten siding west of the divide. I've been collecting them twenty years. A man who knows where the railroad *isn't* can go anywhere the railroad isn't looking."

"Why are you helping me?"

He'd looked at her then, really looked, with eyes the color of wet slate, and whatever he'd seen in her face had made him answer honestly—which she would understand later was rare for him.

"Because I helped build some of those closed lines," he'd said. "And because a man my age gets to choose which debts he pays. Get on the engine, girl. We've got a clear board to the junction."

For six years after that, Vess had been the fixed point in her moving life. Letters poste restante, coded by timetable numbers. Occasional meetings in depots at odd hours, him pressing new map pages into her hands, her pressing news of the country back. He taught her how to read a right-of-way like a sentence, how to hear a bad bearing in the sound of wheels, how to sleep in a different town every night and call it freedom instead of fear.

*Keep moving,* he'd told her, the last time she'd seen him, three years ago on a rain-blurred platform in Kestrel Junction. *Stopping is how they find you. The day you stand still is the day your past catches your shadow.*

She had built her whole life on that instruction. Never the same town twice. The map folded into false ledger pages. Restless competence worn like armor over the fear that stopping meant being found—and worse, that being found meant discovering the running had never protected anything worth protecting.

Now she sat in a lamplit room in Rivergate and looked at his signature certifying a line unsalvageable, fourteen men unrecovered, quicklime going up the gorge road by the wagonload, and the past pressed against the present like water against a door—patient, total, finding every seam.

Vess had taught her to keep moving. She had never once asked herself what he might have been teaching her to move *away from.*

* * *

She stayed until the clerk came back with a second lamp and a meaningful cough. By then she had copied everything worth copying into her field book—the closure order, the settlement dates, the payroll gap, the fourteen names, which she wrote out individually because names were the one thing a ledger couldn't be allowed to forget. The last entry in the section-gang work orders was dated the Monday before the slide. All fourteen men reported present.

All fourteen men, present. And none accounted for after.

One more thing, though. One thing she almost missed, because it was filed wrong—shoved into the back of the freight manifest volume as if someone had meant to destroy it and lost their nerve. A single requisition slip, dated eight days *before* the slide. Blasting powder, forty cases, drawn from the district magazine for "line improvement works, Rivergate Branch." Signed for by the same district superintendent who countersigned the closure.

Forty cases of powder, drawn a week early, for improvements on a line that would be rubble in eight days and condemned in six weeks.

Jude closed the book very carefully, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping.

They hadn't buried the men with a rockslide. They had made the rockslide. And then they had buried the making of it under paperwork, and the paperwork under years, and the years under the simple human reluctance to dig up a thing that everyone in the valley had been paid not to remember.

And now a government survey team had walked up that same gorge with instruments and codes and official sanction, and walked into a camp and out of existence, leaving their boots dry by the fire.

She understood the shape of it now, or most of it. Whatever was buried at Milepost Seventeen wasn't fourteen men. It couldn't be—or not only. Dead men were a horror, but they weren't worth killing a second team over, twenty years on. Something else had gone into that canyon. Something the powder was for, or the vaults, or the silence. Something worth a fleet of salvage barges and a forged contract with her name on it.

Her name. She turned that over as she packed her field book away. Her contract had come through channels too clean, references too easily verified, terms too generous. Someone had wanted a licensed surveyor on that spur, and had wanted it to be *her*—the woman with the map of dead lines, the woman Vess had armed with every secret right-of-way in the territory. Which meant whoever was behind this knew about the map. Knew about Vess. Knew exactly what thread to dangle.

The clerk locked the archive behind her and pocketed the key. "Find what you were looking for?"

"No," Jude said. "I found what I was afraid of. It's different."

Outside, the harbor lay dark under a rising wind, and beyond the breakwater, far upriver, the glacier stood in its valley like a held breath. Two days, maybe three, before the calving season opened the gorge for the first tide. She had counted the same arithmetic the salvage fleet had counted, and now she counted one number more: eight days between the powder requisition and the slide. Eight days was enough time to stage anything. Enough time to make a mountain lie.

She walked back toward the waterfront with her collar up, and that was when she saw the boat—a long, low river craft sliding silent along the pilings, a single lantern at its bow, and at the tiller a wiry figure who raised two fingers off the wheel in lazy salute, as if they'd arranged this meeting years ago and he'd simply arrived early.

Jude stopped walking. She knew that silhouette. She had last seen it six years ago, cutting engines in a fog-bound channel, hauling her out of a town where Crane's men had finally come for her.

"Well," said Tam of the river pilots, grinning like a man who'd never once doubted she'd end up here. "Told you I'd find you eventually, Thornbury. Question is—who told me where to look?"
