## 5. The Fleet at Anchor

The fog off the Sound had not yet burned away when Jude came down to Rivergate's harbor, and for a while she was grateful for it. Fog made a person anonymous. It softened the edges of the town—the fish houses with their peeling paint, the cannery whistle tower, the long timber flume that ran down from the hills like a wooden vein—and it let her walk the waterfront without feeling watched, which by now she understood was a luxury rather than a right.

She had been in Rivergate four days. Long enough to file her survey notice with the county clerk, short enough that nobody should have formed opinions about her. But opinions were forming. She could feel them the way she felt a rail going soft under a wheel load—not visible yet, but there in the vibration.

The harbor itself sat at the mouth of the gorge like a cupped hand. Beyond the breakwater, the river narrowed into the canyon where the old spur climbed toward the high country, and somewhere up in that white silence lay the glacier whose calving season would open the route for six tides and no more. The harbormaster had told her the dates without being asked twice, which should have told her something. Helpful men in small towns are either kind or bought, and Jude had learned—expensively—to check which before accepting directions.

This morning the fog was lifting off the water in rags, and through the tearing gaps she saw the fleet.

Three barges and a tug, anchored in the outer roads where the deep water held. They hadn't been there two days ago. They had come in overnight or in the gray hours before dawn, riding low in the water, their decks stacked under tarpaulins that even from the wharf she could read by their shapes: compressor housings, cutting rigs coiled in canvas, crates too long and too heavy for fishing gear. And on the lead barge's stern, stenciled fresh enough that the paint still looked wet against the salt: *Meridian Salvage & Recovery, Portland Registry*.

Jude stopped walking. Not because anyone would notice—a woman pausing on a wharf to watch boats is the most ordinary sight a harbor town produces—but because her body did what it always did when the numbers went wrong. It went still, the way an engine goes still before you decide whether to shut it down or run it harder.

She counted. Three barges. A salvage operation for a wrecked locomotive and whatever cargo the government team had been hauling might justify one barge, maybe two if they expected to pull plate steel. Three barges meant they expected volume. Cutting gear meant they expected obstruction. And dynamite—

She'd seen the dynamite cases being swung aboard by a donkey crane as she came down the hill, each crate stenciled with the red diamond and the word *FUSEE* in letters a man could read across a room. Nobody blasting rock needed that much dynamite to clear a survey spur. Nobody salvaging a wreck needed dynamite at all, unless what they really wanted was to make sure nothing ever got *read*.

The cold clarity arrived the way it always did—not as a flash but as a slow drop in temperature, a settling, like frost forming on the inside of a window. This wasn't about a wreck. The wreck was thirty years of rust and river stone. Whatever the fleet wanted up that gorge, it was something that could be destroyed, and something worth destroying was something worth knowing.

She walked the length of the wharf slowly, hands in her coat pockets, and let herself look like a woman with nowhere particular to be.

* * *

The charter office occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, a granite building the color of wet ash with a brass plaque gone green: *PORT OF RIVERGATE — WHARFAGE & TONNAGE*. Through its fly-specked window Jude could see a clerk at a high desk and, standing over him, a man in a good coat doing the kind of talking that involved pointing at paper.

She gave it ten minutes. Then she pushed open the door, letting the bell above it announce her, and asked the clerk—in the flat, bored voice of a contractor with a schedule—for the tide tables and the current gauge readings for the gorge approach. Official business. Her permit number opened doors; the county had stamped it in triplicate, and triplicate stamps made clerks helpful.

While he dug for the ledger, she let her eyes do quiet work. On the desk beside the clerk's elbow lay a stack of charter filings, weighted with a brass inkwell, the top sheet face-up. She didn't reach for it. She read it upside down, which is a skill every engineer develops eventually, because half the documents in the world are handed to you backwards.

*Private Wharfage & Salvage Charter — Meridian Salvage & Recovery, lessee.* Cargo declared: *rail iron, rolling stock components, general freight recovery.* Waters: *Rivergate Gorge approach, upper tidal reach.* And at the bottom, in a hand that was not the clerk's and not the fleet captain's—firm, slanted, written by a man accustomed to signing things that became other people's problems:

**A. Corvane-Hale, for the Rivergate Improvement & Development Trust.**

Jude kept her breathing level. The name landed in her like a spike driven into a tie.

She knew it from the county records—knew it the way you know a shape you've only seen in the dark. Three nights ago, in the records room in Harrow Bend, with the lamp turned low and the 1890s ledgers open on her knees, she had traced the closure of the Rivergate spur through its paperwork like a surgeon following a bullet. The rockslide report. The engineering assessment signed by a man whose other signatures appeared on bridge contracts he'd never visited. And threaded through all of it, appearing first as a notary witness, then as a bond holder, then—by the final year—as the signature that mattered: **A. Corvane-Hale**, trustee, *Rivergate Improvement & Development Trust*, the entity that had purchased the closed right-of-way for less than the cost of the rails still buried in it.

A trust. Not a company. A trust exists to hold things quietly, to keep names off titles, to let money own land without money's owner owning the question. She had noted it then with the mild professional interest of a mapmaker marking a dead line. Now the dead line had a pulse, and the pulse was loading dynamite onto three barges in the harbor outside.

"Here you are." The clerk slid the tide tables across. "Gauge readings are posted daily on the board out front. Anything else?"

"The Meridian boats," Jude said, casual as weather. "They'll want the same windows I will. Do they file their own pilot requests?"

The clerk's pen paused. Just a fraction—half a second, the time it takes a man to remember instructions. "They've got their arrangements," he said. "Charter traffic handles its own pilots."

"Of course." She paid her nickel, took her tables, and touched her hat brim. At the door she paused, as if struck by an afterthought. "Who's the trustee these days? The Improvement Trust? I'm told they hold some of the old right-of-way I'll be crossing."

"They're in Portland," the clerk said. "You'd write them, not visit." And then, because he was a small-town man and she had asked politely: "Corvane-Hale's the name on everything, far as I know. Been on everything since my father's day."

Since his father's day. Thirty years of one signature, holding one valley shut.

* * *

She spent the middle of the day where a surveyor has a legitimate reason to stand and stare: the breakwater, the gauge board, the headland path above the harbor with its view down onto the anchorage. She sketched. She actually sketched—hull profiles, crane positions, the draft marks on the barges' bows—which was both cover and habit, because drawing a thing taught her more about it than staring did.

By noon the fog was gone and the fleet lay naked in the sun, and she filled three pages of her field book with facts dressed as sketches.

The lead barge carried the cutting plant: two compressors, a gantry, acetylene bottles racked and chained. The second carried the dynamite—she counted forty-one crates before the crane swing blocked her view, and forty-one crates at fifty pounds to the crate was better than a ton of high explosive, which was not a salvage quantity, it was a demolition quantity, and the only structure in the upper gorge worth demolishing was the mountain itself. The third barge rode deepest. Its deck load sat under full canvas, lashed flat, and when the wind lifted a corner of the tarp she caught a gleam of new pine—crates, long ones, coffin-shaped, built recently and built well. Not equipment. Containers. Built to carry something out.

Or to carry something back that someone else had left behind.

She thought of the camp on the spur. Boots by the fire, instruments zeroed, food untouched, and not one footprint leaving in any direction. The government team had gone into that valley carrying instruments and authority and come out of the world entirely. And now a private fleet with a ton of dynamite and a set of empty boxes was racing the ice to get up there first.

Put the numbers together and they stopped being numbers. They became intent.

The sixth tide closes the gorge. After that, nothing goes up and nothing comes down until next season. If the fleet sealed the valley behind it—if the dynamite's purpose was not to open the wreck site but to close the *evidence*—then whatever the survey team found, or was finding, or died finding, would sit under a hundred tons of brought-down canyon until the glacier relented again next year. And next year, a different crew, a cleaner job, a story about unstable rock.

And her contract—her nice, legal, county-stamped survey contract—would be the paper that proved a licensed engineer had examined the site and found nothing worth reporting. She was not racing the fleet. She was its alibi.

The realization settled over her with a terrible calm, the way ballast settles a hull. She had taken this job to have a reason to be here, and someone had counted on exactly that. Someone who knew a surveyor would come sniffing along a closed line and wanted the sniffing done by the right sort of person—the sort who files reports, whose stamp makes things official, whose presence turns a burial into a formality.

Or the sort who, once she understood what she was stamping, might refuse.

That was the question underneath everything, and she turned it over carefully, the way she'd turn a cracked axle in a pit lamp. Did Corvane-Hale's people believe she was safe? Had someone vetted her, followed her, read her habits—the map of dead lines folded beneath false ledger pages, the never sleeping twice in the same town? Or was she simply convenient, a random instrument picked up because instruments are cheaper than questions?

Either way, the arithmetic had changed. Two days ago this had been a survey with a deadline. Now it was a race, and the prize was not rail iron. It was proof—of what, she couldn't yet say, but the county records had already shown her the shape of it: a slide that killed no one officially, a right-of-way sold for nothing, a trust that had held a valley's silence for thirty years. Evidence was the only cargo in this gorge that anyone would spend a ton of dynamite to bury.

She looked at her own hands, ink-stained at the knuckles, steady on the field book. Cold clarity, Vess used to call it, though he'd called it something rougher. *When the fear stops shouting and starts doing sums,* he'd said once, over a camp stove in a town whose name she'd let go, *that's when you know you're in it for true. Fear shouts. Understanding counts.*

She was counting now.

* * *

Toward evening she took a table at the Harbor Light, the saloon nearest the wharf stairs, where the windows faced the anchorage and the beer was local and indifferent. Fleet crews drink somewhere on their first night in port, and drinking men talk sideways even when they're careful.

Two of them came in at dusk—barge men by their boots and their rope-burned hands—and took the corner table. Jude nursed her glass and listened with the patience of a woman reading a slow gauge.

Most of it was noise: wages, a captain named Dreech who worked his crews hard, complaints about the tide windows. *"Six tides and the old man wants the whole job done in five"*—that much she filed without surprise. But near the end, when the younger one leaned in and dropped his voice, the words carried anyway, the way important words always do across a quiet room:

"—not the loco, I'm telling you. Dreech won't even rig for it. We're going up to the junction, and what we lift goes straight in the boxes, and the blast crew follows us out. That's the whole charter. Lift, seal, done."

"And the surveyor?"

"What about her?"

"Dreech says leave her alone unless she gets ahead of us. Says she's covered. Says her report's already—" the younger man made a writing motion with two fingers, "—being handled."

Her glass was halfway to her mouth and she completed the journey slowly, because a woman who froze at the wrong moment remembered afterward that she'd frozen. *Being handled.* Her report, the one she hadn't written yet, was already being handled. Which meant either arrogance so complete it circled back to stupidity—or a channel into the county clerk's office, or the state engineer's, or wherever her finished survey was supposed to land. Someone had arranged the whole pipeline in advance: her stamp at one end, a filing cabinet at the other, and between them a valley full of something that must never see daylight.

She set the glass down. Outside the window, the anchorage lights had come on, three barges strung across the dark water like beads on a wire, and beyond them the gorge stood black against the last of the sky, waiting for the ice to give it six tides of mercy.

Five tides until the first opening. She'd done the math on the train, done it again at the harbormaster's board, done it a third time tonight against the fleet's obvious schedule, and the answer never changed: she could not beat them upriver on foot, and she had no boat, no pilot, and no claim to the channel except a piece of paper that was already worthless.

What she had was a map of every dead line in the territory, a head full of the county records, and the knowledge that in a river town there is always someone who knows the water better than any charter can buy.

She was still turning that over when the door opened and the harbor's evening cold came in with it, along with a lean man in a river pilot's oilskin, hat pushed back, grin arriving a half-second before the rest of him did.

"Well," said Tam Voss, in a voice pitched just loud enough for her table and no louder, "if it isn't the only engineer on this coast who reads tide tables for pleasure. Buy you a real drink, Miss Thornbury? I hear you're going upriver."

Jude looked at him—at the easy shoulders and the eyes that weren't easy at all—and felt the cold clarity sharpen another degree, because she had not told a soul in Rivergate that she intended anything of the kind.

"I haven't said so to anyone," she said.

"No," Tam agreed, settling into the chair across from her uninvited, turning his glass base-down on the wood like a man closing a deal. "But the river already knows. It always does."
