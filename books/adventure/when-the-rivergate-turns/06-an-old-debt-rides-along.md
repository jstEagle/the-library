## 6. An Old Debt Rides Along

The rain came into Rivergate sideways, the way it always did off the gorge, and Jude stood under the awning of the freight office watching it hammer the harbor flat. Three days since she'd watched the salvage fleet load its dynamite. Three days of doing nothing, which for her was a kind of agony worse than any work.

She had spent those days the way she spent all her waiting: measuring. She'd walked the harbor road twice, counting bollards, noting which moorings were sound and which were rotten. She'd read the tide tables pinned behind glass at the harbormaster's office until she could recite them. She'd priced passage on every boat in the fleet and found that every one of them had stopped taking passengers the same afternoon—someone had bought up the river entire, the way a man buys up a bridge to control who crosses it.

Which meant if she wanted to reach the spur before the fifth tide, she would have to find a boat that wasn't for sale.

"You're still here."

Jude didn't turn. She knew the voice the way you know a voice that once pulled you out of something drowning-shaped—low, unhurried, with a rasp at the bottom of it like gravel shifting in a current. Tam Voss had a voice built for talking over engines and through storms, and it hadn't changed in six years.

"I'm still here," she said.

"Harbormaster says there's a woman asking after charters nobody's selling. Says she pays cash and asks too many questions about ice." He stepped under the awning beside her, close enough that she could smell river water and pipe smoke on his coat. "Says she looks familiar."

"Your harbormaster talks too much."

"He's paid to." Tam smiled, and the smile was the same too—crooked, warm on the surface, with something patient underneath. "Everybody in this town's paid to do something. You noticed that yet?"

Jude turned then and looked at him properly. Six years had silvered him at the temples and put new lines around his eyes, but he was still a big man gone lean, all rope and wire, with hands that looked like they'd been carved from dock pilings. He wore a pilot's oilskin older than she was and a brass whistle on a lanyard that she remembered from a night she tried very hard not to remember.

"I noticed," she said.

"And yet you're still standing here. In the town that's forgetting itself for money." He tilted his head toward the harbor, where the salvage barges sat low and sullen in the rain, their decks stacked with cutting gear under tarps. "That takes either nerve or a reason."

"Maybe both."

"Good." He said it simply, as if she'd answered a question he'd been carrying. "I've got a boat. The *Kestrel*. Flat-bottomed, shallow draft, ugly as sin. I run her upriver when the gorge opens and downriver when it doesn't, and I don't ask my cargo what it's guilty of." He paused. "I've got room for one passenger, and I'm offering her the berth free, which should tell you how much I like money."

"It should tell me what you want instead."

Tam laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of him. "Six years and you're still the same. All right. Fair. Here's what I want." He looked out at the rain, and for a moment his face did something complicated. "I want to be on the river when this thing breaks open. Whatever it is. I've been running this water twenty years, Jude, and I know what a town smells like when it's holding its breath. Rivergate's been holding its breath since before you got here. Since before me. And now there's dynamite on the docks and a survey team missing and a charter signed by a name that made an old man in the county records office go white as chalk when I asked about it." He shrugged. "I want in. That's all. Call it curiosity. Call it whatever you like."

Jude said nothing. Rain drummed the awning. Out on the gray water, a gull screamed once and gave up.

Because here was the problem, and it sat in her chest like a stone: she owed this man. Six years ago, in Corvane, after she'd stood up in a hearing room with shaking hands and testified about what Aldous Crane's crews had done to the brake lines on the mountain division—after the verdict went the wrong way and Crane's men started asking after her by name at every hotel and depot between Corvane and the coast—it was Tam Voss who'd come to her boarding house at two in the morning with a lantern shuttered to a slit and said, *There's a boat leaving. You're on it.* It was Tam who'd run her down forty miles of black water with no lights, past checkpoints where men with rifles stood on the banks, and set her ashore at dawn in a town whose name she never learned, with a roll of bills pressed into her hand and instructions to never come back.

He'd saved her life. There was no version of the arithmetic where that wasn't true.

And because he'd saved her life, he could end it. That was the other half of the arithmetic, the half she'd learned the hard way: everyone who knows how to save you also knows where you sleep. A debt was a leash with the collar on the other end, and she'd spent six years making sure no one held hers.

"I'll pay," she said. "Full charter rate. I don't ride free."

"I don't want your money."

"I know. That's why I'm paying."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, not hurt exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who understood that trust, once it had been broken often enough, became a currency you counted rather than gave.

"All right," he said quietly. "Full rate. And one condition."

"Name it."

"When you figure out what happened to that survey team—and you will, because you're the only person I've ever met who can't leave a wrong number alone—you tell me first. Before the newspapers, before the authorities, before anyone. You tell *me*." He held her gaze. "I ran you out of Corvane without asking why. I never asked what you saw in that hearing room, or what Crane had on the judges, or why a grown woman shook like a leaf testifying about brake linings. I earned the right to stop being the last to know."

The rain kept falling. Somewhere across the harbor, a winch groaned as the fleet loaded another crate of explosives, and the sound went through Jude like a needle finding a nerve.

*Tell him first.* It was such a small thing. Such a reasonable thing. And that was exactly what made her skin prickle, because everything dangerous in her life had arrived wearing the clothes of something reasonable. A contract for a routine survey. A harbormaster helpful with tide tables. A stranger in Harrow Bend asking after her last employer.

But it was Tam. It was the man with the shuttered lantern and the black-water run and the roll of bills she'd used to buy her first map of closed lines—the map that had become her whole strange life. If she couldn't trust him, then the word trust meant nothing anywhere on earth, and she might as well stop moving and let the world take her.

If she could trust him, though—if she could trust him, then why had someone bought up every charter on the river except his? Why was the one boat available to her the one boat offered free by a man who owed her nothing and wanted, he claimed, only to be told things first?

"That's fair," she heard herself say. "You hear it first."

He put out his hand. She took it. His grip was dry and strong and steady as a rail spike, and she hated how much steadier she felt for having taken it.

"We cast off with the second tide," Tam said. "Day after tomorrow, just past dawn. The gorge won't be fully clear yet—first day's always thick with brash ice—but the *Kestrel*'s built for it. We'll make better time than that fleet; they're heavy-laden and their lead pilot's cautious. Cautious men are slow men." He grinned. "You'll want to be aboard tonight. I keep odd hours and earlier ones."

"Tonight," Jude agreed.

* * *

The *Kestrel* lay at the far end of the harbor, past the fish sheds, tied up at a rotten private wharf that nobody official would bother inspecting. She was forty feet of working boat with a patched wheelhouse and an engine housing that had clearly been rebuilt more than once by someone who loved her. Jude walked her length in the evening light, reading her the way she read locomotives—by her wear patterns, her repairs, her honest scars.

"She's beautiful," Jude said, meaning it.

"She's a barge with opinions," Tam said, but he touched the rail as he passed it, the way some men touch a horse's neck, and Jude filed that away with everything else.

She spent the evening in the wheelhouse while Tam checked his engine, spreading her false ledger pages on the chart table and copying figures by lamplight—depths, gradients, bridge clearances from the old county surveys, anything that would help her estimate where the hidden tramway cut might enter the canyon wall. Beneath the ledger pages, wrapped in oilcloth, her real map stayed folded. She did not unfold it. Not even here. Especially not here.

It was near midnight when she heard his boots on the deck and looked up to find Tam standing in the wheelhouse doorway, watching her with an expression she couldn't parse.

"You copy figures like a woman saying prayers," he said.

"It's how I think."

"Mm." He came in and leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. "Can I ask you something, and you can throw me overboard if you don't like it?"

"You can ask."

"The night I took you out of Corvane. You never told me what you saw. What made a railroad engineer risk hanging to testify about brake linings." His voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not asking now, either. I'm asking something else. Are you running from the same thing here? Or toward it?"

Jude looked down at her ledger pages, at the neat columns of numbers that were lies stacked on top of a truth folded in oilcloth.

"Does it matter?" she said.

"To me? No." He said it without hesitation, and that was the strange part—that it rang true. "I've hauled contraband and coffins and one time a senator's mistress, and I never once cared what any of them were running from. But I'll tell you what matters to me, Jude Thornbury." He straightened, and the lamplight caught the old scar along his jaw that she'd never asked about either. "Twenty years on this river, I've watched men with money treat this valley like a purse. They closed that spur up there for a reason, whatever it was, and they paid this whole town to nod along, and now they're loading dynamite to make sure nobody ever digs it back up. And the only person who's come through here in years who gives a damn about the *why* of it is a woman I once smuggled to safety who copies dead men's survey figures like scripture." He tapped the doorframe once with a knuckle. "So no, I don't care what you're running from. I care that you're the first person in twenty years who's running *at* something. And I'd like to be aboard for it."

Jude's throat was tight. She looked at the lamp, at the rain beginning again against the wheelhouse glass, at the dark line of the gorge upstream where the glacier waited behind its wall of ice.

"Thank you," she said, and the words came out rougher than she intended.

"Don't thank me yet." He pushed off from the doorframe. "Get some sleep. Second tide comes early, and the river doesn't wait for anybody—not even debts."

She slept in the crew bunk below, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of engine oil and woodsmoke, and she dreamed of Corvane, of a hearing room full of faces, of a lantern shuttered to a slit, and woke before dawn with her heart going like a drive rod.

* * *

The second tide came up the gorge at 6:14, and the *Kestrel* cast off at 6:20 into a river the color of hammered tin.

The gorge was everything the harbormaster had promised and worse. Brash ice rode the current in plates and rafts, grinding against the hull with a sound like teeth, and the canyon walls rose sheer and dripping on either side, so close in places that Jude could have touched them with a boat hook. Tam worked the wheel like a man born to it, reading the water ahead the way she read track ahead—for the lie in it, the thing that wasn't what it appeared.

"See that smooth water there?" he said, nodding at a stretch of river that looked, to Jude's eye, perfectly calm. "That's where the current turns over. Underneath it's hauling like a freight train downhill. Water lies, Jude. Always has. Only thing on earth that lies prettier than water is a railroad company."

They passed the mouth of the side canyon where the spur climbed toward the wreck country, and Jude marked it against her watch and her charts, committing the sightlines to memory: the split rock on the north wall, the leaning pine, the seam of white quartz like a scar. On the return, in whatever boat she could beg or borrow, she'd need to find the tramway cut blind, at speed, with the fleet behind her.

Behind them—or ahead of them, depending on the current's mood—the salvage fleet's lead barge was visible perhaps a mile downstream, a smudge of smoke and tarpaulin, gaining where the channel widened, losing where it narrowed. Tam glanced back at it once and grunted.

"They'll catch us at the narrows tomorrow, if their pilot's any good. He is. That's Marek Brandt's boat—they hired the best on the coast." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white on the wheel spokes. "We'll want a plan for that."

"I have one," Jude said. "There's a derelict lock above the narrows. Stone-built, 1870s, abandoned when they regraded the channel. Every chart marks it as silted shut. It isn't. The gates still swing if you know the trick of them, and I know the trick."

Tam stared at her. "How in God's name do you know that?"

"Because I've spent six years mapping every piece of infrastructure this state abandoned and forgot," Jude said, "and because the man who built that lock wrote a letter to the county engineer complaining about the gate mechanism, and the letter's in the records office in Harrow Bend, and I read it twice." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "Railroads close, Mr. Voss. Paper doesn't."

For a long moment Tam said nothing. Then he laughed, that short surprised laugh again, and shook his head.

"You know what you are, Jude Thornbury? You're the most dangerous kind of passenger there is. The kind who's already read the river's mail." He turned back to the wheel. "All right. Derelict lock it is. And when we're through it and the fleet's cursing on the other side, you and I are going to have that conversation you promised. First. Before anyone."

"First," she agreed.

The gorge narrowed ahead, the walls closing in, the ice thickening on the water, and somewhere upstream in the dark of the sealed junction, whatever the survey team had found—or become—waited with the patience of stone. Jude stood at the rail and watched the canyon swallow the daylight, and she thought about debts and leashes, and about how the difference between the two was only ever proven by what happened when the rope went tight.

Tomorrow, she suspected, it would go tight.
