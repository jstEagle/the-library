## 7. First Tide

Tam Voss had run the Rivergate gorge eleven times in his life, and he had hated it every single time.

Not because of the water. The water was honest. Water told you what it was going to do a half-second before it did it, if you knew how to listen, and Tam had spent forty years learning to listen the way other men learned to pray. No — what he hated was the ice. Ice lied. Ice sat there all winter looking like a road, like something solid you could trust your weight to, and then on the first warm tide of the season it groaned and shrugged and became a thousand tons of moving malice with no opinion of yours in its head at all.

He stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* — his boat, his one possession he'd never sold or lost or walked away from — and watched the gorge do its waking.

It was still dark, that particular gray-dark before dawn when the mountains are only a rumor of themselves. Below him the river ran high and brown-green with melt, and where the gorge narrowed at the throat, the ice shelf that had sealed it since November was letting go. He could hear it. That was the thing nobody who hadn't heard it could understand: the sound. Not a crack, not a boom, but a long low moan like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and then the tick-tick-tick of smaller fractures running through the shelf like fingers walking up a spine.

"First tide's open," said Dree, his deckhand, coming up from the engine well with grease to her elbows. "Barely. You've got maybe four feet of clearance at the Narrows and that's being generous."

"I don't need generous," Tam said. "I need now."

Dree looked at him a moment longer than she needed to. She was a good hand and a better reader of faces, which was why he'd hired her and also why she was trouble. "She's aboard," Dree said. Not a question.

"She's aboard."

"And you're going to tell her."

Tam didn't answer that one, and Dree took the non-answer for what it was and went back down into the engine well, shaking her head in a way that meant *your funeral, old man.*

Jude Thornbury was aft, coiling lines with the economy of someone who had worked boats before or something close enough — railroaders and rivermen spoke the same language of knots and loads, Tam had always thought, both trades built on the same religion: the load goes where you put it, or people die. She'd been up since four. Of course she had. He'd known her three days short of nine years and he had never once seen her sleep past first light, never seen her take a room twice, never seen her sit with her back to a door. She moved through the world like a woman being followed, even when — especially when — nobody was behind her.

He'd told himself, taking the money, that it wasn't really a betrayal. That was the arithmetic liars use. He'd told himself he was doing her a favor, that whoever wanted her in Rivergate couldn't hurt her worse than her own habits already were, that a woman who mapped dead lines for a living was walking toward a closed door eventually and better it be one he could see. He'd told himself the money was for the *Kestrel*, for the new boiler she needed, for Dree's wages through the lean season.

All true. None of it clean.

The ice moaned again, longer this time, and a slab the size of a barn floor calved off the eastern shelf and went under with a sound like a held breath released. The wake of it rolled upriver against the current, wrong-feeling, water moving two directions at once, and the *Kestrel* lifted and dropped on it like a horse deciding whether to buck.

"That'll be the tide," Tam called aft. "We go on this one or we wait six months. Your survey window starts now, engineer."

Jude came forward, tying off the last coil without looking at it. Her eyes were on the gorge. He watched her read it the way he read it — the gap where the shelf had let go, the brown water shouldering through, the white line of current at the throat marking where the river got serious. She had a map-look about her even standing still. Like she was drawing the whole valley in her head and would remember every line of it forever.

"How long to the wreck site?" she asked.

"At this water? Three hours up, if the Narrows behave." He put his hands on the wheel. "They won't behave."

"Good," Jude said, and Tam almost laughed, because that was the thing about her — bad water made her calm. Calm water made her itch. Give the woman a flood and she turned into the steadiest soul on the river; give her a quiet afternoon and she'd be checking the horizon for whatever was coming.

He opened the throttle and the *Kestrel* leaned into the current.

* * *

The gorge at first light was a cathedral with the roof falling in.

Walls of granite rose sheer on either side, five hundred feet, eight hundred, streaked with snowmelt that fell in silver threads and didn't so much fall as blow sideways in the cold air off the ice. The channel ran between shelves of blue-white ice that overhung the water like eaves, and everywhere, constantly, the sound — that groaning, ticking, cracking language of a glacier deciding what to keep and what to give up. Chunks came off the shelves as they passed, small ones, then bigger ones, each splash sending up spray that froze on the rails within minutes. Dree chipped ice off the wheel housing twice in the first hour.

Jude stood at the bow rail the whole way, hood up, watching the walls.

"You've run this in calving season before," she said. It wasn't quite a question. She had a way of asking things that weren't questions, framing statements as openings and letting you decide whether to walk through them. He'd forgotten how tiring that was.

"Eleven times," Tam said. "Twelve, counting today."

"Why does a man run an ice gorge twelve times?"

"Same reason a woman maps dead rail lines, I expect." He said it lightly. He didn't feel light. "Because somebody closed it and wouldn't say why."

She turned then. Just her head, just enough to look at him over her shoulder, and the look lasted half a second too long to be casual. Then she faced the water again, and Tam felt the moment pass the way you feel a rock slide under the hull — no damage yet, but it happened, and the river remembers.

The Narrows came up on them an hour in, and the Narrows were lying.

From upstream they looked passable — a gap maybe sixty feet wide between opposing shelves, the current funneled through it in a single brown rope of water standing up in the middle like a ridgepole. But the ridgepole meant the deep channel was dead center, and dead center was exactly where the ice wanted to meet. As they drew close, a slab calved off the western shelf, slow as a door swinging, and hit the water with a report like a cannon and sent a wave across the whole channel that picked the *Kestrel* up and carried her sideways ten feet before Tam got her nose back into it.

"Port side!" he shouted, and Dree threw her weight on the sweep, and Jude — Jude was already there, hands on the rail, reading the water ahead and calling it back to him in a flat engineer's voice: "Hole on the left, boil on the right, ride the seam between them, thirty feet, then it opens."

So they rode the seam. The *Kestrel* went through the Narrows the way a needle goes through cloth, with the ice breathing on both sides close enough to touch, and then the gorge widened and the current gentled and the three of them stood in the sudden quiet listening to their own hearts come down.

Dree laughed, high and shaky. Jude didn't laugh. She looked back at the Narrows once, the way a person looks back at a door they've just locked, and Tam saw her face in profile and understood that she hadn't been afraid. She'd been *counting*. Timing the passage, measuring the channel, filing it away somewhere in that head full of closed lines.

A woman like that doesn't get lured anywhere, he thought. A woman like that gets *aimed*.

And that was when he decided he couldn't hold it until the wreck site. Because the fleet was behind them — he'd seen the lead barge's lights below Rivergate before dawn, cutting gear and dynamite crated on her deck, and by now she'd be entering the gorge on the same tide, an hour back, maybe less — and when she caught them, and she would catch them, because barges carry more coal than the *Kestrel* ever dreamed of hauling, Jude was going to need to know whose side the man at the wheel was actually on. Better she learn it from him in open water than from a stranger at gunpoint.

"Kill the throttle to half," he told Dree. Then, to Jude: "Come here. I have to tell you something, and I want both hands free while I do it."

* * *

She came aft slowly. She stopped out of arm's reach, which he noted, and put her back to nothing, which he also noted, and folded her arms, and waited. Nine years, and she still waited like that — like patience was a weapon she was very good with.

"Nine years ago," Tam said, "in Corvane, after you gave evidence against Aldous Crane. When there were men on the platform at the east station and the west station and the ferry landing, and every road out of that town had a price on it. You remember how you left."

"The grain barge," Jude said. "Down the slough at night, no lights."

"My barge. My slough. My idea." He kept his eyes on the channel, mostly. "You never asked me why I did it. Why a stranger risked his boat and his neck for a railroad engineer nobody'd ever heard of."

"You said you hated Crane."

"I do hate Crane. That was true and it wasn't the reason." The current pushed at the hull and he corrected for it without thinking, forty years in his wrists. "The reason was a man named Ellery Voss. My brother. He drove maintenance-of-way for the Mountain Division, and in the spring of the year before your testimony, his crew was sent up the Rivergate spur to clear a slide that the company swore was minor. Six men went up. The company's books say five came down. There's no grave for the sixth because officially there was no sixth, and my brother spent the rest of his short life drinking himself toward the truth of what happened up that canyon, and the truth killed him slower than the drink did."

Jude had gone very still. Stillness on her was different from stillness on other people — it was total, animal, the stillness of a creature deciding whether the thing in front of it was threat or kin.

"So when I pulled you out of Corvane," Tam said, "it was because I heard a woman was testifying against Crane's empire, and I thought — maybe this one pulls a thread that unravels the whole coat. Maybe this one finds out what happened to Ellery." He finally looked at her straight. "That part was true. Every mile I've run you since, every favor, every night you slept aboard and ate at my table — that part was true."

"But," Jude said.

"But three weeks ago a letter found me in Graywater Landing. No signature. Money in it — more money than the *Kestrel*'s worth, and I'm not a man who lies to you about sums. And instructions." His mouth was dry. The ice groaned somewhere upstream, long and low, and he let the sound fill the pause because he couldn't. "Bring Jude Thornbury to Rivergate by the opening tide. She'll come if you ask her right. And they knew how to ask me right, Jude. They knew about Ellery. They knew things about you and me that nobody living should know — that you keep a map of closed lines sewn into false ledger pages, that you've never slept in the same town twice, that you were hired to survey the spur. Whoever wrote that letter has been reading your life like a timetable."

The silence between them had weight, like cargo shifting.

"You took it," Jude said. Flat. Not angry yet — anger would come later, he could see it queued up behind her eyes like cars behind a locomotive — but first, assessment. First the engineer weighing the load.

"I took it."

"And brought me anyway. To the same place I was already hired to go."

"That's the part I keep telling myself makes it not what it is," Tam said. "I figured — you're going regardless. At least this way I'm beside you when you find out why everybody wants you there." He shook his head. "But that's not the whole of it either, and you deserve the whole of it. The money scared me, Jude. People don't pay that much to watch. They pay that much because they want you in Rivergate for a reason, and I handed you to them like a parcel, and I've been sick about it since Graywater, and I'm telling you now, mid-river, because the fleet's lead barge entered the gorge twenty minutes behind us and when it catches up I need to know — we need to know — whether you can stand at my wheel trusting the man steering it."

Jude looked at him for a long time. The gorge roared softly around them, ice talking to itself, meltwater threading down black stone. Somewhere behind, miles back, a barge whistle sounded — one long note, flattened by distance, unmistakably a signal, unmistakably aimed at them.

Then she did the last thing he expected. She stepped closer, into arm's reach, and put one hand flat on the wheel housing beside his, and said:

"Show me the letter."

"It's in the chart locker. I memorized it and I've been meaning to burn it for three weeks."

"Don't burn it. Paper talks." Her voice was steady, and colder than the river air, and he realized with a strange sinking relief that she wasn't forgiving him — she was *reassigning* him. From friend to asset, or from asset to accomplice, he couldn't yet tell which, and neither, he suspected, could she. "Whoever wrote it knew my map exists. Nobody knows my map exists. Which means they got it from someone I trusted, sometime, somewhere — and that list is shorter than yours, Tam, and I need to start crossing names off it tonight."

"Am I crossed off?"

"Not yet." She turned back to the bow, to the channel, to the wreck waiting somewhere up that drowned canyon. "You're on probation. Same as everyone."

The whistle sounded again, closer now, and around the eastern bend the lead barge's smoke showed above the ice shelves, dirty against the white, coming on fast with the tide behind it.

Tam opened the throttle all the way, and the *Kestrel* ran.
