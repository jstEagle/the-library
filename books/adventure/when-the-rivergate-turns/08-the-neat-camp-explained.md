## 8. The Neat Camp, Explained

Jude went back to the camp at first light, because the camp was the only thing in the valley that had ever answered her questions honestly.

The morning came down the gorge the way it always did here—late, sideways, filtered through a thousand feet of granite until it arrived gray and thin as dishwater. She had slept three hours in the lee of a fallen trestle with her coat buttoned to the throat and the survey team's brass theodolite case pulled close like a dog she didn't trust but couldn't turn out. Twice in the night she had heard the glacier speak from upriver: a long grinding syllable, then silence, then a crack like a rail joint failing under load. The ice was talking to itself. Counting down.

She ate hardtack and cold tea standing over the fire ring, and she made herself look at what she'd refused to look at yesterday.

Boots by the fire. Two pairs, set side by side, laces tucked in—the way a careful man stores boots so scorpions don't move in overnight. Not kicked off. *Placed.* Instruments zeroed and cased, the theodolite's leveling bubbles centered so perfectly that when she'd checked them against her own instrument they agreed to within a hair. Food in oilcloth, tied. A coffee pot rinsed and set upside down on a flat stone to drain. Six bedrolls, six mess kits, six tin cups hanging from a line strung between two alder saplings—and every cup hanging at the same height, spaced by eye with a surveyor's instinct for even intervals.

No tracks leaving. She had walked the perimeter twice more in the gray light, then a third time when the sun finally cleared the rim and showed her everything. Soft river silt on the downstream side, ground that took a print like wax takes a seal. It held the tracks of her own arrival, crisp as engraving. It held deer sign, fox, one set of bear prints days old. It held nothing else. Six men had lived here for weeks—she'd found their latrine trench, their woodpile cut and stacked with military neatness, the shallow pit where they'd burned their paper waste—and then one evening they had apparently risen from their beds, put on their boots, zeroed their instruments, rinsed their cups, and evaporated.

The engineer in her kept doing what the engineer in her always did with data that refused to add up. It stopped treating the problem as impossible and started treating it as mislabeled. *You are asking the wrong question,* Vess used to say, back when he was alive and whole and teaching her to read ballast like scripture. *When a bridge won't carry its load, you don't curse the load. You find out what the bridge is actually made of.*

So: not *how did six men leave no tracks*, but *what if they never left at all*.

Jude stood in the center of the dead camp, turning slowly, and let her eyes stop reading it as an ending and start reading it as a doorway.

She looked at the canyon wall.

It rose sheer on the north side, two hundred feet of gray-green stone seamed with ice, and she had catalogued it yesterday the way she catalogued all rock—as context, as scenery, as the thing that wasn't track. Now she read it properly. There was a band of talus at its base, broken rock spilled from some ancient fall, and the talus did not lie naturally. Nature spills. Nature scatters and slumps and finds its angle of repose and stops. This had been *arranged*. Somewhere under the spill of stone there were edges too regular, blocks seated too deliberately, a slope that shed water instead of drinking it. And above it, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen seep, the rock face carried a vertical scar—a seam of lighter stone, unweathered, running straight up through forty feet of cliff as though someone had once cut into the mountain and sewn it shut again.

Her heart began doing something slow and enormous.

She crossed to the wall and put her hand flat against the scarred seam. Cold came through her glove like a message. She followed the base of the talus north, testing each block with her boot heel, listening—for hollow, for echo, for the drum-sound of stone over space rather than stone over stone. Twenty yards. Thirty. At the forty-yard mark, behind a slab furred with moss, her heel struck iron.

Not rock. Iron. A flat plate, rust-bled, set flush into a block, and when she knelt and scraped the moss away with her knife she found the plate was a door facing, and bolted beside it, folded back against the rock like a wing, a bracket that had once held a lamp.

A tramway. Her mind assembled it before her eyes confirmed anything: a hidden tramway cut into the canyon wall, its portal buried under staged talus, its approach disguised as slope. She had surveyed closed lines for eleven years. She knew every way a railroad could be made to disappear—torn up, trestled over, flooded, plowed under, renamed. But this was the oldest trick of all, the one from before her time and maybe before the law's time: you didn't close a line. You *hid* it. You built it into the mountain and told the world the mountain was empty.

And the government survey team, whose job was to map exactly such things, had found it.

They hadn't vanished. They had *descended.*

* * *

Getting the portal open took her four hours and cost her skin.

The talus was stacked, not fallen—that confirmed itself within minutes, once she started pulling stones and finding chisel marks beneath—but whoever had sealed it had done the work of men who expected never to come back this way, or who wanted very much for no one else to. The lower courses were mortared with frozen mud and packed snowmelt refreeze; the upper ones were simply weight, tons of it, balanced on a keystone logic she had to respect even while cursing it. She worked from the sides, prying with a wrecking bar from her kit, easing each stone out and setting it aside in order so she could rebuild if she had to. The mountain watched her do it. The glacier spoke twice more, closer now, a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

Behind the third course of stone, the air changed. It came out of the gap cool and mineral and utterly still—the breath of a place that had not exchanged air with the sky in years—and it smelled of iron, creosote, old smoke, and something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit rotting in a cellar.

Behind the fifth course, she saw lamplight-colored stone. Cut stone. Dressed blocks laid in courses, with a keystone arch overhead, and carved into the keystone, worn nearly smooth, letters she had to trace with her fingers to read:

**R.G.M.R. — JCT. 9**

Rivergate Mountain Railway. Junction Nine. A name that appeared in no ledger she had ever copied, on no map she had ever bought or stolen or drawn herself. Eleven years of collecting dead lines, and here was one that had never been listed among the living at all.

The last stones came away at midday. Beyond them lay a short adit—twenty feet of arched tunnel, dry-floored, its walls bearing the soot-shadow of torches and lamps—and then the adit opened into the mountain, and Jude stood at the threshold with her lamp raised, and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

It was a junction. A full underground junction, built inside the mountain's ribs: two tunnels converging on a chamber wide enough to hold a wye, rails curving away left and right into darkness, a signal gantry still standing with its lamps dark, a water column folded against the wall like a sleeping bird. The scale of it hit her first—the sheer audacity of carving a working railroad junction into solid granite and then erasing every record of it—and the silence hit her second, a silence with texture, the silence of a cathedral or a tomb. Her lamp threw a small warm circle onto the nearest rail, and the rail gleamed back at her, oiled. *Maintained.* Someone had kept these rails ready for years after the world above decided they had never existed.

And on the floor, in the dust of decades, there were footprints.

Fresh ones. Booted, recent, weeks old at most, dozens of them, going in.

None coming out.

Jude stood very still and let the relief arrive, because it arrived whether she wanted it or not—the hot, shaking flood of it, *they're alive, they went down, nobody died here, the neat camp was a message not a grave*—and then, right on schedule, the relief curdled. Because the footprints went in and did not come out, and the camp above had been packed with the care of men who meant to be gone a long time, and the air rising out of the junction was sweet-rot wrong, and somewhere down in that darkness were six men—or five, or fewer—who had looked at this same threshold and chosen it over the daylight, chosen it *deliberately*, and whatever they had found down there was worth sealing themselves in with.

She thought about turning around. She gave the thought its honest minute. Then she thought about the salvage fleet provisioning in Rivergate harbor, cutting gear and dynamite and a charter signed by a name out of the county records, and about the six tides, and about the fact that she was standing in the only place in the valley where the answer to all of it might actually live.

She went in.

* * *

The junction chamber told its story in layers, the way ruins always do, and Jude read it the way she read everything—bottom up, oldest first.

Bottom layer: the railway itself. Rails, ties, the wye, the signal mast, a dispatcher's alcove cut into the wall with a desk and a telegraph key, its brass still bright under dust. She ran her thumb along the desk's edge and found grooves worn by decades of sleeves. Men had worked here. Men had worked here *for years*, quietly, while the valley above forgot them.

Middle layer: the closure. Near the northern tunnel mouth stood a barricade—not the casual kind, but engineered, a masonry plug with a steel door in its center, and the door was open. Opened recently, from the outside, by tools whose marks still shone bright on the hinges. The survey team had come through here. But the door had been sealed *before* them—she found the date stenciled on the jamb, faded: a month and year eleven years gone, the same season as the rockslide, the same season the spur officially died. Someone had shut this junction the same week the mountain supposedly buried it. The rockslide hadn't closed a line. The rockslide had been the *alibi*.

Top layer, freshest: the team. Their gear was stacked in the dispatcher's alcove with the same terrible neatness as the camp above—bedrolls rolled, rations inventoried in pencil on the crate lids, rope coiled in hanks. They had made a home of it. Weeks of it. Candle stubs in rows, a card deck spread face-down mid-game and never finished, a jacket hung on the signal lever. And pinned to the dispatcher's desk under a brass paperweight, weighted against a draft that could not exist in air this still, was a notebook.

Oilcloth cover. Government issue. On its flyleaf, in a small precise hand, a string of characters that were not words:

**K-7 / R-3 / T-11 / W-1 / JCT-9 / FIFTH**

Coded. Of course coded. One member of the team had sat at this dead man's desk, in a junction erased from history, and written down what they had found—in cipher, because he had known even then that what they found must not fall into casual hands. And he had signed his work with a number, not a name. *Fifth.* As if he'd already begun counting which of them would still be standing when the arithmetic was done.

Jude sat down in the dispatcher's chair—it was absurdly, horribly comfortable, worn to the shape of another man's body—and opened the notebook by lamplight.

The early pages were survey notes in plain hand: elevations, chainages, tunnel condition reports, all meticulous. Then, a third of the way in, the plain hand stopped mid-line, and the cipher began, dense and disciplined, page after page, sometimes thirty entries a day, the writer clearly racing to get it all down. She flipped toward the end and found the last entry undeciphered but dated—dated *after* the night the camp above had gone silent. Whoever wrote it had stayed below alone, keeping records in the dark, for days.

She pressed the notebook flat between her palms and felt her own pulse knock against it.

Below the desk drawer, wedged in the corner where the wall met the floor, she found the rest of it: a second lamp, unused. A tin of matches, full. And scratched into the plaster of the alcove wall, low down, at the height of a man sitting with his back against it—five tally marks, grouped like prison counts, and beneath them a single word in the same small precise hand:

**WAITING.**

The lamp in her own hand trembled once. She steadied it against her knee.

Six men had climbed down into a junction that officially did not exist, and found whatever the rockslide had really buried, and split—she could feel it in the notebook's rhythm, in the way the cipher entries clustered and thinned, in the unfinished card game and the single unused lamp—split into those who would sell and those who would not. Five tallies. One man waiting. And down one of these two black tunnels, past a vault or a wreck or a grave that eleven years of money and dynamite had been spent to keep shut, the truth of Rivergate sat in the dark, breathing.

From far up the gorge, muffled through two hundred feet of stone, the glacier spoke again—and this time the sound did not fade. It went on. A deep, sliding, gathering roar, like a door being dragged open somewhere above the world.

The first tide was opening.

Jude folded the logbook into her coat, took up her lamp, and turned to face the two tunnels—one trending upstream toward the glacier, one down toward the river and the sea—and understood that she no longer had the luxury of choosing wrong.
