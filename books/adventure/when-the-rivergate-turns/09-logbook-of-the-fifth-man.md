## 9. Logbook of the Fifth Man

The tramway cut held its cold like a held breath. Jude had been down there an hour before she dared light the second lamp, and even then she set it behind her pack so the flame wouldn't gutter in the draft that moved through the old junction — a slow, tidal breathing from somewhere deeper in the mountain, as if the rock itself were inhaling and exhaling on the rhythm of the river far above.

She'd carried the logbook out of the sealed survey camp wedged under her arm like contraband, which, she supposed, was exactly what it was. The fifth man's code — if it was a code — had defeated her for the first mile of the climb back to their bolt-hole above the waterline: a shelf of collapsed timber and tarpaulin where Tam's boat could lie hidden beneath overhanging alder. Now, with the lamp trimmed low and her notebook open beside her, she spread the little canvas-bound book flat on a crate and looked at it the way she looked at any machine that refused to run.

Not broken. Just built by someone who didn't want it understood.

The pages were filled with columns of figures. That was the first thing, and for a long while the only thing. Numbers in tight, disciplined rows — elevations, chainages, bearing readings, all of them plausible, all of them the ordinary arithmetic of a government line survey. A stranger flipping through would see nothing but diligence. But Jude had spent eleven years reading other people's surveys, checking their math against the ground, catching the small honest errors and the large dishonest ones, and something about these numbers itched at her the way a misaligned rail joint itches under a wheel.

They were too good.

Survey teams make mistakes. They smudge entries in the rain. They transpose digits when they're tired and cross out and recompute. These figures marched across the page without a single correction, page after page, forty-one pages of flawless transcription — and no survey crew alive worked that cleanly. Which meant these weren't field notes at all. They were a fair copy of something else, dressed up to look like field notes, written by a man who wanted anyone who found this book to stop reading after the first three pages.

Jude turned back to the beginning and started again, slower, reading not the values but the *shape* of them.

* * *

It came to her on page nine, the way most things came to her — not as a flash but as a slow settling, like ballast finding its level.

The third column. Every entry had three columns: distance, elevation, and a third figure that the column header labeled *deviation*. Standard practice. You record how far the actual grade deviates from design. But look at the deviations themselves: they weren't random. Real terrain produces noise — a fraction here, a hair more there, jitter like static. This column rose and fell in smooth, deliberate waves. Sine curves. Someone had generated those numbers from a formula, not from a theodolite.

And once you saw that the third column was fake, the question stopped being *what do the numbers say* and became *what are they hiding*.

She took out her pencil and began subtracting. If the deviation column was invented, then the first two columns might be real — or real up to some transformation. She tried differences. She tried ratios. On the fourth attempt — elevation divided by distance, then multiplied by a constant she pulled out of thin air and adjusted until the results stopped being garbage — the columns resolved into something else entirely.

Dates.

Every row encoded a date and a time. The whole book, all forty-one pages, was a calendar. A diary wearing a surveyor's clothes.

Her hands went still on the crate. Outside, high above, the glacier spoke — a long groan rolling down the gorge like a door dragging over stone — and she let it finish before she touched the pencil again.

* * *

Decoding the dates was the work of two hours and cost her the last of the daylight. Once she had the key, the entries opened like locks, each one short, terse, written by someone trained to report facts and fighting his own training to report anything else.

*Day 1. Camp established at marker 14. Six of us. Orders: confirm grade and clearance for salvage traffic. Harbormaster cooperative.*

*Day 3. Found cut blocked by slide debris. Debris composition wrong. Rock type does not match canyon walls above. Asked R— where the stone came from. He said ask the county. Nobody laughed.*

*Day 5. Cleared first twenty yards of the cut. Found timber shoring underneath the rubble. Shoring is older than the closure. Somebody built inside this hill before the slide ever happened.*

*Day 8. Broke through into a gallery. It is not a mine. It is a junction — rails, turnouts, a platform, all of it finished, all of it dry. The spur doesn't dead-end at the slide. The slide was built to make it look like it dead-ends.*

Jude read that entry three times. Her lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the river ran its dark errand.

A buried junction. Not a wrecked line — a *hidden* one. The rockslide hadn't closed the spur; it had been the closing, a manufactured tomb of stone dumped over a working tunnel so the railroad could strike it from the maps and let the mountain keep its secret. And the government team, sent to certify the line safe for salvage, had walked straight through the lie into whatever the lie was protecting.

*Day 9. Gallery runs four hundred feet to a steel door. Riveted, not welded. Railroad plate, pre-closure stamping. Lock is a time-lock pattern — I have seen its twin on pay cars. This is a vault. This is a payroll vault.*

*Day 10. Voted on whether to open it. Four yes, two no. I was a no. R— said the charter covers "recovery of assets." I said the charter says nothing about opening sealed doors in unreported tunnels. He said the charter says nothing because nobody knew. I said that is exactly my point. We opened it anyway.*

*Day 11. Payroll vault confirmed. Coin, scrip, ledgers. The ledgers matter more than the money. Wages drawn monthly for a full crew — sixty-two names — for fourteen months AFTER the closure. Sixty-two men were paid to work a line that officially did not exist. The payments stop the same month the county records show the final demolition order. There is no demolition debris in the gallery. There was no demolition. There was a burial.*

Sixty-two names.

Jude sat back and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw sparks. She thought of every closed line on her map — the ones she'd ridden at night, the ones she'd slept beside in culverts, the ones whose closures she'd never once questioned because a closure was just weather to her, just a fact of the landscape, like frost heave. Somewhere in those pages of hers were other spurs, other slides, other neat official endings. How many of them ended like this one — not in rockfall but in arithmetic?

*Day 12. Counted the ledger against the county death rolls. Forty-one of the sixty-two appear nowhere. No death certificates. No accident reports. No graves we can find. The closure killed men and the paperwork was written so it killed no one. The company saved the pension fund and the liability both. I am writing this in the dark because I could not sleep with the lamp lit.*

Grief arrived for her the way water arrives in a tunnel — not all at once but rising, finding every low place. She had come up this river chasing a vanished survey team, telling herself it was a contract, a puzzle, a professional curiosity. Now she sat in a hole in a mountain holding the diary of a man who had counted dead men the way she counted ties, and the dead had no names she knew, and that was somehow worse. Strangers. Men who clocked in on a ghost line and were erased so cleanly that even their own families — if the ledger was right, and it had the terrible tidiness of truth — had been paid to stop asking.

Paid. The word snagged, and she went back to the entries with new attention.

*Day 14. R— called a meeting at the vault door. He has been to the surface twice without saying where. He brought back an offer. Not from the company — from a private party. A salvage interest. Payment for silence, each man, signed individually, in exchange for our report confirming the spur ends at the slide and contains nothing. He says it is found money and no skin off anyone. D— took it before he finished talking. So did H—. K— asked how much. I did not ask anything. I told them what I intend to do instead.*

*Day 15. Two signatures on the payment schedule now, maybe three. K— wavers. I copied the ledger pages that prove the wages and the missing men. Copies go where no one will think to look. If anything happens to me, the originals stay in the vault, and the vault stays shut, and the mountain keeps them better than any archive would.*

*Day 16. I have decided how to disappear. It is easier than people think. Everyone believes a man who leaves no tracks has left entirely. Let them believe it.*

So that was the answer to the camp with no footprints. Not vanishing — *descending*. One man had walked into the sealed junction and closed the door behind him, and the others had walked out and signed their names, and between those two motions lay the whole rotten architecture of it: a staged slide, a buried payroll, sixty-two unrecorded deaths, a town paid annually to forget, and now a salvage fleet loading dynamite in Rivergate harbor to blast the evidence into gravel before the ice closed the door for another year.

Jude turned the last pages. The coded entries thinned out, then stopped mid-thought — the final dated entry was sixteen days old, and after it came only blank paper, and then, folded into the binding at the back, a single loose leaf she hadn't noticed before.

It wasn't coded. It was a list. Seven lines, seven names — the six-man team plus one more, a name added later in different ink, cramped and hurried. Beside five of the names stood a figure: an amount of money. Beside the sixth name — the writer's own, she realized, the fifth man's own hand — stood a single word: *refused*.

And beside the seventh name, the one added later, in that cramped, hurried ink, stood not a figure and not a word but a signature.

She recognized the handwriting before she consciously recognized the name. Of course she did. You learn a man's letters the way you learn his walk — on long nights in caboose bunks, on the backs of worksheets he corrected with a red pencil, on the single letter he mailed her every year for eleven years, always postmarked from a different town, always containing the same advice in the same slanting hand: *keep moving, girl; a map of where you've been is a map of where they'll find you.*

Vess.

Her mentor's name sat on the payoff list of a buried massacre, signed in the ink of a man buying silence, and the lamp flame bent in the draft and the mountain breathed its slow tide around her, and Jude Thornbury — who had crossed eleven years and four hundred closed miles on the strength of everything that man taught her — discovered that grief has a floor, and that she had just hit it.

The river, far below, kept moving. It occurred to her, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that she had spent her whole life mistaking motion for innocence.

She folded the loose leaf away, closed the logbook, and began, methodically, to plan how to get into the vault before the fleet's dynamite got there first.
