## 10. Vess's Signature

The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil by the time Jude understood what she was holding.

She had worked through the cipher the way she worked through anything mechanical: patiently, in layers, trusting that a thing built by a human hand could be undone by one. The fifth man's logbook—she had begun thinking of him that way, *the fifth man*, because the government roster listed five names and the camp had been laid out for five and only four sets of gear had shown any sign of recent use—used a railman's cipher. Of course it did. Not a military code, not a surveyor's shorthand. A switch-and-block cipher, the kind yardmasters used to log movements they didn't want porters reading over their shoulders. Jude had learned it at seventeen, sitting on a signal box in the Harrow Bend yards with a man who smelled of coal oil and peppermint, a man who told her that the railroad's true language was written in what it refused to say.

Vess.

She had not let herself think the name yet. She had been circling it for two days the way water circles a drain, and now, in the grey hour before dawn, in the abandoned lockhouse above the junction, the circle closed.

The cipher broke on a repeated phrase. *Block seven, block seven, block seven*—a train order repeated three times in the margin of the third entry, which no dispatcher would ever do, because a repeated order was how you flagged a page for a second reader. It was an invitation. Once she looked for it, the second layer unfolded like a blade: every fourth word, read downward, spelled out the true log beneath the false one.

She read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, because the lockhouse was empty and the river below made its own noise and she needed to hear the words in a human voice to believe they had been written by one.

*Day one. We entered by the tramway cut as planned. The junction is intact. The water never reached the lower gallery. What they buried is not wreckage.*

*Day four. The vault is a payroll vault. Meridian & Coast Line, Rivergate district. Ledgers, pay rolls, correspondence. Enough paper to hang a dozen men.*

*Day nine. Corrigan has made his arrangement. He says we were sent to confirm, not to recover. He says the survey was always a fiction, that our mandate was written by the same office that wrote the closure. I have asked him who. He smiled at me the way a man smiles at a switch he has already thrown.*

*Day eleven. Three of five have signed. I will not write what they were paid, because the number is obscene and numbers that obscene should have to be spoken.*

*Day twelve. I have faked my own going. The camp will be found neat. Neatness is a message to anyone clever enough to read it: we did not struggle, we did not scatter, we were taken by nothing that walks. I have gone down instead of out. If you are reading this, you found the tramway, which means you are either very good or very expendable. Check the signatures. Then decide which you are.*

The signatures began on the next page.

There were five of them, arranged in a column like men lining up at a pay window, because that was exactly what they were. The first three were the ones the log had already named—Corrigan with his confident, slanted hand, and two others whose names meant nothing to her, engineers and instrument men who had traded their testimony for a figure she would never earn in a lifetime of survey contracts. The fourth signature was the fifth man's own, written in cipher, a deliberate forgery of consent that fooled no one and was meant to fool no one: a record that he had been offered the same price and had answered it with a lie instead of a yes.

The fifth signature was at the bottom, where a paymaster signs.

Jude knew the hand before she knew the name.

She knew it the way you know a scar on your own body—by feel, by the particular drag of the downstroke, by the way the letters leaned as if walking into a wind that never let up. She had watched that hand draw track diagrams on butcher paper. She had watched it sign her own journeyman's card, pressing hard enough to emboss three sheets below. She had spent eleven years reading that handwriting in her sleep, in the margins of her maps, in the phantom voice that narrated every mile of track she surveyed: *Check it twice, girl. The rail remembers what the man forgets.*

**A. Vess.**

Not a witness. Not a co-signer. The signature at the bottom, the authorizing line, the hand that had received the payment on behalf of the others and distributed it—the paymaster's own.

The lamp guttered. Jude did not move to refill it. She sat in the failing light with the logbook open on her knees and felt something give way inside her with the slow, grinding patience of a bridge pier settling into bad ground—not a collapse, nothing so merciful as a collapse, just a structural surrender that had been coming for years and had finally, quietly, arrived.

* * *

She had been twenty-two the night Vess came for her.

She had testified—she could still feel the courtroom's particular silence, the way a rail baron's lawyers had made the word *derailment* sound like a child's accusation—and the verdict had gone the wrong way, and everyone in Rivergate's sister towns knew what happened to witnesses who lost. She had been packing to run, doing it badly, crying while she folded shirts, when the knock came. Vess stood on the landing with a lantern and a timetable and no explanation at all except: "There's a freight leaving in forty minutes and a siding where nobody checks manifests. You'll be in Harrow Bend by morning and nobody will know which way you went, because I'm going to make sure there are three wrong ways that look right."

He had spent the next eleven years teaching her how to disappear. How to keep a map of closed lines. How to never sleep twice in the same town. How to read a town's silences the way you read a switchstand, and how to be gone before the blade of attention fell. *Keep moving, girl,* he'd said, the last time she saw him, pressing a folded map into her hands on a platform in the rain. *A woman standing still is a woman who can be found. And a woman who can be found can be bought, or buried. Move.*

She had built her whole life on that sentence. She had built her whole life on him.

And now she sat in a dead lockhouse above a buried junction and understood, with the terrible clarity of the newly ruined, what the teaching had actually been. Not protection. *Concealment.* He hadn't taught her to survive. He had taught her to stay lost—because a witness who keeps moving is a witness who can never be deposed. Every town she'd fled, every contract she'd abandoned mid-survey, every friendship she'd let starve rather than let it root her somewhere findable—eleven years of it, and the whole architecture of her restlessness might have been load-bearing masonry in someone else's cover-up. He had buried the truth under a rockslide and then buried *her* under a habit, and called the second burial a gift.

The oil ran out. The lamp died. Jude sat in the dark and let the river speak for both of them.

* * *

Dawn came up grey through the lockhouse's broken shutter, the color of old ledger paper. Jude lit a match off the striker plate—Vess had taught her that trick too, *the rail always gives you a spark if you ask it right*—and read the signature again in daylight, as if the sun might disagree with the lamp.

It did not.

She made herself do the work. That was the only prayer she knew: when the world broke, do the work. She took out her own notebook and copied the signature three times, comparing it against the letter Vess had sent her two years ago, the one she still carried folded in her map case because she had never been able to throw away a single artifact of him. The *V* with its long leading stroke. The double *s* like a set of points closing. The ink pressure, heavy at the start of each word and lifting at the end, the hand of a man always already leaving.

Identical. Not similar. *Identical.* She was a surveyor; she did not deal in similar.

Then she made herself do the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was worse than the signature and she had earned the right to look at it straight. The closure order was dated—she had pulled it from the county records in chapter upon chapter of dust—eleven years ago. Eleven years. She had testified against the baron ten years ago. The closure had come *first*. The rockslide, the buried vault, the payoffs, Vess's signature—all of it had been set in motion a full year before she ever stood in a courtroom and said the word *derailment* into that terrible silence.

Which meant her testimony had not been the beginning of anything. It had been a *middle*. It had been one moving part in a machine that was already running, a gear that turned and turned and accomplished nothing except to teach a young woman to run. Maybe Vess had smuggled her out of town because he loved her. She believed that; she would always believe that; love and treachery were not, she was learning, mutually exclusive substances—they alloyed, the way metals alloy, and the alloy was often stronger than either pure thing. But maybe he had also smuggled her out because a runaway witness was useful. Because a woman who kept moving, who mapped dead lines obsessively, who never stopped asking about closed spurs, was a woman who would eventually—inevitably, predictably, *exactly as she had*—be drawn back to Rivergate by the one closed line she could never stop thinking about.

Her contract had been bait. The harbormaster's arrangement had been bait. And the hook—the hook had been Vess's teaching, Vess's map, Vess's voice in her head saying *keep moving* for eleven years, steering her like a pilot steers a barge through fog, one small correction at a time, until she arrived precisely where someone had paid for her to arrive.

She thought of the salvage fleet at anchor, the cutting gear, the dynamite. She thought of the name on the private charter, the name from the county records, and the way it had sat in her stomach like a swallowed nail. She thought of Tam, and of the question she had been refusing to ask since the first tide: *who told you I was coming, Tam, and what did they say I was worth?*

The match burned down to her fingers. She let it.

* * *

She climbed out of the lockhouse into wind that smelled of ice and iron. Below her, the gorge held its breath between tides, the river running low and brown over the drowned tramway cut, the glacier upvalley groaning like a ship working loose from its moorings. Somewhere downstream, the fleet's lead barge was loading its second charge of dynamite. Somewhere upriver, Tam was doing something with a boat and a motive she still couldn't read. And somewhere in the ground beneath her feet, past the collapsed rim and the flooded gallery, a man who had faked his own disappearance was waiting in the dark with a vault full of paper, trusting a stranger to come back.

Jude opened her map case.

She had carried it for eleven years. Every closed line she'd ever walked, every dead spur, every siding where the rails had been pulled up and the silence poured in after—she had recorded them all with a fidelity that had always felt like devotion and now felt like evidence. Because the map had a pattern. She had never let herself see it, the way you never let yourself see the shape of a thing you love when the shape is wrong. But the closed lines clustered. They clustered around Rivergate. They clustered around the Meridian & Coast's old district like scar tissue around a wound, and at the center of every cluster, if you traced the closures back through the county records, you found the same three names, over and over, and one of them signed with a long leading stroke and a heavy hand.

She had not been mapping dead lines. She had been mapping the crime, mile by mile, for eleven years, in her own hand, with her own initials in the corner of every sheet.

She laughed. It came out wrong, one dry note, and the wind took it.

Then she sat down on the lockhouse step with her back against cold stone, took out her notebook, and did what she had been trained to do by the only father the rails had ever given her: she checked it twice. She copied the fifth signature. She copied the charter name beside it. She drew the connecting lines—closure to rockslide, rockslide to vault, vault to payoffs, payoffs to the fleet, the fleet to her contract, her contract to the man who had taught her that stillness was death. When she finished, the page looked like a switching diagram for a yard where every track led into the same wall.

The tide was turning. She could feel it before she could see it—the river's note changing, the groan of the glacier answering, the six-tide clock ticking down toward its fourth hour. Two tides left. Maybe three, if the meltwater ran slow.

She folded the notebook into her coat, next to the logbook, next to Vess's two-year-old letter, and stood. Her legs were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to be done with her after a night like that, but legs were honest machinery: they carried what you asked them to carry, and they didn't care what your heart thought about the freight.

*Keep moving, girl,* said the voice in her head, warm and peppermint-sweet, and for the first time in eleven years she answered it aloud, to the wind, to the river, to the buried man and the buried paper and the buried truth.

"No," she said. "I'm done moving where you point."

Downstream, around the first bend of the narrows, a whistle sounded—two long, one short, the salvage fleet's signal, and it was far too close, and coming on fast.
