## 11. Interception at the Narrows

Tam Voss had run the Rivergate gorge in every season a man could name and two that hadn't been named yet, and he knew the sound the water made when it was lying to him. It made that sound now.

He stood at the wheel of the *Maggie Bright* with his hands light on the spokes, letting the current do most of the talking while he listened underneath it. Ahead, the gorge narrowed toward the Narrows—two granite shoulders leaning close enough that a barge could nearly shake hands with itself—and between them the river ran green-black and fast, the meltwater already thickening with the second day of the calving season. Ice groaned somewhere upstream, a long tectonic complaint, like the mountain clearing its throat before it said something permanent.

Behind them, three miles back and gaining, came the fleet.

"Tam." Jude stood at the rail on the port side, her survey case strapped across her body like a bandolier, binoculars raised. She'd been standing there for an hour without shifting her weight, which was its own kind of warning. "Three barges. The lead one's running double boiler pressure—I can see the smoke standing straight up even against the wind."

"I see it."

"They're not even trying to hide anymore."

"No," Tam said. "They're not."

That was the part that sat wrong in him, heavier than the water, heavier than the confession he still hadn't finished making. A salvage fleet that wanted to look legitimate ran slow and flew no colors and pretended to be fishing boats if anyone asked. This one ran hot and abreast, spread across the river like a hand closing. Whoever commanded it had stopped caring what Rivergate thought days ago—which meant either they'd already bought everyone worth buying, or they'd decided there wouldn't be anyone left to ask questions by the time the ice closed.

Both possibilities pointed at the same place: the junction. And both pointed at the same woman standing six feet from him with a map of dead lines folded into her coat.

He'd told her on the first tide. *I was paid to bring you here.* He'd watched her take it the way she took everything—not flinching, not forgiving, just filing it somewhere behind those gray eyes where she kept all the arithmetic she didn't say out loud. She hadn't thrown him overboard. She hadn't forgiven him either. They were running a river together on the thinnest possible contract: he would get her where she needed to go, and she would decide afterward whether he was a friend or a leash. He still didn't know which one he was himself, and that not-knowing was starting to feel like drowning in slow water.

The Narrows opened ahead of them, and Tam's stomach dropped the way it always did, because the Narrows was where the river stopped being negotiable.

* * *

The gorge pinched to forty yards. The walls rose sheer on either side, streaked white with old high-water marks, and the current through the throat ran better than ten knots with the melt behind it. There was exactly one line through—the deep channel hugging the north wall where the rock had undercut and the water ran smoothest—and every pilot who'd ever worked this river learned it the same way: by watching someone else get it wrong.

"There." Jude lowered the binoculars. "They're not following our line. They're ahead of us."

Tam saw it then, and his mouth went dry. The lead barge wasn't behind them at all. It had cut upriver through the night on the outside channel—the wide, slow water east of Gull Rock that cost you four miles but saved your hull—and now it sat anchored just above the Narrows' upper lip, broadside to the current, deliberate as a door being shut. Two more barges were coming up fast behind the *Maggie Bright*, close enough now that he could read the lettering on their bows through the spray: *Cormorant*. *Iron Meridian*. No company names he trusted. No names at all, really, just paint.

"They're going to close the river," Jude said. Not a question. An engineer reading a diagram.

"Aye." Tam spun the wheel two points to starboard, taking them out of the racing center current toward the slack water under the south wall. "A barge athwart the throat and we're done. We go around Gull Rock or we don't go at all, and around takes until tomorrow's tide, and tomorrow's tide is one tide shorter than we need."

"So we don't go around."

He looked at her. She was watching the anchored barge with an expression he'd only seen once before, years ago, in a courthouse corridor in Kelso, right before she stood up in front of a room full of men who owned everything she had left in the world and told the truth anyway. It wasn't fear. It was the face of a person doing math that frightened everyone except her.

"Jude." He kept his voice level, pilot to passenger, though his hands had gone cold on the wheel. "There's no line through the Narrows with that barge sitting on it. I've run this water since I was fourteen. There's me and there's the rocks and there's the current, and that's the whole inventory."

"There's the lock," she said.

The word landed in the wheelhouse and sat there.

"The Harrow lock's been dead for thirty years," Tam said. "My father helped pull the gates off it. There's nothing left but the cut and the stonework and maybe half a gate rusted into the cliff."

"The lower gate's still hung. I checked it yesterday when you were sleeping." She unslung the survey case and opened it on the chart table, and there it was—not the map she showed people, the false ledger with its neat fictional lines, but the real one, the true one, the map of closed lines she carried under everything else like a second skeleton. Her finger found the south wall of the Narrows, a hairline scratch of ink no wider than a thread. "Here. The old canal cut. It runs parallel to the gorge for eight hundred yards and rejoins below the pinch. Your father pulled the *gates*, Tam. Somebody else pulled the paperwork. The lock chamber's still flooded—it's been holding river water since before either of us was born because nobody ever bothered to drain it. The masonry's sound. I sounded it."

"You *sounded* it."

"With a weighted line off your stern, yes, while you were snoring and telling yourself you weren't." She looked up at him, and for the first time since he'd confessed, something almost warm moved through her expression—brief, dry, gone again. "You taught me to read water. You just never asked what else I was reading."

The *Maggie Bright* slid into the slack eddy under the south wall, and the roar of the main current fell away so suddenly it left Tam's ears ringing. Above them the granite went up two hundred feet, seamed with ice, dripping. Below them, half-hidden by alder and slide-rock, a dark horizontal slot opened in the cliff face: the mouth of the Harrow cut, twenty feet wide, its stone lips furred with moss, its water black and utterly still.

It looked like a grave. It looked, Tam thought with a lurch, exactly like the kind of place a woman who never slept in the same town twice would know about and no one else alive would.

"The upper end's blocked," he said. "Has to be. Thirty years of rockfall—"

"One slide, at the head of the chamber, and it's loose scree, not bedrock. I can see daylight through it from the lower approach." She was already moving, pulling the case shut, buckling it. "But the lock has to be worked to lift us through the chamber to the upper cut, and the gate machinery's hand-cranked, and there are two gates, and I need hands."

"How many hands?"

"Every one you've got, and mine besides, and we need to be inside before the *Cormorant* closes the last quarter mile, because once they're abeam of this eddy they'll see the cut mouth and post a boat at it."

Tam looked at the slot in the cliff. Then upstream, where the lead barge lay athwart the river like a padlock. Then at Jude Thornbury, who had walked into his wheelhouse three days ago carrying a debt he'd thought was settled and a secret he hadn't known she had, and who was now proposing to drive a working riverboat through a dead canal that existed on no chart but hers.

"You've had this planned since the first tide," he said. "Maybe since before."

"I plan," she said, "because stopping means being found. Get us in the cut, Tam. Then crank."

* * *

Getting in was the easy part, and the easy part still took ten years off his life.

The eddy under the south wall turned lazy and brown-green, and Tam brought the *Maggie Bright* around it stern-first, using the reverse current like a hand on the small of her back, easing her tail toward the cut mouth with two feet of water under the screws and his breath held so long his vision sparked. The boat's stern swung. The alder branches brushed the pilothouse roof like fingers. And then the black stillness of the cut took them, and the noise of the river shut off behind them as completely as a door, and they were inside the mountain.

The Harrow cut was older than the railroad spur, older than the town, dug by some canal company that had gone bust before the century turned—a hundred yards of ambition abandoned mid-dream. The walls rose straight and wet on either side, close enough that Tam could have touched both at once with the boat's poles. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with a patient, metronome patience. The air smelled of stone and cold iron and something green and ancient, like the inside of a well.

And there, thirty feet in, rising out of the black water: the lower gate.

It was oak and iron, crusted white with mineral scale, hanging in its frame like the jaw of something asleep. Beyond it, visible through the gaps where boards had rotted away, the lock chamber stretched back into the gloom—a stone box eighty feet long, flooded to within a few feet of its copings, holding thirty years of river in its cupped hands.

"Gate winch is on the south wall," Jude said, already over the rail and onto the coping, rope in her teeth, moving with the quick economy of someone who'd climbed things she shouldn't have her whole life. "Counterweight chain's probably seized. We'll have to break it loose. Take the starboard crank—I'll take port. On my count, and don't stop until I tell you, even if it feels wrong. Especially then."

"What does feeling wrong mean?"

"It means the gate will fight you like it's alive, and then it will give all at once, and if you stop when it gives you'll lose the crank and possibly a hand." She set her boots against the stone, wrapped the crank handle, and looked at him across the dark water. "Ready?"

Above them, faint through the stone, came a new sound: the deep-throated blast of a steam whistle, bouncing down the gorge. Then another. Then the rattle of an anchor chain being paid out fast, and voices—thin, distorted, echoing off the canyon walls, too many voices, none of them friendly.

They'd seen the *Maggie Bright* vanish. Now they'd find the cut.

"Now," Jude said, and cranked.

The gate fought. God, how it fought. The winch was a fossil, its gears packed with grit and rust, and for the first full turn Tam's arms screamed and the handle didn't move a hair, and he understood with total clarity that this was the moment the whole mad plan died—here, in the dark, against thirty years of corrosion. Then something cracked deep in the mechanism, a sound like a knuckle popping in a giant's fist, and the gate shuddered and began to rise, inch by screaming inch, water geysering through the widening gap beneath it, and the *Maggie Bright* lifted on the surge and pressed her bow against the far gate like a dog nosing a door.

"Keep going!" Jude's voice, ragged, relentless. "Don't you dare stop!"

They cranked. His palms split; he felt the skin go and didn't care. The whistle blasts above came faster now, angrier, and a lantern beam swept across the top of the cut mouth and slid away. The lower gate rose past its sill. The boat surged forward into the chamber, and Jude leapt for the upper gate's winch while Tam threw the wheel hard over to keep the *Maggie*'s hull off the chamber wall, and the boat ground along the stone with a sound like a coffin lid.

The upper gate was worse. Half its height was jammed with slide-rock, exactly as she'd promised—loose scree, rattling down into the chamber as the gate moved, but moving, moving, daylight showing through the gap in a thin bright blade that widened foot by foot.

The first shout came from the cut mouth behind them. A man's voice, amplified by stone: *"Boat in the lock! Boat in the lock—they're in the Harrow cut!"*

"They know," Tam gasped. "Jude, they know where we—"

"That was always the price." She didn't stop cranking. Sweat cut clean lines through the grime on her face, and her eyes were fixed on the blade of daylight with an intensity that looked almost like prayer. "Better they know the road than own the road. Crank."

The gap reached boat-width. Tam slammed the engine telegraph to full ahead, and the *Maggie Bright* drove through the scree in a shower of stone and rotten timber, scraped the chamber's upper sill with a shriek of tortured planking, and burst out of the mountain into open water and afternoon light so sudden and bright that Tam flinched as if shot.

Below the Narrows. Below the anchored barge. Below the padlock, through a door its owners hadn't known existed.

The main current took them instantly, snatched the boat like a hawk taking a rabbit, and Tam fought her nose into the channel with hands that left red prints on the wheel, and the gorge walls blurred past, and behind them the shouting faded and faded and became the river's noise again.

Only then did he look back. The lead barge's deck was black with men. One of them stood at the rail with a glass trained on them, and even at this distance, even across the churn of the Narrows, Tam felt the weight of that gaze settle on the *Maggie Bright* like a hand. The barge began to weigh anchor. The other two wheeled in the current below the pinch, turning downstream after them, boilers already climbing back to pressure.

They knew the road now. That was the price, and it had been paid.

Jude stood at the stern rail, watching them come, her split-knuckled hands resting on the survey case. The wind pulled at her hair and she let it.

"They'll run us down before the third tide," Tam said. "Two barges, deeper draft, more power. We can't outrun them to the junction."

"I know." She didn't turn around. "So we won't outrun them. We'll arrive first anyway. There's a derelict lock at Miller's Bend, too, Tam. And one at Cold Spring. And a tramway cut your charts have never heard of." Now she turned, and there it was again—that courthouse-corridor calm, the arithmetic that frightened everyone but her. "They have a river. I have a map of everything the river forgot. Keep us off the rocks, pilot. I'll keep us ahead."

Upstream, muffled by miles of granite, the glacier calved. The sound rolled down the gorge like the closing of an enormous book, and the water under the keel shivered, and rose, and began to run faster than any of them liked.

The second tide was coming in, and it was bringing the fleet with it.
