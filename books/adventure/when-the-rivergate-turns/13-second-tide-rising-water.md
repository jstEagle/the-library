## 13. Second Tide, Rising Water

The river woke angry.

Tam felt it before he saw it—through the soles of his boots, through the hull of the *Maggie's Grace*, a low tremor like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the water. He stood at the wheel with the dawn still gray behind the eastern peaks and watched the gorge ahead of them change its mind about letting them through.

Yesterday's channel was gone. Where the first tide had opened a lane of black glass between the ice shelves, this morning the meltwater came down from the high country in a brown-green flood, thick with rock flour, carrying whole trees end-over-end like matchsticks. The current ran against them at six knots where it had run at three. The ice walls had retreated another fathom on either side, but what they'd given back in width they'd taken away in temper—the river was higher, faster, louder, and every hour it would get worse until the calving season peaked and the gorge sealed itself for another year.

"Throttle her up," Tam called to Pell, his engineer, who was already at the lever. "We've lost the tide's first two hours. We don't lose more."

Pell didn't answer with words. He answered with the engine—a deepening of the beat under the deck plates, the old sternwheeler leaning into the current like a mule into a hill. Tam felt the boat's answer come up through the wheel: a shudder, then grip, then that slow, stubborn forward crawl that meant they were making way against water that did not want them here.

Jude Thornbury stood at the bow rail with her map case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, hair whipping in the cold wind off the ice, watching the water the way she watched everything—not with fear but with arithmetic. He'd known her for eleven years and she had never once looked at a river without calculating it. Depth against draft. Current against power. Distance against time. She was doing it now, he could see it in the set of her shoulders, and he hated himself a little for knowing exactly which sum she was working: how many hours until the fleet's lead barge caught them, or until the junction door closed forever, whichever came first.

He'd lied to her once. He'd told her the truth once. Both had cost him, and neither had settled the debt.

"Water's up four feet since yesterday," she said, coming aft along the rail with the easy balance of someone who'd spent half her life on moving things. She stopped beside him at the wheel but didn't touch it. She never touched his wheel. It was one of the small courtesies between them that he treasured and distrusted in equal measure. "That puts the narrows rapids at class four by afternoon. Maybe five."

"I've run five."

"You've run five in a boat you weren't being chased in." She looked at him, and there it was again—that flat gray assessment, the look of a woman reading a bridge for stress fractures. "Tam. If we can't outrun them, we have to out-think them. What do you know about their barge that I don't?"

*Everything,* he almost said. *I know the man who chartered it. I know the number he wrote on the paper. I know I took it.* Instead he said, "She draws too much for the upper channel above the narrows. They'll have to lighter their cargo at the landing below the falls if they want it past. That buys us half a day, maybe."

"And after that?"

"After that," Tam said, "we're all swimming in the same direction."

* * *

They made the first bend by midmorning, and that was where the river gave them their first gift and their first warning in the same breath.

The gift was the channel itself. The rising water had lifted the *Grace* over the gravel bar at Miller's Reach that would have grounded them at any other season—one of the cruel jokes of the gorge, that the flood that could kill you also carried you over the shallows. The warning was what came riding down on the flood: debris. Not just trees now. Planking. A shattered crate stamped with a shipping mark Tam recognized and wished he didn't. And then, snagged against an ice shelf on the outside of the bend, a ship's boat, swamped, oars shipped neatly inside it, riding high and empty.

"Pell," Tam said quietly. "Slow her."

They drifted close enough to see. The boat was fleet-issue—he knew the yard that built them, knew the paint scheme of the Harrow & Vane Salvage Combine, because he'd stood on the dock in Rivergate while their agents loaded dynamite under a charter signed by a name out of county records nobody was supposed to read anymore. The boat was empty. But lashed to the thwart was a canvas kit bag, and stenciled on the canvas, half worn away, was a man's name: **R. OKONKWO**.

"That's one of theirs," Jude said. "A crewman. I counted faces at the dock—Okonkwo was the one who asked me about tide tables. Polite fellow. Kept his own log."

"Then where is he?"

Jude's jaw tightened. "Either overboard, or gone over the side on purpose. And those aren't the same thing."

Tam signaled Pell to hold station while Jude hooked the kit bag aboard with a boathook. Inside: a oilskin-wrapped notebook, a tin of matches, a change of clothes wrapped in sacking—and nothing else. No rations. No tools. A man planning to leave a boat does not pack light unless he plans to be picked up.

Or unless he plans to swim for the other side.

It was Jude who spotted him. She said nothing at first—just went very still at the rail, head cocked, listening past the roar of the current the way she listened to rails. Then she raised one arm and pointed upstream along the eastern shelf, where the ice had calved back from the canyon wall and left a strip of scree and deadfall exposed at the waterline.

A man stood on it. Small with distance, dark against the pale rock, waving both arms over his head with the desperate rhythm of someone who knows that being seen is the only thing keeping him alive.

"He jumped," Jude said. "From the fleet. He jumped and he's been walking the shelf since before dawn." She turned to Tam, and her eyes had gone hard and bright as coupler steel. "Pick him up."

"Jude—" Tam started, because everything in him that had taken the fleet's money was screaming that this was a complication, a variable, a thread that once pulled would unravel the careful lie he was still living inside—

"We are not leaving a man on the ice," Jude said, in the voice she must have used on crews, on barons, on the whole rotten machinery of the railroads, "to drown for wanting off that boat. Come about."

And Tam, who had spent eleven years telling himself he was the kind of man who saved people, put the wheel over and went to get him.

* * *

Okonkwo came up the boarding ladder with his own strength, which told Tam something before the man said a word. His hands were cut raw from the scree, his boots soaked through, his lips blue with cold—but he climbed like a man who had decided, somewhere in the dark hours of the night, exactly what he was willing to pay for his decision. Pell got a blanket around him and coffee into him, and for a long moment the only sound was the engine's labor and the river's roar and the man's shaking breath.

Then Okonkwo looked up at Jude, and then at Tam, and said, "You're the engineer. The one the charter's really about." His voice was rough but level. "Good. Because I didn't jump ship to warm my hands. I jumped to talk."

"Talk, then," Jude said.

Okonkwo wrapped the blanket tighter. "I've been with Harrow & Vane six years. Wrecks, salvage, recovery work. I'm not a saint—I've helped clear cargo out of drowned boats and never asked whose it was. But this charter's wrong. Wrong in the bones." He pulled the oilskin notebook from his shirt, water-spotted but intact. "I keep the blasting inventory. Every stick of dynamite, every fuse, every charge placement has to be logged. That's regulation. So I logged it."

He opened the book on the deck between them, and Tam leaned in despite himself, and what he saw made the cold go deeper than the river ever could.

Charge placements. Dozens of them. But not along the wreck line, not at the salvage site, not anywhere a sane operation would put powder to free cargo. The placements ran along the canyon wall above the old tramway cut. Along the mouth of the hidden junction. Deep into the approaches.

"They're not salvaging," Okonkwo said. "There's nothing down there they want. There's something down there they want *buried*. The captain got his final orders two nights ago, sealed, from the charter holder. We're not cutting the junction open. We're collapsing it. Sealing it shut behind enough rock that no survey crew, no court, no government team finds what's inside for the next hundred years." He looked up, and his face was the face of a man who had done his own arithmetic and not liked the total. "The evidence comes out of that valley in a coffin of stone, or it doesn't come out at all. And anyone inside when the charges blow—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

"The survey team," Jude said quietly. "The ones who vanished."

"Some of them took money," Okonkwo said. "I heard the names passed around the wardroom like a dirty joke. But not all of them. And whoever's still down there—or whatever's still down there, records, papers, God knows—it dies with the mountain otherwise." He closed the notebook. "I logged the placements because logging is what I do. Now I'm giving the log to you, because it's the only thing I could think of that might matter more than my silence. The captain thinks I fell overboard during the night watch. Let him keep thinking it."

The river roared. The ice groaned somewhere upstream, a sound like a giant clearing its throat. Tam stood with his hand on the wheel and understood that the shape of the race had changed entirely.

Because he had thought—God help him, he had *told himself*—that this was a salvage job gone crooked, a race for cargo, a contest of speed between two boats on a brown river. Money chasing money. That was a world he understood. That was a world where a man like Tam could take a payment and tell himself it was just business, just a ferry fee, just a pilot doing pilot work.

But this wasn't cargo. This was a grave being dug in reverse—evidence of dead men and stolen payrolls and a town bought off for thirty years, and the men who owned the charter meant to entomb it with high explosives before the sixth tide, and if anyone stood in the way, well, accidents happened on rivers. Accidents happened to lone engineers and washed-up pilots and polite crewmen who fell overboard on night watch.

"Jude," Tam said, and his voice came out lower than he intended. "This changes the math."

"It changes everything," she said. She was already at the chart table, spreading Okonkwo's charge log beside her map of the closed lines, her pencil moving fast, crossing the fleet's planned blast positions with the tramway cut, the junction approaches, the derelict locks. "If they seal the upper approach, we come in from below. If they seal both—" Her pencil stopped. She looked up. "Tam. How much powder does that log say they're carrying?"

Okonkwo answered for him. "Enough to bring down half the canyon rim. Enough that they won't need precision. They'll just bury the whole valley mouth and let the glacier finish the job."

Jude nodded slowly, and Tam watched something happen in her face that he had seen only twice in eleven years: the moment when fear finished burning off and left only purpose underneath, hard and clean as forged steel. She wasn't running anymore. He realized, with a strange sinking clarity, that maybe she hadn't been running since the day she found that neat, trackless camp. She was done escaping. Whatever she did next, she was going to do *at* the thing, not away from it.

"Then we stop being ahead of them," she said, "and start being inside their plan. Okonkwo—you know their timetable. Their signal arrangements. Their fallback positions. You're going to give me all of it."

"All of it," Okonkwo agreed.

"And Tam." She turned to him, and for a moment the gray assessment softened into something older, something from a night eleven years ago when a river pilot had smuggled a terrified witness out of a town full of hired guns, and neither of them had ever quite named what that made them to each other. "How fast can the *Grace* run these waters with the flood behind us?"

Tam looked upstream, at the brown water climbing the ice walls, at the gorge that wanted to kill them, at the mountain downstream hiding its dead.

"Faster than is sensible," he said. "Which is the only speed worth having today."

He rang the engine bell twice—full ahead—and the *Maggie's Grace* leaned into the rising current, carrying a defector, a charge log, and a woman who had finally stopped running, straight toward the thing they were all afraid of.

Behind them, far downstream, faint even over the river's voice, came a sound that none of them needed to hear twice: the flat crack of a test charge, echoing off the canyon walls.

The fleet had started drilling.

They had less than a tide and a half.
