# 14. The Lockkeeper's Daughter

The house sat where the river bent away from the old locks like a man turning his back on a debt—low-roofed, timber-shingled, its porch posts wrapped in morning glory that had been climbing the same boards for forty years and had won. A garden went to seed in orderly rows. A rowboat lay overturned on trestles, hull patched so many times it looked like quiltwork. And above the door, nailed up with the same iron square-nails that had held the lock gates together, hung a lantern with green glass, unlit.

Jude stood at the gate a long time before she touched it.

She had come up from the river at second light, walking the towpath while Tam took the boat into the willows to wait. The path was grown over but not gone—the railroad had built this stretch before it built anything else, and things the railroad built first lasted longest. She could feel the grade of it under her boots even through the grass: the faint cant of a bed engineered to carry weight toward water. Her father's trade had been timber. Her mother's had been mending. But the ground itself had raised her, she thought, the way some children are raised by weather.

The derelict locks rose out of the gorge below the house like the ribs of something that had lain down to die and been too stubborn to finish. Three chambers, cut stone, their gates long rotted to black lace. Moss filled the mortar lines. In the lowest chamber the river still pooled, green and patient, and Jude could see the old capstan mounts, the iron collars worn silver by decades of rope. Her map had this place on it—a small annotation in her own hand, three years old: *locks dead, keeper gone?* She had never known until now what *gone* meant. It meant this woman. This garden. This lantern.

She opened the gate. It swung without sound, which told her it was oiled, which told her someone expected visitors, or feared them, or both.

The knock was answered before she finished it.

"You're the one who worked the derelict lock." The woman in the doorway was old the way driftwood is old—grayed, hardened, lighter than she looked. Small bones, big hands. Her eyes were the pale river-gray that Jude had only seen twice before, both times in people who had spent their lives reading water for a living. "Tuesday last. Don't deny it. My neighbor's boy fishes the bluff above the narrows and he says a boat came through the third chamber like a ghost remembering how to walk."

"It was open," Jude said. "Somebody greased the racks."

"I grease them." The woman stepped back, an invitation that was also an inspection. "Forty years I've greased them. Come in before the flies do."

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and linseed and, beneath both, of paper—old paper, the dry sweet dust of documents kept past all reason. Jude catalogued it without meaning to; she had spent enough years around county vaults and rail offices to know the smell of records that were being kept alive on purpose. There was one chair by the stove, one table, one bed behind a curtain, and then shelves. Shelves everywhere. Not books—ledgers, folios, oilcloth-wrapped bundles, each one labeled in a copperplate hand gone brown with age.

"Sit," the woman said. "I'm Aldith Crane. My father was Osric Crane, lockkeeper here thirty-one years. You'll have heard of him if you've heard anything, which you have, or you wouldn't be standing in my kitchen looking like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

Jude sat. "I don't know what I'm doing here yet."

"Yes, you do." Aldith put a kettle on the stove without hurrying. "You've come about the closure orders. Everyone comes about the closure orders eventually. The salvage men sent a fellow two springs back—smooth coat, city boots. I set the dog on him. Before that, a lawyer. Before that, a man from the county who thought I wouldn't recognize a bribe because he'd folded it inside a hymnal." She turned around. "You're different. You look like you'd rather burn the papers than sell them. That's the only kind of person my father said they could ever safely be shown to."

"Why?" Jude asked. "Why show them at all?"

Aldith studied her the way Tam read water—not the surface but the set of the current underneath. "Because of your face," she said finally. "I knew a girl once, years back, who used to come down the towpath with her father hauling timber contracts. Thornbury, the family was. The daughter had a habit of counting things under her breath—spikes, ties, steps. Counting when she was frightened. You're counting now. Your lips barely move, but they move."

Jude made herself stop. Made herself breathe. "That was a long time ago."

"Long enough that the baron's dead and the company's dissolved and the locks are moss." Aldith poured the tea—black, bitter, no ceremony. "And not long enough at all, because here you sit." She lowered herself into the other chair, and for the first time Jude saw how much the sitting cost her, the slow negotiation between will and hip and knee. "My father kept these locks working eleven years after the company abandoned them. Unpaid. He said a lock is a promise, and promises don't rot just because the men who made them do. Then the rockslide came, and the spur closed, and the company posted orders sealed with more wax than sense, and my father read them and went quiet for a week. At the end of the week he took the original documents—the true ones, signed before the copies were doctored—and he hid them. And he told me: *Aldith, someday a person will come up this path who counts when they're scared. Give them the box.*"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, the river moved against the lowest gate with a sound like slow applause.

"He never said why me," Jude said quietly. "Or why anyone."

"He didn't know who. He knew *what*." Aldith rose, joints announcing themselves, and went to the shelf nearest the stove—the one bundle that had no label. She brought it down and set it on the table between them, oilcloth tied with tarred twine. "He said the truth is heavy cargo. It doesn't need a fast line. It needs a line that holds. He waited thirty years for a line that held." She pushed the bundle across. "Open it."

Jude's hands were steady. She noticed that, and it surprised her; she had expected them to shake. Perhaps it was the engineer in her—paper was paper, orders were orders, and a document did not care whether it broke your heart. She cut the twine with her belt knife and unwrapped the oilcloth, and inside were three things.

The first was a ledger page, the company's own stock paper, watermarked with the wheel-and-wing. Closure order, dated nine days after the rockslide. *By direction of the Office of the General Manager, the Rivergate spur is declared unsafe beyond remediation. All traffic suspended in perpetuity. No survey to be conducted. No recovery operations authorized.* Signed at the bottom in a hand Jude knew better than her own heartbeat—knew it from warrants, from telegrams, from the night in Harrow Bend when she had stood in a courthouse gallery at nineteen years old and watched that same hand sign a deposition denying everything.

*Aurelian Voss.*

The baron. The name she had testified against. The man whose lawyers had hounded her across four counties until Tam had smuggled her out of Kelver's Ford in a coal barge with a false manifest, and after whom she had measured every town she ever left—how far, how fast, which lines were dead enough to hide in.

The second document was older. Dated six days *before* the rockslide. An internal directive, unsigned by Voss himself but initialed—*A.V.* in the corner—instructing the spur's paymaster to consolidate the quarterly payroll shipment into the junction vault rather than forward it downstream. *Hold pending revised routing.* The payroll had gone into the mountain. Two days later, the mountain had come down on it.

The third thing was not a document. It was a photograph, stiff and foxed, of a work crew posed on the spur roadbed—forty-odd men, squinting into sun—and Jude's eye went down the faces the way it went down a timetable, and stopped. Third from the left, holding a level staff, younger than she had ever known him, hat pushed back, grinning like a man who has just solved something.

Vess.

Her mentor. The man who had found her in Kelver's Ford with nothing but a stolen surveyor's transit and a price on her testimony, who had taught her grades and gauges and the sacred arithmetic of load against grade against curve—who had taught her, above all, *keep moving, girl, a moving target can't be served papers*. Vess, whose signature she had found days ago in the fifth man's logbook among the payoff names, and whose presence there she had been carrying like a stone in her boot, telling herself it could be explained, that signatures got forged, that old men signed things they didn't read.

Here he was. Young, grinning, holding a level staff on the crew that buried the payroll. Standing in a photograph taken—she turned it over—eleven days before the slide. Taken by the company. *Rivergate Spur, final grading, payroll detail.*

The room tilted very slightly, the way a bridge tilts when a load comes onto it unevenly, and Jude gripped the table edge until it steadied.

"Take your time," Aldith said. "Tea's there."

"He taught me everything." Jude heard her own voice as if from the next room. "Every rule I live by. Keep moving. Trust the data, not the men. Never sleep twice in the same town." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it, and hated the sound. "I thought those were survival rules. They're—they're *his* rules. He built them into me. I've spent twelve years running along rails he laid, and I thought I chose the direction."

"Did he know about the payroll?" Aldith asked, gentle and merciless at once. "In the photo, or in the logbook?"

"I don't know." Jude laid the photograph face-down, carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "That's the worst of it. I don't know if he was a conspirator or a coward or a man who signed one bad paper in a hard winter and spent the rest of his life—" She stopped. Thought of Vess's hands, scarred and sure, setting her fingers on a level bubble. *Feel that? That's true. Everything else is negotiable.* Had his hands shaken, ever, once, signing? "It doesn't matter for the evidence. It matters for me, and I'm not the evidence."

"No," Aldith agreed. "But you're the reason my father hid it. He said the truth would need someone who couldn't be bought and couldn't be scared. He said most people are one or the other, given time." She sipped her tea. "Which are you?"

"Both," Jude said. "Given time."

Aldith smiled with half her mouth. "Honest. Good. My father liked honest liars best—he said they at least knew what they were lying about." She leaned forward. "Now listen, because the tide won't wait and neither will the fleet. There's a fourth thing in that bundle. Under the false bottom of the folio. My father's addition, not the company's."

Jude lifted the folio. The bottom layer gave against her thumbnail—a panel, glued and aged to invisibility. Inside lay a single sheet in Osric Crane's copperplate, dense, dated across years, an addendum in the manner of a man correcting a survey:

*Closure executed per Voss directive, not per damage report. Damage report falsified by Asst. Engineer R. Meech (see signature comparison). Slide triggered by charge set at the cut above mile 9 — powder crew paid double, sworn silent, two later drowned 'accidentally' at the fish weirs. Payroll vault intact beneath junction collapse — Voss ordered it left, as recovering it would require survey, and survey would find the rest. Annual payments to Rivergate township administered via harbor office, harbormaster's discretion. Locks closed same season to prevent river access to junction. O.C. witnessed payment ledger 3rd year. God forgive us all.*

Jude read it twice. The second time was slower, because the first time her mind had snagged on one clause and refused to pass it: *Locks closed same season to prevent river access.*

The derelict locks. The route she had run Tuesday last, the secret door only she seemed able to work. Closed deliberately—by Voss's order—to seal the approach. Which meant every year since, the only way to the junction by water had been a dead line. And she had a map full of dead lines. She had spent twelve years collecting them, cataloguing them, sleeping in towns no one wanted, and the whole time—

"The fleet knows about the locks," she said aloud. "They chartered pilots. If they can't work the chambers, they'll blast the rim. That's why the dynamite."

"That's why the dynamite," Aldith said. "And that's why my father's note matters. It isn't just proof of the theft. It's proof of the *method*. Charges, drownings, bought silence, a whole valley paid annually to forget. Destroy the vault and you destroy the only record that the method happened. Understand? They aren't hiding money anymore. They're hiding the shape of the crime."

Jude folded the papers along their original creases, wrapped the oilcloth, tied the twine. Her hands were still steady. She understood now why they were steady—it wasn't calm. It was purpose finding its load path. Every force she had carried for twelve years, all that restless motion, all those towns slept in once and fled, had been traveling toward this table, this kitchen, this bundle. She had thought she was running away from Voss's enemies. She had been running *for* Voss's crime, all unknowing, a courier delivering herself to the truth one dead line at a time.

"Aldith." She looked up. "Your father said give the box to someone who counts when they're scared. Why did he think that kind of person would carry it?"

"Because counting is hoping," the old woman said simply. "A man who's given up stops counting. You count because you believe the numbers will come out right if you just keep at them." She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the door, and opened it onto the morning and the sound of the river working at the lowest gate. "Now. Copies. You'll make copies before that paper goes another mile—I have ink, I have a steady hand, and I have forty years of practice forging my father's handwriting for county forms, so don't look shocked. One copy goes downstream today with the fish buyer, addressed to nobody, left at the Harrow Bend depot strongbox under a name only I know. Insurance. The rest goes with you."

"And if I don't come back?" Jude asked. "If the fleet takes it off me at the narrows?"

Aldith looked at her with those pale water-reader's eyes, and something almost tender moved through them and was gone. "Then the copy surfaces, and the truth arrives late instead of on time. Trains run late all the time, Miss Thornbury. They still arrive." She stepped onto the porch and nodded down at the gorge, at the three dead chambers filling with the rising tide. "Third tide turns within the hour. Your pilot's smoking in the willows—I can smell the pipe from here, tell him the wind's wrong for it. Go get the rest of it. The vault, the fifth man, whatever's left of the government team. Finish the survey my father was never allowed to run."

Jude stood. Tucked the bundle inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel it with every breath—a cargo with weight and heart, the heaviest thing she'd ever carried and the first she'd ever carried *toward* something instead of away.

At the gate she paused. "The lantern over your door. Green glass. What does it mean?"

Aldith had turned back into her kitchen, gathering ink and paper, and she answered without turning around.

"It means the lock is ready," she said. "It's been ready forty years. We were only waiting for traffic."

Jude walked down the towpath with the tide's pull in her ears and the baron's signature warm against her ribs, and for the first time since Kelver's Ford, she was not counting.
