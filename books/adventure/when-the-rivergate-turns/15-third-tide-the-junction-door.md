## 15. Third Tide: The Junction Door

The tramway cut was older than the closure. Jude could read it in the stone the way she read everything—by its wear, by what friction had polished and what neglect had left rough. The rails that once carried ore carts up this shelf of canyon had been pulled long ago, but the sleepers remained, blackened with creosote, and the cable anchors were still set into the rock at intervals like the teeth of some enormous jaw. Someone had maintained them. Someone had oiled the pulleys recently enough that her fingers came away greasy.

She climbed with a lantern hooked to her belt and Tam's rope coiled over one shoulder, and the river spoke below her in the dark, third tide rising, filling the gorge throat by throat.

*You'll know the door when you find it,* the logbook's cipher had said, once she'd finally broken it sometime past midnight on the fourth day, sitting in the wrecked survey camp with her hands around a tin cup of cold coffee. *Not because it's hidden. Because it isn't. Because they wanted it found by anyone who could read grade.*

That phrase had kept her up the rest of the night. *Anyone who could read grade.* Not a password. A profession. The fifth man—the one whose handwriting filled the last third of the logbook, the one who'd signed himself only as an initial, **A**—hadn't been hiding from everyone. He'd been hiding from everyone except an engineer.

So here she was, climbing a dead tramway above a living river, following a line someone had graded a century ago and someone else had kept true for weeks.

The cut narrowed. The canyon wall leaned over her until the sky was a gray ribbon overhead, and then the tramway turned—not descending toward the water as she'd expected, but running *into* the mountain through a slot she would have walked past if she hadn't been counting anchor points. The rock face there wasn't rock face at all. It was masonry, fitted so carefully and stained so thoroughly that it had become the cliff, or the cliff had agreed to become it. And set into the masonry, at the height of a man's shoulder, was a plate. Iron. Riveted. With letters stamped deep enough to survive a hundred years of weather:

**RIVERGATE JUNCTION — INTERIOR LINE No. 3 — AUTHORIZED PERSONS ONLY**

Below that, smaller, almost apologetic:

*In event of closure, seal per Order 44-C.*

Order 44-C. She had that order now, folded in her breast pocket next to the map, courtesy of the lockkeeper's daughter—crisp where the logbook was soft, official where the logbook was desperate. Two documents from two different kinds of fear, pointing at the same door.

Jude stood in front of the plate for a long moment, listening. The river below. The ice groaning somewhere upstream, the glacier shedding its winter weight into the gorge. Her own breath. And then—faint, so faint she might have invented it—a sound from inside the mountain. Not machinery. Not water.

A cough.

Her heart did something complicated. She put her hand flat against the iron door and felt, through her palm, nothing. Cold metal, cold stone. But the cough had come from beyond it, and it had sounded like a man who had been breathing dust and darkness for a very long time.

"Hello," Jude said. Her voice fell flat in the slot of the canyon, swallowed immediately. She tried again, louder. "I'm looking for the interior line. I can read grade."

Silence. Long enough that she started composing her second approach, her third, cataloguing arguments the way she catalogued switch alignments. Then, from behind the door, a voice—dry, cracked, careful:

"Grade of the approach tramway."

"Four percent," Jude said without hesitating. "Rising east to west. Cable-assisted, counterweight pit on the lower landing. Whoever built it was cheap with timber and generous with iron, which means they expected the iron to outlive the argument."

Another silence. Then a sound she recognized with a jolt: the clack of a bolt, heavy, hand-thrown, the kind of lock you put on something you intended to keep closed for decades. The door moved inward six inches and stopped, held by a chain from the inside, and through the gap came air that smelled of lamp oil, cold stone, and a human being who had lived too long in his own company.

One eye appeared in the gap. Brown, bloodshot, ringed with skin gone pale as a mushroom's underside. It looked at her lantern first, then at her hands, then—last, longest—at her face.

"You're not government," the voice said.

"No."

"You're not salvage fleet."

"No."

"Then you're either crazy or you're her." The eye narrowed. "The one with the map. He said—if anyone ever came, it'd be someone with a map of closed lines. Said she'd know how to read a thing like this." He paused. "I've been betting on crazy. Crazy comes faster."

"I'm the one with the map," Jude said. "My name is Jude Thornbury."

The chain rattled. The door opened wide enough for a body, and the man behind it stepped back into his dark and waited, which was either trust or exhaustion or both.

She went in.

* * *

Inside, the junction was a cathedral built by accountants.

Her lantern found the space in pieces: a vaulted chamber of dressed stone, high enough that the light didn't reach the ceiling; three tunnel mouths opening off it like the arms of a Y, each one choked partway with rubble from the old rockslide; and along the near wall, the bones of the interior railway—two tracks, gauge narrower than mainline, converging on a turntable whose iron deck still gleamed faintly with old grease. Sidings. A signal bridge, its lamps long dead. Stenciled numbers on the tunnel walls fading like breath on glass.

And everywhere, evidence of the fifth man's tenancy. A camp stove, vented through a crack in the masonry with an ingenuity she noted professionally. Crates of the government team's supplies, stacked with the same terrible neatness as the camp above—this man had been the neat one, then; the tidy stacks had been his signature all along, a habit that had outlived his team. A cot. A table. And on the table, under a stone paperweight, papers. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

He watched her look at all of it. He was maybe fifty, gaunt in the way of a man who'd been rationing himself, with a beard he'd clearly stopped having opinions about weeks ago. His hands, though—his hands were steady, and they never strayed far from the table.

"Aldous Wren," he said. "Junior surveyor, Department of Interior Lines. Except there is no Department of Interior Lines anymore, hasn't been since before the closure, which my superiors apparently forgot when they sent us." He gestured at the tunnels with his chin. "Six of us came up the spur to survey for salvage rights. Officially. Unofficially, our charter was signed by a man named Corliss Hane, and on the fourth day we found the vault."

Jude went very still. "Hane. The salvage charter in the harbor is signed Hane."

"His son runs the fleet now. Same arithmetic, different handwriting." Aldous pulled out the stone weight and slid the top sheet toward her across the table, an offer and a test in one motion. "Go on. You came all this way. Read it."

She read it standing, because sitting down next to him felt like a commitment she hadn't earned yet. Payroll ledgers. Crew rosters for the spur's last operating year, with names struck through in red—struck through, she realized after a moment, not because they'd left but because they'd died, and the deaths were annotated in a clerk's cramped hand: *paid out to widow, marked as transfer.* Transfer. They'd buried men in the rockslide and entered them on the books as freight that changed address.

"The closure wasn't safety," Aldous said quietly. "It was accounting. The baron's line was bleeding money on this spur for years—bad rock, bad grades, accidents they paid to make quiet. Then they found the vein underneath, copper, rich enough to matter, and the company couldn't legally hold mineral rights on a rail corridor. So they staged the slide, sealed the junction, wrote the whole valley off as unstable, and filed a separate claim on the mineral estate through a shell company." He tapped the ledger. "Every year since, the town gets its annuity to keep asking no questions, and every year the shell company ships copper out through arrangements I still don't fully understand, and every year the books balance beautifully on the backs of eleven dead men."

"Eleven."

"Nine in the slide. Two more later, when the widows asked too loudly." His voice didn't waver, but his hands did, just slightly, and he pressed them flat on the table to stop it. "We found this on day four. Our team lead, Meric Voss—he took one look and started negotiating. Hane's people reached him within the week; I don't know how, I don't want to know how. Three of the others took the buyout. Money to walk away, money to report the team lost to the river, gear left neat so the searchers would have something to find." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "Neatness was my job. Inventory and documentation. So I made the neatest disappearance in the history of the department, and while they were all congratulating themselves on how clean it looked, I came down here through the maintenance gallery and locked the door."

"And they let you?"

"They think I'm in the river with the others. Or they don't think about me at all—I was the junior man, the one who sharpened pencils." He looked at her then, straight on, and there was something in his face that she recognized from her own mirror on certain mornings: the expression of a person who has stopped trusting the ground under other people's feet. "I've been down here five weeks, Ms. Thornbury. I've eaten half my rations and most of my pride. I copied every document twice and hid the copies in two places I'll tell you about never. And every day I've asked myself the same question, which I will now ask you, because you're the first person to reach this door in thirty years and the answer matters more than food."

"Ask it."

"Why?" His voice cracked on the word and he steadied it. "Why would anyone climb a dead tramway on a rising tide to find a door nobody knows exists? Nobody pays for that. Nobody sends a stranger for that. So either you're here for Hane—and if so, I've made five weeks of mistakes—or you're here for the truth, and I need to hear you say it, because I have spent five weeks learning that people will say anything."

Jude set her lantern on the table. In its light, the two of them faced each other across the ledgers of the dead, and she understood that whatever she said next would be weighed by a man who had nothing left to weigh it with but his own survival.

So she told the truth, and she told all of it, because half-truths were currency in a country she was done trading with.

She told him about the contract, and the bait in it. About the harbor and the fleet loading dynamite under Hane's son's signature. About the town bought silent, the harbormaster's comfortable shame, the annuity that ran through Rivergate like water through a flume. She told him about the lockkeeper's daughter and Order 44-C, signed by Baron Ashgrave himself—the same baron she'd testified against at nineteen, the same testimony that had put her on a night boat out of a burning town with Tam Voss poling for the dark side of the channel. She told him about Vess. Her mentor. The man who taught her that staying still was dying. His name was in the payoff ledger too—she'd found it in the ciphered pages, dated years after the closure, a retainer renewed annually like a subscription to silence. Every mile she'd ever run, purchased at wholesale by the men who needed her running.

When she finished, the chamber was very quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the glacier calved, and the whole mountain seemed to shrug.

"The tide," Aldous said at last. "How many are left?"

"This is the third. The route closes after the sixth."

"Then we have time to do this properly, which is more than I expected and less than I need." He began gathering the ledgers into a canvas satchel with quick, practiced motions—inventory man to the bone—but his eyes stayed on her. "Understand me, Thornbury. I don't trust you. Five weeks ago I trusted six people and four of them sold me. Trust is a currency I've stopped carrying. But I believe your story, because it has the shape of ruin, and ruin is the only shape I've seen lately that doesn't lie." He cinched the satchel. "So here's my proposal. We're allies the way two men on a sinking hull are allies—not from affection, from arithmetic. You get me and these records out. I get you the testimony that burns Hane, the baron's estate, the shell companies, and every signature in these books, including your mentor's."

"Including Vess's," Jude said. It wasn't a question.

"Especially his. A partial fire is how they won the first time." Aldous shouldered the satchel and looked at her, and beneath the pallor and the beard and the five weeks of dark, something stubborn and alive was burning in him, banked low but burning. "Can you get us out? The gallery I came in by floods on the fourth tide. After that there's only the main portal, and the main portal—" He stopped. He'd heard it too, or felt it, the way you feel a change in pressure before you hear the cause.

From far up the canyon, thin and wrong, came a sound neither of them had any trouble identifying.

Dynamite.

Not the blast itself—too distant for that—but the echo of it rolling down the gorge, and beneath the echo, a deeper sound, structural, the mountain clearing its throat. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling and lay fine and gray on the ledgers of the dead.

"That's early," Jude said. Her mind was already moving, laying the geometry of the canyon over the sound, calculating charge size from echo delay, and the answer it returned made her mouth go dry. "They're not waiting for the sixth tide. They're shooting the rim *now*—they're closing the upper approach before the water even reaches it." She grabbed her lantern. "Which means they know exactly where this door is, and they're sealing us in with the evidence."

Aldous had gone white. "The gallery—"

"Flooded or blocked, either way it's gone." She was already moving toward the tunnel mouths, playing her light into the darkness, reading the grade of each one the way she'd promised the door she could. "But this is a junction, Wren. Three lines. Railroads always build more doors than they admit to." Her light caught it—far down the eastern bore, half-buried in slide rubble, the rusted gleam of a handcar frame and, beside it, stenciled on the stone: *VENTILATION ADIT — KEEP CLEAR.*

"Keep clear," Jude repeated softly, and allowed herself one second—one full second—of the wild, electric feeling she got when a line that should be dead showed life in it.

Then the second blast came, closer, and the mountain began to close its hand.
