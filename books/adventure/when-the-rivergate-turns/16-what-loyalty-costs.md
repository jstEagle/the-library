## 16. What Loyalty Costs

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Tam finally said it out loud.

They were camped in the lee of a rock shelf above the river, the fourth tide still hours away, and Tam had spent the whole evening pretending to check his lines and his oar-locks and the pitch on the hull of the *Maggie Bright* while Jude sat across from him sharpening a surveyor's pencil with a knife, which was the kind of thing she did when she was thinking, and the kind of thing that made a man feel like he was being whittled too.

He'd been paid in Harrow Bend. That was where it started, though it had roots older than that—roots in Rivergate, in a debt he'd carried since the year the spur closed, when his father's boat had been one of the last to run cargo up the gorge before the ice took the route for good. But the money itself, the clean folded money with the fleet's wax seal on the band, had been pressed into his palm in a back room off the Harrow Bend freight yard by a man with a ledger voice who never gave a name.

"Three hundred now," the man had said. "Seven hundred when you deliver her to the gorge at first tide. She'll come if you ask her right. You're the only one she'd follow upriver."

And Tam had taken it. Not because three hundred was a fortune—it was, but that wasn't the reason. He'd taken it because the man had said *she'll come if you ask her right*, and something in Tam had wanted to test whether that was true, whether Jude Thornbury, who trusted no road twice and no town once, would really walk into a boat because an old friend held the tiller.

She had. That was the worst part. She'd looked at him across the dock at Rivergate with all her suspicion banked down like a stove turned low, and she'd stepped aboard anyway, because eight years ago he'd run her out of Corvane ahead of the baron's men with the law two streets behind them, and some debts you don't get to stop paying just because someone offers you a purse to stop.

"Say it," Jude said now, without looking up from the pencil. The knife whispered along the wood. "You've been circling it since the narrows. Say it or I'm going to start guessing, and my guesses are worse than your truth."

Tam fed a stick to the fire. Watched it catch.

"I was paid to bring you here."

The knife stopped. Didn't slip. Just stopped, the way a clock stops—not broken, but deciding.

"By the fleet," Jude said. Not a question.

"By whoever chartered the fleet. Same purse, I think. A man in Harrow Bend, no name, ledger voice. Three hundred down, seven hundred on delivery." He made himself look at her. "First tide. I was supposed to have you at the gorge mouth when the ice opened, where their lead barge could keep you in sight the whole run."

"And instead?"

"Instead I ran us ahead of them. Instead I've been losing them in the fog and shooting rapids they won't shoot and burning my own margin to stay a day in front." He heard his own voice climb and pulled it down flat. "I meant to double-cross them from the start, Jude. I want that understood. I took their money so they'd believe I was theirs, and every stroke since has been mine, not theirs."

The knife resumed. Whisper, whisper, against the wood.

"You know what the difference is between a double-cross and a betrayal?" Jude said quietly. "Timing."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Fairness hasn't been on this river in twenty years." She set the knife down at last and looked at him, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of the water in the deep channels—dark, and moving over things you couldn't see. "When did you mean to tell me?"

"When it mattered."

"It matters now."

"It mattered at the narrows. It mattered at first tide." He spread his hands. "But if I'd told you at the dock, you'd have gone alone. You'd have gone without me, without the *Maggie*, without anyone who knows the gorge in flood—and you'd be dead in the White Stair by now, or locked in a cabin on the lead barge while they ride your map to the junction. I told myself the lie was the price of keeping you alive long enough to hear the truth."

"And how much did you pay yourself for that lie? What was your cut, Tam? Above the eight hundred?"

"There's no cut."

"There's always a cut." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Jude angry; she went quieter, more precise, like water finding the crack. "Everyone on this river's been paid something. The harbormaster gets his envelope every spring. The town gets its school roof and its silence. Vess got—" She stopped herself. He watched her stop herself, jaw working, and file Vess away in whatever locked drawer she kept the people she couldn't afford to grieve yet. "What did you get?"

He could have lied. God knew he'd rehearsed enough versions on the water—the noble version, the tragic version, the version where he'd only ever been a pawn. But lying to Jude Thornbury was like laying track over a hollow: it would hold until the moment it catastrophically wouldn't, and then it would take everything down with it.

"The route," he said. "That was the other half of the payment. They offered to reopen the gorge charter. Legal runs, year-round, my father's old route under my own name. No more smuggling pilots, no more watching barges with the wrong flags take the deep channel while I poach the shallows." His throat tightened around it. "Twenty years I've wanted that river back. They knew it. That's why they picked me. Not because I'm greedy. Because I'm hungry in a specific place."

Jude was silent a long time. The river spoke below them, the low constant argument of water against stone, and somewhere upstream the glacier groaned—a sound like a door being forced in a house where nobody should be home.

"So they bought you with the thing you love most," she said finally. "Same as they bought the town. Same as they bought Vess." She laughed, short and bitter, no humor in it at all. "I keep looking for the man behind this who invented something new. There's nothing new. It's the same purchase, made over and over, in different hands. They don't buy evil. They buy loyalty. And loyalty is cheaper, because people give it away believing it's priceless."

"I never delivered you," Tam said. "Whatever else is true—that's true. I ran us ahead of them every mile."

"You ran us ahead of them because running ahead is what I asked for. If I'd asked you to lag, would you have lagged?" She held up a hand before he could answer. "Don't answer. I don't want to know tonight. Maybe I don't ever want to know. That's the trouble with a confession, Tam—you can't unhear it, and after you hear it, every kindness a man ever did you has to be re-examined for the hand that paid for it."

"Then re-examine this one." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight on his face. "Tomorrow is the fourth tide. The fleet will make the junction by fifth tide at the latest—they've got blasting powder enough to close that tunnel forever, and the records inside it with the bodies if that's what it takes. Everything we've bled for—the logbook, the lockkeeper's orders, the fifth man if he's alive—all of it ends under a hundred tons of canyon unless we're there first, or unless someone downstream already has copies moving." He paused. "Do they? Copies moving?"

Jude said nothing.

"That's what I thought," Tam said softly. "Nothing's sent downstream. Nothing's safe. It's all still riding on us—on this boat, on this fire, on the two of us and whatever's left of the truth between us." He shook his head slowly. "So here's my confession finished, and here's my question with it. When it comes to it—if it comes to it, tomorrow or fifth tide, on the water with their guns out—what do you protect? Me, or the evidence?"

The question hung in the smoke.

Because that was the knife-point of it, and he'd known it would be, and he'd hated himself for asking even as he knew he had to. A pilot learns to read water the way a priest reads souls, and Tam had read the current coming for days now: sooner or later Jude Thornbury would stand at a fork where saving him and saving the records were two channels, and only one boat, and she would have to choose. He'd wanted to hear her answer while it was still hypothetical, while mercy was still cheap.

Jude picked up the pencil. Looked at it. Turned it in her fingers—the little stub of cedar and graphite she'd been sharpening all evening, worn down to almost nothing.

"Do you know why I keep moving?" she said. "Never sleeping in the same town twice. Never running the same line twice. Everyone thinks it's fear. Being found. And it was, at the start—after Corvane, after the testimony, after the baron put a price on my name, yes, it was fear." She turned the pencil over. "But Vess taught me the habit, years before any of that. He told me: *a mapmaker who stays still starts drawing the world as she wishes it were.* Motion keeps you honest. Every new town is a correction against the last one." Her mouth twisted. "And now I learn the man who taught me to run was paid to bury the very thing I've been running toward. So maybe motion was never honesty. Maybe it was just the leash they liked best, because a woman who never stops never digs."

"Jude—"

"I'm answering your question." She looked up. "You asked what I protect. Here's my answer: I don't know. That's the honest answer, and you're owed honesty now more than you're owed comfort. If it comes to a choice on the water, I don't know what I'll do, and I won't promise you either way, because a promise made tonight would be a lie dressed as loyalty, and I am done carrying those." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and looked down at him with the fire behind her. "Eight years ago you saved my life and asked nothing. Last week you sold my location and called it a plan. Both of those are true, and I can't weigh them against each other, and I won't pretend I can. What I can tell you is this: the evidence goes through. Whatever happens to me, whatever happens to you, whatever it costs—the records reach daylight. That's the only promise I have left in me. Make of it what you will."

Tam sat with it. Let it settle into him like cold water into boot leather.

It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't condemnation. It was worse than both and better than both—it was the truth, unsoftened, from a woman who had decided he was worth the truth even now, which was perhaps the only currency left between them that hadn't been counterfeited by somebody's purse.

"All right," he said at last. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "No promises either way. But hear mine, such as it is: tomorrow, when the fourth tide opens, I take the *Maggie* up the channel my father used—the one that isn't on any chart, the one I never showed another living soul. If the fleet wants the junction, they come through the Narrows, and the Narrows cost them an hour they don't have. We'll be at the door before they're through the throat." He rose too, and stuck out his hand—an old riverman's gesture, palm open, nothing in it. "Not a promise of loyalty. A promise of position. Where I'll be, and when. You can decide what that's worth."

Jude looked at the hand a long moment. Then she took it—one grip, hard, brief, a handshake like a knot tied under load.

"Fourth tide," she said. "Before the fleet clears the Narrows."

"Before they clear the Narrows."

She released his hand and turned toward the boat to check the lines one more time, because Jude checked things when she couldn't trust anything else, and Tam stood alone at the fire feeling lighter and heavier at once, the way a man feels after cutting loose a load he'd forgotten he was carrying—free, and suddenly aware of how much of his balance had come from the weight.

He was kicking dirt over the coals when Jude spoke again from the darkness by the waterline. Her voice was quiet, and level, and it reached him with the clarity of a bell across still water.

"Tide's turning early tonight. Feel that? The air's coming down-canyon wrong." A pause. "And Tam—there's a light on the far bank. Third one tonight. Same place each time. Somebody's signaling somebody, and we're camped between the sender and the receiver."

Tam went cold from the boots up. He turned, scanning the black wall of the far shore—and there it was, small and steady as a star that had made a mistake, a lantern-glow winking once, twice, holding, in a notch of the canyon where no camp should be and no trail led.

Below them, deep in the dark, the river began its slow, patient rise toward the fourth tide—and somewhere between here and the junction, between the signal light and the morning, the fleet was already closer than either of them had counted on.
