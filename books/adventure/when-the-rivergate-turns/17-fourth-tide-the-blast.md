## 17. Fourth Tide: The Blast

The sound arrived before the mountain understood it.

Jude was halfway up the tramway cut, one hand on the rusted cable guide, when the canyon rim above the junction door coughed out a column of dust and shattered granite. The boom came a half-second later—a flat, ugly concussion that rolled down the gorge and slapped the river flat. Birds went up from the pines in a black ragged sheet. Somewhere below, Tam shouted her name, but the word was swallowed by the echo still ricocheting between the walls.

She didn't scream. That was the strange part, the part she would remember afterward in the courthouse and in the years of staying still that followed. She simply stood on the cut with her hand burning against cold iron and watched the dust plume rise like something exhaled, and inside her chest a voice she'd carried since Harrow Bend said, with terrible calm: *They went early.*

The fleet wasn't supposed to fire until the fifth tide. She'd built her whole arithmetic on that—their caution, their need to stage charges properly, the captain's visible reluctance to bring dynamite near open water without a survey. She had counted on greed being patient. She had forgotten that greed, cornered, stops doing math.

Because they knew now. The narrows had told them everything. She'd taken the derelict lock past them like a thief walking through a wall, and Captain Brannock's face across the water had not been angry so much as *informed*. He knew where she was going. He knew what was inside. And he knew that every hour he waited was an hour in which a railroad engineer with a map of dead lines might find a way to make his cargo worthless.

So he'd fired at the rim instead of the door. Not to seal the junction—she could see that even from here—but to bring half the cliffside down across the approach, burying the tramway, the switchback trail, the low gallery where the fifth man had lived in the dark for weeks with a coded logbook and a payroll vault's worth of truth. Seal it with stone, not with charge. Let the mountain do the work the dynamite couldn't reach.

"Jude!" Tam was climbing toward her, rope over his shoulder, river spray frozen in his beard. His face was gray under the weathering. "Jude, the whole face came off—there's no approach left, there's nothing—"

"There's a man in there."

"I know. I know there is." He caught her arm. "And there are four barges coming around the bend behind us with enough powder to finish the job, and the tide turns in—" he looked at the water, reading it the way she read rail grades, "—three hours. Maybe less with the melt coming down."

She pulled free of him. Not roughly. But free.

"Then I have three hours," she said, "to go in through the only door they don't know about."

* * *

There was always another door. That was the whole religion of her life, the catechism Vess had taught her on night freight runs when she was nineteen and running from a baron's lawyers: *Every line has a service entrance, girl. The men who build things always leave themselves a way back in.* She'd spent eleven years proving him right, mapping closed spurs and abandoned grades, and she had never once hated how well he'd taught her until this moment, standing before a wall of collapsed rock and remembering that Vess's signature sat in a payoff ledger beside the men who'd staged the rockslide that started all of it.

The ventilation adit was forty feet above the junction door, a black slot in the canyon wall no wider than a coffin, marked on none of the county surveys because it hadn't been county work. It was railroad work—1890s, the first construction era, when the crews who bored the original tunnel had needed air and hadn't trusted anyone downstream to provide it. She'd found it two days ago by reading the talus slope the way other people read handwriting: the scree below that slot was sorted wrong, too fine, too regularly slumped. Water had been dripping out of it for decades. Where water drips, a passage breathes.

Now she stood beneath it with Tam's rope around her waist and the meltwater rising behind them, and the slot looked exactly as wide as a coffin, because that was what it was. A coffin-shaped door into a mountain that had already eaten one crew whole, officially killed no one, unofficially buried everything.

"You don't have to do this," Tam said. "We can come back at fifth tide with the lockkeeper's people, with—"

"With what? Shovels?" She tested the anchor point, a stub of the old hoist beam, and found it honest. "Brannock fires again at fifth tide and there won't be a man or a paper left to save. This is the only tide we get." She looked at him then, really looked, and saw the thing he'd been carrying since the narrows—the confession still fresh, the money he'd taken and meant to betray, the friendship and the leash worn down to the same rope. "You hold the line here. If I'm not out when the water starts rising, you get downstream and you tell the lockkeeper's daughter everything. Don't come in after me. Promise me."

"Jude—"

"*Promise me,* Tam. One of us has to be able to testify."

He promised. She heard him mean it. Then she put her shoulders into the slot and began to climb into the dark.

* * *

The adit was worse than she'd calculated, which was saying something, because she had calculated it honestly.

Forty feet of crawl, the ceiling pressing her shoulder blades, the rock sweating cold through her jacket, her carbide lamp throwing a circle of light no bigger than a wagon wheel. She moved by engineer's habit—one elbow forward, breathe, drag, breathe—counting her progress in feet the way she counted miles, because numbers were the only thing keeping the animal terror in its cage. At twenty feet the passage narrowed and she had to exhale fully, turn her head, let the rock touch her cheek like a hand. At thirty-one feet she stopped entirely, lamp guttering, and listened to the mountain.

It groaned. Not dramatically—no movie-of-the-mind groan, nothing theatrical. Just a deep structural complaint somewhere above and ahead, the sound of tons of fractured granite settling onto new supports after the blast, finding its new shape, deciding whether it liked it. Dust sifted down through her lamplight in slow golden threads.

*It's settling,* she told herself. *Settling means it's done moving. A bridge that creaks stands; a bridge that's silent falls.* Vess had said that too. She was beginning to understand that nearly everything she knew had come from a man who helped bury nine people under a false rockslide, and that she would have to carry that knowledge into the dark with her along with everything else.

At forty feet the adit opened, dropped, and became stairs—railroad stairs, cut blocks, descending into the old construction gallery. And there the smell hit her: wet stone, lamp oil, woodsmoke gone stale, and underneath it the faint sweet rot of apples. Someone had been living here. Living here *well*, in the way a careful man lives in hiding—nothing wasted, nothing loud.

She followed the smoke smell down two flights and around a blasted corner where the ceiling dipped to five feet, and the lamplight found him.

He was wedged under a fallen slab the size of a dining table, pinned from the waist down, and he had heard her coming—of course he had, he'd had weeks to learn every sound this mountain made—and he was pointing a revolver at her with a hand so steady it broke her heart.

"Don't," he said. "Whatever you are, whoever sent you—don't come closer. I've got powder stored back there and I will drop this roof on both of us."

"I'm Jude Thornbury," she said, and kept her hands wide and empty and her voice level, the voice she used on frightened brakemen. "Railroad engineer. I surveyed your camp on the spur. Your boots were by the fire. Your instruments were zeroed. There were no tracks leaving." She took one step. "I'm the one who figured out you went down instead of away."

The revolver didn't waver. But his eyes did—something moved behind them, a recalculation, a man re-reading a document he thought he'd memorized.

"The boots," he said slowly. "Nobody ever asked about the boots."

"Engineers ask about boots. Boots tell you whether a man walked out or was carried." Another step. "What's your name?"

A long silence. The mountain settled again overhead, and both of them flinched, and somehow that flinch did what words couldn't—it made them the same kind of creature in the same kind of trouble.

"Hollis," he said. "Emmett Hollis. Fifth man." The revolver lowered an inch. "And unless you brought a crane down that rabbit hole, Miss Thornbury, you should know the slab isn't the problem. The problem is the charge Brannock's crew set at the far end of the gallery last night, before the main blast. They came in through the junction door while I was sleeping. Two of them. They wired the vault chamber and left. The blast brought the rim down on top of the approach, and the second charge is sitting thirty yards behind me with a fuse head that got cracked in the shock." His voice went very quiet. "When the river comes up with the turn of the tide, the seepage reaches it. I've been listening to the water rise for six hours. It's maybe eight feet below the wire now."

Jude felt the fear arrive—not as panic but as data, cold and complete, filling her the way floodwater fills a gauge. Terror transmuted, in the space of one heartbeat, into purpose. Because this—*this*—was the thing she was actually for. Not maps, not running, not the elegant art of disappearing. This: a broken system, a rising deadline, and one person who could read the machinery.

"Show me," she said. "Everything. The slab, the charge, the seepage path, the vault door. I need all of it, and I need it fast, because I'm getting you out and I'm getting those records out, and then I'm going to rig that tunnel to survive whatever Brannock does next."

Hollis stared at her. In the lamplight his face was hollowed and bearded and sane in the way of men who have made peace with dying and just been handed a reason to un-make it.

"You can't do all that," he said.

"No," Jude agreed, already pulling the coil of rope and the kit of tools off her shoulder, already turning toward the black throat of the gallery where the water was whispering upward in the dark. "But I've got three hours, a dead line nobody else wanted, and a map of every way into this mountain that the men who built it tried to forget."

She knelt at the edge of the rising water and held her lamp low, and far down the flooded passage, glinting like a coin at the bottom of a well, she could see it: the pale seam of the vault door, and taped beside it, glistening wet, the cracked fuse head of a charge that would bury the truth forever.

The tide had begun to turn.
