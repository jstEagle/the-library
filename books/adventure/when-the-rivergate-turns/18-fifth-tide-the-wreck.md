## 18. Fifth Tide: The Wreck

The water took the tunnel in stages, the way it took everything in Rivergate—slowly, then all at once.

Jude stood at the lip of the collapsed entrance with the fifth man's lamp burning low in her hand and the tide's arithmetic running in her head like a train schedule she couldn't afford to miss. Four hours of slack water, maybe less. The meltwater from the high country had already found the junction's old drainage channels, and she could hear it moving in the dark below her—a long, patient breathing, the sound of a place deciding whether to stay a tomb or become a grave.

"Brayden," she called down into the shaft. Her voice came back off wet stone, flattened, doubled. "Brayden Corliss. I know you're down there. I know you're alive, because dead men don't leave logbooks with the last page still wet."

Nothing. Then, from somewhere below, a sound she'd have known anywhere, in any language, in any century: the scrape of a boot sole against iron. A man deciding to climb toward a voice instead of away from it.

"I'm coming down," she said. "Don't shoot. I only brought the lamp."

* * *

The shaft was worse than she'd remembered from the tramway cut—narrower, the rungs furred with a century of mineral bloom, the walls weeping cold water that found every gap in her coat and made a home of it. She counted rungs the way she counted everything now. Two hundred and forty to the first landing. The water was at rung sixty and rising at a hand's width every ten minutes, which meant she had perhaps three hours before it swallowed the landing, and the landing was the only place wide enough to work.

Brayden Corliss was waiting for her at the bottom, in a pocket of dry air that smelled of lamp oil and old iron and the particular staleness of a man who had lived alone in the dark for six weeks. He was thinner than his logbook handwriting had suggested—tall, hollowed, with a beard that had gone past unkempt into something geological. But his eyes were steady, and when he raised his own lamp to look at her, his hand didn't shake.

"You're the surveyor," he said. "The one the fleet is paying to legitimize their claim."

"I was." She set her lamp on the landing's iron lip and shrugged off her pack. "Now I'm the one who's going to get you out."

"Out." He said the word the way a man says the name of a country he'd stopped believing existed. "The entrance is down. The fleet put a charge into the rim."

"Half down. The rest is a chimney of loose rock, and loose rock is the one material I've never met that doesn't have a second use." She was already moving, running her hands along the landing's edge, reading the junction's bones by touch the way Vess had taught her to read a bridge by the sound of its rivets. "This whole junction was built as a working space. Air shafts, drainage, a maintenance gallery running parallel to the main bore. The railroad didn't seal a tomb, Corliss. It sealed a machine. And machines have doors."

"There are no doors. I've spent six weeks—"

"You've spent six weeks surviving, which is a full-time occupation and leaves no time for surveying." She found what she was looking for—a riveted plate set into the wall, its bolts sheared, its face stamped with the faded ghost of the old line's crest. Behind it, when she put her shoulder to it and drove with her legs, it groaned and gave six inches. "Maintenance gallery. Runs uphill toward the old air shaft on the canyon wall. The fleet's charge came down through the main bore. This gallery wasn't in their drawings, because it isn't in anyone's drawings. It's only on mine."

He stared at her. "Your map."

"Every dead line I ever found, I found because someone tried to make it disappear. Disappearing things leave seams." She drove her shoulder into the plate again, and this time it swung inward on a hinge that had been oiled, deliberately, recently—Corliss's work, she realized, or his attempt at it, the man preparing his own escape route and running out of time before he could finish. "You've been through here."

"I got as far as the gallery's second junction. There's a collapse past it. And the water—" He stopped. His composure, which had held through her arrival, held through her inspection, bent now around one word. "The records are in the vault. The main vault, down in the flooded bore. I couldn't carry them. There are four crates of them, and the water was coming, and I had to choose."

"Then we're going to get them." She said it simply, the way she'd say *the bridge will hold* or *the tide turns at six*. "And then we're going to make sure the fleet's second charge lands in a tunnel full of nothing worth burying."

* * *

The flooded bore was exactly as bad as she'd promised herself it would be.

She went down alone—Corliss's weeks in the dark had left him strong but slow, and the dive through the submerged section of the main line was a young swimmer's errand, not a survivor's. The water was snowmelt cold, the kind of cold that stopped thought after thirty seconds and stopped breath after ninety, and she gave herself a strict budget: down through the bore's drowned throat, into the vault antechamber where the crates waited, one trip, two crates, no heroics.

The vault room was a cathedral of bad decisions. She saw it by the light of the waterproof lamp strapped to her chest: the great steel door hanging open, the shelving the railroad had installed and then abandoned, and the crates—four of them, just as Corliss had said, lashed to a maintenance dolly that some long-dead section crew had left parked against the wall. The payroll ledgers. The correspondence. The death tallies from the rockslide that officially killed no one. Forty years of arithmetic, sitting in water that rose past her knees.

The first two crates came up on a line she rigged from the landing, hand over hand through the black water while Corliss hauled from above, his breath ragged, his grip certain. The second trip she made with the water at her waist in the antechamber, and by the third trip it was at her chest, and the cold had gotten into the place where her decisions lived, and she caught herself thinking, with a strange floating clarity: *this is what Vess felt like, maybe. This is what it costs, going back for the last of it.*

She came up the line on the third haul with the final crate strapped to her back and her lungs full of knives, and Brayden Corliss pulled her over the landing's edge and held her there, gasping, while the water below rose to claim the room they'd emptied.

"That's everything," he said. "That's all of it. Four crates. I counted them every day for six weeks. Four crates."

"Then we're done with the vault." She got her feet under her. Her hands were past feeling, so she worked the next part by memory and by the stubborn mechanical intelligence that had never once left her, even in the years when she'd wished it would. "Now we're done with the tunnel."

* * *

Rigging a tunnel to survive a blast was, in the end, a conversation with the rock.

She'd learned it from Vess, in the years before she'd learned what Vess was: that demolition was honest work, that a charge didn't destroy a structure so much as ask it a question, and the structure's answer depended entirely on how the question was asked. A charge against a solid wall brought the wall down. A charge against a wall already full of seams, already cracked, already carrying its own private weather of fracture—well, the rock answered along the lines you'd drawn for it.

The fleet's second charge, she reasoned, would come down the main bore again, or be set against the chimney of rubble at the entrance. Either way, the blast wave would travel the path of least resistance. Her job was to make sure the path of least resistance led nowhere that mattered.

She worked the landing and the gallery with Corliss hauling and holding lamps, and she built her answer out of the junction's own body. She dropped the old maintenance dolly down the shaft to form a plug of iron at the bore's throat, then packed the space behind it with the loose rock she and Corliss pried from the gallery's collapsed section—a countercharge of rubble, absorbing the wave the way sandbag revetments absorbed shellfire. She cut the gallery's timber shoring loose and wedged it into the side adits at angles that would deflect the pressure pulse upward into dead ends, spending the blast's fury on empty stone. And at the gallery mouth—the route she and Corliss would take out—she built a baffle: a double wall of dolly iron and rock with a dogleg between, the geometry of survival, the same principle that kept a powder magazine's entry corridor from becoming a cannon barrel.

"It's not elegant," she said, stepping back from the last of it, her hands bleeding from the rock work, her whole body a ledger of small debts she'd pay in the morning. "But the fleet's engineers will set their charge against the entrance, because that's where the drawings say the tunnel is. And the drawings are wrong. The drawings have always been wrong. That's the whole reason any of us are here."

Corliss looked at the baffle, at the plugged bore, at the deflected adits, and something moved through his hollowed face that she recognized—she'd seen it in mirrors, in the years after Vess, the look of a person encountering competence after too long among people who only wanted things from him.

"You've done this before," he said. "Not the rigging. The running. The—preparing to be hunted."

"I've done a lot of things before." She checked the water line on the shaft wall. Two hours of slack left, and the gallery climb would take one of them. "That's the last time, though. After tonight I'm done being the person who knows how to disappear."

* * *

They came up out of the canyon through the old air shaft as the tide turned.

The chimney of loose rock at the entrance had, as she'd predicted, half-sealed the main route—but the air shaft, a vertical throat of timber and iron no wider than a chimney, let them out onto the canyon wall two hundred yards upstream, behind a screen of alder, with the river below running high and brown and the fleet's lead barge visible in the narrows, its deck lamps burning, its cutting gear stacked and ready.

Jude lay in the alder with Corliss breathing beside her, and she watched the barge, and she did the arithmetic one last time.

The fleet would run the narrows on the next tide—the sixth and last before the ice sealed the gorge for the season. They would come up to the junction, set their charge against an entrance that now protected a tunnel full of nothing, and detonate it, and the rock would answer along the lines she'd drawn, and the evidence would already be gone. Four crates, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped to a handcar she and Corliss would run down the derelict tramway in the dark, to the derelict lock, to Tam Voss's boat, and from there—

From there, copies. The lockkeeper's daughter was already moving the first set downstream, and the crates held the originals, and the originals were going to a courthouse, and she was going to stand in that courthouse and say names out loud.

"They'll know," Corliss said quietly. "By morning they'll know the tunnel was empty. They'll know someone beat them."

"They've known since the narrows." She got to her feet, and the alder let her go reluctantly, branches dragging at her coat like hands. "Let them come. Let them blow their hole. Let them stand around in the smoke and realize they spent a fortune in dynamite burying a room full of water." She shouldered her pack, and the weight of the first crate settled against her spine, forty years of dead men's arithmetic riding between her shoulder blades. "We beat them by one tide, Corliss. That's all we needed. One tide, and a tunnel that lies."

Below them, in the narrows, a lamp moved on the barge's deck—a man walking a post, a watch being kept, a hunt already turning toward the empty dark where its quarry had been.

Jude turned uphill, toward the tramway, toward the locks, toward the last night of the life she'd been living since Harrow Bend.

"Move," she said. "We've got four hours of dark and forty years of evidence, and only one of those things keeps."
