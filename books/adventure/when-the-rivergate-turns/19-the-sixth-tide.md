## 19. The Sixth Tide

The ice spoke first.

Tam Varrow had spent thirty years listening to rivers, and he knew their languages the way a priest knows liturgy—the mutter of shallows, the groan of pressure ridges, the particular wet whisper of water finding a new way around an old obstacle. But the glacier above the gorge had a voice all its own, and on the morning of the sixth tide it was talking in its sleep. Long, tectonic syllables. The sound of something vast deciding whether to let go.

He stood at the wheel of the *Maren's Grace* and watched the gorge open.

It happened the way it always happened, according to the lockkeeper's daughter and every pilot who'd run this stretch before the closure: the river backing up behind the ice dam would rise through the slack of the tide, and for six hours—no more—the channel through the calving field would float clear enough for a hull with a shallow draft and a fool at the helm. Six tides a year. The railroad men who built the spur had timed their whole operation to that arithmetic, hauling payroll steel and company secrets upriver in those narrow windows, and now Tam was doing the same math they'd done, except his cargo was a woman with a canvas satchel full of other people's sins and a face like a closed ledger.

Jude Thornbury stood beside him at the wheel, saying nothing. She'd been saying nothing since dawn, since the fifth tide brought them down from the junction with the fifth man wrapped in oilskins and shivering so hard his teeth clicked like a telegraph key, since the records came aboard in their waterproof tubes and Tam had lashed them under the pilothouse bench with the same care he'd have used on dynamite. Which, in a sense, he had.

"You're going to want to take us wide of the eastern shelf," she said finally. Her voice was rough. She'd been underwater twice in eighteen hours, and it lived in her throat now. "When the fleet forms up."

"They won't form up," Tam said. "They'll be waiting at the narrows again."

"They were waiting at the narrows yesterday." Jude's eyes stayed on the ice. "Yesterday they still thought they could stop us before we reached the wreck. Today they know we've already reached it. Different problem. They'll come out to meet us because they can't afford to let us reach Rivergate first—but they can't blast us in the channel without sinking the records along with us, and Brannock is many things, but he didn't get that charter by being stupid about salvage law. Wrecked cargo stays crown property. Recovered cargo belongs to whoever recovers it." A pause. "He needs us intact. Or he needs the records destroyed before anyone official sees them, which means he needs to board us somewhere quiet."

"Then we don't go somewhere quiet."

"That," said Jude, "is the plan."

Behind them, in the shelter of the deckhouse, the fifth man—Aldous Wren, though he'd answered to nothing but a number for six weeks underground—slept like a man catching up on a decade. His hands, even in sleep, were curled around the empty space where the logbook had been, as if some part of him still couldn't believe he'd handed it over. Tam understood that. There were things you carried so long they grew into the skeleton, and letting go of them was its own kind of wound.

The *Maren's Grace* slid into the current, and the sixth tide began.

* * *

The fleet was exactly where Jude said it would be.

They came around the bend below the narrows and there it was: four barges and the lead steamer *Iron Meridian*, riding at anchor in the widened pool where the river caught its breath, smoke standing straight up from her stack in the windless cold. Cutting gear gleamed on her decks. Her rail-mounted crane was slewed toward the channel like a gun that hadn't been told yet what it was allowed to shoot.

And Brannock stood on her foredeck, hands clasped behind him, watching them come with the patience of a man who owned the water and everything trying to cross it.

"Signal him," Tam said quietly. "White rag. Tell him we're coming alongside, unarmed."

"I know what I'm doing, Tam."

"I know you do. I'm telling you anyway, because my hands are on this wheel and if this goes wrong, they're the hands that go wrong with it."

Something moved across Jude's face—not quite a smile, not quite forgiveness, but the shape of a thing that might become either, given time and calm water. Since the narrows, since his confession, since she'd chosen neither to protect him nor to hand him over, they had been renegotiating trust stroke by stroke, the way you bring a boat through rapids: not by fighting the current but by reading it, adjusting, committing. He'd been paid to deliver her here by someone who knew her map existed. He'd taken the fleet's money intending to double-cross them. Both true. Both unforgivable and forgivable in the same breath, depending on the hour.

"Bring us alongside," she said. "Slow. Let him see the tubes."

The *Maren's Grace* drifted in. Fifty yards. Thirty. Tam could see Brannock's face now—weathered, competent, the face of a man who'd spent his life pulling value out of drowned places—and beside him, half-hidden in the shadow of the deckhouse, a figure Tam recognized and wished he didn't: the charter's silent partner, the name from the county records, the man whose signature sat at the bottom of the annual payments that had bought a town's silence for twenty years. Hollis Crane. Older than his photographs. Paler, too.

Twenty yards. Ten. Fenders went over the side. Lines were thrown, caught, made fast.

Brannock stepped to the rail. "Miss Thornbury." His voice carried flat across the water, no heat in it, which was worse than heat. "You have property of mine aboard."

"Recoverable cargo," Jude said. "Salvage law's very clear. You wrote half of it into your own charter—I read it while you were chasing me up the gorge."

"The charter covers wreckage. Not theft."

"From whom?" Jude asked, and her voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was louder than shouting. "Show me the owner of record. Show me one document, signed by anyone living, that says this cargo belongs to your charter. Because I've been reading documents for two weeks, Captain, and every single one of them says the opposite."

Crane moved forward then, into the light, and Tam saw the crew of the *Maren's Grace* stiffen as one. "You are a surveyor," Crane said. "A contractor. You were hired to produce a report, not a judgment."

"And I'm delivering both." Jude reached into the pilothouse and came out with the tubes—not the originals. Tam had watched her copy them by lamplight through the night, page by page, her handwriting steady even when her eyes weren't. She held the copies up where the whole foredeck could see them. "These leave this river whether I do or not. That was decided before I ever came back down."

Brannock's jaw tightened. "You're bluffing. Copies need a courier. Couriers can be stopped."

"Not these ones." And here Jude did smile, small and cold and absolutely certain, and Tam felt the whole balance of the morning tip like a deck in a swell. "Ask yourself why I let you catch sight of us at the second narrows yesterday instead of running dark. Ask yourself what the lockkeeper's daughter has been doing with her father's skiff and her father's knowledge of the downstream channels while you were anchored here waiting for me. Ask yourself how many copies exist, and where, and who's holding them, and whether any plan you make in the next ten minutes changes any of that."

Silence, except the ice talking its slow language overhead and the river muttering underneath.

"You can't prove any of it," Crane said, but his voice had gone thin.

"I don't have to prove it. It only has to be possible, Mr. Crane, and you know it's more than possible, because you've spent twenty years paying an entire town to forget what happened here, and people who buy silence that expensive already know exactly how loud the truth gets when it gets out." She set the tubes down on the deck between them, deliberate as a woman laying cards. "Here's what happens now. You stand down. You let us run for Rivergate. When the state investigators arrive—and they're arriving; I wired them from the junction on the fleet's own line, thank you for the courtesy of leaving it connected—you can tell them whatever story you like. But if one shot is fired, if one line is cut, if my boat so much as scrapes paint getting past you, then every copy goes out tomorrow with affidavits attached naming your charter, your captain, and you personally, dated and witnessed. Destruction is pointless now, gentlemen. The truth is already moving faster than your dynamite. All you control is whether you're standing in front of it when it lands."

Tam watched Brannock look at Crane. It lasted three seconds, maybe four, and it was the longest conversation he'd ever seen pass between two men without a word. Brannock was a salvager; he understood wrecks, and he understood, in the marrow-deep way of his trade, the difference between a thing that could still be saved and a thing that was already lost. Whatever calculation ran behind his eyes, it ended the way calculations about lost things end.

He stepped back from the rail. "Cut the lines," he said to his crew. Then, to Jude: "Rivergate's yours, Miss Thornbury. For what it's worth—" and here something honest crossed his weathered face, briefly, like a fish under ice— "I read enough of the old charts to know what was buried in that junction. I told Crane I'd take the contract, not the crime. I'll be having words with the investigators myself."

The lines dropped away. The *Iron Meridian*'s engine turned over, and the fleet began, barge by barge, to give them the channel.

Jude stood at the rail until the last of them was hull-down around the bend, and then her shoulders came down—not much, an inch, maybe less, but Tam had spent thirty years reading bodies the way he read water, and he knew surrender when he saw it, even the good kind. The kind where you finally put down something heavy.

"You knew," he said. "About the copies. Before we ever came down off the mountain."

"I hoped," Jude said. "Halden's father kept those locks working for forty years. Her family knows every eddy and cutoff on the lower river better than the river does. If anyone alive could move paper fast and quiet, it's her." She looked at him then, fully, and Tam braced himself for the accounting he'd owed since the narrows. "You were paid to bring me here. You took their money."

"Yes."

"And you meant to turn it against them."

"From the start. Which doesn't clean it. I know that."

She was quiet a long moment. The glacier boomed somewhere above, distant as artillery in another war. "No," she said. "It doesn't. But you ran the river for me when you didn't have to, and you stayed at the wheel through the blast, and you went back in after Wren's logbook when the water was rising." Her hand landed on his shoulder, brief and hard, a pilot's grip. "We'll settle the rest in front of the investigators. Same table. Same truth. That's the only fair water left."

"Aye," Tam said, and found his voice wasn't entirely steady. "Same table."

He put the wheel over, and the *Maren's Grace* turned her bow toward Rivergate, running easy on the last of the sixth tide, the ice closing behind them inch by inch like a door that wouldn't open again for another year.

* * *

They reached Rivergate as the light went long and copper over the harbor.

The town had changed in the two weeks since Tam had first nosed the *Grace* against her pilings. Word traveled faster than boats, faster than dynamite, faster than anything except perhaps shame. People stood along the waterfront in the cold—not crowds, exactly, but clusters, the way people gather when they've heard something and haven't yet decided what to feel about it. The harbormaster was on his dock, hat in his hands, looking older than he had at the start of all this, looking like a man who'd been carrying twenty years of annual payment in a drawer and had finally heard it rattling.

At the head of the pier, wrapped in a wool coat too big for her, stood Halden, the lockkeeper's daughter. Her cheeks were red from the river. Behind her, visible in the open door of the harbor office, Tam could see a courier's satchel, empty now, and a telegram form pinned to the wall with the state investigator's reply already inked beneath it: *EN ROUTE. HOLD ALL PARTIES. DO NOT LET THE RIVER CLOSE BEHIND YOU.*

"Do not let the river close behind you," Tam read aloud, and laughed despite everything—a short, cracked sound. "As if anyone could."

Jude didn't laugh. She stood at the gangway with the original tubes under her arm, looking at the town that had sold its silence and was now, visibly, reckoning with the bill coming due. Aldous Wren came up beside her, steadier than he'd been, blinking at the sky like a man relearning the concept of distance. Somewhere up the valley, the glacier gave one final long groan, and down at the gorge mouth, white and inevitable, the ice began to seal the channel for another season.

"Hold all parties," Halden called down. "That includes you, Miss Thornbury. Investigator's orders. There's a room at the courthouse and a witness list with your name at the top of it."

"I know," Jude said. "I wrote half of it for them."

She started up the gangway, then stopped, halfway, and looked back at the river—at the sealed gorge, the copper water, the whole drowned geography of the thing she'd spent her life mapping and never once understood until now. Tam saw her hand go to the inside pocket of her coat, where the map rode, the real one, every dead line she'd ever used to disappear drawn in her own careful hand.

Tomorrow she'd testify. Tomorrow she'd name names, including, if she kept her word, his. Tonight the river was closed and everyone in Rivergate was holding their breath, waiting to find out what kind of woman had come down off the mountain.

But that evening, as Tam made the *Grace* fast and the courthouse lamps were lit one by one along the street, a boy came running down the pier with a message for the investigators' office, and Tam, signing his landing papers at the harbormaster's desk, saw the slip before it went inside. Just a name and a place, in a hand he almost recognized.

*Vess. Harrow Bend. He's asking for her.*

Tam looked up through the office window at Jude Thornbury standing alone in the lamplight outside the courthouse, and wondered whether the truth, having traveled so far so fast, was about to ask her to pay for it in the one coin she'd sworn she'd never spend again.
