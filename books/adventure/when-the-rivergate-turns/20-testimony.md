## 20. Testimony

The courthouse in Rivergate had been built by the same men who built the rail line—cut granite hauled up the gorge on flatcars, cornices carved with wheat sheaves and wheel spokes, a clock tower whose hands had not moved in eleven years because nobody could be paid to climb it and wind the works. Jude noticed that first, standing on the steps in the gray morning light: the frozen clock, both hands pointing at a quarter past seven forever, as if the whole town had agreed at some point to stop pretending time passed here honestly.

Today, time was going to start moving again.

She climbed the steps slowly. Her survey coat was brushed clean but she had not tried to make herself look respectable beyond that; respectability was a costume for people with something to hide, and today her whole purpose was to stop hiding. Under one arm she carried a leather tube, and inside the tube was the map—the map—the one she'd drawn in ink and graphite over nine years of running, every closed spur, every abandoned junction, every dead line from Harrow Bend to the coast, annotated in her own hand with dates and reasons and, in the last month, corrections that would have gotten her killed if any of three different men had read them.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake.

Inside, the courtroom smelled of lamp oil and cold stone and too many bodies. Word had gone downriver faster than the ice had closed behind the sixth tide; people had come from towns she'd only ever seen as names on her map—Corven's Landing, Ashmill, the ferry settlement at Tern Narrows. Farmers in their good coats. Railmen who wouldn't meet her eye, and railmen who would. The lockkeeper's daughter sat in the third row with her father's tin dispatch box open on her knees like a hymnal, and beside her, wrapped in a blanket he refused to surrender even indoors, pale as paper and blinking at daylight like a creature newly surfaced, sat the fifth man—Aldous Wren, though for weeks he had been no name at all, just a voice in the dark asking her to check the third bolt before she trusted the ladder.

He caught her looking and gave her the smallest nod. *Go on,* the nod said. *I'll hold up.*

She wasn't sure she would.

The hearing room itself was smaller than she'd imagined—county hearings were held in what was normally the assessor's office, and the judge's bench was a raised desk with a rail baron's portrait hanging above it, which someone had thoughtfully draped in burlap overnight. Not removed. Draped. Even now, even here, Rivergate couldn't quite bring itself to take the man's face off the wall. They'd covered it instead, the way you cover furniture in a house where someone died.

Jude understood the impulse. She'd spent nine years doing exactly that to her own past.

"State your name and occupation for the record," said the county attorney, a dry woman named Pell with spectacles and a voice like a ledger being opened.

"Jude Thornbury. Railroad engineer."

"You were contracted last spring to survey the closed Rivergate spur."

"I was."

"By whom?"

And there it was—the first fork in the rails. She could say *the harbormaster*, technically true, and let the whole testimony slide gently downhill toward the salvage fleet and the dynamite and the dramatic parts, the parts everyone had come to hear about. Or she could answer properly, from the beginning, from the rockslide twenty-two years ago that officially killed no one.

"By a chain of signatures," Jude said, "that ends with a name most of this room already knows. But I'd rather start further back than my contract. With yours, if you'll allow it. Everyone in this valley has a signature somewhere in this story. That's rather the point."

Pell looked at her over the spectacles. "Proceed."

* * *

She talked for two hours.

She started with the closure order—photostatic copy, courtesy of the lockkeeper's daughter and her father's tin box—signed by Ambrose Kettering, the rail baron whose face hung under burlap ten feet above the judge's desk. The order cited a rockslide. The rockslide was real; Jude had walked the scar herself, had put her hands on the stone. What the order did not cite was the payroll vault beneath the old junction house, or the forty-one thousand dollars in wages and bonds sealed behind it when the slide came down, or the six men working the night shift who were never dug out because digging them out meant opening the tunnel, and opening the tunnel meant the auditors would find the books that proved Kettering had been skimming the mountain division's payroll for a decade—that the closure wasn't a response to disaster but a way to bury it, along with everything and everyone the disaster had inconveniently preserved.

"The records we recovered," Jude said, "list the names of the six men. I won't read all of them now; they're entered in evidence. But I'll read one." She unfolded the page Aldous Wren had copied out in his careful, cramped hand, the letters still slightly warped from weeks of damp. "Hollis Vane. Brakeman. Twenty-six years old. His sister lives in Ashmill. She was told he died in the slide. He did die in the slide. What she was never told is that the railroad knew the vault was intact, knew the men might have reached the air pocket behind it, and ordered the portal sealed anyway—because an investigation would have cost more than six lives were worth on the balance sheet. There's a memo. It uses almost exactly those words. *Cost more than six lives are worth.*"

The room made a sound—not a gasp, something lower, a collective exhale through teeth, the sound of a town swallowing.

"And the annual payments?" Pell asked. "To the town."

"Two hundred dollars per household per year," Jude said, "disbursed through the harbormaster's office as 'river improvement stipends,' for twenty-two years. The harbormaster has confirmed the arrangement. So has the bank. The condition of payment was silence about the closure's cause. Which means—" and here she made herself look up, made herself meet the eyes row by row, the farmers and the railmen and the women in the back who had taken the money because winter took everything else— "which means nearly every adult in this room has spent two decades being paid to forget six men. I'm not saying that to shame anyone. I'm saying it because I took money from these same men too. My contract was bait. They hired me to legitimize a salvage claim so they could blast the junction shut with the evidence inside. I didn't know that when I signed. But I also didn't ask hard enough questions, because asking hard questions is a habit I gave up years ago, and I want to talk about why."

Pell's pen paused. "Why?"

"Because of Vess."

* * *

His name filled her mouth like river water.

"Hollis Vess was my mentor," Jude said. "When I was nineteen I testified before a state commission against Kettering—I testified about falsified bridge inspections on the coast division. It cost me my job, nearly my life; there were men waiting outside my lodging the night after the hearings closed. Vess got me out. Put me on a night freight dressed as a fireman, rode with me two hundred miles, and taught me the rule that kept me alive afterward: *never sleep in the same town twice, girl. Keep moving. A moving target can't be buried.*"

She heard how her voice changed on the word *buried*, and let it change.

"He saved my life. I've never doubted that and I never will. But nine days ago I found his signature in a payoff ledger recovered from the junction—a quarterly disbursement, dated four years after the closure, marked 'consulting, closure verification.' Hollis Vess helped seal that tunnel. And then he spent the rest of his career teaching the one witness who'd stood up to Kettering how to run away from everything else." She laid the ledger page flat on the table in front of her, turned it to face the room. "I've asked myself every day since whether he protected me or whether he was managing me—whether keeping me moving was love or containment. I don't have an answer. I don't think I'm required to have one today. But I know this: every mile I ran, I ran on rails he chose for me. And some of those miles were bought with the same money that bought this town's silence. That's what I need on the record. Not because it absolves him or condemns him, but because the cost of justice isn't only paid by the guilty. Sometimes the person it costs most is the one telling the truth."

The burlap-draped portrait seemed to lean over the room. Somebody in the back was crying quietly, without drama, the way people cry at funerals for deaths that happened long ago.

Pell consulted her notes. "You've named Kettering. You've named Vess. You've described the town's complicity. Is there anything else you wish to enter into evidence before the committee?"

"Yes." Jude reached for the leather tube.

* * *

Her fingers worked the cap the way they'd worked it a thousand times in a thousand rented rooms—thumb, twist, lift—and for a moment she simply held the map unrolled across the table, both palms flat on its edges, the way you hold down something alive.

Nine years of it. Ink lines fading where rain had gotten into her pack on the Corven run. Graphite corrections over ink. Margins dense with her small slanted hand: *bridge out, passable on foot. Watchman's hut burned '19. Derelict lock, third gate jams—see sketch.* Every secret road she'd ever used to disappear, every gap in the world's attention she'd slipped herself through, catalogued in her own blood-and-pencil handwriting. It was the most honest document she owned and it had always been a lie's best friend: a map of how to vanish.

"This is a map of every closed rail line in the western divisions," she said. "I drew it myself. For nine years it was my private property and my private escape—if I needed to leave a town fast, this told me how. Some of those lines are on it because they're dangerous. Some are on it because powerful people wanted them forgotten. Several are on it because of this case." She turned it so the committee could see the Rivergate spur, marked in red ink with a note in the corner: *closed for a reason — find it.* "I'm handing it over. All of it. Every route, every annotation, including the ones that would let a criminal move unseen through half the territory. Because a map of hidden roads doesn't belong to the person hiding anymore. It belongs to whoever's willing to reopen them."

She rolled it, capped it, and set it in Pell's outstretched hands, and felt the strangest sensation—as if a corset laced for nine years had finally been cut away and she could feel, all at once, how tightly she'd been breathing.

"That's everything?" Pell asked.

"One more thing. Not evidence. A request." Jude turned to face the room fully. "The state will reopen the spur. That decision's already moving downstream faster than the meltwater. When it does, it will need an engineer who knows every foot of that line—including the parts under the mountain. I'm asking to be considered for the post. Not because I'm owed anything. Because I spent nine years learning how lines die, and I'd like to spend whatever's left learning how they live."

For a moment, nothing. Then the lockkeeper's daughter stood up, and then the fifth man stood up beside her, blanket and all, swaying slightly, upright by pure will, and then—slowly, unevenly, like ice going out on a river—row by row, the room rose with them.

The frozen clock in the tower, high above the courthouse roof, chose that moment to shudder. Later, people would argue about whether it was the testimony crowd stamping the floorboards or just the spring thaw settling the tower stones, but the minute hand jumped—one tick, forward—and stuck again at a quarter past eight.

Time, starting to move.

* * *

Outside, the light had turned gold. Tam was leaning against the hitching rail, hat pushed back, watching her come down the steps with an expression she was still learning to read without suspicion.

"Well?" he said.

"They took the map."

"Whole thing? Every dead-end shortcut you ever used to ghost out of a town at three in the morning?" He shook his head slowly, grinning. "There goes the Thornbury legend. Half the territory's drifters are going to wake up tomorrow and find their escape routes published."

"Good," Jude said. "Let them find new ones. Or better—let them stop needing them."

Tam studied her a moment longer. "You know the state engineers arrive Thursday. Survey crew, full instrumentation, the works. They'll want a chief engineer who can walk them the spur end to end." He tipped his head toward the mountain, where the gorge's new mouth showed white in the evening sun. "First official walk-through's at dawn. They're expecting an answer tonight."

Jude looked down the valley—at the river running open and honest between its banks, at the town that had sold its silence and was now, shakily, buying it back with something harder than money, at the rails rusting up the gorge toward a door she'd blown open with her own hands.

"Noon train gets in tomorrow," she said. "Somebody should be on the platform to meet it."

Tam smiled, and spat, and offered his arm like a gentleman, which neither of them was, and together they walked up the street toward the station as the first true dusk in nine unburied years came down over Rivergate like a promise.
