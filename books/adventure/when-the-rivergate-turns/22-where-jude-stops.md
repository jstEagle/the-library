## 22. Where Jude Stops

The northbound freight out of Rivergate ran on time for the first time in eleven years, and Jude rode it standing in the open door of the caboose, one hand on the rail, watching the mountains do what mountains do, which is nothing at all, patiently, forever.

She had a ticket. That was the strange part. She'd bought it at the Rivergate depot from a clerk who knew her name, who stamped it without asking where she was really going, because everyone in Rivergate knew now where she was really going. The ticket said HARROW BEND in clean block letters, and beneath it, in the clerk's careful hand: *one way.*

One way. She'd stared at those two words longer than the price.

For fifteen years she had bought tickets the way other people bought matches—cheap, disposable, always in pairs, the return leg purchased and then quietly torn up or traded or left to expire in a coat pocket while she stepped off somewhere the line bent toward a closed spur or a dead junction or a town that had erased its own station from the county rolls. A round-trip ticket was a promise, and Jude Thornbury had made a religion of never promising a destination anything except her departure. Vess had taught her that. Vess, whose signature sat in a payoff ledger between a harbormaster's and a sheriff's, Vess who had pulled her out of Ashfell the night after she testified, who had put her on a night freight with a bedroll and a warning—*never sleep in the same town twice, girl, the men who count on being forgotten count on you forgetting too*—and who had, she understood now, been teaching her his own survival the whole time. His running had not been wisdom. It had been guilt with a timetable.

The mountains slid past. Somewhere behind them, under four hundred feet of rock and a glacier that had calved twice since the sixth tide, the junction lay sealed—not blasted shut this time, but closed properly, by engineers, with state inspectors and concrete and a bronze plaque that read, in letters small enough to be honest: *Here the Rivergate & Northern buried its dead twice. The second burial was the truth. Recovered.* The records themselves lived in the courthouse at the capital now, behind glass, under lock, in a room that did not flood.

The freight slowed at the gorge. She felt it before she saw it—the change in the rail's song, that deep harmonic shift when wheels cross from cut stone onto the new trestle. She stepped out onto the vestibule platform and looked up.

The gorge was open. Not for six tides this time—for good, or for as long as good lasted, which was the only kind of forever an engineer ever trusted. The state crews had cut the channel wider. The ice that had sealed the river for a generation now calved off in honest pieces and went downstream like everything else that finally told the truth. Water ran white and loud over the rocks where the salvage fleet had anchored, where Tam had held a boat steady in a current that wanted them dead while she climbed back into the dark. The narrows were marked with buoys now. Red and green, cheerful as festival lanterns.

She had not been back to the junction since. She had meant to go once, in the autumn, and had walked as far as the tramway cut and stood there in the cold with her lamp unlit, and then had turned around. Some doors you reopen. Some you certify closed and walk away from. The fifth man—Alder Voss, who signed his logs *A.V.* and had spent thirty-one days in the dark eating tinned peaches and keeping a ledger of his own heartbeat—had understood that without her having to say it. He'd taken the first run as conductor in March, and when the train cleared the gorge he had not looked back either, and the two of them had stood in the cab afterward sharing a thermos, not talking, which is the language engineers speak best.

The freight gathered itself and climbed. Harrow Bend was four hours north, and Jude went back inside and sat down across from a farmer's wife who was taking a prize sow to market and who talked, for four hours, about the sow, and Jude listened with genuine interest, because for the first time in her adult life she was not memorizing the landscape out of habit, cataloguing escape routes, noting which bridges would burn and which culverts would pass a crawling man. Her hands lay quiet in her lap. It was the strangest sensation of the entire affair—stranger than the dark of the junction, stranger than the tide turning under her—this quietness of her own hands.

* * *

Harrow Bend had not changed, which was its way. The grain elevator still stood against the sky like a headstone for wheat. The depot still had its crooked clock, still eleven minutes fast, still wrong in exactly the way everyone had agreed to tolerate. The Harrow House still rented rooms by the week to people passing through, and its landlady, Mrs. Ondrell, still watered geraniums that refused, year after year, to die of neglect.

Jude took the same room. Room six, end of the hall, window on the yard. She had slept in it once before, three years ago, one night, on her way between two closed lines. She had left before dawn. Now she set her bag down on the chair—the whole bag, unpacked nothing, but *set down*, deliberately, as a man sets down a load he intends to pick up again—and she washed her face in the basin and looked at herself in the mirror and thought: *well. Here we are.*

Here we are. Not here I am. She'd been an *I* for a long time. A singular, moving, unfindable point on an endless line.

Downstairs, the dining room smelled of ham and coffee. Mrs. Ondrell brought her a plate without being asked and said, "You'll be wanting the late supper tomorrow, then. I do a roast on Thursdays."

It was not a question. It was a Thursday, and there would be another Thursday after it, and the woman had simply assumed the arithmetic would continue. Jude looked at her, at this small round fortress of a woman who had forgotten her entirely in three years, and felt something in her chest unclench that she had not known was clenched.

"Thursday," Jude said. "I'll be here."

* * *

She stayed a week before she went to the depot, because she was learning, slowly, the grammar of staying. You did not simply remain in a place; you accreted into it, one small obligation at a time. She fixed Mrs. Ondrell's gate, which had sagged since before the war. She sat with the depot agent, old Prewitt, and helped him re-file forty years of waybills that had been organized by a system comprehensible only to a dead man. She walked the yard at dusk and put her hand on the couplers of cars that would move tomorrow, and did not move with them, and found that she survived it.

On the eighth day she took out the map.

She had carried it for fifteen years. Not the official survey—the state had that now, the beautiful corrected version with the junction marked and the closure orders appended—but the first one, the true one, the one she'd drawn herself in a Harrow Bend boarding house not so different from this one, on a night when the fear had been so large she'd had to make it into a shape to survive it. Every closed line she'd ever found. Every dead spur, every sealed tunnel, every station struck from the rolls, every mile of track the railroad had pretended did not exist. She'd hidden it under false ledger pages—*Harrow Bend Grain Co., Receiving*—and carried it through a hundred towns, and it had been, for fifteen years, the truest thing about her. Not a map of places. A map of her own flight. Every line on it was a night she had not slept twice in the same bed. Every junction was a name she'd left behind without saying goodbye.

She carried it down to the depot in the late afternoon, when the light came in low and gold through the west windows and lay across the floorboards like spilled honey. Prewitt was gone home. The crooked clock ticked its eleven-fast minutes. She stood in the empty waiting room, in that honeyed light, and unfolded the map on the ticket counter, all the way, every fold, for the first time in years.

It was bigger than she remembered. It always was.

She looked at it a long time. The Rivergate spur was on it, of course—she'd drawn that line in ink so old it had gone brown, and now someone else had drawn it again, in state blue, and reopened it, and put trains on it. The line through Ashfell was there, where she'd testified, where Vess had put her on the night freight. Vess. They had found him in November, in a rooming house in Corvane, and he had not run this time. He had sat across a table from her with his hands flat on the wood like a man showing he held nothing, and he had said, "I told you never to stop moving because I couldn't bear to watch you stop and look at me. Every town you left was a town where you didn't ask what I'd signed." He had wept, a little, without dignity, an old man in a cheap coat. "You were the best thing I ever did and I spent fifteen years hoping you'd never find out what it cost."

He was serving eight years at Farrowfield. He wrote her letters. She had not answered yet. She would. Staying, she was learning, did not mean the past stopped arriving; it meant you were finally home when it did.

The wind came through the propped door of the waiting room, off the grain fields, smelling of cut wheat and coming rain. It lifted the near edge of the map, and the paper breathed, and the whole thing rippled like water.

Jude put one hand flat on it. Held it down. The wind pushed, patient, insistent, the way wind is, the way the river had been, the way every departing train had ever been.

She thought about what the map had done for her. It had kept her alive. It had also kept her gone. There was a difference, and it had taken her fifteen years, one flooded junction, one old man's signature, and one town full of bought silence to learn it. The map had been armor. She had testified in the Rivergate courthouse in April, and she had handed the state her survey, and she had kept this one—this first one, this private one—telling herself she'd decide later. Armor you keep in case. In case of what? In case the world turned out to be the place Vess had shown her, all betrayal and payoff ledgers. But she had stood in that courthouse and named the baron and named Vess and named the harbormaster and the town, and the world had not ended. It had, in fact, done the opposite of ending. The world had gotten a new trestle over the gorge, and a conductor named Alder Voss, and a Thursday roast.

The wind pushed again.

"Where you headed next?"

The voice came from the doorway, and Jude knew it before she turned, because it was the same voice, the same question, the same stranger—no, not the same stranger, but *a* stranger, a young man in a canvas jacket with a bedroll and the particular hungry look of a person between towns. He had come in to ask about the northbound, and he'd seen her standing over a great unfolded map, and he'd asked the question the way everyone always asked it, casual, companionable, the universal greeting of the moving world.

*Where you headed next?*

Three years ago—no, a lifetime ago—she had been asked the same question in this same room by a man with too many questions about her last employer, and she had answered with a lie, and the lie had set her on the river, and the river had set her on the truth. She looked at the young man. He had dust on his boots and a rail pass sticking out of his pocket and the whole horizon in his face. He reminded her of no one. He reminded her of herself.

"Nowhere," Jude said.

It came out easy. That was the miracle of it. Fifteen years of answers, and the true one was one syllable long.

"Got family here?" he asked, making conversation, leaning on the ticket counter the way travelers do.

"No." She smiled. "I'm the line engineer. Rivergate and Northern. I've got a Thursday roast and a sagging gate I already fixed and a bridge inspection on Monday. I live here."

The young man looked at her the way people look at a clock that has stopped—not with pity, exactly, but with the suspicion that some law has been violated. People who stay are a mystery to people who move. Then he shrugged, the shrug of the road, and asked Prewitt's empty counter for a schedule that wasn't there, and went out into the gold light toward the yard, toward the northbound, toward his own closed lines.

Jude stood alone in the waiting room with the map.

She picked it up. It was lighter than it had ever been, the way a thing is light when you are no longer gripping it. She carried it out the propped door into the evening, into the wind coming down off the fields, and stood on the depot platform where the six o'clock would soon come through, right on time, on the reopened line, going places she no longer needed to go.

She unfolded it one last time, all the way, and held it out.

The wind took it the way the river takes everything, without malice, without ceremony. It lifted the paper whole for one breath—one full second in which every closed line she had ever drawn was open to the sky, every dead junction lit gold by the low sun, every secret road she'd ever used to disappear spread out and visible and finished—and then it peeled the map off her hands and took it up over the grain elevator, over the crooked clock, over the town, tumbling white against the darkening east, and she did not watch where it landed.

She stood a while on the platform. The six o'clock came through on time, whistle blowing, windows full of strangers going somewhere, and Jude Thornbury raised one hand to it—not a wave goodbye, not quite, more the salute one engineer gives another across water—and the train answered with two short blasts, and went on north without her, and the sound of it faded down the valley until it was just the river's own noise again, and then not even that.

The depot lamp buzzed on. Prewitt would lock up soon. Thursday was coming, and after Thursday, another Thursday.

Jude turned up the collar of her coat against the coming rain, and instead of walking out of Harrow Bend the way she'd come, she walked in—down the main street, past the elevator, past the geraniums, to the Harrow House, where a room with her bag in it waited at the end of the hall, and Mrs. Ondrell had left the porch light burning.

She climbed the steps. She went inside.

She stayed.
