## 1. Silt Season

The river told you everything, if you knew its handwriting.

Lior Montrose had learned to read it the way other people learned scripture—slowly, at first, out of obligation, and then with the helpless devotion of the convert. The Rivergate floodplain spoke in layers. Clay over gravel over clay, each stratum a sentence, each band of color a clause in a story the water had been drafting for ten thousand years. Most diggers saw mud. Lior saw grammar.

"Your trench is wrong," she told Halvard's site manager on the fourth morning of the contract, standing at the lip of Cut Seven while the man's surveyor fussed with his tripod behind her.

Dennick looked up from his ledger with the expression of a man who had been told his trench was wrong many times and had never once believed it. "The consortium's models put the old ferry landing forty meters east."

"The consortium's models were built on surveys taken before the floods," Lior said. "The river moved. It always moves. Look at the cut bank." She pointed with her trowel, not touching it to the soil—she never touched soil she was explaining, a superstition she'd stopped examining years ago. "See the banding? That gray seam isn't flood deposit. It's occupation layer. People lived here, cooked here, dropped their garbage here for a generation or more. And it runs—" she traced the seam's lazy diagonal through the air "—that way. Not east. Northwest, toward the willow stand."

Dennick wrote something in his ledger. She suspected it was not her observation.

"The models," he repeated, "put it east."

"Then the models are mourning a ferry landing that's now under a soybean field, and you're paying six diggers to excavate a farmer's fence posts." She said it without heat. That was the trick of it—she'd learned long ago that competence delivered calmly was more unsettling than competence delivered with pride. People trusted anger. Calm they had to sit with.

Dennick closed the ledger. "The consortium pays for what the models say, Montrose. Not for what the dirt says."

"The dirt doesn't charge," Lior said, and walked away before he could decide whether he'd been insulted.

The truth was she'd already decided to leave. She'd decided on the second day, when she'd read the pay schedule taped inside the supply tent and noticed the arithmetic that didn't quite close—the way the per-diem was calculated against a work week that the schedule itself defined as six days but the contract, in smaller print, paid as five. A small theft. A deniable theft. The kind of theft that wasn't meant to be caught, only meant to be felt, so that when the diggers complained, the management could point to the schedule and call them careless with numbers. She'd seen the pattern before, on three other contracts with three other consortiums, and it always ended the same way: a pay dispute in the last week, tempers like dry grass, and someone—usually the foreman, usually the one who'd stood up for the crew—escorted off the site by men whose job titles were too vague to be printed.

So she was leaving. She'd told Tam as much the night before, in the low voice they used for plans, while the cook fire spat and the others argued about cards.

"End of the week," she'd said. "I'll give Dennick notice on Thursday. You should come."

Tam had stirred the fire with a stick, not looking at her. "Ivet's got a sister in Corren who needs the money."

"Ivet can come too. There's work downriver. Always work downriver."

"You say that like the work's the same everywhere."

"It is the same everywhere. That's the point." She'd watched the fire a while. "The work is the same. The people are what changes."

Tam had grunted, which from Tam was a whole paragraph, and said nothing more. But Lior had caught the look—the one that meant he'd follow her, eventually, in his own time, the way he always did, loyal the way stone was loyal: not quickly, but completely.

She had a decade of practice at leaving. Ten years of contract digs and vanishing acts, of learning a river basin just long enough to read it and then being gone before anyone learned to read her. No surname on the manifests where she could avoid it. No stories about her past, because stories were threads and threads got pulled. She had gotten very good at being a woman with no antecedents, a digger who appeared fully formed out of the flood country with a trowel and an uncanny eye and nothing else.

Her mother would have hated it. Her mother, who had signed everything, claimed everything, put her name on every map and every margin like a woman planting flags. Her mother, who was dead, and whose name Lior carried anyway, buried in her like a splinter too deep to draw.

The floods had been the worst in eleven years. Everyone said so. The river had come up through the last week of the wet season like a debt being collected, swallowing the lower fields, turning the dig into an archipelago of tents and spoil heaps, and then—two days ago—it had begun to fall. Not gently. The Rivergate dropped the way a held breath was released, all at once and then in shudders, and the water peeled back from the land it had borrowed, leaving the floodplain scoured and glistening like something newly born.

Which was why, on the fifth morning, Lior was walking the exposed channel at first light, alone, before the others were up.

This was her ritual everywhere she worked: walk the waterline after every flood. The river gave things back when it fell. It gave back lost tools and drowned fences and, once, on the Vell, a cart horse's skeleton standing in the mud like a monument to somebody's very bad day. It gave back pottery, coins, the occasional bone that required paperwork Lior preferred not to file. She walked the waterline because she wanted to know what the river knew before anyone else did.

The light was the color of weak tea. Mist stood in the willows. Her boots broke a crust of new silt with each step, a sound like biting through sugar, and the air smelled of river rot and cold iron and the green, mineral breath of things the water had just uncovered.

She found the chest a half mile below the dig, where the channel made its long bend and the receding water had planed the mud into a smooth gray plain.

She saw it from thirty meters and her first thought was: wrong.

That was the word her mind gave her. Not *interesting*, not *valuable*—*wrong*. Because the chest sat on the floodplain in open silt, unburied, unhidden, and everything about its position said it had been placed there rather than lost there. Rivers did not set objects down neatly. Rivers scattered, tumbled, buried. This chest sat square to the world, level, as if it had been set on a table.

She approached it the way she approached all finds: slowly, circling, reading the ground around it before the thing itself. The silt around it was scoured in a halo, as if the water had refused to touch it. She knelt, and the cold came up through her knees.

Oak. Black with age and water, but oak—dense, close-grained, the kind that survived drowning for centuries. Iron banding gone to bloom and rust-flake. And on the lid—

She stopped.

Her breath went out of her and did not come back in any hurry.

The lid bore a seal, pressed into a bronze boss set flush into the wood, and around the bronze ran a border of carved lettering in a script she had seen exactly once in her life, in a book her mother had burned in the yard when Lior was fourteen, feeding the pages into the flames one at a time with an expression Lior had never been able to name until much later, and had then spent a decade failing to forget.

But it wasn't the script that stopped her heart. It wasn't even the seal, strange as it was—a device like a bridge over water, or a gate over a river, cut with the confidence of a state's own mint.

It was the workmanship of the chest itself.

The joinery. She knew joinery the way she knew soil; her mother had believed a person should be able to build the box that held their life. Dovetails cut at that particular angle—steep, almost aggressive, the pins and tails interlocking like fingers laced tight. A signature cut, taught hand to hand, region to region, and as distinctive to a trained eye as handwriting.

She had grown up watching hands cut those dovetails. She had held the offcuts. She had swept the shavings.

Her mother had built chests exactly like this. Had built them for her own expeditions, for her own archives, had built them the way other scholars built bookshelves—and had taught Lior the angle on a workbench in a house that no longer existed, saying, *the joint has to be tighter than the water can argue with, Lior, tighter than the water can argue with.*

The mist moved in the willows. Somewhere down the channel a bird started up, shrill and stupid with morning, and Lior knelt in the sugar-crisp silt and could not move.

It was impossible. She knew it was impossible. Her mother had died nine years ago and a continent away, on an expedition whose details Lior had gone to great expense and greater effort never to learn, and this chest had been underwater—had to have been underwater, the floods had covered this ground to a depth of four meters—and oak did not float when it was built this dense, and rivers did not place things square to the world, and dead women did not build boxes that outlasted their own burials.

But the angle of the dovetails did not care what was possible. The angle of the dovetails said: *I was here. I made this. I made it the only way I knew how, and you know the only way I knew how, because I taught you.*

Lior's hands, when she finally made herself extend them, were not steady. She was a woman who could hold a trowel against a root for an hour without tremor, and her hands were not steady.

She did not open it. Some instinct older than sense told her not to open it here, on open ground, in the sightline of the willows, with a camp of six diggers and a site manager and a consortium's men half a mile upstream. She had spent ten years learning what happened to people who found things. Her mother had been the first lesson.

Instead she did the practical things, because practical things were how she kept her hands from shaking. She noted the position against the willow stand and the bend. She measured the chest by eye—two spans by one and a half, iron-banded, perhaps eighty pounds, maybe more with whatever it held. She looked around the floodplain, empty in every direction except the mist, and then she went back to camp for a shovel, a canvas groundsheet, and a story.

She passed Tam on the way in. He was up early too, as he always was, mending a net by the cook fire, and he looked at her face and then at her empty hands and then at her face again.

"Find something?" he asked.

"No," Lior said, and heard how badly it came out, and made herself smile, and knew the smile came out worse.

Tam went back to his net. He didn't press. He never pressed. But she felt his eyes on her back all the way to the tool tent, patient as stone, and she understood that the leaving she had planned for Thursday had just become complicated, because the river had finally done the thing she had spent ten years making sure no river could ever do.

It had given her mother back.

The chest was still there when she returned with the shovel. Of course it was. But standing over it again, in the strengthening light, Lior saw what the mist and the dread had made her miss: pressed into the silt beside the chest, half erased by the receding water, was a mark in the mud.

Small. Deliberate. A circle crossed by a single line—the private mark her mother used to scratch into finished work, into the bottom of drawers, into the handle of the workbench, the mark Lior had assumed had gone into the ground with her.

It was fresh. The silt around it had not yet crusted.

Someone had set this chest here. Recently. Someone who knew her mother's hands, or her mother's mark, or her mother's daughter.

Lior stood on the floodplain with a shovel in her hands and the river talking its endless grammar around the bend, and for the first time in ten years, she could not tell whether she had just found something—or whether something had just found her.
