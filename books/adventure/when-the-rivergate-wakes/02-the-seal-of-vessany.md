## 2. The Seal of Vessany

Lior had told herself she would wait until morning.

She lasted until the grey hour before it, when the floodplain was the color of old tin and the herons were already working the shallows, patient as creditors. Sleep had been a negotiation she kept losing. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the lid again—the grain of the wood gone silver with a decade underwater, and burned into it, small enough that only someone who knew to look would ever find it: a circle crossed by three descending lines, like a river falling through a gate.

Her mother's mark. Not Vessany's official arms—those had been scraped from every treaty document and courthouse lintel in two hemispheres—but the private sign Sera Montrose used on her field notes, on the inside covers of her books, once, memorably, on the back of Lior's hand in ink when she was nine and couldn't sleep. *So you always know your own work,* her mother had said, laughing. *Even when other people try to make it theirs.*

The chest sat where they'd left it, under a tarp behind the survey tent, weighted with two sandbags because the ground was still drinking the river's leavings and everything here drifted if you let it. The camp slept around it in the loose sprawl of people paid by the day: Tam's snore from the cook shelter, steady as a pump; the wind worrying the guy-lines; somewhere down the cut, water moving in its sleep.

Lior crouched beside it and did not touch it for a long time.

That was the discipline her mother had taught her too—not the mark, but the waiting. *The ground tells you when it's ready,* she'd say, kneeling over a test pit with her sleeves rolled, dirt to the elbow. *You don't get to decide. You just have to be paying attention.* Lior had spent ten years being very good at not paying attention. She dug for hire under three names, took contracts in basins she'd never worked before, left towns before the pay disputes turned ugly or anyone thought to ask about her family. It had felt, for most of a decade, like survival. Crouched here now, with the tin-grey light coming up and her mother's mark looking at her across four feet of trampled mud, it felt like what it probably was: a woman hiding in the one place no one would think to look for her, which is to say, nowhere at all.

She pulled the tarp back.

* * *

The lock was bronze and had no keyhole she recognized—a disc with a central pin, the kind of mechanism that opened to knowledge rather than force. Her mother had owned one like it; Lior had watched her open it a hundred times without ever being shown how, the way children watch adults do magic and assume it will be explained later. Later had run out eleven years ago, in a river country much like this one, in an expedition whose official report listed Sera Montrose among *equipment losses.*

Lior pressed the pin, then turned the disc counterclockwise, then—remembering, suddenly, her mother tapping the lid twice with one knuckle before every opening, some private superstition or some part of the mechanism she'd never understood—knocked twice.

The lock clicked open like a held breath released.

Inside, packed in wool gone stiff and dark with river water but still doing its work, lay paper. That was the impossible thing, the thing that made her sit back on her heels with her heart going hard. Paper doesn't survive ten years submerged unless someone meant it to—unless it was sealed against the water by someone who understood water, which meant her mother had packed this chest expecting it to drown. Expecting it to wait.

Lior lifted the top layer out into the light. Survey documents, drawn on linen-backed stock: cross-sections of the Rivergate channel, soundings, a grid overlay of the floodplain marked in a hand she knew better than her own handwriting, because it had taught her hers. And beneath them, wrapped separately in oilcloth tied with waxed cord—

A journal. Cloth-bound, spine cracked, corners soft with handling. Not waterlogged. Carried often. She knew before she opened it that the hand inside would match the maps, and it did, and knowing did nothing to blunt it. Seeing her mother's writing was like hearing a voice through a wall: all the words there, none of the warmth, and still it went through her like a splinter finding old wood.

The first entry was dated nineteen years ago. The last was dated six days before the expedition's report listed her among the lost.

And beneath the journal, in a fitted recess lined with felt, lay a seal.

Bronze, the size of a plum, mounted on a short handle worn bright by use. The face bore the circle and the three descending lines—and around them, in an annular band, lettering in a script Lior had seen exactly once in her life, in a smuggled book a university friend had shown her and then burned. A script belonging to a language that belonged to a country that appeared on no map printed in the last sixty years. Vessany. Erased so thoroughly that schoolchildren learned it as a fable, a place that had never existed, and the erasure had been so complete, so *administrative*, that even the word for what had been done to it had been retired from the treaties.

Her mother had carried this. Had used it—handle worn bright—to press into wax, into clay, into whatever needed marking as hers, as true, as *witnessed*. Lior turned it in the growing light and the bronze was warm already from her hand, or seemed to be, and she understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that this chest had not been lost. It had been *left*. Hidden in the one place the funders' divers would never think to search, in the silt of a river her mother had known would rise and fall and keep its secrets between seasons, addressed to whoever could read the mark.

Addressed to her.

She was still holding it when the gravel crunched.

* * *

Halvard came down the tent line early, as he always did, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had never once been surprised on his own payroll. He was dressed, as always, as though the floodplain were a mildly disappointing hotel: canvas jacket brushed clean, boots too good for the mud. Behind him the sky had gone the color of pearl and the herons had gone quiet, and Lior had perhaps four seconds to decide what to do with her hands.

She chose wrong. She closed the tarp—one motion too quick, one rustle too loud—and Halvard's eyes went to the covered shape the way a compass goes north.

"Up early, Montrose." He stopped a polite distance away. His gaze stayed on the tarp. "River dropped another meter overnight. Exposed more of the old channel. My people want to extend the grid."

"Your people can extend the grid," Lior said. "It's their contract."

"It's your name on the methodology." He smiled the way he did in negotiations, showing teeth without warmth, a gesture calibrated rather than felt. "Which reminds me. I've had a wire from the consortium. They're pleased. Unusually pleased." He paused, letting the sentence hang, and Lior heard what lived underneath it: *unusually watchful.* "They've authorized a bonus. Discretionary. For anything... significant recovered ahead of schedule."

"We haven't recovered anything significant."

"No," Halvard agreed pleasantly. "Of course not."

The silence stretched. Down the cut, the river made its low continuous argument with the banks. Lior became aware of her own heartbeat in her ears, of the weight of the seal in her jacket pocket where she'd put it without deciding to, of the fact that Halvard had arrived twenty minutes earlier than any morning in five weeks of contract work, and that he had come alone, and that men like Halvard came alone when they didn't want witnesses.

"Triple," he said.

"What?"

"Your fee. Tripled. Effective today." He said it lightly, a man discussing weather. "In exchange for a small adjustment to your reporting. You'll continue the survey as planned, submit your findings directly to me rather than through the consortium channel, and note in your log that the exposed channel yielded nothing of interest. Routine alluvium. No cultural material." He glanced at the tarp again, briefly, the way a man checks a locked drawer. "Whatever you found last night—or didn't—stays between us. The consortium has a long institutional memory, Montrose, and shorter institutional patience. There are parties involved who prefer certain histories remain hydrological. Silt. Water. Gone."

Lior looked at him and felt grief and suspicion arrive together, inseparable, like thunder and the flash—you never could tell afterward which came first. Because he knew. He couldn't know *what*, but he knew *that*: something had come up out of the river that mattered to the money behind her dig, and the money had sent him down the tent line at dawn to buy it before breakfast. Which meant the consortium had been watching the waterline. Which meant they had known, or feared, or hoped—that after the floods, the river might give something back.

Which meant her mother hadn't hidden the chest from random chance. She'd hidden it from *them*.

"I'll consider the offer," Lior said.

"You have until noon." Halvard smiled again, recalibrated, and turned back up the tent line. Then he paused, half-turned, and added, almost gently, the way a man sets down a card he's been holding: "Your file says you have no family, Montrose. No history. Very clean. I do hope it stays that way. Clean files are so much easier to maintain than to repair."

He walked on. The gravel took his footsteps and gave them back, fading.

Lior stood a long time in the pearl-colored light with her hand closed around the bronze seal in her pocket, its worn handle fitting her grip as though it had been shaped there. Ten years of running, and the past had simply waited, patient as the river, until the water dropped and set it down in front of her feet.

Behind the survey tent, under the tarp, her mother's journal lay open to a page she hadn't finished reading. But she had read enough to know one thing with total certainty: Halvard's offer wasn't an offer. It was a countdown.

By noon, she would either be bought—or she would be a problem.

And problems, in her experience, got solved the way her mother had been solved.
