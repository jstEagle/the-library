## 3. Crew of Strangers

Lior woke before the light did, the way she always did, and lay listening to the river.

It had a new voice since the floods. Where it used to murmur against the pilings it now dragged and gulped, thick with the silt it was still shedding, still deciding what to keep and what to give back. She had spent ten years learning to hear that decision in the water, and every instinct she owned said the river was not finished. It would take the floodplain back soon. Days, maybe a week. Which meant every hour they spent here was an hour borrowed.

She dressed in the dark and went out to check the chest.

It sat where she had dragged it the night before, wedged behind the supply tent where the tarps made a shadow deep enough to hide a coffin. She had covered it with a ground sheet and weighted the corners with stones, and she had done it quickly, badly, the way a woman buries something she doesn't want to think about. The morning mist lay over the camp in a grey wool blanket, and the chest was invisible unless you already knew it was there.

She lifted the ground sheet anyway. Just to look.

The bronze seal winked up at her from the gap in the lid, that small round face of a country that had never been on any map she'd studied, and her mother's private mark burned into the wood beside it — a river with an eye at its source, the line fine as a hair, done with the steady hand Lior had watched make notes in kitchen lamplight for the first twelve years of her life.

She dropped the sheet back over it and stood there with her heart going too fast.

*You buried her*, she told herself. *You buried this with her. It doesn't get to climb out.*

But the river had other opinions, and so, apparently, did the world.

"Lior."

Tam's voice came from behind the cook tent, and she turned with the ease of long practice, though her hands had gone cold. He was coming across the mud with two mugs, steam rising off them, his boots making the sucking sound the ground made now when you asked it to hold your weight. He handed her one without asking. He'd been doing that for three years.

"Sleep?" he asked.

"Enough."

"You're up before the gulls."

"The gulls are lazy."

He drank and looked out at the river, and she watched him not looking at the supply tent, and understood with a sinking, familiar feeling that he had already looked. Tam missed nothing. It was the best thing about him and, this morning, the worst.

"Odd find, that chest," he said, mild as milk. "Way upstream of anything. No wreck near it. No barge debris."

"Rivers move things."

"Rivers move things *downstream*." He said it gently, the way he said most things, a big man handling something fragile. "That came up from under. Or someone put it there."

"Or it's been in the bank for years and the flood cut it loose." She drank her tea. It was too hot and she drank it anyway, wanting something to do with her mouth that wasn't lying. "We'll inventory it with the rest of the salvage when the consortium's assessor comes. It's not ours."

"No," Tam agreed. "It's not." And he looked at her then, straight on, with those patient river-bottom eyes, and she knew he'd heard the seam in her voice where the lie was stitched.

She was bad at this. She'd always been bad at this. Ten years of vanishing had made her excellent at leaving and terrible at staying still under a question, and Tam's questions didn't even have hooks in them. That was what made them dangerous.

"I want night watches," she said, before he could ask another. "Two-person. From tonight. Equipment's worth too much to leave dark, not after the floods, with the camp the only lit thing on this stretch of river. Scavengers come up from the cut towns when the water drops."

Tam considered her. The mist moved between them.

"Scavengers," he said.

"Or worse. You've seen what the cut towns are like since the floods."

"I have." He finished his tea. "I'll draw up a roster. You want to pick the pairs yourself?"

"I'll pick the pairs."

He nodded and turned away, and then stopped, half-turned, the steam from his mug curling up past his jaw. "Lior. Whatever it is you're watching for—" He paused. "I've done watches before. I know what a camp looks like when the person running it is afraid. This looks like that."

"Drink your tea, Tam."

He went. She stood a long time in the mist, listening to the river decide.

* * *

Tam had run crews for eleven years before he'd run hers, and in eleven years he had learned that the trouble in a camp never announced itself. It arrived the way water got into a hull — quietly, below the line you were watching, and by the time you heard it sloshing it was already everywhere.

The trouble in this camp had three names.

The first was pay. The consortium was six weeks behind on wages, and the crew knew it, and the crew talked about it the way crews always did — in low voices at the cook fire, in the particular silence that fell whenever Halvard's name came up. Dessa had a brother in debt in Corrow. Ivet hadn't been paid enough to send anything home since the second month. You could measure a camp's remaining trust in how long the men worked after the wages stopped, and Tam had been doing that measuring every morning, and the number was getting short.

The second name was the river. Everyone could feel it rising back into itself, the way a dog tenses before it stands. The gauge stakes Tam had driven at the cut showed six inches a day and accelerating. When the water came back, it would come fast, and the camp's boats were moored badly because Lior had ordered them moved twice in two days and couldn't or wouldn't say why.

The third name was the chest. Tam didn't know what was in it. He knew it was heavy, that Lior had moved it alone in the night rather than let anyone help, that she'd looked at it the way you look at a grave, and that she had started lying to him — small lies, badly made, the kind she'd never once told him in three years of flood and fever and pay dispute. That frightened him more than the river did.

So on the third morning, when the stranger came up the levee path out of the mist, Tam was already in the mood to count his fingers.

The man came alone, no boat Tam could see, walking the levee like he owned the easement. Good boots, wrong kind — city boots, oiled, the soles too clean for a man who'd supposedly walked in from the ferry landing four miles off. He carried a leather case and a smile he'd clearly practiced.

"Morning. Dr. Anselm Vey, hydrological survey, consortium office." He put out his hand. "You'd be the foreman. Tam, is it? Halvard mentioned you."

"Halvard mentioned me," Tam repeated, and shook the hand. Dry grip, no calluses. A man who'd never held a spade or a line. "Funny. He didn't mention you."

"I was engaged after your last report went in. The flood changed the whole survey picture — sediment loads, channel migration. They want the lower reach mapped before the site's underwater again." Vey smiled. "I'm told I have until the river rises."

"You have until it rises," Tam agreed. "Which is soon."

"Then I'd best get started." Vey looked past him at the camp, at the tents, at the supply line, and Tam watched his eyes move the way an assessor's eyes move — itemizing. They paused, just barely, just for a breath, on the supply tent. On the shadow behind it.

Tam felt the hair on his arms lift.

"Montrose around?" Vey asked.

"Out on the cut. I'll tell her you're here."

"I'll introduce myself, if it's all the same." And Vey walked into the camp like a man walking into a room he'd been given the key to.

Tam stood in the mud and watched him go, and did the arithmetic he'd learned to do young: a consortium six weeks behind on wages did not spend money sending a soft-handed stranger four miles up a levee to look at sediment. Sediment didn't have a shadow behind a supply tent.

He went to find Lior.

* * *

Lior heard Tam out with her arms crossed, watching Vey across the camp — the man had set up a surveyor's tripod with the brisk competence of someone who had indeed been shown a theodolite once, and was taking bearings on the channel with great industry.

"Hydrologist," she said.

"That's what he says."

"You believe him?"

"I believe he knows which end of the instrument points at the water." Tam's voice was flat. "I don't believe that's why he's here. He looked at the supply tent, Lior. Two seconds. The kind of look a man gives when he already knows what's behind it."

Her stomach dropped through the mud and kept going. She made her face do nothing, which was the one skill her mother's life had taught her well.

"Then he's a thief casing the camp," she said. "Which is what the watches are for."

"Sure." Tam looked at her for a long moment. "You want to tell me what's really in that chest?"

The question hung there. She could feel the whole camp behind it — Dessa mending net, Ivet hauling stakes, the fire, the river — all of it balanced on what she said next, and every true answer she owned was a door she couldn't close again. *My mother's seal. A country that never existed. Proof that the people paying our wages killed her, or paid whoever did.*

"Salvage," she said. "Documents. Old ones. The consortium will want them catalogued and I'd rather they didn't rot in the wet before the assessor comes."

It was a bad lie. She could hear it being a bad lie. Tam heard it too; something in his face went quiet and careful, the way it did when a footing gave way under him and he shifted his weight without comment.

"Right," he said. "Documents."

"Tam—"

"You don't owe me the truth." He said it simply, without heat, which was somehow worse. "You pay me, I keep the camp standing. That's the whole contract. But you've got a stranger with clean boots counting our tents, wages six weeks late, a river coming back in three days, and you're not sleeping. Whatever you're not telling me — decide soon whether I'm standing in front of it or behind you. Because right now I don't know which way to face."

He walked off toward the cut, and Lior stood alone with the tripod man's industry and the river's rising voice, and understood that she had perhaps a day, maybe two, before the lie stopped being hers alone — before it either came apart in her hands or dragged every person in this camp into the water with it.

Across the camp, Vey lowered his theodolite, and — quickly, smoothly, thinking no one was watching — turned the lens away from the channel and toward the supply tent.

And took a photograph.
