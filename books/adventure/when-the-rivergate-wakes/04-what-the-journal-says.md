## 4. What the Journal Says

The journal smelled of river and iron, the way old paper does when it has been drowned once and dried badly. Lior sat in her tent with the flap tied shut, a lamp turned low, and did not touch it for a long time. Opening it felt like speaking to the dead, and she had never been good at eulogies.

Outside, the Rivergate ran low and quiet over its exposed bones—sandbars that hadn't seen air in forty years, the ribs of an old barge, the black seam of clay where the chest had lain. The crew was asleep. Tam had taken first watch without being asked, which was its own kind of question.

She opened the cover.

The handwriting hit her before the words did. That slanted, impatient script, letters leaning forward like a woman always about to stand up. Lior had spent ten years not thinking about it, and here it was anyway, filling the page, alive as a voice.

*Day 9. The consortium's survey instruments are better than their manners. Halvard wants depth soundings along the old cut. I want to know why a hydrology contract needs gravimetric readings. Nobody pays for gravity unless they're looking for something heavier than water.*

Lior laughed once, a short ugly sound, and pressed her hand to her mouth.

Her mother. Reading her mother argue with Halvard across twenty years of paper.

* * *

*Day 14. Found it.*

*There is a void under the riverbed at the old cut. Not a pocket—a structure. Vaulted. Dressed stone, pre-Imperial coursing, which is impossible because nothing pre-Imperial exists this far north except in the maps they burned. I sent two of the crew down through the fissure with lamps. They came up gray-faced and would not say what they saw, only that there were shelves. Rows of them. And tablets, or books, or something between, wrapped in waxed linen and sealed with pitch.*

*I know what this is. God help me, I know what this is.*

*Vessany.*

*They will tell me I'm wrong. Vessany is a fable, a cartographer's joke, a name that appears in no treaty, no census, no registry, because the treaties say it never existed. But stone doesn't lie about its age, and neither do archives. Someone built a room under the river and filled it with everything a country was, and then someone else drowned the country and called it geology.*

Lior read that entry three times. The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick with fingers that were not quite steady.

She had grown up on the edges of this story without knowing it. A mother who went away for seasons and came back thinner, quieter, smelling of mud and lamp oil. A mother who once, drunk on plum brandy, drew a coastline on the frosted window and said, *there used to be more world than this,* and wiped it away before dawn. Lior had thought it grief for her father. It had been cartography.

* * *

*Day 16. Halvard knows.*

*He didn't ask me directly. He asked the crew, one at a time, in his tent, with wine. He asked me at dinner whether I believed nations could be "administratively retired." He used the word retired. Like a clerk. Like a man retiring a horse.*

*I told him the void is a karst formation. He smiled at me the way you smile at someone whose arithmetic you've already checked.*

*The consortium's second vessel arrived tonight. It carries no drilling equipment. It carries men with short hair and clean boots, and crates too heavy to be survey gear. I have seen this before, in other countries, other rivers. This is what it looks like when a thing is about to be erased twice.*

Lior set the journal down. Her hands were cold despite the warm night, and the river outside made its low continuous sound, patient as breathing.

She had spent a decade telling herself her mother died in an accident—a flood, a collapse, bad luck in bad country. The official report said so. The consortium had paid for the funeral, generously, which Lior had taken at nineteen for kindness.

Now she read:

*Day 17. I have made my choice. Tonight we sink the entrance. Charges are set low, in the clay seam, enough to bring the overburden down and seal the fissure. The archive will survive—it has survived worse than us—but no one will reach it by this door.*

*Halvard will call it negligence. Let him. Let him dock my fee, blacklist my name, whatever he likes. Some things should not be found until the finding can be survived.*

*If anyone reads this: I am not destroying history. I am refusing to hand a living people's memory to men who retire nations. There is a difference, and someday, I hope, someone will understand it.*

*—M. Zhou*

Lior closed the journal and sat in the dark with her eyes open.

*Someone will understand it.* Her mother had written that like a letter, and Lior had received it twenty years late, sitting in a tent on the same river, holding the same man's contract in her pack.

She was living her mother's last choice in reverse. Her mother had buried the truth to keep it from them; Lior had dug it up and brought it into camp, into the light, into the reach of Halvard's clean-booted men. Every hour the chest sat in her tent, she was unburying what her mother had died—had *been killed*, she understood that now, plainly, the way you understand arithmetic—to keep hidden.

And yet.

Her mother had also written *someday*. Had sealed the door but kept the journal. Had sunk the archive but saved the testimony. Even in the act of refusal, she had left a door open a crack, and Lior was standing in it.

Or she was standing in a trap that looked like a door. She honestly could not tell anymore.

* * *

She was still awake when the shouting started—not alarm-shouting, but Tam's voice pitched low and hard, the way he spoke when he wanted a man to hear him without waking the others.

Lior came out of the tent into gray false-dawn. Mist stood on the river in columns. Near the survey bench, Tam had the stranger—the hydrologist, Serevan, the quiet one with the consortium's laminated credentials—by the strap of his pack, and the pack was open, and the camera inside was open too, its back swung wide to the light.

Tam wasn't a man who lost his temper. He was a man who got precise. "Explain the film," he said.

Serevan did not struggle. He stood very still in the mist, hands slightly out from his body, a man accustomed to being held. "Site documentation," he said. "Standard practice."

"Standard practice at four in the morning?"

"I work early."

Lior crossed the wet grass and took the camera from the pack herself. The film spool told the story without words. Their trench lines. The chest's recovery site, marked with a stake. Her own tent—she recognized the guy-lines—and, most damning, a long-lens frame of the chest itself through the gap where her flap didn't quite close. He had photographed her sleeping thirty feet from the thing Halvard had offered triple pay to pretend didn't exist.

She held the frame up so he could see it. "This one's not hydrology."

"No," Serevan said. Calm. Almost kind. "It isn't."

The mist moved behind him. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine—an outboard, running slow, running dark, the kind of sound a supply run doesn't make at dawn.

Tam looked at Lior. "How many boats came with him?"

"One," Lior said. "One boat, one man." She watched the mist where the engine sound faded. "That's what worries me."

Serevan watched her too, and there was something in his face beyond composure—something almost like appraisal, like a man checking a lock to see if it might hold. "You should know," he said quietly, "that the consortium's position on this site changed yesterday. You may have until the river rises. After that, decisions get made for you."

"The river rises in four days," Tam said.

"Yes," Serevan agreed. "Four days."

Lior folded the film spool into her pocket, next to the journal, next to the bronze seal with its erased nation's mark, and felt the weight of all of it together—her mother's handwriting, a stranger's lens, a countdown measured in water.

Her mother had chosen to bury it. Lior looked out at the low brown river, at the exposed bones of the old cut, at the mist hiding whatever was coming up it, and understood that her own choice had just gotten smaller.

It was no longer whether to bury the past or claim it.

It was how fast she could move her people out from under it.
