## 5. Halvard's Arithmetic

Tam Voss had kept ledgers for men who thought in numbers before he'd ever swung a mattock for a living, and he knew the sound a man made when he was doing sums that included people.

It was a particular quiet. Not the quiet of a man thinking about gravel tonnage or diesel costs—that quiet had a mutter to it, lips moving, pencil scratching. This was stiller. This was a man looking at something across the fire and weighing it the way you'd weigh a cracked pump head: repairable or replaceable, and what either option cost against what it saved.

Halvard stood at the edge of the camp on the third night after the chest came up, hands folded behind his back like a man inspecting a property line, and Tam watched him do his arithmetic from twenty paces away and felt the hair rise along his forearms.

The camp itself had gone strange in those three days. Lior had always run her digs loose—jokes at the sieves, Ivet singing off-key into the wind, someone's dog-eared novel passed hand to hand at supper. Now she moved through the site like weather, present everywhere, explaining nothing. Night watches appeared on the board in her own handwriting, rotations nobody had asked for, and when Rennick grumbled about losing sleep over mud, Lior just looked at him until he stopped. Tam had worked under her long enough to read her silences, and these silences said: *don't ask yet.* But three days was a long time to hold a crew together on *yet* alone.

And Halvard had arrived the morning after the chest, unannounced, upriver by launch with two men who never took off their coats and never seemed to sweat despite the wet heat pressing down on the floodplain like a thumb. He'd smiled at everyone. He'd praised the stratigraphy. He'd asked Lior, twice, in a voice pitched for pleasantness but carrying anyway, whether she'd found anything "of interest" in the river's leavings.

Tam hadn't heard her answer. He'd been too far off. But he'd seen Halvard's face afterward, and it had worn the expression of a man who had received an answer he already knew.

Now the man stood counting his crew the way a butcher counts stock.

There were eleven of them left on site after the pay dispute sent four home. Eleven, plus the hydrologist—the consortium's man, Corvel, soft-spoken, precise, forever taking water samples nobody needed taken. Tam didn't trust the way Corvel held his instruments, reverent as relics, and he trusted even less the way Corvel's eyes went to Lior whenever she crossed the camp, tracking her the way a hawk tracks not prey exactly but pattern, learning her habits so they could be predicted.

Eleven crew. Two of Halvard's coat-men. One hydrologist. One employer doing sums.

Tam spat into the mud and went to check the radio.

* * *

He found the problem at dusk, and it was not a storm.

The radio sat in its crate under the survey tent's awning, and Tam had babied that set for six years—dried it after every rain, kept the contacts greased, wrapped the aerial joint in waxed cloth because the damp here ate copper like rot eats timber. When he lifted the housing flap and ran his lamp along the wiring, he saw the cut immediately. Clean shear, one pass, made with a blade sharp enough that the copper ends gleamed bright instead of frayed. Storm damage tore wire. Storm damage shredded insulation and left a mess a man could read like a wound.

This was surgery.

Tam crouched there a long moment, lamp cupped low, listening to the camp behind him. The generator thudded its steady heartbeat. Someone laughed—Ivet, probably, arguing about cards. The river muttered past in the dark, still running high and brown with the floods' leftovers, though lower every day now, dropping toward whatever new channel the water meant to carve.

He checked the spare aerial lead in the equipment box. Also cut. Also clean. Both lines, severed within hours of each other, while half the camp slept and the other half watched the wrong horizon.

Storm season, Halvard would say. Storm season takes the lines, storm season is unpredictable, best wait it out, best stay put. Tam could hear the words already, smooth as poured concrete, ready to be laid over the truth before anyone could dig beneath them.

He stood, closed the housing gently, and walked the perimeter once with his lamp hooded, telling himself he wasn't looking for anything, knowing perfectly well what he was looking for. At the north edge of camp, where the ground rose toward the old levee, he found boot prints—not his, not any of the crew's patterns he knew by sole—and beside them, pressed into the silt where someone had knelt, the round ghost of a blade guard set down carefully, deliberately, by a man who respected his tools.

Coat-man work. Or Corvel's. On a site this small, the distinction hardly mattered.

Tam went to find Lior.

She was in her tent, and she wasn't pretending to sleep; the lamp inside threw her shadow against the canvas, bent over something flat on the folding table. He scratched the pole twice, their old signal from the Kessarin dig, and heard her move before he heard her voice.

"Come in."

She looked worse than three days should account for. Not sick—worn, the way stone wears, grain by grain, under water that never stops moving. Something lay on the table under a folded oilcloth, and her hand rested on the oilcloth's corner like a woman keeping a hand on a dog that might bolt.

"Radio's dead," Tam said. "Both leads cut. Clean work, one pass. And there are prints on the north levee that belong to nobody I've hired."

Lior didn't look surprised. That was the worst part. She looked like a woman hearing a diagnosis she'd suspected for weeks.

"When?" she said.

"Dusk, maybe earlier. Whoever did it wanted us deaf before dark." He let that sit between them. "Storm season doesn't use a whetstone, Lior."

"No," she said quietly. "It doesn't."

"So say it plain. What's in that chest? Because I've hauled your gear through three countries and buried two friends we don't talk about, and I have never once asked you for your reasons. But my people are on watches you won't explain, our paymaster is doing arithmetic I can hear from across camp, and now somebody's cut us off from the world and left us sitting on a floodplain that's going to fill back up—" he jerked his chin toward the river's black murmur beyond the canvas, "—inside the week, if the gauge upstream means anything. So I'm asking. Once."

For a moment he thought she'd give him the whole weight of it. Her jaw worked. Her hand tightened on the oilcloth until the knuckles showed pale. Then she exhaled, slow, and some door behind her eyes closed partway—but not all the way, and he clung to that crack of light.

"My mother dug this river country ten years ago," Lior said. "With funding from the same consortium Halvard fronts. The expedition ended badly. She didn't come home." A muscle moved in her cheek. "The chest the river gave up has her mark on it. Her private mark, burned into the lid, which means she handled what's inside, which means what's inside matters enough that she hid it rather than turn it over to the men paying for the dig. Men like Halvard."

"And what's inside?"

"Proof," Lior said. "Of something they want forgotten badly enough to have killed for it once." She met his eyes then, and Tam saw that she was choosing, word by word, how much rope to pay out. "I don't know all of it yet. I know enough to know the radio wasn't cut because of storms. It was cut because Halvard wants this site quiet until the water comes back and does his erasing for him. Equipment loss, he can write off. Weather delay, he can explain to his funders." Her voice dropped, flat and cold as riverbed clay. "People are harder to explain. Unless the river explains them first."

The generator thudded. Somewhere out in the dark, the river kept eating its banks, patient as time.

"You're saying he'd let the flood take us," Tam said. It wasn't a question. He wanted to hear her say it anyway, because a thing said aloud became a thing a man could act on.

"I'm saying he's done the sums," Lior said. "And I'm saying we're going to be gone before he finishes them."

"Gone where? Every road out of this valley runs past his launch."

"Not every road." And there—there it was, the ghost of the old Lior, the one who read soil like scripture and always knew the way down and out. But it flickered and was gone, replaced by something more careful. "I have a route. Old one. It'll hold if we move soon and travel light."

"How soon?"

"The gauge says four days. I say we leave in two."

Tam rubbed his jaw. Two days to break a camp, cache what they couldn't carry, and move eleven people—including Rennick, who complained about a blistered heel like it was a mortal wound—out through country none of them knew, pursued by a man with boats, money, and two hired knives. It was madness dressed in a schedule.

But staying was madness dressed in patience, and patience was the kind that drowned.

"All right," he said. "I'll start the crews caching tomorrow. Quiet-like. And Lior—" he paused at the tent flap, "—the rest of it. Whatever's under that oilcloth. When you're ready to tell me, I'll listen. But don't wait so long you're telling me while we're running."

Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You always did hate learning things on the move."

"It's why I dig holes for a living," Tam said. "Ground stays where you put it."

Outside, the night had thickened, clouds stacking up over the western hills where the storm season gathered its strength. He stood a moment in the dark, breathing the smell of wet earth and diesel and river, and looked toward the survey tent where Halvard's lamp still burned late.

Through the canvas, faintly, he could see the shadow of the man pacing. Three steps, turn. Three steps, turn. Counting.

And downstream, almost too low to hear beneath the generator's thud, the river whispered the only number that mattered anymore: rising, rising, rising.
