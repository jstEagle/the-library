## 6. Night Crossing

The river told me first.

I'd been reading its moods since before I could read anything else—my mother's doing, though I'd spent ten years pretending the skill came from nowhere, from nowhere at all. The Rivergate had dropped a hand's width in two days. The silt lines on the pilings of the old ferry landing climbed higher each morning instead of lower, and the water coming down from the cut had gone the color of weak tea with milk stirred into it. Anyone who knew rivers knew what that meant. The highlands were letting go of their rain all at once, and everything we stood on would be under three meters of moving water by week's end.

Halvard knew it too. He'd stopped walking the site and started standing at the levee crest with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the current like a man watching a clock he couldn't stop. But he wasn't leaving. Leaving meant admitting there was something here worth staying for past the point of safety, and Halvard never admitted anything he could deny instead.

I made my decision on the fourth morning, standing over my mother's journal in the dark of my tent, the pages soft as cloth under my thumbs.

*The old survey route runs north along the limestone shelf,* she had written, in that cramped, slanted hand I'd have known anywhere, even now, even with her eleven years dead. *It stays above flood line to the second channel. If you are reading this and the water is rising, do not take the road. Take the shelf.*

She had written it for someone. Maybe for me, though she hadn't known she had a daughter when she wrote it—or maybe she had, and that was a door I kept closed. Either way, it was the only way out that didn't end at a roadblock or a boat Halvard controlled.

So I called the crew together at the cook fire while the light was still gray and thin, and I lied to them.

"Water's coming up faster than the charts say," I told them. "The consortium's hydrologist agrees—we're moving out tonight, north along the ridge, ahead of the rise. It's a safety call."

Tam looked at me across the fire. Tam always looked at me across fires, patient as ballast, and I felt the lie go through him like a fish through still water—a ripple, nothing more, but he marked it. He'd been marking things for days. The chest. The night watches. The way I flinched whenever Sera Voss, the so-called hydrologist, came within earshot.

Sera Voss. That was the name on the papers, anyway. She sat at the edge of the circle now, notebook open, pen moving, recording our departure plan in a neat hand. Recording it for whoever she actually reported to. I let her. By the time anyone acted on what she wrote, we'd be gone, and the route she recorded would be a lie inside a lie—I gave them the ridge road, not the shelf. Let them chase the wrong high ground.

"We'll need both boats for the second channel," said Ivet, who ran our gear like a religion and was already counting crates in his head. "The punt's got a hairline crack in the port strake. I've been meaning to glass it."

"Glass it today," I said. "Everything glassed, lashed, waterproofed by sundown."

They scattered to it, and I stayed by the dead fire and hated myself efficiently, the way you learn to when lying is load-bearing. It was a safety call. That much was true. Everything else—the journal, the chest, the seal stamped with a country no map admitted to—that part they couldn't carry yet. Not because they were weak. Because the truth was heavy, and we had a channel to cross.

* * *

We broke camp at dusk, and the sabotage happened an hour later, while the last light was draining out of the west and the crew was loading the boats at the flooded cut where the old survey route met the water.

I heard Ivet shout before I understood what he was shouting about. Then I saw it: the punt sitting low in the shallows, listing hard, water sheeting over its gunwale. The crack in the port strake hadn't been hairline anymore. Someone had worked it—worked it patiently, with a blade or a chisel, widening it from inside the bilge where no inspection would catch it until the weight of loaded gear found it. Half our food stores went into the channel in ninety seconds. Rice, oil, the medical kit, two crates of rope. I watched a sack of flour swell and sink like something drowning, and I thought: *she did this while we ate lunch twenty meters away.*

"Sera!" I turned. "Where's Sera?"

"Here." She stepped out of the reeds with her notebook against her chest, wide-eyed, perfectly composed. "What happened?"

"What happened," I said, "is that your survey notes just became worthless, because half our supplies are in the river."

"I was at the instrument station," she said. "Alone. You can check the readings—they're timestamped."

They would be. Of course they would be. She was good. She'd been good for nine days, asking careful questions, taking careful measurements, being exactly helpful enough to be unremarkable. And she had opened that strake with the same patience.

There was no time to prove anything. The river was making noise in the dark now—not the quiet lap of evening water but a low, muscular sound, the sound of a thing waking up. Tam put his hand on my shoulder. "Decide fast, boss. We cross now or we don't cross."

We crossed.

* * *

The second channel at night is a black ribbon with a current in it, and we took it in one overloaded boat and one that shipped water through every seam. No lights—lights would tell Halvard's watchers, if he had any posted, exactly where we were and how many. We went by feel and by the sound of the far bank, which I navigated toward the way my mother's journal had taught me: the shelf threw back voices differently than open marsh, a flat stone echo under the wind.

We were forty meters out when Ivet went in.

He'd been bailing with a tin pan, working the seams, and the boat rolled once—a slow, sickening roll as the current grabbed the stern—and he was simply gone, no splash I could hear over the water-noise, just an absence where his voice had been. Someone screamed his name. The boat swung. I was already reaching for the throw line when a shape left the near side of the channel and knifed into the black water without hesitation, without a word.

Sera Voss.

I lost them both in the dark. Ten seconds. Twenty. The current here ran maybe four knots, cold enough to cramp a swimmer in minutes, and Ivet had been wearing his gear belt, his boots, his coat. I counted in my head the way you count when counting is the only useful thing left, and at thirty-one seconds the far-bank reeds thrashed and Sera came up out of the water dragging him by the collar, hauling him onto the shelf with a strength I would not have credited her, driving the water out of him with her fists until he coughed up half the channel and breathed.

We got everyone across. We lost the punt entirely, and with it the tent canvas and most of the tools. We huddled on the limestone shelf under a sky going hard and starry, shivering around a fire we risked only because hypothermia was the nearer death, and Ivet lay wrapped in every dry garment we owned, gray-faced, alive.

And Sera Voss sat apart from us, soaked to the skin, wringing out her notebook paper one page at a time and laying them on stones to dry, and I walked over and stood above her and waited.

"You went in after him," I said.

"He was drowning."

"You sabotaged the boat."

Her hands stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the river, louder now, talking to itself in the dark. Then she looked up at me, and whatever mask she'd worn for nine days came off all at once, like a bandage pulled clean.

"Yes," she said. "I did."

"Why save him, then? Why defect now?"

"Because I read the file before they gave me the sanitized version." She said it quietly, so only I could hear it over the fire-crackle. "The original expedition. Eleven years ago. Seven people went into the Rivergate country and one came out, and the consortium's own internal review concluded the loss was 'operationally acceptable.' I've worked for them four years. I told myself the archives job was preservation." She picked up one of the wet pages—her own careful handwriting, warped now into something illegible—and looked at it. "Tonight I watched a man almost die for a supply run, and I realized I was sent here to make sure more people died the same way, and to write it down neatly afterward."

"So what are you saying?"

"I'm saying I'm done writing it down for them." She held the ruined page up between us. "You've been lying to your crew since the chest came out of the mud. I've been lying to mine longer. Neither of us gets to pretend anymore—so let's not. Tell them the truth. All of it. Tonight, before the river takes the choice away."

I looked back at my crew. Tam was watching me across the fire—he'd heard none of it, but he'd heard the tone, and Tam heard tones the way I read soil. Ivet lay breathing. The others were watching the water rise against the shelf's edge, foot by foot, patient as arithmetic.

Survival had already cost us the supplies, the boat, the easy version of me they'd signed on with. There was nothing left to protect by lying except myself, and I was tired of carrying her alone.

I crouched by the fire where they could all see me, and I reached into my coat and brought out the bronze seal, and set it on the stone where the firelight could find it.

"My mother found something under this river," I said. "That's why she died. And the people who funded this dig intend for it to stay buried—including anyone who's seen it. So here's the truth, all of it, and then we walk, because we've got maybe two days before this shelf goes under."

Behind me, Sera Voss tore the rest of her notebook pages, one by one, and fed them into the flames.
