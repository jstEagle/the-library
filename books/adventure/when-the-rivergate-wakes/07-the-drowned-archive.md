## 7. The Drowned Archive

The river gave them one more gift before it took everything back.

Lior saw it first from the ridge of the old survey route—a seam of pale stone showing through the mud like a scar on a wrist. Her mother's journal had described it in a hand gone careful and small: *the archway sits below the winter mark; you will only see it when the water forgets itself.* The floods had done exactly that. For perhaps two more days, the entrance to the Vessany archive stood open to the air for the first time in eleven years.

"Is that it?" Tam asked beside her. He had the satchel strap crossed over his chest and his boots already black to the knee. Behind them, the rest of the crew picked their way down the slope—six people and a stranger who still hadn't given them a name that stuck, moving through reeds taller than Ivet's shoulders.

"That's it." Lior's voice came out flatter than she intended. "My mother drew this archway from memory. She never said how well she remembered it."

The stone was older than anything else in the Rivergate country—not floodplain masonry, not levee work, but dressed blocks fitted without mortar, their joints so fine she couldn't slide a knife blade into them. Carved across the lintel ran a line of script she didn't recognize: tall letters with hooks at the base, like herons standing in shallow water. Below them, worn nearly smooth by decades of seasonal drowning, a second inscription in trade-tongue:

*WE WERE HERE. WE CHOSE TO BE REMEMBERED THIS WAY.*

Tam read it twice. "Chose," he said. "That's an odd word for a door."

"It's an odd country," Lior said.

They pried the slab loose with the boat hook and two lengths of oar. The stone came away with a sound like a held breath released, and cold air rose out of the dark beneath—air that smelled of dry stone and lamp oil and something faintly sweet, like paper kept too long in a closed room. Not river water. Not rot. The space below had been sealed against the flood by hands that understood the flood better than anyone now living.

Lior went down first, lamp high.

* * *

The stair was cut into the bedrock, forty-one steps by Tam's count, each one dished in the center by feet that had climbed it long ago. At the bottom, the passage opened into a chamber the size of her mother's old kitchen—smaller than Lior had imagined, humbler—and every wall was shelving.

Not gold. Not weapons. Not the treasure Halvard's consortium had spent a decade and at least three lives hunting.

Shelves. And on the shelves, wrapped in waxed linen and stacked with the tenderness of a librarian rather than the desperation of a fugitive, were books. Ledgers. Rolls of vellum tied with cord. Tin boxes stenciled with the heron script. A child's copybook, its cover soft with handling, set apart on its own shelf as if someone couldn't bear to pack it with the rest.

"It's an archive," Tam said quietly. He'd come down behind her and now stood turning slowly, lamp raised. "A whole country's paperwork."

"A whole country," Lior corrected. "That's what this is. Not records *of* Vessany. Vessany itself."

She unwrapped the nearest roll with hands that wouldn't quite steady. Census ledgers, year over year, names in columns—births, marriages, trades, debts forgiven in flood years. Tax rolls. Grain measures. A court record from a town called Serevan Ford, disputes about a mill weir, settled with a patience that made her throat ache. Maps, dozens of them, drawn in the same hooked script and annotated in trade-tongue by a second hand: *here the ferry ran before the treaty; here the school; here the bell foundry.*

And then the last document, laid on top of everything like a hand resting on a finished piece of work. It was written in trade-tongue deliberately, addressed to no one and everyone, signed with a name and a title: *Last Archivist of Vessany.*

Lior read it aloud because Tam needed to hear it and because she could not have read it silently and survived it intact.

*"By the terms of the Treaty of the Three Rivers, Vessany ceases to exist. Our neighbors have agreed that we never did. Our maps will be redrawn; our language will be called a dialect and then nothing; our dead will be buried under other nations' names. This is the price of peace, they say, and they may even believe it. But a country is not its borders. A country is its memory. So I have carried the memory here, below the waterline, where no treaty reaches. Let the river keep what the kings surrendered. Whoever finds this: you are now the nation of Vessany. All of it. Govern it kindly."*

The chamber was silent except for water dripping somewhere far off, patient as a clock.

Tam sat down slowly on the bottom step. "How many people knew?"

"The archivist. Whoever helped carry all this down." Lior touched the child's copybook without opening it. "And my mother."

* * *

Her mother's survey journal was still in the satchel against Lior's hip, and she took it out now because she had to—because the two documents wanted each other, the way evidence wants its confession.

The entries from the original expedition began confident and professional, the handwriting she remembered from grocery lists and margin notes in her schoolbooks. Then, day by day, the entries changed. The pen pressed harder. The sentences shortened.

*Found the archway. Low water won't hold. Halvard's people asking why we're mapping a flood channel instead of the ore seams. Told them sediment study.*

*Went down alone. It's not a tomb. It's not a hoard. It's a country.*

*Halvard has seen the seal impression. His face when he looked at it—I've seen men look at ore bodies that way. He thinks in tonnage. He doesn't understand what he's looking at, and I need him to keep not understanding.*

*They want the site coordinates. I gave them false ones. If the consortium publishes this, Vessany becomes an asset. Assets get extracted. Extracted means sold, means scattered, means a nation dissolved twice—once by treaty and once by auction.*

*I can't stop them finding it. The river will show them eventually; it showed me. But I can choose what they find. I'm going to report the gallery as collapsed. Flooded. Unrecoverable. Let them chase the false coordinates into the hills.*

*If I'm wrong about any of this, forgive me. If I'm right, forgive me anyway.*

The last entry was dated nine days before the accident. Nine days before the expedition's boat went down in weather every other crew on the river had stayed ashore for. Nine days before Lior, nineteen years old and already good at reading soil, learned to read an obituary instead.

"She faked the collapse," Lior said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "She reported the archive destroyed. And they killed her for hiding something they thought was worth—" She stopped. Started again. "They killed her for a lie. They killed her for treasure that didn't exist, because she let them believe it did."

"And it was here the whole time," Tam said. "Under their boats."

"Under everyone's boats. Every flood season, for eleven years, the river has been keeping her secret and giving it back and taking it again." Lior closed the journal. "I spent ten years hating the river for taking her. It was holding the door shut the entire time."

* * *

They worked until the lamps burned low, and they worked carefully, because Tam insisted and because Lior realized halfway through the third shelf that he was right: whatever they decided, they should decide it knowing everything.

The inventory, when Tam scratched it into his notebook, came to four hundred and six items. Census rolls covering ninety years. The full correspondence of the last archivist, including letters begging neighboring scholars to preserve copies—letters whose recipients had evidently chosen safety over memory, because none of the copies surfaced anywhere Lior had ever dug. Schoolbooks. Hymnals. A shipwright's manual. Recipes. Jokes, written down, in a language that officially had no speakers.

"They didn't hide the crown jewels," Tam said, turning a tin box over in his hands. "They hid the *boring things*. The tax rolls. The mill disputes. That's what a country actually is, isn't it? Nobody writes ballads about tax rolls."

"Somebody wrote all of this down," Lior said. "On purpose. Knowing nobody would thank them."

She thought of her mother's last choice, laid out entry by entry: bury the truth, take the blame, die for it if necessary. And she thought of herself standing in Halvard's office a lifetime ago, taking the contract, telling herself the past was a country she'd emigrated from. She had spent a decade doing the opposite of what her mother did—running from responsibility instead of toward it—and the whole time she'd been carrying the same inheritance in her blood without once opening the box.

It wasn't property. That was the thing Halvard would never understand, the thing that made his arithmetic obscene. You couldn't own four hundred and six items and call it a nation. You could only be answerable to them. Her mother had understood that. The archivist had understood it. Somewhere between the stair and the shelves, Lior finally did too.

"We can't move it," Tam said. It wasn't a question; he'd done the same math she had. Four hundred and six items, six exhausted people, a rising river, and armed men somewhere behind them on the route. "Not all of it. Maybe not any of it."

"No." Lior looked around the little room—the copybook on its own shelf, the heron letters standing in their rows like birds at dusk. "But we don't have to choose tonight whether it's destroyed or saved. We have to choose who tells the story. That part comes out with us no matter what."

She took the archivist's testament, her mother's journal, and one census roll—the first year, the founding register, names in columns like a heartbeat—and wrapped them in oilcloth. Everything else went back onto its shelves, waxed linen tucked in, the slab eased back over the entrance until the seam in the world closed again.

Above ground, the light had gone the color of pewter. The reeds were leaning. Downriver, where the sky met the water, there was a line of brown that hadn't been there at dawn.

Tam saw it at the same moment she did. "That's not cloud," he said. "That's water."

The river was coming back. And on the wind off the floodplain, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Lior had been hearing in her nightmares for a week: engines. More than one. Coming up the survey route behind them, between the crew and home.

"Halvard found the false coordinates after all," she said. "Or he followed us. Either way—he's between us and the boats."

Tam was already counting heads, already moving. "Then we'd better hope your mother left more than one way out of her country."
