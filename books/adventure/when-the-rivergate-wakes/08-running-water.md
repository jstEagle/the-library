## 8. Running Water

Tam Voss had spent twenty years reading rivers, and the Rivergate was telling him something he didn't like.

It started with the smell. Mud has a hundred voices and Tam knew most of them—the green sweetness of fresh silt, the mineral bite of clay turned for the first time in a season—but this morning the air carried a different note entirely: cold water from far upstream, snowmelt or a broken sluice somewhere in the high country, moving toward them with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. He stood at the edge of the cut with his boots sinking into ground that had gone soft overnight and watched the channel that had been knee-deep yesterday begin to whisper against its banks.

Behind him, the camp was striking itself down. Canvas folded, crates roped, Ivet's laugh carrying thin across the flat as she argued with Doss about how many lamps they could afford to carry. They'd been packing since before dawn, working off Lior's orders and their own unease, and nobody had asked twice about why. When your boss starts moving you out ahead of schedule through country she's never surveyed, you either ask questions or you pack faster. Tam had done his asking on the levee two nights ago, and what she'd given him—half the truth, wrapped in her mother's handwriting—had been enough to make him pack fastest of all.

The stranger was at his shoulder now, quiet as always. Osk. That was the name he'd finally given them, three days after defecting, when Lior had stopped calling him *the hydrologist* and started calling him nothing at all, which Tam understood to be its own kind of acceptance. He was a lean man with river-country hands and the particular stillness of someone who had spent years being watched.

"Water's coming up faster than she thinks," Osk said.

"She knows."

"Does she know it's not just the melt?" He nodded downstream, toward the distant gray line of the levee works where Halvard's consortium kept its pumps and its men. "They've opened the upper gates. I saw the crews yesterday. They're going to drown this whole reach early—not to drive us out. To bury whatever's left of the archive under six meters of moving water and call it flood damage."

Tam looked at the rising channel and felt the arithmetic of it settle into his bones. Halvard counted losses the way other men counted coins. Equipment: sunk, written off. Site: destroyed, regrettable. People—

"People," Tam said aloud, because Osk was waiting.

"He brought eleven," Osk said. "I counted them loading the boats at first light. Not surveyors' boats. The ones with the shallow draft and the gun mounts stripped off."

* * *

They were halfway through the flooded cut when the shooting started.

The old survey route ran along a shelf of higher ground between the channel and the collapsed terraces of the riverbank—a path Lior's mother had walked thirty years ago and marked in a hand so precise Tam could have followed it blindfolded. It should have taken them two days to the downstream ford. It took them four hours to understand they weren't going to get two days.

The first shot came off the water, flat and cracked, and took a splinter out of the terrace wall a hand's width above Doss's head. Then the second, and after that no one was counting anymore, because counting was a luxury for people who weren't running.

"Off the shelf!" Lior's voice cut through everything, the way it always did—not loud so much as absolutely certain. "Into the cut! Water's waist-deep, move!"

They went. Tam went last, hauling Ivet by the arm as she stumbled, and the moment his boots hit the channel he understood the shape of the trap. Waist-deep meant slow. Slow meant targets. And the boats he'd seen that morning were already sliding up the channel behind them, men crouched along the gunwales with rifles slung, closing the door they'd come through.

Ahead, the cut bent north toward the drowned entrance—the low stone mouth Lior had found exposed by the same receding water that had given them the chest—and beyond it the route climbed toward the ridge and the long walk home. Between them and the bend: four hundred meters of open water, six people, and whatever Halvard's arithmetic said a foreman from the interior was worth.

"They'll herd us to the bend and take us there," Osk said. He'd waded out ahead, and now he turned, walking backward through the current, and there was something in his face Tam hadn't seen before—not fear, but a kind of terrible relief, like a man who had finally been given permission to stop pretending. "There's a spur of rock past the second marker. High ground. If someone draws them while the rest of you make the bend—"

"No," Lior said.

"Somebody has to—"

"I said no." But she was already looking at the spur, already doing the same arithmetic, and Tam watched her do it and hated every number.

* * *

The spur was barely an island—a shoulder of old masonry, some foundation older than the maps, thrust up out of the channel with maybe three meters of dry stone on top. Osk reached it first because Osk was fastest, and when the rest of them swung wide toward the bend he did not follow. He knelt behind the fallen lintel stone, unslung the rifle he'd carried without explanation since the night he'd changed sides, and began to shoot back.

He was good. That was the worst part. Tam, dragging Ivet through water that pulled like living hands, kept glancing back and seeing the muzzle flash steady and unhurried from the spur, seeing the lead boat swing wide, seeing men go flat in the gunwales. Osk wasn't trying to kill them. He was making himself expensive.

"Keep moving," Tam growled, and Ivet, gray-lipped and shivering, kept moving.

Then the third boat came around the bend from the front.

Tam heard Lior swear—a short, ugly word in her mother's language—and saw her look at the satchel strapped across her chest. She'd made copies. Of course she'd made copies; the originals rode in the oilskin roll with the seal, but the journal pages, the testimony, the photographs of the archive walls—she'd copied everything at the ford two nights ago, working by lamplight while the rest of them slept, and the copies rode in a canvas satchel against her ribs because paper could be mailed from anywhere and originals could be lost.

Halvard wanted the paper. All of it. That was the whole war, reduced to its simplest terms.

The crossfire found them at the bend. Bullets stitched the water, and Ivet went down—not shot, just down, her foot finding a hole where the old paving had washed out, the current taking her the way the current takes anything it's offered. Tam lunged and caught her wrist and the river pulled and for one long, clarifying second Tam Voss understood exactly how much a man can hold and exactly how little.

Lior dropped the satchel.

He saw it happen. Saw her hand go to the strap, hesitate for less than a heartbeat—less than it takes to draw breath—and then release it, the canvas sliding off her shoulder into the brown water even as she threw herself flat and got her other hand around Ivet's collar. Together they hauled the girl onto the paving at the bend's elbow, Ivet coughing river water and living, alive, spitting and swearing and alive.

The satchel bobbed once, twice, turning slowly in the eddy.

And then a man from the third boat leaned out with a boathook and took it out of the water as neatly as if he'd been handed it.

"No—" Lior was up, wading, and Tam caught her around the shoulders and held her because holding her was the only thing left to hold. "That's not—that's copies, they're only copies, let me—"

"They're only copies," Tam said into her ear. "Say it. They're only copies."

She stopped fighting him. Stood in the waist-deep water shaking, watching the boat pull away with the satchel dripping from its hook, and Tam felt the exact moment the grief went through her and came out the other side as something harder.

On the spur, Osk fired until he couldn't fire anymore, and then he swam.

They pulled him out at the bend with a bullet through his left side, high, below the shoulder blade—clean through, the old dredgers among them said, which was the best kind of bad news there was. He was conscious, which was worse for him, and white to the lips, and when Lior knelt over him in the mud he caught her wrist with a blood-slick hand.

"The originals," he rasped.

"Safe." Her voice didn't shake. "You're bleeding on my mother's route, Osk. You'll bleed on all of it if you want."

Something that might have been a laugh cost him a rib's worth of pain. "Worth it," he said. "Tell me it was worth it."

Lior looked back once, downstream, at the boats and the rising water and the man leaning over the gunwale with a wet canvas bag in his hands. Halvard hadn't come himself. Halvard never came himself. Somewhere behind those boats a man was opening her mother's copied words with dry fingers, reading the testimony of a nation erased, and learning nothing that would save him.

"It was worth it," Lior said. "We keep the originals. We keep each other. Everything else is just paper."

Then the river spoke—the deep, grinding voice of the upper gates opening wide somewhere in the dark of the high country—and the water around their knees lifted, all at once, like something waking up.

"Move," Tam said. "All of you. Now. Before this whole cut becomes the river again."

They moved—six of them, one bleeding, climbing the ridge trail as the flooded cut filled beneath them, and far behind, on the last high point of the levee road, Tam saw a single figure standing in the rain watching them go, small and patient as arithmetic.

Halvard had the copies.

But tomorrow, Tam thought grimly, tightening his grip on Ivet's arm as they climbed, the papers would be in three newspaper offices, and the river would be over the site, and Halvard would learn what Lior Montrose had learned standing in the water with her crew alive around her:

Some things you cannot buy back once they are told.

The levee was close now. And Lior had one card left to play.
