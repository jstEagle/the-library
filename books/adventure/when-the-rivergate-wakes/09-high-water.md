## 9. High Water

The levee at Corren's Bend was built by men who trusted the river to stay reasonable, and the river had spent the last three days proving them fools.

Lior stood on its spine with the water climbing her boots' laces and Halvard's men spread along the crest like crows deciding whether a thing was carrion yet. Behind her, downstream, the channel they'd crossed at night had become a brown sea, chewing at the pilings of the old ferry landing. Ahead, upstream, the site itself—the dig, the tents, the collapsed shaft where the Vessany archive had breathed its last dry air—was already an archipelago of rooftops and sandbags dissolving into current.

The rain came sideways, fine as needle-spray, needling everything. It had been raining since before dawn, and it would rain until the river finished what it had started.

Halvard walked toward her through it without hurrying, which she had learned was his way of telling you he believed time belonged to him. His coat was dry. She never did learn how he managed that.

"Montrose." He stopped six paces off, close enough to shout over the water and far enough to be polite about it. "You've cost me a season."

"You've cost me more than that," Lior said. "You just haven't paid yet."

His men shifted. She counted them without moving her eyes—four on the levee, two more down at the boats, one holding the horses under the dead willow. Seven. Tam was somewhere behind her with Ivet and the stranger, Vess, whose real name was still a wound none of them had asked her to open. Tam had a boat hook and no illusions. That was the whole of their armament.

"Here's what happens now," Halvard said, in the tone of a man reading a ledger aloud. "You give me the satchel. The copies, the journal pages, whatever your mother left in your keeping. In exchange, my people carry yours out. All of them. Even the one bleeding."

Vess was back at the tree line with a field dressing on her shoulder and a pistol she'd taken off one of Halvard's own men, which Lior suspected was the only reason negotiations were happening at all.

"And if I don't?"

"Then the river does my arithmetic for me." He glanced downstream, almost fondly. "I've written off worse assets than this crew. Equipment, mostly. People only when they insist on being difficult."

*People only when necessary.* Tam had told her that, days ago, in the dark of the cut line, and she'd thought it was paranoia wearing a foreman's face. It was just arithmetic. It had always been arithmetic with him.

"The satchel's gone," Lior said.

Something moved behind Halvard's eyes—not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "Dropped in the channel," he said. "My man pulled it out."

"He pulled out *a* satchel." She let that sit. Let him do the math she wanted him doing. "Paper doesn't survive long in Rivergate water, Mr. Halvard. You know that better than anyone. You've been drowning things for years."

The rain thickened. Downstream, something large gave way—the old boathouse, maybe—and the sound rolled up the levee like distant thunder that had decided to come closer.

"You mailed nothing," Halvard said quietly. "You've been running since you were nineteen years old. Running is all you know. People who run don't stop to post letters."

"People who run," Lior said, "learn every post office between here and the coast. Learn which clerks work Sundays. Learn how many days a parcel takes to reach the *Meridian Courier*, the *Gazette*, and the Harrow Street Press." She held up three fingers, let the rain run off them. "Three newspapers, Halvard. Sealed packets. My mother's journal, complete. The survey coordinates. The names of everyone who funded the first expedition—including the signatures on the treaty annex that erased a country from every map so its archives could be quietly owned. Your consortium's founding documents are quoted in those packets. Verbatim."

The wind took her words and shredded some of them, but not the ones that mattered.

"You're lying," he said.

"I lied badly to my own crew for a week because lying is the one inheritance my mother didn't leave me. Ask Tam." She didn't look back, but she felt him there, solid as the levee itself. "Ask the hydrologist you sent to photograph our site. She'll confirm I'm terrible at it."

Halvard stood very still. Rain ran off the brim of his hat in a thin, unbroken thread. And Lior watched the thing happen in his face that she'd watched happen in mirrors during her worst years: the moment a man realizes the ground under him is not ground.

"If those papers print," he said, "your mother's name is dragged through every court on the coast. Her choice to bury the archive becomes a scandal. Is that what she wanted?"

"My mother chose to bury the archive because she thought silence would protect it." Lior's voice cracked on the last word, and she let it. "She died believing that. I'm choosing differently. Not because I'm braver than she was—because I finally understand what she actually found down there. Not treasure. A record. Forty thousand people, a language, a hundred years of ordinary lives, erased by men with ledgers. The last archivist hid it so it couldn't be burned. My mother hid it so it couldn't be sold. And you—" she pointed at him, and her hand shook, and she hated it and used it anyway—"you would have hidden it so it could never be *read*. Three generations of the same grave, Halvard. I'm done digging."

"It won't matter," he said. "Consortiums weather scandals. I've weathered worse."

"Not this one. Because this one has a body count with a name on it, and the name is *Vessany*, and nobody alive can be bribed into forgetting a country they never knew existed. You can't buy back a story once it's loose. You should know—you've spent thirty years trying."

He turned then, finally, and looked downstream at the flood, and Lior understood he wasn't seeing water. He was seeing the packets sitting in three newspaper offices, waiting for Thursday's editions. Seeing thirty years of careful ownership turning into thirty years of evidence.

"The site's gone anyway," he said at last, almost to himself. "By morning there'll be forty feet of water over the shaft. Whatever's down there stays down there. No proof. No archive. Just your word and some ink."

"That's the point," Lior said. "It was never supposed to be kept. It was supposed to be *told*. My mother got that wrong at the end. The archivist got it right at the beginning. Memory doesn't survive in the ground, Halvard. It survives in people. In print. In some clerk's kid in Harrow City reading about a country that vanished and asking their father why. You can't drown that. Believe me. This river is trying its best."

And as if the river had been listening—as if it resented the comparison—it moved.

They all felt it before they saw it: a shudder through the levee's clay spine, deep, tectonic, patient. Then the sound, a groan of old timber somewhere beneath them giving up an argument it had been losing for a century.

Downstream, the flood found the weak seam in the embankment and went through it the way truth goes through a lie—all at once, and then everywhere. Water burst across the low fields in a broad brown fan, taking a fence, taking a shed, taking the road they'd meant to leave by, and the roar of it swallowed every other sound on the levee.

Halvard's men broke first. Crows, after all. Two of them ran for the horses; one simply stood staring at the fields becoming sea; the one at the boats was shouting something no one would ever hear.

Lior grabbed Tam's sleeve. "The high track. Behind the ridge—we go *now*, before it cuts us off."

Tam was already moving, already shouting Ivet's name back toward the trees. But Halvard hadn't moved. He stood on the trembling levee with the flood tearing his empire apart below him, and he looked at Lior with an expression she would keep for the rest of her life—not hatred, not defeat. Recognition. The look of a man meeting, at last, the daughter of the woman he'd failed to bury properly twice.

"Your mother said something like that," he shouted over the water. "Right there. On the bank. Before the first expedition came apart."

The world narrowed to that sentence, to the rain, to the roar.

"What else did she say?" Lior screamed back.

But the levee answered for him. It shrugged, sagged, and the section under Halvard's feet slid—a slow, terrible liquefaction, clay and history flowing downhill together—and Lior lunged and caught his wrist and held on, boots skidding in the streaming mud, Tam's arms locking around her waist from behind, the three of them a chain against the pull of the water.

For one suspended moment Halvard hung above the flood, held by the woman he'd come to ruin, and his free hand scrabbled at the mud and found nothing, and his eyes went from hers to the water to hers again.

"Let go," he said. "Save yourself. It's the only arithmetic left."

"I'm bad at arithmetic," Lior gasped. "I told you."

She hauled. Tam hauled. And Halvard Montrose's opposite in every ledger came up over the levee's broken lip on his belly, coughing river water, coat ruined at last, while behind them the flood poured through the breach and buried the shaft, the archive, the seal-marks in the stone, forty feet deep by morning—every page her mother had hidden, going home to the country that had never existed, kept now by the only guardian that had never taken a bribe.

They lay in the mud on the landward slope, four strangers bound by nothing but survival, while the river took the rest.

Halvard vomited water and laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound. "The packets," he rasped. "There are no packets. There never were."

Lior rolled onto her back in the rain and looked at the grey sky, and smiled for the first time in ten years.

"Thursday," she said. "Buy a paper."

* * *

They walked out through the hills that night, seven of them and one prisoner they hadn't decided what to do with yet, following the old survey route north while the floodplain behind them became a new sea with a drowned country at the bottom of it. At dawn they crested the ridge, and Vess—who had a smuggler's habit of counting exits—looked back once and said:

"There's a telegraph office in Marrow Ford. We'll beat the papers there by two days."

Lior adjusted the strap of her pack, where her mother's bronze seal rode against her spine, warm from her body.

"Then we'd better decide," she said, "what we're going to tell them when we get home."
