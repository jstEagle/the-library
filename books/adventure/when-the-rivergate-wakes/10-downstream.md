## 10. Downstream

The town was called Ferrow's Bend, and it had nothing to recommend it except the river.

That was, Lior decided on her third morning there, rather a lot to recommend it.

The water ran wide and unhurried past the eastern edge of town, brown-gold in the low sun, carrying leaves and the occasional startled duck downstream with an air of having nowhere particular to be. It was not a dramatic river. It did not flood with the violence of the Rivergate or carve gorges or drown archives. It simply moved, day after day, the way a good sentence moves—clearly, without hurry, toward somewhere worth arriving.

She had rented a room above the chandler's shop on Mill Street. The landlady, a widow named Ostrey with forearms like dock rope, had asked only two questions: could she pay monthly, and did she play music after dark. Lior had answered yes and no, and that was the whole of the interview. No one in Ferrow's Bend knew her name. For the first time in ten years, she found she didn't mind if they learned it.

Her bags sat open on the floor of the room for six days before she unpacked them.

She told herself it was habit. Ten years of living out of canvas taught you to keep everything where you could grab it—the boots by the door, the coat over the chair, the small tin of essentials within arm's reach of wherever you slept. She had left towns at midnight for less reason than a bad feeling. She had once walked out of a boarding house in Kesselmark because the landlady's nephew asked too politely about her next contract. Movement was safety. Stillness was a target painted on your back.

But on the seventh morning she woke to rain on the window and the smell of bread from the bakery below, and she heard herself think, with a clarity that felt almost like grief: *no one is coming.*

Not Halvard. Not his men. Not the consortium's quiet professionals with their quiet questions.

She unpacked the bags.

* * *

The story had run in all three papers.

It ran first in the *Meridian Courier* under a headline Lior would have found absurd if it hadn't been true: THE NATION THAT NEVER WAS. Then the *Kesselmark Gazette* picked it up, then the coastal sheets, then—inevitably, hungrily—the wire services. The journal testimony did what testimony does: it made the abstract unbearable. A country of two million people, erased by treaty in a single winter, its name struck from maps its own cartographers had drawn, its archive sealed under a riverbed by the last archivist who refused to let the erasure be total. Her mother had been twenty-six when she'd stood in that flooded entrance and chosen burial over report. Lior had been four.

The consortium unraveled the way such things do—not loudly, not with arrests, but with withdrawals. Two universities pulled their names. The Meridian Trust dissolved itself into three smaller trusts with cleaner letterheads. Halvard himself issued a statement through counsel expressing deep concern about historical misrepresentation, which was lawyer for *I was never here*, and then he actually was never here. Tam read the statement aloud at the table in Ostrey's kitchen one evening, in a fair imitation of Halvard's dry cadence, and Ivet laughed so hard she knocked her tea into the sugar bowl, and for a while nobody said anything serious at all.

They had scattered, her crew, the way crews do. Ivet had gone home to her sister's farm with a share large enough to buy out the mortgage and a new habit of checking river gauges obsessively. Doss took a contract surveying harbors upcoast. Tam—

Tam stayed two weeks longer than he needed to, helping her sort the camp records, and then one morning at the ferry landing he'd shaken her hand like a man closing a business deal and said, "You know where I am," which from Tam was a speech. He sent a postcard every week or so now. They were always the same: a picture of some bridge or lock, and beneath it, in his square engineer's hand, a single line. *Water's high here.* Or: *Water's low. Fine either way.* It took her three postcards to understand he was asking how she was, and one more to understand that answering honestly was the whole point of him.

* * *

Of everything that had come out of the chest—and later out of the drowned archive itself, in the frantic hours before the river rose and took the site back—Lior kept only the seal.

The lawyers and archivists had wanted everything else: the journal, the survey plates, the Vessany documents with their beautiful doomed script. She gave it all gladly. That was the point, she understood now; that was the entire inheritance. Her mother had kept the truth locked inside herself, carried it alone, died of the weight of it. Lior had done the same thing for ten years without knowing what she carried, and it had nearly killed her too, out on the levee with the water climbing and Halvard's arithmetic running behind her eyes. A secret held is a debt owed to everyone who might have needed it. The moment the newspapers printed it, the debt discharged. The truth lived outside her now, in print shops and library microfilm and the arguments of historians who would still be fighting about Vessany when everyone in this story was dust.

So she burned nothing. There was nothing left that needed burning.

Except the seal, which she kept, because it was hers—not the nation's, not the archive's, not history's. Her mother's private mark, pressed into wax and wood and, once, into the lid of a chest that waited eleven years under silt for a daughter who read soil better than anyone alive. It was bronze, the size of a plum, worn smooth on one side where a thumb had held it thousands of times. On the face, the mark itself: a river bending around a hill, cut with a single vertical line, like a needle through cloth.

Some nights she held it the way her mother must have held it, thumb finding the worn place as if the metal remembered. She did not press it into anything. There was nothing left to seal. But she liked the weight of it, the way it made her hand feel like someone else's hand—a younger woman's, standing in a flooded doorway, deciding what kind of ghost to be.

* * *

She took work, eventually, because hands idle too long start inventing trouble. The chandler's widow mentioned that the town council had been arguing for a decade about the old millrace—silted up, half-collapsed, a nuisance and a hazard—and that what they needed was someone who could read the ground and tell them whether fixing it would cost a fortune or two fortunes.

"They won't pay much," Ostrey warned, counting change for a fisherman. "Council's cheap as river gravel."

"I'm not expensive," Lior said, and discovered it was true. She had stopped pricing herself by how fast she needed to disappear.

The millrace work was slow and pleasant. She spent her days wading the shallows with a probe rod, mapping the old stonework, teaching the council's reluctant clerk—which turned out to be a sharp-eyed girl of nineteen named Pell, who had opinions about drainage that could power a small city—how to tell flood silt from ordinary deposit by color and grain. In the evenings she wrote. Not testimony this time, not evidence. Stories. Adventure stories, the kind she'd devoured as a child before she learned that adventure was mostly wet socks and arithmetic about other people's lives. She wrote about rivers and crossings and people who chose each other over treasure. She was not sure they were good. She was sure she would finish them.

On a Sunday in late autumn, she walked out along the bend road to where the river widened, and stood on the bank doing nothing at all.

The light came down long and amber across the water. A barge moved upstream, slow as weather, its wake folding against the banks in soft bronze ridges. Somewhere behind her, the bakery, the chandler's shop, the room with her books finally on shelves and her mother's seal in the drawer of the writing desk—not hidden, just put away, the way you put away a tool you intend to use again. Downstream, the water went on toward the sea, carrying whatever it carried, keeping what it kept.

She thought about her mother, and for the first time the thought did not arrive like a wound. It arrived like a hand on the shoulder. *You read the soil well,* it seemed to say. *Now read this.*

Ten years she had run, and she had believed she was running from the legacy. But legacies are not chests, she understood now. They are not seals or journals or secrets sewn into the lining of a life. A legacy is a choice, offered again in a new century, to new hands. Her mother had chosen silence and paid for it. Lior had chosen speech and paid less, thanks to a foreman who confronted her, a stranger who defected, a crew that carried her lie until it became their truth. The inheritance had never been the archive. It was the choosing.

The barge sounded its horn, far off, cheerful and meaningless. Lior stayed on the bank until the light went copper, then rose, brushed the grass from her knees, and walked back into town along the river she liked, at the pace of a woman who knows exactly where she will sleep tonight, and tomorrow night, and the night after that.

At the foot of Mill Street, the council clerk Pell was waiting by the lamppost, breath steaming, a rolled survey sheet under one arm and an expression that promised trouble of the best kind.

"Montrose," she said. "You'll want to see what the dredging crew pulled out of the millrace this morning."

Lior looked at the girl, at the roll of paper, at the river glinting behind her like a held breath—and felt something in her chest turn over, not dread this time, not quite. Curiosity, maybe. Or its older, wilder name.

"All right," she said. "Show me."
