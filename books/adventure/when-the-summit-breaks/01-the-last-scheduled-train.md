## 1. The Last Scheduled Train

The rule was simple, and Mara Yarrow had learned it the hard way, which was the only way rules ever stuck: never stay past the permit date. A line lived and died by its paperwork, and when the paperwork died, everything after it — the crews, the paychecks, the law itself — went soft and strange, like milk left out in a warm cab.

Mara had a gift for arriving just before the milk turned.

The Kettle Range spur was supposed to have six weeks left on its inspection permit. That was what the paper said, anyway, folded into Mara's breast pocket behind a laminated card that read **M. HALLORAN — TRACK INSPECTOR, GRADE II** in official blue ink. The name Halloran belonged to a man who had retired to the coast eleven years ago and, as far as anyone knew, had never once looked back at the mountains. Mara had borrowed it the way you borrow an umbrella from a stand: without malice, with every intention of returning it someday to a world that wouldn't miss it.

So far the world hadn't asked.

The speeder rattled along the old Kettle spur at a dignified eight miles an hour, which was four more than the track deserved. Below the rails the Kestrel River ran high and green with snowmelt, loud enough that Mara had stopped trying to think over it somewhere around mile nine. Above, the pines stood in ranks like an audience waiting for something to go wrong.

Something usually did.

That was the other thing about dead and dying lines: they attracted people who preferred not to be found. Section hands with names that changed depending on the county. Welders paid in cash and apologies. Inspectors who inspected nothing. Mara had spent six years drifting down the spine of the mountains from one closed line to the next, always hired, never quite employed, always gone before anyone thought to ask for two forms of identification. It was a living. It was barely a living. But it was quiet, and quiet was worth more than money once you'd had your fill of the alternative.

Mara did not think about the bridge. Mara thought about the bridge approximately forty times a day, but never on purpose, and never while anyone could see.

* * *

The last crew of the Kettle spur consisted of three men and a dog, and by the time Mara reached the maintenance camp at Miller's Siding, the crew was down to one man and the dog.

"Where's Deke?" Mara asked, climbing off the speeder. "And the kid?"

"Gone." Otis Marsh was a section hand of about sixty years who had been planning to retire for roughly thirty of them. He sat on the porch of the bunkhouse shelling sunflower seeds into a coffee can with the patience of a man who had made peace with the fact that nothing he did mattered very much. The dog, a brindled thing named Turnip, thumped her tail twice out of politeness. "Deke got word his brother's sick down in Corridor. Took the morning freight. Kid went with him."

"The morning freight." Mara looked up the line, north, where the mountains were already doing their evening trick of turning purple and pretending to be permanent. "There hasn't been a scheduled freight on this line since March."

"Sure there has." Otis spat a shell into the can. "Just nobody told the schedule."

Mara let that sit. There was a particular kind of arithmetic you learned out here, the kind they didn't teach in engineering school. A crew of three becomes a crew of one. A weekly payroll becomes a promise. A permit expires on the fifteenth, except when the company decides it expired on the first, and no one sends a letter because letters cost money and the whole point of letting a line die quietly is that nobody has to sign anything.

"When's the last run?" Mara asked.

"Friday. Company's sending a locomotive up from the junction to pull the equipment out. Anything still on the spur Friday night stays on the spur forever." Otis shrugged, which for Otis was a whole speech. "You staying till then, Inspector?"

"I'm staying till Thursday," Mara said. "Thursday evening. Before dark."

Otis nodded like this was the most sensible thing he'd heard all year, because it was. Never stay past the permit date. Never be the last name on a manifest. Never sleep in a place where the company knows exactly where you are and has decided, on paper, that you aren't.

"You want supper?" Otis asked. "I got beans. I got beans the way other men got regrets."

"That's a lot of beans."

"It's been a lot of weeks."

They ate on the porch while the light went gold and then gray, and Turnip worked the space between them with the focused professionalism of a creature who had done this exact maneuver at every meal of her life. Otis talked about his knees. He talked about his brother-in-law who ran a hardware store in Corridor and kept offering him a job, and how Otis kept almost taking it, and how there was something in him that couldn't quite believe a store would still be there in the morning, that anything would. Mara listened the way good listeners do, which is mostly by eating beans and occasionally saying *mm*, and felt the familiar ache settle in under the ribs — the one that showed up whenever a camp started feeling like a place instead of a stop.

That was the danger. Not the contractors, not the company men, not even the law, such as it was out here. The danger was catching yourself thinking *I could stay*. Because places didn't hold you. Places just held your shape for a while after you left, the way a chair holds warmth, and then they cooled, and someone else sat down.

"So what do you inspect, anyway?" Otis asked, around bean number four hundred. "Being honest."

"Honestly? I walk the line. I look at things. I write down that I looked at them." Mara tipped the tin cup of coffee Otis had poured — it tasted like it had been poured during a previous administration — and gestured up the valley. "Ties, culverts, the retaining wall at mile fourteen. Somebody at the company wants a report saying the spur's too far gone to save, so they can abandon it without anybody asking why they let it get that way. I'm the somebody who writes it."

"And do you?"

"Do I what?"

"Say it's too far gone."

Mara considered the question honestly, which was more than the job required. "It is too far gone," Mara said finally. "The wall at fourteen's bulging like a bad tooth. Culvert at nine's half full of river. You could fix it, if somebody wanted to spend real money, but nobody does, and me writing otherwise wouldn't make them. So yes. I say it's too far gone. And then I leave before anybody starts wondering why an inspector cares."

Otis nodded slowly. "Lonely work."

"It's free work," Mara said, and both of them understood that was the same sentence said backwards.

* * *

Wednesday, Mara walked the line.

This was the part of the job that was actually real, the part Mara would have done even unpaid, if being unpaid were survivable, which it wasn't, which was the whole tragedy of loving anything in this economy. Walking track was the closest thing to prayer Mara had. You went slow. You touched things. You crouched at the joints and put a palm flat on the rail and felt for the hum of a crack coming, the way you'd feel a fever in a child's forehead. The Kettle spur was dying, sure. But it was dying honestly, tie by tie, and there was a dignity in that that Mara had never found in any room where men in clean shirts decided the fates of valleys.

At mile twelve Mara found a deer trail crossing the right-of-way and stood a while watching the river through the trees. The Kestrel ran bigger up here than most folks knew. Most people thought of it as the brown lazy thing that slid past the mill towns downstream. Up here it was all white water and opinion, throwing itself at rocks like it had a grudge. Somewhere up at its head, according to the survey maps Mara had memorized out of boredom, there was country no permit covered at all. Closed since before the war. The maps just said **WITHDRAWN** across the headwaters in red block letters, which was a funny way to describe a river, as if the water had done something wrong.

Mara ate lunch on a rail joint and did not think about bridges, successfully, for nearly four minutes.

* * *

Thursday came in cold and clear, the kind of mountain morning that made you forgive the mountains everything. Mara packed before breakfast — packing took ten minutes when you'd practiced, and Mara had practiced until the practice itself became a kind of home, the one address that never changed. Bedroll. Tool roll. Tin cup. The laminated card with the borrowed name. Everything else was the world's, and could be left.

"You're really going a day early," Otis said. Not a question. He stood on the porch with Turnip sitting against his leg like a co-conspirator.

"Permit says the fifteenth," Mara said. "My rule says the fourteenth. Rules you break for companies, you end up breaking for yourself."

"That ain't a rule, that's a superstition."

"All rules are superstitions that worked once."

Otis laughed, a dry rattle like gravel in a hopper, and pressed something into Mara's hand on the way past: a paper sack, heavy, faintly warm. Beans. Of course beans. Enough beans for a week.

"For the road," he said. "Inspector."

"Mister Halloran thanks you."

"Mister Halloran," Otis said, looking at Mara with those faded eyes that missed less than they let on, "looks tired of being Mister Halloran."

Mara smiled the smile you smile when someone gets closer to the truth than the arrangement allows, and climbed onto the speeder, and cranked it, and the little engine caught on the second try like it always did, loyal in the way machines are loyal, which is completely and only to whoever feeds them.

The plan was simple. Run south down the spur to the junction, twenty-two miles, arrive by early afternoon, catch the Wednesday—no, the Thursday connection over the divide, and be a new name in a new county by Friday. Clean. Textbook. Mara had done it nineteen times.

The speeder made it eleven.

The trouble announced itself as a sound first — a deep wooden groan from ahead, the sound a trestle makes when it's deciding — and then Mara was off and walking the last quarter mile on foot, and standing at the edge of Miller Creek looking at the trestle that was supposed to carry the speeder over, and was now carrying, instead, a great deal of creek.

Or rather: not carrying it. The middle span had settled. Not fallen — settled, polite, Canadian about it, sunk two feet into the water with the deck awash and the current running straight through the trusses like the river had simply gotten tired of going around. Mara walked out onto the approach span as far as instinct allowed, which was not very far, and crouched, and put a palm on the stringer, and felt the answer come up through the bones of the hand.

Gone. The pile footing underneath had scoured out in the spring melt, and nobody had known, because nobody had inspected, because the inspector the company sent to confirm the line should die had written *trestle serviceable* in a report filed in triplicate, and the river had read the report and disagreed.

Mara sat down on the rail, right there, boots hanging over black water, and laughed — one short bark of a laugh with nothing much behind it. Eleven miles from anywhere. One day ahead of the permit. A speeder on the wrong side of a drowned trestle, a sack of beans going soft in the sun, and the Thursday connection leaving the junction in five hours from a platform Mara would now reach in approximately never.

Somewhere downstream, faint under the wind, the river kept on arguing with the rocks.

Mara took out the map of the line, unfolded it, and studied the eleven miles between here and the junction. There was the road — washed out at the cut, by the look of the contours. There was the river — see above, re: grudges. And there, threading up the east bank in dotted lines that the surveyor had drawn with the enthusiasm of a man being paid by the dot, was the old haul road from the mining days, climbing away from the valley toward a pass, toward a place called, according to the faded ink, **Voss Camp**.

Mara stared at that name longer than a dotted line deserved.

Then Mara shouldered the bedroll, picked up the beans, and started walking — because the rule about never staying past the permit date didn't say anything about which direction you left in, and because a man who inspects nothing might as well inspect a ghost town on the way.

Turnip watched from the far bank, tail going, and did not follow, and Mara tried not to take it personally.
