## 2. A Map for a Promise

The saloon in Corrick Flats was called the Anvil & Ore, which was optimistic on both counts, since the anvil out front had rusted into a modern art installation and the last ore to pass through town had been hauled out eleven years ago by a man who'd skipped his bar tab doing it.

Mara Yarrow liked it immediately.

It had the two qualities Mara looked for in any establishment: it was warm, and nobody asked questions. The stove in the corner ticked like a contented clock. The bartender, a broad woman with forearms like dock rope, poured whiskey without being asked twice and never once asked what brought anyone to Corrick Flats, because the answer was always the same—nowhere else—and asking would be cruel.

Mara took the corner table with the good sightline to the door, ordered the stew despite every instinct, and was surprised to find it edible. Then they ordered a second bowl, purely as a scientific follow-up.

That was when the old man started coughing three tables over.

He coughed the way some men whistle while they work—a steady, rhythmic accompaniment to everything he did, which at the moment was drinking tea he clearly didn't want and watching Mara with the frank, unhurried attention of a man who had decided something and was simply waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

"You're the rail inspector," he said finally, when his cough permitted.

"I'm nobody," Mara said, which was true, and also the closest thing they had to a name.

"Rode in on the maintenance car yesterday. The one that isn't supposed to run anymore." He said it without accusation, almost fondly. "I know that line. Worked the Kestrel spur before you were born."

"Nobody's worked the Kestrel spur in twenty years."

"That's what I said." He smiled. It was a good smile, worn down to the enamel but still structurally sound. "Ede Voss."

And there it was—the name offered freely, like a coin flipped across the table, and Mara felt the familiar flinch. *Never use your real name* was rule two of the survival code, and rule one was *never stay past the permit date*, and both rules existed because of people who gave their names away too easily in saloons exactly like this one.

"Mara," they heard themselves say, and then winced internally. Not the full name. Just enough to be polite. It was a slippery slope, names. You gave them a syllable and they'd take the whole thing.

"Mara." Ede Voss turned it over like a rock he was checking for quartz. "Well, Mara. Sit over here where I don't have to shout. My lungs have opinions about shouting."

Mara should have stayed at their table. They knew this the way you know a bridge girder is cracked—you feel it in your teeth. But the stew was good, the stove was warm, and the old man had the particular gravity of someone running out of time to be interesting, so Mara picked up their bowl and moved.

Up close, Voss was worse than he'd let on from a distance. Yellowed skin stretched over cheekbones like tent poles. Hands spotted and trembling, except when they weren't—when they settled on the table they went perfectly still, the way only a draftsman's or a surgeon's hands do.

"How long?" Mara asked, because dying men appreciated not having to perform.

"Doctor in Halloway gave me till spring. Doctor in Halloway is an optimist." He sipped the tea, grimaced, set it down like a personal enemy. "I need a favor, Mara. And I can't pay for it, which I mention up front because I find honesty saves time."

"Favors I can't afford are my favorite kind."

"This one's simple. There's a girl in the world—my daughter. Ilsa. She'll tell you she hasn't got a father, and she'll be mostly right, but she has got a map, if I can get it to her, and the map has got to reach her before the concessions expire."

"The Kestrel concessions?"

Something flickered across Voss's face—not surprise that Mara knew, but a kind of tired satisfaction, like a teacher whose student has finally raised a hand. "You do know the valley."

"I know the valley's closed. Sealed permits, company fences, signs that say the water's unsafe. Standard procedure."

"It is standard," Voss agreed softly. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it."

He reached inside his coat, and Mara's hand drifted toward their belt knife with the lazy reflex of a life spent around strangers' pockets—but what came out was a leather roll, soft with age, tied with a bootlace. He untied it on the table with those suddenly-steady hands.

Inside was a map. Hand-drawn, ink on oilcloth, the linework fine as spider silk. Mara had seen a thousand survey maps in their years on the rails—grade profiles, watershed charts, the tidy bureaucratic geometry of other people's land. This wasn't that. This was a map drawn by someone who had *walked* every line first. Elevations annotated in a cramped, slanted hand. Little marginal notes: *spring runs brown after rain—ask why.* A stretch of the upper river marked with three small X's and no explanation at all.

And at the top edge, the headwaters themselves—the source of the Kestrel, tucked into a fold of country Mara had only ever seen from the cab of a train going the other way, fast, with the blinds down.

"This is beautiful," Mara said, meaning it.

"It's accurate," Voss corrected. "Beautiful is what they'll call it after I'm dead, if it survives. Accuracy gets you blamed for things." He tapped one spot near the headwaters, a small square marked ADIT — SEALED. "This is where you'd go, if you were going. Which I'm not asking you to do."

"You just said—"

"I'm asking you to carry this to Ilsa. That's all. She's a surveyor—was a surveyor—down at the flooded camps on the lower river. She'll know what to do with it better than either of us." His voice caught, and he covered it with the cough, smoothly, a man who'd had practice converting grief into symptom. "Six weeks. That's when the permits expire and the company seals the valley for good. New concession filings, new fences, new everything. After that, whatever's up there stays up there, and whatever happened down here stays buried under somebody else's paperwork."

Mara looked at the map. Looked at the old man. Felt the trap closing with the slow, courteous patience of all the best traps.

"Ede. I'm passing through. I inspect nothing, I fix nothing, I deliver nothing. Staying invisible is the entire job."

"I know." He said it gently, without argument, which was somehow worse. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to be polite to a dead man. There's a difference, and it's about six weeks wide."

* * *

Here is what Mara told themselves that night, lying in the room above the saloon with the stove gone cold and the wind working at the shutters: *You haven't promised anything.*

They had, technically. When Voss had pressed the roll into their hands at the door—heavy, warmer than it should have been, as if the oilcloth had been carried against a body for years—Mara had said, "I'll see she gets it," which is the sort of sentence that sounds like weather talk until you're lying awake at 2 a.m. parsing it word by word.

*I'll see.* Not *I promise.* See? Nothing binding. A courtesy. The kind of thing you say to old men in saloons so they can die thinking the world still works.

But sleep wouldn't come, and the map sat on the nightstand like a sleeping animal, and around four in the morning Mara gave up, dressed, and went downstairs to get the good chair by the dead stove and wait for dawn like a civilized person.

The saloon was empty except for the bartender stacking glasses, and a stranger.

He sat at the far table with a cup of coffee he hadn't touched, and he had the look Mara knew from a hundred rail yards and company offices: clean boots, clean coat, clean hands, and eyes that did inventory on everything including you. Company men were easy to spot because nothing about them was worn. Everything was issued.

"Morning," Mara said, aiming for bored.

"Morning." The man didn't look up. "Room upstairs. End of the hall. Old fella checked in three days ago—coughs like a bellows. Manager says he stepped out early this morning. Medical emergency, maybe." Now he looked up, and smiled with exactly the muscles required. "Except his gear's still up there, and men having medical emergencies usually take their coats."

"Sounds like a mystery for the law."

"We *are* the law, friend. Concession security, Kestrel Valley District." He produced a badge the way other men produce a knife. "Company land extends forty miles past the fence line. Most folks don't know that. Most folks don't need to."

"Most folks," Mara agreed, and poured themselves a coffee from the pot on the bar with hands that were, they noted with professional pride, completely steady. "Terrible business, theft. Hope you find him."

"Oh, we'll find him." The enforcer stood, unhurried, and Mara saw the holster under his coat open-topped and oiled. "We just need to know what he was carrying. Man like that travels light except for paper. You seen any paper?"

"Only the menu."

The smile again, held a beat too long. Then he climbed the stairs, and Mara stood at the bar listening to floorboards creak overhead, one after another, moving down the hall toward the end room. Toward Voss's room. A drawer opened. Another. Something metal set down on wood.

The bartender leaned close. "Back stairs," she murmured, not looking up from her glasses. "Through the kitchen. They end in the alley by the woodshed."

Mara stared at her.

"He tipped well," she said, by way of complete explanation, "and he coughed polite."

Guilt arrives uninvited—that was the phrase Mara's mother used to use, back when Mara had a mother and a name and a habit of leaving doors open behind them. Guilt doesn't knock. It just appears at your elbow holding your own conscience at knifepoint, and it says: *you said you'd see she gets it.*

Mara went through the kitchen.

The back stairs were narrow and smelled of onions and lamp oil, and halfway down Mara heard, muffled through the wall, the enforcer's voice on the saloon side, pleasant as a bank notice: "Locksmith's coming at eight. Until then, nobody goes up. Anybody asks, the old man's belongings are company property pending review."

*Pending review.* Two words that had ended more lives than dynamite, in Mara's experience.

The alley was gray and freezing. The woodshed leaned against the saloon's back wall like a drunk leaning on a friend, and Mara slipped between them into a gap that smelled of split pine and mouse, heart going hard, mind doing the arithmetic it had spent six years refusing to do.

Option one: walk away. Catch the morning freight south, invisible forever, alive, free. The map was upstairs in a leather roll, and in an hour it would be in an evidence bag, and in a week it would be ash in a company furnace, and Ede Voss's daughter would never know her father had thought of her at the end. Sad. Unfortunate. *Not your problem.*

Option two—

Option two involved going back up those stairs before eight o'clock, through a locked door, past a man with an oiled holster, to steal a dead-or-dying prospector's map back from the company that had already killed him once, legally speaking, twenty years ago.

Mara stood in the woodshed gap and discovered, with genuine irritation, that option two had already been chosen. Somewhere between the fourth stair and the smell of onions, some part of them had signed the papers. The body knew before the mind did; the body was already cataloguing the enforcer's position, the window in the end room, the roofline of the porch awning below it.

"You sentimental idiot," Mara whispered to themselves, in the dark, in their own real voice, which they almost never used.

Then they went back inside.

The hallway upstairs was dim. The enforcer had posted himself at the top of the main stairs with his back to the corridor, watching the room below him instead of the one beside him—amateur mistake, trusting walls. The end-room window was latched but the frame was older than the latch, and Mara's knife blade slid into the gap the way a spike slides into a joint, and the window rose without a sound, because Mara Yarrow had opened a great many windows that were supposed to stay shut.

The room was cold. On the nightstand, where the leather roll had been, lay a folded note in a cramped, slanted hand—the same hand that had annotated every contour line on the map, Mara realized, the same handwriting, of course it was:

*If you're reading this, you already said yes. Room 4, Halloway road, ask for the woman who maps water. Don't trust the ferryman. — E.V.*

Underneath the note, a single train ticket. One way. Downriver.

Mara pocketed both, closed the window, and was three steps along the roofline when the enforcer's voice floated up, cheerful and terrible:

"Morning, inspector. Funny thing—I checked the register. You signed in as *M. Yarrow.*"

The name hung in the cold air like a struck bell.

"Now," the enforcer called pleasantly, "why would a nobody do a thing like that?"
