## 3. Ilsa of the Flooded Camp

The flood had taken everything in Kettle Basin except the things nobody wanted, which was why Ilsa Voss still lived there.

She had a theory about this. Water, she'd tell anyone who asked—and nobody asked—water is honest. It takes the cheap furniture and the drywall and the photographs of people you were glad to stop being related to, and it leaves the cast-iron stove, the stone foundation, the anvil. It leaves what was built to last. A drowned camp is just a town that finally told the truth about itself.

So she lived on the second floor of the old assay office, which had a fine view of the lake that used to be Main Street, and every morning she rowed her leaky aluminum skiff out over the rooftops and took soundings no one had commissioned, for a report no one would read, filed to a licensing board that had taken her license three years ago and never given it back.

"Obsessive," the board had written in its final determination. She'd framed that word too. It hung by the door next to her father's last letter, the one that said *I'll make it right, little bird* and then never did, because he died somewhere upriver instead.

This morning the water was doing something interesting.

Ilsa noticed it before she noticed the stranger, because that was the order of her affections. The eastern arm of the basin had dropped four centimeters overnight. Four centimeters, uniform, across a body of water fed by snowmelt in June. That wasn't evaporation. That wasn't wind set-up. That was a drawdown upstream—something opening, or something failing, slow enough to be patient and fast enough to matter.

She wrote it down. She always wrote it down. Then she looked up to check the old sluice gate at the far shore, because if the gate had shifted she'd need to warn—

There was a man standing on her dock.

Not a fisherman. Fishermen in Kettle Basin owned boats; this one's boots were rail boots, creosote-stained, steel-toed, the walk of someone whose whole life had been measured in gauge widths. He stood with his hands visible and his weight back on his heels, the way you stand when you've been told you're not welcome and have decided to be polite about it anyway.

"You're Ilsa Voss," he said.

"That depends," said Ilsa, "on who's asking, and whether they're carrying anything sharp."

"Mara." The stranger patted their jacket pockets theatrically, turned them half inside-out like a street magician proving there was nothing up the sleeve. "No knife. No gun. No permit, no license, no name worth writing down. I'm mostly pockets full of nothing."

"Mostly?"

"There's a map."

The word went through her like cold water through a wader boot. She kept her face still. Three years of practice: the board hearing, the newspaper piece calling her father a saboteur, the men from the concession company who'd come through the basin buying silence by the pound. She'd learned to keep her face like a sounding line—still water, all the information underneath.

"I don't want a map," she said. "I have maps. I have forty pounds of maps. I have maps the concession company doesn't know exist, drawn by me, better than theirs."

"I believe it," Mara said, and here was the strange thing—the stranger sounded like they meant it. "That's actually why I'm here."

* * *

They sat on the assay office steps with tin mugs of spruce tea, which Ilsa made bitter on purpose, as a screening process. People who complained didn't get the second cup. Mara drank it down without expression and said, "You use spruce needles and—" a pause, a careful sniff "—is that juniper?"

"Juniper berry. For flavor."

"It's very bad."

"Thank you," Ilsa said, and gave them the second cup.

Mara turned the mug slowly in their hands, watching the lake instead of her. "Your father found me in a saloon in Corrick Junction. He was dying. Not dying like a dramatic story—dying like a man whose lungs had been full of rock dust for thirty years and had finally sent his resignation letter. He traded me a map for a promise. Take it to his daughter before the concessions expire. Six weeks, and then the whole Kestrel headwaters get sealed behind company gates forever."

"And you took the promise."

"I took it to be polite to a dead man," Mara admitted. "Then somebody searched his room while he was still warm, looking for exactly that map, and politeness started feeling more like obligation."

Ilsa stared out at the water. The eastern arm had dropped another centimeter since morning; she could see it in where the old bank met the surface, a fresh stripe of wet mineral stain like a tide mark on a bathtub. Something upstream was opening. Something upstream was *failing*, and in six weeks the only people allowed up there to find out what would be the same people who'd sealed the records after the collapse.

"What does the map show?" she asked, carefully casual.

"The headwaters. The river above the falls. And a place marked with an X that your father labeled, in words I copied down because I couldn't read the shorthand around them—" Mara recited it like a freight manifest, flat and exact. "*Adit Nine. What they buried is what I saw.*"

The mug stopped halfway to her mouth.

She knew that handwriting. Of course she knew it—she'd grown up under it, margins full of it, survey notes in it, birthday cards folded into precise engineering triangles in it. But Mara had described the label, and the description was wrong. Not wrong the way a forgery is wrong. Wrong the way a confession is wrong. Her father had spent twenty years saying the collapse at Adit Nine was the workers' fault, that the shoring failed, that he'd signed off on the survey in good faith. That was the official record. That was the record that got him his pension and got eleven families nothing and got Ilsa's own license revoked when she started asking questions with his name attached.

*What they buried is what I saw.*

He'd known. All those years, all those letters, all those careful silences—he'd known, and he'd carried it upriver like ballast until it killed him, and the only confession he could manage was a map handed to a stranger in a bar.

"He lied," Ilsa said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Underneath the level voice something was hardening, cooling, setting like concrete. "Or rather—he told one lie for twenty years and then wrote the truth on a piece of paper and gave it to a person with no name."

"To be fair," Mara said, "he was dying and I was the only one buying."

It was such a stupid, decent thing to say that Ilsa laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh in weeks. She put the mug down.

"No," she said. "I'm sorry. No. You can find whoever the map belongs to, but it isn't me. I don't take gifts from Ede Voss. He spent my inheritance on a lie and my career along with it. When the board pulled my license, do you know what the final citation said? *Unverified claims sourced to E. Voss.* My own work, thrown out because his name was on it. I mapped the seepage under the north levee two years before it breached. Two years. They called me obsessive." She gestured at the drowned town, the whole honest flooded truth of it. "Now look at the neighborhood."

Mara looked. They looked for a long time, and then they said, quietly:

"The eastern arm dropped four centimeters last night."

Ilsa went very still. "How do you know that?"

"Because I walked up from the south fork this morning, and the south fork is running low. Low enough that I crossed on stones that weren't stones last week. Whatever your lake is losing, it's losing it from upriver. From the headwaters." Mara set the empty mug down on the step between them, deliberate as a chess move. "Something up there is coming open, Ilsa. Your father's map says what. The concession says six weeks. The water says sooner. You can stay here and keep taking soundings of a town that's already dead, or you can come find out what he saw."

"I told you. I don't take gifts from Ede Voss."

"It's not a gift," Mara said. "It's a delivery. There's a difference. Gifts you're allowed to refuse."

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She sat in the assay office window with her father's last letter and read it the way she hadn't let herself read it in years—not as a goodbye but as a document, cross-referencing it against the map Mara had spread on the floorboards under the lamp.

*I'll make it right, little bird.* She'd thought it was sentiment. Now she saw the postmark: Corrick Junction, six days ago. Six days ago he'd been alive and dying and sixty kilometers away, and she'd been here, rowing her skiff over rooftops, too proud to write back because the last letter she'd sent had been returned unopened with a company stamp on it.

He'd probably thought she hated him. She had hated him. Both things were true, the way both things are usually true.

Near dawn, Mara woke on the floor—they'd refused the bed twice and then fallen asleep mid-refusal the third time, which Ilsa privately filed under *endearing*—and found her already packing.

"Soundings equipment?" Mara asked, nodding at the kit.

"Sounding equipment, rope, two weeks of food, and my license revocation letter," Ilsa said. "For kindling, if we need a fire."

Mara smiled. It changed their whole face, made them look less like a ghost between jobs. "And the map?"

Ilsa picked it up. Her father's hand crawled across it, confident and cramped, every contour line drawn by someone who had loved this river enough to measure it properly. At the corner, small, almost hidden: *for my daughter, who checks my arithmetic.*

"He was right about that, at least," she muttered. "I always checked his arithmetic."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a yes," Ilsa said, and slung the pack over her shoulder. "But understand something, Mara-with-no-last-name. We're not going up there for treasure. If there's gold in that adit, we leave it. We're going for the truth, and the truth goes to the families in Halloway first, before it goes anywhere near anyone who can buy it."

"Wouldn't dream otherwise," Mara said. "Also, quick question. How do you feel about tunnels?"

"Why?"

"Because the only way upstream ahead of the closure is through the old Kestrel bore, and it collapsed in '09, and I know how to run a train through a collapsed tunnel, but I've never done it without a train."

Ilsa looked at the map, at the thin pencil line threading the mountains, at the mark her father had left like a hand held out across twenty years of silence.

"Then we'd better start walking," she said.
