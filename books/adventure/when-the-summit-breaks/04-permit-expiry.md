## 4. Permit Expiry

Mara had a rule about mornings in company towns: wake before the whistles, read the notice boards before the clerks did, and never, under any circumstances, be the last one out of the boarding house. The last one out was the one who got asked questions.

So it was Mara, at half past five, coffee-less and already dressed, who stood in the frost on the porch of the Kestrel Fork boarding house and read the telegram pasted to the community board with four rusted pins.

*BY ORDER OF KESTREL CONSOLIDATED MINING & TIMBER CO. ALL RECREATIONAL, SURVEY, AND TRANSIT PERMITS WITHIN THE KESTREL WATERSHED ARE HEREBY VOID EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY. THE VALLEY WILL BE CLOSED TO UNAUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED TO THE FULLEST EXTENT.*

*Effective immediately.* The permits had three weeks left on them. Mara had counted. Counting was the whole job. Three weeks was supposed to mean three weeks, the way a rail schedule meant a rail schedule, and the fact that Kestrel Consolidated had torn up its own paper told Mara two things: the company was scared of something in that valley, and the company knew someone was coming for it.

Mara read the notice twice, then a third time for the small print, because the small print was where companies hid their manners. There it was, third line down: *All prior permit holders must surrender documentation at company offices.* Which meant the company had a list. Which meant the company knew a map had left Ede Voss's room, because the enforcer who'd torn that room apart had left a calling card the size of a telegram.

"Mara." Ilsa's voice from the doorway, low. She'd slept in her boots again. She had her father's map rolled in an oilcloth tube slung across her back like a rifle, and she looked at the notice board the way she looked at flood gauges—professionally, hungrily, for what it would do next. "They moved the date."

"They moved the date."

"That's not legal."

"Neither is dynamiting your own dam, but here we are." Mara pulled the telegram free, folded it, and tucked it inside a coat pocket. "Legal is a thing that happens to other people. We've got maybe a day before they work out who bought feed at the livery, and two before they work out which direction we went. Upstream is north. The river doesn't care about permits."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. In the grey light she looked like her father around the eyes, which was a thing Mara had noticed and decided never to say out loud. "There's no road north. The wagon grade washed out in the spring flood. I surveyed it—I *would* have surveyed it, if anyone had paid me to." The bitterness in that was old and worn smooth, a stone she carried in her pocket and rubbed without thinking.

"There's no *road*," Mara agreed. "But there's the old Kestrel Fork tram tunnel. Number Three. It runs under the shoulder of the mountain and comes out above the washout, two miles past where any road ends."

"I've heard of Number Three. It's been sealed for nine years."

"It's been *closed* for nine years. Sealed is a word companies use when they've actually done the work. Kestrel Consolidated never did work it could charge to someone else's ledger." Mara was already walking toward the livery, and Ilsa fell in beside her, and that was the whole conversation about whether they were going, because they had been going since the saloon, since the flooded camp, since a dying man's hand had pressed a paper tube into Mara's and made the whole world heavier by about four ounces.

At the livery, the stable boy—sixteen, ears red with cold, the kind of boy who noticed everything and reported nothing because nobody had ever thought to pay him for either—took their coin and asked no questions, and Mara tipped him extra for the not-asking, which was the most honest transaction either of them would make that week.

They rode north out of Kestrel Fork as the sun came up the color of old brass, and behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound Mara had been dreading: engines. Not rail engines. Motor engines, two of them, heavy ones, coming up the valley road from the company offices.

"Contractors," Ilsa said.

"Contractors," Mara agreed, and did not look back, because looking back was a habit for people who planned to return.

* * *

The tram tunnel's north portal was exactly as Mara remembered it, which was to say: buried under nine years of alder growth, a collapsed spoil heap, and one very opinionated marmot colony. They left the horses hobbled in the trees with a half-sack of oats and enough sense between them to stay put, and Mara stood at the face of the spoil heap with a shovel and the particular joy of a rail engineer looking at a problem everyone else had written off.

"It's blocked," Ilsa said. Not arguing. Measuring. She had her notebook out already, sketching the heap's profile, and Mara felt something warm and unfamiliar in the chest—the feeling of being *believed*, technically, in advance of the evidence.

"The portal's blocked. The portal is forty feet of loose rock they pushed in front of a tunnel that's bored through granite." Mara spat on both hands. "You don't seal a tunnel with loose rock. You seal it with a bulkhead, poured concrete, rebar. This is a *gesture*. Somebody's uncle got paid to push a heap with a dozer and file a report." Mara drove the shovel in. "Granite doesn't settle. The tunnel behind this is exactly the tunnel that was. Nine inches of gauge, timber sets every four feet, and if the timber's held—and it held when I rode this line in '09, and it's had nine years to prove it couldn't—it holds now."

They dug for two hours. It was honest work, the kind Mara liked best: work with a visible result. Ilsa hauled rock without being asked and without comment, and once, when Mara's shovel struck timber instead of stone, Ilsa made a sound—half laugh, half relief—that Mara decided to keep.

The gap, when they'd made it, was barely shoulder-width, a black slot breathing cold air that smelled of wet creosote and old iron. Mara lit a lantern and held it into the dark. The first timber set stood square and true, furred with frost-rot at the base but sound at the crown. Beyond it, the tunnel ran straight as a rule into the mountain.

"Well," Ilsa said.

"Well," Mara said, and went in first, because that was the deal. That had always been the deal, unspoken: Mara went first into the dark, and Ilsa went first into the truth. It divided the labor fairly.

The tunnel was a mile and a quarter, and Mara knew it the way you know a scar—by touch, in the dark, without wanting to. At the third set there was a drip line that would soak your collar. At the fifth, the floor heaved where the mountain had shrugged, and they climbed it hand over hand. Ilsa talked to fill the dark at first—about surveying, about how she'd once triangulated a whole watershed from three points and a stubborn mule—and then stopped talking, and the silence was companionable, which was its own kind of trust.

At the seventh set, Mara stopped so suddenly that Ilsa walked into her back.

"What—"

"Listen."

Behind them, far back at the south portal, faint as a memory: voices. Boots on rock. And a sound that turned Mara's stomach, because it was a sound from the old days, the bad days—the ring of a crowbar on timber.

"They found the gap," Ilsa whispered.

"They found the *horses*." Mara turned the lantern down to a coal. "Or the stable boy talked, or they've simply been behind us since Kestrel Fork and we've been two lucky people having a nice walk." The voices came again, closer now, echoing down the bore so that it was impossible to tell distance, which was the cruelty of tunnels: sound lied in both directions. "How are you at running in the dark?"

"I'm a surveyor. I run in the dark *professionally*."

"Then stay on my heels and touch the left wall. There's a side drift at the ninth set—an old ore pass, goes up and over and rejoins the main bore past a roof fall. If they're coming in behind us, we let them have the main line and we take the drift."

"And if the drift is blocked?"

"Then we have a very honest conversation with our situation." Mara took Ilsa's wrist and pressed it to the wall, and held it there a second longer than the instruction required, and felt Ilsa feel that, and let go. "Left wall. Go."

They went. The ninth set came up out of the dark like a gate, and the drift mouth yawned to the left, and Mara swung the lantern into it and saw—rubble, yes, but rubble with a shape, a climbable shape, a shape with daylight at the top of it, a coin of grey morning no bigger than a hat.

Behind them, in the main bore, the crowbar rang again, and then a voice, carrying flat and clear down the tunnel the way voices do in granite:

"We know you're in here. Voss. Whoever you are. The company just wants to *talk*."

Ilsa's hand found Mara's sleeve in the dark. "That's a lie," she said, and her voice didn't shake at all, and Mara thought: *her father's daughter.*

"It's a lie," Mara agreed, and boosted her toward the daylight.

* * *

They came out of the drift into a hanging meadow two hundred feet above the river, breath steaming, hearts going like stamping mills, and lay flat in the wet grass until their lungs agreed to rejoin the arrangement. Below and behind, the tunnel mouth was a black eye in the mountainside. No one had followed them out. Yet.

Ilsa rolled onto her back and laughed—actually laughed, breathless and slightly unhinged, the laugh of a woman who had run in the dark professionally and survived the engagement. "You knew about that drift. You knew the *ore pass*."

"I inspected this line in '09. Officially." Mara lay beside her, watching a hawk work the updraft. "I inspected nothing, of course. That was the arrangement. But a man can't ride a tunnel a dozen times without learning where the mountain keeps its doors."

"Renn," Ilsa said suddenly, and Mara turned to look at her. "The contractor. In the tunnel—that voice. I've seen him before, at the license hearings. He sat in the back. He used to be a rail man, they said. Before the company." She sat up, and the humor had gone out of her face, replaced by the hard bright thing that had been growing in her since the flooded camp. "They'll come up the valley road. They'll be a day behind us at most. Mara—they're not going to stop."

"No," Mara said. "They're not going to stop. And neither are we." Mara got up, brushed off the grass, and pointed north, where the river showed itself in white threads down a gorge no road had ever troubled. "Six weeks was the promise. They've made it five. We'd best not waste the difference standing in a meadow being dramatic."

They went upstream, two small figures against a very large valley, and somewhere behind them the mountain kept the sound of crowbars, patient as companies, coming on.
