## 5. What the River Keeps

The Kestrel ran wide and brown-green at the old ferry crossing, the kind of water that looked slow and lied about it. Mara stood on the near bank and studied the rope with a surveyor's squint they'd never earned.

"Rope's sound," Ilsa said, already hauling the raft out of the alders where they'd cached it that morning. "I checked."

"You checked by looking at it?"

"I checked by pulling on it. With my whole body weight. Which is more than you did when you inspected that trestle outside Halloway and told me it was 'probably fine.'"

"It was probably fine. It was fine." Mara helped her drag the raft down the shingle. It was a poor thing—four planks, two barrels, a hand-crank winch salvaged from a mine hoist—but it had carried their supplies this far, and their supplies were everything: flour, salt pork, the oilcloth-wrapped map, Ilsa's instruments, Mara's spare boots. The river took notice of such things. Rivers always did.

The crossing was simple in theory. A cable anchored bank to bank, sagging in the middle like a tired hammock. You clipped the raft to a traveler pulley, you cranked yourself across, and the Kestrel went about its business beneath you without comment. Mara had crossed a hundred rivers this way, back when rivers had schedules and names painted on bridges.

They loaded the raft. Ilsa went first because she argued she was lighter and therefore less likely to test the cable, which was either good engineering or an insult, depending on how Mara chose to take it. She chose to take it as an insult, and got on second anyway.

Halfway across, the anchor spoke.

Not broke—spoke. A groan out of the far bank, deep and structural, the sound of a dead tree giving up its argument with the earth. The cable sagged six inches, then a foot. The traveler pulley jammed in the new slack, and the raft swung broadside to the current like a door coming off its hinges.

"Hold the crank," Ilsa said, very calm, the way people are calm when they've already done the arithmetic.

Mara held the crank. The crank held nothing. The current took the raft's nose and began, patiently, to turn them downstream, and the cable paid out through the jammed pulley in little jerks, each one a small promise of a larger one.

"The anchor tree's going," Mara called. "Far side. We need to be off this thing before—"

"I know what before." Ilsa had the pack straps in her fists. "Raft or us, Mara. Not both."

The raft weighed maybe four hundred pounds loaded. The river wanted all of it. There was a moment—a long, clear, cold moment—when Mara understood exactly what Ilsa meant, and hated her for it, and agreed with her completely.

Then the anchor tree finished its argument.

The cable came alive. It whipped off the far bank and lashed across the water, and the raft spun, and Mara went in to the hips before they'd even decided anything. The current closed around them like a crowd. Somewhere above, Ilsa said something short and profane, and then there was a splash, and then Ilsa's arm was around their chest—not gentle, nothing about it gentle—and she was swimming for the near bank with one arm and pure fury doing the work of the other.

They came ashore twenty yards downstream, coughing, tangled together, lying on gravel like two things the river had decided not to keep.

Mara watched the raft go. It rode the current with strange dignity, barrels and planks and forty pounds of flour, dwindling around the bend until the Kestrel folded it away. Their food. Their dry clothes. The spare boots.

"Well," Mara said.

"Don't," Ilsa said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something about the boots."

Mara closed their mouth. The truth was they had been going to say something about the boots, and Ilsa had known it, and there was no future in pretending otherwise.

* * *

The barracks sat a mile upstream where the trail left the river: a long low building of peeled logs, company-built, company-abandoned, one of dozens strung along the Kestrel like beads on a string nobody wore anymore. The door opened onto a room built for forty bunks and holding six, most of them collapsed into themselves like tired men. Someone had left a stove. Someone else, years ago, had left a tin of matches behind the stovepipe collar, still dry, still good, and Mara could have kissed the ghost of that someone.

They stripped wet wool and hung it by the fire they built from broken bunk slats. Ilsa found a blanket that smelled of mouse but not of mold. Mara found, in the corner bunk, a deck of cards missing the sevens and one jack, and dealt them both a hand of nothing just to have something to do with their hands while their clothes steamed.

"We lost the map," Ilsa said finally.

"No. Map's oilcloth. It was in my coat." Mara patted the bundle against their bare ribs. "Wet, but whole. Ink's pencil. Pencil doesn't drown."

Ilsa stared at them. "You saved the map and let the food go."

"I saved the map and *you* saved me, and between the two of us we made some choices." Mara laid down a losing hand. "The food was never going to make it. The river had opinions."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

It was not, quite, a little funny. They were four days from Halloway behind them and unknown days from anywhere ahead, with half a tin of tea, a handful of salt pork that had been in Mara's pocket, and whatever the forest could be persuaded to give up. But there is a specific laughter that lives on the far side of nearly drowning, and it isn't really about the joke. Ilsa fought it and lost, and then they were both laughing at nothing, at the cards, at the boots, at the sheer enormous indifference of the Kestrel, and the barracks felt briefly like a place where people lived.

* * *

The fire burned down to coals and their clothes dried stiff as bark. Ilsa sat wrapped in the mouse-blanket with her father's map unrolled across her knees, and she didn't look at it so much as look *through* it, the way you look through a window at weather.

"He drew this the year of the collapse," she said.

Mara kept sorting cards. "You said."

"I said he drew it after. Everyone says after. That's the story—the disgraced prospector maps the river that killed thirty-one men, trying to find his own redemption in the gravel." Her finger traced the headwaters without touching them. "But look at the date notation. Here, in the margin. He used the old survey convention—the one they retired in '09. Nobody who learned after the collapse would write it that way. He drew this *before*, Mara. He mapped the headwaters the same season the dam went."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse expressed an opinion.

"What does that mean?" Mara asked, though a cold part of them was already assembling the answer.

"It means he knew the valley before the disaster. Intimately. Every chute, every seep, every place the ground was rotten." Ilsa's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading numbers she didn't like. "And when the dam failed, the commission needed a story, and my father was the only man who'd been everywhere the water went. So they gave him the blame and gave everyone else a villain, and he—" She stopped. Started again. "He never once defended himself. Thirty years. Not one letter, not one appeal. I thought it was shame. I thought he couldn't face what he'd done."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was quiet because he knew too much to talk. You don't argue with men who dynamited their own dam. You disappear upriver and you draw maps in a dead man's handwriting conventions and you wait for your permits to expire." She rolled the map with sudden violence. "He wasn't hiding from what he did. He was hiding from what *they* did."

Mara set the cards down. There was a thing in their own chest, an old bridge-shaped thing, that stirred whenever anyone talked about blame and commissions and the arithmetic of who gets named guilty. They had carried it for eleven years without opening it, the way you carry a tool you hope never to need.

"My last job on the books," Mara heard themself say, "was a bridge over the Coal Fork. Inspection report said replace the pilings. My name signed the report. Company shelved it. Bridge held eight more years, then it didn't." They fed a slat to the coals. "Nobody died. Fourteen injured. And when they needed someone to point at, they pointed at the inspector who'd signed the original finding—as if finding the rot was the crime. I walked off the books that winter and never signed my real name again."

Ilsa looked at them across the fire for a long moment.

"So we're both liars," she said.

"We're both *survivors* of other people's paperwork."

"That's what a liar says."

"It is," Mara admitted. "That's how you know it's true."

She almost smiled. Outside, the dark came down on the valley like a lid, and the Kestrel talked to itself in the shallows, patient, keeping everything.

* * *

They slept in shifts, out of habit rather than fear. When Mara woke for their watch, Ilsa was asleep sitting up, the map under her hand like a child holds a parent's sleeve, and Mara sat in the red light of the coals and thought about the word Ilsa hadn't used yet: *company*. As in, the company dynamited its own dam. As in, the same company whose permits were expiring, whose contractors were somewhere behind them on the trail, whose whole fortune might rest on thirty-one men staying blamed.

The map was worth money. Mara had known that since the saloon; it was why Voss had needed a stranger instead of a friend. But sitting in a dead man's barracks, watching his daughter sleep, Mara understood for the first time that the map wasn't worth money at all.

It was worth *proof*. And proof was a different animal entirely—proof couldn't be sold quietly, spent quietly, vanished quietly. Proof wanted daylight. Proof would drag them both out of the shadows they'd built their lives in, blinking, named, findable.

Mara fed the fire another slat and did not like the arithmetic at all.

Toward dawn, Ilsa woke, saw Mara watching the door, and read the stillness correctly.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Nothing," Mara said. Then, because lying to her was getting harder by the day: "Halloway's behind us. The contractors will assume we doubled back for supplies. Nobody pushes upstream past the ferry this fast."

"But?"

"But somebody taught those contractors the river." Mara nodded at the map. "And I want to know who, before they find us."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and turned the map over, and held it close to the coals, and tilted it so the low light raked across the paper.

Faintly, in the lower corner—in pencil, in a different hand, pressed hard enough to leave a groove—there was writing neither of them had seen by daylight.

A name. A date. And beneath them, three words:

*THEY WILL DENY IT.*
