## 6. The Town That Remembers

Halloway announced itself the way old men announce themselves at funerals: quietly, and with the expectation that you already knew who they were.

We came down off the ridge at dusk, following a spur line that had been pulled up for scrap a decade ago but whose ballast still held its shape underfoot, like a path worn by memory rather than feet. The town sat in the crook of the river where the valley widened—a scatter of roofs, one water tower leaning like a tired man against a fence, and a single street of storefronts with their paint gone the grey of driftwood.

"You said we were avoiding towns," Mara said.

"I said we were avoiding *company* towns."

"This is a company town."

"It was," I said. "Look at it. The company left. What's left is just—people." I had read about Halloway in my father's letters, back when I'd still been speaking to him, back when his letters came with return addresses instead of postmarks from nowhere. He'd worked out of Halloway the year before the collapse. He'd hated it and loved it in the same sentence, which was how he felt about most things, including me.

Mara looked at me the way they did when they were deciding whether to argue, then decided the river was argument enough for one day. "Fine. But we're ghosts. In and out. Food, information, gone before light."

"Agreed."

Neither of us believed it. Towns like this don't let you be ghosts. Towns like this take one look at your face and know your grandmother's maiden name.

* * *

The diner was called the Kestrel Room, though there was no room in it shaped like a kestrel and hadn't been since the company built it in 1911. Inside: eight booths, four occupied, and a smell of onions and coffee that hit me so hard in the chest I nearly sat down in the doorway.

The woman behind the counter was maybe seventy, built like a fence post, wiping glasses that were already clean. She looked at us exactly once—the mud on our boots, the rope burns on Mara's hands, the way I kept my father's map rolled tight inside my coat—and her eyes did something I can only describe as arithmetic.

"Two plates?" she said.

"Please," I said.

"Sit in the back booth. It's got the good view of the door." And she turned to the kitchen without waiting for an answer, which was its own kind of answer.

We took the booth. Mara slid in facing the door, because of course they did; I'd stopped commenting on it around the flooded camp. A boy of perhaps twelve brought our plates—stew, bread, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in—and set them down with the solemnity of a man defusing something.

"Gram says eat slow," he told us. "And don't look at the window."

I looked at the window. Of course I did. Nothing there but dusk and our own reflections, two smudged faces over an empty street.

"Your gram gives excellent advice," Mara said, "and you should tell her we're ignoring all of it."

The boy grinned like this was the best thing anyone had said all year and vanished into the kitchen.

The stew was venison and root vegetables and something else I couldn't name, some herb that tasted like the mountainside itself. I ate half of it before I realized I was crying, quietly, into the bowl, and that nobody in the diner was looking at me, and that this too was advice being followed.

"Hey." Mara's voice, low. "Hey. You okay?"

"He ate here." I wiped my face with my sleeve. "My father. He must have. This is—he wrote about the stew. In a letter. He said the cook put juniper in it and that it was the only honest thing in Halloway."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then: "It does taste honest."

I laughed, wet and awful, and finished the bowl.

* * *

The counter woman came to refill our coffee when the diner had emptied, and she didn't ask our names, which is how I knew she already had them.

"Voss," she said, setting the pot down. "Ede Voss's girl. You have his chin. He'd have hated that—he thought his chin was his worst feature."

"You knew him?"

"Everybody knew Ede. Man could talk the ear off a moose." She sat down across from us uninvited, which in a place like Halloway is not rudeness but ceremony. "Name's Odile Marchetti. My husband ran the pump house below the dam. Before."

Before. The word landed in the booth between us like a dropped tool.

"Odile—" I started.

"You're going upstream." She said it flatly, not a question. "To the headwaters. With that map." Her eyes flicked to my coat, where the roll of paper pressed against my ribs. "I'll save you the suspense, girl. We've seen it. Half this town has seen that map, or one like it. Ede drew six copies the winter after the collapse and mailed five of them to people he trusted. Mine burned in the flood along with everything else, but I remember every line of it."

"Why are you telling us this?"

"Because you're going to walk into the Kestrel Room tomorrow morning thinking you're clever, and I'd rather you walked out alive." She leaned forward. "There are men in the valley asking questions. Contractors, concession men, whatever costume they're wearing this season. They came through three days ago. Showed a photograph around. Young person, dark hair, rail-worker's hands." She nodded at Mara. "That's you, isn't it."

Mara's face did nothing at all, which was more confirmation than any confession would have been.

"They have your picture," Odile repeated. "Not a sketch. A photograph. Which means somebody with a camera and access took it, which means they know who you are well enough to want proof. They're calling you a map thief. There's a reward."

"How much?" Mara asked.

"Enough that the wrong person in the next town won't look away." She stood, collected the coffee pot, and paused. "You'll stay tonight. Not here—here's the first place they'd look. Tam Brandt's got the old signal shed past the church. Nobody's used it since '09. He'll show you the way once the street's empty."

"We haven't agreed to anything," Mara said.

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "But you will."

* * *

Tam Brandt turned out to be a broad, silent man in his fifties who met us behind the church with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light and walked us through back lots without saying a word until we reached the signal shed—a squat timber building with a rusted lever frame inside and forty years of dust on everything except one swept corner with two bedrolls and a tin of matches.

"People stay here," I said.

"People pass through," Tam corrected. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Rail workers mostly. Ones the companies decided didn't exist anymore. My brother was one." He looked at Mara a long moment. "He jumped a bridge inspection contract in '14. Never came home. So." He shrugged, as if that explained the bedrolls, and maybe it did.

"Thank you," Mara said.

"Don't thank me yet." He lit the lantern properly, finally, and the light showed his face: tired, lined, and underneath it something fierce. "In the morning, Odile wants you at her house before first light. Back door. She's got something for the girl."

"For me?"

"She didn't say. She never says. You'll live longer if you stop asking questions in this town and start answering them." He paused at the door. "One more thing. The contractors come back through on the morning train. Renn's crew. If you're smart, you'll be gone before the whistle."

"Renn," Mara said softly, and I heard something in their voice I couldn't name—recognition, maybe, or dread wearing recognition's coat.

"You know him?"

"Everybody in rail knows Renn." Mara lay back on the bedroll and stared at the ceiling. "Get some sleep, Voss."

* * *

Odile's house smelled of cedar and baking bread, and she fed us again before she gave me anything, because that was clearly the order of operations in Halloway: stomach first, soul second.

Then she went to a bureau, knelt, and drew out a shoebox wrapped in oilcloth, and set it in my hands, and my arms knew its weight before my mind did. Paper. Old paper. A lot of it.

"Ede wrote to me for eleven years," she said. "After the collapse, when everyone stopped writing to him—including, I noticed, his own daughter—I kept writing back. Somebody had to remember him as a man and not a headline." She watched me open the box with hands that shook. "Letters. Every one he sent. I'm giving them to you because I'm seventy-three years old and I've read them enough times to recite them, and because you're going upstream to a place where you'll need to know exactly what kind of man your father was."

I lifted the top letter. His handwriting—loping, urgent, the letters leaning forward like they were trying to outrun the pen. *Dear Odile. The survey numbers don't add up and I can't make them add up and I don't think they're supposed to...*

"The dam," I whispered. "He knew. Before the collapse, he—"

"He filed a report," Odile said. "Company shelved it. Then the dam broke, and the company needed someone to blame, and a prospector with debts and a temper was easier than a boardroom full of shareholders. Your father spent the rest of his life drawing maps of a valley everyone was paid to forget." She touched the box, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "The past isn't dead around here, girl. It's just been waiting for someone with legs."

Mara stood by the window, watching the grey light come up over the ridge, and said nothing, but their jaw was set in a way I'd learned to read as *planning.*

Down in the valley, faint and far, a train whistle sounded.

Morning. The contractors' train.

Odile met my eyes. "Back door," she said. "And girl—when you find what your father was looking for? Make it loud."

* * *

We were three streets gone, moving fast through the blue half-light, when Mara stopped at the mouth of the old rail yard and grabbed my arm.

Ahead of us, blocking the only clear route out of town, a freight idled on the main line—and beside it, checking papers against a clipboard, stood a broad-shouldered figure in a contractor's coat, talking easily to the station agent while his men fanned out along the platform.

Renn.

And Renn, without turning around, without needing to, raised one hand—not a threat, not quite a greeting—and pointed, deliberately, at the tunnel portal on the far side of the yard.

The one that led upstream.
