## 7. Switchback

The switchback above the Kestrel's third gorge was built in 1911 by men who believed a mountain could be argued with. Mara had always admired that kind of optimism, even when it ended in gravel and grief. The trail clung to the cliff face in three long hairpins, each one narrower than the last, the outer edge crumbling into air that smelled of pine resin and old rockfall. Below, the river ran white and loud, filling the gorge like a held breath.

Mara came around the second bend at midmorning, Ilsa twenty paces behind with her surveyor's stride eating the grade, and stopped so abruptly that Ilsa nearly walked into their back.

"Company," Mara said quietly.

Three men stood on the widest part of the switchback where it flattened before climbing again. Two of them had the loose, balanced stance of people paid to be comfortable with violence. The third stood apart, hands in his pockets, looking out over the gorge as though he'd been waiting for the view rather than for them. He was broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had once done real work and now supervised others doing it, and his boots—Mara noticed this before anything else—were laced in the old rail-yard fashion, double-knotted, heels worn slightly outward from years of walking ballast.

"Morning," the man said without turning. "You're a long way from any permit you hold."

Mara's mind did what it always did under pressure: it went calm and started counting. Three of them. Narrow trail, but not narrow enough. The two flanking men were positioned wrong for an ambush—too far apart, no one holding the high ground behind. Which meant this wasn't an ambush yet. It was a conversation wearing an ambush's clothes.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Mara said. "You know my permits and I don't know your name."

"Renn." He turned then. His face was weathered and unremarkable except for the eyes, which had the flat patience of a man who had spent years watching signals down long straight stretches of track. "Security contractor, Kestrel Consolidated Concession. And you—" he produced a folded paper from his jacket, opened it with the care of a man handling something explosive, "—are traveling on access permit 4471-C, issued to a M. Yarrow, line inspector, decommissioned routes division."

"That's me," Mara said pleasantly. "Inspecting."

"Inspecting what?"

"The trail. It's in terrible shape. Somebody should file a report."

One of the flankers snorted. Renn didn't. He studied Mara the way Mara had once studied bridges—looking for the crack that would tell him everything about the rest of the structure.

"There is no decommissioned routes division anymore," Renn said. "There hasn't been one since spring. Whoever issued this number was either careless or generous, and either way, the number itself is interesting. Because 4471-C was voided in April. Reissued in May. Voided again in June." He tilted his head. "That's a busy little permit. That's a permit that keeps dying and coming back to life."

* * *

They had made the forgery in Halloway, on the kitchen table of the old woman named Sef, using her good lamp and Ilsa's steady hand. Ilsa had copied the concession's letterhead twice until it was perfect, and Mara had supplied the numbers—a sequence pulled from memory, permits Mara had seen posted on station boards across three summers of ghosting up and down closed lines. The trick wasn't forging a document. Anyone could forge a document. The trick was forging a *history*, a paper that looked like it had been handled, amended, argued over by clerks. A permit too alive to question.

It had felt clever at the time. Standing on the switchback with the gorge breathing cold air up at them, it felt less clever.

"I can explain the amendments," Mara said.

"I'm sure you can. You seem like someone who can explain anything." Renn folded the paper away. "Here's my problem, Yarrow. My problem is that I've been walking this river for eleven days, and every town I pass through, somebody has just missed you. The ferryman at Second Bend remembers a traveler who asked careful questions. The trapper at Miller's Fork sold coffee to two people who paid too much and talked too little. You're a rumor with boots." He stepped closer. "And rumors are expensive. My contract says find the map and whoever carries it. It doesn't say how much noise I make finding them."

Behind Mara, Ilsa had gone very still—the stillness of a surveyor calculating load-bearing capacity. Mara didn't turn. Turning would be confirmation.

"So here's what I think," Renn went on. "I think you're carrying something for Ede Voss. I think Voss gave it to the wrong person, or maybe the right person, I honestly don't know anymore. And I think there's a second set of tracks on this trail that isn't yours, because you walk like a rail man and those prints walk like someone who measures things for a living." His eyes flicked past Mara's shoulder, brief and professional. "Someone young. Someone who sets their feet like they're sighting an instrument."

The gorge roared below them. Mara kept their face pleasant and empty, the face of a person discussing freight schedules.

"I travel alone," Mara said.

"Everybody travels alone," Renn said. "Right up until they don't."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and later Mara would replay it and understand that Renn had let it happen—that the whole exchange had been a test with only one honest answer available.

The taller of the two flankers, a man with a shaved head and a jaw like a dropped toolbox, moved to circle past Mara and look at Ilsa directly. Not subtle. Not meant to be. And Mara—without deciding to, without any thought at all—stepped sideways into his path.

Just a step. Just enough. Shoulder squared, weight low, the old yard-walk blocking motion that lives in the body longer than names do. The man stopped. For a moment the switchback was very quiet except for the river.

"Well," Renn said softly.

Mara heard it land: the mistake. The confession. You could lie with papers and words and pleasant empty faces, but you could not lie with your feet, and Mara's feet had just told this man everything—that there was someone behind them worth protecting, that Mara would spend their invisibility on it without hesitation, that the promise to a dying prospector had become a debt with a heartbeat attached.

Renn waved his man back. Then he did something unexpected: he walked to the outer edge of the switchback, looked down at the river for a long moment, and laughed—not cruelly, but with the tired sound of a man finding an old friend in an unwelcome place.

"You blocked me like a brakeman," he said. "Double-knot your boots. Walk the outside rail of every bridge, even the condemned ones—I watched you come onto this switchback, you hugged the cut side. Nobody hugs the cut side unless they've trusted a mountain with their life and lost the argument at least once." He turned back. "I ran section gang out of Corven Junction for six years. Before the concessions bought everything and turned men like us into men like these." A small gesture at his own flankers, who had the grace to look mildly embarrassed.

"Why are you telling me this?" Mara asked.

"Because I'm about to do something stupid, and I want you to know it costs me." Renn reached into his jacket again and this time produced not a paper but a photograph, creased soft from handling. He held it up: a grainy image of a figure at a ferry landing, face half-turned. Mara recognized the coat. Recognized, with a small cold shock, their own posture. "This went out to every contractor on the river four days ago. Description, name, the works. I'm supposed to radio in when I make contact." He paused. "I haven't radioed in since yesterday morning. Equipment trouble, I'll say. These gorges eat signal."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange you give me two days of nothing. No fires after dark. No trail. You go to ground somewhere I can honestly say I searched, and in two days I report contact made, subject fled downstream toward the closed sections, direction uncertain. By then the upper valley will be a different problem—weather's turning, and the company doesn't pay hazard rates for storms." He put the photograph away. "Two days, Yarrow. After that, if you're still on this mountain, you're someone else's paperwork, and I won't be able to help you even if I want to. And I'm telling you plainly: I don't want to. Wanting to is how men like me end up unemployed or worse."

"Why?" The question came out of Mara before they could dress it up. "Why help?"

Renn looked at them for a while. The wind moved along the cliff face, carrying the smell of coming snow from heights neither of them could see.

"Because Ede Voss mapped that river the year my section crew nearly died in it," he said finally. "And because whatever the company is paying me to bury, he spent thirty years trying to dig up. And because—" he glanced past Mara again, at Ilsa, and something in his weathered face shifted, some old courtesy surfacing, "—because whoever she is, she walks like her father. I carried instruments for a man who walked like that, one summer, before everything went bad. Best summer of my life, and I didn't know it at the time." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own honesty. "Go. Both of you. And Yarrow—" 

"Yes?"

"Whatever you're carrying. Whatever he gave you." Renn's voice dropped, and for a moment he sounded less like a contractor than like a rail man standing at the edge of a bridge he didn't trust. "Don't lose it. Things like that are the only cargo worth the freight."

Then he turned and gestured his men back down the trail, and the switchback emptied, and Mara stood alone in the wind with their heart going like a runaway truck on a grade.

Ilsa came forward slowly. She was pale, and furious, and trying to hide both.

"You stepped in front of him," she said.

"He was going to look at you."

"I had it handled."

"You absolutely did not."

She opened her mouth, closed it, and then—unexpectedly—laughed, a short startled sound that the gorge took and multiplied. "No," she admitted. "I didn't. Thank you." She looked down the trail where Renn had gone. "He knew. About me. From the beginning."

"He knew from my feet," Mara said grimly. "Which means I'm done. Do you understand? Fourteen years of never staying past the permit date, never using my name, never standing between anyone and anything—and I blew all of it in one step, for you, without even thinking." They shook their head. "I used to believe invisible was safe. But invisible people can't keep promises. Invisible people can't stand in front of anybody. Voss handed me a job that requires showing up, and showing up is the one thing I taught myself never to do."

Ilsa considered this with her surveyor's patience, then nodded uphill, toward the treeline and the grey sky pressing down on it.

"Then we'd better show up fast," she said. "We've got two days and a storm coming, and my father's valley is above the snowline."

Mara looked up at where the trail vanished into cloud, and understood, with the strange lightness of a person who has finally been seen and survived it, that there was no going back down to invisibility—not for either of them.

"Two days," Mara said, and started climbing.
