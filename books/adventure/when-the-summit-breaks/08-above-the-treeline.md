## 8. Above the Treeline

The trees gave up before we did.

Ilsa noticed it the way you notice a friend going quiet—not all at once, but in stages. First the pines thinned into scrubby things that leaned away from the wind like commuters avoiding eye contact. Then the scrub became knee-high krummholz, then moss, then nothing but lichen and stone and a sky so low and gray it felt like a ceiling pressed down by an unfriendly landlord.

"We're above the treeline," Mara said, as if announcing a change in the weather rather than the loss of everything that had ever sheltered them.

"I can see that."

"Just checking. You've been quiet for two hours. I was worried you'd died politely and didn't want to make a scene."

Ilsa almost smiled. Almost. Her hands were too cold to trust with an expression. "I'm conserving energy."

"You're sulking."

"Surveyors don't sulk. We *reassess*."

Mara made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't frozen somewhere around four thousand feet. They climbed on.

The map—her father's map—showed the headwaters as a day's walk past the saddle, but maps lie by omission, and Ede Voss had drawn this one thirty years ago, in summer, presumably well-fed. What he had not drawn was the storm. It came off the western peaks in the late afternoon like something with intent: not rain at first, just wind with teeth, then sleet that hit sideways and stung like a grudge. By the time they found the overhang—a slab of granite tilted against the mountainside like a dropped playing card—the light was failing and so, Ilsa realized with clinical detachment, were they.

They had food for one more day. Half of one, if they were honest, which neither of them was.

They wedged themselves under the slab, backs to the rock, shoulders touching because there was no room not to touch, and watched the world turn white.

* * *

For a long time neither spoke. The wind did enough talking for both of them, moaning through the gap in the stones like a man rehearsing a complaint he'd never deliver.

"If we turn back," Ilsa said finally, "we lose three days. Maybe four, coming down in this. The permits are already voided. Every day is a day Renn's people close the gap."

Mara said nothing.

"Mara."

"I heard you."

"You're doing the thing where you agree with me silently and then do something else entirely."

"The *thing*," Mara said, "is called thinking. Some of us do it out loud only under duress." A pause. The wind filled it. Then, quieter: "We're not turning back. But I want you to know that I ran the numbers, and the numbers say we should."

"What do the numbers know about it?"

"Nothing. That's the problem." Mara pulled their knees up, wrapped arms around them. In the gray half-light they looked younger and older at once, the way exhausted people do. "Numbers used to be my whole religion. Load ratings. Tonnage. Safety factors. You put your faith in arithmetic and arithmetic doesn't lie to you, it just tells you what you asked it to. I told it what I wanted to hear, once."

Ilsa turned her head. She had spent six days with this person—six days of rope-ferry disasters and barracks fires and Mara's relentless, deflecting humor—and she knew better than to interrupt when the deflection stopped.

"There was a bridge," Mara said. "Eight years ago. The Corben trestle, over the Alder Fork. I signed the inspection report. I inspected nothing—I was tired, and behind schedule, and the company paid by the mile, and the report was a formality, everyone knew it was a formality." Their voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Three months later a freight crew took it at reduced speed, exactly per the posted rating. My posted rating. It held for eleven cars."

Ilsa waited.

"It didn't hold for twelve."

The wind screamed through the gap. Somewhere below them, snow hissed across the scree like sand through an hourglass.

"No one died," Mara went on. "That's the part I tell myself at night. The engineer saw the span sagging and reversed, and the crew walked out, and the company paid for a new bridge, and I was already gone—new line, new name, the usual. And every closed line I've drifted along since, every fake inspection, every permit date I've fled from—it wasn't just staying invisible. It was making sure I never again stood in a place where my signature could weigh anything. Because I knew what it weighed. Eleven cars' worth."

Ilsa found her voice took a moment to arrive. "You could have told me on the river."

"And had you look at me the way you're looking at me now? No thank you. I prefer my shame served cold, in private, with a side of self-pity."

"That's not how I'm looking at you."

"How are you looking at me, then?"

Ilsa considered. "Like someone who finally understands why you flinch every time I call you an engineer."

"I *am* an engineer."

"A retired one."

"A deserter," Mara said, and laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "God. Eight years, and saying it out loud costs less than carrying it did. Nobody warns you about that. All that effort keeping a secret, and it turns out the secret weighs more than the truth."

Ilsa pulled the last of the food from her pack—half a block of pemmican, a fistful of dried apples gone leathery—and split it without being asked. Mara watched her divide it unevenly, giving herself the smaller share, and didn't argue, which was its own kind of intimacy.

"My turn, I suppose," Ilsa said.

"You don't have to—"

"My father mapped this river the year of the collapse." She said it plainly, because she had decided, somewhere on the rope-ferry or in the barracks or maybe under this very sky, that plainness was the only way through. "Everyone knows that. Everyone knows he was blamed for it—that his survey was 'negligent,' that the dam failed where he said it would hold. His license revoked, his name on the inquest report, our family name a door slammed shut in Halloway. What nobody knows—" her jaw tightened— "is that I believed it. For years. I was fourteen, and the company men came to the house with their documents and their solemn faces, and I watched my mother stop defending him, and I thought: they wouldn't lie. Companies don't lie about things like this. Numbers don't lie."

Mara made a small sound. "About that religion—"

"Yes. Yours and mine apparently share a congregation." She stared out at the white. "So I became a surveyor to prove him wrong. Isn't that a terrible thing? I got licensed, and I took contracts, and every flood line I drew, some part of me was saying *see, Papa, this is how it's done properly.* And then the Kestrel concession came through, and I found discrepancies in their hydrology reports—small ones, deniable ones—and I flagged them. Officially. On letterhead." A breath. "They stripped my license in eleven days. Eleven. I learned more about how companies handle numbers in those eleven days than in nine years of practice."

"They lied about him," Mara said quietly. "And when you caught the same kind of lie, they punished you for seeing it."

"I don't know yet that they lied. That's why we're freezing on a mountain instead of drinking coffee in a valley town. I need to see what's at the source. I need the records, or the evidence, or whatever my father found that made him draw this map and hide it for thirty years." Her voice frayed despite herself. "Because here is the thing I've never said to anyone: I don't actually care about my license anymore. I care that he died last month in a rust-belt saloon believing his own daughter thought he was negligent. He traded me that map with his last strength, and I refused it, and I will climb this mountain on my hands and knees if that's what it takes to bring him back his name."

The wind gusted. Snow spattered against the granite above them like thrown rice.

Mara reached over and took the last piece of dried apple from Ilsa's palm and put it back in her hand. "Eat it. You'll need the hands and knees strength."

"That's yours."

"I'm trading it for a secret. Fair price." Mara leaned their head back against the stone. "Here's mine, since we're being reckless: I wasn't going to come past the saddle. Back at the barracks, I planned to get you to the treeline and invent a reason to stop—an injury, a bad omen, anything. Because if we find what you're looking for, I can't sell it. And if I can't sell it, I can't buy the new name, the clean papers, the fresh start I've been running toward for eight years. Finding the truth ends my invisibility. I've been more afraid of that than of Renn, or the mountain, or starving."

Ilsa looked at them—at this stubborn, funny, guilt-ridden person who had carried a dead man's promise through flooded camps and armed contractors for a woman who'd initially thrown the map back in their face.

"So why did you keep climbing?" she asked.

Mara was quiet a moment. "Because somewhere between the ferry and the fire, 'my escape' turned into 'our promise.' And it turns out I'd rather be found than free." They shrugged, embarrassed. "Don't quote me. I'm hypothermic and not responsible for my statements."

"Noted for the deposition."

* * *

The storm broke near midnight, as storms above the treeline do—abruptly, like a door closing on another room. Stars came out in ragged ranks, hard and cold and countless, and the snowfield glowed faintly blue, and the silence after the wind was so complete Ilsa could hear her own heartbeat and, she suspected, Mara's.

They slept in shifts, pressed shoulder to shoulder under the slab. In the morning the world was blinding and still, and the saddle ahead stood sharp against the sky, and beyond it—when Ilsa raised her father's map and checked it against the peaks, hand shaking, breath steaming—the terrain matched. Line for line. Contour for contour. Ede Voss had drawn true.

"He was good," she said. Her voice came out rough. "He was always good."

"He was," Mara agreed, shouldering their pack. "Runs in the family. Come on, Voss. Daylight's wasting, and I want to see the look on your face when we find what he left you."

They started up. The snow squeaked under their boots, and the mountain rose ahead of them, patient as a locked door—and far below, tiny as beetles, three figures had appeared on the trail they'd abandoned yesterday, moving fast, moving straight, following their tracks through the fresh fall.

Renn had found the line. And he wasn't stopping at the treeline either.
