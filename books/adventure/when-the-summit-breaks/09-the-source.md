## 9. The Source

**Mara**

The river stopped being a river an hour before we found where it started.

It thinned to a whisper, then to a seep, then to a dark stain on the rock that Ilsa knelt over and touched with two fingers like a doctor taking a pulse. "There," she said. "That's it. That's the whole famous Kestrel." She sounded almost disappointed, the way you do when you finally meet someone you've hated from a distance and they turn out to be small.

"Every big thing starts small," I said. "Bridges. Rivers. Lies."

She gave me a look that said *don't get philosophical at altitude*, which was fair. We'd been above the treeline for two days, eating the last of a food supply that had been optimistic even when we had it, and my hands still shook from the fall — hers too, though she'd never admit it. The scree slope had nearly taken her yesterday. I'd caught her wrist and held on while the mountain tried to argue, and afterward neither of us had said anything clever about it, because there wasn't any.

The adit was exactly where Voss's map said it would be, which surprised me more than it should have. Men who are dying tend to draw maps of places they wish existed. But there it was: a square mouth cut into the headwall, timbered, sealed with a steel plate bolted flush to the rock, painted the concession gray that means *this never happened*. Someone had stenciled a number on it years ago. The paint had run in the weather so the number read like a scream.

Ilsa stood in front of it for a long time.

"You okay?" I asked, which was a stupid question, and she was kind enough not to say so.

"He drew this," she said, tapping the map. "He drew this the year I turned nine. He was gone all summer. My mother told me he was surveying for the company." Her jaw worked. "He was surveying *against* them. He knew what they did and he came home and said nothing for thirty years because saying something would have gotten us all—"

She stopped. Wind came off the icefield and filled the pause for her.

"We don't know yet what's inside," I said. "Could be nothing. Could be empty crates and old boots."

"It won't be boots."

"No," I agreed. "It won't be boots."

The bolts were the old square-head kind, rusted to lace. I've spent my life around iron that pretends to be permanent, so I knew better than to trust its opinion. We had no wrench worth the name; we had a pry bar, a camp hammer, and Ilsa's surveyor stubbornness, which is worth more than both. It took most of an afternoon. Twice the bar slipped and put me on my back in the meltwater, and both times Ilsa laughed — really laughed, the first time since the rope-ferry — and I decided that if getting thrown into freezing water twice bought that sound, it was a fair exchange rate.

The plate came free at dusk with a groan like a coupler parting under load.

Cold air breathed out of the mountain. Not stale air — moving air, which meant another opening somewhere, which meant the company hadn't sealed this as tight as they'd thought. Inside, past the reach of our lamp, the tunnel went back into the dark the way tunnels do, patient and certain.

Ilsa went first. Of course she did.

* * *

**Ilsa**

My father taught me to read a tunnel the way other fathers teach daughters to read scripture: enter slow, listen first, touch nothing until you understand why it's there. So I entered slow. Mara's lamp threw our shadows huge against the timber, and the water sound followed us in — the little seep outside grown to a steady drip, drip, drip, like the mountain counting something.

Twenty paces in, the tunnel opened into a chamber, and the chamber was full of paper.

Not scattered. *Kept.* Steel cabinets in rows, army-surplus, their drawers labeled in a clerk's careful hand. A folding table with a lamp socket, long dead. A cot with a blanket folded on it, military corners, and beside the cot a tin cup set upside down on a crate, the way a man leaves things when he means to come back.

"He worked here," I heard myself say. "After. He came back and he copied everything."

Mara ran a hand along a cabinet drawer marked **HYDRAULIC SURVEY — UPPER POND — CONFIDENTIAL**. The metal was cold enough to burn. "Thirty years of coming back up here," Mara said quietly. "In secret. On a dead man's permit. To file papers nobody would ever read."

"Somebody's reading them now."

We opened the drawers.

I am a surveyor. Stripped of the license, yes — Halloway saw to that, and the company saw to Halloway — but stripped of the license doesn't strip the training. I know what a benchmark record looks like. I know what a dam inspection log looks like. And I know, the way you know a forged signature, when numbers have been made to agree with each other too neatly.

The upper pond records were beautiful. That's the word for it, ugly as it is: the company's own survey crew had measured the dam's failure points, month by month, and reported them, and the reports climbed the ladder with red annotations climbing alongside — *remediation deferred, cost cited, board review pending* — and then, in the last folder, dated four days before the collapse, a single page in a hand I didn't recognize:

**Detonation schedule approved. Upper pond retaining wall. Charges placed per attached diagram. Blame assignment per attached memo.**

Attached memo. They'd written a memo assigning blame before the charges were even lit. Voss's name was in it. Forty-one other names were in it. The men who died holding the wall they'd been ordered to hold, on a dam the company had already decided to break, so the disaster could bury the cost overruns and the deferred repairs and every report my father had spent his life filing.

"They dynamited their own dam," Mara said. There was no shock in it. Just the flatness of a person confirming a thing they'd suspected since a saloon in the rust belt. "And sold the funerals as an accident."

"And blamed the workers." My voice came out strange and calm. "They blamed my father for a murder *they committed*."

I sat down on the dead man's cot, under his folded blanket, and I laughed. I couldn't help it. Thirty years of shame I'd carried like ballast — the ruined license, the ruined name, every door closed by the word *Voss* — and it had all been built on a memo. A memo somebody typed before breakfast.

Mara crouched in front of me. "Ilsa. Listen to me. We take all of it. Every page. We carry it out if we have to carry it on our backs."

"We're not carrying forty pounds of paper over the scree."

"Then we copy what matters and we—" Mara stopped. Head tilted. That rail-man instinct, the one that hears a train before the rest of us hear wind.

Down the valley, faint but unmistakable: engines. More than one.

* * *

**Mara**

They came up the valley with lights, six of them, roped in pairs on the glacier tongue below the headwall, and Renn led the first pair. Of course Renn led. In my experience the ones who hesitate are always the ones sent first, because hesitation reads as commitment from a distance.

Ilsa wanted to make a stand in the adit. I talked her out of it with the oldest argument there is: paper burns, people don't have to. We took the key folders — the detonation schedule, the blame memo, three years of inspection logs — wrapped them in oilcloth inside Ilsa's coat, and went out the back way, the moving-air way, through a crawl that let onto the rock shoulder above the tunnel mouth. From there we watched our own discovery get surrounded.

Renn reached the steel plate, saw it hanging open, and did something I'll remember longer than the documents: he stopped his team with a raised fist and went in alone. First. A contractor who sends nobody in ahead of himself is either very confident or very tired of being what he is. I'd bet on tired.

We couldn't hear what happened inside. We could see the lamplight through the entrance, swinging, stopping. Long stops. The light stayed on the same spot for minutes at a time, and I knew what it was reading, because I'd read it too. Nobody swings a lamp slowly over old boots.

When Renn came out, he stood in the mouth of the adit with the wind pulling at him, and his team waited behind him in their neat roped pairs, and he didn't give an order. For a long time he just looked down the valley — down at the river, the drowned camps, Halloway's smoke far off, all of it the company's, all of it bought with that memo.

Then he took off his glove and put his bare hand flat against the steel plate, the way you'd check a dying animal for warmth, and turned around.

"Pack it up," he said, loud enough to carry to us on the shoulder. "Storm's coming. Nothing up here but an old survey cache."

One of his men — young, eager, the dangerous kind — stepped forward. "Sir, the map. Voss's daughter has to be—"

"I said pack it up." Renn's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which is worse. "You want to write in your report that you found the files? Go ahead. Write down what the files say. All of it. Read it out loud at the debrief." He let that sit. "No? Then we found an empty tunnel and a storm's coming. Move."

They moved. I watched them go down the glacier with their lights bobbing, a funeral procession walking the wrong direction, and I understood that Renn had just done the arithmetic we'd been avoiding for six weeks: some silences cost more than the truth does. He'd been paid to keep one his whole career. He'd just declined the renewal.

Ilsa gripped my arm hard enough to bruise. "He let us go."

"He let *himself* go," I said. "We just happened to be standing in the doorway."

We came down off the shoulder as the first snow of the storm started sifting through the headlamp beams, fat and slow, almost gentle. At the bottom, where the seep became the stream became the river, Ilsa unbuttoned her coat and checked the oilcloth packet with both hands, the way you check a child.

"Halloway," she said. "The survivors. The last open line. We get this downstream before the passes close."

"Mara Yarrow," I said, and it felt strange in my mouth, my real name, the first time I'd said it aloud in years, "at your service."

She looked at me — snow in her hair, her father's papers against her heart, laughing again, or maybe crying, with Ilsa it was going to be both — and pointed up-valley at the fading lights.

"Then let's go ruin some very expensive people's winter."

Behind us the mountain sealed its throat with snow, keeping its last secret for exactly as long as it takes paper to travel.
