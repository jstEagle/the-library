## 10. Downstream

The morning we came down off the mountain, the Kestrel ran brown and loud with snowmelt, and I stood on the bank for a long time just listening to it.

Six weeks ago I'd have told you a river was nothing but water in a hurry, something you bridged or forded or cursed when it flooded your ballast. Now I knew its whole biography. Every bend had a name Ilsa had given it, or an old one she'd dug out of her father's letters—Voss's Bend, the Widow's Eddy, the Long Shallows where the ferry rope still hung, frayed at the middle where my weight had nearly ended us both. The river had stopped being scenery somewhere around the treeline and become a companion instead, and companions, I'd learned, were not things you walked away from.

Ilsa came up beside me with the oilskin packet under her arm. She'd slept maybe two hours. She looked like a woman who'd been arguing with God all night and won on points.

"You're sure," she said. It wasn't quite a question. It was the question she'd been asking since the adit, since Renn stood down his crew in the mouth of that tunnel and let us walk out with the truth of the Kestrel disaster in a canvas satchel.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm done being sure of nothing."

She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "That's the most engineer answer I've ever heard."

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

* * *

Here is what we could have done, and let me tell you I thought about it hard enough to taste it.

There was a man in Copperline who bought identities the way other men bought boots—fitted to order, broken in by someone else first. A new name, clean papers, a work history stitched together from closed lines no auditor would ever walk. I'd have gone north, maybe across the border, taken a job on some coastal narrow-gauge where nobody asked questions because nobody cared about the answers. I'd have been safe. I'd have been nobody again, which after years of practice felt almost like home.

And Ilsa could have come with me. That was the seduction of it—the two of us, invisible together, the records sold off to some archive that would sit on them for fifty years until everyone they shamed was dead. Voss's name would stay mud. Halloway would keep its dead and its silence. Renn would go back to his contractors and his photograph of my face, and someday, on some platform, he'd either arrest me or nod at me like a man passing an old regret.

I stood in Copperline with the packet in my hands and priced out both futures like scrap iron.

The trouble with vanishing—I mean the real trouble, the one nobody tells you—is that you get good at it. You get so good that you start doing it preemptively. You leave rooms before conversations can end badly. You give false names to people who'd have liked your real one. And then one morning you're standing in a saloon holding a dying stranger's map, and he asks you for one promise, and you say yes mostly to be polite, because politeness is cheaper than connection and you've budgeted your whole life that way.

Ede Voss had died knowing exactly one thing about me: that I said yes. He'd bet his daughter, his name, and forty years of buried guilt on a yes from a woman whose entire profession was leaving.

So no. We weren't selling.

* * *

Halloway smelled the same as it had on the way through—coal smoke, river damp, bread—and this time nobody pretended not to see us.

The old woman's name was Petra Halloran, though I hadn't known it until then; on our first pass she'd been only "the widow who feeds people." Her husband had worked the dam crews the year of the collapse. His name was on the company's list of the blamed, third from the top, right below Ede Voss's.

We laid the survey records out on her kitchen table, and I watched sixty years of careful silence crack down the middle.

Petra read with her reading glasses pushed up into white hair, one finger tracing the figures, lips moving. Behind her, word had already spread the way it does in company towns—not loudly, but thoroughly, the way water finds every seam in a foundation. They came in ones and twos. A man with a millwright's forearms. Two sisters who'd been children in the flood camp. An old trackwalker I recognized from the line, who took one look at the dynamite requisitions—the company's own signed requisitions, dated nine days before the dam "failed"—and sat down slowly on a kitchen chair as if his legs had filed their resignation.

"They made us sign the blame away," he said. "Every man who survived. Severance papers with a fault clause buried in the fine print. I signed mine with this hand." He held it up, trembling slightly, and I understood it wasn't age.

"We know," Ilsa said gently. "Your copy's in here too. My father kept everything."

"He kept *everything*?" the sisters' elder said, half laugh, half sob.

"He was a surveyor," Ilsa said. "Precision was the only prayer he knew."

They stayed until dark. Nobody raised their voice. That was the thing about Halloway I never got used to—grief there was conducted at library volume, forty years deep. But by the time the lamps were lit, the plan had assembled itself the way plans do among practical people: not with speeches, but with a list of names, addresses, and skills, written in pencil on the back of a flour sack.

Petra's nephew drove freight on the last open rail line out of the valley—the very concession spur the company hadn't gotten around to sealing, because paperwork moves slower than dynamite. There was a printing works in Marrow Junction, three hours east, run by a man who owed Petra a debt older than any of us. Copies, hundreds of them. Then the newspapers, the land office, the families scattered from here to the coast who'd signed those severance clauses believing they had no choice.

"And after," Petra said, looking at me over her glasses with eyes that missed nothing, "they will come looking for the two of you. You understand that. Visibility has a price, girl. I've paid it."

"I've spent ten years paying the other kind," I said. "I'd like to try yours."

* * *

The freight left Halloway at dawn, and I rode it the way I ride everything—with one hand on the running board and my ear tuned to the rhythm of the wheels, listening for the flat spot, the tight journal, the lie in the machinery.

It was a good little engine. A Consolidation, older than me, kept alive by love and baling wire, and I told her so aloud, which earned me a look from Petra's nephew, Denny, who was maybe nineteen and had the wide-eyed terror of a young man sharing his cab with someone who talked to locomotives.

"You're really her," he said, somewhere past the second bridge. "Mara Yarrow. The one from the bridge report."

My stomach did something old and practiced. "You've heard of me?"

"Everybody on the lines heard of you. The Kettle Creek span. They said you flagged it yourself. Stopped the night train with six minutes to spare." He shook his head. "Then you just—went. Vanished. Some guys said you died."

"The reports of my death were greatly exaggerated by me," I said.

He laughed, then sobered. "Why'd you go? If you saved all those people?"

Because the inquiry found fatigue in the maintenance logs and a supervisor who'd cut the inspection schedule, and the company needed a scapegoat with a clean enough record to be believable, and I was twenty-six and tired and I let them have my name rather than fight a war I couldn't win. Because running seemed like survival. Because nobody told me that the person you run from follows you forever, riding third class, quiet as luggage.

"Because I was young," I said, "and I thought disappearing was the same as being free."

Denny nodded like that was the wisest thing he'd ever heard, which said more about nineteen than about me.

Somewhere past the water tower at Mile 40, Ilsa climbed into the cab, cold and smelling of coal smoke, and wedged herself against the boiler with the oilskin packet hugged to her chest like a child.

"It's done," she said. "Two hundred copies. The printer's sending more tomorrow. And a reporter from the *Marrow Junction Courier*—a real one, Mara, with a press and everything—wants the originals for the land office filing. Certified. On the record."

"On the record," I repeated. The words tasted strange, like a food from a country I'd left too young to remember.

"There's more." She hesitated, and Ilsa Voss did not hesitate; hesitation on her was like a wobble in a driving rod, alarming and diagnostic. "The surveyor's board sent a telegram ahead. They want to review my case. With the dam evidence public—" Her voice caught, and she stared ferociously out at the river tracking alongside the rails. "They might reinstate me. They might actually—"

"Ilsa."

"What."

"Breathe before you derail."

She laughed, wetly, and wiped her face with the back of a coal-blackened hand, achieving nothing except transferring soot to her cheekbone, and I decided not to mention it, because some things a friend lets you wear awhile.

* * *

We came back into Halloway four days later, and the town had changed the way weather changes—nothing you could point to, everything you could feel.

The concession notices were coming down off the company board outside the depot. Somebody had already nailed up a hand-lettered sheet over the vacancy: a meeting, Thursday, the church hall, *all persons with claims against the Kestrel Consolidated encouraged to attend.* The word *claims* had been underlined twice, in different inks, by different hands, which told you everything about how long that pencil list on the flour sack had been circulating.

Renn was on the platform.

I saw him from a hundred yards and my body did all its old arithmetic—exits, distances, cover—before my brain overruled it and made me keep walking. He was out of uniform, in a plain coat, carrying his hat instead of wearing it. A man between employments. The photograph of my face was nowhere in evidence, and I suspected it was currently serving some humbler purpose, like lining a drawer.

"Yarrow," he said.

"That's the name," I said, and heard it land in the open air, spoken aloud, unguarded, mine. It sounded smaller than I remembered and heavier too, like a tool you'd forgotten you owned and find you still need.

He studied me a moment. "I pulled trains on the Kestrel branch for six years," he said. "Before the company made me security. Best job I ever had." He put his hat on. "The board's reopening the collapse inquiry. They'll want witnesses. Rail men. People who can read a grade and a load table." He glanced toward Ilsa, waiting by the baggage cart, pretending not to listen with the world's least convincing disinterest. "People who know the line."

"Are you offering us work, Mr. Renn?"

"I'm telling you the valley's going to need rebuilding," he said, "and I'm told the new surveyor's license comes with authority to hire." He touched his hat brim—old rail habit, I saw it and recognized it, the small courtesy of one road's people to another—and walked off down the platform without waiting for an answer, which was, I thought, the most honest kind of offer there is.

Ilsa appeared at my elbow. "Was that—"

"That was a man buying back his soul at a discount," I said. "I respect it."

* * *

Thursday evening, the church hall was full past capacity, windows open to the river smell, and I stood at the back where engineers traditionally stand, near the exits, out of habit I no longer intended to honor.

Ilsa spoke first. She read her father's letters—not the survey figures, the letters, the ones where Ede Voss wrote to a daughter he was too ashamed to face, explaining year after year why he couldn't come home yet, why the truth wasn't safe yet, why next season, next season, next season. The hall was so quiet you could hear the Kestrel through the open windows, going about its business, keeping everything, giving it back.

Then Petra read the names. All of them. The drowned and the blamed, both lists, and it turned out they overlapped almost completely, which was the whole obscenity of it in one fact.

When it was over, people milled and wept and argued amiably about compensation claims, and I slipped out to the depot platform because even a reformed woman needs air.

Ilsa found me there, naturally. She always did. She leaned on the rail beside me, and below us the last light lay along the water like fresh rail steel.

"Denny says the branch line might reopen," she said. "Passenger service, even. Freight to the new camps. Someone will have to inspect it." A pause, delicately engineered. "Properly, this time. Not the way certain inspectors I could name inspected nothing."

"Slander."

"Certified slander. Filed with the land office." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Well, Yarrow? There's a valley here. There's a river. There's a line that needs walking. You could be nobody somewhere else, or you could be somebody here."

I looked downstream, where the water went on toward Halloway and past it, toward all the towns that would read the papers tomorrow, and I thought about names. About how I'd spent ten years handing out strangers' names like tickets, and how the first one I'd given back was to a dying man who needed it less than I did.

"Mara Yarrow," I said, out loud, to the river, to the valley, to whatever version of me had been waiting all this time on a decommissioned line somewhere, inspecting nothing. "Resident. Engineer. Staying."

Ilsa grinned. "Staying where? I've seen your idea of staying. You lasted eleven days at Copperline."

"Twelve," I said. "And that was before I had a reason." I nodded upstream, toward the headwaters, toward the sealed adit and the mountain that had finally given up its secret. "Besides, I hear the new surveyor needs someone who knows the tunnels. Someone reliable. Good with rope ferries."

"Terrible with rope ferries," Ilsa corrected. "But I suppose I could train her."

Down the valley, far off, a whistle blew—Denny's little Consolidation, hauling the first real freight the branch had carried in years, the sound rolling up the river like a promise kept late but kept whole. I listened to it the way I listen to everything, one hand on the rail, ear tuned for the lie in the machinery.

There wasn't one. For once, in the whole long song of the Kestrel, every wheel ran true.

"Come on," Ilsa said, pushing off the rail. "Petra's saving us seats, and if we're late she'll feed us anyway, but she'll make comments."

"Heaven forbid," I said, and followed her in, through the door, into the light and the noise and the names—all of them ours now, all of them true.
