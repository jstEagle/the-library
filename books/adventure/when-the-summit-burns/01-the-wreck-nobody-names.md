## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The sea at dawn was the color of old pewter, and Astrid Underhill loved it best that way—when it gave nothing away.

She had been on the water since four, anchored over a spot no chart named correctly. The charts called it a shoal, a hazard, a place for other boats to avoid. The locals called it nothing at all, because in three years of drifting from port to port along this coast she had learned that the surest way to keep a wreck safe was to make sure nobody spoke its name. The *Corvane* was a navy vessel, and navy vessels that sank under circumstances nobody wanted examined developed a kind of gravity. People looked away from them the way you look away from a scar on a lover's body—politely, deliberately, with a small tightening somewhere behind the ribs.

Astrid did not look away. She never had. That was her whole problem, if she was honest about it, and honesty was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago.

She checked her gauges a final time, spat into her mask and rinsed it, and rolled backward off the gunwale into water so cold it closed around her like a fist. Below, the light came down in long gray columns, and out of those columns the *Corvane* rose—or rather, sank upward toward her, its superstructure furred with weed, its hull split along the starboard side where whatever happened to it had happened fast.

Three years. She knew the wreck the way you know a house you grew up in. She knew the corridor that ran aft past the galley, the bulkhead door that jammed at forty degrees unless you lifted as you pushed, the cargo hold where the silt lay like snowfall and every fin-kick raised a slow gray ghost. She knew which portholes admitted enough light to work by and which ones were just black eyes staring at nothing. And she knew the engine room—the way you know the one room in a house you cannot enter without your chest going tight.

Not yet. Work first.

The strongbox was in the officers' wardroom, wedged beneath a collapsed bunk frame where she'd found it on her fourth dive, two seasons back. It was brass-cornered, naval issue, the lock corroded shut but the hinges sound. She'd spent weeks deciding whether to take it. Salvage law was a gray country and she lived there comfortably; a sealed strongbox off a wreck nobody officially acknowledged was exactly the kind of thing that could be sold quietly in the right harbor town, to the right collector, for enough money to keep her boat fueled and fed through another season of not being found.

But taking things from the *Corvane* felt different from taking things from other wrecks. Other wrecks were accidents. This one—

She pushed the thought down where she kept all the others, in the flooded compartment below her own waterline, and got to work.

The bunk frame had shifted since spring; she had to brace her shoulder against it and lift with her legs while her hands worked the box free, inch by inch, the silt blooming around her in clouds that ate the light. Her breathing went loud in her own ears, the regulator's rhythm the only clock that mattered. Air: fine. Bottom time: twenty minutes gone, twelve left before she started her ascent with a safety margin she actually intended to keep. Discipline. That was the word she'd built her life on since—since the engine room. Discipline was what fear looked like when it grew up and got a job.

The box came free with a groan of shifting metal that she felt through her gloves more than heard. Too loud. In open water sound carried, and above her, at the surface, there was a boat.

She'd seen it an hour ago, hanging at the edge of visibility: a gray hull, a white superstructure, the blunt silhouette of a patrol craft flying naval colors. It hadn't approached. It hadn't left either. It had simply been there, the way weather is there, circling in long patient arcs five hundred meters out, close enough that its wake reached her moored dinghy as a gentle rocking, far enough that she couldn't read the numbers on its bow.

Navy. Of course it was navy. Everything about the *Corvane* eventually circled back to the navy, the way everything in a whirlpool circles back to its own center.

She hugged the strongbox to her chest and kicked for the split in the hull, moving through the ship's drowned corridors with the economy of long practice. Past the galley, where a coffee urn still stood bolted to the counter, green with age, absurdly domestic. Past the bulkhead door she lifted as she pushed. Out through the gash in the starboard side into the gray columns of morning light, rising now, watching her depth gauge the way a gambler watches cards.

At fifteen meters she paused, hung in the water, and made herself do the thing she always made herself do on this wreck. She turned and looked down.

The *Corvane* sat on the bottom with its bow pointed out to sea, as if it were still trying to leave. Weed streamed from its rails like hair. Fish moved through the bridge windows in small silver schools, indifferent tenants of a house whose owners had never come home. From this angle, in this light, it almost looked peaceful. Almost looked like any other wreck, any other accident, any other story the sea had swallowed and kept.

Almost.

* * *

She surfaced beside her dinghy, breathing hard, and hauled the strongbox over the gunwale before she even pulled her fins off. Habit. You secured the salvage first; the salvage was the day's argument for existing.

Her boat—the *Kestrel*, thirty-two feet of aging trawler hull she'd bought with cash in a port two hundred miles south, registered under a name that wasn't quite hers—rode at anchor fifty yards off. She rowed to it in silence, loaded the box into the cockpit, covered it with a tarp, and only then let herself look up properly at the horizon.

The patrol boat was closer.

Not dramatically closer. Not threateningly closer, if you were the kind of person who didn't spend three years learning to measure threat distances the way other people measured tide tables. But it had come maybe two hundred meters nearer during her dive, and it had altered course, and its new course would bring it abeam of the *Kestrel* within the hour. Its wake still rolled out long and even across the pewter water, and standing at its rail, visible even at this distance as a darker smudge against the white paint, was a figure with binoculars.

Astrid sat down slowly on the cockpit bench, dripping, and made herself think it through the way she made herself think everything through now: calmly, sequentially, without the animal panic that wanted to start the engine and run. Running drew eyes. Running was a confession. For three years she had survived not by being fast but by being unremarkable—a woman alone on a working boat, diving wrecks, selling odds and ends, paying her harbor fees, keeping her logbook boring. Invisibility wasn't a cloak you put on. It was a craft, practiced daily, and its first rule was that you never moved until you understood why you wanted to.

Why did they want to look here? Nobody dove the *Corvane*. Nobody even talked about the *Corvane*. The fishing fleet gave it a wide berth—not superstition exactly, but something adjacent to it, the way men give a wide berth to a place where something bad happened and nobody was ever punished for it. In three years she had never once seen another dive boat on this spot. So why, on this particular gray morning, was a naval patrol tracing lazy figure-eights within binocular range?

Because someone had finally noticed her. Or because someone had always known, and had simply decided, today, to stop pretending otherwise.

She pulled off her hood and shook out her hair, and the cold wind off the water bit at her ears, and she was grateful for it. Cold kept you sharp. Warmth was for people who could afford to relax.

The strongbox sat under the tarp like a sleeping animal. She should open it—she'd need to know what she had before she could decide anything—but not here, not with a navy hull swinging closer by the minute. She'd run up the coast to the next harbor, the small one, the one with the rain-lashed bar where the fishermen drank and nobody asked questions because questions cost money to answer. She'd open it there, behind a locked cabin door, with the anchor light burning and the storm coming in. There was a storm coming in; she could feel it in the way the wind had begun to carry weight, that low pressure heaviness that meant the monsoon's leading edge was days out, maybe less.

Plenty of time. She had learned to tell time in small safe units—one harbor, one sale, one season—and to never, ever think about the larger arithmetic underneath. Three years of ports. Three years of waking at four, diving, selling, moving on before anyone learned her face well enough to describe it. Three years since the engine room.

And there it was again, the thought she'd pushed down, surfacing now the way dead things surface, patient and inevitable, because she was alone and the sea was quiet and there was nothing left between her and it.

The engine room.

She had gone in on her second dive, against every rule she'd set herself, because the hatch had been open and some part of her—some part older than discipline, some part that had made her a diver in the first place—needed to know what the ship was hiding. The ladders were rusted lace. The space beyond was black and tight and smelled of iron and old oil even through the water, and her lamp had swept across the deck plates and found them littered with things that were not tools.

Bones. And chains.

Men chained below decks when the ship went down. Not drowned trying to escape—chained so they couldn't. She had counted what she could bear to count before her air and her nerve both ran short, and then she had gone up into the light and never spoken of it to another living soul, because whoever had done that to those men was still alive, still powerful, still wearing a uniform or a title or both, and she had understood with total clarity that the sea was not the most dangerous thing she'd ever swum through.

She had been running from that room ever since. Port to port to port, each one smaller, each one farther from wherever the truth was kept.

The patrol boat's wake hissed against the rocks half a mile south. Through the binoculars she'd left mounted on the rail, the figure at its rail seemed to turn—seemed, impossibly, to look directly at her.

Astrid weighed anchor.

* * *

The *Kestrel* ran north with the storm building behind her like a wall of bruised cloud, and the strongbox rode under its tarp in the cockpit, and Astrid stood at the wheel telling herself the lie she told every day: that this time she'd sell the box, take the money, move on, stay invisible, stay alive.

But her hands remembered the weight of the chains, and the sea ahead was the same sea, and somewhere behind her the gray hull had turned to follow.

By the time she reached the harbor bar at dusk, the rain had begun—and there was a man waiting for her there, coughing blood into a rag, who said he had something to trade her that was worth more than gold and would cost her more than her life.

He said he knew she'd been diving the wreck nobody names.
