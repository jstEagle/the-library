## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The bar was called the Saltglass, which was a lie, because nothing in it had ever been clean. Rain hammered the corrugated roof in sheets, and the oil lamps guttered with every gust that found its way through the gaps in the walls, and the whole room smelled of wet wool, diesel, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had bothered to sell. Astrid chose the corner table with her back to the wall and a sightline to both doors, because three years of running teaches you that habits are cheaper than coffins.

She'd come in to dry out, nothing more. Her gear was stowed, the strongbox from the Corvane wrapped in canvas beneath a false floor panel in her bunk, and by morning she meant to be forty miles down the coast before anyone thought to ask what a lone woman with too much dive equipment was doing in a harbor like this. That was the plan. She had learned to hold plans loosely, the way you hold a knife—firmly enough to use, lightly enough to drop when someone bigger wants it.

The man at her table had not been invited.

He'd simply folded himself into the opposite chair while she was watching the door, as if he'd been there all along, as if chairs grew old men the way driftwood grows barnacles. He was gaunt in the way of men who have been eating less than they've been working, with a beard gone gray at the chin and hands that were enormous for his frame—the hands of a man who had spent sixty years pulling on rock and rope and other men's secrets. A rag lay curled in his fist, spotted dark.

"You're Underhill," he said. Not a question. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"I'm nobody."

"Nobody dives the Corvane." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his eyes—pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a fish or a drowned man. "Nobody runs three ports in two years, always ahead of the navy's survey boats. Nobody carries a wrecking bar ground down to look like a tire iron." He smiled, and it cost him; she saw the effort of it pull at something inside his chest. "I prospected this coast for forty years, girl. I know what work looks like, even when it's wearing a disguise."

Astrid did not move. Beneath the table, her right hand rested on her thigh, six inches from the knife in her boot. "Then you know what happens to people who talk about my work."

"What happens is they get visited by men in gray uniforms who smile too politely." He coughed then, deep and wet, and turned his face into the rag, and when he pulled it away she saw the blood—not flecked, not spattered, but soaked through, bright against the dirty cloth. He folded it over with the unhurried care of long practice and went on as though nothing had happened. "My name's Vann. Elias Vann. And I'm dying, which is why I can afford to be honest, and you can't afford not to listen."

Outside, the rain thickened until the harbor lights beyond the window became smears of gold, like paint run under water. Someone fed the stove in the corner and the room filled with the smell of burning kerosene and damp wood. Astrid signaled the barman for another coffee she didn't want, because an occupied table drew fewer eyes than an empty one, and because she wanted her hands around something warm while she decided whether this old man was a trap or a door.

"Talk," she said.

Vann reached into his coat—an oilskin coat older than she was, patched at the elbows with sailcloth—and drew out a package wrapped in waxed canvas and bound with tarred cord. He set it on the table between them but kept one huge hand flat upon it, the way a man keeps his hand on a dog that might bolt.

"Mount Serapine," he said. "You've heard the stories. Every drunk on this coast has. Caves in the mountain's flank, flooded when the war came, and somewhere inside them a cache that the navy has spent thirty years pretending doesn't exist." His pale eyes held hers. "It exists. I put half of it there myself."

The lamp between them fluttered. Somewhere near the stove a man laughed at a joke Astrid didn't hear, because her blood had gone quiet and cold, the way it went when she hung in blue water over a black opening in a hull.

"You hid it," she said carefully. "Or you found it."

"Both. In 'forty-one I was paid to move crates into those caves—crates I wasn't allowed to open, carried by men I never saw twice. I was young and the pay was good and the questions were dangerous, so I didn't ask." His hand trembled on the package, just once. "Twenty years later I went back with my own lamps and my own crowbar, and I opened one. Do you know what was in it?"

"Gold," Astrid said. "There's always gold."

"Gold, yes. Enough to buy this harbor twice." He shook his head slowly. "But under the gold—paper. Naval records. Ship's logs. Signals traffic. Orders signed in ink that certain very important men would burn the mountain down to un-sign." He leaned closer, and she smelled the sickness on him, sweetish, like fruit going bad. "And a manifest of souls, Miss Underhill. A list of every man aboard a certain ship when she went down, and where each one was berthed, and which ones were locked below."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way it did when she thought about the engine room. She made herself breathe. In through the nose. Out slow. The routine of it, the discipline of fear worn smooth as a river stone.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm the last one left who knows the way in, and I'll be dead inside a month—the doctor gave me till the rains end, and the rains started tonight." He pushed the package across the table until it touched her fingers. It was heavier than it looked. Dense. Waterproofed, she realized; sealed against exactly the kind of weather hammering the roof above them. "I've spent ten years looking for the right pair of hands. Not a patriot—they'd hand it to the navy and drown it again. Not a journalist—they'd print half and sell the rest. I need a thief. Someone who knows how to take things quietly and keep her mouth shut, except—" his finger came down on the package like a gavel— "except once. Except for this."

"And if I say no?"

He smiled again, that terrible economical smile. "Then I'll find someone else, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what you left on this table. But we both know you won't say no. You've been diving that wreck for three years, girl. You already know something's wrong with her. You just don't know how wrong."

* * *

She should have walked out. She knew it even as she unwound the tarred cord with fingers that seemed to belong to someone else, even as she peeled back the waxed canvas and saw what lay inside: a sheet of tough plastic, waterproofed and gridded, marked in faded ink with the coastline of Serapine and, threading inland from a cove on its northern shoulder, a sequence of bearings and depths and tide-tables annotated in a cramped, patient hand. A dive plan. A route into the drowned caves, drawn by the only man alive who had ever swum it.

There was a note clipped to the top, written in the same hand:

*Whatever you find down there, you tell the truth about it. All of it. To everyone. That's the price. Swear it.*

"It's not money you're asking for," Astrid said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, distant, as though she were listening through a hull. "Money I understand. This—"

"This is the only thing worth anything," Vann said. "I took their money to hide the truth, and I've been paying it back ever since, one coughed-up lung at a time. I won't die owing them." He gripped the edge of the table, and for a moment the enormous hands stopped shaking. "Swear it, Underhill. On whatever you still believe in. On the dead, if nothing else—you've seen enough of them."

She looked at the map. She looked at the blood-soaked rag, folded small in his fist like a bird he was trying to keep warm. She thought about the engine room, and the chains, and the gray hull turning to follow her out of the strait, and she understood—with the peculiar clarity that comes just before a decision you cannot undo—that the trap was already closed around her, had perhaps been closed since the day she first dropped onto the Corvane's deck, and that this old man was not springing it so much as showing her where she stood inside it.

"I swear," she said. "Whatever I find, I tell the truth about it."

Vann exhaled, long and shuddering, and some of the tension went out of him like air from a punctured hull. He pushed the map fully into her hands. "Northern cove. Dive only at slack water, and never after a storm—the tubes silt fast. And Underhill—" he caught her wrist as she rose, his grip still shockingly strong— "you won't be the only one reading this chart. There's a man. Navy salvage. Name of Reeve. He's been hunting this map longer than you've been diving. If he ever learns you have it—"

"He'll do what?"

Vann's colorless eyes held hers, and in them she saw something she had seen before, in the porthole glass of a drowned ship: the reflection of a person deciding how they will die.

"He'll burn everything," the old man said. "Starting with you."

* * *

She left the Saltglass past midnight, the map sewn into the lining of her jacket, and the rain had settled into a steady, businesslike downpour that turned the dock boards to black glass. She meant to go straight to her boat, cast off, and be gone before dawn—but her feet took her instead along the shanty row above the tideline, past the lean-tos and tar-paper shacks where the harbor's poorest dried their nets and nursed their fevers, because Elias Vann had told her, in the last minutes before they parted, which shack was his, and had asked her—if she wouldn't come, if she wouldn't help carry the cache out—then at least to remember where he lived.

She told herself it was caution. She told herself she wanted to see who else might be watching the old man, so she could avoid them later. Both things were true, and neither was the truth.

The shack sat on stilts above a mudflat stinking at low tide, a crooked silhouette against the gray-black night, one window lit with a candle's worth of glow. As she paused in the shadow of a net-drying rack, she saw figures moving along the shore path below—four of them, maybe five, moving with the unhurried coordination of men who had done this sort of work many times and expected no interference. They carried jerry cans. She smelled the petrol before she understood what she was seeing.

One of them hammered at Vann's door. Words exchanged, too low to carry through the rain. Then the candlelight inside went out, and for a long moment there was only the drumming rain and the slap of water against the mudflat pilings—and Astrid's own heartbeat, loud in her ears, telling her to run, telling her that whatever happened next was none of her affair, that survival was a craft and its first rule was *don't be seen*.

Then fire bloomed in the shack's doorway, orange and sudden, and the rain hissed against it, and a shadow moved behind the flame—a big shape, staggering, silhouetted—and she heard, thin across the water, the sound of an old man screaming.

She did not move. That was the thing she would hate herself for afterward, lying sleepless in her bunk with the map pressing cold against her ribs: she stood in the shadows with her hand over her mouth and she did not move, because moving meant dying, and three years of running had taught her body obedience better than any conscience could teach it defiance.

The shack burned fast, the way tar paper burns, and the men walked away unhurried into the rain, and by the time the flames collapsed inward and the stilts gave and the whole structure slid hissing into the mudflat, the shore path was empty and the harbor was waking, and voices were shouting, and someone was ringing the church bell, and Astrid Nystrom stood alone in the dark with a dead man's map against her heart and a promise in her mouth that suddenly weighed more than all the gold in Mount Serapine.

*Whatever you find, you tell the truth about it.*

They hadn't burned him for the gold. They'd burned him for the truth—and now, God help her, the truth belonged to her.

Behind her, at the far end of the dock, a lantern swung. A figure stood at the head of her gangplank, young, broad-shouldered, soaked to the skin, watching the burning wreckage with an expression she couldn't read—and then, slowly, deliberately, turning to look directly at her hiding place in the shadows, as if he had known all along exactly where she stood.
