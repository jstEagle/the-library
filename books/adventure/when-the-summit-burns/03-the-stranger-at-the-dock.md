## 3. The Stranger at the Dock

Tomas had been watching the woman for two days before he let himself be seen.

He'd learned patience from his grandfather — or from the man he'd believed was his grandfather, back when belief was a simple thing and Elias Vann was just an old prospector who smelled of pipe smoke and kerosene and came home twice a year with river mud on his boots and stories that never quite added up. Patience was the first lesson: *Sit still longer than the thing you're watching thinks you can sit still.* The second lesson had come later, in a letter that arrived after the funeral, in handwriting Tomas didn't recognize: *If you ever need to find me, look for the woman who dives where divers don't dive.*

So Tomas sat still. He sat in the shade of a fish-drying rack at the end of the harbor road with a chipped mug of coffee he never drank, and he watched Astrid Underhill work her boat like a surgeon works a body she's already given up trying to save.

She was smaller than he'd imagined, and harder. That was the word that kept coming back to him — not tough, not weathered, but *hard*, like driftwood, like something the sea had spent years sanding down to its essential grain. She moved through her morning routine with a precision that looked casual until you counted it: checking the mooring lines first, always the port line before the starboard; testing the bilge pump by sound alone, head tilted, eyes elsewhere; scanning the harbor mouth every eleven minutes, give or take, without appearing to look at all.

Three years of that rhythm, he thought. Three years of ports like this one — salt-bleached towns where the navy's shadow reached thin and the questions people asked were lazy ones. He knew because Vann had told him, in the last month, coughing blood into a rag while Tomas held his shoulders and begged him to see a doctor in the city. *Find her,* the old man had said. *Not for the map. The map is nothing. Find her because she saw what I hid, and she doesn't know yet why it matters.*

The rain started around noon, the way it did here — not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the strait. Most sensible people went indoors. Astrid Underhill loaded salvage crates onto her deck as if the monsoon were a rumor she'd declined to believe.

Tomas set down his untouched coffee and stood up for the first time in six hours.

* * *

He crossed the dock while she was below, in the cabin, and made himself visible the honest way — standing in the open, hands loose at his sides, letting her find him when she surfaced. He'd rehearsed this. Not the words so much as the posture: a man with nothing to hide looks like a man with nothing to hide, and everything else follows.

She came up the companionway ladder fast, which surprised him. He'd expected caution. What he got instead was a woman who moved like someone who had already decided how she'd kill him if it came to that, and was simply waiting to see whether it would.

"You've been sitting by the fish racks since yesterday," she said. No greeting. Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had trained herself out of surprise.

"Two days," Tomas admitted. "You only noticed yesterday."

Something flickered behind her eyes — annoyance, maybe, or respect. "Who sent you?"

"No one."

"Nobody sits in the rain for two days for free." She stepped fully onto the deck and stood between him and the cabin door, arms folded, rain running off her elbows. "Vann?"

The name landed in Tomas's chest like a dropped anchor. "You knew him."

"I knew a dying man who talked too much in a bar." Her gaze didn't waver. "That's not knowing."

"He was my grandfather."

It was the truth, or close enough to the truth that saying it aloud hurt in the old familiar place. He watched her weigh it. He could almost see the arithmetic running behind her face — the calculation of what Elias Vann's bloodline might be worth to her, or cost her.

"He never mentioned family," she said.

"He never mentioned you either. I found your name in his papers." A half-truth. The papers had been a single letter, and the name had been written in a hand that shook. "Astrid Underhill. Diver. Moves ports every few months. Last seen working a wreck off—" he paused deliberately— "the Corvane."

The change in her was small and total. Like watching a lock throw. Her weight shifted back onto her heels, her hands came apart and settled at her sides, and the harbor suddenly felt colder than the rain could account for.

"That wreck has no name," she said quietly. "Ask anyone here. Ask the harbormaster. There's no Corvane in these waters."

"There's a wreck three miles east with naval plating and a hull number someone ground off badly," Tomas said. "I grew up on boats. I know bad grinding when I see it."

For a long moment there was only the rain drumming on the dock boards, filling the silence the way water fills a hull — steadily, from below.

"The map is mine," Tomas said. "Whatever he gave you, whatever he promised you — it's my inheritance. I'm not asking you to hand it over. I'm asking you to tell me what he asked of you."

* * *

Later, Tomas would replay this moment a hundred times and understand that this was where it turned — not at the ambush, not at the gunshots, but here, in the pause where she decided whether to lie to him.

"He asked me to tell the truth about what I found," she said finally. "Whatever it was. Wherever it led."

"And you took the promise?"

"I took the promise."

"Then we have a problem," Tomas said, "because the truth is exactly the thing that gets people killed around here, and my grandfather knew that better than anyone alive."

Her mouth twitched — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, gone before it committed to anything. "Then he asked a dying man's favor. Those are always expensive."

Tomas opened his mouth to answer, and that was when he saw them.

Two men coming along the dock from the harbor road, moving wrong. It wasn't anything dramatic — that was the point, that was the whole craft of it. They weren't hurrying, weren't looking at the boat, were dressed like fishermen and walking like accountants, and their eyes were doing the thing his grandfather had taught him to spot decades before he understood it: reading reflections. Checking windows, checking the water, checking the chrome fitting on a piling, anywhere a face might appear without facing them directly.

"Tomas," Astrid said, and he realized she'd seen his attention shift and followed it. "How many?"

"Two. Coming from the road. There'll be a third covering the water side if they're any good."

"They're any good," she said flatly. "They've been anchored off the point since dawn. Navy tender, no markings."

His stomach dropped. "Reeve."

"You know that name?"

"I know my grandfather stopped sleeping through the night four months ago, and the name he said in his sleep was Reeve."

The men were thirty meters out now. One of them had let his jacket fall open, casually, the way a man does when he wants you to see the shape under it before he needs to use it. Behind Astrid, the harbor lay gray and empty in the rain. Ahead of them, the two walkers closed the distance with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this before and been paid well for it.

Astrid moved. There was no decision in it that Tomas could see — no glance, no word — she simply became motion, stepping past him, pulling a coiled mooring line off its cleat as she went, and then she was walking toward the two men with the line hanging loose from her fist and her expression arranged into the bland, forgettable face of a salvager annoyed at being bothered.

Tomas understood a half-second too late what she was doing. She was making herself ordinary. Visible, harmless, unremarkable — the exact opposite of what he would have done, which was run. Running confirmed you were worth chasing.

The taller man stopped her ten feet away. Words were exchanged — Tomas couldn't hear them over the rain, and hadn't been meant to. Then the second man drifted wide, flanking toward the boat, toward Tomas, and his hand went inside his jacket, and the whole careful theater of it collapsed at once.

"Down!" Tomas shouted, and threw himself sideways off the dock.

He hit the black water between the pilings as the first shot cracked the rain open. It wasn't aimed to kill — it was aimed to herd, he realized, treading water under the dock's shadow, salt stinging the graze along his forearm where a splinter of dock wood had caught him. They wanted her alive. Or they wanted her *taken*, which was worse. You shot to kill a witness. You shot to herd an asset.

Above him, through the gaps in the planking, he heard the mooring line sing — Astrid's line, whipping — heard a man grunt and go down hard, heard the splash of the second man stumbling back from the edge. Then her boots on the deck above, fast, and the deep mechanical cough of an engine turning over.

Tomas swam for the stern.

* * *

She hauled him aboard by the collar with a strength that suggested she'd hauled heavier things out of deeper water, dumped him on the deck plates, and put the boat hard out of the harbor before she said a single word. Rain lashed the wheelhouse windows. Behind them, the two men stood on the receding dock, not pursuing, not waving — just watching, which frightened Tomas more than pursuit would have. Men who chase you are worried about you. Men who watch you leave already know where you're going.

Astrid steered with her jaw tight and her eyes cutting constantly to the rearview of the harbor mouth. Only when the point had swallowed the town did she finally speak.

"You knew they were there," she said. "Before I did. Before they showed."

"My grandfather taught me to read a dock."

"Elias Vann taught you to read a dock." She said it like she was filing it somewhere permanent. "And Elias Vann left you a letter with my name in it, and you found me, and twenty minutes later men with navy guns try to take me off my own boat." She turned and looked at him fully for the first time — really looked, the way she must have looked at wrecks, assessing structure, judging what would hold. "Either you're the best bait I've ever seen, or you're the only person in this story who isn't lying to me. I genuinely can't tell which."

"Neither can I," Tomas said honestly. His arm was bleeding onto the deck plates. "That's the problem."

Something shifted in her face then — not softening, nothing so generous, but a kind of grim recalibration. The arithmetic changing. Two people who trust no one, deciding — provisionally, expensively, with guns still echoing somewhere behind them — to trust each other anyway, because the alternative was drowning separately.

She tore a strip off a rag, tossed it to him. "Bind that. Then get below and tell me everything your grandfather wrote in that letter. Every word." She turned back to the wheel, and her voice dropped, almost to herself. "Because I think we just found out what the promise costs."

Ahead of them, far up the strait, Mount Serapine stood with its head in the storm clouds, and the first true bands of the monsoon were reaching out across the water like fingers closing.
