## 4. Commander Reeve

The rain had not stopped in nine days, and Commander Aldous Reeve had stopped noticing it somewhere around the fourth.

He sat in the back room of the customs house at Port Calloway, a room that smelled of mildew, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of men who had been sitting too long in wet wool. The window was shuttered against the storm. A single electric bulb hung from a wire, swinging faintly with the building's breathing, throwing his shadow across the table in long, patient arcs.

The man across from him was called Ferro, and he had been an informant for eleven years, which in Reeve's experience meant he had been a coward for eleven years and had simply found a way to be paid for it. He was a small man with a diver's ruined ears and hands that would not stay still. They were not still now. They turned his cap around and around on the tabletop like a man winding a clock that told no time he wanted to hear.

"Say it again," Reeve said. "Slowly."

Ferro swallowed. "The Underhill woman. She dove the Corvane. Dawn, day before yesterday. I saw her tender anchored over the wreck myself—right over the number three hatch, if you want it precise. She was down maybe forty minutes. Came up with something wrapped in canvas. Stowed it under her forward berth before she even bailed out the bilge."

"You're certain it was her."

"I've watched her three seasons now, Commander. Same woman. Dives alone, sells quiet, never drinks where the navy drinks." Ferro's laugh came out cracked. "She's careful. That's how I know she's worth watching."

Reeve said nothing for a moment. The bulb swung. The shadow crossed Ferro's face and left it again, and the little man flinched at nothing, which Reeve considered the most honest thing he'd done all evening.

Forty minutes on the Corvane. Alone. Down through the number three hatch, which meant past the collapsed galley, along the port corridor, into the country of the dead. Most divers couldn't do it. Most divers who tried came up gray-faced and never went back, and they talked about what they'd seen for the rest of their lives in the low voices men used for ghosts.

This woman had gone down and come up with cargo.

"And Vann," Reeve said. "Elias Vann. What has he been doing?"

"Selling. Everything. His boat, his gear, his claim stakes upriver. He's sick—you can hear it across a room, that cough. And he's been asking after divers. Good ones. Ones who don't ask questions." Ferro hesitated. "There's talk he passed something to someone. A paper. Sealed in wax and oilcloth. Nobody's seen it close, but—" He spread his damp hands. "A dying man doesn't sell maps, Commander. He gives them away. And he gave one away."

The cap stopped turning.

Reeve rose, crossed to the shuttered window, and stood with his back to the room. Through the slats he could see a slice of the harbor: masts swaying, running lights smeared by rain, and beyond the breakwater the black water where the strait deepened toward Serapine. Somewhere out there, under two hundred feet of cold green dark, lay the answer to a question he had spent thirty-one years making sure no one would ever ask aloud.

"Do you know what the Corvane was, Ferro?" he said, without turning.

"A cruiser. Went down in the war. Bad weather, they said."

"They said." Reeve's voice was level, almost gentle, which those who knew him understood to be the most dangerous register he possessed. "I was aboard her when she went down. Junior gunnery officer. Twenty-four years old, and so new my uniform still smelled of the tailor's shop." He touched the window frame, lightly, the way a man touches a scar. "Two hundred and six souls. The official record says she struck a mine in heavy seas and sank with all hands lost in the line of duty. There is a wall in the capital with her name carved into it, and every year men in dress uniforms lay wreaths, and every year the sea keeps its mouth shut."

"That's—" Ferro began.

"That is order," Reeve said. He turned then, and the light caught his face, and Ferro looked away from it. "You have never served, so let me explain something to you about institutions. An institution is not its ships or its uniforms or its victories. An institution is a story the living agree to tell about the dead. Break the story and you break the institution, and when the institution breaks, everything it holds up breaks with it. Borders. Supply lines. The price of bread in towns these people have never heard of." He came back to the table and leaned on it, both palms flat. "I did not survive that sinking, Ferro. I was *ordered* off her. Ordered, by a captain following sealed instructions, while the engine-room watch was secured below. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

Ferro's throat worked. "No, sir. I don't think I do."

"No," Reeve agreed quietly. "And that is precisely why you will forget everything you've just told me the moment you leave this room. You are very good at forgetting. It's why I keep you."

* * *

After Ferro had gone, pale and eager to be elsewhere, Reeve sat alone with the lamp and allowed himself, as he allowed himself perhaps twice a year, to remember.

Not the sinking itself—he had made a discipline of not remembering the sinking, the way other men made disciplines of prayer. He remembered instead the morning after, when the rescue cutter had picked him out of the water among eleven other survivors, all officers, all topside men, all ordered off. He remembered standing on the cutter's deck in a blanket, watching the oil slick spread on the swell, and hearing Lieutenant Commander Halse—the salvage officer sent down from the admiralty, brisk, competent, already composing his report—say to another survivor, within Reeve's hearing:

*Mine damage. Tragic. The board will confirm it.*

And Reeve, twenty-four years old and half-dead with cold, had understood. Not all at once—it had taken him years, and a career, and a slow climb through the salvage directorate to understand fully—but in that moment he had understood enough: that the truth of the Corvane was not an accident waiting to be discovered. It was a decision. Made in rooms like this one, by men like himself, and defended ever since by men like himself.

He could have hated it. Some part of him, in the early years, had. But hatred was a luxury for men without responsibilities, and Reeve had come to see his work plainly for what it was: custodianship. The dead of the Corvane were beyond harm. The living were not. Two hundred and six names on a wall bought a great deal of stability, and stability, Reeve had learned, was the only thing standing between the strait and the chaos that lived just beneath the surface of all human affairs, patient as pressure.

If a map existed—if Elias Vann, that treacherous old scavenger, had spent thirty years sitting on proof and had now, in his dying, begun handing it to strangers in harbor bars—then the story was in danger. And if the story broke, it would not be the admiralty that drowned. It would be the widows' pensions, the fleet appropriations, the treaties signed on the assumption that the navy's word was good. It would be everything.

He was not a villain. He wished people would understand that, though of course no one ever would, because history was written by whoever surfaced last. He was a man holding a door shut against a flood, and the fact that the flood was truth changed nothing about the drowning.

Reeve took out a cigarette, considered the rain outside, and did not light it. He had quit seven years ago. Discipline was the whole architecture of his life; one did not renovate the load-bearing walls.

Instead he opened the harbor registry—a courtesy copy kept in the customs house—and ran his finger down the mooring assignments until he found her. *Kestrel's Wake*, thirty-foot ketch, single owner-operator, registered out of three different ports in five years, each transfer clean, each departure quiet. Astrid Underhill. No debts. No family listed. No vices on record except solitude.

Three ports in five years. She moved the way a woman moves when she is listening behind her.

She had taken something off the Corvane. She might already hold Vann's paper. And she was, at this moment, the single greatest threat to everything Reeve had built his life upon—not because she was clever or dangerous, but because she was *invisible*, and invisible things were the hardest kind to kill cleanly.

He pulled a fresh form toward him and wrote in a small, exact hand: *Flag Kestrel's Wake for harbor inspection. Priority. Discreet.* Then, after a moment, a second line: *Surveillance only. No boarding until I arrive.*

Because a woman like that would run at the first hard hand laid on her, and a running woman with a map was worth ten dead informants. Let her sail. Let her think herself unseen. Watch where she pointed her bow, and the sea itself would testify against her.

He blotted the form, rang for the duty clerk, and stood at the shuttered window while the rain went on hammering the coast like a fist on a door.

Out past the breakwater, far to the north, Mount Serapine stood invisible inside the storm, and somewhere beneath it, in drowned caves that appeared on no chart, thirty years of patience waited in the dark.

Reeve had waited thirty years too. He could wait a little longer.

But not much.

By morning, the flag order would reach the harbor watch. By nightfall, word of it would travel the way such words always traveled—through dockhands and barkeeps and small frightened men like Ferro—to the ears of a woman who trusted no one.

Which meant, Reeve reflected as he buttoned his coat, that whatever happened next would happen fast.

He intended to be there when it did.
