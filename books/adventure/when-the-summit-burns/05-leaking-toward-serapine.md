## 5. Leaking Toward Serapine

They cleared the harbor at slack water, under a sky the color of wet slate, and Astrid did not look back.

Looking back was a habit she'd trained out of herself years ago. Ports were doors you closed behind you; harbors were mouths that swallowed names. She kept her eyes forward on the gray channel, hands steady on the wheel of the *Kestrel*, while Tomas crouched in the cockpit behind her with Vann's map spread across his knees beneath an oilcloth, studying it the way other men studied scripture.

"The strait first," he said. "Then north along the coast until we pick up the old survey markers. Grandfather said there's a river mouth below Serapine where we can hide the boat."

"Your grandfather said a lot of things to a stranger in a bar," Astrid said. "You'll forgive me if I don't chart my life by them."

"He was dying. Dying men don't lie about maps." Tomas folded the oilcloth with exaggerated care, as if patience could be demonstrated into existence. "They only lie about themselves."

She had no answer for that, so she gave him none. The wind was building out of the southwest, long white streaks dragging across the water ahead of them like fingers testing a surface. The monsoon's edge. Every fisherman in the last three ports had told her the same thing: two weeks, maybe less, and then the sky would close for a month. Whatever they meant to do at Serapine, they would do it inside that window or not at all.

Reeve's navy would be inside it too. That thought she kept where she kept all her worst ones—down deep, in the cold compartment where the engine room lived.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the sea had opinions.

The swell came in long and confused from two directions at once, the kind of water that found every weakness in a hull and worried at it like a dog with a bone. The *Kestrel* was thirty years old and honest about nothing; Astrid had patched her twice in two ports with materials she preferred not to think about. She ran her anyway, because running was what they both knew how to do.

Tomas proved better than she expected. He didn't complain, didn't ask questions she couldn't answer, and when she barked an order he moved without needing it repeated. He took the wheel through the worst of the cross-sea while she went forward to double-lash the dive gear, and when a wave broke green over the bow and knocked him half off his feet he simply stood back up and kept steering, jaw set, eyes on the compass.

"Where did you learn to handle a boat?" she shouted over the wind.

"River barges. Upcountry." His knuckles were white on the spokes. "Grandfather said salt water gets in your blood and never leaves. I'm starting to believe him."

The admission cost him something—she could see it in the set of his shoulders—and she understood why. Trusting her with even that much was a coin spent. She filed it away next to everything else she knew about him: that his hands were diver's hands, scarred at the fingertips; that he flinched, very slightly, at the word *navy*; that he had appeared at her dock within hours of Vann's shack burning, which meant either remarkable luck or remarkable information.

Around dusk the wind backed two points and began to blow with intent. Astrid shortened sail, then shortened it again, and they ran north under a scrap of canvas with the first real rain coming down in slanting sheets. The temperature dropped. The light went. And somewhere in that failing gray, the sea reached down and found the *Kestrel*'s weakness after all.

She heard it before she felt it—a sound like a knuckle cracking, but larger, deeper, felt through the soles of her boots more than heard. Then the bilge alarm, which she'd rigged herself from salvage parts, began its thin insistent screaming.

"Tell me that's the float switch sticking," Tomas said.

"It's not the float switch sticking."

She got the floorboards up. The lantern showed her the truth in one ugly glance: a seam aft, port side, just forward of the engine bed, had opened a hand's width of black gap, and the sea was coming through it in pulses timed to the swell. Not gushing. Insisting.

"We're taking water faster than the pump likes," she said. "Get the buckets."

"Buckets."

"Buckets. Welcome to sailing."

* * *

They bailed through the night.

It became a rhythm, and rhythms were things Astrid trusted more than people. Tomas on the bucket, passing full ones up and over the rail, hauling empties back down; her at the pump when her arms allowed, then back to the wheel, then down again with the lantern held between her teeth to watch the water line. The rain hammered the deck. The wind screamed in the rigging with a sound like a woman being unreasonable. Somewhere behind them, the coast they'd left was dissolving into storm, and somewhere ahead, Mount Serapine waited under its own private weather, patient as a debt.

Somewhere past midnight, soaked to the skin and shaking with cold and effort, Tomas started talking.

She didn't ask him to. That was the thing she noticed later—the way the danger loosened something in him that daylight and caution kept shut. People talked in shipwrecks. She'd seen it before. The sea stripped away whatever it was men used to keep their histories buttoned up, and what floated free was usually simpler and sadder than anything they'd have said ashore.

"He wasn't always a prospector," Tomas said, hauling another bucketful of black water over the rail. "My grandfather. That's the story he let people have. Scruffy old man, picks and pans, lucky now and then. It kept questions away."

"What was he really?"

"A salvage man. Before your time, maybe before mine." A wave slapped the hull; the crack in the seam seemed to answer it, and Tomas grimaced and worked faster. "He worked contracts. Wartime mostly. When the navy wanted something recovered quietly—or something hidden quietly—he was one of the men they called. He used to say he had the best hands in the archipelago and the worst luck in knowing what those hands were holding."

Astrid fed the pump another stroke. Her shoulders burned. "Hiding things is easier than finding them. You just put it somewhere and drown it."

"That's what he said. Almost word for word." Tomas looked at her across the sloshing bilge, rain streaming off his hair, and there was something raw in his face that hadn't been there at the dock. "He hid things for them for eleven years. And then one job—he wouldn't say which one at first, not for years—he couldn't do it anymore. Because of what the thing was."

The bilge water sloshed against her boots, cold as confession. "What was the thing?"

"Records." He said it carefully, watching her. "Paper. Crates of it. Naval paper—logs, orders, manifests. Things written by officers' own hands that the officers very much wanted unwritten. He was paid to take it out to Serapine and seal it in the caves where no one would ever dive. Paid well. Paid by—" Tomas hesitated, and the hesitation told her more than the words that followed. "Not by the navy. That's what he finally told me. The money came from families. Crew families. Mothers and widows who'd been told their men were lost with honor, and who didn't believe it, and who scraped together what they had to buy the proof someone upstairs was trying to bury."

The wheel felt suddenly far away. Astrid gripped the pump handle and made herself keep working, because if she stopped moving she was afraid of what her face would do.

Families. Widows scraping coins together to buy the truth of how their husbands died, hiring one old salvage man to drown it safely until the world was ready—which was to say, until the officers who wrote the orders were dead or promoted beyond reach. And instead the old man had kept it hidden for decades, dying slowly in a shack by the water, coughing blood into a rag, waiting for someone he could hand it to.

"Why hide it?" she asked. "If the families paid him to preserve it, why not give it back? Why sit on it thirty years?"

"Because giving it back gets people killed." Tomas's voice was flat and certain, the voice of a man repeating a lesson learned young. "He tried once, early. Sent copies to a newspaper office in the capital. The reporter who opened the envelope drowned within the month. Officially an accident. After that, Grandfather understood: the paper stays safe exactly as long as it stays lost. He was waiting for the men who signed the orders to die off. Waiting for the institution to change enough that the truth could survive being spoken." He wiped rain from his eyes with the back of his wrist. "And then he got sick, and he ran out of waiting."

The wind gusted. The *Kestrel* staggered, wallowed, righted. Astrid looked at the boy—not a boy, he was near thirty, but he seemed younger tonight, hollowed by bailing and grief—and felt the strange, unwelcome warmth of something she hadn't felt in three years. Not trust. Nothing so reckless. But the sense of standing next to someone in the same cold water, which was its own kind of company.

"You knew about the Corvane," she said. "At the dock. You knew too much."

"I know what Grandfather knew. That the Corvane didn't sink. She was sunk." He met her eyes steadily. "With her crew aboard. Chained below, some of them. And I know that the officer who gave that order signed his name to it, and that officer is now the navy's chief of salvage, and has spent thirty years making sure no one ever reads what's in those crates." A pause, and then, quieter: "I know you've seen inside that wreck, Astrid Underhill. Grandfather watched you dive her for two seasons before he ever spoke to you. He said you were the only one who came up looking like you'd seen a ghost and refused to forget its face."

The rain filled the silence between them. Astrid turned back to the wheel because her hands needed work and her face needed hiding, and steered the broken boat north through the dark.

"I don't talk about what I saw," she said finally.

"I know. He said that too. Said it was why you were the one."

* * *

Dawn came gray and shivering, and with it the mountains.

Serapine rose out of the sea haze ahead like a fist, green-black slopes running straight down into churning water, its summit wrapped in cloud that was not cloud—she could see the rain falling from it in solid curtains, the monsoon sitting on the peak like a thing nesting. Below the clouds, near the waterline, a notch in the coastline marked the river mouth Vann's map promised. It was barely a slot. It would have to do.

The *Kestrel* limped toward it, riding low, the pump going constantly now. They'd slowed the leak with sailcloth and tallow and one of Astrid's dive weights jammed against the seam, but slowing wasn't stopping, and they both knew the arithmetic of boats: eventually the sea wins on points.

"We beach her, careen her, patch her proper," Astrid said. "Then we dive."

"And if the monsoon closes before we're done?"

"Then we dive faster."

Tomas almost smiled. It transformed his face, and she looked away from it, back to the channel, because warmth was a liability and she had a rule about liabilities.

But rules, she was learning, bent in storms.

They were a mile off the river mouth, close enough to see white water boiling over the bar, when Tomas went still beside her. He was looking past her shoulder, south along the coast, and his stillness had a quality she recognized from bad nights in bad ports.

"Astrid."

She followed his gaze. Far off, at the edge of visibility, a gray shape was working up the coast against the weather—steady, deliberate, unhurried, the way nothing civilian moves in a rising monsoon. Even at that distance she knew the lines. A patrol boat. Navy hull, navy bearing.

And it was closing.

"They flagged us in Port Callow," she said quietly. "Reeve. He flagged the *Kestrel* before we ever sailed."

Tomas's hand found the map beneath the oilcloth, holding it down against the wind as though the paper might bolt. "Can we make the river mouth first?"

Astrid did the arithmetic—their speed, the patrol's, the distance, the bar. The numbers came back tight. Very tight. The kind of tight that meant the next hour decided everything.

"Hold on," she said, and put the wheel over, and ran for the mountain.
