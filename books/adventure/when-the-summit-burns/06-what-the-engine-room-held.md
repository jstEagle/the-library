## 6. What the Engine Room Held

The cove had no name on any chart Astrid owned, which was precisely why she'd chosen it. A crescent of black sand cupped between two shoulders of volcanic rock, screened from the open water by a reef that would tear the bottom out of anything larger than her boat. The monsoon's edge dragged across the strait to the north, a gray wall of rain she could watch from inside the storm's shadow without being inside it. For now.

She ran the *Kestrel* in shallow, dropped anchor twice—once for the bow, once astern, so the hull wouldn't swing—and then spent twenty minutes on the engine while Tomas stood on the beach and pretended not to watch. The crack in the planking was worse than she'd admitted. She'd stuffed it with oakum and resin at dawn, working by flashlight with her hands shaking from cold and adrenaline, and now she let the patch cure under a coat of tar while the tide sat low. It would hold. It had to hold. She was tired of things that almost held.

Tomas built a fire from driftwood and what remained of Vann's stores: hardtack, a tin of salt fish, coffee stretched so thin it tasted like the memory of coffee. He cooked without being asked, moving around the small flame with an economy that told her he'd fed himself badly in a lot of places. When he handed her the tin cup, their fingers didn't touch. He'd been careful about that since the dock. Since the ambush. Since the moment they'd become, provisionally, the only two people in the world either of them could trust.

They ate in silence while the light went copper and then gray. The rain wall to the north flickered with heat lightning, silent at this distance, like a war happening behind glass.

"You keep looking north," Tomas said finally.

"I keep listening north." She turned the cup in her hands. "There's a difference."

"And?"

"And nothing yet. Reeve's boats will run the shipping lanes first. He'll want us at sea where there's no one to see him do whatever he does." She said it flatly, the way she'd say *the tide turns at six*. Three years of practice at saying terrible things flatly.

Tomas poked the fire. Sparks went up and died. "You knew my grandfather three days," he said. "I knew him thirty years, and I never got more than fragments. He talked around the Serapine cache like a man talking around a wound. But you—" He looked up at her, and his eyes in the firelight were steady and unblinking, a prospector's patience in them, the willingness to sit by a seam of rock for a week waiting for it to give something up. "You've been running longer than three days. I can see it. You move like someone counting exits."

Astrid drank the bad coffee. She had known this conversation was coming since the night they bailed seawater together in the dark, when he'd stopped asking questions and started simply waiting for her to answer them. There was only one question left that mattered, and they both knew it.

"The Corvane," she said.

"The Corvane."

* * *

Three years ago, in a port she still refused to name even in her own head, Astrid Underhill had been broke in a way that made recklessness look like prudence.

She'd heard about the wreck from a salvage broker who dealt in rumors the way other men dealt in stock—the HMS Corvane, lost with all hands off the Serapine coast during the last year of the war, officially a casualty of mine-laid waters, unofficially nothing at all because no one official ever spoke its name. The navy had surveyed the site once, marked it restricted, and never returned. Restricted wrecks meant restricted salvage, which meant high prices for anyone willing to be nobody.

Her first dive was routine. The Corvane lay on her starboard side in forty fathoms, settled into a shelf of the seafloor like a sleeping animal, her hull furred with decades of growth. Astrid had dived enough dead ships to know their grammar. Most of them drowned honestly—torpedo holes blown outward, hatches sprung by panic, the archaeology of men trying to live. You could read a wreck the way you read a face after bad news.

The Corvane read wrong from the very beginning.

The damage was inward. That was the thing she couldn't stop seeing, that first dive and every dive after. The plating near the keel was deformed from the inside, buckled in a pattern that no mine or torpedo leaves. And the scuttle valves—not battle damage, not corrosion, but opened. Opened deliberately, from within, and then wedged. Someone had wanted this ship on the bottom and had made sure the sea couldn't be shut out again.

She should have taken her brass and left. She told herself, later, that she stayed because of the strongbox in the captain's cabin, but that was the lie she used; the truth was that the wrongness pulled at her the way a loose tooth pulls at a tongue.

On her fourth dive, working down the portside corridor toward the engineering spaces with her light cutting through water the color of old tea, she found the hatch to the engine room dogged shut from the outside.

Ships don't work that way. Engine rooms are kept open. They are the loudest, hottest, most alive compartment on any vessel, and the men who work them need to get out fast when things go wrong, so the hatches stand ready, the ladders stay clear. This hatch was shut and dogged—every wheel spun down tight—from the corridor side.

Her air gauge said she had time. Her fear said she didn't want to know. She went in anyway, because curiosity in Astrid Underhill had always been stronger than sense, and because some part of her had already understood what she would find and needed to see it confirmed before she could make herself believe it.

The engine room was dim even under her light, silt hanging in the water like breath held for forty years. She swam the length of the compartment past gauges frozen at zero, past the great dark shapes of the engines themselves, and there, at the far end, near the escape trunk that led up toward the aft compartments—

Men.

Not skeletons scattered the way drowning scatters things. Not men caught running for the hatch. They were seated. Some of them were lying down, deliberate, arranged by exhaustion rather than violence, in the lee of the machinery where the air would have lasted longest. And on two of them—she made herself go closer, made herself put her light on it, made herself look—there were chains. Short ones. Wrist to pipe, wrist to bulkhead fitting. Rust had welded iron to bone, but the manacles were unmistakable, and so was the meaning: these men had been locked below before the ship went down. Locked below in a compartment whose hatch was dogged from the outside, in a ship whose scuttle valves had been wedged open, and left.

Forty fathoms down, in the dark, for forty years, the Corvane's stokers and engineers had waited for a rescue that their own officers had made impossible.

Astrid had hung in that black water with her heart slamming against her regulator and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was looking at a murder that had been wearing a uniform. And that whoever had done it—if he still lived, and men like that lived long, men like that got promoted—would do anything, anything at all, to keep those chains rusting quietly in the dark.

She took photographs. Of course she did. She was a professional; documentation was reflex. Then she took the brass she'd come for, ascended, and discovered over the following weeks that the photographs had changed her life in ways she hadn't planned. Because she couldn't sell them—selling them meant surfacing, meant questions, meant the navy learning that someone had been inside the restricted wreck reading its grammar. And she couldn't destroy them, because destroying them meant agreeing with whoever had chained those men to the pipes, meant letting the sea finish the job.

So she'd kept them. Hidden, copied, hidden again. And she'd run. Port to port, name to name, three years of invisibility practiced as a craft: pay cash, dock late, leave early, never dive the same wreck twice, never let your face settle anywhere long enough to be remembered. She had told herself she was waiting. Waiting for what, she couldn't have said. A moment. An opening. Someone else brave enough to go first.

Then a dying prospector in a rain-lashed bar had pressed a waterproofed map into her hands and made her promise to tell the truth about whatever she found, and the waiting had ended whether she was ready or not.

* * *

By the time she finished speaking, the fire had burned down to coals and the cove had gone fully dark except for the orange heartbeat of the embers and the cold scatter of stars above the reef. Tomas hadn't interrupted once. He sat across the fire with his forearms on his knees, and when she reached the part about the chains, he closed his eyes—not in horror, she thought, but in confirmation, the way a man closes his eyes when a diagnosis he's dreaded is finally spoken aloud.

"Chained below," he said quietly. "That's why Grandfather hid the records instead of selling them."

"He told you that much?"

"He told me nothing. But I found things, going through his effects after the shack burned. Papers he thought he'd destroyed. A letter fragment—no signature, just a payment schedule and a list of names. Widow's names, mother's names. Families of Corvane crew." Tomas looked up. "I thought he'd extorted them. I'm ashamed of how long I thought that. But they weren't paying him to keep quiet, Astrid. They were paying him to keep it *safe*. To hold the proof until someone could use it. He was hired by the families of the men in that engine room."

The words landed in Astrid's chest like a weight settling onto a scale. All three years of running, and she had never once imagined that the other end of her secret was held by grieving women writing letters to a prospector in the hills. She had thought she was alone with it. She had built her whole life on being alone with it.

"I photographed them," she said. "The chains. I've carried the negatives through nine ports. I sleep with them sometimes, literally, in a waterproof roll under my mattress. Do you know what that's like? Being the only person alive who knows how those men died?"

"No," Tomas said. "But I know what it's like to be the only person alive who knows why my grandfather drank himself coughing blood in harbor bars, telling me stories about caves that sounded like madness." He fed a stick of driftwood to the coals. "We're carrying halves of the same thing. That's all either of us has been doing—carrying half and calling it survival."

Astrid looked at him across the embers and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. It wasn't relief exactly. Relief implied safety, and there was no safety within a hundred miles. It was simpler and stranger than that: the secret, spoken, weighed less. Shared, it was still heavy, but it was no longer hers alone, and a burden halved between two people who both intended to carry it was a different object entirely from a burden hoarded by one woman in the dark.

"Reeve was there," she said. "Whoever ordered it. Whoever signed the order to scuttle her with her own people aboard—he was junior officer rank, Vann said. Young enough to still be serving. Old enough to be chief of naval salvage now." She stared into the coals. "He's been hunting that map for years, Tomas. Not because of gold. Gold he could buy. He's hunting it because somewhere in those crates at Serapine is a piece of paper with his name on it."

Tomas nodded slowly. "And tomorrow we sail into the monsoon to go get it."

"And tomorrow we sail into the monsoon to go get it."

They banked the fire and took watches, as they had every night since the dock—two hours each, back to back, neither quite sleeping when the other was awake. Astrid took first watch, sitting against the hull with the patched plank at her back, listening to the sea work the reef and the rain wall grumble to the north.

It was deep into her second hour, the sky just beginning to think about gray, when she saw it: a single white light far out beyond the reef line, moving slowly along the edge of the shipping lane. Too steady for a fishing boat. Too patient for weather-running traffic. It hung there, surveying, the way a man's eyes move along a shoreline looking for a campfire.

Then it blinked twice—deliberate, rhythmic—and began, very slowly, to turn toward the cove.

Astrid was already reaching back to shake Tomas awake.
