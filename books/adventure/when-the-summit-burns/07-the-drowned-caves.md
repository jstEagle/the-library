## 7. The Drowned Caves

Tomas had imagined the mountain a hundred times since his grandfather first told him about it, and every imagining had been wrong.

Mount Serapine did not rise so much as it leaned — a black shoulder of basalt shrugging out of the sea, its summit wrapped in cloud like a man hiding his face. The monsoon's edge dragged rain across the strait in long gray curtains, and through them the island appeared and vanished, appeared and vanished, as if it could not decide whether to be found. The boat came apart beneath them as they approached. Not all at once; that would have been almost merciful. It came apart in increments, each wave taking a little more of the hull's argument with the water, until Astrid was steering with one hand and bailing with the other and Tomas was pressed against the transom with both palms flat on the deck, feeling the sea come up through the planks to meet him.

"There." Astrid's voice carried over the wind without effort, the way voices do when they've stopped being afraid of being heard. She pointed to a notch in the coastline where the cliffs folded inward around a strip of black sand. "The cove Vann marked. We beach her there and pray she floats when we need her again."

"She won't," Tomas said.

"I know. But we pray anyway. It's traditional."

They ran the bow onto the sand at half throttle, and the sound the hull made was the sound of something giving up. Tomas waded into the surf to haul the line taut while Astrid killed the engine, and for a moment they stood together in the shallows, ankle-deep in foam, looking up at the cliff walls that enclosed the cove like the inside of a cupped hand. The rain fell straight down here, soft and steady, and the air smelled of wet stone and something older — sulfur, maybe, or just the deep mineral breath of a mountain that had been burning once and never entirely stopped.

"Vann used to talk about this place like it was alive," Tomas said. "He'd get this look. Like he'd left something here he shouldn't have."

"He told you about the caves?"

"He told me everything except how to find them." Tomas looked at her. In the gray light she seemed carved from the same stone as the mountain, salt-stiff hair plastered to her skull, eyes narrowed against the rain. "You have the map."

"I have the map." She turned toward the treeline. "Come on. If we're diving today, we dive before the storm closes the window. And if we're not diving today—" she didn't finish it, and Tomas understood why. There was no *if we're not*. The map had been in his grandfather's hands for thirty years, and men had burned an old man's shack over it within hours of his death. Whatever waited inside Serapine, someone wanted it badly enough to make ash of a life. They were already committed. They had been committed since the bar, since the dock, since the moment Elias Vann coughed blood into his handkerchief and made a dying prospector's bargain with a stranger.

The trail up from the cove was barely a trail — a seam of erosion in the cliff face, switchbacking through ferns that grew thick as a man's arm. Twice Tomas lost his footing and caught himself on rock still warm from some buried vein of the mountain's old fire. At the top, the land opened into a shelf above the sea, and there it was: the mouth of the lava tube, a ragged oval in the cliffside, half-drowned, breathing.

That was the only word for it. The cave breathed. Swells pushed into the tube and withdrew, and the air that came back out was cold and mineral-dark, carrying the smell of deep water and deep time. Astrid crouched at the edge and studied the water below the entrance, where the swell rose and fell across submerged rocks.

"Tide's coming in," she said. "In two hours that entrance is a washing machine. We go now, we're back before it turns." She began checking her gear without looking at her hands, the way a person does who has done a thing ten thousand times. Tank. Regulator. Knife. Light. "Rules. You stay on my light. You don't touch anything until I've seen it. And if either of us gets separated, you go up, not deeper. Promise me."

"You promise me something first." His voice came out harder than he intended. "Whatever's down there — my grandfather spent his whole life keeping it safe. He died keeping it safe. When we find it, we don't leave it. Not any of it."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin.

"You sound like him," she said finally. "He made me promise the same thing. Truth, no matter what it costs." She pulled her mask down over her face, and her voice went muffled and strange. "I'm starting to think it's the most expensive thing in the world."

Then she rolled backward off the ledge into the sea, and Tomas followed her into the dark.

* * *

The lava tube swallowed light the way the sea swallows ships.

Tomas had dived wrecks before — nothing serious, a few coastal sites, a sunken barge off the fishing islands — but he had never been inside the ocean before. That was the difference. A wreck sat *in* the water; this was the water itself, turned to stone around them, a throat of black glass narrowing and widening as the mountain's ancient anger had cooled. Their lights swung across walls furred with pale growth, across archways of rock that opened onto deeper darkness, and everywhere the sound of their own breathing, amplified, intimate, slightly panicked.

Astrid moved ahead of him like a woman swimming through a memory. She checked her gauge, checked him, checked the line they'd run from the entrance, and kept going. Once she paused at a junction where the tube split, and her light found a mark chiseled into the rock: three lines, slanted, deliberate. A prospector's sign. His grandfather's sign. Tomas felt something tighten in his chest — grief and pride tangled together, indistinguishable.

*He was here. Thirty years ago, an old man with bad lungs swam through this same dark and marked the way home.*

The tube descended, then turned, then opened.

It happened all at once. One moment the walls were close enough to touch; the next they were gone, and their lights reached out into a chamber so large the beams simply dissolved into it. Air space glittered far above — a pocket of trapped atmosphere near the ceiling, silvered and trembling. Below, the floor of the chamber sloped away into blackness, and scattered across the visible slope, catching their lights and throwing them back in dull gold and rust, were the crates.

Dozens of them. Stacked in rows, weighted with stone, sealed with tar and canvas gone stiff as bark. Some had slumped and split open over the decades, spilling their contents across the rock: ingots wrapped in oilcloth, dull with age but unmistakable. And paper. Paper everywhere — bundled, boxed, waterproofed in waxed leather, more paper than Tomas had ever seen outside a government office, preserved by the cold and the dark and the same care that had brought them here.

Astrid surfaced into the air pocket first, pulling herself onto a shelf of rock, and Tomas came up beside her, dragging off his mask. The air tasted of stone and iodine. Their breathing echoed off the ceiling high above.

"God," he whispered, and the word came back to him twice.

Astrid didn't answer. She had pried open the nearest crate with her knife and was lifting out a bundle of documents, handling them the way other people handle infants. Her light moved across the pages. Tomas watched her face change — watched the awe drain out of it and something else arrive in its place, something colder.

"What?" He climbed onto the shelf beside her. "What is it?"

She handed him the top sheet without a word.

It was a naval manifest. Cargo list. Routine. But stamped across it, in faded violet ink, was an authorization countersigned by an officer's hand, and beneath the stamp, a date — the date the Corvane went down — and beneath the date, a notation in the same officer's hand, brief and businesslike:

*Material transferred. Crew complement retained aboard per orders. No survivors expected or required.*

Tomas read it three times. The words didn't change. *Retained aboard.* *No survivors expected or required.* His grandfather's voice came back to him suddenly, from years ago, from a kitchen that smelled of coffee and pipe smoke: *Some truths are heavy, boy. That's why men bury them. And why somebody has to dig.*

"They knew," he said. His voice sounded foreign to him. "The crew was chained below. They scuttled their own ship with their own people in it, and they wrote it down, and they—"

"They hid it." Astrid's light swept the chamber, crate after crate after crate. "This isn't treasure, Tomas. This is a court-martial. This is evidence. Decades of it." She pulled another bundle free — letters this time, civilian handwriting, dozens of different hands — and unfolded one, and went very still.

"What?"

She passed it to him. The letter was old, the ink browned, the paper soft as cloth. It was a receipt. Payment received — a sum that made Tomas's eyes sting — and it was signed *E. Vann*, and above the signature, in a woman's careful script, a single line:

*For our sons. Keep it safe until we can make them listen. — The widows and mothers of HMS Corvane.*

Tomas sat down hard on the wet rock.

Not the navy. Not some syndicate, not smugglers, not a fortune hidden by thieves. The families. The families had pooled everything they had — pensions, savings, wedding rings, he could see it in the inventory lists spilling from the second crate — and paid a broke prospector to hide the proof that would have been destroyed, because the navy couldn't be made to listen, only to fear. His grandfather hadn't been hired to bury a secret. He'd been hired to keep a promise. For thirty years. Alone.

"He never told me," Tomas said. "All those years. He let me think he was just — a prospector. An old man with a map and a grudge." His throat closed. "He was a vault. He was a whole cathedral of the dead, and he never once asked to be thanked."

Astrid put her hand on his shoulder. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to; the chamber said it for them, in the rustle of preserved paper and the drip of water from a ceiling that had held this weight for three decades.

And then her hand tightened.

"Tomas." Her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Turn off your light."

He did. Darkness, total. But not silence — through the water below them, faint and rhythmic, came a sound he knew from a hundred harbor docks. Bubbles. Regulator exhaust. More than one set.

Astrid's light snapped back on, aimed downward, shuttered to a thin blade. Far below, at the mouth of the lower passage, lights were rising. Three of them. Four. Moving with the unhurried discipline of men who knew exactly where they were going.

"They followed us," Astrid breathed. "Or they were already coming. Reeve's divers." She was already moving, stuffing documents into her waterproof bag, her hands fast and precise even now. "We can't outrun them through the tube. Too narrow. Take what you can carry — the manifests, the signed orders, the letter. The letter most of all."

"And the rest? We can't just leave it—"

Bubbles, closer now. A light swept the far wall, and in its glow Tomas saw, silhouetted against the chamber's distant entrance, the shape of a diver rising toward them with a speargun held low and level.

"Astrid," he said. "We have a problem."

Her light found the secondary passage Vann's map had marked behind the crates — a crack in the chamber wall, barely wide enough for a body, exhaling a thread of moving water. An exit. Maybe.

"Take the bag," she said. "Go first. Don't look back."

The lights below rose steadily toward them, patient as the tide, and somewhere above the drowned heart of Mount Serapine, the monsoon slammed into the coast like a door being locked.
