## 8. Cut Off

The storm arrived the way Reeve did everything—deliberately, and with the patience of a man who had already decided how it would end.

Astrid felt it before she heard it: a pressure change in the flooded lava tube, a slow pulse in the water that made her ears ache inside the mask. The cave breathed differently when weather came. The sea drew back into itself like a fist closing, and every current in the drowned mountain reversed direction at once.

She was forty feet down in the second chamber, her dive light playing across the stacked crates, when the pulse came again—harder this time, a shove of cold water from the seaward entrance that sent silt blooming off the floor like smoke.

Tomas's hand closed on her shoulder. He pointed toward the tunnel mouth they'd come through, and she saw it even through the murk: lights. Not the blue-green shimmer of their own lamps reflected off the walls, but white beams, three of them, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who had done this before.

Reeve's divers.

They'd been on the surface an hour ago—she'd seen their boat anchored beyond the point, riding low, too many men aboard for a fishing crew. She'd told herself they had time. The monsoon was still building; the navy wouldn't risk a dive in open water with the barometer falling.

She'd been wrong about that. She'd been wrong about a great many things since Elias Vann pressed a waterproofed map into her hands and made her promise to tell the truth.

* * *

They killed their lights and drifted behind a shelf of basalt, and Astrid listened to the strangers' progress through the first chamber by sound alone—the hiss of regulators, the scrape of a tank against stone, the muffled percussion of a slate being tapped. Navy hand signals. Navy spacing between swimmers. She'd spent three years avoiding exactly these sounds.

The crates around them held everything Vann had spent his last years protecting: naval manifests in oilcloth, a captain's log bound in canvas, pay ledgers with names written in a clerk's careful hand—names of men who had gone down with the *Corvane*, chained below decks while their own junior officer gave the order to scuttle. And beneath the records, wrapped in tarred canvas, the gold the families had scraped together to buy its hiding place. Payment for silence, but not theirs. Payment for preservation.

The truth, weighed in sovereigns, hidden better than any lie.

Tomas touched her arm and tapped twice on his wrist gauge. Air check. She showed him hers—a third remaining—and he nodded, then pointed deeper into the cave system, away from the approaching lights, toward the secondary passage Vann's map had marked as an emergency exit. A narrow fissure that climbed through old lava toward a blowhole on the cliff face above the high-tide line.

It was marked with a warning, too. *Narrow. One diver at a time. Do not attempt in heavy swell.*

The swell was already heavy. In another hour it would be suicide.

But staying was not survival either. The white beams were closer now, and one of them swept across the far wall of the chamber, close enough that Astrid could see the letters stenciled on the nearest crate blur through the disturbed water. She heard a voice, distorted but intelligible, barking something through a full-face mask comm. Then a second voice answering, and a word that needed no clarity at all:

"*Contact.*"

* * *

They ran.

There was no other word for it, though running underwater meant finning until her thighs burned, dragging themselves through the connecting tunnel toward the fissure while their bubbles rose in silver ropes and the navy lights swung behind them like searchlights through fog. The passage narrowed. Tomas went first because he was bigger and if he couldn't fit, no argument would follow them through anyway. He wedged through the tightest section with a grunt she heard through the water, and then it was her turn, her tank scraping rock, her shoulder screaming where the basalt bit through her wetsuit.

Then they were through, into the rising fissure, and for ninety seconds she thought they might actually make it.

That was when the first shot came.

Not a gunshot—not yet. It was the crack of a flare gun, fired from the chamber behind them, and the magnesium light turned the whole tunnel white as bone. In that terrible illumination Astrid saw the fissure ahead of them split into two branches, saw the map's notation in her memory—*left fork rises to blowhole, right fork dead-ends*—and saw, silhouetted in the white glare below, a diver entering the fissure after them.

He wasn't wearing dive gear.

He was wearing a half-mask and carrying a speargun, and he moved through the flooded passage like a man who knew it, which meant Reeve hadn't just found the caves tonight. Reeve had known about them for years. Had probably mapped them himself, decades ago, when he was still young enough to believe that drowning a ship's company was a price an institution could pay without ever having to look at the invoice.

The second shot was a gunshot.

Muffled by water and stone, it was less a sound than a sensation—a punch against her chest, a bloom of red in the water ahead of her that she understood only slowly, stupidly, because her mind refused the arithmetic. Tomas had turned back. Tomas had put himself between her and the fissure mouth, wedged into the narrowest point with his body, and he'd taken the spearman's second shot—or the surface man's bullet, fired down through the blowhole shaft, she would never know which—in the meat of his upper arm, and possibly worse, because there was so much blood, spreading through the water like ink through paper.

His eyes found hers through the masks. He didn't gesture. He didn't signal. He simply pointed—down and back, toward the main chambers, toward the records—and then pointed up, at her, alone.

No.

She grabbed for him. Her fingers found his sleeve, his harness, and he tore her grip away with a strength that surprised her, that came from somewhere beyond muscle, and shoved her toward the left fork. His regulator hose had been cut or torn—she saw the free hose whipping loose, streaming bubbles—and he clamped his hand over the wound in his arm and pulled his backup regulator into his mouth with the other, and even now, bleeding into the dark, he was calm. Calm the way Vann had been calm in the rain-lashed bar, coughing blood and making bargains.

*Whatever you find,* the old man had said, *you tell the truth about it.*

The truth was in the crates below. The truth was in the hands of men who were coming for it with guns. And the only person who could carry it out was currently floating in a flooded fissure doing nothing at all.

Tomas spat the regulator long enough to shout—one word, distorted almost past recognition, but she heard it.

"*Go!*"

* * *

She went.

She wanted to write here, in the honest ledger of her own memory, that she fought the decision, that some part of her refused. But the truth—the truth she had promised a dying man she would tell—was simpler and uglier. Survival instinct is older than loyalty. Her body chose before her heart finished arguing, and she was finning up the left fork with tears she couldn't feel behind the mask, following the faint gray promise of daylight above, while the man who had saved her from a dockside ambush, who had bailed seawater beside her all night with cracked and bleeding hands, who had called Elias Vann *Grandfather* with a grief that had never once sounded performed—that man sank back into the dark with the records, alone, to keep them out of reach for as long as his blood held out.

The fissure narrowed. She stripped her tank to push it ahead of her through the final choke, exhaling in a controlled scream of bubbles, scraping through stone that flayed the skin from her shoulders. Above her, the blowhole thundered—each swell driving spray and wind down the shaft like the mountain breathing out its fury.

She surfaced in a churning well of foam and black air, hauled herself onto wet rock as a wave detonated below her, and lay there gasping, deafened by the storm, feeling the whole summit tremble above her.

Rain hit her face like thrown gravel. Through the blowhole's throat, thirty feet up, she could see the sky: a boiling bruise of cloud lit from within by lightning, and the silhouette of the cliff camp above—Vann's old shelter, the lean-to where they'd staged their gear—already bending under winds that snapped the tarpaulins like whips.

And somewhere below her feet, in the drowned heart of Mount Serapine, Tomas was holding a passage with his body and whatever ammunition he'd carried, buying her minutes with blood.

She had the records' location memorized. She had the strongbox from the *Corvane*—the sealed box she'd salvaged at dawn three years into her running, the one thing she'd never managed to sell because something in her gut wouldn't let her—stashed in the lean-to above. She had a promise older than both.

The monsoon hit full force then, a wall of wind and water that flattened her against the rock and filled the world with white noise, and through it, faintly, from the direction of the cove, she heard engines. More boats. Reeve was committing everything.

Astrid Nystrom pulled herself to her feet on the wet stone, alone for the first time in three years without wanting to be, and started to climb.
