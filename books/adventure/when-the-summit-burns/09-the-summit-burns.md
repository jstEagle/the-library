## 9. The Summit Burns

The smoke came before the dawn did.

Astrid woke in the fisherman's hut above the cove with her lungs full of it—acrid, chemical, wrong. Woodsmoke smelled like home and hearth; this smelled like diesel and intent. She was on her feet before her mind finished the sentence, bare feet on cold planks, one hand already closing around the canvas bag she'd packed three times in the night and unpacked twice, unable to decide what a person carried when she was about to stop running.

Through the shutters, the ridge above the landing was burning.

Reeve's men had chosen their hour well. The monsoon had paused—the strange, breathless lull that came between squalls—and in that stillness the fire climbed the slope with nothing to argue against it but wet stone and stubborn pines. The camp where Tomas lay was a scatter of tents and tarps below the cave mouth, and the flames were walking toward it in patient lines, like soldiers advancing under orders.

"Tomas." She said his name aloud because saying things aloud made them real, and she needed this to be real.

She went down the trail at a run that wasn't quite a run—the path was slick as oiled glass, and a broken ankle now would be a death sentence for both of them. The heat reached her before the light did, pressing against her face like an open oven door. Below, silhouetted against the burn, two figures moved between the tents with cans in their hands, feeding the fire methodically. Not panicked. Professional. This was not chaos; this was housekeeping.

One of them turned, saw her, and shouted something she couldn't hear over the roar. The other dropped his can and started up the slope toward her.

Astrid did the arithmetic she'd been doing all her life: two of them, one of her, a mountain on fire, and Tomas bleeding somewhere below with a bullet through his shoulder and the records stacked around him like sandbags. Every instinct she had honed over three years of ports and false names screamed the same instruction—*go to the water, go to the boat, go dark.*

But Vann's voice was in her head, wet and rattling, in a bar that smelled of rain and rust: *Promise me you'll tell the truth about whatever you find.* And beneath that, older and colder, the engine room of the Corvane, the chains, the bones of men who had been locked below decks by their own navy so they couldn't testify to how their ship died.

She had spent three years being invisible. Invisibility had kept her alive, and it had cost her everything else.

She turned and ran—not down, but along the contour of the slope, toward the secondary passage, the crack in the lava tube that Vann's map had marked with a single word: *breath*. Behind her she heard boots on stone, a shout, a shot that cracked off the rocks wide of her left ear. She didn't flinch. Flinching cost time.

* * *

The passage was exactly where the map promised—a fissure barely wider than her shoulders, exhaling cool air from deep inside the mountain. She squeezed in sideways, the canvas bag scraping rock, and let the darkness take her. Above, muffled by forty feet of basalt, the world burned. Inside, there was only the drip of water and the sound of her own breathing, which she made herself slow down, because panic spent air faster than effort ever could.

She had a light. She had a knife. She had the strongbox.

That last item was the whole reason she'd hauled it out of the caves yesterday instead of leaving it buried with the gold. The Corvane's strongbox—salt-welded, brass-cornered, its lock long since surrendered to her crowbar—had held more than coin. It held the ship's emergency beacon, the old pattern, hand-cranked, built to survive anything except the order to sink. She'd found it wedged beneath the ledger crates, and she'd understood immediately why Vann had hidden it here rather than sell it: it wasn't salvage. It was a voice. A machine for shouting into the dark, and every ship in the strait would hear it.

Reeve had spent decades making sure no one shouted. So she would shout loud enough that he couldn't unshout it.

The passage opened into the main chamber, and the sight of it stopped her even now, even with men hunting her above. The flooded tube gave way to a cathedral of black stone, and along its floor, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax against sixty years of water, the records waited in their ranks. Ledgers. Orders. Requisitions signed in a young officer's careful hand—she knew that hand now, had studied it by lamplight until she could have forged it. Reeve's signature, authorizing the Corvane's "disposal" with a skeleton crew aboard who were never told they were the crew being disposed of.

And beside the crates, propped against the stone with his shoulder bound in strips of sailcloth, sat Tomas.

"You're supposed to be unconscious," she said.

"I'm supposed to be dead," he rasped. "Grandfather always said I never did what I was supposed to." His face was gray, his lips bloodless, but his eyes found hers and held. "They're burning the camp."

"I know. I ran through it."

"They'll come for the cave next. Once the fire's done, they'll seal the entrance with charges and call it storm damage. Nobody will ever—" He broke off, coughing, and pressed his good arm against his ribs.

"Nobody will ever need to dig," Astrid said. "Because we're not going to hide it. We're going to broadcast it."

She knelt beside him and pulled the beacon from the bag. It was heavier than memory, colder than the stone, and when she set it down between them it seemed to change the room—as if the dead of the Corvane had shifted slightly closer to listen.

"The antenna's gone," Tomas said. "Salt took it years ago."

"We don't need the antenna." She unwrapped the coil of copper wire she'd stripped from the boat's radio before they abandoned it, and began paying it out along the chamber floor toward the fissure that led up to the cliff face above the sea. "We need height, and we need the fire."

He stared at her. "The fire?"

"Smoke columns carry signal interference—every radio operator knows it. But they also draw every eye on the water." She worked as she talked, splicing wire with numb fingers, teeth on insulation. "Right now, half the strait is watching this mountain burn and wondering whose problem it's going to become. Fishing boats. Coastal freighters. The mail steamer out of Port Calloway, if she's running her schedule. If a distress beacon starts transmitting from inside a naval fire zone, people are going to look. People are going to come. And Commander Reeve cannot shoot witnesses fast enough once there are too many to count."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then, hoarsely: "You've been hiding for three years. You're telling me your plan is to be seen."

"I'm telling you my plan is to be *undeniable*." She tested the splice, then the next. "There's a difference. Anyone can be silenced. Even me. Especially me—I made myself easy to erase. But a beacon broadcasting the coordinates of these caves, keyed to the Corvane's own callsign, picked up by a dozen ships at once?" She looked up at him. "He can't kill a frequency, Tomas. He can't court-martial the air."

* * *

Climbing the fissure with the wire took everything she had. The walls scraped her shoulders raw; twice the passage narrowed until she had to exhale fully and force herself through, the rock gripping her ribs like a fist. Above her, growing louder with every meter, the fire roared—and beneath the roar, worse, voices. Reeve's men at the cave mouth. The scrape of something heavy being dragged into position. The flat metallic click of equipment she didn't want to identify.

She reached the upper vent—a chimney of stone opening onto the cliff face just below the summit, half-hidden by scrub—and pulled herself out onto a ledge no wider than a coffin. The world was orange. Heat rolled up the slope in waves that tried to peel her from the rock, and the smoke column stood over the mountain like a black tree, bending east on the returning wind.

Below, at the cave mouth, four men worked with military economy, stacking charges. And apart from them, hands clasped behind his back, coat immaculate despite the ash sifting down around him, stood Commander Reeve.

Even at this distance she knew his posture. She'd seen it across harbors for three years without knowing why it pricked at her—the stillness of a man who believed the world owed him order. Now she knew. He was looking at the fire the way other men looked at altars.

Astrid wired the beacon to the antenna line, anchored the wire to a spur of rock, and cranked.

The handle fought her, stiff with sixty years of salt, then gave. Somewhere in the strait, on whatever receivers were listening, a signal began to pulse. Three dots, three dashes, three dots—the oldest cry in the world—followed by the Corvane's callsign, letter by letter, and then the coordinates she had memorized until they lived behind her eyes like scripture. SOS. CORVANE. These waters. These waters. These waters.

She cranked until her arms burned worse than the fire. She cranked past the first shot that sparked off the ledge below her. She cranked while Reeve turned, slowly, and shaded his eyes against his own smoke, searching the cliff face for the source of the sound—and found her.

For a moment neither of them moved. Forty meters of burning air between them. She saw the recognition land in his face, saw the exact instant the salvage chief became the junior officer again, the man who had signed the order and spent a lifetime pretending the ink was someone else's. His hand rose toward the men at the cave mouth—

And stopped. Because out on the water, lights were answering. First one, far south—a fishing boat altering course, her running lamps swinging toward the mountain. Then another. Then the deep horn of a coastal freighter, unmistakable, deliberate, blowing fog signals of acknowledgment into the smoky dark. The beacon pulsed. The ships answered. The strait itself had become a witness list, and every name on it was steaming toward Serapine.

"Commander!" one of his men shouted, pointing at the water. "Vessels inbound, sir—half the strait's got our position—"

Reeve didn't answer. He was still looking up at her, ash settling on his shoulders like snow, and Astrid saw the war finish itself behind his eyes. She watched him understand that the charges in the cave mouth were no longer demolition—they were evidence. That the fire was no longer erasure—it was spectacle, visible from every deck in the strait. That the woman on the ledge, the ghost he'd chased across three years of harbors, had done the one thing he could never undo.

She stopped cranking only when her arms gave out, and even then the beacon's last transmission seemed to hang in the smoke like a bell's dying note.

"It's done," she called down to him, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack. "You can't drown it twice, Commander. The Corvane's talking. Everyone can hear her."

Reeve's lips moved. Whatever he said was eaten by the fire. But his hand lowered, and his men froze at the cave mouth, glancing between the incoming lights on the water and the man who had always known exactly what to do—and for the first time, didn't.

Behind Astrid, from the darkness of the fissure, faint and ragged, came the sound of Tomas dragging himself up the stone toward her voice.

The first searchlight swept the summit.
