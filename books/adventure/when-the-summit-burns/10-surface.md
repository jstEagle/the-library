## 10. Surface

The harbor at Port Callow smelled different in the dry season—tar and salt and woodsmoke instead of the green rot of monsoon—and Astrid stood on the dock for a long time before she could name what had changed.

It wasn't the harbor. It was her. For three years she had arrived in ports like this one at night, tied off in the shadows of working piers, paid her fees in cash, and learned the exits before she learned the names of streets. She had made a discipline of not belonging, the way other divers made a discipline of their air supply: check it twice, never trust it to hold, always know how much you have left and when you'll need more.

Now she was standing in full daylight on a public dock, waiting for a man who knew her face, and no part of her wanted to run.

* * *

Tomas walked with a cane now, a plain thing of driftwood he'd carved himself during the weeks the naval hospital kept him. The bullet had gone through his shoulder and out the back, missing everything that mattered by what the surgeon called "a diver's margin"—the same margin Astrid lived inside every time she slipped through a wreck's collapsed bulkhead. He'd joked about it from the first day he was conscious enough to joke, which was sooner than anyone liked, and he was joking about it now as he came down the ramp, sunburned and thinner and unmistakably alive.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm checking your air supply," she said. "Habit."

"Full tank." He lifted the arm that still worked all the way. "Mostly."

They'd had this conversation a dozen times in the hospital, in a dozen registers—the joke, the near-confession, the silence where the real thing sat. On the mountain, when Reeve's men came through the secondary passage an hour after she'd left him bleeding among the crates, Tomas had wedged himself into a lava shelf and waited them out while the fire turned the world above him orange. He'd come out coughing, half-blind, carrying a satchel of records against his chest like a child. He had not let go of it even when they put him on the stretcher. Even under sedation, the nurses said, his hands stayed curled around nothing, holding something that wasn't there anymore because it was everywhere now—inquiry offices, newspaper archives, the hands of lawyers who represented families that had spent forty years being told their fathers and husbands were lost to enemy action.

"Have you read today's?" he asked, and held out a folded paper.

She didn't take it. She'd read it at dawn, standing in the galley of the new boat with coffee going cold in her hand. *CORVANE INQUIRY OPENS: SURVIVORS' FAMILIES DEMAND FULL CREW LIST.* Below the fold, smaller: *Salvage Chief Reeve Relieved of Command Pending Tribunal.* And below that, smallest of all, in the kind of type newspapers reserve for lists of the dead: forty-one names. Ordinary names. Engine-room ratings, a cook, two boys of seventeen listed as "acting stokers." Men who had been chained below decks on a calm night in wartime by order of the officer who was supposed to bring them home.

"Acting Stoker J. Ferrow," Astrid said quietly. "Seventeen years old. I found his bunk, you know. Three years ago. His kit was still packed."

Tomas said nothing. He had learned, over these weeks, when she needed silence more than words.

"They gave him a name," she said. "That's all I ever—" She stopped. Pressed her palm flat against the dock rail, weathered wood under her hand, solid and real. "That's all I ever thought anyone would do. Name them. And it took a mountain burning down to manage it."

"It took you," Tomas said. "Vann used to say the truth doesn't need rescuing. It just needs someone willing to carry it up."

"He also said whatever I found, I had to tell the truth about it." She looked out at the water, at the light lying on it like hammered tin. "I've been thinking about that promise. About how cheap it seemed when I made it. One sentence, I thought. One honest sentence and a dying man gets his peace."

"And?"

"And it cost me a boat, a bullet I didn't take, three years of running I can't get back, and nearly cost me you." She turned to face him. "Worth it. That's the strange part. Every piece of it, worth it, and I don't know when that happened. I don't remember deciding."

"Nobody decides," Tomas said. "You surface or you don't. Everything else is just the dive plan."

* * *

She sold the boat on a Tuesday.

The *Kestrel's Wake* had been hers for four years, and she had loved it the way you love a thing that has kept you alive without ever asking whether you deserved keeping. The hull crack from the run to Serapine had been patched properly, the engine serviced, the charts replaced. A young fisherman bought her, a boy with quick hands and a wife who was already painting the wheelhouse door blue, and when he asked why Astrid was selling, she told him the truth, because that was easier now than lying.

"She got me where I needed to go," she said. "I need somewhere slower."

The slower boat was a converted trawler called *Patience*, thirty years old, broad-beamed, with a galley big enough to stand up in and a lazarette deep enough for gear she no longer had to hide. She bought it from a widow who wept a little at the transaction and then, unexpectedly, hugged her, and Astrid stood on the deck afterward holding the keys and feeling something she hadn't felt since before the Corvane: that a possession could be a home rather than an escape vehicle.

She moored *Patience* at the long-stay berth on the north wall, the one with the flower boxes the harbormaster's wife maintained, and paid for six months up front. The harbormaster raised an eyebrow—he remembered her, she realized; they all remembered her, the quiet woman who never stayed—but he took the money and said only, "Good season coming. Calm water till November."

Six months. She signed the paper with a hand that did not shake, and afterward she sat in the cockpit and laughed until she cried, alone, in daylight, in a port whose name she intended to learn properly this time. All the streets. Not just the exits.

* * *

The tribunal hearings began in the provincial capital, and the inquiry sent her a letter asking—politely, formally, with the navy's whole institutional weight behind the politeness—whether she would be available to testify regarding her discoveries aboard the wreck of HMS Corvane.

Three years ago she would have burned the letter. Two years ago she would have answered it with a lie and moved ports within the week. She read it three times at the galley table while rain tested the new seams of her new life, and then she wrote back:

*I will attend. I will tell the truth about what I found, including what I found in the engine room. I request only that the names be read aloud in full, and slowly.*

They wrote back within days: *The names will be read in full.*

* * *

On the morning she left for the capital, Tomas met her at the dock with his cane and a canvas bag, and she looked at the bag and understood before he said anything.

"You're coming."

"My grandfather hid those records for thirty years," Tomas said. "He hired on with the families when he was nineteen, carried gold and paper into a hole in a mountain, and spent the rest of his life dying of it by inches—coughing, drinking, moving, never telling anyone, not even me, especially not me. He gave you the map because he was out of time and you were the first person in decades who dove wrecks without asking who owned them." He tapped the bag. "There are things in these files Vann never saw. Letters from the crew. Last ones. The families should hear them from someone who was there when they were found. From both of us."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. The fear rose in her the way it always did—old, disciplined, well-practiced, the fear that had kept her invisible and alive—and she examined it the way she examined a gauge reading, and found it reading empty. There was nothing left for it to protect. The secret was out. The dead had names. The man who had chained them was in custody, gray-faced, insisting to anyone who would listen that he had saved the service, saved the fleet, saved something, anything, from the truth.

"All right," she said. "But you're on bilge watch. Diver's margin or not, nobody rides my boat for free."

Tomas grinned and stepped aboard, careful with the cane, sure with everything else, and cast off the line himself.

*Patience* moved out of Port Callow on a slack tide, slow and steady, her wake spreading wide and unhurried across water so calm it held the mountains upside down. Astrid stood at the helm and did not check the horizon for patrol boats. Did not count the exits. Did not calculate how far it was to anywhere else, because she was not going anywhere else.

Survival, she had thought for three years, was the price. Keep breathing, keep moving, keep silent—that was the toll, and she had paid it daily, faithfully, like dock fees.

She had been wrong. Survival had never been the price. Forgetting was. Forgetting what she'd seen in the engine room, forgetting the packed kit in a seventeen-year-old's bunk, forgetting the sound the Corvane made as she went down with her own crew chained inside her—that was the bill, and she had been paying it in installments of silence, and the interest had been eating her alive.

She was done paying.

Ahead, the strait opened toward the capital, toward a hearing room where forty-one names would be spoken aloud into official record, slowly, in full, and stay there. Behind her, Mount Serapine stood black and scarred against the sky, its drowned caves sealed at last by the storms, its treasure already spent—not gold, though there had been gold, but paper, ink, testimony, truth, hauled up into the light by a dying prospector's last bargain and a woman who had finally stopped running.

Astrid Nystrom turned the wheel, set her course, and for the first time in three years, breathed all the way out.
