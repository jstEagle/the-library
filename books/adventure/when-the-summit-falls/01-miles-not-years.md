## 1. Miles, Not Years

The river woke before Rafael did.

He heard it through the hull of the *Marta Cielo*—that low, constant conversation of water against steel that had become, over three years, the only voice he trusted to still be there in the morning. The San Vero ran high with snowmelt this time of year, brown-green and muscular, carrying silt from the high country down toward the delta like a man carrying a grudge he intends to deliver personally. Rafael lay in his bunk a while longer, listening, the way other men listened for weather or for trouble. The river told him everything he needed to know about the day ahead: fast water, poor visibility, cold enough at depth to make his hands stupid inside the gloves.

Good conditions, in other words. His conditions.

He made coffee on the single burner and drank it standing at the stern window, watching the light come up gray-gold over the water. The wreck sat forty meters below him, right where it had sat for sixty years—the *Corazón del Norte*, a river freighter out of the old Velorian trade, gone down in the winter of the last war with eleven souls aboard, or so the fragmentary record claimed. She lay on her port side in the channel's deep scour, half-buried in sediment, her superstructure furred with zebra mussels and her holds full of river dark. Salvage companies had looked at her twice in the decades since and passed both times; nothing aboard worth the cost of raising, the surveys said. Too deep for sport divers, too far from anywhere for academics. So she belonged to Rafael, in the way that forgotten things belong to whoever remembers them.

He kept a logbook for her the way a man might keep a journal for a sick relative. Dive 214. Bottom time projected: fifty-two minutes. Objective: continue clearing the debris jam aft of hold two, assess hull integrity at the bulkhead penetration. He wrote it in his careful salvager's hand, dated it, and did not write what he actually felt, which was that he was looking forward to it more than he had ever looked forward to anything involving another human being.

That was the arithmetic of his life now, and he had stopped pretending it was temporary. Miles, not years. He measured time in bottom minutes and river kilometers the way other men measured it in birthdays and anniversaries. Three years since the last salvage contract had dried up and he'd bought the *Marta Cielo* with the last of his father's money and pointed her upstream into a stretch of the San Vero where nobody asked questions. Three years of contracts small enough to stay under the tax office's attention and dives deep enough to keep the silence in his chest from getting loud. He was thirty-four years old and he could hold his breath four minutes static, read a sonar screen like a newspaper, and go eleven days without speaking aloud to anyone but himself.

He'd stopped counting that last one as a problem somewhere around dive one-fifty.

* * *

The morning dive went the way good dives go, which is to say uneventfully, which is to say perfectly.

Visibility at depth was maybe a meter and a half—meltwater season, the river thick as tea—and Rafael worked by feel and by the beam of his primary light, which cut the murk into a cone of drifting particulate that glittered like slow snow. He dropped through the broken skylight of the engine room, past the great dead heart of the main engine with its brass fittings gone green, and followed his guideline aft along the overhead that was really the deck, because the ship lay on her side and his body understood the geometry better than his mind did now. Two hundred and fourteen dives. His hands knew the *Corazón del Norte* the way they knew the rail of his own boat.

The debris jam aft of hold two had shifted—river currents rearranged the interior of a wreck the way wind rearranged dunes—and he spent forty minutes working slabs of collapsed plating and a tangle of chain loose enough to establish that the bulkhead behind it was cracked but holding. He found a ceramic cup fused into the sediment by six decades of mineral patience, lifted it, turned it in his light. No markings. He set it back exactly where it had lain. Some things you left where the river had decided to keep them.

At minute forty-six, checking his gauges out of habit rather than need, he drifted toward the forward end of hold two to close out the survey grid he'd been building all spring—a patient, square-by-square mapping of the wreck's interior, not for any client, just because unmapped things bothered him the way an unswept floor bothers someone else's mother. And that was when the light caught something that shouldn't have been there.

A seam.

He knew this section of hull the way he knew his own teeth. Riveted plate, Velorian yard standard, every seam accounted for on the profile drawings he'd reconstructed from fragments of the original builder's plans—one page of which, mercifully water-stained but legible, had surfaced in a scrap dealer's bin in Puerto Hondo two years ago and cost him a month of contract money. He had drawn the *Corazón del Norte*'s body the way a doctor draws an anatomy chart. There was no seam there. There was flat plate, continuous, unbroken, running from frame forty-one to frame forty-eight.

Except there wasn't. Not anymore. Not quite.

Rafael hung in the green-black water and played his light along the line again. Silt had built up against the plating in a smooth apron, and the apron had a straight edge to it—too straight, geometrically straight, the kind of straight that meant something underneath was holding the current off the metal. He swept his glove across it and the silt peeled away in a soft brown cloud, and beneath it was not plate at all but a plate bolted over plate—an overlay, a patch, riveted flush and then coated, long ago, in something that had weathered to the same color as the surrounding hull. A concealment job. Done well. Done by someone who had wanted it never found, and had nearly succeeded, except that sixty years of river had a patience greater than any craftsman's, and the coating had finally worn through along one edge no wider than a knife blade.

His heart was doing something complicated against his ribs. He made himself run the checklist. Air: fine. Depth: fine. Line: fine. Narked? No—he was shallow enough that nitrogen was a rumor, not a fact. Then look again.

It was real. A false plate, bolted over a void, on a ship whose every compartment was logged in a registry that had been burned with the rest of Veloria's records thirty years ago—which meant that whatever this compartment was, it hadn't been hidden after the sinking. It had been hidden before. Built secret, loaded secret, welded shut, disguised, and sent down a river in wartime wearing a lie for skin.

Rafael had pulled cars out of reservoirs and bodies out of canals and one memorable armored truck out of a flooded quarry, and he had a professional's immunity to the drama of sunken things. But he hung there in the cold brown water with his light on a knife-edge seam, and he felt the specific thrill that he suspected archaeologists and burglars shared: the moment the ordinary world revealed a drawer in it that no one knew existed.

His computer began its slow warning chirp. Fifty-two minutes gone. He marked the location on his slate—frame forty-five, port side down, two meters above the deck line—pressed his glove once against the cold metal, almost a greeting, and ascended into the bright confusion of the surface world.

* * *

The afternoon he spent the way he spent most surfaces: usefully, and alone.

He ran the side-scan sonar pass he'd been meaning to run all week, a slow figure-eight over the wreck site with the towfish humming forty meters below the *Marta Cielo*'s keel. It was routine work, the kind of thing he could do with half his mind while the other half cooked rice—but when the return painted itself across the screen in its ghostly amber relief, he set the spoon down.

There it was. Even in sonar's blind poetry he could see it once he knew to look: a density anomaly along the freighter's lower flank, a mass that returned signal too clean and too uniform to be sediment and too regular to be collapsed structure. Roughly four meters long. Rectangular. Sitting inside the hull's outline like a word inside a sentence, and like a word, meaningful only in context.

He ran the pass twice more, from opposing headings, and got the same shape both times. Then he dug out his paper charts—the old Velorian hydrographic sheets he'd collected the way other men collected stamps—and laid the freighter's general arrangement drawing under a lamp, and traced compartments with his finger until he reached the space at frame forty-five.

On the drawing, that space was labeled, in faded blueprint script: BALLAST — WING TANK P/S.

Ballast. Four meters of solid, sonar-opaque ballast, bolted behind a false plate, on a ship that had sunk with a cargo manifest that had never existed because the nation that would have filed it had been erased from the map like chalk from a board.

Rafael sat in the wheelhouse as the light went long and orange over the water, and he let himself feel the thing he felt, which was not fear—not yet—but a kind of gravitational pull, the sense of standing at the edge of a story that had been waiting sixty years for someone with a light and a slate and no one to report to. He thought about who he would call, if he were the kind of man who called people. A maritime authority. A university. A newspaper, even.

He thought about how each of those calls would end with other men arriving on the river in boats with radios, and how the *Corazón del Norte* would stop being his, and how the quiet he had built his life inside like a diver builds a habitat would fill up with other people's noise, their procedures, their claims, their cameras.

He thought, also—and this was the truer thought, the one he turned over last, in the dark of the evening, anchored midstream with the river talking to itself around the hull—that in three years he had not once wanted company until today. That the anomaly at frame forty-five was the first thing in years he'd wanted to show somebody. That he had caught himself, on the ascent, composing sentences in his head—*you should see what I found down there*—addressed to no one, to everyone, to the particular person he had never met and had quietly stopped believing in.

He wrote the day's log entry by lamplight. *Dive 214 complete. Bulkhead cracked, stable. Anomaly noted at frame 45; further investigation required.* He weighed the pen over the last part and then, because the logbook was the only witness he trusted, added a line he had never written in three years of entries:

*Something is buried in this ship that someone did not want the river to find.*

Outside, the San Vero moved in the dark, patient as money, carrying the mountain down to the sea. Rafael banked the stove, checked the anchor light, and lay in his bunk listening to the water, telling himself—as he fell asleep, as he had told himself for a thousand nights—that this life was enough. That a man could live on miles instead of years, on bottom-time instead of touch, and want nothing.

The river said nothing back. It was already carrying his answer downstream, in the form of a question he wouldn't ask for another two days, at the harbor office in Puerto Hondo, of a registrar whose face would go carefully blank when Rafael mentioned the name *Corazón del Norte*—a blankness he would remember afterward, and understand, and wish he had never seen.

But that was days away. Tonight there was only the anomaly, the lamp, the logbook, and the long brown dark of the water, keeping its oldest secret one more night.
