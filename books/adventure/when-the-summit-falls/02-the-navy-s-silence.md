## 2. The Navy's Silence

The Harbor Registry Office smelled of mildew and old ink, the way all government buildings in river towns smell—like paper slowly returning to pulp, like the water winning a war of attrition it had already won everywhere else.

Rafael had dressed for the occasion, which meant he'd put on a shirt with buttons and rinsed the river out of his hair. He owned exactly one pair of shoes that weren't rubber-soled dive boots, and they pinched at the heel as he climbed the stairs to the second floor, where a sign in flaking gold leaf read *REGISTRO MARÍTIMO — SECCIÓN FLUVIAL*. The *O* in *FLUVIAL* had lost its gilt entirely, so that the word seemed to be missing something at its center, an absence he found himself staring at while the clerk behind the counter pretended not to see him.

He knew this kind of waiting. He'd been invisible his whole adult life, and invisibility had a grammar: you did not make eye contact first, you did not fill silence, you let the world's attention slide off you like water off waxed canvas. Usually it worked. Usually the world was happy to look through him.

But the clerk—a thin man with a mustache the color of dust and a nameplate reading *A. Ferreyra*—was not looking through him. He was looking *at* him, and there was something in the look that Rafael couldn't parse until later, when he would lie awake cataloguing it: the clerk looked frightened.

"Señor Sterling." Ferreyra said it before Rafael had spoken, and that was the first wrong thing. Rafael had never given this office his name. He paid his mooring fees in cash at a window downstairs, to a different man, on the first Tuesday of every month. His boat was registered under a hull number, not a person. That was how he liked it. That was how he'd built it.

"That's me," Rafael said. "I have a question about a wreck."

"We handle registrations here. Vessel transfers. Mooring assignments." Ferreyra's hands were flat on the counter, very still, the way a man keeps his hands still when he doesn't trust them to do anything else.

"A wreck survey," Rafael said. "I'm a salvage diver. I've been working the freighter downriver—the one that went aground at Kilometer 112, maybe forty years back. I need her registry. Original tonnage, construction date, compartment layout if it exists. Standard stuff before I cut into anything."

Something happened to Ferreyra's face. It didn't move, exactly. It *tightened*, the way canvas tightens when the humidity drops, every line drawing taut at once.

"The *Andorinha*," Ferreyra said.

Rafael hadn't mentioned the ship's name. He hadn't known it. The nameplate on the stern was gone, eaten by decades of silt; he'd been calling her *the hundred-and-twelve* in his logs, after the river marker nearest her grave. "Is that what she's called?"

Ferreyra opened a drawer. Closed it. Opened a ledger instead, ran a finger down a column without appearing to read anything, and said, "There is no registry for that vessel."

"There's always a registry. She was a working freighter. She had cargo insurance, port fees, a crew manifest—"

"She has no registry," Ferreyra repeated, and now his voice had acquired the flat, over-rehearsed quality of a sentence someone had been made to memorize. "No record exists. I suggest you direct your salvage operations elsewhere. There are many wrecks. This one is—" he paused, searching for the word, or performing the search— "*closed*."

"Closed," Rafael said. "Wrecks get closed?"

"Wrecks get closed." Ferreyra closed the ledger with a soft clap, like a door. Behind him, through a frosted-glass partition, Rafael could see the shadow of another figure, motionless, in the inner office. It had been motionless since he arrived. He'd assumed it was furniture.

"I'll need something in writing, then," Rafael said. "An official statement that no record exists. My insurer requires documentation before I work any site."

For three full seconds, A. Ferreyra did not breathe. Rafael watched the man's chest refuse to rise. Then: "We don't issue statements about vessels that were never registered."

"*Was* she never registered? Or does no record exist?"

"They are the same thing," Ferreyra said, and his eyes came up from the counter for the first time, and what was in them wasn't hostility. It was a plea. *Stop asking. Please stop asking. I am a small man in a small office and you are going to drown us both.* "Good morning, señor."

Rafael stood there a moment longer, because leaving quickly felt like losing, and he was a man who had learned to measure everything in small victories. Then he turned and went down the stairs, past the cash window, out into the light, and the whole way he could feel the shadow behind the frosted glass watching him go.

Outside, the river ran brown and patient, carrying its secrets seaward one grain at a time. He stood on the steps and told himself it was nothing. Bureaucracies lost things. Paper rotted, floods took archives, clerks misfiled forty years of history into oblivion. There was no reason a sunken freighter should unsettle anyone.

Except that Ferreyra had known the ship's name.

Except that nobody had asked him to stop asking until he'd started asking about *her*.

Rafael walked back along the quay with his pinching shoes and his hands in his pockets, and the river kept pace beside him, and for the first time in three years he felt the particular weight of being observed—not by the water, which watched everyone equally and forgave everyone equally, but by something with intentions.

He did not go home by the direct route. He hadn't realized he was doing it until he was doing it: doubling back through the fish market, cutting through the net-menders' yard, standing for ten minutes in the shade of the co-op watching reflections in the harbor water for a face that appeared twice. Nothing. No one. Just gulls and diesel smoke and the ordinary machinery of a town that had never once cared what he did.

By the time he reached the *Mariposa*—his boat, his home, forty feet of converted trawler with a decompression chamber bolted where the fish hold used to be—he had almost talked himself back into calm. Almost.

Then he saw that someone had been aboard.

Nothing was missing. That was the tell. Divers notice displacement the way other people notice faces: a coil of rope re-wound against its habit, the hatch dogged a half-turn tighter than he'd left it, the faint chemical ghost of someone else's soap in the cabin air. On the chart table, his logbook lay open to the page he'd torn out and burned two nights ago—the page with the sonar anomaly, the unlogged compartment—and beneath it, squared neatly to the table's edge, sat a business card.

White card. Black lettering. No logo, no title. Just a name—*M. Corven*—and a telephone number, and beneath the number, in smaller type, nothing at all except the sense that the blank space itself was a message.

Rafael stood in his own doorway for a long time, listening to the *Mariposa* creak against her lines, to the water slapping the hull, to the town going about its evening behind him. He picked up the card. Good stock. Heavy. Expensive paper for a man who left no title.

He turned it over. On the reverse, in pencil, in a hand so controlled it might have been typeset:

*Km 112 is a navigation hazard. Hazards are marked, charted, and closed. Divers who mark uncharted hazards themselves create liability — for the port, for the navy, and most of all for themselves.*

Underneath, one more line:

*I'll call Thursday.*

* * *

He didn't sleep much. He lay in his bunk listening to the river and running the day backward like tape, looking for the moment the current changed. The clerk who knew his name. The shadow behind the glass. The logbook opened to a page that no longer existed—which meant they'd photographed his logs before, which meant the visit to the registry office hadn't been the beginning of anything. It had been the middle. He'd been inside something for days without feeling the walls.

Thursday came gray and windless. At noon, precisely, the telephone in the harbormaster's shack rang, and the boy who minded it came down the quay calling his name—his actual name, again, that name traveling through the town like a lit fuse—and Rafael took the receiver and said nothing.

"Sterling." A voice like a filing cabinet closing. Calm, unhurried, with the flattened vowels of a man who'd spent his career being listened to rather than listening. "You got my card."

"I got your card."

"You visited the registry office Tuesday. You asked Mr. Ferreyra about a vessel at Kilometer 112. He told you we hold no record." A pause, measured out like medicine. "That was accurate. I want to reassure you of that. There is no record. Whatever you believe you found down there—sonar ghosts, silt formations, old wrecks all look alike on a screen—it isn't anything. And men who spend their lives around the water understand that some things aren't worth bringing up."

"Is that a threat?" Rafael asked.

"It's arithmetic," Corven said. "You're a solitary operator. No company, no partners, no family on record. Your income is modest and irregular. Your equipment is aging. A man in your position has perhaps five good working years left, and every year you spend chasing a hole in the riverbed is a year you don't spend making money. I'm authorized to offer you compensation for the fishing equipment you recovered from the hazard site. Salvage rights, properly documented, fair market value plus twenty percent. You file a form, you take a check, you move on to honest work. Everyone profits. The river stays a river."

"And if I want the registry anyway?"

The pause this time was longer, and colder, and when Corven spoke again the filing-cabinet calm had developed a fine hairline crack of something harder underneath. "Sterling. Do you know what happens to men who pull things up out of that river?"

"Things come up," Rafael said. "That's the job."

"Some things," Corven said, "were put down for a reason. The men who put them down are patient, well-resourced, and *grateful* to those who help them, and thorough with those who don't. You seem like a reasonable man. Be reasonable. The offer stands until Sunday." And the line went dead before Rafael could decide whether the click was a hang-up or a door closing on a cell.

He stood in the harbormaster's shack holding the dead receiver, and the boy looked at him with round eyes, and Rafael put the handset back in its cradle gently, the way you set down something that might still be alive.

* * *

That evening he sat on the deck of the *Mariposa* with a beer he didn't drink and the river going bronze and then violet and then black, and he tried to remember why he'd ever believed he was safe.

Invisibility, he understood now, had never been a wall. It had been an agreement—one he'd made silently with the world, terms never written: *I will stay small, and you will leave me alone.* Three years on this boat. Cash wages, no fixed address, a name used only when necessary. He'd thought he was free. He'd actually thought that. And now a stranger with naval shoulders and expensive stationery had strolled through his agreement like it was wet paper, had sat in his cabin, had read his logs, had called him by name across a public quay—and the whole architecture of his quiet life had turned out to be not a fortress but a curtain, and curtains only work on people who agree not to look.

They were looking now.

He thought about taking the check. God, he thought about it honestly, turning it over the way you turn a stone on the riverbed to see what's underneath. Five years of diving left, Corven had said, and the man was right; his shoulders ached in cold water now, his ears took longer to clear, the nitrogen hung in him heavier every season. Twenty percent over market for a chest he hadn't even opened. He could buy new gear, a newer boat, three quiet years somewhere farther downriver where nobody knew the shape of his hull.

But underneath the stone, when he lifted it, was the sonar image. That clean rectangular void in the freighter's belly, too regular for collapse, too deliberate for accident. A compartment built to be forgotten. And Ferreyra's tightening face. And *there is no record*, said twice, in a voice rehearsed to say it. Governments did not seal empty rooms. Navies did not send men in pressed shirts to buy scrap iron from a diver. Whatever slept at Kilometer 112 had been put down with intent, and intent like that had weight, and weight like that meant someone, somewhere, had needed it never to surface.

Which meant it mattered.

Rafael finished the beer without tasting it and went below and pulled his charts, spreading them under the lamp, his fingers finding the river channel at Km 112, the deep scour hole where the freighter lay on her side like a sleeping animal. Forty years of silt. Forty years of official nothing. He thought of the corroded fittings he'd brushed clean on the outer hull—utilitarian rivets, working steel—and then of that one plate near the waterline, deeper than the rest, stamped with a maker's mark he hadn't recognized: a crest, worn nearly smooth, that might once have been an eagle or a double-headed bird or just damage.

Sunday, Corven had said.

The moon was rising over the breakwater, three days short of new, which meant three nights of darkness thick enough to work in. The thermocline would be stable. His mix tanks were charged. And whatever was in that sealed compartment had waited forty years in the dark, patient as the river itself, and it could wait for no one else—because no one else was coming, and no one else even knew it was there.

Rafael looked at the black water, and the black water looked back, and for the first time since he'd become invisible he understood that the choice was no longer whether to be seen.

It was what they'd see him carrying, when they caught him.

He began checking his dive gear.
