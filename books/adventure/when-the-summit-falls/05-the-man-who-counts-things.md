## 5. The Man Who Counts Things

Elias Brandt had spent eleven years learning to be no one, and he had become rather good at it.

The flat above Verhoeven's bookshop in the harbor quarter of Puerto Ceniza was narrow as a coffin stood on end, and he had furnished it the way a man furnishes a life he does not intend to keep: a cot, a kettle, one chair, and four hundred books stacked in columns that reached the ceiling because there was nowhere else for them to go. He had brought nothing across the border when they revoked his post and his name appeared on the list of persons whose opinions were inconvenient to three governments at once. Nothing but his memory, which was the one archive they could not burn, though God knew they had tried to make him wish it were.

Each morning he descended the iron stair into the shop, where old Verhoeven—deaf as a sea wall and kind in the way of men who have stopped expecting anything from the world—let him shelve, repair bindings, and sit by the window with a jeweler's loupe pressed to his eye, examining watermarks in paper that came through like flotsam. Collectors brought him things. Smugglers brought him things. Once, memorably, a customs officer had brought him a sheaf of documents seized from a dead man's luggage, asking casually whether they were worth anything, and Elias had examined them for an hour with hands that did not tremble because he had trained them not to, and then said, *reproductions, worth nothing*, and watched the officer carry Velorian military correspondence out to be pulped, and gone upstairs afterward and been sick into the sink.

That was the arrangement. He saw everything. He kept nothing. He said nothing. In exchange, they let him live in the small gray country of his anonymity, population one.

He told himself it was enough. Some mornings he even believed it.

* * *

The knock came on a Tuesday, in the hour when the light through the shop window turned the color of weak tea and Verhoeven had gone home to his supper, leaving Elias to lock up. Three knocks, spaced evenly—not the knock of a customer, which is always slightly apologetic, nor the knock of authority, which never apologizes at all. It was the knock of a man who had learned that doors open or they don't, and that either answer is information.

"Closed," Elias called, without rising.

"I'm looking for someone who reads old notation." The voice was low, river-roughened, patient. "Salvage contacts down on the Muelle Tres gave me your name. They said if I needed someone who could read what nobody else can read, there's a man above the bookshop who counts things."

Elias stood very still among his shelves. Down at the piers, the salvage men knew him only as the Dutchman, the quiet one who paid fair prices for pre-war paper and asked no questions about provenance. He had built that reputation plank by plank, the way a man builds a raft, and in eleven years no one had ever sent anyone up his stairs. Whoever this was had asked questions until the planks gave way.

"We're closed," he said again, but he was already moving toward the door, and he hated himself a little for how quickly his feet answered.

Through the frosted glass he could see a shape: broad-shouldered, weathered, standing with the particular stillness of men who work in cold water. He unlocked the door.

The man who entered was perhaps forty, sun-browned to the color of old rope, with the salt-cracked hands and permanent squint of a working diver. His clothes were good once and mended often. He carried a canvas satchel against his chest the way other men carried infants—both arms, angled inward, protective—and when their eyes met, Elias felt something turn over in his chest, some long-dormant organ of recognition that had nothing to do with faces.

Because he knew that look. It was the look of a man carrying something true and terrible and having no one on earth to show it to.

"You're Brandt," the diver said. Not a question.

"I'm the man who counts things," Elias said. "Whether I'm Brandt depends on who's asking, and I don't think you've told me yet."

"Rafael Sterling." The diver glanced at the darkened windows, the street beyond, the way a hunted animal checks wind direction without seeming to. "I dive the river. Wrecks, recoveries, whatever the insurance companies need. Three weeks ago I pulled a chest out of a freighter that isn't supposed to exist."

*Isn't supposed to exist.* Eleven years ago those words would have made Elias reach for his gloves and his catalog cards. Now they made him reach for the bolt on the door. He slid it home, drew the shade, and turned on the single lamp behind the counter—the one with the green glass shade, under which he did all his real work.

"Open the satchel," he said.

* * *

The chest itself sat in a rented locker at the harbor, Sterling told him; he'd brought samples. He unwrapped them from oilcloth on the counter with the care of a priest handling relics: a strip of corroded metal bearing a stamped seal—a mountain over crossed keys, wreathed in wheat—and then, wrapped separately, dry as a moth, a bundle of papers.

Elias put on his white cotton gloves. Old habit. Then he bent over the pages under the green lamp, and the years fell away from him like water off oiled steel.

The paper was Velorian state stock, war-years issue, coarse fiber, high rag content—you could feel the mountains in it, the pulp of a landlocked nation stretching its forests thin. The ink had oxidized to the brown of dried blood, which was normal, which meant nothing, which he told himself twice. And the notation—

He stopped breathing for a moment. He made himself start again.

It was the Kessler-Revi system. Triple-entry, cipher-keyed, with the date rendered not in the Gregorian calendar but in the Velorian administrative reckoning—year counted from the founding of the Protectorate, months numbered in the old style, each entry cross-referenced by a letter-number code that looked like nonsense unless you knew that the letters mapped to regional command offices and the numbers to cargo classes. He had spent six years of his life, the best ones, the young ones, mastering exactly this system, cataloguing exactly these archives, before the fires. He had written the reference manual. Forty pages, mimeographed, distributed to every provincial records office in the country. There were perhaps nine people alive who could read this notation fluently, and he had spent eleven years assuming most of them were dead.

"This is—" His voice came out wrong, and he cleared his throat. "Where did you say you found this?"

"A freighter on the river bottom. Sunk with all hands, near the bend below Vell's Crossing. No registry. No wreck report. The navy charts mark shoals there, nothing else." Sterling watched him with those narrowed river eyes. "You recognize it."

"It's a transport ledger." Elias turned a page with a gloved fingertip. The columns marched: dates, manifest numbers, tonnage figures, origin points. "Prisoner transport, by the class codes. Class Seven is—was—detainee movement. See here, the tonnage column doesn't record weight. It records headcount. They used the same field so the ledgers would look like ordinary freight if anyone ever audited them." He tapped a line. "Forty-one units, loaded at the Kolven Road depot, autumn of the last war year. Destination: Camp Meridian, Eastern Reclamation Zone."

"And did they arrive?"

Elias looked up. The diver's face was impassive, but behind the squint something was burning, steady as a banked fire, and Elias understood suddenly that this man had already read enough of the ledger—in fragments, in guesswork—to know the answer, and had climbed these stairs anyway, needing to hear it confirmed by someone whose confirmation would mean anything.

"No," Elias said. "They didn't arrive. None of them arrived. Look—the receiving entries stop two years before this date. Camp Meridian was closed and its records destroyed in the spring. These people were loaded onto a ship bound for a place that officially no longer existed, logged as freight, and then the ship sank, and somewhere in a ministry a clerk closed the file, and that was the end of forty-one human beings. On paper, it's very tidy. That's what I do, Mr. Sterling. I read the tidiness. The tidiness is where they hide."

The lamp hummed. Outside, a tram bell rang along the harbor road, and somewhere a gull complained about the century.

Sterling set both hands flat on the counter, as if steadying himself against current. "There are more pages. A whole chest of them. Names, dates, manifests—hundreds of entries. And a seal I didn't recognize, and numbers I couldn't crack, and I've spent three weeks asking everyone I trust, which took me about a day, and everyone pointed me here." He paused. "They said you were an archivist once. Before."

"Before," Elias agreed.

"What happened?"

What happened. What a question. What happened was that he had stood in the reading room of the National Archive of Veloria on the night the capital fell, holding the master key ring, while officers of the new provisional government fed forty years of a nation's memory into the boilers—one cart at a time, methodically, politely, a clerk named Duval checking items off a list as they burned. What happened was that Elias had filled his coat and his satchel and finally his trousers' pockets with index cards until he could barely walk, and walked out past the guards because he was nobody, a small gray man with dust on his sleeves, and no one stops a nobody. What happened was that the cards in his pockets were the only surviving catalog of an entire country's crimes, and that within five years two of the men whose signatures appeared on those cards were cabinet ministers, and that when Elias began writing letters—careful, documented, unanswerable letters—he had learned precisely how far the reach of comfortable men extends, and it extended to his office, his pension, his citizenship, and very nearly his life.

"They burned my country twice," Elias said. "Once with fire, once with silence. I objected to the second burning more loudly than was wise." He stripped off the gloves, finger by finger. "Why are you here, Mr. Sterling? You could take that chest to a newspaper. To a tribunal. To any of a dozen governments that would love the leverage. Why bring it to a disgraced archivist above a bookshop?"

Sterling was quiet for a long moment. Through the shaded window the harbor sounds came muted, softened, as if the world outside had agreed to lower its voice for what was being said inside.

"Because I took it to a newspaper," he said finally. "A friend of a friend, an editor in the capital. Two days later a man with a naval bearing visited my boat and asked me questions I didn't like. Because I asked a harbor official about the wreck's registry and watched him lie to me badly, which means he was ordered to lie well and wasn't good at it. Because whoever sunk that ship has spent thirty years making sure it stays sunk, and they're still paying attention." He looked down at the ledger page between them—at the row of ciphered numbers, the headcount disguised as tonnage, the forty-one who had never arrived anywhere. "And because when I opened that chest, I thought—I know this sounds strange—but I thought, *this needs someone who counts things*. Someone who won't just see the story. Someone who'll see the *record*. Because stories get disputed. Records convict."

Elias found that his throat had tightened. It was absurd. He was fifty-eight years old; he had been threatened, exiled, erased; he had not been moved to tears by a sentence since the night the archive burned. But no one in eleven years—not Verhoeven, not the smugglers, not the occasional sympathetic academic who bought pre-war paper and pretended not to know his face—no one had said to him: *your skill matters, your counting matters, come back from the dead and count.*

"The seal," he said, to occupy his hands, turning the corroded strip toward the lamp. "Mountain over crossed keys, wheat wreath. That's the Office of Special Transport, Velorian Interior Ministry. The office that didn't officially exist. Which means this ledger is one of perhaps four documents in the world bearing its seal, and the other three are ash." He looked up. "Mr. Sterling. Do you understand what you pulled out of that river? This isn't evidence of a crime. This is the *accounting department* of a crime. Every name, every date, every order, cross-referenced and ciphered in a system only a handful of living people can read. If the right tribunal saw this authenticated—"

"Then why hasn't anyone come for it in thirty years?" Sterling asked. "If it's sitting ten meters down in river mud, and they know it's there—"

"They don't know." Elias was already certain of it, certain the way he was certain of arithmetic. "If they knew, the wreck would have been demolished, not merely forgotten. Explosives leave less doubt than mud. No—they lost it. The ship went down off the books, in wartime chaos, and the men who signed these orders rose in the successor governments and let the river keep their secret, because rivers are patient and ministers are not." He smiled, and was surprised to find the smile had teeth in it. "But rivers also give things back. That's the trouble with water, Mr. Sterling. It remembers everything, and eventually it tells."

* * *

They worked until midnight.

Elias fetched paper and his old decoding templates from beneath the floorboards—of course he still had them, buried under a false bottom with his spare spectacles and the mimeographed manual, the whole buried archive of his former self—and they spread the sample pages across the counter, and the shop filled with the whisper of turning leaves and the scratch of Elias's pencil establishing the key. Sterling proved quicker than expected; a man who reads currents and sonar returns reads patterns easily, and by the second hour he was tracing column heads with a blunt finger, sounding out the cipher like a language half-remembered from childhood.

"Entry 41-Kolven," he read at one point. "Forty-one units. Is there a list? Names?"

"Class Seven manifests rarely carried names. Detainees were numbered at intake. That was the point of the system—people became tonnage, and tonnage has no family to write letters." Elias said it flatly, because he had learned long ago that flatness was the only way to say such things aloud. "But the intake logs might survive somewhere. If the camp's records burned, the depot records might not have. Everything in Veloria's system was duplicated—that was their religion, redundancy. They were fanatics about paper and monsters about people. It's a common combination."

Sterling nodded slowly, and Elias watched him absorb it, watched the diver understand that the chest in the harbor locker was not an ending but a door, and that behind the door were hundreds of doors more, and that opening them would require exactly the kind of attention that got men like Elias Brandt exiled in the first place.

Around one in the morning, Sterling repacked the satchel. At the door, with the bolt half drawn, he stopped.

"The men at the pier said you don't talk about before," he said. "They said you're invisible on purpose." He hesitated. "So am I, mostly. Three years now. A boat, a river, no fixed address, no history. It works fine until it doesn't." He met Elias's eyes. "Tonight it stopped working fine for both of us, I think."

"Yes," Elias said. "I believe it did."

After the door closed, Elias stood a long time in the dark shop, listening to the harbor breathe. Then he climbed his coffin-narrow stair, but he did not sleep. He sat in his one chair in the dark and held the mimeographed manual in his lap, forty pages in his own handwriting, the only book of his authorship in the world, and he felt the strangest sensation: as though a door long painted shut in the wall of his life had quietly swung open, and through it came the smell of river water and old paper and, impossibly, purpose.

They will come for him now, he thought. They will come for the diver first, and then for me, because I am the only key left to this lock, and keys frighten men who have changed the locks. Let them come. He was old, and poor, and legally nonexistent, and he had never in his life been less afraid.

Toward dawn he took a pencil and a fresh card from his hidden store, and in the small precise hand that had catalogued a nation, he wrote:

*Item one. Ledger, Velorian Office of Special Transport, recovered from river wreck, location withheld. Contents: prisoner transport manifests, war's final year. Status: unique. Provenance: a diver who could have sold it and didn't.*

And beneath it, after consideration, a second line, the first thing he had catalogued in eleven years that was not for sale:

*Item two. Allies: one, so far.*

Three days later, a woman arrived at the bookshop asking, in careful accented Spanish, for pre-war Velorian paper—and left a note on the counter unsigned except for a number: *41*.
