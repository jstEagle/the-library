## 12. The Smuggler's Map

The airstrip at Puerto Ceniza was not an airstrip so much as a scar—a strip of crushed coral and packed clay where the mangroves had been cut back and never allowed to return. Tomás Iriarte had been landing on it for forty-one years, first in a borrowed crop-duster with the registration painted over twice, now in the Cessna 208 he called *La Viuda*—the Widow—because she had outlived three owners, two governments, and every man who'd ever told him to retire.

He was under her fuselage when they came, checking the drag link he didn't trust, when he heard footsteps on coral that crunched in the wrong rhythm. Tourists walked like they were sorry to disturb the ground. Soldiers walked like the ground owed them something. These four walked like neither—which meant smugglers, or worse, or people who had learned to walk carefully from someone who'd taught them well.

He slid out on his creeper and looked up into the sun.

The tall one he knew by reputation before he knew by face: Rafael Sterling, the salvage diver who'd pulled something out of the river that had the navy's fixers driving down from the capital in cars too clean for the road. Tomás heard things. That was most of what a smuggler was, in the end—a man who listened.

"You're Sterling." He stood, joints announcing themselves one by one, knees first, then the shoulder he'd broken over the Cordillera's foothills in '89. "You're shorter than your trouble."

"I'm told I am," Sterling said. He had river-tan skin and eyes the color of water over silt, watchful without being nervous. Beside him: an old man with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who'd spent decades being careful in libraries, carrying a canvas bag the way other men carried infants. And a woman, mid-thirties, dark-haired, with the particular stillness of someone who translated for tribunals and had learned that words could be weapons if you held them still long enough.

"And you brought friends." Tomás wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since the previous administration. "I don't fly strangers."

"We're not strangers," the old man said. "Not entirely. You knew Aurelio Sandoval."

The name went through Tomás like a fishhook finding old scar tissue. He kept his face still. Forty years of practice at keeping his face still.

"Aurelio Sandoval is dead," he said.

"Thirty years dead," the old man agreed. "In the winter of the last year of Veloria. Which is why we're here."

Tomás looked at each of them again, slower this time. The diver. The archivist—he recognized the type now; he'd flown enough of them out of burning countries to know how they held their bags. The translator. And behind them, half-hidden in the shade of the Widow's wing, a fifth figure: a mountain woman with wind-burned cheeks and a climbing rope coiled over one shoulder like a sleeping snake, who watched him the way a hawk watches a field—not hungry yet, but keeping count of everything that moved.

"Get in the shade," Tomás said finally. "All of you. It's a hundred degrees on that coral and I'm not having anyone faint on my airstrip. People talk."

* * *

His hangar smelled the way it always smelled: avgas, engine oil, salt, and the ghost of the coffee he'd spilled on the workbench in 1997 and never fully scrubbed. He made fresh coffee anyway, because some rituals were load-bearing, and poured it into tin cups while the old man—the archivist, Elias Brandt, a name Tomás had heard whispered in customs offices from here to the border—unrolled a map across the workbench, weighting the corners with four empty cups.

The Cordillera del Viento ran down the center of the map like a spine that had been set badly and healed crooked. Southern approach. The glacier country. The white silence where men went in winter and maps went blank.

"You want me to fly you to Estero Blanco," Tomás said. "That's not an airfield. That's a cow pasture with ambitions."

"It's the closest strip to the southern approach," Sterling said. "We've done the research."

"Research." Tomás snorted. "I've landed at Estero Blanco eleven times. Twice on purpose. The last time, a mule was standing in the middle of my rollout and I decided we were both lucky to be alive. What's at the end of this flight? Because nobody flies to Estero Blanco for the scenery."

Nobody answered immediately. The translator—Mirena Kovač, Brandt had said, though Tomás hadn't needed the introduction; he knew the name Kovač from lists he'd memorized once and never managed to forget—looked at the diver, and the diver looked at the archivist, and the archivist looked at the map as if the answer might be written in its margins.

Tomás waited. Waiting was another thing a smuggler was, in the end.

"There's a chest," Sterling said at last. "A second chest. Carried over the pass thirty years ago by a Velorian officer. It holds evidence. Names. Proof. Enough to bring down men who are currently ministers and admirals."

Tomás set his cup down very carefully, because his hand wanted to shake and he did not permit his hand to shake. Not in front of witnesses.

"The war's last winter," he said. "Sandoval came through Puerto Ceniza with a box heavier than a coffin and a guide named Anand, and they went up into the ice and only the guide came partway back, and what came back of the guide wasn't all there." He looked at Mirena. "Your father was on those prisoner transports. Number forty-one."

Her breath caught—an audible thing in the quiet hangar—and he saw Brandt put a hand near hers without touching, the way you steady a lantern in wind.

"How do you know that?" she asked.

"Because I've spent thirty years knowing things I couldn't say out loud," Tomás said, "and it turns out today the bill comes due." He turned to the map. "Estero Blanco. Weather window. Calving season opens the icefall corridor for maybe six days, maybe four, maybe two—you don't get to pick, the glacier picks for you. After that, nothing crosses until spring. Nothing. Not planes, not prayers."

"So you'll take us," Sterling said.

"I didn't say that."

"You flew for Sandoval's people. In the war."

Tomás laughed, and it came out wrong, rusty, like a door on a house no one lived in anymore. "Is that the story? That I flew for the resistance?" He shook his head. "Here's the story, diver. In the last winter of Veloria, I was twenty-six years old and I was paid—in fuel, in favors, in the kind of currency that doesn't show up on manifests—to move cargo for men whose names I pretended not to read on the paperwork. One night they gave me a flight plan north and a sealed crate and told me not to ask. And I didn't ask. I was good at not asking. It was practically my profession." His voice flattened. "The crate was cold. I remember thinking that was strange. Cargo isn't usually cold. I strapped it down and I flew it and I never asked, and years later I found out what the cold crates were, and I have been flying away from that knowledge ever since, in ever-smaller circles, until now my whole world is this airstrip and a widow of an airplane. So don't tell me what I did in the war. I keep better records than the archive did."

Silence. Even the wind outside seemed to lean closer through the corrugated walls.

It was the mountain woman who spoke—Sefa Anand, granddaughter of the guide, Tomás realized, seeing the jaw, the same stubborn line of it in the photographs he'd once studied too long. "My grandfather never talked about what happened after," she said. "You did. Just now. First time in thirty years, I'd guess."

"Second time," Tomás said. "First time was to a priest who charged me for it."

Something almost like a smile moved through the group—there and gone, the first shared thing between five strangers—and Tomás hated how much it loosened something in his chest.

* * *

He laid out terms the way he laid out cargo: everything weighed, nothing hidden, straps checked twice.

"Rule one. I decide when weather grounds us. Not you, not your deadline, not your tribunal, not your dead. If I say we sit, we sit. If I say we turn around, we turn around, and you can hate me at ten thousand feet or on the ground, your choice. I've buried passengers who argued with weather. I don't intend to bury more."

"Agreed," Sterling said, and the others nodded, and Tomás noted that the diver answered fastest—the reflex of a man used to making decisions underwater, where hesitation has a body count.

"Rule two. Weight. Every kilo you bring is a kilo of fuel I can't carry, and the approach to Estero Blanco climbs the whole way. You pack like monks. If it doesn't keep you alive on a glacier, it stays."

"The chest stays with us," Mirena said. Flat. Final. A sentence with a locked door in it.

"The chest weighs?"

"Eleven kilos," Brandt said.

"Then the chest flies. But understand—if we go down, the chest burns with us, because I will not have it recovered by whoever finds the wreck. There are men in the capital who would pay a great deal for that wreckage." He let that sit. "Which brings me to rule three. We fly low, we fly the terrain, and if anything military lifts off from any base along our route, we land and we wait, even if waiting costs us the window. Dead couriers deliver nothing."

"And your price?" Sterling asked. "Because we don't have much money."

Tomás looked at them for a long moment—at the diver who'd scuttled his own boat, he'd heard, drowning his own life to carry a dead nation's ledger; at the archivist exiled for asking questions; at the translator who'd spent her whole career giving other people's grief accurate grammar; at the guide carrying her grandfather's unfinished story; at the canvas bag with the ledger inside, which sat against Brandt's ribs like a second heart.

"My price," Tomás said, "is that when this is over—whatever 'over' means—you tell me what was in the cold crates. All of it. Every name. I carried them; I'll carry their names too. That's the fare. That and fuel costs, which you can pay me in whatever currency survives the next year."

Brandt's eyes were wet when Tomás glanced over, and the old man disguised it badly by pretending to study the map. "Mr. Iriarte—"

"Don't." Tomás held up a hand. "Don't thank me. Thanking makes it noble, and I've spent forty years making sure nothing I do is noble. Noble gets you investigated." He jabbed a finger at the map, at the crooked spine of the range. "Now look here. This is how we go."

He traced the route with a blunt finger stained with avgas: south along the coast where radar coverage lapsed behind the headlands, then inland through the river valleys, hugging terrain so closely the Widow's wingtips would seem to comb the treetops, then the long climb to Estero Blanco through a pass that required threading a saddle between two peaks with a margin, on a bad day, of nothing at all.

"Corven's people will expect the direct corridor," he said. "Everyone expects the direct corridor. So we go the way smugglers go—the ugly way, the slow way, the way that adds six hours and subtracts every pair of eyes watching for us." He tapped the saddle. "Weather permitting."

"When do we leave?" Sefa asked.

Tomás walked to the hangar door and looked at the sky. It was the sky he'd been reading since before these four had been born—except the girl, maybe—and it said what it always said this time of year: high cirrus moving in from the northwest like a rumor, pressure falling, the mountains putting on their cloud caps the way old men put on hats before rain.

"Tomorrow," he said. "First light. The window's opening early this year—I've had three radio reports from herders on the southern slopes, and the calving's already started, which means the icefall corridor may be open sooner than anyone planned." He turned back and found them all looking at him with that expression he remembered from other flights, other desperate cargoes, other winters: hope, wearing the mask of practicality.

"One more thing," he said, and dug into the lockbox bolted beneath the workbench, past the cash and the false papers and the pistol he hadn't cleaned since the last government, and came out with a photograph gone soft and gray at the edges. He set it on the map, in the middle of the white of the range.

Five young men in front of a mail plane. Himself, twenty-six and grinning like the future owed him money. And at the end of the row, arm slung over Tomás's shoulder, a broad-faced man laughing at something outside the frame: Aurelio Sandoval, who had asked him, three weeks before walking into the ice, whether Tomás would fly one last cargo if it mattered, and Tomás—who had already carried too many cold crates—had said no.

"He asked me for one honest flight," Tomás said. "One. And I told him I was done flying for lost causes, and he said, 'It's not lost, Tomás. It's just late.' And then he went up the mountain with my grandfather's guilt and a box heavier than a coffin, and I stayed down here and got old." He picked up the photograph and handed it to Mirena, because it belonged to her more than to him—it belonged to everyone whose names had become numbers. "So yes. I'll fly you. But understand what you're buying. You're not buying a pilot. You're buying thirty years of a debt, and debts like that, they don't come cheap, and they don't come calm."

Mirena held the photograph in both hands, the way Brandt held the ledger.

"What was in the crate Sandoval carried?" she asked quietly. "Do you know?"

"No," Tomás said. "But I know what he told me the night before he left, when he bought me a drink I didn't want and made me drink it anyway. He said: 'If I don't come back, the truth will. It's patient. It's the most patient thing there is.'" He looked out the hangar door at the gathering clouds, at the mountains already wearing their white crowns beyond the haze. "Thirty years, and tomorrow morning we go find out if he was right."

Outside, the first drops of an early rain began ticking on the Widow's wings, soft as fingertips, like something knocking politely at a door long shut.

Tomás began pulling her covers off. They'd fly at first light—and he had a plane to make ready, and a debt that had finally, finally come calling.
