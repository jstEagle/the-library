## 13. Leaving the Water

The tide went out at four in the morning, and Rafael Sterling went out with it.

He had chosen the hour the way he chose most things—by what the water allowed. Low tide exposed the mudflats below the harbor wall in a long gray smear, and the boats that sat on their keels in the ooze looked like animals sleeping upright, dreaming of deeper channels. His boat was among them, third from the pilings, listing slightly to port as she settled into the mud that had held her for three years. *Marejada*. He had named her himself, with a can of white paint and a steady hand, on a night he could no longer quite remember—the night after he'd bought her with salvage money and decided, without ever saying it aloud to anyone, that this was where his life would happen now: here, in the space between the waterline and the sky, going nowhere in particular.

He walked out across the flats in rubber boots, carrying a canvas bag that clinked softly with every second step. The mud took each footfall with a sound like a slow kiss, then released him grudgingly, sucking at his heels as if it knew something he hadn't told it yet. Gulls stood scattered across the flats like an audience waiting for a show. Above the harbor, the town was still dark except for the bakery on the corner of the fish market, where a light burned yellow behind salt-fogged glass, and the smell of bread had already begun its slow drift down toward the water, the way it did every morning, indifferent to whatever the people below it were doing with their lives.

Rafael stopped ten meters from the boat and just looked at her for a while.

She was not much to see. Twenty-eight feet of river freighter hull converted badly to coastal work, a wheelhouse patched with fiberglass where a storm had put a branch through it two winters ago, paint gone chalky at the boot stripe, a rust stain running down from the anchor hawse like an old tear track. Her name was flaking. He'd been meaning to repaint it for a year. There was always a year.

But she had kept him alive. That was the thing about a boat that nobody who didn't live on one understood—not fishermen, not even sailors, but people who *lived* aboard, who slept inside the sound of water moving against planking. She had kept him alive in the way that mattered most, which was not the way of pumps and bilges and seaworthy lines, though she'd done all that too. She had kept him alive by being small enough to maintain alone, mobile enough to leave when leaving was wise, and anonymous enough that no one had asked him a real question in three years. She had been his invisibility made wood and diesel. And now he was going to open her veins and let the harbor take her, because staying invisible had stopped being enough, and the only way left to hide was to stop existing altogether.

He climbed aboard over the rail, boots leaving dark prints on the deck, and stood in the cockpit breathing the familiar smell of her—diesel and damp wool and the faint chemical sweetness of the epoxy he used on everything, and underneath it all the deep green smell of the river silt that never fully washed out of any hull that worked these waters. Home smelled like this. He had never once called it home, not out loud, not even to himself, but there it was anyway, the word surfacing now precisely because he was about to lose it, the way a man's hand remembers a shape only when the shape is taken away.

*Three years,* he thought. *Three years, and I'm about to drown you in six inches of harbor mud.*

He got to work.

The first thing was the seacocks. Every boat has them—the valves below the waterline, the places where the sea is always trying to get in and the builder's job was to give the owner a way to say no. Rafael went below into the cabin, into the low green darkness that smelled of his own sleeping body and old coffee, and knelt on the sole plates one by one, opening each access panel with the small brass key he kept on a ring with his dive knife. Engine intake. Head intake. Galley drain. He opened them all, and then, instead of closing them again, he wrapped each valve handle in friction tape and jammed it open with a wooden wedge, so that when the time came, nothing short of a wrench and twenty minutes underwater would shut them again. It felt strange, doing violence so carefully. Like loading a gun he intended to point at a friend.

Then the through-hulls themselves. He worked a drill bit into the softest of them—the galley drain, bronze gone thin with years of electrolysis—and drilled it out to a hole the width of his little finger. Water did not come in yet; they were aground, sitting in the mud, and the sea was still forty meters away and falling. But the hole was there now, patient, a mouth waiting for the tide to come back and fill it.

He sat back on his heels in the cabin and looked around at the interior of his life.

It was astonishing how little there was, and how much. A bunk with a wool blanket worn thin at the shoulder. A shelf of paperbacks swollen with damp, spines cracked white. The galley with its single burner and its dented kettle. On the bulkhead above the chart table, taped in a neat grid, the photographs he permitted himself—a photo of his mother's house before the fire, which was to say a photo of a place that no longer existed anywhere but in this image and in his memory; a photo of a dive boat crew from another life, faces he no longer wrote to; a postcard of a lighthouse sent to him by no one, bought by himself at a gas station, mailed to himself from the next town over, because some mornings a man needs proof that someone, somewhere, thought of him, even if the someone was also him. He had built a whole architecture of solitude in this eight-foot-wide box, and it had been warm, and it had been enough, right up until the moment a corroded chest came up out of the river full of dead men's names, and the warmth had turned out to be a kind of sleep.

Elias's voice came back to him from three nights ago, in the back room above the bookshop, lamplight on the archivist's ruined hands: *You understand what you're holding. You understand they will come for it, and for anyone near it.* And Mirena, quieter, in the doorway with her coat already on: *They don't need to find us, Rafael. They need to find the chest. Everything else is arithmetic.*

Arithmetic. He had done the sums lying awake in this bunk, listening to the water slap the hull. Stay, and Corven's people would come to the harbor, polite and patient, and ask their questions, and eventually stop asking. Run, and run visibly, and lead them straight to Elias and Mirena and Tomás and the whole fragile conspiracy of ordinary people who had agreed, against every instinct, to matter. Or disappear—become a drowned man, a cautionary tale, a line in the harbor master's log: *Freighter Marejada found foundered at moorings, owner missing, presumed lost overboard during the blow.* And while the navy's fixers spent their weeks confirming a death that suited everyone, a chest would be moving south under tarpaulin in the bed of Tomás Iriarte's truck, toward a mountain pass that opened for days and closed for a season.

There had been no fourth option. He had looked for one the way he searched a wreck, methodically, compartment by compartment, and found nothing but water.

Rafael stood up. His knees cracked in the cold cabin air. He began to take the boat apart.

Not violently—that was the discipline of it. A man scuttling his own vessel could do it like a butcher or like a surgeon, and he found, working in the gray pre-dawn light with the gulls beginning to complain overhead, that he wanted to do it like a surgeon. He stripped the electronics first: the depth sounder, the VHF, the GPS unit he'd bought secondhand and never fully trusted. Each one came off its bracket with four screws and went into the canvas bag, along with its wiring harness coiled and tied. The bag was for Tomás. A smuggler pilot could use a marine radio, and besides, taking them meant the boat would be found naked, stripped, consistent with a sinking rather than an abandonment. Details mattered. Corven struck him as a man who read details the way other men read newspapers.

He took the tools too—all of them, the good wrenches, the dive spares, the box of bronze fittings he'd been hoarding against a refit that would now never happen. He took the charts, folded into a waterproof tube. He took the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, from its hiding place under the sole beneath the water tank, and felt the weight of it settle against his spine when he stowed it in the pack. Forty-one names. Forty-one men who had boarded a Velorian transport and arrived nowhere, recorded in a clerk's careful hand by someone who had known, even then, that paper might be the only grave those men would ever get.

And then there was the rest of it. The things that weren't equipment. The blanket—he rolled it and hesitated, and finally shoved it into the pack, because Sefa Anand had told him the high camps dropped below freezing even in calving season, and sentiment was a luxury the glacier would bill him for later. The kettle. Two mugs, because three years alone and he still owned two mugs, and he had never let himself examine why. The paperback with the broken spine, a western he'd read four times, dog-eared to the page where the drifter rides out of town alone. He looked at that page for a long moment. Then he left the book on the bunk, face-down, exactly as if he'd only stepped out to check the weather and would be back before the coffee cooled.

Let whoever searched her find that. Let them wonder.

* * *

By six the light had come up the color of pewter, and the tide had turned.

Rafael sat in the wheelhouse with the engine idling, feeling the vibration come up through the deck plates into the soles of his feet, that familiar tremor he had fallen asleep to more nights than he could count. The mud was still holding her, but the water was creeping back across the flats in long silver fingers, filling the channel, climbing the pilings, and in an hour—two at the most—*Marejada* would float free on her mooring, and the seacocks would be under, and the little drilled mouth in the galley drain would begin its slow, patient work.

He had planned to simply cast off and let her sink at the mooring. That had been the plan: clean, deniable, a boat that failed at her lines in the night, a tragedy of maintenance deferred. But sitting there with his hands on the wheel he had gripped through three years of weather, he found he couldn't do it. Not here. Not in front of the bakery window and the harbor wall and the gulls and the whole small audience of a town that knew his boat if it didn't know him. If she sank here, she sank in public, and public things got investigated, raised, picked over. No. She would go down the way he'd lived aboard her—out past the breakwater, alone, where the channel dropped off into the old riverbed and the water went from brown to black in the space of a boat length. Thirty meters of cold estuary water. She'd sit in the mud down there with the wrecks he knew by heart, and divers would find her someday, maybe, and find nothing aboard but a bunk and a book and a story that added up to an accident.

He put the wheel over and backed her off the mud.

The hull came free with a shudder and a sucking groan, mud peeling away beneath her keel, and then she was floating, riding light and wrong with her tanks empty and her insides gutted, and the harbor slid past the windows—the bakery light extinguished now, the fish market shuttered, the moored dinghies knocking their small wooden hearts out against their buoys. Nobody saw him go. That was the point of the hour, and it should have felt like victory, and instead it felt like the last door of a house closing behind him while he was still standing in it.

At the breakwater he stopped the engine and let her drift.

The swell came through the gap in long low rollers, lifting her gently, setting her down, and beyond the wall the estuary lay flat and enormous under the brightening sky, the far shore a smudge of pine, the water the color of hammered tin. This was the view he had woken to a thousand mornings. He knew every buoy in it, every sandbar's mood, every place the current ran funny on an ebb tide. He had measured his life here in dive-hours and river miles, and it had been a small life, and he had told himself small was safe, and the telling had almost been true.

Rafael went below and opened the last seacock by hand—the main intake, the big one, the artery. Cold harbor water gushed over his boots, shocking, immediate, and he watched it swirl around the sole plates and begin to find the drains, and he made himself stand there and feel it, because a man ought to feel the thing he's choosing. Then he climbed out, closed the companionway hatch behind him, and started the engine one final time.

*Marejada* moved out past the breakwater at idle, trailing a thin wake, riding lower by the minute. He steered her with one hand. With the other he held the wheelhouse key ring—the brass key that opened every panel on the boat—and when the channel marker came abeam and the bottom fell away beneath them, he dropped the keys over the side. They went down without a sound. Small things, keys. A whole life fits on a key ring, and weighs less than a fish.

At the edge of the deep channel he throttled back to neutral and let the engine die, and in the sudden silence he heard the water coming in below decks, a soft insistent gurgle, the sound of a boat drinking. He stepped to the rail. The water alongside was black and glassy and very cold, and his reflection floated in it, distorted, a man-shaped smudge about to be erased.

"Three years," he said out loud, to the boat, because there was no one else to say it to. "You were enough. I want you to know that."

*Marejada* settled by the stern. Water walked up the transom in a slow silver sheet. The wheelhouse windows went under one at a time, each one flashing once with reflected sky and going dark, and then the deck was awash and the rail, and then she wasn't a boat anymore, just a disturbance, a spreading ring of ripples, a swirl of bubbles rising from thirty feet down where his home was folding itself into the mud beside the bones of older wrecks. He watched until the bubbles stopped. It took eleven minutes. He counted every one of them, because somebody should.

Then Rafael Sterling pulled his cap down, shouldered the pack with the ledger in it, and rowed the inflatable the half mile to the rocks below the coast road, where Tomás Iriarte's truck was supposed to appear before seven.

His name was dead now. Rafael Sterling, lost overboard, presumed drowned—by tonight it would be in the harbor log, and by tomorrow in Corven's files, and the file would close, and the hunt would turn elsewhere, toward ghosts instead of a man. He had traded his name for the chest's survival, and it was, he told himself, a fair price. He had never liked the name much anyway. It had belonged to a man who wanted to be left alone, and that man had drowned very quietly this morning, in eleven minutes, in cold black water, and what was walking up the rocks through the gorse with mud on his boots was somebody new—somebody with no boat, no mooring, no harbor, no self to protect, and therefore nothing left to lose.

That was the theory, anyway. But halfway up the cliff path, hearing the gulls cry behind him over the empty water where *Marejada* used to be, Rafael had to stop and press his hand flat against the rock and breathe, because grief had arrived after all, punctual as the tide, and it did not care that the funeral had been faked. You could bury a boat. You could bury a name. What you couldn't fake your way out of was the knowledge that the man who had slept to the sound of water on planking, who had owned two mugs and needed only one, who had been lonely for three years and called it freedom—that man was really gone, and Rafael had killed him himself, carefully, like a surgeon, before dawn.

Headlights swung onto the coast road above him, an old truck grinding down through the fog with its cargo bed covered in tarpaulin. Tomás, early, exactly as promised. Rafael wiped his face with the back of his glove, once, briskly, the way you'd flick water off after a dive, and climbed the rest of the path toward the road and the mountains and whatever was left of his life.

Behind him, the estuary lay smooth and unbroken, keeping its newest secret with all the others.
