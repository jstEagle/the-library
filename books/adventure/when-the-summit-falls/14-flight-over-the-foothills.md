## 14. Flight Over the Foothills

The plane was older than three of the four people climbing into her, and Tomás Iriarte loved her the way you love a thing that has never once lied to you.

*La Golondrina* — the swallow — a forty-year-old de Havilland turboprop conversion with a cargo belly that had carried everything from coffee to contraband to, on one memorable night in '89, a prize bull who had opinions about turbulence. Her paint was peeling down to bare aluminum in long silver streaks, like an old man's scalp showing through white hair. Her instruments were half original, half cannibalized from aircraft that hadn't been so lucky. Her port-side door still bore the scorch mark from the night at Puerto Ceniza that Tomás did not talk about and would not talk about and had, in fact, paid two separate men to stop talking about.

"Is she safe?" Mirena Kovač asked, standing on the airstrip grass with her arms crossed against the dawn wind, looking up at the swallow the way a woman looks at a dog that has just bared its teeth.

Tomás considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"No," he said. "But she is honest. There is a difference. A safe airplane lies to you — tells you nothing can go wrong, right up until it does. This one warns you about everything. She groans before she complains. She shudders before she breaks." He patted the fuselage the way you'd pat a horse. "You will always know exactly how much trouble we are in. Most people find that comforting. The ones who don't tend to fly with other pilots."

"And where are they?" Elias Brandt asked mildly, from behind a wall of crates he was pretending he could lift alone.

"Exactly," said Tomás.

Rafael Sterling said nothing at all. He had been saying very little since they'd burned his boat and buried his old life in the river silt, and Tomás had watched him do it from the cockpit window with the particular ache of a man who recognized the ceremony. He'd sunk his own vessel once too, twenty-six years ago, off a coast he still couldn't name out loud. You didn't scuttle a ship because you were done with the water. You did it because the water was done being safe for you.

He climbed the boarding ladder last, after the chest was strapped down amidships under cargo netting and two of Sefa Anand's glacier ropes, and he ran his preflight the way he'd run it ten thousand times: fuel, flaps, trim, magnetos, the little brass compass bolted to the dash that had never once pointed north when anyone important was watching. Outside, the foothills were going gold at their eastern edges, the mist burning off the river valley in slow ropes, and somewhere behind them — a day's drive, maybe less — Corven's people were waking up in cheap motel rooms and discovering that four ghosts had grown wings.

"Everyone buckled?" he called back.

"We're buckled," Rafael said.

"Sefa?"

The guide sat wedged between the chest and the fuselage frame, her pack between her boots, her eyes already closed. "I have climbed in storms that screamed," she said without opening them. "A machine that hums does not frighten me."

"She'll scream too, eventually."

"Then I will be impressed."

Tomás laughed — a real laugh, rusty at the hinges — and pushed the mixture rich, and *La Golondrina* woke up with a cough and a roar and a shudder that traveled the whole airframe like a dog shaking off rain. The propeller blurred into a disc. The dials trembled toward life. And the swallow rolled forward onto the grass strip that wasn't really a strip at all, just a mile of mowed pasture with a windsock some farmer's kid had sewn from a bedsheet, and gathered herself, and lifted.

The ground fell away. The river appeared below them, a brown ribbon stitching together all the country they were leaving, and Tomás banked south, and the morning opened up in front of him like a book no one had read yet.

* * *

By the second hour they had settled into the rhythm that Tomás privately thought of as *the flock finding its order* — every bird jockeying, every bird adjusting, until suddenly there was a formation and nobody remembered arranging it.

Elias had claimed the jump seat beside the cockpit, ostensibly to navigate, actually because it was the only seat with a reading light, and he had spread three maps across his knees and the instrument console and Tomás's flight bag, weighted at the corners with a tin cup, a wrench, and a hard-boiled egg he had produced from nowhere and refused to explain.

"This contour line," Elias said, tapping, "is wrong. Or the mountain is wrong. One of the two."

"The mountain," Tomás said. "That survey's from the fifties. The whole southern approach has risen since then — the glaciers push the valleys around like furniture. Where does your map say the pass is?"

"Here. Nine thousand feet."

"And where will the pass actually be? Somewhere between eight and ten, depending on the year, depending on the melt, depending on whether God got enough sleep. Which is why," he said, reaching over and flipping one of the maps upside down purely to watch Elias's eye twitch, "we don't navigate by paper. We navigate by the range itself. The Cordillera doesn't move. Everything else is gossip."

"You enjoy this," Elias said. It wasn't quite an accusation. It had the tone of a man cataloguing a specimen.

"I enjoy flying. I tolerate passengers. I *love* annoying archivists." Tomás eased the yoke left around a building thunderhead, white-topped and purple-shadowed, dragging its skirt of rain across a valley of green tea-colored fields. "Thirty years I flew this route with men who only ever asked two questions: how fast and how much. You lot ask questions like *is that contour line accurate*. It's a holiday."

From the back, Mirena's voice: "What was the cargo, usually? On this route?"

And there it was — the question under the question, the one she'd been carrying since the safehouse, polite as a knife wrapped in a napkin. Tomás let the silence stretch just long enough to prove he'd heard it.

"Coffee," he said. "Medicine, sometimes, when the villages were cut off. Radios. Once, a piano." He glanced back at her. She was watching him with those tribunal-translator eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "And once or twice, things that didn't have manifests. People leaving places they couldn't stay. That was after the war, mostly. Velorians. There were a lot of roads closed to them and one sky that wasn't."

Mirena held his gaze for a moment longer. Then she nodded, slowly, and looked out the window, and Tomás understood that the interview was not over — merely adjourned. Translators, he suspected, were like that. They kept every conversation in escrow until the exchange rate improved.

Sefa slept. Or performed sleeping. Rafael moved between the chest and the windows in a slow patrol that he probably believed was casual, checking straps, checking sky, checking the faces of the people he had, without ever saying the word aloud, made himself responsible for. Tomás watched him in the chrome trim of the instrument panel — a curved, warped little mirror — and thought: *there goes a man learning to count past one.* It had taken Tomás thirty years and a dead archive to learn the same arithmetic. Some lessons you could only teach yourself by losing everything flat first.

"Storm cell ahead-left," Elias said, pointing. "Do we go around or—"

"We go under. Watch." Tomás fed in a little nose-down, felt the swallow pick up speed the way she liked, and dropped beneath the cloud deck into a corridor of hard golden light and rain-smell, skimming above fields that flashed past green and black and silver. The turbulence hit them in soft punches, and the cargo netting creaked, and behind him he heard Mirena make a small involuntary sound and then, furiously, laugh at herself for making it.

"There she is," Tomás said. "There's the honest airplane telling you exactly how much trouble we're in. Comforting, isn't it?"

"It is not," Mirena called back.

"It grows on you."

"Like mold."

"*Like family,*" Tomás corrected, grinning, and pulled up out of the corridor into clean blue air, and the whole cabin laughed — even Rafael, a short bark of surprise at himself, quickly smothered but not quickly enough. Tomás filed the sound away. First laugh. Pilots kept logs of such things.

* * *

They saw the navy aircraft at noon.

Elias saw it first — of course he did; the man noticed errata in reality the way other people noticed weather. "Tomás. Eleven o'clock high. Is that traffic normal for out here?"

Tomás tilted his head back and found it: a speck, resolving as it grew, twin-engine, gray-green, moving with the unhurried arrogance of something owned by a government. Patrol configuration. Lazy circles. The kind of flying you did when you weren't hunting yet but wanted everyone to know you *could*.

"That," Tomás said, "is a coastal surveillance bird wearing army paint, which means it's navy wearing army paint, which means someone is paying for fuel they shouldn't be buying." He checked the swallow's shadow on the hills below — small, faithful, incriminating. "Question is whether we're interesting or whether we're Tuesday. Light aircraft, southern heading, middle of nowhere. Could be smugglers. Could be geologists. Could be a rich idiot going fishing."

"Could be us," Rafael said quietly.

"Could be us," Tomás agreed. "So we become Tuesday."

He throttled back, dropped the flaps a notch, and let the swallow slump into the sky's background noise — slower, lower, sloppier, the aerial equivalent of a man slouching past a checkpoint whistling. He trimmed her nose-down toward a patch of broken country where the foothills threw up thermal chaos, and he flew badly on purpose, wobbling, correcting late, the portrait of a weekend pilot in over his head. Beside him Elias watched the gray speck with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hated.

"It's turning," Elias said. "It's — yes. Coming this way. Slowly."

"Mm." Tomás's hands were perfectly calm on the yoke. His mouth had gone dry as ledger paper. "Everyone keep loose. If they come alongside, nobody waves. Waving is memorable. We are committed to being forgettable."

The speck became a shape. The shape became an aircraft — a twin-boom patrol type, military gray, roundels painted over with civilian registration numbers that fooled absolutely no one, flying with the bored menace of a dog on a long leash. It came down out of its circle in a wide descending arc that would bring it abeam of them in perhaps ninety seconds, close enough to read faces through glass.

Tomás had been stopped twice at gunpoint, boarded once, shot at three times, and betrayed more often than he allowed himself to count, and his body knew the ladder of fear the way his hands knew the yoke. This was rung three. Uncomfortable. Not yet fatal. Rung three meant your options were still open; it was rung six where the options started closing like doors in a draft.

"Radio's quiet," he murmured. "Good. Quiet means they haven't matched us to a name yet. The moment they have a name, they'll call it in, and then we're not Tuesday anymore."

"What do we do if they match us?" Mirena asked. No tremor in it. He was starting to respect that woman the way you respect a blade you find in an unexpected drawer.

"Then I show them why this valley system has eleven exits and I've used all of them." He tapped the windscreen. "But look — look at the range."

And they looked.

The Cordillera del Viento had been growing on the horizon for an hour the way mountains do — first a suggestion, then a rumor, then a fact, then the only fact left in the world. White on white on white, ridge stacked behind ridge into a haze of distance, the massif shouldering up into the stratosphere with its head wrapped in lenticular clouds that stood still while the world streamed past them. And along its southern flank, catching the midday sun, the icefalls burned — rivers of frozen light pouring down the valleys, seracs glittering like a city built by something that had never heard of mercy.

The navy aircraft reached the edge of that whiteness and stopped.

Not turned away, exactly. It *stopped* — flattened out, waggled its wings once in what might have been acknowledgment or contempt, and began a slow, sulking climb back the way it came, hugging the legal edge of the restricted zone like a cat avoiding water. Whatever orders its pilot carried, they ended at the ice. The Cordillera belonged to no ministry. Its weather answered to no admiral. Even Vess, Tomás thought, even a man who could erase a nation from three governments' archives, could not erase the mountain, and there was a fierce, childish joy in that — in watching the gray machine shrink against all that indifferent white.

"They won't follow past the foothills," Tomás said. "Not in that airframe. Those valleys eat twins for breakfast. And whatever fixer sent them knows it — which means the rest of the way belongs to men on foot." He glanced back at Sefa, awake now, watching the ice with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to. "Which means it belongs to you, *guía*. From here down, the sky's finished and the ice takes over."

"I know," Sefa said. "I have been watching it since takeoff." A pause. "It looks different from above. Smaller. That is a lie the distance tells. Do not believe it."

"I never believe anything," Tomás said cheerfully, "except my airplane and my altimeter, and lately I have doubts about the altimeter."

The tension broke the way it does in small rooms full of tired people — not all at once but in seams. Elias went back to his maps, annotating margins in a pencil worn to a stub. Mirena slept, finally, her temple against the window, her breath fogging a small circle on the glass that opened and closed, opened and closed, like something patient breathing. Rafael sat down next to the chest and put one hand flat on the netting, and left it there, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you'd promised to see home.

Tomás flew south.

Below him the foothills rolled away in folds of green and rust, rivers braiding, roads thinning to tracks, tracks thinning to game trails, the world steadily forgetting the shapes men impose on it. Ahead, the Cordillera filled half the sky and all of tomorrow. Behind him, the gray speck dwindled to nothing over a country that had already forgotten it.

He had flown this heading once before, twenty-nine years ago, in a different plane, with a different cargo, running from a fire he'd refused to light. He'd sworn then that he'd never come back — that some debts were paid by staying away. But debts, he had learned, are patient as glaciers. They wait. They advance a few inches a year, invisible, unstoppable, until one morning you wake up and find they've reached the village.

Somewhere under that ice was a chest, and inside the chest was the fire he'd refused to set, banked and waiting thirty years for someone brave enough to carry it the rest of the way.

"Almost there," he told the cabin, though no one had asked. "Two hours to the strip at Punta Fría. Then the walking starts."

No one answered. He hadn't expected them to. But he felt the four of them behind him — the diver, the archivist, the translator, the guide — quiet and alert and bound to him now by altitude and shared danger and the particular intimacy of trusting one aging airplane with all their lives at once, and he thought: *well, old man. You spent thirty years flying alone so you'd owe nothing to anybody.*

Look at you now. Four mouths, one chest, one mountain, and a debt coming due.

*La Golondrina* droned south into the white, and Tomás Iriarte, smiling for the first time in years for reasons he couldn't have explained to a court, flew straight into the teeth of everything he owed.
