## 15. The Town That Forgot Its Name

The town had no name on the map. That was the first thing Mirena noticed, studying Tomás's chart by the light of the Cessna's dome lamp while the foothills scrolled beneath them like something a child had crumpled and then tried, too late, to smooth flat. There was a river fork, a cluster of buildings rendered as a smudge of ink, and beside it a notation in Tomás's cramped hand: *fuel if you know who to ask.*

"No name," she said.

"Never had one that stuck." Tomás didn't look up from his instruments. His hands were steady on the yoke in a way that his hands were not steady anywhere else; she had begun to understand that the air was the only room where the old man felt entirely at home. "The river people called it one thing, the miners another, the army a third. Then the army stopped coming and the miners left and the river changed course a little, and whatever it was called, it stopped being true. Now it's just the last place before the ice. People who live there don't need a name for it. People who don't live there never need to say it."

"Every place needs a name," Mirena said. "Without a name you can't be summoned. You can't be recorded. You can't—" She stopped herself.

"Can't be erased," Tomás finished, not unkindly. "Or can't be *found*. Depends which side of it you're standing on, I suppose."

She turned to the window. Below, the green was giving way to grey, the forest thinning into scree, and the air itself seemed thinner, as though the world were holding its breath before the mountains. Somewhere in the seat behind her, Elias slept with the ledger bag strapped to his chest like a child, and Rafael sat upright and watchful, doing what he always did, which was count things—fuel gauges, cloud bases, the distance between himself and other people.

Mirena had spent eleven years as a translator in the capital, and in eleven years she had learned that names were the whole war. Not territory, not gold, not even the truth itself—names. A man could be shot and buried and it was a crime. A man could be shot and buried and *renamed*—turned into a number, a cargo line, a rounding error in a manifest—and it became policy. Her father had been Prisoner 41 for thirty years. He had been a man for twenty-nine. Somewhere between those two facts, her whole life had happened.

The plane dropped through a layer of cloud and the town appeared all at once, the way things do in mountains: not approached but *revealed*. A bend of grey river. A dozen rooftops of corrugated iron and old timber. A chapel with a steeple like a raised finger. A single dirt street that followed the water and then gave up, dissolving into the track that went, visibly, nowhere—just a brown line climbing toward the white wall of the Cordillera del Viento until the scale defeated it and it became indistinguishable from the mountainside itself.

Tomás brought the plane down on a strip of flattened gravel beside the river, with a nonchalance that Mirena suspected was entirely performance, and killed the engine into a silence so complete she could hear her own pulse.

"Fuel if you know who to ask," she said.

"And lodging if you know who to ask," Tomás said, unbuckling. "And stories, if you ask the right one. There's always one. Every town like this has a keeper. Not the mayor—mayors are for towns with names. The keeper. The old one who stayed when everyone with sense left, and who remembers why."

* * *

The keeper found them before they found her.

They had walked up the single street toward the chapel, drawing the attention of the town the way weather does—faces in doorways, a dog that followed at a distance with the professional detachment of a dog that had judged many strangers and been wrong before. The air smelled of woodsmoke and cold river and, faintly, of the glaciers themselves, a mineral sweetness like the inside of an old church.

The woman was sitting on a bench outside the chapel as though she had been placed there, like the bench, at the founding of the town. She was small and immensely old, wrapped in wool the color of undyed wool, and her face had the particular geography of a life spent at altitude: deep-cut lines, skin like tanned leather, eyes a pale, watery blue that did not seem weak so much as *boiled down*, reduced over decades to their clearest possible concentration.

She looked at Mirena, then at the men behind her, then back at Mirena, and said something in a dialect Mirena half-knew—the river dialect of the foothills, close enough to lowland speech to be followable, far enough to be a lock with a different key.

"You're going up," the old woman said, when Mirena had answered her in careful, formal lowland. It was not a question.

"We're going up," Mirena agreed.

"No one goes up this time of year except the ice."

"We have our reasons."

The old woman's eyes moved to Rafael, to the way he stood—weight on the balls of his feet, hands loose, a man who measured exits. Then to Elias, clutching the bag. Then to Tomás, who had hung back and was looking at the chapel with an expression Mirena couldn't read.

"You have a soldier's way of standing," the old woman said to Rafael. "And a scholar's way of holding that bag," she said to Elias. "And that one," she said to Tomás, "has been here before."

Tomás went very still.

"Thirty years ago," the old woman said. "A plane. It came in low over the chapel in a snowstorm and everyone thought the mountain had thrown it down. But it didn't. It took off again. And a man came into the chapel after, soaked, shaking, and asked for kerosene and silence, and paid for both."

"Grandmother," Tomás said, in the river dialect, and his voice had changed—it had gone old, gone home. "I was the man."

The old woman looked at him for a long moment. "I know," she said. "I've been sitting here every afternoon for thirty years in case you came back to explain yourself."

* * *

Her name was Berta, and she fed them in the chapel's back room, which served as her kitchen, her parlor, and the town's unofficial archive. The room was warm and dim and smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs hanging from the beams, and there was a stove that had been burning, Mirena suspected, continuously since before any of them were born. Berta moved through the small space with the authority of a general, setting out bread and cheese and a stew of something gamey, refusing help, refusing thanks, refusing everything except attention.

It was when she brought out the plates that Mirena saw it.

The plates were old, mismatched, most of them plain tin or cracked ceramic. But the one Berta set in front of Mirena was different—thick porcelain, heavy, with a glaze the color of old milk, and at its rim, in faded gilt, a design Mirena knew better than her own signature. She had spent eleven years in tribunal archives. She had seen that design on document headers, on customs seals, on the letterhead of a government that officially, in the records of three successor states, had never issued a letterhead at all.

A mountain above a river, crossed by a single star.

The seal of Veloria.

Her hand stopped halfway to the bread.

"You know it," Berta said. It was not a question either. The old woman was full of statements shaped like questions and questions shaped like statements, and Mirena was beginning to understand that this was how memory survived in a place like this—in the spaces between the official grammar of things.

"I know it," Mirena said. Her voice came out strange. "Where did you get this plate?"

"Where does anyone get anything? It was left." Berta sat down heavily across from her and folded her hands. "Everything in this town that's old was left. Left by miners, left by soldiers, left by people passing through who didn't come back for it. The town is a pocket, señorita. Things go in. Things don't come out." She tapped the plate's rim with one brown finger, right on the faded star. "Soldiers left this. A long time ago. The winter the column came through."

Mirena heard her own heartbeat. "What column?"

Berta looked at her for a while, the boiled-down blue eyes steady and unblinking, and Mirena had the sense of being weighed, measured, assessed for something—not trustworthiness exactly, but *capacity*. Whether she could hold what was about to be given to her without dropping it.

"You're not from the government," Berta said at last. "Government men ask different questions. They ask what happened *here*. You ask about the plate. You ask like a person who already knows the answer and needs to hear it said out loud." She leaned back. "So I'll say it. And you'll understand that I'm an old woman with a bad memory, and if anyone ever asks, I'll have told you about the weather."

"The weather," Mirena said. "Understood."

Berta told it plainly, the way people tell things they have carried a long time and have worn smooth from handling, the way a river stone is smooth—not because it was always soft, but because it has been turned over so many times.

"It was the last winter of the war. The winter the passes almost never opened. We heard the war the way you hear a storm over the mountains—distant, other people's trouble. Then one morning in deep snow, soldiers came down the track from the south. Not many. A dozen, maybe fifteen. Velorian uniforms, but wrong—winter-white, half of them, and the rest just rags. And they were carrying a box.

"A box heavier than a coffin." She said it the way you say a thing you said to yourself a thousand times, and it had become the only sentence the memory would fit into. "Six men to carry it, and they rested every fifty paces, and they rested long. It wasn't the weight of it, you understand. It was the way they carried it. Coffins, men carry like they're sorry. This they carried like it was angry. Like it could go off. Like if they set it down wrong it would do something to them."

Elias had gone very still across the table. Rafael's eyes had not left Berta's face. Tomás sat with his hands flat on the table, as if holding it down.

"They came to the chapel," Berta went on. "An officer asked for the priest. There was no priest—there hadn't been for a year; he'd gone downriver and not come back. So the officer asked for me, because I was the one who kept the keys. And he asked me a strange question. He asked: *if a man carries the truth over a mountain, and no one is on the other side, is the truth still heavy?*

"And I didn't know what to say. I was twenty. I said something foolish. I said, *truth has no weight, señor.* And he smiled—sad, you know, like a man smiling at a child at a funeral—and he said: *everything has weight. That's what makes it real.*"

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the dog had started up at something and then thought better of it.

"They stayed one night," Berta said. "In the chapel. They wouldn't sleep in the houses—didn't ask, didn't explain. They melted snow for water. They shared out food that was better than ours, army rations, chocolate, real coffee, and they gave the children the chocolate, and the children remembered that for years, the way children do. And in the morning they went up. All of them. Toward the ice."

"And they didn't come back," Rafael said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Two came back." Berta held up two fingers. "In spring. Or what I thought was spring—there was still snow to the eaves. Two soldiers, walking down out of the white, and one of them was carrying the other, or half-carrying; the other one's feet were gone, black to the ankle. Frostbite. The one who could still walk came to my door and he didn't ask for food or a doctor. He asked for the plate." She nodded at the porcelain in front of Mirena. "The officer had left it with me that night, with instructions. *If men come asking about Veloria,* he said, *give them the plate. They'll know what it means.* And then he said: *if no one ever comes, give it to no one. Let it be a plate. Plates are honest. They hold what you put on them and nothing else.*"

"He left it as a sign," Elias said. His voice was rough. "A recognition token. For whoever came after."

"He left it as a *test*," Berta said. "He said the men who come asking will be one of two kinds. The kind that wants to know what happened, and the kind that wants to make sure no one ever finds out. And the second kind," she said, "never asks about the plate. The second kind asks about the weather." She looked around the table, at each of them. "You asked about the plate."

"We asked about the plate," Mirena said.

Berta nodded slowly, and something in her face eased—not softened, exactly, but settled, like a load shifted to a shoulder that could finally carry it. "Thirty years I've kept it washed and turned face-up," she said. "Thirty years, and my neighbors think I'm a foolish old woman with one fancy plate. And maybe I am. But I'll tell you something, señorita, since you're the one who asked." She leaned forward, and the stove light moved in the boiled-down blue of her eyes. "The soldier who came back—the one with the dead feet—before they took him downriver on a mule, he sat where you're sitting, and he told me one thing more. He said the officer made it to the ice. He said the officer was the last of them walking. And he said the officer wasn't carrying the box anymore, because he'd *hidden* it—hidden it somewhere in the high country, somewhere only he knew, and carved the way to find it into the rock, into the ice, somewhere no burning and no minister's order could ever reach it. And then he went up alone to die near it, so no one could be made to talk."

Berta sat back. "He said the truth was on the mountain. He said it was *in* the mountain. He said—" and here the old woman's voice did something Mirena had never heard a voice do, it went young for a moment, thirty years falling away, the twenty-year-old with the keys speaking through the old woman like light through dirty glass— "he said, *one day someone will come who asks about the plate, and you will tell them: it's heavier than a coffin, and it's still up there.*"

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the light was going the color of the glacier ice, that deep, cold, mineral blue, and the white wall of the Cordillera filled the window over Berta's shoulder like a held breath.

Mirena looked down at the plate. At the mountain above the river, crossed by a single star. Her father's number was in the ledger in Elias's bag—Prisoner 41, one line among hundreds, cargo on a manifest. And somewhere above the snowline, in a cave of ice, was the thing that would give him back his name.

"You kept it face-up for thirty years," she said.

"I kept everything face-up for thirty years," Berta said. "That's the whole job. The archives burn, señorita. The ministers sign their papers and the admirals get their statues and the paper burns so clean, so *officially*. But the mountain doesn't burn. And an old woman with a bad memory—" she smiled, and the smile was iron— "an old woman with a bad memory forgets whatever she is told to forget, and nothing else."

* * *

They stayed the night in the chapel's back room, and Mirena did not sleep so much as lie awake in the dark listening to the mountain.

It made sounds. That was the thing no one had told her, or that she had never believed: the Cordillera was not silent. It groaned, it ticked, it boomed somewhere far off in the dark like great doors closing—ice settling, ice calving, the glacier moving in its sleep the way an enormous animal moves. She lay in her bedroll and listened to it and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that the sound she was hearing was the sound of the clock. Every groan was a day coming off the calendar. Every boom was the corridor narrowing.

Rafael was awake too. She could tell by his breathing, and after a while he said, quietly, from his corner: "You should sleep."

"So should you."

"I sleep on boats. There's no boat here." A pause. "She was something, that old woman."

"She was an archive," Mirena said. "A living one. Thirty years, and no one thought to burn her, because no one thought she existed. That's the whole lesson, Rafael. That's what they never understood. You can burn every paper in a country. You can rename every prisoner. But you can't burn a woman on a bench outside a chapel who watched soldiers carry a box heavier than a coffin up a mountain, because she doesn't know she's evidence. She just thinks she's remembering."

In the dark, she heard him consider that. "And Tomás?"

Mirena was quiet a moment. "Tomás paid for kerosene and silence thirty years ago," she said. "I think he's been paying for it ever since. I think that's why he's here."

Another silence, longer. Then Rafael said, "The pass opens soon. Berta's nephew runs mules to the ice line. He says the guides are already talking about the calving—the icefall's moving early this year."

"Early is good?"

"Early is a door opening," Rafael said. "Early is also a door that closes without asking anyone." He shifted, and she heard the particular creak of a man settling in to keep watch rather than to sleep. "Get some rest, Mirena. Tomorrow we ask Berta about the track up, and the day after, we ask the mountain."

She closed her eyes. Below the floor, beneath the chapel, beneath the town with no name, the river went on talking to itself in the dark, carrying everything down toward the sea except the things that mattered, which had gone up.

And high above, through the timber walls, through the cold starlight, the glacier groaned again—a long, deep, patient sound, like something turning over in its sleep. Like something waiting to be asked.
