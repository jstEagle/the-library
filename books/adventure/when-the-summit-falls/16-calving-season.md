## 16. Calving Season

The town had a name once, Elias was almost certain of it, but the sign at its edge had been repainted so many times that the letters beneath showed through like scars—*Vellamora*, then *Puerto Seco*, now something newer and blander that no one used. He stood in the square with his collar up against the wind coming off the mountains, holding a cup of something hot and terrible, and watched the guides argue.

There were four of them gathered around the map Tomás had spread on the back of a cart: three men and a woman, all of them brown as old leather, all of them wearing the particular patience of people who had been asked stupid questions by outsiders their whole lives. The woman was the one who spoke first, and she spoke to Sefa, not to any of them—a quick exchange in a dialect Elias couldn't follow, guttural and clipped, full of consonants that sounded like stones being turned over.

Then the woman looked at the rest of them and said, in careful Spanish, "You want the icefall."

It wasn't a question. Elias felt Mirena go still beside him.

"We want the pass," Rafael said. "However it's done."

"The pass is done through the icefall or not at all." The woman crouched over the map, her finger finding the notch in the range where the glacier spilled down from the high col like a tongue of grey glass. "The Serac Corridor. In summer it is closed—leaning towers, moving every day, nothing alive goes through. In deep winter it is frozen solid, but the snow buries everything, and the wind—" she made a gesture like a knife drawn across the throat. "In calving season, the glacier gives birth. The ice below breaks off and falls away, and for a few weeks the pressure releases, and the seracs lean apart instead of leaning together. A corridor opens." She looked up. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of someone who spent her life looking into glare. "Days. Not weeks. And only if the season is kind."

"How many days?" Mirena asked.

"Last year? Eleven. The year before, six." The woman shrugged. "One year, when I was young, four days, and two climbers went in anyway and we buried what the ice returned."

Elias set down his cup. His hands were steady—he had trained them to be steady through thirty years of handling documents older than nations—but inside him something had begun to tick, faint and insistent, like a clock discovered behind a wall. *Days.* They had crossed half a country on the assumption of weeks.

"And after?" he asked. "If we miss it?"

"After the snows come, nothing passes until spring." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd tell someone the sun sets in the evening. "The mountain does not negotiate. It does not care what you carry or who is chasing you. It opens when it opens and closes when it closes, and the people who argue with it are still up there." She paused. "Some of them. What the mountain keeps, it keeps."

Tomás laughed—not his real laugh, Elias had learned the difference, but the short bark he used to break tension. "Charming place. Remind me why we didn't take the coast road."

"Because the coast road has checkpoints," Mirena said quietly, "and checkpoints have photographs, and photographs have names."

The laughter died. Yes. That was the arithmetic they all carried now, the cold sum beneath everything: Corven somewhere behind them, patient and well-funded, and Admiral Vess above Corven like weather, and the chest's contents burning a hole through every plan like acid through cloth. Elias had spent last night re-wrapping the ledger pages in oilcloth for the fourth time, checking the seals, and had caught himself whispering the names from the manifest like a rosary. *Prisoner 9. Prisoner 17. Prisoner 41.* Somewhere in this town there might be grandchildren of those numbers. Somewhere ahead, in the ice, there was a dead officer who had carried a box heavier than a coffin.

"You'll need a guide," the woman said. "No one crosses alone. Not even the ones who think they can."

"We know," Rafael said. "We've asked. Everyone's refused."

"Not everyone." Something moved behind her face—hesitation, or memory. "There is Sefa Anand. She knows the route better than anyone living. But she will say no."

"Why would she say yes, then?" Tomás asked.

The woman straightened, folding the map's corner with a precise, almost tender gesture, as though the paper were a body to be laid out. "Because sometimes," she said, "people say no their whole lives waiting for a question worth saying yes to."

* * *

They spent the afternoon in the town's single guesthouse, a low stone building whose windows rattled in the wind, and Elias sat by the stove with the ledger open across his knees and tried to do what he had always done: count.

Counting was his discipline and his comfort. Thirty years an archivist, eight years in exile above a bookshop whose owner pretended not to know what Elias kept in the floor safe, and through all of it the same faith—that things could be ordered, numbered, dated, and thereby rescued from chaos. The Velorian archive had been fire because someone understood that faith. Someone had understood that a ledger is not paper; it is a machine for making the past undeniable. Burn the machine and the past becomes negotiable. Elias had built his whole ruined career on the belief that negotiable pasts could be made undeniable again.

Now he counted the wrong things. Days of food. Grams per ration. Hours of daylight, which were shrinking—the light came later and left earlier each morning, the sun dragging itself over the eastern ridge like a man late for his own funeral. Rope. Fuel. The weight of the chest they hadn't found yet, assuming it existed, assuming bronze bindings meant anything, assuming a letter fragment quoted correctly by a dying man thirty years ago pointed to a real object in a real cave rather than a rumor wrapped in grief.

"You're doing the thing," Mirena said, settling onto the bench across from him. She had a chipped enamel mug and steam rising past her face, and in the stove-light her exhaustion looked almost peaceful. "Where your eyes go somewhere I can't follow and your lips move."

"I'm calculating our margin of error."

"What is it?"

"Negative," Elias said.

She smiled, and it was the smile that had undone him a little from the first safehouse onward—not cheerful, never cheerful, but stubborn, a smile worn like armor over the wound of hope. "Good," she said. "I've never trusted positive margins. My father—" she stopped. She always stopped there, at the edge of the sentence, the way you stop at the edge of ice and test it with your weight. "My father taught mathematics. I decided long ago that if I ever learned what happened to him, it wouldn't be through arithmetic."

"It will be through a ledger," Elias said gently. "I'm sorry. It's always ledgers. Bureaucracy is how evil keeps its receipts."

"Then let's collect them."

Outside, the wind changed key. Elias had noticed it that morning—a new note in it, deeper, rhythmic, like something enormous breathing in its sleep. He'd assumed weather. Now, listening, he understood. The glacier was talking. Somewhere above the town, in the cathedral of leaning ice the guide had described, masses the size of buildings were shifting, groaning, settling into the slow convulsion of calving season. The mountain was opening its mouth. And it would close it.

He thought about the officer. He had been thinking about the officer for days now, reconstructing him from fragments the way he'd once reconstructed burned registries from carbon copies and shipping invoices. A Velorian officer, name unknown, war's last winter, carrying a second chest over the Cordillera with a local guide—Sefa Anand's grandfather, if the old woman's stories braided true—and a campsite somewhere in the high ice where the story ended. Elias had spent his exile learning that history was mostly absence: the document not saved, the witness not believed, the grave not marked. But here was a rare inversion. Here the absence itself was evidence. A man had walked into the ice carrying proof, and the proof had not yet been destroyed, and therefore the man had won—for thirty years, without a word, he had been winning.

"All right," Elias said aloud, to the room, to the stove, to the breathing glacier. "We need to leave within two days. Three at most. After that we're gambling with a window measured in single digits, and I don't gamble."

"You gamble constantly," Mirena said. "You just call it archival reconstruction."

"That's informed wagering. There's a difference."

Rafael came in from the yard shaking sleet off his shoulders, and behind him Tomás, and the room filled with cold air and the smell of wet wool and engine oil that clung to Tomás no matter the altitude. Rafael looked at Elias, and Elias saw the question arrive in him before he spoke it—the diver's instinct, reading conditions before committing to the water.

"Well?" Rafael said.

"The corridor opened early this year, according to the guide's cousin, who watches the icefall through a telescope out of boredom. Which means it may also close early. We have perhaps nine days of margin, and I'd prefer not to spend six of them standing in this square being told no by guides."

"Everyone will say no," Tomás said, dropping onto the bench. He said it lightly, but his hand went to his chest in that small unconscious gesture Elias had begun cataloguing without wanting to—the pilot rubbing at a pain he refused to name, the way old men refuse to name pains because naming makes them real. "Everyone always says no. That's why the crossing costs so much. Scarcity pricing."

"Then we find the one who says yes," Mirena said.

"She's at the end of the valley," the guesthouse keeper offered from the doorway, where she had been pretending to sweep for some time. She was a small woman with a face like a walnut and the universal expression of someone who heard everything. "Sefa Anand. Up past the mill. But I wouldn't knock before noon. She shoots first and asks questions of the corpse."

* * *

They went up the valley the next morning, the four of them, along a track that climbed beside a river the color of milk—glacial flour suspended in meltwater, the mountain grinding itself into the sea one particle at a time. Elias walked at the rear, as he always did, cataloguing. The marmots whistling from the scree. The lichen maps on the boulders, orange and black, growing at a rate of a millimeter per century, patient as archives. The light on the icefall itself, visible now whenever the valley bent: a white wall miles high, fissured blue-green, with clouds of spindrift peeling off its crest and the sound reaching them seconds late, flat cracks like artillery fired in another war.

And threaded through it, the breathing. The deep, tidal groan of the glacier giving birth.

Mirena walked beside him for a while without speaking. She had gotten better at silence since the safehouse; or perhaps she had simply stopped performing composure for strangers. When she did speak, it was barely above the wind.

"Elias. If we find it. If it's really there." She watched the path, not him. "What happens to us afterward?"

"Afterward," Elias said, "we become visible. All of us. Permanently. Names in newspapers, names in tribunal filings, names on lists that men like Vess keep. There is no version of this that ends with us handing over the chest and going back to our corners. That version died the moment Rafael lifted the first one out of the river."

"I know. I wanted to hear you say it."

"Why?"

"Because you're the one who counts things." She glanced at him, and there was something raw under the stubbornness. "If you say the cost out loud, then it's real, and if it's real, then I'm not crazy for being afraid of it."

Elias considered that. Thirty years of being the man who made things real by writing them down, and here was a woman asking him to perform the same office on fear. Very well. He was good at offices.

"The cost," he said, "is that none of us will ever be invisible again. The cost is that Vess has already killed for less than what we're carrying, and will kill for more than what we're carrying, and the only protection we have is speed and witnesses. The cost is that the mountain doesn't care about any of it, and the icefall will try to kill us regardless of politics." He paused. "And the cost is that we might fail, in which case the cost is paid by everyone who came after us too, forever, because the window closes and the evidence stays in the dark and the names stay numbers."

Mirena nodded slowly. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've just made us both miserable."

"No." Almost a laugh. "You've made it *countable*. That's different."

Above the mill, the track ended at a stone house with a slate roof and a woodshed stacked with the geometry of a careful mind, and outside it, splitting logs with an economy of motion that made Tomás wince sympathetically, stood a broad-shouldered woman of perhaps fifty with grey shot through her black hair and a face that gave away nothing except to other faces that gave away nothing.

She set down the axe when they were twenty paces out—not casually, Elias noted, but deliberately, within reach.

"Sefa Anand?" Rafael called.

"No."

"We haven't asked anything yet."

"Doesn't matter. The answer's no." She picked up another log, stood it on end. "You're the ones asking about the icefall. Whole town's been gossiping since yesterday. You want me to walk four strangers into the Serac Corridor during calving season, and one of them is sixty years old and one of them flies drunk on stories and one of them—" her eyes flicked to Mirena, then to Elias, cataloguing them the way he catalogued documents—"is carrying something under his coat that he holds like it's a sleeping child. No."

Elias stepped forward. It was, he would reflect later, either the wisest or the most reckless thing he had done since leaving the archive, and he had done both in great quantity.

"Madame Anand," he said. "Your grandfather guided a man across this ice in the winter of the last year of the war. A Velorian officer. Carrying a box."

The axe stopped mid-swing.

"He never spoke of it," Elias continued, keeping his voice level, keeping the archivist's cadence, the tone that made facts land like laid stones. "Not once in the rest of his life. And I know that because I have read the ledger of a ship that carried prisoners who never arrived anywhere, and I know that the box your grandfather helped carry was the answer to that ledger, and I know that your grandfather spent forty years not telling anyone because whatever happened up there broke something in him that never healed. I am not asking you to guide us for money. I am asking whether you want to die knowing as little as he did—or whether you want to walk up there and finally learn what he carried, and why he couldn't say it, and what it cost him."

The wind moved through the valley. Somewhere far above, the glacier exhaled—a long, tectonic sigh, the sound of a door deciding whether to open.

Sefa Anand set the axe down against the chopping block. She looked at each of them in turn, longer at Mirena, longest at Elias, and Elias had the distinct sensation of being read—thoroughly, unsentimentally, the way she probably read weather.

"My grandfather," she said at last, "died with his boots on by the stove and my mother had to bury him in them because he wouldn't let them off. He talked in his sleep his whole life. One word, over and over." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "*Anand*, which is our name, and *peso*, which means weight. Our name and weight. Our name and weight." She picked the axe back up, and Elias's heart dropped—until she leaned it against the wall and turned away from it entirely.

"Come inside," Sefa Anand said. "You'll want tea. And you'll want to hear what the corridor sounds like when it starts closing, because if you flinch at the description, you'll certainly flinch at the reality, and I don't guide people who flinch."

They followed her toward the house. Behind them, above the valley, the icefall groaned again, and this time Elias could have sworn the sound resolved into something almost like language—slow, vast, indifferent, counting down.

The clock had started.
