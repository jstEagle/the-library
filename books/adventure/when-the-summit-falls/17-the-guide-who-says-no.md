## 17. The Guide Who Says No

The town had no name on most maps, and the ones that carried it spelled it wrong. Kestrel's Rest, the cartographers called it—a tidy invention, as if the place had been founded by a bird. In truth it had been a staging camp for mule trains hauling silver down from the high valleys, and when the silver ran out it kept the road and forgot everything else. The buildings leaned into each other like old men sharing a secret. The wind came off the Cordillera del Viento in long, patient gusts that smelled of stone and distance, and it never entirely stopped. You learned to talk with your shoulders hunched and your words short.

Rafael had been in town three days, and in those three days he had learned one thing with absolute certainty: no one would take them up.

He sat now in the back room of the only establishment that served food after dark, a low-ceilinged tavern whose walls were papered with decades of faded climbing photographs—roped teams grinning on summits, seracs the size of cathedrals, faces burned brown by altitude sun. Mirena sat across from him, her hands wrapped around a clay mug of something bitter and hot. Elias was at the bar, doing what Elias did: talking. He had a gift for drawing men out, this disgraced archivist, a way of asking questions that sounded like curiosity rather than interrogation. Rafael watched him work on the tavern keeper, a heavyset man named Bruno with forearms like dock rope and a face weathered into permanent skepticism.

"—and you're telling me there's genuinely no one," Elias said. "Not one guide in this valley."

"I'm telling you what I'm telling you." Bruno wiped the bar with a rag that had outlived several governments. "You want to cross the Viento, you wait for spring like a sensible person."

"We can't wait for spring."

"Then you can't cross." Bruno shrugged, an entire philosophy contained in the gesture. "It's not personal, friend. It's arithmetic. The pass doesn't open for anyone this time of year except during calving, and calving season is a coin toss with a glacier. Every guide who works these routes has buried someone. Ask them why they say no. They'll show you the graves."

Rafael had asked. That was the trouble. He'd spent two days walking the length of the settlement, chest tight under his jacket where the ledger rode against his ribs even here, even now—he couldn't seem to leave it anywhere, couldn't sleep unless he knew its weight was against his heartbeat—and he'd asked every guide, every muleteer, every retired climber drinking tea on their porches. He'd offered money first, then more money, then money that made Mirena wince when she counted it out. The answers had a sameness to them that began to feel liturgical.

*The icefall is unstable in calving season.* Yes. That was the point.

*The corridor through the seracs lasts days, not weeks.* We know. Days are enough.

*People die up there.* People died in Veloria too, he wanted to say. Thousands of them, marched onto river barges with manifests stamped by men who became admirals. But he said nothing, because these people hadn't erased anything; they'd just survived the mountain, which was more than could be said for most.

One guide, an old woman with cataract-clouded eyes, had taken his money, looked at it a long moment, and handed it back folded. "My son is under the icefall," she said. "Nineteen years ago. Calving season. He thought the window would hold." She'd patted his hand once, gently, as if he were the one grieving. "Find another way down the mountain, diver. Or find another mountain."

So here they were, three days in, and the arithmetic was winning.

Mirena spoke quietly, not looking up from her mug. "Maybe we go without a guide."

"No," Rafael and Elias said together.

She looked up then, and there was steel under the exhaustion in her face. "I've read every account of this crossing that exists in any archive, including three that were supposed to be destroyed. I can navigate by bearing and altimeter. I'm not suggesting we wander. I'm suggesting we stop pretending someone local is going to sign their own death warrant for strangers."

"She's not wrong about the last part," Elias admitted. He came over and lowered himself into a chair that groaned under him. He looked older tonight, Rafael thought. The altitude was getting into him, or the fear was. "But going unguided into a calving icefall isn't courage, it's suicide with extra steps."

"Then we need someone who hates the mountain less than they hate saying no," Rafael said. "Or someone with a different reason."

Bruno, passing with a tray, snorted. "There is no different reason. There's money, family, and madness. Money won't do it. Family—all the families here have already given the mountain someone. And madness—" he set down the tray, and for a moment his eyes went distant, somewhere up-valley, somewhere up-altitude, "—madness mostly ends up in the same place as the other two."

Then, because he seemed to feel he'd already said too much, he added: "There's Sefa Anand. But don't waste your time."

"Why not?" Mirena asked.

"Because Sefa Anand says no harder than anyone in this valley. Her grandfather was the best guide this range ever produced—Tomasin Anand, they still argue about routes he cut. And Sefa's better than he was, if anything. Reads ice like other people read weather. But she hasn't guided the high route since her grandfather died, and she turned down a rescue party two winters ago that would've paid her weight in silver." Bruno collected his rag, his verdict delivered. "Sefa Anand says no the way the mountain does. Completely."

* * *

They found her the next morning at the far edge of town, where the built world gave up and the trail began.

She was working on ropes—not coiling them, Rafael saw as they approached, but inspecting them, running each meter through gloved hands with a attention that bordered on devotion, feeling for core shots and sheath damage the way a physician palpates for fracture. She was perhaps forty, compact and weather-dark, with black hair pulled back severely and eyes the flat grey-green of glacial meltwater. She didn't look up when they stopped in front of her. She finished the rope section she was on before she acknowledged them at all, which told Rafael something about her before she said a word.

"You're the divers," she said. "The ones asking about the high route."

Word traveled fast in a town with one street. "We're the ones asking," Rafael admitted.

"Then hear my answer so we can all get on with our mornings. No." She stood, coiling the rope with quick, economical turns. Up close Rafael could see the fine web of lines around her eyes, the particular leanness of someone who burned everything she ate at altitude. "No to the icefall in calving season. No to whatever's in your pack that you keep touching like it might escape. No to the way your friend keeps glancing at the trailhead like it owes him something. No."

Elias stepped forward, and Rafael braced himself, because Elias's method was momentum—he talked people across their own thresholds before they noticed they'd moved. But Sefa Anand held up one gloved hand, and Elias stopped.

"I know who you are," she said to him. Not unkindly. "The archivist. My father read your monograph on the Velorian trade registries before they stripped your credentials. He said you were the only honest man in a dishonest field." Something flickered across her face—there and gone, a door opening a centimeter and closing. "That doesn't change my answer. It just means I'll remember saying it to you."

"Why did you come look us up?" Mirena asked suddenly.

Sefa paused, rope half-coiled.

"You didn't wait for us to find you," Mirena pressed. "Bruno mentioned your name an hour ago. This town has one street. You were already at the trailhead, which means you walked here knowing we'd come. Why?"

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the scrub above the treeline, that dry whispering like paper being sorted somewhere out of sight. Sefa looked at the mountains. Rafael followed her gaze—the Cordillera rising white and blue-shadowed into a sky so hard and clear it looked like it could cut you, the icefall a distant glittering scar between two walls of rock, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful, indifferent the way only mountains and institutions know how to be.

"My grandfather," Sefa said, "guided a man across this range in the last winter of the war. A Velorian officer. Carrying something."

Rafael felt his pulse change. Beside him, Elias had gone very still.

"He came to our house in the night," she went on, her voice level, reciting something she'd clearly rehearsed in silence for years. "Frostbitten, half-starved, carrying a box strapped to his back like it was made of lead. My grandfather took the contract because the officer paid in gold and because in those days we didn't ask questions about men coming over the passes—we were the road everyone used, smugglers and soldiers and priests, all the same to a guide. They went up in the worst month of the winter. Two others went with them. Porters." Her hands had stopped moving on the rope. "Only my grandfather came back."

The wind gusted. Somewhere above, faint as a held breath released, the icefall boomed—an avalanche, or a serac calving, the mountain clearing its throat.

"What happened?" Mirena asked softly.

"That's the thing." Sefa finally turned and looked at them directly, and Rafael saw that her eyes weren't flat at all; they were full of something held down hard, the way a river holds down its current beneath ice. "He never said. Forty years guiding this range, and he never once spoke of that crossing. Not to my grandmother, not to my father, not to me when I begged him—I was young, I wanted stories, and he gave me every story he had except that one. He taught me the routes, the reading of ice, all of it. And the one thing he wouldn't give me was the winter he went up with the Velorian and didn't come down whole." She looked back at the mountain. "He died with it. Whatever happened up there, whatever that box was, whoever those porters were—it went into the ground with him, and I have spent my whole life walking past the place where it's buried."

Rafael reached slowly into his jacket. He didn't break eye contact with her. He drew out the ledger—wrapped now in oilcloth, tied with cord—and held it between them.

"Your grandfather's officer," he said, "carried documents proving what Veloria did. What the men who run three countries now did, when they were younger and hungrier. Prisoners transported upriver who never arrived anywhere. Names. Dates. Manifests. We're taking the proof of it to people who will make it matter." He turned the ledger slightly, and the morning light caught the corroded stamp on its cover: the seal of a nation that officially had never existed. "This is what was in the box. Or the brother of it. There's a second chest, up there, hidden by the officer himself. Your grandfather may be the last person alive—or almost alive—who knew where."

Sefa stared at the ledger for a long time. When she reached out and touched the oilcloth, her glove trembled—just once, just barely, controlled immediately, but Rafael had spent years reading small movements underwater, where a life could depend on noticing a tremor, and he saw it.

"He never spoke of it," she said again, quieter now. "And I hated him a little for that, when I was young. Then I grew up and understood he wasn't keeping a secret. He was keeping a wound." Her jaw tightened. "You're asking me to open it."

"I'm asking you to finish what he started," Rafael said. "Whatever that is. If it's getting a chest down off the mountain—if it's just standing where he stood and finally knowing—I'm asking you to walk up there and let the mountain tell you what he couldn't."

Sefa Anand was silent for a long time. Below them the town smoked gently into the cold morning; above them the icefall glittered, patient as arithmetic. She looked at the ledger, at Elias, at Mirena—who had put a hand unconsciously over her own heart, over the name she'd carried thirty years—and last at Rafael, and whatever she found in his face seemed to settle something.

"The calving window opens in six days," she said at last. "Maybe seven, if the winds stay northerly. After that, the snows close the pass until spring, and no power on earth gets you across." She began, deliberately, to coil the rope again, her hands steady now. "I'll take you up. Not for money." She glanced at the ledger. "For him. For the porters. For whatever he carried down that mountain besides his body." She tied off the coil and slung it over her shoulder. "Meet me at the trailhead at first light, day after tomorrow. Bring less than you think you need and more water than seems reasonable. And understand this—" her voice hardened, guide's voice, mountain voice, "—once we're on the ice, my word is the only law. If I say drop it, you drop it. If I say run, you run. If I say leave me, you climb on. Swear it now or don't come."

"We swear," Mirena said, without hesitation, and Rafael heard in her voice the same thing he felt in his chest: that after years of invisibility, of rivers and archives and false names, they had just been seen by someone who mattered, and had passed some test he hadn't known he was taking.

Sefa nodded once, curtly, and turned back to her ropes. Then, as they turned to go, she spoke again, not looking up.

"Diver."

Rafael paused.

"The night my grandfather came home from that crossing, my grandmother said he sat by the fire until dawn holding his hands in front of him like they belonged to someone else. And once, near the end, when the fever had him, he said a name over and over." She finally looked up, and the held-down thing in her eyes was closer to the surface now. "He called it *the box that remembers*. I used to think it was fever-talk." She shouldered her rope. "Now I think he was trying to tell us the truth the only way left to him. Whatever's up there in that ice—it remembers. And so, apparently, do we all."

She walked away toward the gear shed, and Rafael stood watching her go, the ledger heavy against his ribs, the mountain enormous and silent above them both.

Behind them, faint but distinct, the icefall groaned again—long, deep, almost conversational, as if something frozen for thirty years had heard its name spoken at last, and was beginning, very slowly, to answer.
