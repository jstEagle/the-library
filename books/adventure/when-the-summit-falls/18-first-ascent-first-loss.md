## 18. First Ascent, First Loss

The mountain did not care that they had come for justice.

Sefa Anand understood this the way she understood her own heartbeat—not as a lesson learned but as a truth absorbed before language, in the years when her grandfather carried her up these slopes on his back and spoke to the ice as though it were a difficult relative. *It does not hate you,* he would say. *It does not love you. It only is.* She had been six years old and furious at the unfairness of it, and she was forty-one now and still furious, but the fury had changed shape. It had become a kind of fuel.

They left the last settlement at first light, five of them roped into a single argumentative line: Rafael Sterling with his diver's shoulders and his habit of counting things under his breath; Elias Brandt, who moved like a man apologizing to the ground for touching it; Mirena Kovač, whose breath came in short disciplined pulls because she refused to be the slowest; Tomás Iriarte, sixty-eight years old and lying about it; and Sefa herself, carrying the rope's weight and everyone else's lives in the coil at her hip.

Behind them, the glacier's calving booms rolled up the valley like distant artillery—*whump, whump*—the sound of ice giving birth to itself, of the corridor opening that would close again within days. Ahead, the icefall waited: a frozen riverfall of seracs leaning against each other like drunks holding each other upright, blue-green in the shadowed crevices, white-hot where the sun struck.

"Beautiful," Elias said, and then, quieter, "and completely insane."

"Yes," Sefa agreed. "Both are true. Keep both."

She had told them the rules the night before, over tea that tasted of smoke and glacial flour: they would move at dawn only, when the cold still held the ice together; they would not speak on the ice except to call danger; they would trust the rope more than their own judgment, because their own judgment was exactly what killed people here. And they would leave caches—food, fuel, rope—at every camp, so that the mountain could not take from them twice.

"Why caches?" Mirena had asked. "We're coming back this way."

"We hope," Sefa said. "The mountain doesn't care what we hope."

Now, three hours into the morning, the icefall rose around them like the inside of some drowned cathedral. The seracs were the size of buildings, some of them larger, tilted at angles that made the eye water and the stomach clench. Between them ran corridors of packed snow and blue ice, and through those corridors the wind funneled with a low continuous moan, like the mountain breathing through its teeth.

Sefa called the rest stops. Every fifty minutes, four minutes, no negotiation. At the second stop she found Tomás sitting apart, his elbows on his knees, his face the color of tallow.

"Pilot," she said. "Breathe."

"I am breathing." His voice had a wheeze threaded through it that hadn't been there yesterday.

"Show me your hands."

He showed her. They were steady enough. But when she pressed two fingers to his wrist, his pulse stumbled like a man walking downstairs in the dark—quick, quick, catch, pause. She looked up at him and he looked back with the flat defiance of a man who has been told bad news by doctors before and has decided, on balance, to treat the news as a rumor.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since I was fifty-five." He shrugged, which cost him something; she saw it in his eyes. "The heart does what it does. I take my pills. I fly anyway."

"You didn't tell me."

"You didn't ask if any of us were dying, guide. You asked if we could climb." He managed half a grin. "I can climb. I've climbed out of worse places than this with less air than this."

"That wasn't climbing. That was falling with style."

That got a real laugh out of him, brief and rusty, and he stood, and she let him stand, because the alternative was turning the whole expedition around, and she watched his face as he took the first steps and judged—correctly, barely—that he could continue. But she moved him to the middle of the rope, ahead of Rafael, where the strongest arms could reach him fastest, and she said nothing to the others, and hated herself a little for the silence, and knew it was the right silence anyway. For now.

* * *

They made the first cache site by early afternoon—a sheltered hollow beneath a serac the size of a chapel, where an old crevasse bridge had healed into a shelf of solid blue. Sefa dug into the slope with her shovel while the others ferried loads, and they built the cache the way her grandfather had taught her: supplies sealed in stuff sacks, buried chest-deep, marked with two wands crossed and tied with red flagging, the location fixed against three landmarks and written down in Elias's careful archivist hand, because Elias insisted on writing everything down, in triplicate, as though paper could argue with a glacier.

"It can't," Sefa told him, watching him copy coordinates a third time.

"No," Elias admitted. "But it's the only weapon I have. I've spent thirty years learning what happens when nobody writes anything down." He tucked the notebook inside his jacket, against his body, where it would stay warm and dry. "So I write."

They marked the route as they climbed—wands every hundred meters through the icefall's winding corridors, bamboo poles with flags that snapped in the wind like small desperate birds. By late afternoon they had gained eight hundred meters and emerged onto the broad shoulder of snow below the upper seracs, and Sefa allowed herself ten minutes of looking back.

Below them the icefall sprawled like a shattered staircase, and beyond it the foothills faded into haze, and beyond that, invisible now, the world they had left—the river, the wreck, the tribunal, the men with guns. From here it all looked very small. Problems always did, from altitude. That was the trap of mountains: they made human affairs look manageable, right up until the moment they reminded you that you were made of meat and breath.

Tomás stood beside her, hands on hips, breathing carefully.

"Your grandfather," he said. "The one who guided the Velorian officer. He came up this way?"

"Higher. Much higher, in winter, which no one should ever do." Sefa nodded at the route above. "And he never said what happened after. Forty years I knew him. Not one word about the box or the officer or why he stopped guiding foreigners after that winter." She paused. "Until the end. At the end he talked a little. Enough for me to know there's something up there worth finding, and enough to know it cost him something he never got back."

Tomás was quiet a long moment. "Everyone up here is carrying something," he said finally. "The mountain just makes you feel the weight."

Then his face went grey.

It happened fast. One moment he was standing, squinting into the wind; the next his hand was at his chest and his knees buckled, and Sefa caught his harness as he folded, and shouted the word that needed no translation in any language on any mountain: *"Rope!"*

Rafael was there in seconds—he'd been watching Tomás all day too, Sefa realized later; the diver counted everything, including breaths—and together they lowered him gently against a pack, and Sefa knelt and got the pill bottle from his inner pocket, and put the nitroglycerin tablet under his tongue while Mirena held his hand and Elias hovered with the useless, terrible energy of a man who cannot fix anything.

"Chest," Tomás managed. "Like a... a hand squeezing."

"Breathe slow. Small sips." Sefa kept her voice level, the voice she used with frightened clients, the voice her grandfather had used with her. Underneath it her own heart was slamming. "Look at me. Just me. Slow."

The spasm passed in minutes that felt like hours. Tomás lay against the pack, grey going slowly back toward pink, and grinned up at them with terrible cheerfulness. "Well. Now you all know. Sorry about the drama."

"You could have died," Mirena said. Her voice cracked on it.

"People do," Tomás said. "Better up here than in some bed with tubes. Better doing something." He looked around at their faces, at the four of them knelt and standing around him in the wind, and something in his expression softened into something Sefa recognized and had no name for—gratitude, maybe, or the particular peace of a man who has finally stopped running. "You should know I'd have done this even with a good heart. Whatever's in that chest—it matters. I know better than most how much it matters."

Elias went very still. "What do you mean, you know?"

But Tomás only closed his eyes. "Later," he said. "When we're down. When it's done. I'll tell you all a story about fire, and a young soldier who was given an order." He opened his eyes and looked at Elias. "You'll want to hear it, archivist. It's about what gets burned and what doesn't."

The wind gusted, hard, and Sefa looked west and felt her stomach drop.

The weather was coming in off the range like a wall—cloud boiling up over the western summits, lenticular caps stacking themselves above the peaks like plates, the light going strange and bruised. She had grown up reading these skies. This was not an afternoon squall. This was the front her grandfather had called *the white hammer*, and it would arrive within the hour, and they were eight hundred meters above their cache with a sick man and no shelter.

"Storm," she said, and the word ended every other conversation. "We bivouac. Now. Dig in before it hits or we dig graves."

* * *

They cut a platform into the lee of a serac wall and built the bivouac the old way: snow blocks quarried and stacked into a knee-high windbreak, tents pitched and immediately buried to the poles, packs arranged as buttresses. Sefa worked them like a drill crew, and they responded—Rafael cutting blocks with the tireless rhythm of a man who had hauled salvage for years, Mirena shoveling with grim fury, Elias doing the slower work of organizing gear inside the tent so that everything they might need in the night was within arm's reach of someone's sleeping bag.

The storm hit at dusk like a door slamming.

The wind came down the glacier in pulses that hit the fabric like fists, and the temperature fell so fast that the snow squeaked underfoot when Sefa stepped outside to check the anchors. Inside the two-man tent they crowded four—shoulders touching, boots in faces, the stove hissing between them—and Tomás lay propped against Rafael's pack with his feet elevated and his color holding, barely.

By full dark the storm had found its voice. It screamed. It was not wind anymore but a living thing hurling itself against the nylon, and the tent bowed and cracked and recovered, bowed and cracked and recovered, and Sefa lay listening to it and doing the arithmetic she hated: rations, fuel, time, the window closing, the hunters behind them, the sick man beside her.

"Talk," she said into the dark. "Quiet talk. It keeps the fear small."

So they talked. Mirena told them about her father—not the number, the man she imagined, the voice she invented for him over thirty years of inventing. Elias told them about the archive, the smell of burning paper he could still summon at will, the exact weight of the ledgers he had failed to save. Rafael, pressed against the tent wall, said almost nothing, but when Mirena asked him directly why a man lives alone on a river, he was silent so long she thought he wouldn't answer, and then he said, "Because water doesn't pretend to be anything. People do. I got tired of pretending," and the storm swallowed whatever came after.

Tomás slept in fits. Twice in the night Sefa woke to check his pulse and found him awake too, staring at the tent roof, and the second time he whispered, "Guide. If it goes bad—if I go bad—you take them up. Don't turn around for me. Promise."

"I don't make promises I intend to break," Sefa said. "Sleep."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get."

He laughed softly, and then coughed, and then was quiet, and the storm hammered on, indifferent, magnificent, utterly without malice or mercy, and Sefa lay in the dark listening to five hearts beat in a space the size of a coffin lid and thought about her grandfather's words: *it does not hate you, it does not love you, it only is.*

The mountain would take what it took. All any of them could decide was what they carried, and for whom, and whether they turned around.

* * *

Dawn came grey and savage, the storm still blowing but diminished, the sky scoured to a hard metallic light. Sefa crawled out into a world remade—drifts waist-deep where the path had been, their wands snapped or buried, the whole landscape rewritten overnight. Somewhere below, their first cache sat under a meter of new snow, findable only by Elias's triplicated notes and Sefa's memory.

They lost the morning digging out and re-marking. By noon the wind had dropped enough to move, and Sefa made the decision she had been turning over all night: they would push for the high camp today, plant the second cache, and put themselves in position for the icefall corridor at tomorrow's dawn. Slower groups turned back. Faster groups died. Her grandfather's rule, applied to a team with a failing heart and hunters behind them: steady, steady, steady.

They climbed. Tomás climbed. He climbed badly, stopping every twenty minutes now instead of fifty, leaning on his axe, breathing in the careful measured way of a man rationing himself, and each time Sefa checked his pulse it stumbled a little worse, quick-quick-catch-pause, like a clock running down.

At the high camp—a col between two rock ribs where the wind funneled but the snow lay deep enough to dig proper platforms—they built the second cache and Tomás sat on a pack and watched them work, and when Sefa glanced over he was looking at the view south with an expression she couldn't read.

"My grandfather flew this route once," he said, apropos of nothing. "Before I was born. Cargo run over the range in bad weather. He said the mountains looked like the spine of something sleeping." He patted the snow beside him. "Sit, guide. Two minutes. The work will still be there."

She sat. Below them the clouds had broken, and the world fell away in layers—glacier, moraine, foothills, haze—and somewhere down in all that haze were men with rifles following their trail, and somewhere beyond that a capital full of admirals who wanted them erased, and somewhere above them, behind the icefall, a bronze-bound chest waiting thirty years in the cold for someone to keep its promise.

"Heavy," Tomás said, following her gaze upward. "Whatever he carried. The officer. My grandfather said the men who came down after that winter were never the same." He shook his head slowly. "Mountains keep what's given to them. That's the thing nobody tells you. Everyone thinks the mountain takes. It doesn't. It keeps."

Sefa looked at him. His face was calm, and his hands in his lap were trembling very slightly, and she understood suddenly that he knew—knew in the way sick men know—exactly what his body was doing and exactly what it was costing, and had known since the first grey moment yesterday, and had chosen to keep climbing anyway.

"Pilot," she said quietly. "How bad?"

He smiled at her, and it was the kindest, saddest smile she had seen in years. "Bad enough that I'm glad I'm here instead of anywhere else. Bad enough that I need you to promise me something different now." He leaned closer, and the wind tore his words and she had to lean in to catch them. "If my heart quits on the ice—in the corridor, in the open—I want to be left facing the mountain. Not turned away. Facing it. Can you give me that?"

"I can give you my best effort," Sefa said. "Nothing more."

"From you," Tomás said, "that's plenty."

* * *

They moved at dawn the next morning into the corridor itself, and Sefa learned what terror transfigured looks like from the inside.

The icefall above the high camp was worse than anything below—a cathedral of leaning seracs, some the height of church towers, groaning and ticking as the warming day loosened their joints. The calving booms came more frequently now, deep concussions rolling through the ice beneath their boots, and between the towers the route wound through corridors of blue shadow where the light came down green and submarine and the silence between gusts was so complete Sefa could hear her own blood.

They moved in absolute silence, as she had ordered. Rope tight. Steps placed, not stamped. Fifty meters apart at the wand-marked turns, each person alone with the ice and their own heartbeat.

Halfway through the worst section, the mountain spoke.

It began as a crack—a single sharp report like a rifle shot, high and to the left, followed by a lower groan that rose in pitch until Sefa's fillings ached. She froze, fist raised, and the whole column froze behind her, five figures stilled against the blue, and above them a serac the size of a house leaned a degree further out of true and shed a sheet of powder that hissed down the face like smoke.

Nobody breathed. Nobody moved. The groan peaked, hung, and died away, and the serac settled back into its ancient lean, and Sefa exhaled and moved them on, faster now, her eyes flicking constantly upward, reading the ice the way her grandfather had taught her to read it—as a text, as a threat, as a text again.

They cleared the corridor by mid-morning and came out onto the broad glacier plateau beyond, and the relief was so physical that Mirena actually laughed—a short wild bark of a laugh—and Elias sat down right where he stood in the snow, and Rafael stood looking back the way they'd come with an expression Sefa couldn't parse, and Tomás—

Tomás was on his knees in the snow.

Not resting. On his knees, one hand pressed flat to his chest, the other braced on his axe, his head bowed, and when Sefa reached him he looked up at her and his face was the grey of old ice and his lips were already going blue.

"Sefa—" he said, and his voice was a thread. "Don't—"

She caught him as he folded. The pills were in his pocket, she got one under his tongue with hands that were steadier than she deserved, and Rafael was there, and Mirena, and they laid him flat on a pad with his feet up and the wind came across the plateau and found them all.

For an hour they fought for him. Oxygen from the emergency bottle. Rubbing warmth into his limbs. The nitro, then more nitro, until the bottle rattled empty. Sefa monitored his pulse and watched it degrade by degrees—quick-quick-catch-pause becoming quick-catch-longer-pause—and Tomás drifted in and out, and once, surfacing, he found Elias's face above him and gripped the archivist's sleeve with surprising strength.

"The story," Tomás rasped. "About the fire. About the soldier." A wet, rattling breath. "Ask me. Ask me now."

"Later," Elias said, and his voice broke clean in half on the word. "When you're stronger. You'll tell it yourself, at the tribunal, in a warm room—"

"Elias." Tomás smiled, and it was the smile from the col, the kind and terrible one. "There isn't a later. There's just this." His grip tightened. "Thirty years ago I was given an order to burn a building full of paper. Ledgers. Photographs. Names." Another breath, longer, harder won. "I burned the building. Empty. I'd emptied it first—for weeks, little by little, crates at a time, into a friend's truck, into the hills. Then I lit the fire they ordered, and I walked away from the army, and I've been flying cargo and running from that night ever since." His eyes found Sefa's. "The chest you're looking for—the officer who carried it over the pass—my friend's truck drove him to the trailhead. I know where he entered the mountains, because I drove him there myself. I'm not just your pilot. I'm the last link in the chain."

The plateau was silent except for the wind. Mirena had both hands over her mouth. Rafael stood like a man carved from salvage iron, and tears ran down his weathered face without his seeming to notice them.

"The ledger," Tomás whispered. "Check the manifests. The driver's name. It's mine. It was always mine." His breath caught, held, released. "I didn't come for redemption. Too late for that. I came to finish the delivery."

His eyes were closing. Sefa pressed fingers to his throat and felt the stumble become a stutter become a long, arrhythmic staggering, and she did everything her training knew, and Rafael did everything his stubbornness knew, and none of it mattered against the arithmetic of a heart that had been running on will and nitroglycerin for thirteen years.

Near the end he surfaced one last time, and his gaze went past all of them, up-slope, toward the icefall and the heights beyond, and he smiled.

"There it is," he said. "The spine of something sleeping." A breath. "Tell my grandfather's ghost I finally flew the route."

And then the mountain, which does not hate and does not love and only is, took the weight of him gently, the way it keeps everything, and Tomás Iriarte was gone.

* * *

They buried him that afternoon, as well as anyone can bury a man in ice.

There was no digging deep enough; the permafrost laughed at shovels. So they built him a cairn at the edge of the plateau, where the view south opened over the whole layered world—glacier, foothills, haze, home—and they stacked the stones with the same care they'd used on the caches, and Elias wrote his name on a strip of flagging tape in his careful archivist hand, and Mirena read aloud, in a voice that shook and then steadied, the few facts of him they knew: pilot, smuggler, soldier, keeper of secrets, deliverer of names.

Rafael set Tomás's compass on top of the cairn, needle swinging free, pointing nowhere and everywhere.

"He said to face the mountain," Sefa said, when the wind allowed speech. "So we built him facing it."

They stood in a row before the stones, five ropes' worth of grief now reduced to four, and the wind came off the summit fields and streamed their flags and said nothing, promised nothing, kept nothing but what was given to it.

Sefa looked up the route, at the icefall looming above, at the pass beyond it opening for days that were now fewer, and she thought about the men with rifles in the foothills, and the window closing, and the chest waiting, and the promise she had made—or half-made—to a dying man on a col.

"We move at dawn," she said. "All of us. We finish the delivery."

And far below, faint on the wind, carried up from the foothills like a rumor, came the sound none of them wanted to hear: engines. Many engines, moving fast, closing on the mountain.
