## 19. Corven Follows

The radio woke him before the cold did.

Rafael had been sleeping in the shallow lean-to Sefa called a *garañón*—a windbreak of stacked stones and woven brush that looked like nothing at all until you crawled behind it and the wind simply stopped existing. He'd learned to sleep that way in the navy, curled against anything solid, one ear open. Three years of salvage work had refined the skill into something closer to instinct: the body of a man who expected the world to interrupt him.

It was the hiss of static that did it. Not the alarm on the set itself, but the particular quality of the static—layered, rhythmic, carrying the ghost of a voice inside it like a body carried inside ice.

He lay still for a moment in the blue pre-dawn, listening to his own breath fog and vanish, and told himself it was nothing. Foothill stations bleeding into each other. A trucker somewhere on the valley road. The mountains were full of voices at this hour; the cold carried them for miles.

Then he heard the word *Iriarte*.

He was out of the sleeping bag and across the stones before the rest of him had finished waking, bare hands finding the radio's dials in the dark, tuning with the slow, greedy precision of a man cracking a safe. The frequency was one Tomás used—a smuggler's channel, old and half-forgotten, supposedly swept clean. The voice inside the static was speaking Spanish with a coastal accent, the flat, unhurried delivery of a man reading a grocery list.

*—three of them, possibly four. The pilot is Iriarte, ex-air force, grounded twice for falsified manifests. The others unknown. Payment as agreed, half on confirmation of contact, half on recovery of the materials. No bodies required unless they force the issue.*

A pause. A different voice, farther from the transmitter, indistinct. Then the first voice again:

*Understood. We know the range. We'll be on them before the icefall. Corven out.*

Rafael sat with the handset in his hand long after the channel dissolved back into pure static, the sound of it like sand pouring through a broken hourglass.

*Corven out.*

He'd known, of course. Some part of him had known since the boat was searched in the harbor, since the photographs of his dive logs, since the second offer with its careful mention of Elias's bookshop and Mirena's visa. Men like Corven did not make offers twice. They made them once, and then they made arrangements.

But knowing a thing and hearing it were different animals. Knowing was a stone you could carry. Hearing was the stone dropping into your stomach and staying there, cold as river gravel.

*We'll be on them before the icefall.*

He crawled back into the lean-to and lay staring at the underside of the brush roof, where frost had formed in feathers along the twigs, and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do.

Corven would have hired trackers. Ex-military—the voice had the texture of a man who'd given orders in rooms with maps in them, and the phrase *we know the range* had not been bravado. It had been a résumé. Which meant men who could move at altitude, who carried radios that didn't crackle, who had probably been paid enough that the mountain itself was a line item rather than a threat. Days behind, yes. But better equipped. Better fed. Moving with purpose while Rafael's crew moved with hope, and hope was a lighter load but a slower one.

He got up and put the kettle on the stove, because the hands needed something to do, and because in a few hours he was going to have to say out loud the thing he'd heard, and he wanted to have rehearsed it against the ordinary sound of water heating, the ordinary ritual of morning, the ordinary lie that today would be like yesterday.

The frost on the brush roof began to melt as the sky lightened, dripping onto the stones in a rhythm too regular for rain. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Like footsteps, he thought, and then made himself stop thinking it.

It was going to be a long climb.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, when the peaks to the east went from grey to rose to a white so clean it hurt to look at directly. Sefa was already packed—she slept less than any person Rafael had ever met, and he suspected she slept less than she admitted—and she watched him with those flat, patient eyes as he stowed the radio.

"You heard something," she said. Not a question. She'd seen his face.

He gave it to them straight, around the stove, while the tea went cold in their hands. Corven. Trackers. Ex-military. Days behind. The channel had been swept, Tomás insisted—*swept, I've used it eleven years, nobody listens*—and Rafael didn't argue the point, because arguing it would mean admitting that eleven years of habit had just become a liability, and Tomás's habits were the last thing the old man had left to be proud of.

"So they know we're on the mountain," Mirena said. She had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, against her chest, the way she carried it everywhere now, and her hand went to it without her seeming to notice. "They know the route."

"They know the *pilot*," Sefa corrected. "The route they'll have to guess. There are four ways onto the glacier from the south and only one of them is sane. If they're soldiers, they'll take the sane one." She said it without pride, the way she said everything, as though the mountain were simply a set of facts she was reading aloud. "Which means they'll be below us on the moraine by tomorrow night. Maybe the day after."

"Can we outrun them?" Elias asked.

Rafael looked at him. The old archivist had lost weight on the trail; his collarbones stood out above his sweater like the frame of a boat under construction, and his hands, always moving when he talked, were still now, folded around his tin cup as if around the last warm thing in the world.

"Not on foot," Rafael said. "Not carrying what we're carrying."

"Then we don't outrun them." Elias said it mildly, the way he said everything that terrified him. "We outlast them. Or we outthink them. Those are the only two verbs left."

Tomás laughed from where he sat against his pack, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on a beat too long, and everyone pretended it hadn't. "Outlast," he said when he could. "I like that. I'm seventy-one years old, I've been outlasting things since before any of you were born. The air force. The customs men. Two wives." He grinned at Mirena. "The mountain doesn't scare me. Mountains are honest. It's the men with radios you want to watch."

Rafael didn't say what he was thinking, which was that Tomás's heart had stuttered twice since the bivouac in the storm, and that outlasting required a body willing to cooperate, and that the arithmetic of days behind and better equipped did not care about anyone's pride or anyone's history.

He said instead: "We move fast today. We make the high moraine before dark, and from there we see the icefall, and Sefa tells us what the calving season is doing." He looked at her. "How many days do we have?"

She considered the peaks the way she considered everything—without hurry, without flattery. "The window was seven days when we started. We've used two. If the weather holds." She glanced at the west, where a lens of cloud was building over the far ridgeline, thin and high and white as a scraped bone. "The weather may not hold."

* * *

They climbed.

Rafael had spent his life underwater, and he had thought he understood silence—the deep, pressurized hush below the thermocline, where your own heartbeat was the loudest thing in the world and your exhaled bubbles rose like a string of silver accusations. But the high moraine had a different silence, a silence with texture and teeth. The glacier groaned somewhere below them, a sound like a door in a house too large to have doors, and the sound carried for miles across the rock and came back changed, so that you could never be sure which groans were the ice and which were echoes of groans, and which were neither.

By midday he was hearing footsteps.

He knew they weren't footsteps. He knew the scree fields up here shed stones all day as the sun loosened the frost that had glued them overnight; he knew the ptarmigan exploded from the rocks in whirring brown hearts; he knew the wind moved through the boulder fields and made them tick and knock like furniture in an empty house. He knew all of it, and still, every hour or so, a sound would come from behind them—below, on the route they'd climbed—and his whole body would stop, mid-stride, mid-breath, a puppet with its strings cut, and he would turn and stare down the grey fall of rock and see nothing, nothing, nothing, and the nothing would be worse than a figure would have been.

A figure you could count. A figure you could plan around. The nothing could be anything.

"You keep doing that," Mirena said, at the midday halt. She wasn't unkind about it. She'd been watching him the way she watched everything—like a translator, cataloguing, converting.

"Doing what."

"Stopping. Turning. Like a man checking whether the river is rising." She handed him a strip of dried meat and sat down beside him on the boulder, her back to the view, facing the way they'd come. He noticed that. She'd chosen the seat that faced the threat. "My mother did that. After my father was taken. Every knock at the door, every car on the street, every footstep on the stairs—she'd stop, just like you. Mid-motion. Like the world had a pause button and someone else was holding it." She chewed, looking out at the scree. "I used to think she was a coward. I was a child; I didn't have a better word. Now I think she was the bravest person I ever knew, because she kept living in a house where every sound was a question, and she never once stopped answering the door."

"Did they ever come?"

"Once. Two men, plain clothes, very polite. They asked about my father's associates. She served them coffee." Mirena's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "She said later it was the hardest thing she ever did, pouring that coffee with a steady hand. That the hand wanted to shake and she made it not shake, because if it shook, they'd know she had something to hide, and what she had to hide was her whole heart." She stood, brushing off her trousers. "Pour the coffee, Rafael. Keep the hand steady. That's all any of this is."

He wanted to believe it was that simple. He watched her walk back to her pack, small and straight-spined against the immensity of the moraine, and he thought: *she carries her father in oilcloth against her chest and she pours the coffee.* And he thought: *Corven's men are two days behind us and better equipped.* And both thoughts were true at once, and the mountain did not care, and the coffee, metaphorically, still had to be poured.

They climbed on.

* * *

The storm Sefa had seen building over the western ridge arrived at dusk—not as a storm at first, but as a change in the light, the long alpenglow on the peaks going from rose to a bruised copper, and the temperature falling so fast that the snow under their boots squeaked like new leather. They made the high moraine as the first flakes came down, fat and slow, each one landing with an almost audible weight, and Sefa called the halt with a jerk of her chin at a hollow beneath a house-sized boulder where the wind, for once, could be argued with.

Rafael set the stove in the lee of the stones and got water heating, and while it heated he did something he hadn't done since the harbor: he turned on the radio.

Not the smuggler's channel. He swept. Slowly, band by band, the way he'd once swept a sonar screen over a wreck, looking for the anomaly, the shape that didn't belong. Most of the dial was static and the far-off music of valley stations, thin and warped by distance. Then, low in the band, a carrier wave—strong, disciplined, no music, no voice. Just a tone, repeating, the kind of beacon a coordinated team uses to find itself in broken country.

He marked the frequency. He didn't need a direction finder to know what the strength of the signal meant.

"They're on the mountain," he said.

The others looked up. The stove hissed. Somewhere below them, out in the falling dark, the glacier groaned its door-in-an-empty-house groan, and this time nobody mistook it for anything else.

"Tonight?" Elias asked.

"Close enough. That signal's strong. They've got a basecamp on the lower moraine, maybe the tree line. They'll move at first light, same as us." He turned the radio off, and the silence it left was enormous. "They don't need to catch us. That's the thing I keep turning over. They don't need to fight us at all. They just need to follow us to the chest, and take it at the top, when we're tired and committed and there's no way down but through them." He looked around the circle of faces lit orange by the stove: Elias with his boat-rib collarbones, Mirena with her hand flat against her jacket, Tomás propped against his pack with his eyes half-closed and his breath a little too shallow, Sefa watching the snow come down as though reading it. "We're not the prize. We're the guides. We're going to lead them straight to the thing they want, and thank us for it."

"Then we don't lead them straight," Sefa said.

"They're ex-military trackers on home ground. We can't shake them on this terrain."

"No." She shook her head once, a small precise motion. "But the icefall can." She pointed with her chin toward the head of the valley, where the glacier rose in its long white staircase, and above it, invisible now in the dark and the snow, the seracs waited—the leaning towers of ice that calving season had spent weeks destabilizing, that groaned and shifted and fell, that opened a corridor for a few days and then erased it. "No tracker follows anyone into the icefall. Not a good one. The good ones know better. We go in at dawn, in the quiet, when it's coldest and the ice is most asleep. We move fast and we don't stop and we don't make noise." She looked at Rafael, and for the first time since he'd known her there was something in her face that wasn't flat—something that might have been fear, worn the way she wore everything, close to the skin. "And we trust the mountain to be more frightening than the men behind us."

Tomás opened one eye. "I've been frightened by both," he said. "Give me the men. Men you can shoot."

Nobody laughed. It was the kind of joke that was true, and true jokes about the thing you were about to do sit in the air like the smell of a storm.

Rafael took first watch, because he wanted it—wanted the cold and the dark and the job of looking. He sat at the mouth of the hollow with the rifle across his knees he'd carried for three weeks and never fired, and watched the snow fall through the beam of his headlamp, and listened.

The mountain gave him everything it had. The tick of cooling rock. The whisper of snow on snow. The long groan of the glacier, nearer now, patient, enormous. And underneath it all, at the very floor of his hearing, the thing he could not rule out and could not confirm: a rhythm in the static of the world, regular as a bootfall, coming up the dark.

He knew it was the wind. He knew it was the scree. He knew it was his own pulse, translated.

He watched until his relief came, and then he lay down behind the stones with the ledger's oilcloth rustle audible every time Mirena turned in her sleep, and he did not close his eyes for a long time, and when he finally did, he dreamed of the river—of the wreck, of the sealed compartment, of the moment his hand first touched the corroded lid—and in the dream, behind him, in the black water, he heard footsteps.

They broke camp at first light, into the teeth of the icefall.

The seracs rose above them like the organ pipes of a drowned cathedral, blue-green and groaning, leaning over the route the way a crowd leans over a tightrope. Sefa went first. Rafael counted his breaths and kept his hand steady, and did not look back.

But he listened. All the way up, he listened.
