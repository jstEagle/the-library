## 20. The Icefall Corridor

Sefa Anand woke before the alarm and lay listening to the glacier breathe.

It was a sound she had known since she was six years old, when her father first brought her up the moraine in the blue hour before dawn and told her to close her eyes. *What do you hear?* And she had said, *nothing,* because that was what children said, and he had laughed—not unkindly—and said, *no. Everything. The mountain is never silent. It only speaks slowly.* She had spent forty years since learning its vocabulary: the tick of ice contracting in the cold, the long subterranean groan like a door opening in a house with no rooms, the sudden crack-and-boom of a serac letting go somewhere out on the face, rolling through the valley like thunder that had lost its way home.

This morning the glacier was speaking constantly. This morning it was almost conversational.

Calving season. Three days early by her reckoning, which meant three days less than they had planned for, which meant everything they did from this hour forward would be done against a clock that did not care about them.

She crawled out of the tent into air so cold it felt solid, a cold you could lean against. The sky above the col was the color of a blade—pale steel at the horizon deepening to something harder overhead, no clouds yet but the promise of them stacked behind the western peaks like a debt waiting to be called. Frost had grown on the tent fabric overnight in feathers and ferns, and when she brushed it away it fell as glittering dust that hung in the stillness a full second before dying.

Below her, in the basin where the icefall began, the route waited.

She had seen the corridor form twice before in her life, both times with her grandfather, both times from a distance neither of them had any business surviving. It happened only in the weeks when the great icefall above—the hanging tongue of the glacier where it poured over the shoulder of the mountain—shed weight faster than it could hold itself. The calving opened fissures along the stress lines, and the fissures drained, and the drainage carved, and for a handful of days the ice stood apart from itself in a leaning cathedral of seracs, towers the size of buildings tilted at angles no architect would sign his name to, braced against one another in a mutual agreement to fall that could be revoked at any hour, by any tremor, by nothing at all.

Her grandfather had called it *la catedral*. He had also called it *la boca del diablo*, the devil's mouth, and Sefa had learned young that the two names were not contradictory. Beautiful things and deadly things were, in the high country, usually the same things wearing different light.

She looked at it now and felt the old fear rise in her—not the animal fear of falling or freezing, which she had made peace with decades ago, but the other kind, the fear that lived in her chest beside her grandfather's silence. Because somewhere inside that cathedral of ice, thirty years ago, a Velorian officer had walked with a box heavier than a coffin, guided by a man who came down the mountain afterward and never spoke again of what he had seen. Her grandfather. Her blood. His guilt, handed to her like an inheritance nobody would have chosen, sitting in her all these years like a splinter too deep to dig out.

And now here she was, about to walk the same route, carrying strangers who carried papers, following a dead man's carvings toward a cave that might not exist, while men with rifles came up the valley behind them.

*The mountain doesn't care about your reasons,* her grandfather used to say. *It will kill you for a wrong step taken for the best cause in the world, and it will let a murderer pass if he steps true. That is its mercy and its cruelty, and they are the same thing.*

She broke the ice off the water pot, filled the stove, and began waking the others.

* * *

They ate without speaking, the way she had told them to. Not because words were forbidden—she wasn't a priestess of the ice, whatever Tomás's jokes implied—but because mouths full of cold oatmeal and voices still rough with sleep tended to say stupid things, and stupid things spoken at altitude had a way of becoming decisions.

Rafael ate like a man who had learned hunger underwater: steadily, without fuss, eyes moving. Elias ate like a man reading, which was remarkable given that he held no book; his attention was always somewhere in the ledger, turning some notation over, and Sefa suspected half his calories went to fueling thought rather than muscle. Mirena ate carefully, completely, finishing everything in her bowl and then holding the warm tin in both hands as though it were an object worth studying. And Tomás—

Tomás sat propped against his pack, gray around the mouth, breathing in little careful sips of air, and ate perhaps a third of what he needed.

She had watched him all yesterday on the approach. The heart. He hid it well, better than most men his age would bother to, but she had guided enough failing bodies up mountains to know the signs: the pauses disguised as admiring the view, the way he saved his breath on the flats, the faint bluish cast his lips took on at anything above a stroll. Last night in the bivouac he'd sat with his hand pressed flat against his sternum like a man holding a door shut.

He caught her looking. He winked.

*Old smuggler,* she thought. *You'll be the death of my patience before you're the death of yourself.* But she said nothing, because he had made her promise nothing, and because there was nothing to say that the mountain hadn't already heard.

"Route talk," she said instead, and unfolded the map on her knee, weighting the corners with stones. "We move at first light. Not before—I want sun on the upper icefall so we can see what's shedding. We go roped, four lengths between us. Single file, my line, no one steps anywhere I haven't stepped. If I raise a fist, everyone stops and stands still. Not crouches, not looks around—stands *still*. Ice reads vibration. You are guests in a house that hates noise."

"And if it starts coming down?" Rafael asked. Not fearfully. Practically. She was beginning to understand that about him—he metabolized fear into procedure, the way some men metabolized it into prayer.

"You run," she said. "Not away from the sound. Perpendicular to it. The ice falls in lanes. You want to be between the lanes, not under them." She tapped the map. "Two hours through the corridor, maybe three if it's shifted overnight. Then the moraine beyond, and camp below the officer's bearing line. Questions?"

"How do you know it hasn't shifted?" Mirena asked quietly.

"I don't." Sefa met her eyes. "Nobody knows. That's the price of the ticket. The corridor exists because the ice is falling apart. We're walking through a building while it demolishes itself, and the demolition crew works on its own schedule." She rolled the map. "One more thing. In there, nobody talks. Not whispers, not prayers, not jokes. Your breath is loud enough. If you need to say something, touch the rope twice."

Elias raised his hand like a schoolboy, and despite everything Safa felt the corner of her mouth twitch. "What if we need to say something longer than two tugs?"

"Then it can wait until you're out," she said, "or it isn't important enough to die for."

Tomás laughed—a short, wheezing laugh that turned into a cough he smothered in his sleeve, and Sefa saw Rafael's jaw tighten, and saw Tomás see Rafael see it, and saw the old man pat the younger man's arm once, twice, like quieting a horse.

"Lead on, guide," Tomás rasped. "I've flown through worse cathedrals than this one."

"No, you haven't," Sefa said, and shouldered her pack.

* * *

First light came up the valley like water filling a bowl, and the icefall caught it and burned.

Sefa had seen it a hundred times and it stopped her breath every time: the moment the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the seracs, and the whole cathedral lit from within, blue fire and green shadow, ten thousand facets throwing light at each other in a conversation older than any language. The towers leaned. God, how they leaned—some of them past forty degrees, hanging over the route like doors left open in a wind, buttressed by their neighbors, sculpted by meltwater into flutes and organ pipes and arches that belonged in a dream someone was having too fast.

*La catedral.* Her grandfather had been right about the name. Standing at its threshold, Sefa felt the word settle over her like snow.

She clipped into the front of the rope and stepped onto the ice.

The world changed immediately. Underfoot the glacier was not the white of postcards but a pale, bruised turquoise, ancient and dense, shot through with veins of darker blue where meltwater had frozen in its own channels. Crampons bit with a sound like teeth. The air tasted of stone and cold and something mineral-sweet, the smell of ice that had been compressing snowfall since before anyone alive was born.

Behind her, four sets of crampons found their rhythm. Good. They had listened. Even Tomás moved quietly, economizing, placing his feet with a pilot's precision—as if the mountain were a runway he intended to land on rather than crash into.

An hour in, the walls rose.

The corridor narrowed and the seracs closed overhead, and suddenly they were walking through architecture: vaulted passages of ice fifty meters tall, transverse crevasses bridged by snow that glowed from beneath with a light like drowned lanterns. Sefa moved through it the way she imagined a diver must move through a wreck—Rafael had described it to her once, on the approach, in one of his rare fluent moments: *you learn the hull's grammar, where it groans means strain and where it sings means current*—and she understood now that ice had the same grammar. Groan was strain. Sing was current. Crack was a sentence ending.

A crack came then, sharp as a rifle shot, somewhere high on their left.

Her fist went up. Four bodies froze. She counted—one, two, three, five—listening past her own heartbeat, past the wind, into the structure of the thing above them. A secondary groan followed, long and descending, the sound of mass settling, finding a new argument with gravity and losing it slightly slower than before. Then silence.

She dropped her fist. Moved. Behind her, boots resumed.

Twice more in the next hour the mountain spoke and they stopped, statues on a rope, breath rising in silver columns that hung in the windless cold like offerings. Once the crack was close—very close, directly overhead, a serac the size of a chapel tilting by degrees she could almost see—and she felt the whole rope go rigid, felt Elias trembling through the line, not with panic but with the effort of absolute stillness, a man holding himself together by will alone. And then the tower settled back into its lean, groaning like an old man into a chair, and gave them passage.

Mirena touched her shoulder at the next rest halt and pointed up.

Sefa followed her finger and forgot, for a moment, every rule about keeping moving.

Light was falling through a gap between two seracs—a single shaft, narrow as a church window, striking the corridor floor and turning the ice there into a pillar of living blue, glowing from within like something alive, like a heart, like the lantern her grandmother had kept in the village shrine. Snow motes drifted through the beam, unhurried, each one burning briefly gold before passing back into shadow. It fell across the route exactly where they had to walk, and Sefa understood that they would pass through the light, all of them, one after another, and that there was no way to describe this to anyone who had not stood in it.

Mirena walked into the shaft and stopped there, just for a moment, her face tipped up, frost in her eyelashes, lit so completely that she seemed made of the same substance as the ice around her—translucent, ancient, enduring. Then she moved on, and Rafael passed through after her, and Elias, and Tomás last, and Sefa watched each of them burn gold for one stride and return to ordinary flesh, and thought: *this is why my grandfather came down the mountain silent. Not because of what he saw happen here. Because of this. Because how do you speak of a place that kills you and shows you heaven in the same hour?*

Terror and reverence, she realized, were not opposites. They were the same nerve, struck at different depths. The cathedral demanded both of you at once—your fear and your awe—and refused to let you keep only one.

* * *

They were ninety minutes through, near the corridor's far throat where the seracs leaned closest, when the glacier decided to remind them whose house this was.

There was no warning crack this time. There was a *shift*—a deep, structural exhalation that Sefa felt through her boots before she heard it, the whole icefall drawing a breath, and then above and behind them, with a sound like a city clearing its throat, a serac calved.

She didn't look back. Looking back was for people with time. She threw her fist up and then swept it sideways—*move, perpendicular, NOW*—and hauled the rope leftward toward the corridor wall, toward a rib of older ice that jutted like the prow of a ship, and behind her she heard the others' crampons scrabble and scrape as they ran her line, and the roar built and built, and the light in the corridor went strange, dimming, going green, the way light does when a mountain of ice puts its shoulder in front of the sun.

The serac hit the corridor floor two hundred meters behind them.

The shockwave came through the ice like a heartbeat, one massive pulse that lifted them on their toes. Powder snow exploded down the corridor in a white ghost-wave, blinding, deafening, filling the air with cold smoke, and Sefa pressed herself and her rope-team against the ice rib and pulled her hood tight and waited, counting, counting, while the world roared and the glacier settled its argument somewhere behind them in a long rumbling cascade of secondary collapses, like furniture falling downstairs in an empty house.

Then quiet. The powder drifted. Light returned, pale and shaken.

She turned. Counted heads. Rafael, already counting too, his eyes meeting hers with a question she answered with a nod. Elias, white-faced, glasses fogged, gripping the rope with both hands like a man holding onto a decade. Mirena, powder-white, wide-eyed—and steady. Steadier than Elias. Steadier, maybe, than Rafael. There was something in her face Sefa recognized from mirrors: a person who had already lost the thing they most feared losing, and discovered they were still standing.

Tomás last. Tomás, leaning on the rope, breathing hard, too hard, his lips distinctly blue now even in the flat light, one hand pressed flat against his chest.

Their eyes met. He straightened, deliberately, theatrically, and gave her a thumbs-up, and grinned with what little breath he had, and Sefa thought: *stubborn old man, you will lie to us with your body all the way to the top of this mountain if we let you.* She made a note. Tonight, at camp, she would check him properly, whether he liked it or not. Some debts to the dead you paid forward by keeping the living alive.

"Two tugs," she said aloud, breaking her own rule, because some things mattered more than silence. "Everyone. Two tugs means stop. From now until we're through, we don't stop for views, we don't stop for fear, we don't stop for anything except my fist. Thirty minutes to the exit. Move."

They moved.

And the cathedral, having spoken, seemed satisfied—or indifferent, which amounted to the same thing—and let them pass. The seracs leaned and groaned and shed small pieces of themselves in ticking whispers, and the light came down in shafts and blades, and the five of them went through it single file on a rope, small as stitches, sewing themselves across the white silence of the mountain.

At the corridor's mouth, where the icefall gave way at last to the long moraine beyond, Sefa stopped and turned and looked back once—the only look she allowed herself.

The cathedral stood in the morning light, blue and green and impossibly beautiful, leaning towers and singing arches, the whole impossible architecture of it gleaming like the inside of a jewel. Somewhere in there, thirty years ago, her grandfather had walked with a dying man and a box heavier than a coffin. Somewhere in there, minutes ago, a hundred tons of ice had come down where they would have been if they had been twenty minutes slower, or twenty minutes faster, or simply unlucky.

*Thank you,* she said to it, silently, in the language of her childhood. *For letting us through. For showing us the light.*

The glacier groaned, long and low, and did not answer, and did not need to.

She turned up the moraine toward the bearing line, toward the officer's carvings, toward the cave that might hold the truth that powerful men had killed to bury—and behind her, far down the valley, thin as a needle's thread in the clear cold air, she heard it:

A sound that did not belong to the mountain.

Voices. Distant, faint, carried up the wind channel of the corridor they had just left.

Someone else was in the cathedral.
