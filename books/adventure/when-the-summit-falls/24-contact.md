## 24. Contact

The moraine came out of the glacier's flank like a spine—a long ridge of gray stone and shattered ice, deposited over centuries by a glacier that had been rearranging the mountain since before anyone thought to name it. Sefa called it the Old Man's Backbone. She said it with a kind of grudging respect, the way you'd speak of a dangerous animal that had never once bothered you.

We walked it at midday because she said midday was safest. The sun had been up long enough to soften the frost that glued the scree together in the dark hours, but not so long that meltwater was running freely beneath the stones. There was a window, she told us, an hour maybe, when the Backbone held still.

"Everything on this mountain holds still for one hour a day," Tomás wheezed beside me. "The rest of the time it's deciding whether to kill you."

He was worse. He'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere above the second camp, and now he carried his breath in short, rationed sips, like a man counting coins he knew were running out. But his eyes were still sharp, and when we crested the first rise of the moraine and he looked back down the valley we'd climbed out of, I saw him go very still.

"What is it?" Mirena asked.

"Birds," Tomás said.

There were no birds. That was the point. We'd seen choughs all morning, black commas against the snow, wheeling and complaining above the icefall. Now there was nothing. The sky behind us was empty in the particular way a sky gets empty when something large has passed through it and everything small has decided to be elsewhere.

Sefa had already dropped to a crouch. She didn't say anything. She just pointed—down-valley, past the shoulder of the icefall, to where the glacier's lateral moraine bent around a spur of dark rock. And there they were.

Five figures. Maybe six. They moved the way soldiers move, not the way climbers move—too upright, too deliberate, spaced like beads on a string. They wore white, but it was the wrong white, the clean unpatched white of equipment bought for a single operation rather than earned over years. Even at this distance I could see the glint of optics.

Corven hadn't turned back. Corven had followed us onto the ice.

* * *

"Down," Sefa breathed, and we went down, all five of us flattening against the leeward side of a boulder the size of a truck cab, our packs still on, our breath steaming in the thin cold. The wind here funneled along the moraine's crest, and it covered sound—our voices, their footsteps, whatever either side might have heard otherwise. It also meant we couldn't hear them coming.

Elias had gone gray. Not from altitude—from recognition. "That's military movement," he whispered. "Not contractors. Contractors walk like men who want to go home."

"You can tell that from here?" Mirena said.

"I spent eleven years cataloguing after-action reports." His mouth twisted. "You learn what men look like when they've done this before."

I put my binoculars up over the top of the rock, slow, slow, just enough glass to see through. Six of them. Ropes coiled at their shoulders, crampons on their boots, and two of them carrying rifles slung across their backs—not hunting rifles, not the battered bolt-guns the valley guides used for ibex. These were short, black, and modern, and my stomach dropped through the glacier when I saw them.

"They're not trying to catch us," I said. "They're trying to end us."

Nobody answered that, because nobody needed to. Vess's order had traveled up the mountain faster than we had. *The mountain can be blamed.* A fall into a crevasse. A night caught outside a storm. Bodies found in spring, or not found at all, and a report filed in triplicate saying six climbers had attempted the pass against advice. The mountain takes its toll. Everyone knows the risks.

It would even be mostly true.

"We split?" Tomás asked. It wasn't cowardice; it was arithmetic. One group of five makes one target. Two groups make two problems, and trackers hate problems.

"No," Sefa said flatly. "The route ahead doesn't allow it. Past the Backbone there's a traverse—" she pointed east, along the glacier's flank, to where the ice steepened into a wall of blue-green seracs leaning over a slope of loose rock, "—and you don't run that divided. You run it together or you don't run it at all."

"How far?" I asked.

"Two kilometers. Maybe three. If the slope holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

She gave me a look that said the question answered itself.

Mirena had the chest strapped inside her pack, wrapped in the last of our foam padding, and her hands were resting on it the way you'd rest your hands on a sleeping child. Elias crouched beside her, and I watched him do something I'd seen him do only once before, in the safehouse: take off his glasses, breathe on them, polish them with a handkerchief that had once been white, and put them back on. A ritual. A man putting on his armor, such as it was.

"The documents survive us," he said quietly, to no one in particular. "Whatever happens on this slope, the documents survive us. Four copies. Four bodies. Any one of us reaching the far side is enough."

"That's a comforting thought," Tomás muttered.

"It's not meant to comfort," Elias said. "It's meant to focus."

Below us, the trackers had stopped. One of them raised binoculars. Even through my own glass I could see the sweep of the lenses as they quartered the moraine ahead—their route, our route, the same road with different intentions.

Then the lead figure raised an arm, and the whole line pivoted toward us like a compass needle finding north.

They'd seen something. A flash of color, a footprint, a shape against the skyline—I'd never know which. It didn't matter. What mattered was that the hour of stillness was over, on both the mountain and the moraine, and everything on it was moving now.

"Run," Sefa said.

* * *

We ran the way you run at four thousand meters, which is to say badly, in a stumbling half-trot that burned the lungs and accomplished less than it felt like. The Backbone rose and fell under our feet, a chaos of boulders and ice-glued gravel, and every stride was a negotiation between speed and survival. Behind us, faint under the wind, I heard the crack-crack-crack of boots on stone, quickening.

Tomás fell twice in the first hundred meters. The second time, Mirena hauled him up by the arm and kept hold of him, and they ran linked together like that, her smaller frame braced against his stagger, neither of them saying a word about it.

The rifle shot, when it came, was smaller than I expected. A flat snap, almost polite, swallowed instantly by the wind. Then the echo came back off the icefall a half-second later, doubled and distorted, and the mind did its slow work of understanding what the ears already knew.

The bullet hit stone somewhere to our left. A puff of gray dust jumped off a boulder.

"Down!" Sefa screamed, and we dropped behind a ridge of moraine gravel no higher than our knees, which would stop shrapnel and nothing else, and lay there gasping while the second shot cracked overhead and went whining away down-valley like a struck tuning fork.

"They're shooting to herd," Sefa said into the gravel. Her face was inches from mine, and I could see the fury in it, cold and precise. "Watch. They'll push us left, toward the icefall side. Open ground. Where they can see us."

She was right. The third and fourth shots came in a tight pair, both landing right of our position, walking us leftward with the patient geometry of men who had done this to other men before. Corven's people weren't marksmen taking shots; they were sheepdogs with bullets.

And the ground to our left was exactly where Sefa had said not to go.

"There," I said, pointing right instead—east, downslope, toward the traverse she'd warned me about. The unstable slope. The one that doesn't hold if it doesn't hold.

Her jaw tightened. For a moment I thought she'd refuse—that the mountain was hers, that her rules were rules, and I was another client telling the guide how to guide. Then she looked down-valley at the six white figures closing in a spread line, rifles level, and something in her face shifted. Not surrender. Recalculation. The arithmetic of a woman who knew every death this mountain offered and was choosing the least worst one.

"If we go down there, we go down there *light*," she said. "Everything that isn't rope, food, shelter, or the chest stays. Now."

We shed the mountain.

It took ninety seconds, maybe less, and I will carry those ninety seconds longer than anything else that happened that day. Tomás unbuckled his spare rope coil and let it slide down the scree without watching it go. Mirena pulled out her spare gloves, her repair kit, the little tin of stove fuel—and then, after a breath, the folded photographs of her father that she'd carried since the capital, and I saw her hesitate over them, and then thrust them deep into an inner pocket instead, against her skin, where no weight but blood could make her leave them.

Elias stood holding his field kit—notebooks, forty years of marginalia, the reference volumes he'd lugged across a border and through an exile—and I watched him understand, in real time, that he could not carry them down that slope.

He set them down gently. All of them. He squared the stack against a stone so the wind wouldn't take it, the way you'd tidy a desk, and then he took off his glasses, polished them, put them back on, and said, "Right."

I left my dive knife. Twenty years on my belt, through three rivers and a hundred wrecks. It seemed suddenly like an artifact from another man's life, and perhaps that's what it was.

"Go," Sefa said.

* * *

The traverse was worse than she'd described, and she had described it honestly.

It began as scree—loose slate-gray rock lying at its own angle of repose, the angle at which stone stops being ground and becomes a landslide waiting for permission. Each step sank half a boot-length and sent a hissing trickle of pebbles downslope, and the trickle became a stream, and the stream became a low continuous roar, the sound of several tons of mountain quietly relocating itself beneath our feet.

Above us leaned the seracs. From below, on the glacier, they'd looked like architecture—towers and buttresses of compressed ice, blue-veined, impossibly graceful. From inside the traverse, at their base, they looked like what they were: a hundred thousand tons of frozen water held up by friction and habit, groaning in the afternoon heat, shedding shards that rang off the rocks around us like broken bells.

Behind us, the shooting had stopped. That frightened me more than the shooting. Sheepdogs who've lost the flock stop barking and start running.

"Spacing!" Sefa called. "Ten meters! Nobody bunches!"

We strung out along the slope, five dark beads on a gray thread, and the mountain tested us the whole way. Halfway across, a serac calved above us—not a big one, a chunk the size of a car—and it came down in a long bouncing arc, hitting the slope fifty meters behind Elias, detonating into fragments that sprayed past him with a sound like applause. He didn't flinch. He didn't even turn around. He just kept placing his feet, one after another, a man crossing a room full of sleeping dogs.

Then Tomás's legs went.

I heard it before I saw it—a cry, short and involuntary, cut off fast—and turned to see him sliding. Not falling; sliding, on his back, arms wide, plowing a furrow through the scree toward the edge where the slope steepened into nothing. Mirena lunged for him and missed. I threw myself flat, got one hand on his wrist, and the world contracted to that grip—to the burn in my shoulder, the grind of my boots seeking purchase, the terrible patience of gravity.

His weight pulled me a meter downslope. Two. My ribs scraped stone. Somewhere above me Sefa was shouting and I couldn't parse the words, and then her ice axe came down in front of my face, driven into the scree like a tent peg, and she looped the sling around my forearm and threw her whole body backward against it.

We stopped.

For a moment none of us moved. Tomás hung below me with his boots over air, breathing in raw wet pulls, and I hung above him with his pulse hammering through his wrist into my palm, and the mountain hissed and settled around us like a house shifting on its foundations.

"On your feet," Sefa gritted. "Both of you. Before it decides again."

We got him up. It took all three of us and cost us two minutes we didn't have, and when I looked back along the traverse, the way we'd come was marked by a trail of abandoned gear scattered down the slope like the debris of a wreck—ropes, a tent fly, a fuel bottle spinning end over end into the void, catching light as it fell.

Everything comfortable. Everything replaceable. Everything that made the cold merely unpleasant instead of lethal.

Gone.

* * *

We came off the traverse at dusk, dropping over a rib of solid bedrock onto the blue ice of the upper glacier, and Sefa made us keep moving another twenty minutes before she let us stop—into a hollow between two pressure ridges, walled on three sides, invisible from the moraine unless someone stood directly above it.

Only then did we count ourselves.

Five. All five. Mirena had the chest. Elias had the ledger pages, waterproofed, strapped against his back. Sefa had her rope, her axe, her stove. Tomás sat against the ice with his head tipped back and his eyes closed, and his breathing had a whistle in it that hadn't been there that morning.

And I took inventory of what remained, because someone had to, and because the numbers were the only prayer I knew.

One tent, minus its fly. One stove. Food for eight days, stretched thin across five mouths, and that was before the altitude tax. Three sleeping bags for five people. No spare rope. No radio—Tomás's set had gone down the slope in the first minute, and with it any way of knowing whether Corven's team was behind us, beside us, or already ahead.

No way home except forward.

Mirena passed around the water bottle, each of us taking measured sips, and nobody spoke about the gear scattered across half a mountainside. You don't mourn out loud on a glacier. You mourn with your hands—checking buckles, re-packing straps, touching the things that are left to confirm they're real.

"He'll come at night," Sefa said finally. "Or dawn. Men like that don't rest well with unfinished work."

"Then we don't rest at all," I said.

Tomás opened one eye. "Speak for yourself. Some of us have hearts with opinions."

Despite everything, Mirena laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. And I understood, sitting in that ice hollow with one tent and eight days of food and six armed men somewhere in the dark behind us, that this was what the mountain had been doing to us since the pass opened. Stripping us. Taking the boat, the shop, the archive, the gear, the comfortable weights, until what remained was exactly this: five people who would not put each other down.

Elias broke a ration bar into five pieces with great precision, as though the proportions mattered, and perhaps to him they did.

"We should know by morning," he said, "whether they crossed the traverse behind us."

"Why?" Mirena asked.

"Because if they did," he said, polishing his glasses one more time in the failing light, "they'll have found my notebooks. And Corven will know precisely how much we know—which means he'll stop trying to catch us, and start trying to bury us. And the storm front I've been watching build over the western peaks all day will give him his excuse."

I looked west. Above the seracs, above the shoulder of the range, the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the wind had begun to carry that dry mineral smell that every climber learns to dread—the smell of weather being born.

Sefa followed my gaze, and her face, in the last of the light, said everything her voice didn't.

The corridor was starting to close.
