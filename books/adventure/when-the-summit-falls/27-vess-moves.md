## 27. Vess Moves

The Admiralty had been built to outlast the republic that commissioned it, and it had. Its granite blocks were quarried from a mountainside that no longer appeared on any map under its old name; its corridors were laid out on a grid so rational that a man could walk them for forty years and never once be surprised. Admiral Edvard Vess had walked them for thirty-one of those years. He knew every seam in the marble, every portrait's blind spot, every door that opened silently and every one that announced him. He had long ago stopped noticing any of it, the way a fish stops noticing water, and this—this absolute absence of friction between himself and the architecture of power—was the closest thing he had ever felt to happiness.

He arrived at his office at 5:40 each morning, before the building woke, because the hour before dawn was the only time the capital belonged to him alone. He would stand at the window with his coffee going lukewarm and watch the river take the first light, and he would think about order.

Not justice. Not legacy. Order. The distinction mattered, though he had never needed to explain it aloud to anyone. Justice was a sentiment, and sentiments were weather—they blew through, they soaked everything, and then they passed and left the world exactly as crooked as they'd found it. Order was masonry. Order was what you built when you understood that the alternative was not freedom but flood.

On this particular morning, the flood had a name, and the name was Sterling.

* * *

Vess did not read reports the way other men did. Other men read for information. Vess read for shape—the silhouette a problem cast against the future—and he had learned, over three decades of quiet work, that most problems could be identified by their shape long before they could be named. A lieutenant's indiscretion had the shape of a crack in plaster: cosmetic, containable, painted over and forgotten. A journalist's inquiry had the shape of damp: persistent, spreading along seams you couldn't see, best handled by finding the source and cutting it out whole.

The file on his desk had the shape of a foundation fissure.

It was thin—Corven's people worked clean—but thinness meant nothing. Fourteen pages. A salvage diver named Rafael Sterling, licensed, unremarkable, three years on the same stretch of the Ravel with a boat older than he was. Photographs of dive logs. A transcript of a harbor registry inquiry. A surveillance still of a bookshop above which lived a disgraced archivist whose name Vess had personally struck from the civil rolls eleven years ago, back when striking names was still done with a signature instead of a keystroke. And a third photograph, grainy, taken through glass: a woman at a café table, mid-gesture, her face turned toward someone outside the frame. Mirena Kovač. Tribunal translation staff. Daughter of Prisoner 41.

Vess set the photographs down in a row, squared their edges, and looked at them the way he had once looked at charts of minefields.

Thirty years. That was the number that occupied him—not fear, nothing so warm as fear, but arithmetic. Thirty years since Veloria had been dissolved in a conference room in Geneva by men who shook hands for the cameras and divided the corpse by committee. Thirty years since the archives burned—or were reported to have burned, which in practical terms amounted to the same thing, because a fire only needs to be believed in. Thirty years during which the ledger of what had actually happened on those prison barges had been reduced, page by page, name by name, to ash and rumor, until the whole apparatus of it—the transports, the manifests, the fourteen hundred souls who stepped off the gangplanks of history and into no record whatsoever—had been absorbed into the official silence of three successor governments like a body absorbed into a riverbed.

He had not done it alone. That was the thing Corven's generation never understood, the thing that made them crude where he was precise. They imagined evil as an act, something you did, some night when you signed your name in blood. But Vess had never signed anything in blood. He had signed requisition forms. Transport authorizations. Fuel allocations. A commendation, once, for a quartermaster who had kept exemplary records right up until the records stopped being kept. The horror had been accomplished in ballpoint ink, in triplicate, by men who went home afterward to their families and slept soundly because the forms had all been countersigned. Evil was not a deed. It was a procedure, and procedures survived because no single man could be blamed for a machine.

And now a diver with a crowbar had pulled a piece of the machine out of the river.

* * *

At six-fifteen, his aide brought in the second coffee and the morning signals folder, and Vess dismissed him with a nod and did not open the folder. There was a discipline to this. The signals could wait; they were always waiting; the machinery of the fleet turned whether or not Edvard Vess watched it turn. What could not wait was thinking, and thinking required the particular quality of silence that existed only in this room before the staff arrived—a silence with weight, almost with texture, broken only by the tick of the ship's chronometer on the mantel, a relic from a flagship scrapped before Vess had made captain.

He considered the diver first, because divers were simple. A man who lived alone on a boat, who measured his life in bottom-time, was a man with few attachments and therefore few levers—but also few vulnerabilities, which was the trouble. Men with nothing could be bought cheaply or frightened easily, but Sterling had already refused money. Corven's report noted it without editorializing: *Subject declined initial approach. Subject lied about contents recovered.* The lie was the interesting part. An honest man who found documents turned them in, or sold them, or panicked and burned them. A man who lied had decided the documents were worth protecting, and a man who had decided that was no longer operating in the market of incentives. He was operating in the market of conviction, and Vess had spent thirty years learning that convictions were the one currency the Admiralty could not print.

Then the archivist. Brandt. Vess remembered the dismissal hearing—he attended, informally, from the gallery, because he made it a practice to attend the small funerals personally. A meticulous man, Brandt. Dangerous precisely because he was meticulous, because he had spent his career inside the notation systems of the old Velorian ministries and could read their ledgers the way priests read scripture. When Brandt had begun asking questions about gaps in the transferred holdings—gaps Vess himself had authorized, years earlier, in a memo whose language had been so blandly administrative that it might have concerned office furniture—the solution had been obvious and gentle: ruin him quietly, pension him invisibly, and let exile do the rest. Exile was efficient. Exile made a man's voice small and his audience smaller.

But exile had not made Brandt stupid, and now he was sitting in a border-town safehouse with a salvaged ledger and, if Corven's intercepts were accurate, two accomplices and a theory.

And the theory was the third photograph. Kovač.

Vess picked up her image again and studied it longer than he had studied the others. She was the variable that didn't fit the pattern, and patterns that didn't fit were how institutions died. The diver was a fluke—an accident of sonar and current. The archivist was a known quantity, contained, monitored at the low simmer the budget allowed. But the daughter of Prisoner 41 working inside the tribunal's own translation service meant that the accident had found its audience. It meant the documents, wherever they were, had somewhere to go. A ledger in a diver's cabin was contraband. A ledger delivered into the tribunal's hands became evidence, and evidence, once authenticated, became history, and history was the one thing Vess could not requisition, countersign, or burn.

Because here was the truth he had carried for thirty years, folded flat and carried close, the way other men carried grief: the crimes themselves had never frightened him. He had reviewed the transport schedules in the last winter of the war with the detachment of a man reviewing freight tonnage, because that is what they were—freight—and because the alternative to detachment was a feeling, and feelings, in a man charged with order, were a failure of professional discipline. No. What frightened him, what had always frightened him, was the reading of names aloud. Names were the unit of memory. Ash erased paper, but a name spoken in a public chamber, entered into a record, witnessed by strangers—that could not be countersigned. That was the flood.

Fourteen hundred names. One of them belonged to the woman in the photograph's father.

Vess finished his coffee. It was cold. He noticed and did not care, which was itself unusual, and he filed the unusualness away with the efficiency of long habit.

* * *

The call came through at seven sharp, on the secure line that terminated in a room in the capital that officially housed a maritime insurance consultancy. Corven answered on the second ring, as he always did, and said nothing, as he always did, because the protocol was that the principal spoke first.

"Status," Vess said.

"They crossed the border four days ago. Iriarte's aircraft, southern approach, landed short of Anselm's Reach. They've acquired a local guide—a woman, Anand. Good ones up there, unfortunately." Corven's voice was dry as a customs stamp. "They're staged for the high route. Weather window opens with the calving."

"You've let them get this far deliberately."

"I've let them commit. Men who've climbed half a mountain argue harder about turning back than men still packing." A pause, the sound of a match. Corven smoked when he talked, and Vess permitted it because permitting small vices was another form of ownership. "Also, following them up is cheaper than digging them out of a tribunal witness list later. Up there, there are no witnesses but ice."

Vess stood and moved to the window. Below, the capital was waking—trams, gulls, the gray glitter of the river he had spent his life keeping orderly. Somewhere downriver, a wreck sat in the silt with a plaque-sized hole in its silence. Somewhere south, four people and a guide were packing rope and rations against a window of days.

"Understand what we're doing," Vess said, and his voice did not change register, because it never needed to; the menace lived entirely in the content, delivered in the tone of a man approving leave requests. "This is not an assassination. Assassinations generate inquiries, inquiries generate martyrs, and martyrs generate the exact thing we are preventing. What this is, is weather."

Silence on the line. Then, carefully: "Sir?"

"The Cordillera del Viento kills experienced parties every season. Every season, Corven. Guides die, pilots die, tourists with too much confidence and too little oxygen die, and the mountains are never blamed because mountains cannot be blamed. They are mourned. Candles, perhaps. A paragraph in the foreign press. *Tragedy in the high passes.*" He watched a tram cross the bridge below, small and punctual, swallowed by the city's noise. "If four people fail to come down off that glacier, no tribunal convenes. No newspaper sends anyone. Their governments issue condolences and their families hold funerals, and the documents they were carrying stay under the ice until the ice gives them up in fifty years to some other century that will care less, not more. The mountain can be blamed, Corven. The mountain wants to be blamed. It has the appetite for it. We need only be patient enough to feed it."

"And if they turn back? If the window closes early and they retreat to the village?"

"Then they winter there, and winter is merely the mountain working slowly." Vess returned to his desk and drew the file toward him, squaring its edges again, a habit his wife had once found endearing and had died before she could find tiresome. "I want no signatures, no orders in writing, no equipment traceable past the surplus dealer in Tarnow who sells to hunters. You will hire local men whose grievances are their own and whose names will never appear adjacent to ours. You will be a search-and-recovery contractor if anyone asks, and mostly no one will ask, because everyone believes the mountains kill people. That belief has been paid for, Corven—in salaries, in silence, in thirty years of careful nothing. Do not spend it recklessly."

"The archivist and the woman," Corven said. "If they come down alive—"

"Then they come down to a country where their visas have lapsed, their funding has dried, and their testimony has no corroborating documents, because the documents are in a crevasse. Evidence without proof is a memoir. Memoirs are not jurisdictional." Vess opened the signals folder at last, a gesture of closing rather than opening. "Only the documents matter. Remember that above all. Bodies are regrettable; bodies are even useful, in the way that weather is useful. But if a single page comes down off that ice authenticated, then everything else we have built—" he paused, and for one uncharacteristic moment the clock's ticking seemed loud in the room "—becomes a very long list of signatures, and signatures can be read aloud."

"I understand," Corven said.

"Do you?" Vess looked at the photograph of Mirena Kovač one final time—at the gesture frozen mid-sentence, the face turned toward someone just outside the frame, toward a conversation he could not hear and would not permit to continue. "My father fished this river, Corven. Before the war. He used to say the water keeps everything, that nothing given to a river is ever truly lost—it only moves downstream, patiently, waiting for someone to look." He closed the file. "I have spent my life making sure no one looks. See that the mountain does the same."

He set the receiver down without waiting for acknowledgment. Outside, the morning had fully arrived; the aides would be at their desks, the fleet's machinery turning, the day's small orders queuing for his initials. All of it orderly. All of it, still, his.

In the south, the calving had begun. He knew this the way he knew the tide tables—by report, by rhythm, by the quiet certainty that ice, like rivers, gave up what it held only when it chose to, and only briefly.

The window would be open for days.

Vess began to sign the morning's papers, and the signatures came out steady, and the room stayed silent, and far away the seracs leaned over their corridor like judges who had not yet decided.
