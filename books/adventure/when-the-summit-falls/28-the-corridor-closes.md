## 28. The Corridor Closes

Sefa Anand had learned to read the mountain the way other people read faces—by the small betrayals that preceded the large ones. A change in the pitch of the wind through the col. The way the snow squeaked underfoot instead of sighing. The smell of the air itself, thin and mineral and wrong, like a room before a door slams.

She had been reading it all morning, and all morning it had been telling her something she did not want to hear.

They had made camp at the base of the icefall's lower tongue, a shelf of blue ice wedged between two moraines, sheltered enough from the wind to boil water without burning half their fuel. Rafael was checking the ropes. Elias sat with the chest's bearings copied onto three separate scraps of paper, tucked into three separate pockets, because he trusted nothing—not paper, not pockets, not memory—to survive alone. Mirena was melting snow, her hands red and steady. And Tomás lay against a pack, breathing in the shallow, careful way he had adopted since the whiteout, as if each breath were something he'd negotiated for.

Sefa stood apart, at the edge of the shelf, looking up.

The icefall rose above them like a city built by something that hated geometry. Seracs leaned against seracs, towers of compressed snow and ancient air, some of them the size of chapels, some the size of cathedrals, all of them groaning. That was the thing no photograph ever captured: the sound. The glacier spoke constantly—a creak here, a crack there, long subterranean groans that came up through the boots and into the teeth. During calving season, when the summer melt lubricated the deep ice and the whole frozen river began to move, the corridor between the seracs opened like a door held ajar. For days, sometimes a week, a path existed where no path could otherwise be.

Her grandfather had called it *la ventana*—the window. He had also called it the mountain's lie, because a window implies you can see through it, and the icefall showed you only what it wanted.

And now, watching the light on the upper towers, Sefa saw the lie ending early.

The clouds were coming in from the northwest, but that wasn't what stopped her breath. It was the color beneath them—a bruised, greenish cast in the high haze, the kind of sky that meant cold falling out of the stratosphere in a single night. Storm air. And below the storm air, the glacier itself: the seracs on the eastern flank had shifted since dawn. She had marked their silhouettes against the sky an hour ago, the way her grandfather taught her, memorizing the skyline the way you'd memorize a face. Two of the towers had already changed. One had lost its crown entirely; a fresh scar of white showed where the top had sheared off sometime in the last hour, quietly, while they ate.

The window was closing. Not in two days, as the guides at the last settlement had promised. Now. Today. Perhaps within hours.

"Sefa." Mirena's voice behind her. "Your tea."

"Leave it," Sefa said, and heard how flat her own voice came out, and turned. "Pack everything. We're moving now."

Mirena looked at her, then past her, up at the icefall, and Sefa watched the understanding arrive in her face the way weather arrives—first the shadow, then the weight of it. "How long do we have?"

"If I'm wrong? Three days." Sefa picked up her pack and shrugged it on, cinching the waist strap hard enough to hurt. "If I'm right? Six hours. Maybe less."

* * *

Rafael took the news the way he took everything—without argument, which she had learned was not calm but a kind of discipline, a man who had spent years underwater learning that panic is just a slower way of drowning. He broke camp with quick hands, kicked snow over the stove's scar, and came to stand beside her at the edge of the shelf.

"Show me," he said.

She pointed. "There. The route goes up through the middle channel—the one we scouted yesterday—and traverses east below the big leaning tower, then down the far side through the melt channels. Yesterday that tower was solid. Look at it now."

He looked. Even from this distance, even to eyes less trained than hers, the change was legible: the great serac had settled, its lean grown steeper, a fissure of deep blue opened along its flank like a wound.

"And if we wait for it to stabilize?"

"It won't stabilize. Calving season doesn't stabilize—it accelerates until the storm kills the melt, and then everything that's been loosening for weeks comes down at once, all along the fall, like a building deciding it's tired of standing." She kept her voice level because his was level, because Mirena was listening, because Elias had stopped copying his papers and was looking at them both with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "My grandfather crossed this pass eleven times. He told me once that the window is not a gift. It's a debt. The mountain opens the door so it can close it on someone."

Rafael was quiet a moment. Below them, Tomás coughed—a wet, rattling sound that had gotten worse every day since the bivouac, and that he pretended, with tremendous dignity, not to notice.

"Then we go now," Rafael said. "All of us. Together."

"The old man shouldn't—" Sefa began, and stopped, because Rafael had turned and looked at her, and there was something in his face that ended the sentence better than any argument. She had seen men look at brothers that way. She had never seen anyone look at a smuggler pilot that way.

"He crosses," Rafael said. "We all cross. Or none of us do."

"That's not how the mountain works."

"Maybe not." He shouldered his rope coil. "But it's how we do."

* * *

They entered the icefall at noon, into the cathedral.

Sefa led, as she had led since the moraine, and tried—as she always tried—to hold both truths at once: that this place was trying to kill them, and that it was the most beautiful thing she would ever walk through. The seracs rose on either side like walls of frozen lightning, blue at the base where the ice was oldest and densest, white at the crowns where the sun struck, and everywhere shot through with veins of meltwater that ran silver and sang. The light came down in shafts between the towers and lay on the snow in blades. Their crampons bit. Their breath smoked. And underneath everything, constant, the glacier groaned—a sound like a ship's hull flexing in heavy sea, vast and structural and patient.

"Left," she said, at the first fork. "Stay left. The right channel looks shorter. It isn't. It's a trap with good lighting."

Elias, behind her, huffed something that might have been a laugh. "Like most archives."

"Keep your mind on your feet, archivist."

"My mind is exactly where it needs to be. If I think about my feet, I'll think about the ice, and if I think about the ice, I'll stop walking, and I find I very much want to keep walking."

Ahead, the channel narrowed between two leaning towers whose summits met overhead, or nearly did, leaving a seam of sky the width of a hand. Sefa stopped at the mouth of it and listened. The others waited, obedient now in the way people become when they've surrendered navigation to someone else—a surrender she felt the full weight of, every hour, and carried the way you carry a sleeping child: carefully, and without complaint, and with the knowledge that putting it down would break something.

From deep inside the glacier came a sound she knew. Not a groan. A *tick*—a sharp, percussive crack, like a knuckle the size of a house. Then another. Then silence, which was worse.

"What was that?" Mirena whispered.

"The ice adjusting," Sefa said. "Or failing. From inside, they sound the same." She looked up at the seam of sky, judged the angle of the towers, the age of the snow bridges welded between them. "We cross this section fast. No talking. Ten meters apart. If I raise my hand, you freeze where you are—don't run, don't turn around, don't do anything but stop. Running tells the ice you've noticed it, and the ice respects attention."

"That's superstition," Elias said.

"Yes," said Sefa, and stepped into the narrows.

They crossed in ninety seconds that felt like a season. Above them the towers leaned, shedding fine streams of powder snow that hissed down the flanks like sand in an hourglass. Halfway through, the glacier cracked again—closer, sharper, directly overhead—and Sefa raised her hand and froze, and behind her four bodies stopped as one, and for three heartbeats the whole world was the sound of their own breath and the slow, grinding patience of ten thousand tons of ice deciding what to do next.

It decided nothing. The moment passed. She walked, and they walked, and the narrows released them into a wider gallery where the light fell open and everyone remembered how to breathe.

Mirena caught up beside her. Her face was gray under the windburn. "You've done this before," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I grew up on this mountain. My family has guided these passes for four generations." Sefa checked the terrain ahead, found the line she wanted, kept walking. "My grandfather guided the last man who came through carrying a box heavier than a coffin. You know that story by now."

"You never finished it."

"No," Sefa said. "I haven't."

Below them, faintly, carried up the glacier's throat, came another sound—one that had nothing to do with ice. A distant, rhythmic crack, too regular, too mechanical. Rifle fire? No. She strained, listening. No—wind against rock, maybe. Or maybe not. Rafael had heard it too; she saw him stop, head cocked, and saw him reach the same arithmetic she had. Corven's trackers were somewhere behind them on the mountain, and sounds traveled far in cold air, and there was no longer any way to know whether what they heard was weather or men.

"Hurry," she said softly, to no one, to everyone, and climbed.

* * *

They reached the traverse below the great leaning tower at midafternoon, and Sefa knew, before she saw it fully, that her morning reading had been right and worse than right.

The tower had moved again. Not settling now—*leaning*, actively, its base eroding in the afternoon melt, a curtain of meltwater pouring from a fissure near its crown and freezing again in midair into hanging curtains of new ice that would not hold anything, that were decorative, that were lies. The route passed directly beneath it. There was no alternative; she had spent the morning's climb confirming that, hating every step of the confirmation. The traverse was forty meters of exposed slope beneath perhaps sixty thousand tons of leaning ice.

She gathered them at the entry point, in the lee of a boulder of grey gneiss older than any nation, older certainly than Veloria, which had existed and been erased in less time than this stone had been waiting.

"Listen to me," she said. "One at a time across the traverse. Rope on, but spaced—if it comes down, the rope must not tie us into one grave. Fast feet, soft steps. Do not look up. Looking up slows people, and slowing is what kills you here." She looked at each of them. Tomás last. He was leaning on his axe with both hands, his lips bluish, his breathing audible even over the glacier's murmur. "Tomás. You cross second. Behind me, before the others. Understood?"

"Understood," he said, and grinned at her with what strength he had left, and the grin cost him visibly. "Though I'll note, guide, that in my profession we call this a bad approach pattern."

"In your profession you fly over mountains. In mine, we go through them. Different rules."

"Mine are better," he wheezed, "until they aren't."

She went first, setting the pace, planting each axe strike deliberately, counting her own steps because counting was the oldest trick she knew—numbers left no room for fear. Twenty steps. Thirty. The tower above her shed a ribbon of powder that pattered across the slope like applause. Forty. She reached the far anchor point, clipped the line, turned, and waved Tomás across.

He came well. Slowly, but well—steady feet, steady rhythm, a man who had spent thirty years running toward something and refused to stumble in sight of it. He was fifteen meters out when the glacier spoke.

Not a tick this time. A *crack*—enormous, intimate, directly overhead, the sound of a seam letting go somewhere in the tower's heart. Sefa's hand was up before her mind caught her body. Tomás froze, mid-slope, perfectly still, a man who had clearly been frightened before and knew the posture.

Above him, high on the tower, a block the size of a truck bed tilted out of the face, hung, and fell.

It missed him. It missed him by the length of a body, striking the slope forty meters downslope, shattering, and the report of it rolled down the glacier like thunder rolling along a valley floor, and the echo came back, and then—silence, total, absolute, the held breath of six people and one mountain.

Tomás did not move.

"Tomás," Sefa called, low and even. "Walk. Don't run. Walk."

He walked. He reached the anchor, and she pulled him into the lee of the stone, and for a moment he simply stood there with his forehead nearly touching the rock, breathing, and she put her hand flat between his shoulder blades and felt the racing bird of his heart and thought: *not today.* Not today, old man. Not on my mountain.

Behind them, across the traverse, Rafael was already bringing Mirena across, then Elias, then the re-roped gear, and the tower groaned its long complaint and held, and held, and held.

When they were all through, Elias sank onto his pack and laughed—a strange, cracked sound, almost embarrassing, the laugh of a man who has just watched death miss by forty meters and doesn't know what his face is supposed to do about it. "I'd like it noted," he said, "that I have devoted my life to paper precisely so that I would never have to do *that*."

"Noted," said Mirena, and she was shaking, Sefa saw, shaking as she coiled rope with hands that wouldn't quite obey, and Rafael put his hand over hers and stilled them, and neither of them said anything, and nothing needed saying.

Sefa looked back up the route they'd come. The tower still leaned. By tomorrow it would be down; she was certain of it now, certain in the marrow-deep way that needed no instrument. Which meant the route behind them was closing as surely as the route ahead.

And ahead—

She turned and looked south, down the glacier's descending course toward the far side of the range, and her stomach dropped.

The clouds had come while they were in the narrows. Not the high haze of morning—real cloud now, a wall of it boiling up over the southern cols, slate-colored and organized, feeding on itself, the leading edge already streaked with virga that never reached the ground. Storm cloud. And not tomorrow's storm. Tonight's.

The window wasn't narrowing. It was slamming.

* * *

She called them together at the next sheltered bend, and did not soften it, because softening wasted minutes and minutes were now the only currency that mattered.

"The storm will hit tonight," she said. "Full dark, maybe earlier. When it hits, the melt stops, the temperature drops, and everything this season has loosened comes down—the icefall, the slopes, the cornices, all of it. After that, this corridor does not exist. Not for us, not for Corven, not for spring melt. Months." She let that land. "We have to be through the upper icefall and off the glacier proper before the first snow. That means moving through the night. That means the descent route I know—the steep one, the one I haven't told you about yet."

"The vertical gully," Rafael said. He'd guessed; of course he'd guessed. He'd been watching her mark waypoints on the map with a precision that exceeded any normal route.

"A gully," she confirmed. "Steep. Exposed. Three days of climbing down instead of one day of walking. But it's on the leeward side, it holds no seracs, and it's the only way down that the storm cannot close." She paused. "It is also, I will say plainly, terrifying, and I have only taken one person down it, and that person was me."

Silence. Wind. Somewhere above them, the glacier ticked and ticked.

"And Corven?" Mirena asked. Her voice was steady. Sefa had come to admire that steadiness enormously—a woman who translated atrocities for a living and had learned to keep her voice level inside the worst rooms. "Where are they?"

"Between us and the gully mouth," Sefa said. "Or close to it. Those sounds on the glacier weren't wind. They've cut our line, or they've guessed it. They're better equipped, better rested, and they don't have an old man slowing them down." She looked at Tomás, who met her gaze without flinching, and hated herself a little for saying it, and said it anyway, because he deserved truth more than kindness. "Which means we can't outrun them and we can't outwait them. All we can do is move faster than the storm and arrive at the gully before they understand where we're going. Once we're on the rock, numbers matter less. Rock is honest. Ice lies; rock just is."

Rafael nodded slowly. "Then we don't stop. Not for rest, not for food except in motion. We move until we're off the ice."

"We move until we're off the ice," Sefa agreed, and started repacking the rope, and stopped, because Elias had gone very still, holding one of his three scraps of paper, staring at it as though it had begun to glow.

"Elias?"

"The officer," Elias said. His voice had gone strange—thin, urgent, the voice of a man reading a letter over someone's shoulder. "The Velorian officer who carried the second chest. My grandfather's notation—the dates. He crossed in winter. The pass was closed. Everyone assumed he died in the icefall, in the corridor, during calving season." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and burning at once. "He didn't die in the icefall. He couldn't have. The corridor was shut—he took the *other* way down. Your gully, Sefa. Your grandfather's secret route. It's how the chest survived. It's how *he* survived long enough to hide it."

Sefa stood motionless. The wind picked at them. High above, the great tower finally began to go—she heard it before any of them, the rising note, the deep structural groan of sixty thousand tons surrendering—and she grabbed Mirena's arm and pointed upslope and shouted *move, MOVE,* and they ran, all five of them, down the glacier's throat while behind them the cathedral they had walked through folded one tower at a time into the valley of its own ruins, dust and powder boiling up in a white tidal wave that chased them, licked their heels, and stopped.

When the air cleared, the route behind them was gone. Completely, permanently gone, erased as if it had never existed.

There was only forward now. Forward, into the coming dark, down a dead man's secret road, with the storm descending and the hunters somewhere below, and hours—only hours—left in the world as they knew it.

Sefa spat on her palms, took the first rope, and led.
