## 29. Running the Seracs

The mountain told us before the sky did.

It was a sound I felt in my teeth first—a deep, tectonic groan rolling up through the soles of my boots, as if the glacier were a ship's hull taking water somewhere far below. Then a crack like a rifle shot, then another, sharper, from the direction of the icefall we'd climbed through three days ago. Sefa stopped so suddenly that Mirena walked into her pack.

"Listen," Sefa said.

We listened. Above us, the seracs of the icefall corridor stood against a sky gone the color of dirty wool—those leaning towers of blue-green ice we had threaded between on the ascent, cathedral pillars that had groaned and shifted but held. Now they were not groaning. They were *talking*, a constant low conversation of stress and fracture, and beneath it ran a new sound, a hiss, like water moving where no water should be.

"The calving's starting early," Sefa said. Her voice was flat, which from Sefa meant fear. "The meltwater's undercutting the bases. That corridor we came up through—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Elias pulled out the barometer he'd been checking obsessively since dawn and read it twice. "Falling fast," he said. "The storm window's closing. The whole thing is closing."

I looked back the way we'd come, down the long white ramp of the glacier toward the icefall, and felt something cold settle in my chest that had nothing to do with altitude. Corven's team was somewhere behind us, below us, between us and the descent. And now the descent itself was becoming a thing that would not wait for anyone—not for them, not for us, not for the chest wrapped in canvas and rope across Elias's shoulders, not for the film inside it that thirty years of ice had kept safe and that one warm afternoon could destroy.

"How long?" I asked.

Sefa squinted at the icefall, reading it the way Tomás used to read weather off a windshield. "Hours," she said. "Maybe six. Maybe four."

* * *

We did not discuss it. There was nothing to discuss. We repacked in silence with the efficiency of people who had spent two weeks learning each other's rhythms—Mirena cinching the chest's ropes while Elias broke camp, me melting snow into the last of our fuel because bodies descending fast still need water, Sefa standing watch with her eyes on the icefall the whole time, her hand resting on the old brass compass her grandfather had carried up this same mountain half a century ago.

Tomás should have been here to fly us out. My mind kept snagging on that absence the way a boot snags on hidden rock. Two days dead on the moraine, buried under stones because the ground was too hard for anything deeper, and I still caught myself turning to say something to him, still expecting the rasp of his laugh, some joke about how the navy couldn't find its own ass with both hands and a chart.

"Rafael." Mirena's voice, quiet. She was looking at me with those dark eyes that missed nothing. "Where do you want us?"

Not *what do we do*. Not *are we going to make it*. She was handing me the decision, and I understood that she'd been handing me decisions for days now, and that at some point without noticing I had become the person this small battered family looked to when the world narrowed to a single bad choice.

I had never asked for that. Three years ago I'd been a man who answered only to tide tables and dive computers, whose longest conversation most weeks was with a harbor master about mooring fees. Leadership was a thing that happened to other men, men who wanted it, men who liked the sound of their own voices giving orders. But there was nobody else. Sefa knew the ice but she was carrying her grandfather's ghost along with her gear; Elias knew the documents but his hands shook in the cold and his body was sixty-two years of bookshops and exile; Mirena was the strongest of them in spirit and the least experienced on ice. And Corven was behind us, and the mountain was coming apart ahead of us, and someone had to choose the line.

"Single file, twenty meters apart," I said. "If I stop, you stop. If I run, you run. Nobody ties in through the icefall—if one of us goes down with a rope, we all go." I looked at each of them. "And if the ice starts coming down and it's me or the chest—"

"Don't," Mirena said.

"—you keep moving," I finished. "That thing is the only reason any of this happened. It's the only reason Tomás—"

"It's not worth more than you," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and for a moment none of us moved, four people on a white slope with a storm building behind the peaks and death ticking under our feet.

Then Sefa said, "He'll say yes if you ask him again tomorrow too. Move."

* * *

The first hour was almost easy, and that frightened me more than chaos would have. The glacier above the icefall was smooth, wind-scoured, and we made good time roped loosely in a line, boots crunching, breath hanging in flags behind us. The light was strange—flat, shadowless, the sun a pale disc behind the thickening cloud—and in that light the icefall ahead looked almost beautiful, a frozen waterfall the size of a city, its seracs glowing faintly green-blue like something alive seen through deep water.

I had dived wrecks for eleven years. I knew what it was to move through a structure that could collapse on you, to feel a hull shudder under your hand and trust your life to metal that had already failed once. I told myself the icefall was just another wreck, vertical instead of horizontal, and that I knew how to read broken things.

But wrecks don't calve.

We reached the top of the corridor at midday, and Sefa called a halt at the lip, and I saw immediately that the route we had climbed was gone.

The narrow ramp between the two great leaning towers—the one she'd scouted, the one we'd ascended with fixed lines and an hour of careful footwork—was buried under fresh debris. A serac the size of a house had come off the eastern wall and shattered across the passage, filling it with blocks the size of trucks, and even as we watched, smaller pieces were still trickling down the face of the fall zone, pattering onto the debris like the last drops of something being poured.

"Route's erased," Sefa said simply. She wasn't panicked. She was already reading, her eyes tracing the new geometry of the icefall the way a river pilot reads a flooded channel. "There." She pointed left, downslope, where a different gap opened between two pillars—one we hadn't used on the ascent because it funneled into a crevasse field. "It's longer. It crosses the flow line. But it's open."

"For how long?"

"That's the wrong question." She met my eyes. "The right question is: can we cross it faster than the mountain changes its mind?"

I looked at the gap. I looked at the sky. Somewhere behind us, several miles back, Corven's trackers were making their own decisions, and every hour we bought was an hour they lost to the same collapsing route—or an hour they gained on us if they found another way down. I thought about Tomás saying, once, grinning through a cigar: *the sea doesn't hate you, Rafa, it just doesn't know you're there.* The mountain was the same. It wasn't hunting us. It simply didn't care, and there was a terrible freedom in that.

"We cross," I said. "Fast and quiet. No talking on the traverse. Hand signals only."

* * *

The gap was worse than it had looked.

From the lip it seemed a straightforward descent—a steep ramp of compacted snow between ice walls, maybe three hundred meters, dropping into the crevasse field below. But snow lies. Ten steps in, my boot punched through a crust into hollow space, and I froze with my heart slamming against my ribs, listening to the void breathe under me. Snow bridges. The whole ramp was laced with them, thin roofs over black depths, and every step was a wager.

I went to work the only way I knew how: like a diver reading a wreck. Probe with the axe shaft before every step. Listen—snow over a crevasse has a sound, a faint drum-note, hollow where solid snow thuds. Weight low, weight slow, distribute, commit. Behind me I heard the others copying my rhythm, and after a while I stopped hearing them as separate people and started hearing us as one animal with eight limbs and four hearts, moving as a single cautious creature down the throat of the icefall.

Halfway down, the mountain changed its mind.

The crack came from directly overhead—a sound like the sky splitting—and I looked up in time to see the western wall shed a block the size of a bus. It didn't fall so much as *pour*, disintegrating as it came, and the air filled with ice shrapnel and powder snow, and the world went white and roaring.

"MOVE!" I screamed, all discipline gone, and we ran.

There is no dignity in running down a snow bridge over a crevasse field. We floundered, plunged, fell, got up. A chunk of ice the size of a stove hit ten meters to my left and exploded into shards that stung my cheek like buckshot. Mirena went down hard and Elias hauled her up by the pack straps with a strength I wouldn't have credited him. The roar behind us swelled—I didn't look, looking costs time—and then the sound changed, deepened, became the vast grinding boom of tons of ice meeting the glacier floor, and the shockwave hit us like weather, knocking Elias flat, and then it was over except for the dust and the ringing silence and the sound of five people gasping.

Six people. I counted twice. Sefa was already ahead of me, twenty meters down, waving us on with urgent sweeps of her arm. Of course she hadn't stopped. Stopping was how you died here.

We moved.

* * *

The crevasse field nearly ended us.

Below the icefall the glacier spread out into a maze of fissures, some wide enough to swallow a truck, their lips draped in fragile snow. On the ascent we'd crossed this section on Sefa's marked route, wands planted, bridges tested. Now the wands were gone—buried or knocked down by the calving tremors—and the geometry had shifted, new cracks opening, old ones widening. Twice Sefa stopped us at a bridge she remembered and found it sagging, concave, a gray tongue of snow drooping into darkness, and twice she rerouted us along the crevasse edges themselves, on shelves of blue ice barely wide enough for one boot.

It was during the second reroute that I heard the radio.

It was clipped to my shoulder strap, the cheap set we'd salvaged from Tomás's plane, mostly static since the storm. Through the hiss, faintly, a voice—human, male, speaking in short clipped phrases. Corven's team, coordinating on a channel we could overhear. I couldn't make out words, only cadence, and the cadence was calm, professional, close.

I raised two fingers: hold. We crouched behind a serac base, five shapes in white, breathing steam, while the voice murmured somewhere in the miles of ice behind us. Mirena's hand found my sleeve. Her face asked the question none of us would say aloud: *how close?*

Close enough to hear. Far enough that the icefall was between us and them—which meant the collapsing route was slowing them too, or killing them, and I found I could not decide how I felt about that. These were men hired to bury evidence and probably to bury us with it. But they were also men under that same groaning sky, and some tired diver's part of me understood exactly why a man takes that kind of work: because invisibility pays better than visibility, until it doesn't.

"Move," Sefa whispered.

* * *

The traverse nearly killed us at the end.

To reach the lower glacier—the long clean ramp that ran down toward the moraines and, beyond them, the foothills—we had to cross one final feature: a lateral shelf where the icefall's debris fan met the valley wall, a hundred-meter stretch of tilted ice plastered over rock, hanging above a drop into a blue-black crevasse that yawned like a mouth. On the ascent we'd avoided it entirely, climbing a different line. Now that line was rubble, and the shelf was the only way, and the afternoon sun—what little of it pierced the cloud—was actively loosening the snow that glued the shelf to the mountain.

Sefa went first and set a handline off a buried rock horn, forty meters of rope, and we crossed one at a time, front-pointing on crampons with nothing but air and depth below our heels.

Elias crossed second. He was slow—agonizingly slow, each kick placed like a man signing his name—and halfway across his crampon ballled up with snow and his feet shot sideways and for one endless second he hung on the rope with both axes skittering on bare ice, legs swinging over the void, and I heard him make a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, a small apologetic grunt, as if falling were an imposition on *us*.

"Elias! Axes in! KICK!" I roared, and he kicked, once, twice, and the crampon bit, and he hauled himself flat against the slope and stayed there, trembling, sixty-two years old and alone on a shelf of dying ice.

"You're fine," I called. "You're fine. Left foot high. Small steps. I've got the rope."

He finished it. When he came off the end of the line his legs folded and Mirena caught him, and he laughed—a cracked, disbelieving laugh—and said, "In forty years of archives, no document ever tried to kill me," and Mirena laughed too, high and shaky, and I turned back to the line because someone had to be last and it was going to be me.

Mirena crossed. Sefa crossed. And then I stepped onto the shelf alone, and that was when the mountain took its last shot at us.

I was fifteen meters out, mid-traverse, when the entire upper icefall let go.

It didn't happen all at once. It happened everywhere at once—a chain reaction of collapse, serac after serac folding, the whole cathedral coming down in a rolling thunder that I felt through the ice in my chest and my teeth and my bones. Debris avalanched down the fan behind me. The shelf shuddered. Somewhere below my heels, deep in the glacier, something massive shifted, and the snow underfoot began to creep, slowly, the whole slope beginning to let go of the rock beneath it.

Prayer isn't a thing I do. I hadn't done it since I was a boy, since before the water took everything I'd loved the first time. But crossing that shelf, with the mountain roaring and sliding beneath me, I prayed—not in words, not to anyone in particular, just a raw animal plea thrown into the noise: *not yet, not today, let me get them down, let me get them down.*

I kicked. I swung. I moved faster than I have ever moved on ice, past fear into some place beyond it where the body simply does what it must, and the slope sloughed away behind my trailing foot in a soft hissing rush, and then my axe hit rock under thin snow, and then grass under thinner snow, and then I was over the lip and rolling onto the moraine, onto stone, onto the earth, alive.

The others dragged me the rest of the way clear. For a moment the five of us lay in a heap on the rocks, tangled, gasping, laughing or crying or both, while behind us the Cordillera del Viento dismantled the icefall we had just escaped, pillar after pillar thundering down into the corridor, erasing every trace of our passage, closing the door behind us with a sound like the end of the world.

When it finally quieted, Sefa sat up and looked back at the smoking ruin of ice and said quietly, "Nobody follows us through that. Not Corven. Not God."

Elias was staring at the mountainside with tears freezing in the creases of his face, clutching the canvas-wrapped chest to him like a child. Mirena had her forehead pressed against the stone, mouthing something—her father's name, I realized. Saying it over and over, the name she'd only learned a week ago, planting it in solid ground.

And I lay on my back on the moraine with my heart still slamming and looked up at the gray sky, and I understood something with total clarity: I had led them through. Not because I was brave or wise, but because when the moment came, somebody had to speak, and I spoke, and they moved, and we lived. Leadership, it turned out, was not a thing you wanted or earned. It was a thing you did, the way you breathe, when the people you love are watching you.

I got up. I checked the radio—dead static now, the icefall's collapse having swallowed whatever channels remained. I counted heads, checked the chest, checked the rope.

That was when Sefa, scanning the valley below us with her grandfather's binoculars, went very still.

"Rafael," she said. "You need to see this."

She handed me the glasses. I put them up, and swept down the long valley toward the tree line, toward the smoke of the far-side villages, toward safety—

—and found, instead, a figure standing on the moraine two hundred meters below us, alone, unarmed, hands raised, waving a scrap of red fabric against the snow.

Corven.
