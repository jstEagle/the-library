## 30. Ambush at the Moraine

The moraine was a gray tongue of stone laid down by the glacier centuries ago, and it was the only flat ground for miles.

Rafael saw it first as salvation—a place to rest, to redistribute weight, to let Sefa re-tape her ankle where the gully ice had bitten through her boot. They came down onto it in single file, boots crunching on scree that had been broken off mountains before any of their nations existed, and for ten minutes Rafael allowed himself something he had not permitted since the whiteout: the thought *we might make it*.

It lasted exactly as long as it took Tomás to stop walking.

The old pilot stood with his head cocked, listening the way he listened to engines—whole body tilted toward the sound, eyes half-closed, reading trouble in frequencies no one else could hear. The wind moved through the rocks around them. Somewhere below, the glacier calved with its distant artillery boom. And beneath all of it, faint as a hairline crack in an engine block—

"Voices," Tomás said. "Downwind. Two hundred meters, maybe less."

They scattered into the boulder field without being told. Sefa pulled Mirena behind a slab of granite streaked green with lichen; Elias folded himself into a hollow between stones with the chest's contents strapped against his spine like a second ribcage. Rafael went low beside Tomás and crawled to the edge of the moraine where the rock gave way to the glacier's blue shoulder, and looked down the slope they had just descended.

Four figures. Dark technical gear, white overgarments rolled at the waist, moving with the unhurried economy of men who had done this in worse places than this. One carried a rifle slung across his back. Another held something small and rectangular that caught the light and threw it back in pulses.

A radio direction finder. Sweeping for them.

"They cut the switchbacks," Sefa breathed, materializing at Rafael's elbow. "We were supposed to be a full day ahead."

"The storm closed the pass behind us," Elias whispered. "It didn't close it behind *them*. Corven would have flown them to the southern approach the moment the window opened. We climbed out of one trap straight into another."

Rafael counted again. Four visible. Corven's trackers had numbered six when the radio intercept confirmed them in the foothills; two could be flanking already, working up the moraine's blind side while the others fixed their position. That was how he would do it. That was how anyone competent would do it.

"We can't outrun them," Sefa said. "Not with my ankle. Not down the icefall with the route erasing itself."

"We can't fight them," Elias said. "Look at what they're carrying. Look at what we are."

What they were: a diver, an archivist, a translator, a guide with a bad ankle, and a smuggler past sixty whose heart had already tried to quit once above the officer's camp. What they carried: film, photographs, testimony sealed in waterproof sleeves, and a flare cache Tomás had insisted on hauling from the last settlement because, in his words, *a man who flies cargo learns never to land empty-handed*.

Tomás was looking at the flare cache now.

Rafael saw him looking and understood, and the understanding arrived like cold water in the lungs.

"No," he said quietly.

"There's a draw," Tomás said, in the same low register, as if they were discussing fuel mixture. "Northeast side, where the moraine drops toward the lateral crevasse field. You saw it coming down. The smoke will pool there, drift across the ice. In this light, with the wind shifting—" He shrugged. "They'll think the mountain's burning. They'll go to it. Men chase fire. It's older than language."

"And you'll be standing next to the fire when they come to look."

"Somebody has to light it late." Tomás smiled, and it was the same crooked smile he'd worn over the foothills, when the navy aircraft had turned back at the range's edge and he'd laughed like a man who had gotten away with something for thirty years. "I'm the slowest of you anyway. Be honest, Rafael. I've been the slowest since the icefall."

"You're not staying on this mountain so I can run faster."

"I'm staying on this mountain because thirty years ago I was ordered to burn an archive, and I ran instead, and I have been running ever since in aircraft and trucks and boats and every other machine a coward can hide inside." His voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "This is the first time in my life the running stops in the right place. Don't you dare take that from me."

Below them, the man with the radio finder stopped. Raised a hand. The four figures clustered, and even at two hundred meters Rafael could read the geometry of it: they had a bearing. They had a fix. Minutes, not hours.

Mirena crawled forward on her elbows until she was between them, her face pale and set in the way it had been set since she learned her father's name. "There has to be another way. There is always another way. We haven't tried—"

"Mirena." Tomás took her hand. His fingers were cracked and blackened with rope burn and altitude, and he held hers as gently as if it were made of the paper he'd refused to burn. "You spent your whole life looking for a father who did one brave thing carrying a box through the snow. Don't argue with an old man doing his one brave thing too. It's in the family business."

She made a sound Rafael would remember for the rest of his life—not a word, not a cry, something between the two, torn out of her—and then she pressed her forehead to Tomás's knuckles, once, and let go.

Sefa was already sketching in the scree with a finger: the draw, the wind line, the timing. She worked fast and without comment, because grief that arrived later was a luxury and the mountain did not extend credit. "Flares go in three clusters, twenty meters apart, upwind edge of the draw. Smoke banks downhill into the crevasse field. You light the third cluster last and you walk away from it low, along the east rim, and we take the traverse north while they're committed to the smoke." She looked up at Tomás. "You light it and you follow. Thirty seconds behind us. You swear it."

"I swear nothing," Tomás said cheerfully. "But I'll try."

Elias had not spoken. He knelt in the scree with his hands pressed flat against the stone, and when Rafael looked at him the archivist's face was wet and utterly calm, the way it had been calm in the safehouse when he'd explained how cleanly Veloria had been laundered out of history. "Thirty years," Elias said. "He told me on the flight that he owed the dead a debt. I thought he meant money."

"He meant this," Rafael said.

"He meant this," Elias agreed, and took off his own pack, and from its deepest pocket drew the letter—the one they had found in Tomás's jacket after the moraine ambush that hadn't happened yet, the letter that explained everything, that Rafael now understood Tomás had carried for decades waiting for someone worth giving it to. No. Not yet. The letter was still in the jacket. The jacket was still on the man. Rafael shut the thought away like a compartment flooding, because if he opened it now he would drown on this moraine with the sun shining.

"Go," Tomás said. "All of you. The wind's turning."

* * *

They moved along the east rim in a crouch, stone to stone, while behind them Tomás Iriarte walked upright into the open with a canvas bag of flares over his shoulder like a man going out to check fence lines.

Rafael looked back exactly once.

The old smuggler had reached the upwind edge of the draw. He struck the first flare against his boot heel and the red star of it arced out onto the dry scrub, and then the second, and then he turned—not toward the escape route, but toward the trackers on the moraine below—and raised both arms wide, the burning flare in one hand like a torch, and shouted something the wind tore to pieces but that Rafael's heart translated anyway: *Come and see. Come and see what I kept.*

Smoke boiled up thick and chemical-orange, pooling in the draw, spilling across the glacier like a wound. The trackers broke formation instantly, exactly as promised—men chasing fire, older than language—and Rafael grabbed Mirena's sleeve and hauled her north along the traverse, Sefa limping hard beside them, Elias bringing up the rear with the evidence pressed to his spine.

They were four hundred meters gone, maybe five, when the first shot cracked off the rock walls.

Then a second. Then a third, spaced and deliberate, and the fourth shot was different—flatter, followed by silence that had a shape to it, and Rafael knew the shape the way he knew the weight of a flooded compartment. He had heard men die on radios. He had pulled bodies from rivers. He knew what a hit sounded like from four hundred meters through mountain air.

Mirena screamed and turned, and Sefa caught her, and Rafael did not turn at all because if he turned he would go back, and if he went back the chest would be taken and Tomás would have burned for nothing, and so he did the hardest thing he had ever done in a life measured in dive-hours and river miles: he kept walking north, one boot in front of the other, while behind him the smoke rose over the Cordillera del Viento like a signal only the dead could read.

* * *

They found the shallow cave Sefa remembered from the ascent—an overhang beneath a leaning slab, barely deep enough for four bodies—and they crowded into it as the light failed, and no one spoke for a long time.

Mirena sat with her knees drawn up, staring at nothing. Sefa cleaned her rifle with mechanical precision she did not feel. Elias unwrapped the evidence, checked it, rewrapped it, checked it again, as though repetition could hold the world together.

Rafael dug through the shared pack and found the stove, the fuel, half a brick of rations. His hands shook. He let them shake. There was no one left to be steady for except these three, and steadiness, he was learning, could wait until morning.

"He's not—" Mirena started, and stopped, and started again. "We didn't see. We didn't *see* him. He could have—people survive things, people survive—"

"Mirena." Sefa set down the rifle. Her voice was gentle in a way Rafael had not heard from her before; the guide who said no had a daughter's tenderness buried somewhere after all. "Four shots. The last one flat. He lit the fire so they'd be looking at the smoke, not at us. If they'd wanted to capture him they wouldn't have fired four times."

"But we don't *know*—"

"We know," Elias said softly. "We know the way we know everything else in this story. By what the powerful leave behind and what the powerless carry forward." He closed his eyes. "He carried a refusal for thirty years. He finally got to finish it."

Rafael fed the stove a measured thread of fuel and lit it, and the small blue flame threw their shadows huge against the stone, four shapes huddled together that had been five that morning, and the arithmetic of it was unbearable, and he bore it.

"He made me promise to fly them home," Rafael said at last, to no one, to the flame. "Back in the foothills. Drunk on thin air, laughing. Said if anything happened to him I should get the boat, the real one, the one he keeps in Puerto Cabral under a false name. Said I should paint something on the wheelhouse." His voice cracked and he let it. "I asked him what. He said, 'Paint the name I should have had all along.'"

"What name?" Mirena whispered.

Rafael shook his head. "He wouldn't say. Said we'd know it when we earned it."

The wind rose outside the overhang, moaning through the seracs below, and somewhere far off the glacier calved again—that great booming exhalation, the mountain shrugging off its own weight—and Mirena leaned against Sefa's shoulder and finally wept, not quietly at all, and none of them told her to be quiet, because the mountain had taken enough silences from enough people, and this one, at least, they would not give it.

Elias opened the evidence wrap one more time and looked at the photographs, the film, the testimony that named admirals and ministers, the proof that Tomás Iriarte had died on a gray moraine to keep moving north.

"They'll report it as a mountaineering accident," he said. "Corven will make sure. Four climbers lost in the calving season. A footnote, if that."

"Then we make it loud," Rafael said. "Louder than smoke. Louder than the navy's silence. We get this down the mountain and into hands that don't let go, and every newspaper on three continents prints his name next to Vess's, and the footnote becomes the headline."

Elias nodded slowly. Then he reached into his coat and drew out the radio—the small battered receiver they'd used to intercept Corven's traffic—and turned the dial with a thumb gone clumsy from cold.

Static. Wind. Static.

And then, faint, breaking up, a voice with a naval bearing saying coordinates Rafael recognized as the moraine, and the word *confirmed*, and the word *one*, and then a pause, and then:

"—the old man's effects. There's a letter here. Corven wants it brought down intact. Says it names names."

Rafael looked at Elias. Elias looked at Rafael.

Tomás's letter—the confession he'd carried for thirty years, the one naming the order he'd refused, the archive he'd saved, the men who gave it—was in the enemy's hands.

And Corven wanted it *intact*, which meant Corven's patron wanted to know exactly how much a dying smuggler had confessed, which meant for the first time since the river, the hunters needed something from the hunted.

Sefa was already lacing her boots in the dark.

"How far," she said, "to the moraine?"
