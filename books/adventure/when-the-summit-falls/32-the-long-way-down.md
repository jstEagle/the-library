## 32. The Long Way Down

The gully was not on any map. It was not even a route, properly speaking—it was a wound in the mountain's flank, a vertical seam of rock and old ice that my grandfather had shown me when I was nineteen and arrogant enough to think the Cordillera owed me nothing. He had called it *la garganta*—the throat—and he had said, in his flat way that made everything sound like weather: "You do not climb this because you want to. You climb it because the mountain has left you no other door."

I had never brought anyone here. Not clients, not colleagues, not the two men I had loved badly and left gently. The throat was mine the way grief is yours—carried alone, visited rarely, spoken of to no one.

Now I stood at its mouth with four people who had become, without my permission or my consent or anything so clean as a decision, the people I would die for.

Rafael knelt at the base of the first pitch, retying a knot for the third time. His hands were steady but slow, and I had learned on this mountain to read slowness as a language. Elias sat against a boulder with the chest strapped to his back like a hunchback's burden, breathing through his mouth in the shallow, careful rhythm of a man rationing his own lungs. Mirena had wrapped her frost-nipped fingers in strips cut from her spare shirt and said nothing about them, which was its own kind of lie, and I let her keep it because we were all keeping lies now—the small merciful ones, the ones that said *I'm fine* in a voice pitched just high enough.

And Tomás was dead, and none of us had said his name aloud since the moraine, because saying it would have opened something none of us could afford to fall into. Not yet. Grief, like the icefall, had to be crossed at dawn, quickly, in silence.

"Show me again," Rafael said, looking up at me.

So I showed him. The throat began as a chimney—a crack between two walls of granite furred with verglas, maybe three meters wide at the bottom, narrowing as it rose. Above the chimney came the traverse, forty meters of sloping ledges where the rock had shed its skin in plates, and above that the long ramp of blue ice, steep enough that a falling climber would not stop until the glacier below, six hundred meters down. There were anchors—old ones, pitons my grandfather had hammered in before I was born, their heads bloomed with rust like iron flowers—but anchors half a century old are promises made by dead men, and you test every one.

"We go in pairs," I said. "Rafael with me. Elias and Mirena follow. We pitch it in three moves. Nobody climbs tired. If your hands stop working, you say so, and we stop, and we wait, and the mountain can wait too."

"The mountain," Elias said dryly, "has been waiting thirty years. It can have another hour."

It was the first joke anyone had made since Tomás fell, and it landed strangely—not as laughter but as permission. Rafael's shoulders dropped a centimeter. Mirena exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh, almost a sob. I turned away so they wouldn't see what my face did, which was both.

We had lost most of our supplies on the traverse below—two ropes' worth of gear, the tent, half the food, the stove fuel we'd cached against exactly this kind of disaster. What remained fit into one pack and part of another: nine hundred grams of nuts and dried fruit per person, a tin of fat, the medical kit, the chest, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against Rafael's spine where his body heat would keep the pages from cracking. Three days of climbing ahead if the weather held. Two days of food if we were honest, which we had stopped being to each other somewhere around five thousand meters.

I divided it anyway, evenly, because uneven rations breed resentment and resentment breeds mistakes, and mistakes on the throat did not cost points. They cost people.

"Eat," I told them. "All of it. Now."

They ate. Even Elias, who had taken to giving half his share to Mirena with the clumsy discretion of a man who thought he was fooling everyone. I watched him press the extra handful into her palm and watched her accept it without comment, and I understood then something I had been circling for days: that love on a mountain is not a feeling. It is a logistics problem. It is who gets the last of the water, whose rope you trust, whose weight you carry when their legs give out. Everything else—the tenderness, the jokes, the way Rafael had started sleeping between Elias and the wind without being asked—was just the word for the logistics done well.

My grandfather would have understood. My grandfather, who had guided a Velorian officer up this same range carrying a box heavier than a coffin, and who had come home silent and stayed silent for forty years, and who had taught me the throat anyway, as if some part of him knew that someday the silence would need an exit and I would be the one holding the rope.

"All right," I said. "On me."

* * *

The chimney took us four hours.

It should have taken two. But my left arm—the one Corven's bullet had torn open on the moraine, the wound I had cleaned and bound and lied about—had begun to send its complaints upward past my shoulder and into my neck, and every jam of my fist into the crack sent a white flare of pain from wrist to elbow. I climbed anyway. You climb anyway. That is the whole secret of mountaineering and, I suspect, of everything else: you climb anyway, and afterward you tell the story where the climbing looked graceful.

Rafael saw. Of course he saw; he had spent three years reading rivers and rivers teach you to read everything that moves. Halfway up the second pitch he stopped beneath me on the ledge and said quietly, "Let me lead the ice."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding through the bandage."

"It's a scratch."

"Sefa." Just my name, in that low river voice, patient as water. "The ice is the hard part. Let me carry it."

I wanted to argue. Pride is a warm coat and it is very hard to take off at altitude. But I looked at his face—sunburned, cracked-lipped, the eyes calm and absolutely without pity—and I heard my grandfather saying *the mountain does not care how strong you are, only how honest*, and I gave him the rack.

He led beautifully. I had known he would—he moved the way he dove, economical, unhurried, conserving everything, spending nothing he didn't have to—but knowing it and watching it were different things. He placed each screw with a care that bordered on ceremony, tested it twice, clipped the rope with cold-stupid fingers that still found the gate on the first try. Twice he stopped, hanging on front points, and simply waited for his breath to come back rather than pushing through it, and I realized I was watching a man who had decided, somewhere in the last weeks, that dying was no longer an acceptable outcome. Not for himself. For the others. It changes how a person climbs. It changes how they do everything.

At the top of the ice ramp there was a shoulder of snow, and beyond it the descent proper began—which sounds wrong, after all that climbing, but the throat was a pass through the range's buttress, not a summit: we went up to go over, and down the far side into the valley that fed toward the village, toward safety, toward the radio office and the tribunal and the end of running.

We rappelled the first two pitches as the light went amber. Elias came down last, the chest swinging against his back, and when he reached the anchor his legs folded under him and he sat down hard in the snow and laughed once, a strange barking sound.

"Thirty years," he said, patting the bronze-bound lid. "Thirty years frozen, and now it gets to ride on an old man's spine down a cliff. History has no dignity."

"Hold onto it anyway," Mirena said, and crouched beside him, and checked his pulse at his throat with her wrapped fingers, and I saw Elias look at her hand and then at her face and then away, quickly, the way you look away from something too bright.

* * *

The second night we dug a snow cave into a drift against the buttress wall, because the tent was gone and the temperature was falling through minus twenty and falling faster than the almanac promised. Digging a cave is brutal work—half an hour of full-body effort that leaves you soaked in sweat you must immediately freeze-dry or die of—but it is also, in its way, the most intimate thing four people can do together. You carve a space barely large enough for your bodies. You arrange yourselves like spoons in a drawer because there is no other geometry. You share breath until the walls glaze silver, and you listen to each other sleep, or fail to sleep, and there is nowhere to hide any of it.

Mirena slept against the wall with her hands tucked into her armpits. Elias slept in the middle, which we had arranged without discussion—warmest spot, oldest bones—and Rafael slept at the mouth, by the entrance, where the cold came in, and I lay awake listening to the wind try the doorway and understanding that he had chosen the coldest place on purpose and would deny it if asked, and I added it silently to the ledger of things this man had given us without ever presenting the bill.

In the morning my arm had gone bad. Not catastrophically—the bleeding had stopped, the edges weren't angry yet—but the swelling had crept past my elbow and the skin above the bandage had taken on a shine that meant fluid, and fluid meant infection, and infection at altitude with no antibiotics and two days of climbing left was a mathematics problem with an ugly answer.

I bound it tighter. I took half a ration of the fat and ate it for the calories. I said nothing.

But Mirena had been a translator for tribunal witnesses for six years, and translators learn to hear what bodies say underneath words, and she caught me rewrapping it behind a boulder and did not ask permission, simply took the bandage from my hands and unwound it herself, and looked at it for a long moment with her jaw set.

"How long?"

"Since the moraine."

"Since the—" She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Sefa. You've been leading us on a rope with a hole in your arm for two days."

"It's holding."

"It's *weeping*." She repacked it with the last of the gauze, firm and quick, hurting me without apology, which I respected. Then she looked up. "When we get down, you see a doctor. That's not a request. That's a condition of the route."

"You don't set conditions on my mountain."

"Then consider it a condition of my friendship," she said, and tied off the knot, and stood, and offered me her hand—her ruined, bandaged hand—to pull me to my feet, and I took it, because refusing would have cost more than accepting.

Friendship. She had said it plainly, the way she translated plain things, and I carried the word up the next pitch like a stone in my pocket, turning it over, testing its weight. I had not had friends in years. Guides have clients; guides have colleagues; guides have a reputation that stands between them and other people like a serac stands between the sun and the ice. Somewhere in the last three weeks the reputation had melted and these strangers had walked through the gap, and now there was a woman binding my wounds and an archivist humming Velorian lullabies under his breath and a diver giving up the warm spot in the snow cave, and all of it had happened while I wasn't watching, the way calving happens—you hear the groan for days and then the whole face of the ice comes down at once.

* * *

The third day was the day the mountain tested whether we meant it.

A storm front had been building in the west since dawn—a bruise-colored line along the horizon that I had been pretending was cloud and knowing was not—and by mid-morning the wind had swung around and begun to push spindrift across the upper slopes in long white banners. Not a storm yet. A warning of one. The mountain clearing its throat.

We were on the lower ramps by then, moving fast, roped in a single line: me leading on the broken ground because I knew where the hidden crevasses slept under their snow bridges, then Rafael, then Elias with the chest, then Mirena. And it was Elias who slowed us, though none of us said so—his hips had begun to seize in the cold, and on the steeper sections his boots skated on the crust, and twice he went down to a knee and got up slower each time.

The third time, Rafael unclipped from the line.

"What are you doing?" Elias panted.

"Trade packs."

"Don't be absurd, I'm perfectly—"

"Elias." Rafael already had the chest off the older man's back and was shrugging it onto his own, over his own pack, so that he wore sixty pounds of bronze and paper stacked on top of forty pounds of survival. "You carried it out of the ice. That was your part. Mine's the downhill."

"That's not how parts work—"

"It's exactly how parts work." Rafael clipped back in, adjusted the straps, and looked at Elias with something that was not quite a smile. "Tomás used to say it. Everybody carries until they can't, then somebody else carries. That's the whole religion."

Elias stared at him. His eyes had gone bright and wet, and he blinked hard, twice, and said, in a voice roughened to gravel: "He never told me that."

"He didn't have to. You watch a man long enough, you learn his liturgy."

We moved on. And an hour later, when Mirena's wrapped fingers failed her on a downclimb and she hung for a terrible ten seconds with her feet skating on verglas before Rafael's outstretched arm found hers and locked—when she came over the lip shaking and furious at her own body—I took the pack off her shoulders without asking and put it on mine, and Rafael took the extra coil of rope from my harness, and Elias walked point on the easy ground where the footing was simple and his pride could survive, and that was how we went down the mountain: passing the weight hand to hand, person to person, nobody carrying more than they could and nobody carrying less, the whole human cargo of us flowing downward like water finding its level.

Endurance, I thought, somewhere in the white noise of the sixth hour. This is what endurance actually is. Not teeth and stubbornness. Hands. Other people's hands.

* * *

We camped the third night in a shallow overhang below the last headwall, and the storm arrived while we cooked snow over the little stove, and it arrived angry.

Wind screamed down the valley and found our hollow and filled it like a bellows. Snow came sideways, then upward, defying its own nature out of sheer malice. We pressed ourselves against the rock and took turns holding the tarp—our last shelter, salvaged from the lost kit—over the stove, and the flame bent horizontal and burned blue and somehow, absurdly, we had hot water, and we drank it with the last of the sugar, and it tasted like a feast.

Nobody spoke much. The storm spoke enough for all of us.

But late, in the black hours when the wind dropped a register and the snow hissed instead of howled, Mirena began to sing. Very quietly. A Velorian song—I recognized the mode, the descending fourth, the same shape as the lullabies Elias hummed—and I felt Elias stir against the rock, and I saw in the stove-light that he was crying without any movement of his face, tears simply standing in the seams of his eyes and freezing at the corners, and that Mirena sang on, her ruined hands folded in her lap, her voice thin and clear as wire.

She was singing for her father. Prisoner 41. The man with a name now, a name restored to her on a glacier by a dead officer's testimony—singing the songs he might have sung to her, the ones the archive couldn't burn because they lived in daughters' mouths instead of filing cabinets.

I looked at Rafael across the stove. He was watching her too, and his face in the firelight was open in a way I hadn't seen it in the weeks I'd known him—all the river caution gone, all the diver's sealed compartments opened—and when he caught me looking he didn't look away. Neither did I. Something passed between us across the small orange circle of heat, wordless, an acknowledgment: *this is what we're carrying down. Not the chest. This.*

The chest was evidence. This—four people singing and shivering in a hole in a mountain, having lost blood and food and Tomás and almost themselves, and still choosing each other—this was the proof that whatever Vess and his admirals and his fixers had tried to erase, they had failed at the deepest level. You cannot burn what lives in people. They had known it in Veloria. The old woman in the town that forgot its name had known it. My grandfather, guiding a doomed officer up the ice with a box heavier than a coffin, had known it, and had kept the knowledge alive the only way it survives: by handing it to someone young, and trusting.

Tomorrow we would reach the gully mouth. Tomorrow the valley, the village, the radio.

I slept, finally, with my bad arm cradled against my chest like something rescued from a fire.

* * *

We came down out of the throat in the grey of the fourth morning, the storm spent, the air scoured clean and ringing cold. Below us the valley opened—white floor, dark thread of forest, the far smudge of the village smoke—and I let myself feel it for exactly three seconds, the way you allow yourself a breath before the last move of a pitch.

Then I saw the tracks.

They came up the valley from the south—fresh, hours old at most, half-filled with drift but legible: boot prints in military gait, spaced for load, several men moving together. They did not enter the valley and wander. They went directly to the base of the gully, and there they stopped, and waited.

Corven had not needed to cross the icefall. He had only needed to know, as I knew, that the throat was the only other door.

I crouched at the gully mouth with the wind pulling at my hood, and Rafael came up beside me and followed my gaze down, and I watched him understand it—the way his breath stopped, the way his hand went slowly to the strap of the chest on his back.

"They're between us and the village," he said.

"Yes," I said. "They are."
