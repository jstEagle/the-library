## 33. Corven's End

The gully mouth opened onto the moraine like a wound that had finally stopped bleeding, and Rafael stood at the edge of it, blinking against the flat white glare of late afternoon, and understood in one cold instant that they had run out of mountain.

Three days of vertical climbing lay behind them—three days of Sefa's secret route, of chipped handholds and frozen rope and Mirena's breath coming in ragged silver plumes, of Elias hauling himself upward on arms that had never been built for it, of Rafael carrying Tomás's letter against his chest like a second heartbeat. Three days of the gully narrowing above them until they climbed in single file through a slot of blue ice so tight Rafael could touch both walls at once. And now it spilled them out here, onto this broad shoulder of rock and rubble where the glacier bent around the mountain's flank like a river deciding its course, and the descent to the valley lay open below them—a long, gentle ramp of snowfield, two hours maybe, three, and then trees, and then the village with its woodsmoke standing straight up in the still air.

It was too easy. He had known it before his boots were fully out of the gully. The mountain did not give gifts.

"Stop," Sefa said quietly, behind him. She had said almost nothing for three days. Her silence was not emptiness; it was a held breath. "Don't spread out."

Rafael raised a hand. Mirena froze mid-step, her pack straps creaking. Elias, gray-faced and swaying, leaned against a boulder streaked with orange lichen and did not argue, which from Elias was itself an alarm.

The moraine stretched away toward the snowfield, a spine of shattered stone deposited by centuries of ice, boulders the size of houses tumbled among smaller rock, all of it gray and grinding and patient. And at its far end, perhaps four hundred meters distant, where the moraine narrowed between the glacier's edge and a wall of dark cliff—

Men.

Four of them, small with distance but unmistakable: the deliberate, economical way they moved, the rifles carried low and ready, the orange tents already pitched in the lee of the largest boulder. They had come up the snowfield while Rafael's crew clawed down the gully, and they had simply waited, because waiting cost nothing when your quarry had nowhere else to be.

Corven stood apart from them, nearer, maybe two hundred meters off, positioned on a knob of rock with the unhurried posture of a man who had already won. Even at this distance Rafael could see the binoculars lowered now, the faint tilt of the head that meant he was studying them, cataloguing. The navy coat was gone; Corven wore expedition gear now, anonymous and expensive, but he wore it the way he'd worn everything—as a man wearing a costume he considered beneath him.

"There's another way," Mirena whispered. "Tell me there's another way."

"There is no other way." Sefa's voice was flat as slate. She crouched, unshouldering her rifle—the old bolt-action she'd carried since the foothills, the one she'd cleaned every night by stove-light without ever quite explaining. "That snowfield is open ground. We cross it, they pick us off like animals in a meadow. We go back up the gully, we starve. We stay here—" she glanced at the sky, at the sun sliding west behind the peaks, dragging long blue shadows across the ice, "—we freeze."

"So we talk," Elias said. His voice was hoarse from three days of thin air and thinner rations, but steady. "He wants the chest. He's wanted to talk since the harbor."

"He wants us dead," Rafael said. "The talking is just how he prefers to do it."

But even as he said it, he was looking at Corven—at the man's stillness, at the way none of the four trackers had moved to close the distance—and something in the geometry of it bothered him. If Corven wanted them dead, why wait? Why let them stand here in the open, breathing, while the light died? A man with four rifles and open ground does not wait unless waiting serves him.

Unless he thinks time is on his side.

Rafael turned slowly, scanning the slopes above the moraine, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the ice of him. The cliff wall to the east rose sheer and dark, shedding its recent snowfall in long wind-carved slabs. Above the moraine, on the flanking slope, the storm snow of the past week lay thick and unconsolidated over the older, smoother pack—two layers that had never bonded, a slab sitting on a bed of sugar, loaded and waiting, the whole flank of the mountain holding its breath the way Sefa held hers.

And Corven's camp sat directly beneath it.

* * *

Corven came alone.

That was the thing Rafael would remember afterward, the detail he would turn over for years: that Corven sent his trackers back to the tents and walked the four hundred meters of moraine by himself, picking his way between the boulders with a mountaineer's careful tread, his rifle slung, his hands visible, his breath streaming behind him like a banner. A man coming to negotiate. A man who believed, absolutely, in his own position.

He stopped ten meters away. Close enough to talk without shouting. Far enough that everyone's weapons stayed pointed at gravel.

"Sterling." Corven's voice was exactly as Rafael remembered it from the harbor office, from the boat, from every conversation that had been a knife wrapped in velvet: courteous, level, patient. Three days at altitude had carved him leaner, weathered his face, but the eyes hadn't changed. They were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guys," Rafael said.

Something flickered at the corner of Corven's mouth—not quite a smile. Behind Rafael, he heard Sefa shift her weight, heard the soft click of her rifle's safety going off, deliberate, audible. A message. Corven ignored it.

"I'm going to make this simple," Corven said. "Because we're all tired, and the light's going, and I have no interest in shooting anyone on this godforsaken rock if I don't have to. You give me the chest. All of it—the documents, the film, whatever you found down there in that cave. You give it to me, and you walk. All five of you." His gaze touched each of them in turn: Rafael, Sefa, Mirena, Elias, and lingered a half-second on the empty space where Tomás should have been, and Rafael saw him register the absence, and file it. "Down the snowfield, into the valley, gone. No one follows you. Vess gets his proof back, you get your lives. That's the trade."

"And if we say no?" Mirena asked. Her voice cracked on the last word, but she didn't look away, and Rafael felt something twist in his chest—pride, fierce and useless.

Corven looked at her for a long moment. "Then tomorrow morning my people take it from your bodies. And the report I write says you died in an avalanche. Mountains kill people all the time up here. It's practically a service I'm performing—keeping the statistics honest." He said it without heat, without cruelty even, and that was the worst part. It was logistics. It was a line item. "So. The chest."

"The chest isn't here," Rafael said.

Corven's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Excuse me?"

"It's not on the mountain anymore. We split it. What you want is already in the valley, already moving, and killing us doesn't get it back—it just makes the tribunal very interested in the men who killed four witnesses on a glacier." It was a bluff, mostly. One copy of the film rode against Mirena's ribs, wrapped in oilcloth. But Corven couldn't know how much of what Rafael said was true, and doubt was a currency, and Rafael had learned to spend it.

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—Rafael thought it might work. Corven's jaw worked. His eyes went distant, recalculating, and Rafael watched the arithmetic stall behind them.

Then Corven exhaled through his nose, slow and controlled, and shook his head once. "No. You're lying. You're a diver, Sterling—you think in terms of salvage, of things that can be carried. Nothing's left this mountain. There's been no traffic on the southern approach in nine days. I checked." He took a step closer, and his voice dropped, and for the first time Rafael heard something underneath the courtesy—not anger, exactly. Frustration. The frustration of a professional whose clean solution keeps refusing to resolve. "I've read your file. Salvage diver, no family, no politics, no history. You were supposed to be the easiest kind of problem. You were supposed to take the money." He paused. "What happened?"

Rafael thought about it honestly, because the question deserved honesty. What happened? A chest in a river. A ledger full of numbers where names should have been. An archivist above a bookshop with fire-scarred hands. A translator who had spent thirty years looking for a father who was only ever a number. A smuggler who owed the dead a debt and paid it on a moraine with a flare pistol in his fist.

"People," Rafael said. "People happened."

Corven stared at him. And then, distantly, absurdly, the mountain answered first.

* * *

It began as a sound Rafael felt rather than heard—a deep, subterranean crack, somewhere above and to the east, like the mountain clearing its throat. Everyone's head turned. On the cliff face, high up where the storm slabs lay, something shifted. A seam of powder snow released and slid, small, harmless, a white ribbon unspooling down the rock and dissipating on the wind.

Loose snow. A settling. Happens a hundred times a day on any mountain.

But Corven's trackers, jumpy after days in hostile country, reacted like soldiers do. Two of them scrambled for cover behind their boulder. One raised his rifle. And the fourth—Rafael never learned his name, never knew which of the four he was—did the thing that sealed everything: he fired a flare.

Whether it was nerves or protocol or simple panic, the flare went up with a whump and burst red above the moraine, and the sound of it—a sharp concussive pop echoing off the cliffs—rolled up the eastern slope like a hand slapped against a sleeping giant.

Above, on the loaded flank, the mountain woke.

Rafael heard it start: a low rumble, deepening, the sound of tons of snow beginning to move as one body. Then the slope itself seemed to shudder, and a fracture line shot across the slab like lightning drawn in white, and the entire face of the mountainside let go.

"DOWN!" Sefa screamed. "BEHIND THE ROCKS! GO GO GO!"

The world became noise. Rafael grabbed Mirena by the pack and hurled her toward the biggest boulder within reach, diving after her, slamming his back against stone as the avalanche came down the flank in a boiling white torrent. It hit the moraine two hundred meters east of them with the force of a collapsing building—a rolling, thundering mass of slabs and powder that swallowed Corven's camp whole. The orange tents vanished. The boulder the trackers hid behind might as well have been a grain of sand. Rafael caught one glimpse of the men running—running flat-out along the moraine ahead of the slide, and for a heartbeat he thought they might outrun it, the slide's path angling away from them—

Then the slide hit the glacier's edge and banked, spreading, and the white tongue lapped out across the moraine and took them. One moment they were running; the next the surface of the world simply closed over them, smooth and terrible, and the roar rolled on and on, minutes of it, the air filling with snow-dust that turned the afternoon to fog.

When the sound finally died, the silence rang worse than the noise.

Rafael lifted his head. The dust hung everywhere, fine as flour, settling on his eyelashes, in his mouth. Where Corven's camp had been there was only a vast smooth apron of debris, featureless, fifty meters deep in places, stretching from the cliff base halfway across the glacier. No tents. No tracks. No men.

No Corven.

"Corven!" Rafael was on his feet before he'd decided to move, stumbling out onto the fresh debris, sinking to his knees in the settled snow. "CORVEN!"

Nothing. Just the ringing quiet, and the dust sifting down like ash.

Behind him, Sefa's voice, low and grim: "Rafael. Don't."

But he was already digging. Because he'd seen it—in the last second before the slide buried everything, a flash of movement far to the left of the trackers' path, near the cliff base where the slide's edge thinned: a lone figure, thrown clear or lucky, crawling. And now, in the settling dust, Rafael saw it again—a dark shape against the white, half-buried, one arm moving weakly.

He dug. Ten minutes of bare-handed digging through set snow, Mirena and Elias joining him, until they uncovered Corven to the waist—one arm broken, hanging wrong, his face bloodied, his rifle gone, his eyes open and fixed on the sky with the blank astonishment of a man who has just watched his own certainty die. The trackers were gone. Truly gone. Somewhere under fifty meters of mountain, and nothing was digging them out.

Corven looked up at Rafael, and Rafael braced for hatred, for curses, for the arithmetic to resume.

Instead Corven started to laugh. It was a horrible sound—wet, disbelieving, edged with shock. "You—" he coughed, spat pink. "You pulled me out. I was going to kill you. I was going to write you down as an avalanche statistic, and you—" Another laugh, breaking apart. "Why?"

Rafael knelt in the snow beside him, and the answer rose in him from somewhere deeper than thought—from the river, from the ledger, from every name he'd read aloud to strangers in a snow cave. Because someone had to stop counting bodies and start counting names. Because vengeance was just another way of letting the dead own you. Because Tomás had spent thirty years running toward this and paid for it with everything, and what Tomás had bought with his life was not supposed to end with Rafael kneeling over a broken man with murder in reach and choosing it.

"Because the tribunal needs witnesses," Rafael said. "Dead men can't testify. And you're going to testify, Corven. Every name. Every order. Everything Vess told you to bury." He reached out and gripped Corven's collar, not cruelly, but firmly, the way you hold something you refuse to lose. "I'm not saving your life. I'm confiscating it. It belongs to the record now."

Corven stared at him. The laughter had died. Something moved behind his eyes—something that might, in a different life, in a different man, have been relief.

"You're insane," Corven whispered.

"Probably," Rafael said. "Help me get him up. Sefa—splint that arm. Mirena, rope."

They bound the broken arm against his chest, tied his hands—not tightly, just enough, a formality more than a restraint, because a man with one working arm and no rifle and fifty meters of mountain between him and his employers was not going anywhere—and got him upright between them. Corven swayed, looked back once at the smooth white apron where his team slept under the snow, and did not look again.

They started down the snowfield as the light failed, five figures and a prisoner descending through the blue dusk, the mountain silent behind them at last. Mirena walked beside Rafael, and after a while she said, quietly, so only he could hear:

"That wasn't mercy."

"No," Rafael admitted. "Mercy would've been easier."

She nodded slowly, understanding. Killing a man ends him. Saving him for the record—that makes him carry it. Down the snowfield, in the gathering dark, Corven stumbled between his captors, and every step was a step toward a courtroom he could already see waiting for him at the bottom of the world.

By the time they reached the tree line, the first stars were out, and woodsmoke drifted up from the village below, and Mirena squeezed Rafael's arm once—brief, hard, wordless.

Tomorrow they would reach the village. Tomorrow the snows would seal the pass behind them, sealing Corven in with them, and the long winter of testimony would begin.

But tonight, for the first time since the river, Rafael Sterling felt the strange, unfamiliar weight of having chosen right instead of easy—and found, to his surprise, that he could carry it.
