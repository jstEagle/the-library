## 34. Snowbound Village

The village had no name on any map Mirena had ever seen, and by the time they staggered into it, she understood why. Some places survive precisely by refusing to be written down.

It sat in a bowl of the valley like a cupped hand: forty-odd houses of timber and stone, roofs bowed under snow, smoke rising from chimneys in thin gray threads that stood almost vertical in the windless cold. Behind it, the pass they had come through was already closing—a white curtain of spindrift pouring off the ridgeline, erasing the route hour by hour, as if the mountain were tidying up after them, folding away the evidence of their crossing.

Mirena's legs stopped working somewhere near the first fence post. She didn't fall so much as sit, abruptly and completely, in a drift to her waist, and found she lacked both the will and the strength to stand again.

"I think," she said, "that I live here now."

Sefa Anand, ten meters ahead with her rope coiled over one shoulder and frost in her eyelashes, turned back. For a moment she said nothing—Sefa rarely wasted words, especially not kind ones—and then she walked back, took Mirena under the arms, and hauled her upright with the effortless strength of someone who had been carrying people for three days.

"Not yet," Sefa said. "First you eat. Then you can live wherever you want."

Rafael came up behind them, half-carrying Elias Brandt, whose long archivist's frame had gone stiff and unreliable in the cold, his hands curled into claws he could no longer fully open. Tomás was not with them. Nobody said this. The absence walked beside them the whole way down the valley, taking up more room than a man ever had.

The villagers emerged the way people do in high country—slowly, from doorways, assessing. An old man with a face like weathered root wood looked at Sefa, then at the strangers, then at the sky, and made a decision with a single nod. Doors opened. There was soup. There was a stove in the communal house large enough to warm all of them at once, and Mirena remembered thinking, with a strange distant clarity, that she had never in her life been so grateful for heat, or so unable to feel it.

Her body had spent everything it had. She ate what was put in front of her—barley soup, dark bread, tea with something resinous in it—and wept without sound through most of it, tears running down into the bowl, while around her the villagers talked quietly about weather and sheep and nothing at all, granting her the mercy of pretending not to notice.

* * *

They learned the shape of their captivity over the next two days, mostly from the old man, whose name was Davor and who had been the village's unofficial keeper of weather for longer than anyone could remember.

"The pass is shut," he told them, standing at the window of the communal house with his hands behind his back, watching the ridgeline pour white. "Shut early, too. That storm you ran ahead of—it settled in. Nothing goes over now until the thaw. April, maybe May."

"April," Elias repeated. He was wrapped in three blankets by the stove, his fingers slowly recovering, and there was something in his voice between horror and relief. "That's eleven weeks."

"Twelve," Davor said. "If April comes late, which it does."

Mirena watched Rafael absorb this. He stood apart, near the door out of habit, and she saw him do the arithmetic she was doing—the hunters behind them, Corven bound and feverish in the woodshed under guard, the evidence in their packs—and arrive at the same strange conclusion she did.

Trapped meant safe. No one could follow them over the pass either. Whatever Corven's masters wanted done, they would have to wait until spring, and by then—

By then, if Mirena did her work right, it would be too late for anyone.

"We need paper," she said. "Ink. A table. And every hour of light we have."

* * *

The copying took nine days.

They worked in the loft above the communal house, where the roof sloped low and the stove pipe threw a column of warmth, and they worked like people possessed, because they were. The chest's contents had survived thirty years in the ice—film reels, photographs sealed in waxed packets, and the officer's testimony, pages of careful Velorian script naming names, dates, methods, orders given and orders obeyed—all the way up to Admiral Vess himself, then a young captain signing transport manifests with a steady hand.

But the ice that preserved it could also take it. One fire, one flood, one careless moment, and thirty years of waiting would end in smoke. So they copied it four ways.

Elias handled the documents, transcribing the testimony in duplicate, his ruined hands cramping until Mirena massaged them each night with melted tallow, complaining the whole time about archival standards and the criminal absence of acid-free paper in mountain villages. Mirena translated—the Velorian script yielding to her tribunal-trained eye faster than even Elias expected, the notation system he'd taught her unlocking page after page. Rafael built frames and packed the film against damage, learning the chemistry from Elias with the focused patience of a man who had spent years reading water and now read emulsion instead. And Sefa organized, rationed, guarded, and kept them fed, moving through the loft like a quiet engine.

Four copies. One would stay buried in the village, in a cache Sefa's family knowledge helped design. One would go with each of them when the pass opened—separate routes, separate hands, so that no single fire, no single ambush, no single betrayal could end it.

On the sixth day, Mirena copied her father's name for the fourth time.

*Prisoner 41 — Aldo Kovač — carried second chest, Cordillera crossing, winter of the last year.*

She had known it for weeks now. She had said it aloud once, at the high camp, and Elias had held her while she shook. But writing it, copying it, making it multiply across pages that would scatter to four corners of the world—that was different. Each time her pen moved through those letters, Aldo Kovač became slightly harder to erase. Thirty years of official silence, undone by a daughter with a pen in a snowbound loft.

She finished the line, set down the pen, and pressed her ink-stained fingers flat against the page.

"There," she said softly, to no one. "Now they have to say it too."

* * *

Corven nearly died in the second week.

The wound in his side, sustained on the moraine and neglected through the descent, turned septic. Sefa found him shivering under his blankets in the woodshed, skin hot and gray, and stood over him for a long moment with an expression Mirena couldn't read.

"He dies, we answer for it," Sefa said finally. "And I didn't drag him off that mountain to let rot finish what the ice started."

So they saved him. Sefa lanced the wound with a boiled knife; the village herbalist brought moss dressings and willow bark; Rafael sat with him through the worst nights, because someone had to, and because—as he admitted later, in the flat voice he used for confessions—he needed to look at the man sent to bury the truth and confirm that he was only a man.

Corven surfaced from the fever on the ninth day, weak as a kitten, and lay staring at the rafters of the woodshed.

"You should have left me," he rasped, when Rafael brought him water.

"Yes," Rafael agreed. "Probably."

"Why didn't you?"

Rafael considered the question honestly, which Mirena had learned was his way. "Because I've spent three years being nobody to anybody. It turns out I'm bad at it now." He set the cup down. "When you're well enough, you're going to tell a tribunal everything you know. That's why."

Corven laughed—a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it. "You think Vess lets witnesses live?"

"I think," Rafael said, "that Vess is about to learn what happens when you try to burn something that's already in four places at once."

* * *

The laughter returned in the third week, and it surprised them all.

It began small. Elias discovered the village kept a shelf of books in the communal house—forty volumes, salt-damaged, arranged with loving disorder—and appointed himself their librarian within a day, cataloguing them on scraps of wrapping paper and delivering scathing lectures on their condition to anyone who would listen. Davor listened, delighted, and began bringing him more things to catalogue: a box of buttons, a broken clock, his grandfather's letters. Elias, exiled archivist, rediscovered his vocation in a room the size of a kitchen, and Mirena caught him one evening explaining proper storage of correspondence to two fascinated children, his hands flying, his face lit like a lamp.

Then Rafael fixed the mill.

He hadn't meant to. He'd been studying the frozen waterwheel the way he studied everything, with silent attention, and one morning he simply started taking it apart, and by afternoon it was turning again, groaning and cracking the ice, and the whole village came out to watch it run like it was a festival. Davor's wife pressed a jar of honey into his hands and refused to hear a word of protest. Rafael stood there holding the jar like it might detonate, and Mirena saw the exact moment the loneliness he'd carried for three years cracked open—not all at once, but visibly, like river ice in thaw.

He started eating meals with the rest of them after that. At the same table. Without being asked.

Even Sefa softened, in her way. She spent the weeks teaching Mirena to read the snowpack, walking her out past the fences each morning, showing her how the wind wrote its history on the drifts. "Your father crossed this range," she said one day, apropos of nothing, eyes on the ridgeline. "In worse conditions than ours. With a box heavier than a coffin." She glanced sideways. "You come from strong stock, translator. You should know it."

Mirena looked out at the mountains that had taken her father and given her back his name, and felt something settle in her chest that she suspected was peace—not the absence of grief, but grief finally given a place to live.

* * *

One evening in the fifth week, the whole crew ended up in the communal house while a storm rattled the shutters, and Davor brought out a bottle of plum brandy that had been waiting, he said, for an occasion worth forgetting responsibly.

They drank. They talked. And somewhere between the second glass and the third, Tomás Iriarte entered the room—not his body, which lay under a cairn on the moraine where Sefa had built it, but his presence, summoned inevitably by the brandy and the company.

Elias told the story of Tomás's letter—the soldier ordered to burn the Velorian archive who refused and ran, spending thirty years flying cargo and contraband, running toward a redemption he never believed he'd reach. Rafael described the diversion at the moraine: the flare cache going up in orange bloom, Tomás standing tall in the open, waving his arms and shouting insults at the trackers with theatrical contempt, buying them ninety seconds with his life. Mirena found herself laughing and crying at once, which seemed appropriate.

"He'd have hated this village," Rafael said. "No airstrip."

"He'd have found one," Sefa said. "Or made one."

"He'd have catalogued these books wrong just to annoy Elias," Mirena added, and Elias raised his glass in solemn agreement—"Deliberately. Alphabetically by color"—and the laughter that went around the table was loud enough that the storm outside seemed briefly irrelevant.

Tomás's name went onto the wheelhouse of nothing yet—but Mirena promised it silently, there at the table, that it would. Somewhere, someday, a boat or a plane or a foundation with his name painted where the weather couldn't reach it. The dead of Veloria were owed more than justice. They were owed remembrance, loud and permanent and impossible to burn.

* * *

Near the end of the seventh week, with the copies complete and hidden and distributed among their packs, Mirena climbed to the village's highest point—a shrine of stacked stones above the last house—and looked north toward the pass.

It was gone. Not closed—gone. The ridge she had crossed on her own two feet, bleeding and terrified and alive, had vanished beneath fifty feet of winter. Somewhere under all that white lay the icefall corridor, the officer's camp, the melt-cave with its bronze-bound chest, the cairn where Tomás slept. The mountain had folded it all away, keeping it, the way rivers keep wrecks and archives keep ash.

In spring, she would walk back into the world with her father's name in her pocket and four copies of the truth in four directions. In spring, the tribunal would receive what it had waited thirty years for. In spring, Admiral Vess would learn that some fires only scatter the sparks.

But that was spring. Tonight there was only the storm, the village, the lamplight in the windows below, and the voices of her unlikely family drifting up from the communal house—Elias lecturing, Rafael laughing, Sefa's low steady answers—and Mirena stood a moment longer in the falling snow, letting the cold hold her, memorizing the feeling of being exactly where she was supposed to be.

Then she heard footsteps on the shrine stones behind her, and turned, expecting Sefa.

It was Rafael, breath steaming, something in his hands.

"Radio office got a signal through the storm," he said. "Davor's boy runs it. Shortwave, patched and crackling." He held out a slip of paper, snow already settling on it. "It's addressed to you. Tribunal seal on the transmission header."

Mirena took the paper. Her fingers, still ink-stained, trembled slightly as she unfolded it.

The message was short. Three lines. But the third line made the blood sing in her ears and the winter suddenly feel very close:

*Transmission received. Authentication confirmed. Tribunal convenes extraordinary session on receipt of physical evidence. Be advised: subject VESS has filed motion to block session. Hearing scheduled — 40 days.*

Forty days. The pass wouldn't open for another month after that.

Below her, the lamplit windows glowed. Above her, the snow fell on the buried pass, patient as an archive.

Mirena folded the paper carefully, the way Elias had taught her to handle anything irreplaceable, and smiled into the dark.

"Then we'd better make sure," she said, "that our copies travel faster than his lawyers."
