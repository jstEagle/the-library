## 35. Testimony

The radio office in Kaltbrunn occupied the ground floor of what had once been the village's telegraph station, a stone building with a slate roof furred green with lichen and a door that stuck in winter. Inside, it smelled of cold iron, machine oil, and decades of pipe smoke soaked into the plaster. A single window looked out on the village square, where snow was falling again—soft, unhurried, indifferent—the kind of snow that meant the pass would stay shut for months.

Elias Brandt stood at that window with a cup of tea going cold in his hands and watched Mirena prepare to end his life as he knew it.

She didn't know that's what she was doing. Or perhaps she did, in her precise, careful way. She sat at the operator's desk with her back straight as a plumb line, sleeves rolled to the elbow, arranging documents in the order she'd rehearsed three times since dawn: ledger facsimiles, photograph negatives sealed in wax paper, the officer's testimony copied out in Elias's own hand because the original could not be risked twice over the airwaves. Four complete sets. Four destinations. The tribunal in Meridian. The *Meridian Courier*, the *Coastal Standard*, the *Alpenwald Gazette*. And the Descendants' Association of Velorian Victims, an organization of eleven hundred families that had met for thirty years in borrowed halls, keeping lists no government would acknowledge.

Four sets. That was the arithmetic Mirena had insisted on, tapping the table with one finger. *One set can be burned. Two can be stolen. Four means there is always one more copy than they have hands.*

Old Henrik Voss, the radio operator—a retired man with a white beard and hearing aids he turned off when his wife talked too long—had been paid in firewood, mutton, and gratitude. He'd shown them the transmitter's range, the frequencies, the authentication protocols for tribunal traffic. He'd asked exactly one question, which was whether what they were sending would make trouble, and when Mirena said yes, he'd nodded slowly and said, "Good. This valley's had thirty quiet years. Quiet's just another word for buried."

Now Henrik sat by the stove with his mended socks and his newspaper, pointedly not listening, while the wind worried the eaves and the antenna wire hummed faintly above the roof like a struck string that wouldn't quite stop ringing.

"You're sure about the order?" Mirena asked, not looking up. It wasn't really a question. She'd asked it four times.

"Tribunal first," Elias said. "Then the newspapers, staggered by twenty minutes so none of them can claim exclusive rights and bury it. The association last."

"Why last?"

"Because they're last," Elias said. "Because for thirty years everyone else has gone first—the governments, the archives, the men who signed the orders. Let the families be the ones who receive it knowing it's already loose in the world. Let them open it like people opening a window after a long illness."

Mirena considered this. Then she wrote it down, because Mirena wrote everything down, and Elias loved her a little for it, the way you love a lock for doing precisely what it was made to do.

He turned back to the window. Across the square, smoke rose from the bakery chimney. Two children were building a fort against the church wall, arguing about architecture. Rafael was helping them—not officially, not consciously; he'd simply drifted over an hour ago the way he drifted toward any task that let his hands work while his mind went somewhere else—and now the fort had a proper glacis and a drainage channel, because Rafael Sterling could not build anything without engineering it against flood.

Sefa was in the stable, re-wrapping her rope coils for the fourth time, unable to sit still now that the mountain had released them into stillness. And Tomás—

Tomás was everywhere and nowhere. His name was written in pencil inside the cover of the second document case, where he'd scrawled *property of T. Iriarte, if found please return to nearest bar* during some idle hour in the snow cave. His letter was folded into Elias's breast pocket, over his heart, where he'd carried it since the gully. He owed Tomás a debt he intended to pay in the only currency left: the truth, transmitted, unburnable.

"Frequency's warm," Henrik said from the stove, without turning around. "Take your time. Air's clear tonight. You'll reach Meridian like shouting across a lake."

* * *

They began at dusk.

The tribunal transmission came first, and it was the slowest—authentication codes exchanged in the old Velorian cipher that Elias had spent two years of his disgrace reconstructing from fragments, the same notation system the ledger used, the system the perpetrators had believed died with their country. There was a poetry in that, Elias thought grimly. They had burned the archive and dissolved the nation and laundered their names into ministries and admiralties, and all of it undone by a notation they'd assumed was too obscure to matter, preserved by a disgraced archivist nobody had thought worth watching.

The key clicked. The signal went out into the dark above the mountains, past the closed pass, past the icefall that had nearly killed them, past the moraine where Tomás lay under a cairn Sefa had built with her bare hands.

Mirena read the manifest aloud in a low, level voice as the encoder processed it—names, dates, cargo numbers, the transport records of prisoners who had never arrived anywhere. Prisoner 41. Prisoner 17. Prisoner 88. Her father's name—his true name, restored to him at a high camp on the Cordillera del Viento—she read last, and her voice did not break, but it changed register, the way water changes sound when it passes over a deeper stone.

Elias watched the needle of the transmitter's power gauge tremble with each burst and thought: *somewhere south of here, in a warm room full of lawyers, a machine is writing this down.* Somewhere, men who had built careers on the comfortable fiction that Veloria was never real were about to receive, authenticated and timestamped, documentary proof that it had been real, and that its crimes had addresses, and that some of those addresses were theirs.

"There," Mirena said, when the acknowledgment tone came back through the static. "The tribunal has it. Timestamped, logged, receipted." She allowed herself one breath, then moved to the next frequency. "Newspapers."

* * *

The *Courier* took forty minutes. The *Standard* took less, its night editor apparently a woman who asked no questions and confirmed receipt in three words. The *Gazette* was asleep and would find everything waiting at dawn, which suited Elias fine; let the morning edition land on breakfast tables like frost on glass.

Between transmissions, Henrik made tea and refused, again, to ask questions. Rafael came in snow-crusted and silent, stood behind Mirena's chair for a moment watching the documents go out into the world one packet at a time, and then said quietly, "Do you feel different?"

"No," Mirena said. "That's how I know it's real. Real things don't feel like anything at first. They feel like weather."

Rafael nodded as though this were the wisest thing he'd ever heard, and went back out to the children and their fort, and Elias understood that Rafael was asking a different question entirely—whether the invisible life was truly over—and that Mirena's answer had given him permission to grieve it.

The last transmission was the hardest.

The Descendants' Association's frequency was an old shortwave band, maintained for decades on donations and stubbornness, a channel that had carried nothing but names for thirty years—families reading the lists of their missing into the static on every anniversary, because reading a name aloud was the only monument anyone had ever permitted them. Elias had listened to those broadcasts in exile, alone above the bookshop, night after night, telling himself it was research. It had not been research. It had been penance.

"Now?" Mirena asked, her hand on the key.

"Now," Elias said.

She sent the full set: the ledger, the photographs, the officer's testimony, and beneath it all, appended in Elias's hand, the complete list of names—every prisoner, every number, matched wherever possible to recovered identities. Forty-one lines. He had written them out that morning while the others slept, and his handwriting had shaken only once, at line forty-one.

The acknowledgment came back in nine minutes. Not a machine tone. A human voice, old and cracked, reading back the authentication code and then saying, in accented but formal Velorian, the language of a country that no longer existed:

*"Received. Confirmed. We have waited thirty years. Tell whoever sent this: we never stopped listening."*

Mirena put her head down on her arms on the desk and wept without sound, the way people weep who learned young that grief must be quiet to survive. Elias put his hand on her shoulder and looked out the window at the falling snow, at the lit windows of a village that had forgotten its own name, and felt something in his chest come loose that had been clenched since the day they burned his career and told him to disappear quietly.

*There is no way now to burn it all.* Four copies, scattered like seed. Three newspapers, a tribunal, eleven hundred families. Somewhere in a capital office, Admiral Vess might already be reaching for the telephone—but whatever he ordered now would be too late, and both of them knew it. You could burn an archive. You could not burn a broadcast. You could not burn the memory of eleven hundred people who had just been told, after thirty years, *you were right, it happened, here are the names.*

"They'll come for us eventually," Mirena said at last, wiping her face with the efficiency of someone resuming work. "When the pass opens. Corven's people, or worse."

"Yes," Elias said. "But they'll come to witnesses now. Not ghosts." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, like a coat returned from storage. "I spent twelve years being invisible on purpose. Tonight I ended it deliberately, on my own terms, with receipts." He tapped the transmit log. "Every packet timestamped. Every destination confirmed. If anything happens to us now, it proves nothing except their guilt. Invisibility was their gift to themselves. Visibility is ours."

Outside, the children had abandoned the fort for dinner. Rafael stood alone in the square, looking up at the antenna wire humming against the sky, and raised one hand—not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. A man saying goodbye to a way of living and meaning it.

Henrik turned his hearing aids back on, poured three glasses of the plum brandy he kept for funerals and weddings, and said, "Well. Whatever that was, the whole valley will know by morning. Old Petrov still listens to that band." He raised his glass. "To the ones who never stopped listening."

They drank. The snow fell. Somewhere far to the north, in offices with good lighting, telephones were beginning to ring.

And in the radio office in Kaltbrunn, under the hum of the cooling transmitter, Elias Brandt opened Tomás's letter one more time, read the words of a man who had refused to burn an archive thirty years ago and had finally, at the cost of his life, delivered it instead, and whispered into the empty room, in the dead language of a dead country:

"It's done, old friend. Nothing left to hide."

The transmitter light blinked once, twice, and went dark—as if the machine itself had exhaled.

Three days later, the first reply came down the mountain with the mail sledge: an envelope bearing the seal of the International Tribunal at Meridian, addressed not to a title or an alias but to *Elias Brandt, Archivist*—the first time in twelve years anyone had called him that in print.

Inside was a summons.

And stapled to it, a single sheet of tribunal letterhead, unsigned, marked *EYES ONLY*: a list of names under active investigation—including one Admiral A. Vess—and a handwritten note in the corner in blue ink:

*We received everything. So did they. Come home before they come to you.*
