## 36. The Tribunal Summons

The mountain let them go the way a fist opens—slowly, finger by finger, each ridge of snow surrendering its grip on the road below.

Rafael felt it first as sound. All winter the village had lived inside a hush so complete he could hear his own pulse in it, and then one morning in late March there was water. Water ticking off the eaves of Sefa's cousin's house. Water muttering under the crust of the packed snow in the lane. Water running somewhere far down-valley with the long, insistent voice of a thing that had been waiting all winter to speak. He lay in the narrow bed beneath the slanted ceiling and listened until the light came up gray through the frost-ferned window, and he understood that the pass had decided.

By noon the whole village knew it too. The old men stood at the bridge over the melt-swollen stream and squinted down the valley at the dark seam of road emerging from the drifts, and the children ran the length of the snow-wall on either side of it, trailing their mittens along the top like runners along a rail. Somewhere a dog that had spent four months as a rumor became audible again. The world was reassembling itself piece by piece, and Rafael stood in the doorway with a tin cup of chicory cooling in his hands and found, to his surprise, that he was not afraid of it.

He had been afraid all winter. That was the honest accounting, the one he'd learned to keep during the whiteout when lying to yourself was just another way of freezing. He'd been afraid in the snow cave when Tomás's breathing went shallow and ragged, afraid on the gully wall with Mirena's weight on the rope above him and the drop yawning below, afraid at the gully mouth when Corven stood in the falling snow with a pistol and the mountain groaned overhead like a ship breaking up. But those fears had been clean. They had shapes. You could put your hands against them and push.

What frightened him now was flatter and wider: the valley opening, the road opening, the world with its telegraph poles and its newspapers and its tribunals coming back up the mountain to collect them.

* * *

They descended over two days, the five of them and Sefa's cousin's mule, down a road the thaw had chewed into something between a path and an argument. Elias rode the mule for the last stretch, insisting he didn't need to and accepting no contradiction, his bad knee propped on a rolled blanket, a paperback dictionary of Velorian legal terminology open on his lap that he'd carried up the mountain in his pack along with everything else he refused to leave behind. Mirena walked the whole way. She walked the way she did everything now—deliberately, chin level, as if the ground were a document she was reading closely for the first time. She had not stopped translating since the radio office. Rafael had watched her do it some nights after the others slept: sitting by the stove with the photostats spread around her, moving her lips, converting the ledger's dry columns into the language of the tribunal, into testimony, into names said aloud in rooms with microphones. Prisoner 41 had a name now. Anton Kovač. She said it sometimes in her sleep, softly, the way you'd wake a sleeping person rather than startle them.

Sefa led. She always led; it seemed less a choice than a fact of her body, the way she read the slope ahead three turns before it arrived. Her grandfather's route, her grandfather's guilt, her grandfather's mountains—and she carried all of it without complaint, which Rafael understood now was its own kind of grief, the kind that doesn't make noise.

And Tomás came down the mountain too, though not the way any of them wanted. His ashes rode in a biscuit tin wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of Rafael's pack, because Tomás Iriarte had left instructions once, drunk and laughing in a border-town bar, that if anything ever happened to him he wanted to be scattered somewhere with a view of a runway, and the only runway within reach was the cracked strip at the valley mouth where the mail plane landed twice a month. It wasn't much of a memorial. Tomás would have complained about it at length, and then flown them anywhere they asked.

The village at the valley floor smelled of mud and woodsmoke and, faintly, of green—the first green any of them had seen since autumn, willow catkins along the stream bank like small held breaths. And waiting for them, wedged under the door of the single-room post office with a stone against the wind, were the envelopes.

Four of them. Heavy cream paper, official crests embossed in the corners, addressed in a clerk's immaculate hand. One for each of them. Sefa took hers without expression and slit it with a thumbnail. Elias held his up to the light first, the old archivist's reflex, checking for tampering, and laughed—a short, disbelieving bark—when he saw the seal.

"The International Commission," he said. "Not the national court. Not the navy's own judge-and-pardon machinery. The Commission." He looked at Rafael over the envelope's edge, and behind the exhaustion in his face something was burning. "Do you understand what that means? They can't bury this one. There are eleven nations on that commission and half of them owe Vess nothing and the other half hate him."

Mirena opened hers standing very still, the way she'd once told him she opened letters about her father—for years there had only ever been one kind of letter possible, and her hands had learned to brace for it. She read hers twice. Then she passed it to Rafael without a word, and he read it, and understood why.

*SUMMONS TO APPEAR BEFORE THE INTERNATIONAL COMMISSION OF INQUIRY INTO THE DISSOLUTION OF THE VELORIAN STATE, SESSION FORTY-SEVEN, AS WITNESS AND FINDER OF FACT.*

Finder of fact. Him. A salvage diver whose formal education had ended at fifteen, whose name appeared in no registry he'd ever bothered to check, who had spent three years constructing a life out of exactly the things institutions couldn't see. They wanted him in a hearing room, under oath, on the record.

His hands were steady holding the paper. That surprised him most of all.

* * *

The news had traveled faster than the mail. The village inn—four tables, a stove, a landlady named Petra who had fed them their first hot meal in October and never once asked what they'd come down the mountain carrying—had a radio, and by evening every table had heard it. The capital station, broadcasting the session list for spring.

"Admiral Edvard Vess," the announcer said, in the flat tone announcers use for the largest facts, "retired, formerly Chief of Naval Logistics, was taken into custody yesterday morning at his residence pursuant to warrants issued by the Commission. He is charged with crimes against humanity in connection with the transport and disposition of prisoners in the final year of the Velorian war."

Petra turned the radio down, then up again. Nobody ate.

"And in related proceedings," the announcer continued, "the man known publicly as Corven—identified to the Commission as Dario Corven, formerly of the Naval Intelligence Directorate—has agreed to testify for the prosecution. Sources close to the inquiry describe his cooperation as, quote, extensive."

Elias set down his spoon. "Extensive," he repeated. "He gave us up in the gully and they're calling it extensive." But there was no real bitterness in it. Rafael had watched him bind Corven's wrists with climbing tape while the avalanche debris still smoked below them, had watched him look a long time at the man who'd burned—or ordered burned, or profited from the burning of—every archive Elias had given his life to, and Rafael had watched him choose. *You come down as a witness,* Elias had said, hauling him upright out of the snow. *That's the only version of you that walks out of these mountains.* Corven had wept, or maybe the wind had just been in his eyes. It hardly mattered now. What mattered was that somewhere in a cell in the capital, the man who counted things was counting for the other side.

"They'll give him a deal," Mirena said quietly. "A reduced sentence for full testimony. That's how it works."

"And?" Sefa said.

"And it works," Mirena said. "It isn't clean. Nothing about this is going to be clean. But it works." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "My father's name is going to be entered into a permanent international record because a man who helped hunt us decided to save himself. I've made my peace with the arithmetic. Mostly."

Rafael said nothing. He was watching the window, where the last light was going copper off the wet fields, and doing the thing he'd done all winter whenever the future got loud: measuring it in distances. The capital was six hundred river miles and one mountain range away. The hearing was nineteen days off. The room where he would stand up and say what he'd pulled from the river was, right now, a fact like any other fact—so many miles, so many days—and somehow that made it bearable. Things with measurements could be crossed. He'd crossed worse with less.

* * *

He climbed to the ridge above the village on the third morning, alone, the way he'd begun allowing himself to do—one hour, marked, back before anyone worried. The trail switchbacked through melting drifts, and everywhere the snow was letting go of things: a fencepost, a rusted sled runner, a boot from some earlier decade, black and hollow. The mountain was returning its inventory. By summer it would have given back everything except what it kept.

At the top he sat on a warm rock with the whole valley laid out below—the river, silvering in the sun, running north toward the lowlands, toward the town where his boat lay scuttled in the shallows where he'd sunk her, toward the harbor office with its evasions and the pier where Corven had once offered him money for fishing equipment. Six hundred miles of water between here and that life. He tried to feel the pull of it, the old gravity of the solitary life, the dive-hours and river-miles that had measured him so comfortably for three years, and found it strangely faint. Like a radio station drifting out of range.

Because the truth—the one he'd confessed in the snow cave with the stove hissing and Tomás coughing in the corner—was that invisibility hadn't been peace. It had been anesthesia. He had gone under the water and stayed under it because down there nothing could ask him to belong to anything. The river didn't need him to be brave or loyal or present. It only needed him to be useful, and usefulness was a loneliness you could live inside, the way you could live inside a wetsuit, briefly, at depth.

Tomás had called him on it the first week they'd known each other, back when Tomás was still alive and still pretending to be merely a smuggler with a debt. *You dive like a man hiding,* he'd said, feet on the dashboard of the plane, and Rafael had laughed it off, and Tomás had let him, because that was the kindness Tomás specialized in: telling you the truth early enough that you had time to grow around it.

Well. He'd grown around it. And now the Commission wanted him in a marble room with stenographers, and the choice—the one Corven had never been given, the one Vess had spent thirty years making sure nobody got—was his to make in the open.

Rafael took out the letter again. He'd read it a dozen times but he read the last paragraph once more, the one that mattered:

*You are requested to appear as finder of fact regarding the recovery of documentary evidence from the wreck designated in naval records as hull 77-C. Your account of the circumstances of recovery will be entered into the permanent record of the Commission.*

Hull 77-C. The navy's quiet erasure, still sitting in the files, still pretending the freighter had never carried what it carried. And now his name would be stapled to that lie forever, prying it open. Rafael Sterling, finder of fact. It sounded absurd. It sounded, he realized, sitting in the sun with the meltwater talking all down the valley, like the truest job description he'd ever had. He had found the fact. All winter, five people and a dead man's map had carried the fact over a mountain so that it could be found by everyone.

He folded the letter away and stayed a while longer, because below him he could see the others coming up the trail—Mirena first, walking fast, then Elias leaning on a stick, then Sefa ranging ahead and doubling back the way she did—and he wanted to watch them for a minute unobserved, the way you look at a thing you almost lost. His crew. His impossible, assembled-out-of-wreckage family. None of them had planned each other. All of them had been invisible in different ways, and somewhere between the icefall and the gully they had become visible to one another, and that, he thought, might be the actual finding—not the chest, not the film, not even the names. This. People who see each other and stay.

Mirena spotted him and raised a hand. He raised his back.

* * *

They spent the remaining days preparing, because Elias insisted preparation was a form of armor. He drilled them gently, mercilessly, in the inn's back room: dates, manifest numbers, the notation system of the Velorian archive, the chain of custody from riverbed to glacier to radio office to the four sealed copies that now existed in four separate hands. "They will try to make you doubt your own memory," he told Rafael, pacing with the stick. "Not cruelly—that's the sophisticated version. They'll be polite. They'll suggest a simpler explanation. A mislabeled compartment. A coincidence of seals. They will offer you a smaller story to stand inside, because a smaller story asks less of you." He stopped at the window. "Don't take it. I took the smaller story for twenty years. It fits beautifully and it doesn't hold anything."

"I know what I pulled up," Rafael said.

"You know what you pulled up," Elias agreed. "Now say it so a room full of lawyers can't unknow it."

Mirena coached him on procedure. Sefa said almost nothing about the hearing at all, but the night before they were to take the mail plane north she pressed something into his hand at the door: a short length of climbing rope, the exact rope, frayed and scarred, from the gully descent. "For your pocket," she said. "When the room gets small, hold something that remembers what you survived." Then she was gone up the stairs before he could answer, because Sefa distributed tenderness the way she placed pitons—quickly, securely, without ceremony.

The morning they flew out was hard blue and windless, the kind of day Tomás would have called a gift and then billed them for anyway. The mail plane was a battered twin-prop with a young pilot who chewed gum and asked no questions, and they loaded themselves and the document cases aboard with the biscuit tin between them, and Rafael stood a moment on the cracked apron looking south at the Cordillera del Viento, white and enormous and indifferent, keeping its dead and its secrets both.

"Ready?" Mirena asked.

He thought about saying yes and meaning it halfway. Instead he took the rope fragment out of his pocket, closed his hand around it, and felt the whole winter in it—the seracs groaning, the stove in the snow cave, Tomás's laugh, the names read aloud by lamplight. Steady. Not calm; he suspected he would never be calm in a hearing room, or a courtroom, or anywhere the powerful gathered to decide what a life was worth. But steady, the way ice is steady, the way a route is steady once you've committed to it and there is nothing left to do but move well.

"No," he said honestly, and smiled. "But I'm going anyway."

The engines caught. The plane rolled down the strip Tomás should have had a view of, lifted, and turned north toward the capital, toward the summons, toward the room where Rafael Sterling—who had spent three years being no one, carefully, on purpose—would stand up under the lights and say, into the permanent record of the world: *I found them. Here is what the river kept. Here are their names.*

Below, the river caught the sun and threw it back, all the way to the horizon.
