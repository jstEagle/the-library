## 37. Names Read Aloud

The tribunal chamber had been a courthouse once, before the war, before the dissolution, before it became a granary and then a garrison and then a ruin that pigeons owned outright. Now the scaffolding was down, the windows reglazed with pale new glass that turned the morning light the color of skimmed milk, and Mirena stood at the translators' desk below the dais and watched the gallery fill.

They came in slowly, the way people enter a church they are not sure still belongs to them. Old women in coats too heavy for spring. Men who had traveled two days on buses to sit in rooms like this one and hear a word—*Veloria*—spoken aloud by an official voice for the first time in thirty years. A boy of perhaps ten holding his grandmother's hand so tightly that Mirena could see the small knuckles whiten from where she stood. Families of the ledger. Descendants of numbers. People who had spent their whole lives being told, gently or not so gently, that there was nothing to remember because there had never been anything at all.

She had translated for tribunals before. She knew the acoustics of official grief: the coughing, the rustle of coats, the terrible politeness of it all. But this room was different. This room breathed. She could feel it against her skin, the collective inhale of three hundred people waiting to learn whether the world would say their dead out loud.

Elias sat in the second row, hands folded on his knee like a schoolboy called before an examination board he had spent decades dreading. He wore a suit that fit him badly; Sefa had bought it for him in the snowbound village from a widower with broader shoulders, and no tailor south of the pass had been willing to take the job on short notice. He looked, Mirena thought, exactly like what he was—a disgraced archivist in borrowed clothes—and she loved him for it. He had refused every offer to dress him differently. *Let them see what they made me,* he'd said. *Let them look at the man they threw away and understand that he kept everything.*

Rafael stood at the back, near the doors, as if he hadn't decided yet whether to stay. Three months ago he would have left. Two months ago he would have stayed but pretended to be furniture. Today he stood with his weight even on both feet, his diver's hands loose at his sides, watching the gallery the way he watched water—as something that could kill you if you misread it, and deserved your full attention anyway. When Mirena caught his eye he didn't smile. He nodded, once, the way you nod to someone across a dangerous current: *I'm here. I see you. Keep going.*

Sefa was not in the room. She had said she would listen from the steps outside, where the mountain habit of her blood could breathe, and Mirena understood. Some people carry grief standing up.

* * *

The president of the tribunal was a thin woman with a voice like dry paper, Judge Ondine Marchetti, who had spent eleven years documenting disappearances in another country entirely and had come to this one, she said in her opening remarks, "because erasure is contagious." She read the procedural preamble in a monotone that somehow made it sacred. Then she looked down at the document on her desk—the ledger, the corroded chest's contents, photographed page by page in Rafael's boathouse under a single lamp while rain hammered the river—and she said:

"We will now read into the record the names of the deceased as recorded in Manifest Seven, recovered from the wreck designated Vessel 114."

Mirena's hands were on the translation console, but her work today was almost nothing. The original ledger had been written in Velorian, and Velorian was her mother tongue, the language her father had sung to her before she was born, the language the three successor governments had spent thirty years pretending had never existed. There was nothing to translate. There was only to say.

Judge Marchetti began.

"Prisoner One. Anselm Ferreol. Age thirty-four. Occupation: schoolteacher."

A sound went through the gallery—not a gasp, not a cry, something lower than either, a note struck on an instrument none of them had known they were carrying. Somewhere in the third row a woman began to weep without any movement at all, tears simply arriving on her face as though they had been waiting years for permission.

"Prisoner Two. Dalia Ostrova. Age twenty-nine. Occupation: nurse."

Mirena had read these names alone a hundred times. In safehouses, in the snowbound village, by lamplight while the pass roared shut behind them, she had traced each entry with her finger and spoken them into the cold air so they would exist somewhere besides paper. But reading them alone was a private act, a vigil. Reading them here, into a microphone, into a record that would be archived and published and defended by international law—this was something else. This was the opposite of the thing that had been done to them. The men who built Veloria's machinery had believed that a person could be converted into a number, a number into a line item, a line item into smoke. And here was a courtroom full of witnesses demonstrating, name by name, that the conversion ran backward. That smoke could be unsmoked. That a number, given back its name, became a man again.

"Prisoner Nine. Tomas Brandt. Age forty-one. Occupation: ferryman."

In the second row Elias closed his eyes. Not his relative—he had checked, months ago, with the methodical ache of a man who expected nothing—but a Brandt nonetheless, and names have gravity.

"Prisoner Seventeen. Ivo Marchetti. Age fifty-one. Occupation: stonemason."

Judge Marchetti paused for half a breath at her own surname, then continued without comment. Later, Mirena thought, someone would ask her about it, and she would say what she always said: that the dead did not arrange themselves for anyone's comfort.

* * *

They read for two hours. Mirena's voice took over when the judge's frayed—Marchetti read in blocks of twenty, then passed the manifest to the junior rapporteur, and when his voice failed too, near the nineties, Mirena herself was called to the reading stand, because the chairwoman wanted the names heard in Velorian as well as in the trade tongue, "so that the record contains them as they were written."

Standing at the microphone, looking out at the gallery, Mirena felt the strangest sensation of her life: that she was not speaking but being spoken through. Her father's language poured out of her in its old cadences, the vowels rounded the way coastal Velorian rounded them, and faces in the gallery changed as she spoke—old faces especially, faces that had not heard their mother tongue in an official building since before some of them were born. An elderly man in the front row mouthed the words along with her, half a syllable behind, keeping time like a man walking a road he knew blindfolded.

"Prisoner Forty-One," she read, and her own voice cracked on the number, and she let it crack, because hiding it would have been a kind of lie and she had promised herself, on the glacier, in the dark of the snow cave with Tomás dying by the stove, that she was done with lies of every size.

"Prisoner Forty-One. Aurel Kovač. Age thirty-seven. Occupation: railway engineer. Village of origin: Ternova nad Stikom."

Her father's name, in the record, forever.

She had learned it in the high camp, in the way Elias had reconstructed it—cross-referencing the ledger's village codes against the old woman's account in the town that forgot its name, against a pay chit fragment frozen in the officer's camp, against a signature on a requisition for rope that only a railway engineer would have countersigned. *He helped carry the chest,* Elias had told her, his voice breaking on a mountain at four thousand meters. *Your father carried the truth on his shoulder through the ice, Mirena. He was not cargo. He was the courier.* And she had knelt in the snow and pressed her bare hand flat against the ice, as if she could reach through thirty years and the whole thickness of the world to touch him.

Now she finished the entry—"transported north, winter route, no arrival recorded"—and stepped back from the microphone, and the gallery rose.

Not all at once. First the old woman in the third row, the one who had wept without moving. Then the man mouthing the Velorian. Then row by row, the whole slow tide of them, three hundred people standing in silence while the junior rapporteur read on, frightened and moved and unsure whether protocol permitted it, until Judge Marchetti said quietly, "Let them stand. The record will show that the gallery stood for the reading of the names."

And the record showed it. Mirena would read that line a hundred times in the years after. *The gallery stood.*

* * *

There was more. Of course there was more—a tribunal is mostly procedure, and procedure is how institutions protect themselves from feeling anything—but the day belonged to the names. In the afternoon session the prosecution presented the corroborating evidence: the photographs from the bronze-bound chest, the film reels catalogued by Elias with hands that shook only once, the testimony of the Velorian officer transcribed from his carved marker and from the letter pages preserved in wax cloth. Admiral Vess's name entered the record at 14:40 by the tribunal clock, spoken by the chief prosecutor in the flat tone reserved for facts, and Mirena watched the admiral's counsel—a silver-haired man with the complexion of someone who slept well—write a note and pass it down the table, and watched the note go unread, because what was there left to strategize? You cannot cross-examine a photograph. You cannot subpoena ice.

Corven testified late in the day, brought in bound and pale, his naval bearing gone soft around the edges like a man whose skeleton had been quietly removed. He answered questions in a voice barely above a whisper. When the prosecutor asked him who had ordered the destruction of the recovery operation, he looked—for one moment, across the whole length of the chamber—directly at Rafael, standing at the back by the doors.

"He knows," Corven said. "Ask the diver. Ask him why he saved me."

And Rafael, who had spent three years being invisible and one winter learning to be seen, met the eyes of the man sent to bury the dead a second time and said, clearly enough for the microphones: "Because the mountain already judged his friends. Someone had to bring him down alive to answer for the rest."

The gallery did not applaud. Tribunals do not permit applause. But the sound that moved through the room instead was older than applause, older than courts—a low exhalation, three hundred people breathing out together, the sound of a held breath finally released after thirty years.

* * *

It ended at dusk. Judge Marchetti adjourned the session with the verdicts still days away—there would be more sessions, more procedure, more of the machinery grinding forward—and the gallery emptied slowly, reluctantly, families gathering in the vestibule to touch each other's arms and say names to each other, testing them, saying *Anselm* and *Dalia* and *Aurel* aloud in the ordinary air as if trying on clothes that had hung unworn in a closet for a generation.

Mirena found her grandmother's cousin's widow on the steps—she had never known the woman existed until the descendants' association wrote to the village radio office last spring, following the transmission, following the names—and the old woman took Mirena's face in both hands and said, in Velorian, "You have his chin. I danced at his parents' wedding. I thought I was the last one who remembered his face."

"You're not," Mirena said. "You never were. That's what today was."

The old woman laughed and cried in the same breath, which Mirena would learn was simply what happened to people that week.

Elias came down the steps last, moving carefully in the ill-fitting suit, and stopped beside her without quite touching her shoulder—he was still learning that touching was allowed now, that the rules of exile did not apply among the crew. Behind him, at the bottom of the stairs, a cluster of young archivists waited with the shy hunger of students approaching a legend they'd only read about in banned footnotes. One of them held out a card. "Professor Brandt? The institute is reopening the Velorian collection. They're asking—if you would consider—"

"Consider what?" Elias asked, though Mirena could see he knew.

"The chair. Your chair. It's been empty since—"

"I know how long it's been empty," Elias said, not unkindly. He took the card, turned it over twice in his fingers, and put it in his breast pocket over his heart, where Tomás's letter had ridden down the mountain. "Tell them I'll answer in writing. I've gotten rather good at documents that can't be burned."

Rafael appeared at her other side, silent as ever, smelling faintly of river water even here, even now, as if the river had signed him permanently. Below the steps, Sefa detached herself from the stone balustrade where she'd stood all day listening through the open doors, and fell into step beside him, and the four of them stood for a moment in the milk-colored evening light while the crowd thinned and the city noises rose around them—trams, gulls, a street musician playing something in a major key, the ordinary music of a world that had, without most of its inhabitants ever noticing, become slightly less lied-to today.

"Hungry?" Rafael asked.

"Starving," said Sefa.

"Starving," said Elias, with the surprise of a man rediscovering appetite after decades of mere sustenance.

Mirena started to answer, and that was when the boy came running up the steps—the ten-year-old from the gallery, the one who had held his grandmother's hand—with a folded piece of paper clutched in his fist, thrust up toward her with the absolute urgency of childhood. "My grandmother says you have to have this," he panted. "She says it's from Ternova. She says your father fixed her family's roof the winter before."

Inside the paper, folded into eighths and worn soft as cloth, was a photograph: young men on a railway platform, squinting into a sun sixty years gone, and third from the left, hand raised mid-wave, a face Mirena had spent her whole life reconstructing from nothing and now recognized instantly, completely, the way you recognize your own reflection.

Her father, waving. Her father, caught in the act of greeting someone. Her father, before the number, before the ledger, before the ice—simply a man saying hello.

She held the photograph while the others waited, and the street musician played, and somewhere across the city the tribunal's copyists worked late under lamps, entering names into ledgers of their own, the good kind, the kind meant to be found.

"Come on," Rafael said gently. "We'll get dinner. You can tell us about him."

And Mirena, folding the photograph with the care owed to a rescued thing, followed her family down the steps into the noise and light of the living world—already knowing that tomorrow the summons would come for Rafael, that the tribunal was not finished asking things of them, and finding, for the first time in her life, that she was not afraid of what would be asked.
