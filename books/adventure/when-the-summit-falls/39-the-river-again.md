## 39. The River Again

The river had not changed.

That was the first thing Rafael understood, standing on the shingle bank where he'd moored the *Marta Kay* for three years of his life—or a boat shaped like her, anyway, because the *Marta Kay* herself lay at the bottom of the channel now, scuttled by his own hands in a harbor forty miles downstream, and this was her sister hull, bought quietly in spring with tribunal money that still made him uncomfortable to touch. Same gray water moving with that patient brown-green patience. Same smell of silt and diesel and rotting willow. Same gulls arguing over something dead on the far bank. The river kept its own counsel, the way it always had. It did not know it had become famous. It did not care.

He carried the plaque down to the water in a canvas duffel slung across his back, and even that felt familiar—the weight of metal, the awkward distribution, the way his shoulders remembered every load they'd ever borne into current. Behind him, up the bank near the road, a car sat with its engine off and two people inside who were absolutely not pretending to read a map: Mirena and Elias, come to watch him work, or perhaps just to be near him while he did it, because that was what families did now, apparently. They showed up. They waited in cars. They drank terrible coffee from a thermos and let a man carry his grief down to the water alone if that was how he needed to carry it.

Sefa was there too, somewhere up on the ridge with binoculars, doing what she called "the boring work"—watching the road, watching the treeline, watching out of habit for men like Corven, though Corven was in a cell in the capital now giving testimony in a voice so flat and obedient it had made the prosecutors uneasy. Old habits. Sefa said mountains taught you that vigilance wasn't fear; it was respect. He was learning.

Rafael waded in to his knees and let the cold climb him like a slow animal. Spring runoff. The water was higher than it had been the autumn he'd first found the anomaly on his sonar—a smudge, an unlogged compartment, a shape the navy's charts insisted did not exist—and colder, milked white with glacier flour from ranges he had since stood inside of. He could taste iron and stone. He could taste three years ago.

* * *

The descent was the same descent his body had made four hundred times, and yet nothing about it was the same, and both truths sat in him at once like passengers who refused to speak to each other.

Ten meters and the light went from silver to pewter. Fifteen and it went to memory. The freighter rose out of the gloom the way she always did—bow first, listing hard to port, her superstructure furred with zebra mussels, her name plate long since pried away by scavengers or time so that she was just *the wreck*, just the ship the records said had never carried what she carried. He leveled off above her rail and hung there in the green silence, breathing, listening to the only sound left in the world: his own regulator, in and out, in and out, the tide of a small contained sea.

He had dreamed about this deck all winter. Not nightmares, exactly. In the dreams he was usually working—running a line, checking a valve—and the prisoners were there, the ninety-one names, standing along the rail in their coats, patient, unhurried, waiting for him to finish the job. They never spoke. In the dreams that had frightened him at first, until Mirena told him, gently, over the radio from the snowbound village, that in her family's tradition the dead who appear in dreams and wait quietly are not haunting you. They are keeping you company.

So he worked. That was the whole of the ritual, and he had planned it for weeks with Elias, who approached the design of a memorial plaque the way other men approached state funerals. Bronze, because bronze outlasts. Bolted, not glued, because bolted means someone decided. And the names—Elias had verified each one twice against the ledger, against the tribunal record, against the descendants' association's own lists, correcting two spellings that thirty years of bureaucratic erasure had mangled. Ninety-one names. Prisoner 41 among them, third column, seventh row: **ANDREJ KOVAČ**, restored at last to the alphabet.

Rafael swam the length of the main deck to the cargo hold where he'd cut through the false bulkhead a lifetime ago, where the sealed compartment had breathed its first air in thirty years against his face mask. The hole was still there, edges rusting orange, and he paused at it the way you pause at a door you once walked through as a different person. Then he found the mounting points he'd drilled on his reconnaissance dive in April—four holes in the deck plating beside the hold entrance, in the spot the ledger said the prisoners had been loaded, in the dark, in the winter, in chains—and he set the plaque against them.

It was smaller than it had looked in Elias's bookshop flat. A meter of bronze. But when he lifted it free of the bag and the current took its weight, it moved like something alive, like something that wanted to go down and stay, and his eyes stung behind the mask in a way that had nothing to do with pressure.

He bolted it. Four bolts, torqued by hand, each one a small deliberate violence against forgetting. The wrench turned. The bronze settled flush against the deck. And then it was simply there—a rectangle of pale metal in the green dark, already beginning its long conversation with the river, already letting the water have it.

He ran his glove over the engraved surface. He couldn't read it down here—he didn't need to. He knew every line by heart. *Here the freighter VELORIA STAR, sailing under false registry, delivered ninety-one prisoners of the dissolved Republic of Veloria to no destination. Their names follow. Remember them.* And then the names, all ninety-one, in letters deep enough to outlast the mussels, the rust, the navy's silence, the men who had ordered the holds sealed.

The last line, which Elias had fought the tribunal's legal office for three weeks to include, read simply: *They were here.*

*They were here.* Two words doing the work of a war crimes conviction, because the conviction had already happened, and this was the other thing—the older thing—that courts had never been able to do.

* * *

He stayed longer than he'd planned.

There was no reason to stay. The job was done; the bolts were set; his gauges said ascend. But he drifted instead along the deck, past the winch, past the collapsed funnel, and let himself do the thing he had forbidden himself on every previous dive: he looked for them. Not their bodies—the river had taken care of that decades ago, and the tribunal divers had surveyed every compartment in the autumn—but their traces. The scratch marks inside the hold hatch, photographed and catalogued and argued over in The Hague. The boot heel embedded in the congealed bilge muck. The child's shoe, one shoe, no larger than his palm, that Mirena had held in both hands for eleven minutes without speaking before she wrapped it in acid-free cloth.

He found the place where the shoe had lain. Of course it was empty now. But the sediment held a shadow of it, a paler rectangle in the dark floor of the hold, and Rafael hovered above that small absence and understood, finally, in his chest and not his head, why he had come back.

Not for closure. He'd learned enough words in the last year to distrust that one; closure was what you bought when you wanted to stop carrying something, and some things you were meant to carry. He came back because grief that has nowhere to go curdles, and grief given a task becomes something else entirely. For three years this wreck had been his secret, his anomaly, his private unease—the thing that had cracked open his careful invisible life. Now it belonged to ninety-one families, to a public archive, to a history that would be taught in schools in three successor nations whose ministers had once sworn it never happened. His part was finished. The river's part went on.

He thought about Tomás. He thought about Tomás more than he expected to, here especially, because Tomás had been a man of vehicles—planes, trucks, a cigarette boat he swore he'd once outrun a customs cutter in—and would have had opinions about a river grave, probably irreverent ones, probably something like *you know she'd make a fine reef, Sterling, the fish are grateful.* And then, because that was how Tomás worked on you even a year gone, the joke would turn in your hand like a knife and show you its true edge: the fish are grateful, and so am I, and somebody ought to say a word over the ones nobody counted.

Somebody had said a word now. Ninety-one words, actually, cast in bronze.

Rafael hung in the water above the plaque and gave them the only eulogy he trusted—one breath, released deliberately, a silver column of bubbles rising through the green toward a sky he couldn't see, unwinding upward like something set free. The river took it. The river took everything, eventually, and kept what mattered.

* * *

He surfaced into late afternoon light so golden it seemed poured rather than fallen, and the world came back in layers: the cold shock fading, the shingle bank, the willows, the car on the road with its doors now open and two figures standing at the waterline trying very hard to look casual and failing completely.

Mirena got to him first. She didn't ask how it went. She just took the tank off his shoulders before he could protest, which was her way—she had spent a career translating other people's unbearable sentences into bearable ones, and she had extended the same craft to him without ever announcing it. Elias stood a few steps back on the stones, hat in hand, which for Elias was the equivalent of weeping. He had removed his hat. Outdoors. Rafael had seen him do it exactly twice before: at Tomás's grave, and at the reading of the verdicts.

"It's done," Rafael said. His voice came out roughened, river-flavored.

"Four bolts," Elias said. "You'll forgive me for counting."

"I counted too."

"The corrosion report says the bronze is good for two hundred years in fresh water." Elias put his hat back on, and his voice steadied itself around the fact like a hand around a railing. "Longer than any of the governments involved have lasted, statistically speaking. There's a certain poetry in that I intend to exploit in the archive's anniversary publication."

"You would," Rafael said, and Mirena laughed, and the laugh went out over the water and came back changed, softer, the way sounds do over rivers at evening.

They walked up the bank together, three people who a year and a half ago had been strangers arranged by accident around a stolen chest, and Rafael felt the strangest sensation, one he was still not fully fluent in: being accompanied. Being visible. There had been a version of him, not long ago, who measured his life in dive-hours precisely because hours underwater owed him nothing and asked nothing and never watched him leave. That man had scuttled his boat and his solitude in the same act, and some mornings he still missed them the way you miss a room you grew up in after the house is sold—not the room itself, maybe, but the person you got to be inside it, unobserved and sufficient.

But then Sefa's truck bumped down the track from the ridge, dust rising, and she leaned out the window with that look she wore when the boring work had turned up something interesting, and Rafael felt the old alertness rise in him like a diver answering a downline.

"Road's clear," she said. "Has been all day." A pause, deliberate, the pause of a woman who had guided enough clients to know exactly when to deliver news. "But there's a letter in my glovebox. Came to the foundation's post box this morning. Postmarked from the capital."

"From the tribunal?" Mirena asked. "The annex hearing isn't until—"

"Not the tribunal." Sefa reached through the window and held up an envelope, heavy paper, a wax seal cracked at the flap. Rafael recognized the crest before he recognized anything else, because he had spent one night in a snow cave studying it under a dying headlamp: the arms of the Republic of Veloria, the state that no longer existed, pressed into wax by someone who still had the matrix to press it with.

"There's more," Sefa said, and her voice, which had not shaken on the icefall, not once, was not quite steady now. "The return address. It's signed by a name Elias has been looking for since the archive opened. One of the officers listed in the second ledger. The ones who were never accounted for."

Rafael took the envelope. The river moved behind them, patient, keeping what it kept. Some things, he was learning, the water gives back.

He broke the seal.
