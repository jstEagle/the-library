## 1. Miles, Not Years

The squall came in off Greywater Point the way old debts do—slow at first, then all at once, and never when you're ready.

Bram Coldharbour stood at the wheel of the *Halcyon* with salt drying white on his forearms and watched the sky go the color of a bruise healing wrong. Behind him, the storm wall built itself out of nothing: a gray-green rampart of cloud stacked from horizon to heaven, flickering inside like something thinking hard about a bad idea.

"Pressure's dropping like my mother's opinion of sailors," Pell called up from the deck, where he was lashing down the spare kite spars. He said it every time. It wasn't even true—Pell's mother had thought the world of him and told him so weekly by letter—but it was the kind of lie that kept a crew loose, and Bram had learned long ago that loose crews survived tight situations.

"How far?" Bram asked.

Juno didn't look up from her charts. She never did; looking up cost you the wind line. "Twelve miles to the eye track. Fifteen if the current's feeling contrary." A pause, her pencil scratching. "It's feeling contrary."

"Then we've got time for one pass."

"One pass," Juno repeated, in the tone of a woman writing it down so there'd be proof later.

That was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement for twelve years now: they chased storms off Greywater Point, rode the edges where the wind ran cleanest, and sold what they caught. Not fish. Wind. The kite-ship trade had made sense to Bram from the first day someone explained it—a ship that flew instead of floated, hauling cargo on the backs of weather other captains paid good coin to avoid. You couldn't outrun your problems on a conventional hull. On a kite-ship, you could make your problems into ballast.

He'd believed that once. He'd believed a lot of things twelve years ago.

"Wren!" he called down. "Lines!"

Wren was already moving. She was seventeen and moved like a rumor—quiet, everywhere at once, gone before you could ask what she'd heard. She took the high lines without being told twice, bare feet finding the rigging the way other people found doorframes, and if she said anything as she climbed, it was too soft for the wind to carry. She'd been with them three years now, fished half-drowned out of a cove after the Fever Summer, and in three years Bram could count her spoken sentences on two hands and have fingers left over.

Juno said the girl listened the way other people talked. Bram suspected that was generous. He suspected Wren listened the way other people *prayed*.

The first gust hit like a shoulder check.

* * *

There is a moment in every chase—Bram had logged hundreds of them in his head, an almanac of near-misses—when the storm stops being weather and becomes a place. The air changes texture. The light goes strange and underwater. The sea stops being a surface and becomes a country you are trespassing in.

They crossed that border at full sail, the *Halcyon*'s great wing-kites snapping taut overhead like the sails of some cathedral deciding to walk. The rain came sideways. The world went white, then green-white, then the particular bruised gold that meant lightning was close enough to have opinions.

"Pell! Trim the mizzen kite—we're carrying too much on the turn!"

"Carrying too much on the *turn*, he says," Pell muttered, loud enough to be heard over the roar, which was his way of agreeing. He hauled the line hand over hand, a squat barrel of a man with forearms like mooring cable and a face that looked permanently mid-argument. "You know what I miss? Dry socks. Remember dry socks? Whole civilizations were built on dry socks."

"Less civilization, more trimming!"

"I'm doing both! Multitasking!"

The *Halcyon* leaned into the squall's shoulder and *ran*. This was the part Bram loved—had always loved, back when love was still a thing he permitted himself—the part where the ship stopped being wood and canvas and became pure velocity wearing a disguise. The bow wave went silver. The kites sang. For ninety seconds, maybe a hundred, there was no debt and no past and no miles left uncounted, just the wheel humming under his palms and the whole furious sky pulling them forward like the sea itself wanted to see what they were made of.

Ninety seconds. Then the squall's edge, and the wind easing, and the ordinary grey afternoon reasserting itself like a landlord.

And then the skiff.

Bram saw it before anyone hailed—saw it because he'd spent twelve years learning to see things he wished weren't there. A low black hull sitting fat and patient in their wake shadow, riding water that should have been too rough for any sane vessel, flying no colors at all. No colors was its own kind of flag.

"Company," he said.

Juno's pencil stopped. "Whose?"

"You know whose."

"*Bram.*"

"I know whose," Pell said grimly, squinting astern. "Look at the hull lines. That's Optimist work. Nobody else builds ugly that expensive."

The skiff came alongside with the smooth, unhurried confidence of a thing that has never once been told no. Three figures stood in it, dry—impossibly, insultingly dry—in the middle of a dying squall's aftermath, and even at fifty yards Bram could see they were smiling.

He killed the kites. There was no point running; Optimist collectors didn't chase. They arrived. Chasing implied the outcome was in doubt.

"Ahoy the *Halcyon*!" The lead collector's voice carried across the water bright as a dinner bell. He was a broad, pink-faced man in a coat too fine for the sea, and his smile had the specific warmth of a hearth in a house you owed money on. "Lovely weather for it! We do admire a captain who keeps his skills sharp."

"We weren't aware we'd ordered visitors," Bram called back.

"Oh, we're not visitors, Captain Coldharbour. Visitors knock." The man's smile widened by exactly one tooth. "We're more like... reminders."

* * *

They boarded the way tide boards—everywhere at once, politely, with total inevitability. Two of them came up the side ropes while the third held the skiff steady, and all three kept smiling through the whole maneuver, which should have been physically exhausting and somehow wasn't. Bram had met a lot of dangerous men in his years on the water. Most of them scowled. The scowlers, at least, were honest about the business.

The Optimists smiled because the smile *was* the business.

"Master Orlin," the lead one said, extending a hand to each of them in turn—to Bram, who shook it because refusing would cost more than the touch; to Juno, who shook it like she was testing his pulse; to Pell, who wiped his palm on his trousers first with elaborate insolence; to Wren, who looked at the offered hand until the man, still smiling, withdrew it. "And this must be the famous crew. The navigator with the charts everyone wants to steal. The engineer who once rebuilt a transmission housing out of a church bell—" here Orlin paused, and something flickered behind the smile, quick as a fish under ice, "—and the young listener. Yes. We keep excellent files."

"Congratulations," Pell said. "So does the tax office."

Orlin laughed, a big warm rolling laugh, and produced from inside his beautiful coat a ledger bound in green leather, and opened it on the *Halcyon*'s chart table as though he owned the table, the ship, and possibly the ocean it sat in. "Now. Business, and then I'll let you get back to your weather. Captain, your account stands at—" he turned a page with one finger, savoring it, "—four thousand, two hundred crowns. Principal, interest, and the handling fee from the *Kestrel* incident, which I think we can all agree was regrettable but contractual."

"The *Kestrel* incident," Bram said evenly, "was your people cutting a mooring line while a man was aboard."

"And yet the contract specified mooring-line services," Orlin said pleasantly, "and the contract was fulfilled. That's the wonderful thing about the Optimists, Captain. We are *always* fulfilled." He tapped the ledger. "Four thousand, two hundred. But—and here is why I sailed out personally, in weather, because I value you—we are prepared to discuss terms. Restructuring. New schedules. The Optimists believe deeply in second chances."

"What's the schedule?" Juno asked. Her voice was flat and careful, the voice she used for reefs.

Orlin beamed at her. "Forty days."

Nobody spoke. The *Halcyon* rocked gently on the settling swell, and somewhere aloft a wet halyard ticked against the mast, counting.

"Forty days," Bram repeated.

"To clear the balance in full. After that..." Orlin closed the ledger gently, lovingly, the way you'd close a coffin lid. "Well. Assets become available. Ships. Equipment. And I'm afraid certain skilled persons become available to *other* contracts, under our management, at destinations of our choosing. Nothing violent! We're not barbarians. Just... relocation." His smile returned to full wattage. "Think of it as a long voyage with poor scenery."

"You're all heart," Pell said.

"We really are. It's in the charter." Orlin gathered his men with a little gesture, a host ending a party, and paused at the rail with one boot on the top rope. He looked back at Bram, and for just a moment—half a heartbeat—the smile thinned into something older and hungrier underneath, the way light through a window shows you the wire in the glass. "Forty days, Captain. Spend them well."

Then they were over the side, and the skiff was pulling away, and the three of them stood on the deck of the *Halcyon* in the wet grey silence, listening to oars fade.

* * *

Juno broke it first, because Juno always broke silences first—it was how she navigated everything, including conversations.

"Forty days," she said. "We don't have forty days' work in forty days. Not at chase rates. Not even if we ride every squall between here and the Cape."

"I know."

"Four thousand two hundred. That's not a debt, Bram, that's a mortgage with teeth."

"I know."

"He knew about Wren." Pell had dropped onto the hatch coaming, elbows on knees, staring at the empty water where the skiff had been. "Called her 'the young listener.' We never filed her name with anyone. Never registered her as crew. So either the Optimists have eyes on this deck—which, fine, probable—or somebody sold them a description." He rubbed his jaw. "I hate being described."

Bram said nothing. He was watching Wren, who had come down from the rigging at some point during the boarding—he hadn't seen her descend, he never saw her descend—and who now stood at the stern rail looking back along their wake with her head tilted, the way she did when she was hearing something none of the rest of them could.

"Wren," he said. "What is it?"

She didn't answer right away. Then she turned, and her face was carefully blank in the way that only very quiet people and very good liars can manage, and Bram had never decided which she was.

"Nothing," she said. "The water sounds different today."

"That'll be the squall stirring things up," Pell said comfortably. "Storms shake the seabed. Old Pell knows. Storms shake everything."

But Wren kept looking at the wake a moment longer, and Bram felt something cold slide down between his ribs—not fear exactly, more the memory of fear, the way an amputated limb remembers weather. Twelve years was a long time. Long enough that he'd almost convinced himself the Siren Shoals were just a place on a chart, a bad memory with coordinates.

Almost.

* * *

They anchored that evening in the lee of Greywater Point itself, in the little roadstead where the fishing boats clustered like gulls around a breadcrumb, and Bram climbed to the headland with a lantern because he always climbed to the headland, and because tonight he needed the height the way other men needed drink.

From up here the town was small and warm-lit and utterly indifferent to him. From up here the sea was a black plain with the last grey light dying on it, and somewhere out there—forty miles east, maybe less—the Shoals waited under their shallow water, singing whatever they sang to themselves when no one was fool enough to listen.

Tam had been fool enough to listen.

Bram set the lantern down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the dark went red. Forty days. Four thousand crowns. He'd run numbers like these across a dozen oceans, and he knew the arithmetic cold: there was no honest haul in forty days that cleared it. There was no dishonest one either, not within sailing distance, not with the Optimists' collectors thick as barnacles on every port from here to the Spur. They'd built it that way on purpose. Debts at the Optimists weren't mistakes. They were leashes, and leashes were only ever the first installment.

Which meant somebody, somewhere, already knew what came next. Debts this size didn't get called unless the caller expected them to be payable.

"Bram."

Juno's voice, behind him. She'd climbed quietly, which for Juno meant loudly by anyone else's standard; she announced herself the way she did everything, on principle.

"There's a boat coming in," she said. "Fast launch, dark hull, riding high and mean. Dockmaster says she asked for us by name." A pause. "Not your name. Mine."

Bram lowered his hands. Far below, a single lamp was swinging up the harbor road toward the tavern, moving with the brisk, entitled stride of someone who had never once in her life arrived anywhere unwelcome.

"Vess Karrow," he said.

"You remember."

"I remember everything about Vess Karrow. That's the problem." He picked up the lantern. Its light caught the lines of his own face in the glass—older than he felt, harder than he'd planned—and beyond it, the black water, and beyond that, faint as a thought, the east.

"Come on," he said. "Let's find out what she's selling."

Down in the harbor, the tavern door opened, spilled yellow light across the cobbles, and swallowed her.
