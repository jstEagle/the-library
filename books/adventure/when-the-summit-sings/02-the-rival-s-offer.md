## 2. The Rival's Offer

**Bram**

The Salt-Whistle Tavern smelled of tar, fried fish, and the particular despair of men who had bet against the weather and lost. Bram Coldharbour sat in his usual corner—back to the wall, sightline to both doors, a habit he'd picked up in ports where the Optimists collected—and told himself the knot in his chest was just the fish.

Forty days. That's what the smiling man on the storm-chase had said, holding up four fingers and then folding one down with theatrical slowness while the rain tried to drown them all. *Thirty-nine now,* Bram thought, watching the door. He'd been watching the door for two days, which was two days longer than any man should watch a door in a town like Greywater Point, where nothing ever came through it except cold air and disappointment.

The door opened. Cold air came through it. And then something else entirely.

Vess Karrow entered the Salt-Whistle the way weather enters a valley—not walking so much as arriving. She wore a coat the color of wet slate that probably cost more than the Halcyon's rigging, and she carried a leather chart case under one arm like a dueling pistol. The tavern went quiet in stages, the way it does when people recognize trouble but can't quite agree on how much of it.

"Bram." She smiled, and it was the same smile she'd worn at nineteen, across a regatta starting line, right before she'd luffed her sail and stolen his wind and half his mast. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive," Bram said. "One of those is an improvement."

She crossed the room without waiting to be invited, because Vess Karrow had never once waited to be invited anywhere in her life. She dropped into the chair across from him, signaled the barkeep with two fingers, and said, "Whatever he's drinking, and whatever you're drinking, and put them both in clean glasses for once."

"I'm not drinking with you."

"Then I'll drink twice. It'll be like old times—you watching me have fun."

Bram looked past her shoulder to the corner table where Juno sat with her charts and her tea, pretending very hard not to listen, which was Juno's way of listening with her whole body. Pell was in the back booth, ostensibly repairing a lantern, actually eavesdropping with a wrench in hand like a man who expected to need it. Wren wasn't there, thank every salt-blessed god. Some things children shouldn't hear.

"Whatever you're selling," Bram said, "the answer's no."

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You will. And then the answer's no."

Vess leaned back, and for just a moment the performance flickered—the smile dimming into something older and more tired, something that had also been at that regatta twelve years ago, standing on the dock afterward, watching the Halcyon come home one crew member light. Then it was gone, sealed away behind the slate-colored coat and the expensive grin.

"Forty days," she said. "That's what they gave you. Forty days before the Optimists start breaking things. Legs first, generally. They're traditionalists that way."

"Word travels."

"Word travels when I pay for the postage." She set the chart case on the table between them like a card played face-up. "I know your books, Bram. I know what you owe. I know what the interest does after forty days—it stops being interest and starts being ownership. They'll own the Halcyon. Then they'll own the crew's contracts. Then they'll own *you*, and the Optimists don't resell. They keep."

The drinks arrived. Vess drank hers. Bram watched his like it might be bugged, which, knowing Vess, it might be.

"So," she said. "Here's my offer. Triple your debt. Paid tonight, in full, cash, no interest, no collectors, no smiles. In exchange, one job."

"The answer's no," Bram said again, because repetition was free and everything else she was offering wasn't.

"You haven't heard the job."

"Don't need to. Any job worth triple our debt is a job worth dying for, and I've already got a full schedule of dying-for planned. It's called the next forty years, and I intend to spend them alive."

Vess opened the chart case anyway. Of course she did. She unrolled it across the table, weighting the corners with her cup, his untouched glass, a coin, and Bram's own forearm, which she moved without asking because asking had never occurred to her.

The chart showed the Siren Shoals.

Bram's stomach did something complicated and unpleasant, the way it always did at the sight of those waters—the curling reef lines drawn in faded ink, the warning annotations in three dead cartographers' hands, and at the center, circled in fresh red: a wreck symbol, and beside it, in Vess's sharp handwriting, *Meridian Bell.*

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Bram became aware of his own heartbeat, and of Juno's teacup, three tables away, having stopped halfway to her mouth.

"No," he said, and this time it came out quieter, which was worse.

* * *

**Juno**

Juno Merrow had been navigating by other people's silences for thirty years, and Vess Karrow's silence—there, at the edge of the conversation, when Bram said *no*—was the loudest thing in the tavern.

It was the silence of a woman who had expected the no. Who had *planned* around the no. Which meant the real offer hadn't been made yet, and wouldn't be made to Bram at all.

Juno sipped her tea and did arithmetic.

She knew the numbers better than Bram did, because Bram kept the debt the way he kept grief—in a locked box he never opened, so nobody could see how big it was. But Juno did the ship's accounts, and the ship's accounts included the Optimists' ledger whether Bram liked it or not. Eleven thousand crowns, principal. Interest compounding monthly at rates that should have been illegal and probably were illegal somewhere, just not anywhere the Optimists operated. Thirty-nine days left. After that, seizure of vessel, indenture of crew, and a lifetime of cheerful servitude to people who hummed while they worked.

Triple the debt. Thirty-three thousand crowns. Cash.

And the price: the Siren Shoals.

Juno set down her cup and looked at the chart from across the room, because looking at it directly felt like touching a burn. Twelve years since anyone she loved had sailed those waters. Twelve years since Tam.

Wren had been six when Tam died. She'd grown up on stories about the Shoals the way other children grew up on ghost tales—except in their family the ghost had a name, and a laugh, and a habit of stealing the last biscuit and leaving the crumbs arranged in a smiley face, because Tam had been exactly that kind of idiot, God rot him, God keep him, both.

And now Vess Karrow stood in their tavern rolling out those waters like a merchant unrolling cloth, saying *one job* as if jobs were small things, as if the Shoals were a place instead of a wound.

Juno watched Bram push back from the table. Watched him say something low and final. Watched Vess smile the smile of a chess player whose opponent has just knocked over his own king and called it strategy.

Bram walked out. That was Bram—forty days from ruin and he'd walk out of the only rescue on offer, because his pride weighed more than the ship. Beautiful, infuriating man. Juno had followed that pride through hurricanes. She was not going to let it sink them in a tavern.

She rose, folded her napkin into a precise square, and crossed the floor.

"Navigator," Vess said, not looking up from re-rolling her chart. "Still drawing straight lines through curved water?"

"Still buying ships you can't sail yourself?"

Vess's mouth twitched. "Touché."

"Show me the rest of it," Juno said, sitting down in Bram's abandoned chair. "Not the part you showed him. The part after the no. You didn't come all this way to be refused by one stubborn man; you came with contingencies. I've read your sailing style, Karrow. You never carry one plan."

For a moment Vess studied her, and Juno watched the mercantile warmth drain out of her face until what remained was simply intelligent and hard and honest in the way knives are honest.

"Triple the debt," Vess said quietly. "Paid tonight. Plus a salvage share—ten percent of whatever the Bell yields, split among the crew. And I'll throw in the thing your captain won't ask for: I'll buy out Wren's contract separately and burn it, so even if the job goes wrong, the girl walks away free. Clear title. No strings."

"And the catch."

"The catch is that the corporate fleet reaches the wreck in six tides. So we leave tomorrow."

Juno looked at the red circle on the chart. The Shoals looked back, the way they always did—patient, certain, full of a silence that had a name.

If she said no, the Optimists took everything in thirty-nine days anyway. If she said yes, they might lose everything in six tides instead—but they might not, and either way they'd lose it *choosing*, which was the only difference that had ever mattered to Juno Merrow. A navigator's whole life was choosing which danger to steer toward. This was just a bigger chart than usual.

Besides. Someone had to go along to keep Vess Karrow honest, and someone had to go along to keep Bram from doing anything noble, and those were the same job.

"Juno." Vess's voice softened by a degree, which from Vess was practically tenderness. "I know what I'm asking you to sail back into. I was there too, remember? I lit the beacon on the point the night you came home. I saw what was missing from your deck."

"You don't know," Juno said. "None of us knows. Not really. Only Bram knows how it happened, and he's never said, and I've stopped asking because asking makes him flinch, and a flinching helmsman is worse than a silent one."

"Then maybe this voyage shakes it loose."

"Maybe it shakes *us* loose." Juno held out her hand. "Triple the debt. Tonight. Wren's contract burned. Ten percent share. And one condition of mine, non-negotiable: if Bram says turn back, we turn back. Even mid-channel. Even if it costs you the Bell. His word on those waters outranks yours, mine, and God's."

Vess shook her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and entirely too pleased. "Done."

"One more thing."

"Name it."

"When Bram finds out—and he will find out, within the hour—I'd appreciate it if you didn't enjoy it."

Vess smiled, and this time there was nothing performed about it at all, which somehow made it worse. "Oh, Juno. I'm going to enjoy it enormously."

* * *

**Bram**

Bram found out within the hour, because Greywater Point was a small town and money made a sound when it moved—a particular clink and whisper that traveled faster than any rumor.

He came back into the Salt-Whistle soaked from a squall he'd walked into on purpose, needing the excuse to be wet and grim, and stopped dead just inside the door.

Vess was counting coins onto the table. Real ones. Stacked in towers with the Optimist-mint crowns on top, the ones with the smiling face stamped on the obverse, because the syndicate had a sense of humor like a boot has a sole. Beside the towers lay a paper: a contract, signed, with Juno's neat hand at the bottom and Wren's name in the clause about contract burns.

Pell stood beside the table holding the wrench again, though now he was using it to gesture, which meant he'd been arguing. Wren—

Wren was there. Sitting on the bench beside Juno, legs swinging, reading the contract upside down with the fierce concentration of a nine-year-old who'd recently learned that words could be traps.

"Wren," Bram said. "Outside."

"She signed for me," Wren said, not looking up. "Did you know that? Juno signed and put my name in it. It says my contract gets *burned*. Like actual fire?"

"Metaphorical fire," Juno said.

"That's less good."

"*Wren.* Outside."

The girl slid off the bench, and as she passed Bram she did the thing she sometimes did—touched two fingers lightly to his elbow, quick as a bird landing, gone before he could react. It was her version of a hug. He'd never figured out when she'd started doing it or how to stop her, and privately, in the locked box where he kept things, he hoped he never figured out either.

Then it was just the three of them, and the coins, and the chart.

"You went behind my back," Bram said.

"Yes," Juno said.

"To Vess *Karrow*."

"Yes."

"With *them*—" he gestured at the chart, at the red circle, and his voice cracked on the word he refused to say, so he said the water instead: "*There.*"

"Yes," Juno said, third time, calm as a lighthouse. "Because in thirty-nine days the Optimists take the ship, take our contracts, and take you, and there is no chart out of that. I've looked. I've looked for years, Bram. This is the only heading that doesn't end in chains."

"There are always other headings!"

"Name one."

Bram opened his mouth. Closed it. The truth was he'd been naming headings for eleven months, since the debt had gotten teeth, and every one of them ended at the same rocks. Run farther, work harder, sell the kite-rig—they'd tried, they'd failed, the Optimists' interest grew like kelp. There was no heading. There was only this one, and it led through the place where Tam wasn't anymore.

"Triple the debt," Vess said gently, and Bram hated how gentle, because gentleness from Vess Karrow was a rudder jammed hard over. "Cash, on this table, tonight. Your crew eats. Your ship stays yours. The girl goes free regardless. All you have to do is sail to the one place you swore you'd never go again, and pull a bell out of the water for me."

"Why?" Bram asked, and it came out raw. "Why the Bell? Why now? Why us?"

"Because you're the best storm crew on this coast. Because you're the only crew desperate enough to say yes. And because—" she paused, and gathered up the coins, and pushed the stacks toward Juno with one finger, "—because I knew you'd say no, Bram. I counted on it. You're predictable that way. It's your best quality."

Bram looked at Juno. Juno looked back at him, steady, unapologetic, and sorry—but only a little sorry, the amount a navigator allows herself when she's sure of her bearing.

"You've betrayed me," Bram said.

"I've *rescued* you," Juno said. "It looks similar from inside. I know. But it isn't."

Behind them, the tavern door creaked, and Wren's voice floated in, bright and terrible:

"Bram! Pell says the Shoals sing! Is that true? Did *Tam* hear it?"

The name landed in the room like a dropped anchor.

Bram closed his eyes. Six tides, Vess had said. Somewhere out past the harbor bar, the water was already pulling, and the whole coast was leaning toward the Siren Shoals like iron filings toward a magnet—and he was going to sail straight into the middle of it, owing his soul to a rival and his silence to a grave.

"We leave tomorrow," he said, to no one, to everyone. "And Karrow—if you've lied to me about even one line of that contract, I will scuttle your plans so thoroughly they'll find pieces of them in three oceans."

Vess raised her untouched second drink. "To honest sailing, Captain."

Bram didn't drink. Outside, the tide was turning, and it sounded—for the first time in twelve years—almost like singing.
