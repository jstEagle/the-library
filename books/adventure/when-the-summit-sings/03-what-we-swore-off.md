## 3. What We Swore Off

The Halcyon smelled like tar and old arguments, which was how a ship should smell.

Bram stood in her belly with a lantern hooked to a beam, running his palm along the keel timber the way you'd check a horse's leg before a long ride. Sound wood. Sound enough. He'd spent twelve years keeping this ship alive and only six days trying to decide whether he wanted to be aboard her when she sailed into the one stretch of water he'd promised—out loud, with witnesses, more than once—never to enter again.

Above him, boots on deck. Pell's uneven gait, Juno's precise ones, Wren's quick light patter that never quite committed to a direction. His crew. His family, if he was honest in the privacy of his own skull, which he tried not to be, because honesty up there had a way of leaking out his mouth at the worst moments.

"Keel's singing again," Pell called down through the hatch, meaning the faint creak the ship made in still air, the one nobody else could hear and Pell refused to stop hearing.

"She's always singing. She's a kite-ship. It's in the job description."

"She's singing *flat*."

"Then she's mourning my bank account. Hand me the pitch kettle."

He heard Pell laugh, and something in Bram's chest unclenched by a single degree. That was the trick of a crew, he'd learned. You didn't heal. You just distributed the weight until no one part of you had to carry it all.

The Halcyon was a storm-chaser by trade, which meant she was built wrong for everything else: too much sail area aloft, too shallow a draft, a spine like a whip. She'd been designed to run *at* weather while every other sensible hull ran from it. Twelve years ago there had been five of them to sail her. Now there were four, and the fifth berth—starboard side, aft, where the lamplight pooled warmest—had been kept empty so long it had become less a berth than an argument everyone had agreed to stop having.

Bram looked at it anyway. He always did. Habit, or penance, or both wearing each other's coats.

"Bram?" Wren's head appeared upside down through the hatch, hair hanging like dark kelp. "Juno wants to know if we're taking the spare spars."

"What spare spars?"

"The ones in the loft over the chandlery. The good ash ones."

"We can't afford the good ash ones."

"She says Vess is paying."

Bram closed his eyes briefly. "Tell Juno we're taking the spare spars."

Wren grinned and vanished. That was another thing about crews: they remembered your refusals better than you did, and they knew exactly which ones were load-bearing lies.

* * *

Twelve years ago, the Halcyon had come out of the Siren Shoals with four souls aboard instead of five, and Bram Coldharbour had been at the helm.

That was the fact he carried. Not the storm, not the engine, not even Tam—not exactly. The fact was the wheel under his hands and the Shoals behind them and the arithmetic of who came back. Everything else he'd folded up small and sunk deep, and for twelve years it had stayed down there, mostly, the way most drowned things do. Mostly.

They'd gone in chasing a rumor, back when rumors were all they chased and money was something that happened to other people. A resonance hum off the outer ring, strong enough to bend a compass needle. Tam had been the first to hear it properly—he'd always had the best ears on the crew, ears like a harbor bell, Juno used to say—and Tam had been the one who talked them past the marker buoys and into the dead water where the currents cancel and the sails go stupid and a ship becomes a piece of driftwood with opinions.

Bram had known better. That was the part that surfaced sometimes at three in the morning, turning over like something bloated coming up for air: *he had known better.* The charts said the ring was a throat. Juno had said it plainer than that. And Bram had let himself be talked into it because Tam had been laughing, because the whole crew had been young and broke and drunk on the idea that the sea owed them one miracle, and because Bram Coldharbour had never once in his life been able to say no to a miracle with Tam Oduya standing in front of it.

The wreck they found wasn't on any chart. A hull like a cathedral ribcage, coral grown through her in white branches, and inside her, hanging in the flooded dark, a bell that wasn't a bell—a machine shaped like one, humming a note so low you felt it in your teeth before you heard it in your ears. Tam had gone down to touch it. Of course Tam had gone down to touch it. Tam touched everything; it was how he loved things.

And the note had changed. That was what Bram remembered last, always last, the memory he shoved down hardest: the hum shifting key mid-breath, the water around the line going strange and heavy, Tam's voice in the speaking-tube saying something Bram had never been able to make himself repeat aloud—not *help*, that was the cruelty of it, not *help* but something wondering, something almost glad—and then the line going slack in a way lines don't go slack unless—

Unless.

They'd cut the wreck loose and run. Bram had steered them out on a current he shouldn't have found, on instinct and grief and the animal stupidity of a man who would rather die steering than die sitting, and the sea had let them go, and that was the worst mercy anyone ever showed him.

Only Bram knew about the changed note. Only Bram knew what Tam had said into the tube. He had kept it the way you keep a stone in your boot: painfully, deliberately, because putting it down felt like putting *him* down.

Now Vess Karrow wanted to sail them straight back to it, and had paid them to, and Bram had said no, and the crew had said yes, and here he was, checking a keel.

* * *

By late afternoon the deck looked like a quartermaster's fever dream. Coils of new line. Water barrels trundled and stowed. Pell had the engine hatch open and was elbow-deep in the winch gearing, swearing at it in the affectionate register he reserved for machinery he intended to forgive.

"You know," Pell said, without looking up, "most captains, when their crew signs them onto a suicide run, at least get a vote."

"Most captains' crews don't sign for them."

"Juno signed fast."

"Juno always signs fast. It's the slow parts she hates." Bram coiled line and pretended his hands weren't remembering the weight of a different rope, twelve years older. "She thinks she's saving us."

"She thinks she's saving *you*. There's a difference. One of those is possible."

Bram threw the coil at him. Pell caught it against his chest with a grunt, grinning, grease to the elbow and joy all over his face, because Pell had never met a catastrophe he couldn't reorganize into a project.

Wren sat cross-legged on the forehatch with the shoal charts spread around her like a card game she was losing. She was nineteen and had joined the crew four years ago off a sinking grain barge, and she had the particular silence of someone raised by people who punished questions. But she asked them anyway, in her sideways fashion, when she thought no one was counting.

"The Siren Shoals," she said now, to the charts, to nobody. "People say ships hear voices out there."

"People say a lot of things," Bram said.

"Do they? Hear voices?"

"Pell hears the keel singing flat. I wouldn't put much stock in the general public's ear."

Wren didn't laugh. She traced the outer ring of the Shoals with one finger, around and around, a small orbit. "What does it sound like? The hum. The real one."

The question went through the deck like a splinter finding old wood. Pell's wrench stopped. Up at the rail, Juno went very still over her sextant case, stillness being her loudest voice.

"Like machinery," Bram said. "Old machinery, underwater. That's all."

"That's not what Tam's letters said."

The name landed on the deck between them, and nobody picked it up. Wren hadn't known Tam—hadn't been born into their grief, arrived after it had fossilized—but she'd found his letters in the chart locker two years back, read them the way young people read anything, completely, and now she carried pieces of him around like shrapnel none of them had put there.

"He wrote that it sounded like a choir tuning up," Wren said. "Before the downbeat."

"Wren." Juno's voice, quiet, from the rail. Not sharp. Just a hand laid flat on a table.

"I'm allowed to ask."

"You're allowed," Juno said. "We're allowed not to answer. That's the treaty."

Wren looked at Bram. She had her mother's stubbornness somewhere in her, whoever her mother had been, and it lived entirely in her eyes. Bram held the look as long as he could, which was not long, then turned and busied himself with a cleat that needed no attention.

"Choir tuning up," Pell said at last, softly, into his engine. "Yeah. That's about right."

"Pell."

"I'm just agreeing with a letter. Letters are allowed."

* * *

Evening came down grey-gold over Greywater Point, and the crew ate on deck because the cabin still smelled of the bilge treatment. Juno dealt out stew like cards, exact portions, no favorites, though everyone knew Wren got the biggest potato and no one had ever caught Juno doing it.

"So," Juno said, when the bowls were half empty. "Tide tables."

"Romantic," said Pell.

"Six tides until the corporate fleet reaches the Bell. We sail on tomorrow's ebb or we don't sail at all." She said it evenly, eyes on Bram, giving him the opening the way she always did—the captain's veto, offered like a door left unlatched. "Last chance to say no properly. Out loud. With witnesses."

Bram set down his spoon. Four faces turned to him, and the empty fifth berth behind them, and the whole horizon doing its evening performance of beauty, which he distrusted on principle.

"If I say no," he said, "you'll go anyway."

Silence, which was agreement.

"And if I say yes," he went on, "I want it understood that we go in, we take what Vess is paying for, and we come out. No detours. No listening. Nothing sings to anybody twice." He heard his own voice go rough on the last words and hated it, and pushed through. "We treat that place like a job site. Hard hats. Short hours. Nobody touches anything that hums."

"Captain," Pell said gently, "we're salvage crew. Humming is the product."

"Then we'll be very rich deaf people."

Wren laughed, sudden and bright, and the tension cracked just enough to breathe through. Juno gathered the bowls. The wind came around with the dusk, cool off the water, carrying the smell of the Point—fish oil, pine tar, someone frying onions badly—and for a moment it was just an evening, an ordinary evening, the kind Bram had spent twelve years learning to survive inside of.

He stayed on deck after the others turned in. He always did. He checked the mooring lines twice, told himself once was inspection and twice was insomnia, and ended up at the starboard rail, aft, beside the empty berth's portlight.

From here you could see the low black line of the Shoals' direction, far off, indistinguishable from any other darkness except to a man who'd memorized it. Somewhere under that darkness, a machine shaped like a bell had been humming for twelve years. Waiting, maybe. Machines didn't wait. But songs did. Songs were nothing *but* waiting.

"Tuning up," he said quietly, to the water, to the letter, to the man. "Before the downbeat."

The sea said nothing, which was its way of saying everything.

Behind him, the hatch creaked. Wren, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose, watching him with those stubborn eyes.

"Captain," she said. "When we get there. If it really is a choir—" She stopped. Started smaller. "Will you tell me what he said? Into the tube. When you find out I'm ready."

Bram looked at her a long moment. The honest answer was *never*. The true answer was *I don't know if I'll survive telling it*. What he said was:

"Go to bed, Wren."

She went. But at the hatch she paused, and said, almost to herself, "It sounds like it knows us already," and was gone below before he could ask what she meant.

Bram stood alone at the rail a while longer, the Shoals' darkness ahead, the debt behind, the tide tables waiting in Juno's neat hand. Tomorrow's ebb would carry them out whether he was ready or not.

Down the dock, a lantern bobbed—Vess Karrow's launch, arriving early, uninvited, riding at anchor like a promise she hadn't asked permission to keep.

Bram watched it swing, and felt the clock begin.
