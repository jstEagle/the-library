## 4. Six Tides

Pell found the wind dying before anyone else did, because that was his job and because he'd spent thirty years learning to hear what the Halcyon heard.

The kite-lines went slack first. Then the great wing-sail overhead began to sigh, the way a tired animal sighs when it's been asked to work past its strength. He stood at the base of the mast with his palm flat against the hull and felt the ship's heartbeat slow, that faint tremor of speed running down through the timbers into his boots, and he thought: *there it goes. There goes our margin.*

"Wind's quitting on us," he called up to the deck. "Like everything else in this town."

"Cheerful," said Juno, not looking up from her charts. She never looked up from her charts when he was being cheerful. It was a system they'd worked out over years, and it worked.

They were three days out from Greywater Point, running south with the Shoals still a rumor on the horizon, and the whole crew had that particular quality of silence that comes from everyone knowing exactly what they're sailing toward and nobody saying it out loud. Bram had the wheel. Wren was in the bowsprit netting, which she wasn't supposed to be, doing something with her face turned into the wind that Pell chose not to examine too closely, because the girl had been strange since the tavern and stranger since the charts came out, and strangeness in a twelve-year-old was usually just twelve.

The dying wind was the problem. The Halcyon was a kite-ship, built to run on the high steady trades that came off the open water like rivers of air, and without them she was a very expensive barge with opinions. They had six tides to reach the Meridian Bell before the corporate fleet did, and the sea had apparently decided to spend one of them napping.

Pell went below to check the trim, which is what he told himself, and to worry in private, which is what he actually did.

* * *

The Halcyon's belly was the only place on the ship Pell had ever considered his. Bram had the helm, Juno had the charts, and Pell had ninety tons of ballast, bilge, and stubbornness arranged in the dark like a cathedral built by a drunk engineer. He loved it down there. It smelled of tar and salt and old iron, and nothing down here asked him questions about his feelings.

He was halfway along the port ballast channel, tapping hull plates with his knuckle in the old rhythm — *sound, sound, sound* — when his knuckle told him something was wrong.

Every plate had a voice. That was the first thing they taught you in the yards: iron sings, and it sings differently when it's sound and differently when it's cracked and differently when it's been *moved*. Pell had spent so much of his life with his ear against iron that Juno claimed he'd rust from the inside out, and he'd never once bothered to argue.

This plate sang wrong.

Not cracked. Not loose. *Wrong* the way a room is wrong when the furniture's been shifted two inches while you were out. He crouched, got his lamp in close, and saw it.

The ballast blocks were seated in cradles of oak and iron, and every cradle had a set of shims — thin wedges, tuned by hand, by *him*, over years, so the weight sat exactly where the Halcyon wanted it. Somebody had pulled the shims. Not all of them. The forward port cluster only — maybe a dozen blocks, shifted aft and outboard by the width of two fingers each.

Two fingers. In ninety tons of ballast. It was nothing. It was everything. It would cost them maybe a knot and a half of speed in a light wind, and it would do it invisibly, and it would make the ship handle just a little bit drunk on every port turn, and nobody would ever be able to prove why.

Pell sat down on the bilge stringer and looked at it for a long time.

Somebody wanted them slow.

* * *

He climbed back into the daylight and stood there blinking like something hauled out of a well, and the first thing he saw was the launch.

It came up over the horizon about an hour past noon — a long grey knife of a boat, running under auxiliary screw with her sails furled like folded wings, and she held their wake at exactly a mile back with the patience of something that had all day and knew it. Vess Karrow's launch. Vess Karrow, who had stood in the Drowned Lantern with her charts and her coin and her smile like a knife laid flat on the table, and who had then — and this was the part Pell kept chewing on — *invited herself along*.

Bram had said no. Bram had said no in the particular flat voice he used when a thing was already decided. And Vess had smiled, and said something about how the sea was free, and how she'd hate to get lost on the way to the Shoals, and how it would be a shame if a certain syndicate heard from a certain anonymous correspondent about a certain salvage job. And then her launch had been provisioned before the Halcyon's lines were even cast off, which meant she'd never planned to ask.

"She's back," Wren said, from the netting.

"I see her," Bram said.

"She's *always* back. She's like weather."

Pell grunted. He crossed to the rail and stood beside Bram at the wheel, and for a while they watched the grey knife hold its distance, and neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation.

"Wind's dying," Pell said finally.

"I know."

"Ballast's wrong."

Bram's head came around. "Wrong how?"

Pell told him. All of it — the shims, the cluster, the two fingers, the knot and a half. He watched Bram's face do the arithmetic, because Bram was a weather-reader and a debt-reader and a people-reader, and Pell could practically see the sums running behind his eyes. *Who. When. How long have they known where we'd be.*

"Could've been the yard crew," Bram said. "Greywater's full of Optimist ears."

"Could've been." Pell scratched under his cap. "Yard crew wouldn't have known to take *those* shims. That's not sabotage, that's *tuning*. That's somebody who knows kite-ships. Somebody who wanted us just slow enough to matter and never slow enough to notice."

"Vess."

"Vess has a fast launch and a screw engine. She doesn't need us slow. She could pass us in her sleep." Pell turned and looked back at the grey knife on the horizon. "Somebody wants the *Halcyon* slow. Somebody who wants us to get there late. Or wants us to get there *tired*."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. The dying wind made the rigging creak overhead, a sound like an old house settling, and out in the bowsprit netting Wren had gone very still, her head cocked the way she'd been cocking it for three days now, like she was listening to something under the water.

"Tell no one," Bram said at last. "Fix it tonight, quiet, and tell no one. If it's someone aboard, I want to see their hands twitch."

"And if it's not someone aboard?"

"Then it's someone who was in the yard," Bram said, "and I'll want to know who paid them, and I'll want to know it before the Shoals. Because Pell —" and here his voice dropped into that register he got sometimes, the one that made the hair on Pell's arms stand up, the one that had nothing of the cheerful debtor about it and everything of the man who'd lost Tam Coldharbour's brother to the water — "because somebody's playing a longer game than the tide, and I'm tired of being the piece that moves."

* * *

Pell fixed it that night, alone, by lamplight, with the ship rolling gentle and windless under him like a sleeping animal.

It took four hours. He re-shimmed every block, checked the work twice, and then sat in the dark of the ballast channel with his back against the hull and did what he always did when his hands were finished and his head wasn't: he thought.

Twelve years ago, the Halcyon had gone into the Siren Shoals with five souls aboard and come out with four. Pell had been at the engine that day. He'd heard the singing come up through the hull — that low, sweet, impossible note — and he'd felt the ship *want* to follow it, the way a dog wants to follow a whistle, and he'd spent twelve years telling himself it was the current, it was the pressure, it was anything but a song. Tam had gone into the water. Tam had not come out. And Bram had never once, in twelve years, said how.

Pell had stopped asking. That was the deal none of them had spoken aloud: Bram didn't say, and they didn't ask, and the Halcyon stayed out of the Shoals, and the four of them became a family the way shipwreck survivors become a family — by agreeing never to discuss the shipwreck.

And now here they were, sailing back in, six tides on the clock, with a rival riding their wake and a saboteur's fingerprints in the ballast and a corporate fleet three days behind them and a syndicate of smiling leg-breakers holding their paper. And a girl in the netting who kept tilting her head like she could hear her name being called from under the water.

Pell didn't believe in curses. He believed in iron, and iron had never lied to him. But he sat in the dark that night with his palm flat against the hull, and the hull was quiet, and somehow the quiet was worse.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and windless, the sixth morning of nothing, and the sea lay around them like a held breath.

Pell came up with his tea and found the whole crew gathered at the rail, all of them looking south, none of them talking. The horizon had changed. Not with weather — with *sound*. Or the beginning of sound. A hum, so low it was less a noise than a pressure, coming up through the deck boards and into the soles of their feet.

Wren heard it first. Of course Wren heard it first.

"There," she said, and pointed at nothing, at the empty shining water south of them. "Do you hear it?"

Nobody answered. Bram's jaw was set. Juno's hands had gone still on her charts. Vess stood at the stern rail by her trailing launch, and Pell watched her — watched her closely, because his knuckles had learned to hear iron and his eyes had learned to hear people — and he saw her smile.

It was a small smile. A satisfied one. The smile of a woman checking a pocket watch against a clock she trusted.

Pell looked at her, and then back at the empty water, and then down at his own boots, where the hum was rising through the deck like something coming up for air, and he counted what was left.

Six tides, Vess had said in the tavern. Six tides before the fleet.

The first one was already gone.
