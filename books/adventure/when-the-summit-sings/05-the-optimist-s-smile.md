## 5. The Optimist's Smile

The horizon off Greywater Point had a way of lying to you.

Bram Coldharbour stood at the Halcyon's rail with his third cup of chicory going cold in his hand and watched the morning do its best work: gold light laid flat across a sea so calm it looked poured rather than grown, a few gulls stitching lazy seams overhead, the wind out of the southwest at a polite six knots—enough to fill the kite-sails without ever asking anything of anyone. It was the kind of day that made you forget you were sailing toward water that had already taken one of your people.

The sea was good at that. So were smiles.

"Captain's drinking again," Pell announced from the deck below, where she was elbow-deep in the winch housing with grease to her elbows and, somehow, one streak of it across her forehead like war paint applied by a drunk artist. "Third cup before eight bells. That's either dedication or dread."

"It's chicory," Bram said.

"It's *dread* with chicory in it."

Wren laughed from her perch on the cargo netting, where she'd been sitting for an hour with a chart across her knees, copying Juno's soundings into her own notebook in handwriting too careful for a seventeen-year-old who claimed not to care about anything. She didn't look up when she said, "He's counting."

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting," Wren said. "You do the thing with your thumb. Against your fingers. You've done it four times since I sat down."

Bram looked at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. Sure enough, his thumb was resting against his index finger, mid-count. Five tides left. He put the hand flat on the rail like he was pinning it there.

Juno appeared at the companionway with the morning's position worked out and the expression of a woman who had slept exactly as much as the situation deserved, which was to say barely. "Current's set us half a point north of my line," she said. "I've corrected. We'll raise the outer shoal markers by tomorrow's low tide if this wind holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we drift, and I navigate by argument." She took the cold chicory from his hand, drank it without asking, and made a face. "This is a crime against beans."

"It's chicory."

"It's *punishment* with chicory in it." She handed it back and leaned on the rail beside him, shoulder almost touching his shoulder, which from Juno was a whole conversation. "You slept?"

"Some."

"Bram."

"I slept," he lied, warmly, and she let him have it because they both knew the ledger of small lies between old friends balances out eventually.

That was when Wren said, "Sail," in the flat voice she used for things that mattered, and the beautiful morning stopped being beautiful.

* * *

It came out of the sun-glare to the west, which was either very rude or very deliberate—a low skiff with a black hull and a sail dyed the color of fresh cream, running clean and fast with more canvas than any honest vessel would carry. Bram knew the silhouette before he knew the flag. Everyone on these coasts knew the silhouette. You learned it the way you learned the smell of a storm: early, and in your bones.

"Optimists," Pell said, spitting over the windward rail, which was redundant given how much of the sea she'd already cursed into it.

"They're not due," Juno said, already moving to the wheel. "Collection isn't for thirty-five days."

"They're not collecting," Bram said quietly.

He watched the skiff come on. It wasn't running an intercept course—not exactly. It was running a *courteous* one, a course that would bring it alongside in its own time whether the Halcyon cooperated or not, the way a tide is courteous. The cream sail caught the light and seemed to glow. Somewhere under that glow, Bram knew, were men whose job title included the word *happiness liaison*, and who could break your wrist so gently you'd thank them for the lesson.

"Wren, below. Take the log book with you."

"I can help—"

"That wasn't a request dressed up as a discussion. Below."

She went, but slowly, the way young people go when they want you to know they're obeying under protest, and Bram let her have that too. There were only so many battles worth having on a ship this size, and most of them were with yourself.

The skiff closed. At two hundred yards, Bram could see them at the rail: three figures in pale linen, unhurried, one of them raising a hand in a wave so friendly it made the back of Bram's neck itch. At a hundred yards, he could see the man at the bow clearly, and his stomach performed a slow, professional drop.

He knew that face. Merrit Stave. Senior collector. The man who had smiled at Bram across a tavern table four years ago while explaining, with genuine warmth, what would happen to the Halcyon's keel if payments slipped again. Merrit Stave had never once raised his voice or his hand in Bram's presence. He had never needed to. His smile did all the lifting.

"Halcyon!" Stave called across the water, cheerful as a man greeting a cousin at a wedding. "Might we come alongside? The sea's being generous today and I hate to waste generosity!"

Bram weighed his options, all two of them. Running was pointless; the skiff carried more sail than pride should allow. Fighting was worse than pointless; it was arithmetic. "Come alongside, then," he called back. "Mind my paint."

"We always mind your paint, Captain! It's one of your finest qualities!"

* * *

They came aboard the way Optimists did everything: pleasantly, in numbers, and taking up more space than their bodies strictly required. Stave stepped onto the Halcyon's deck like a man stepping into his own garden. Two of his people stayed at the skiff's rail, smiling at nothing. A fourth came over the side last, carrying a wicker picnic basket, which was somehow worse than a weapon would have been.

"Captain Coldharbour." Stave beamed. He was a broad, comfortable man with silver at his temples and the eyes of someone who counted other people's breaths for a living. "You look well. Sailing agrees with you."

"Sailing *is* me, Merrit. What do you want?"

"Straight to business! I admire that. May we?" He gestured at the deck, the rail, the entire concept of hospitality, and Bram nodded once, stiffly. Stave's people arranged themselves along the port side with the ease of men who had done this on many ships, and the one with the basket began setting out cups.

"We're at sea," Bram said. "Nobody's drinking tea."

"Nonsense, it's chamomile. Very calming. I insist." Stave poured anyway, four cups, and offered one around until Pell, watching from the winch housing with a wrench still in her hand, finally took hers just to make him stop offering. "There. See? Civilization survives even out here." He sipped, sighed with pleasure, and turned his smile on Bram full-force. "Now. Business. We heard a rumor."

"Rumors are cheap."

"This one cost us a great deal, actually. Rumors about the Meridian Bell don't come cheap anywhere." Stave said it lightly, the way you'd mention weather, and Bram felt the deck tilt under him though the sea was flat as glass. "A wreck that sings. A resonance engine, intact, twelve years drowned and still humming. Do you know what our actuaries did when that rumor crossed their desks, Captain? They laughed. Then they stopped laughing. Then they started *counting*."

"We're a salvage crew under contract," Bram said. "That's all."

"Oh, I know. I know exactly what you are. Vess Karrow's contract, isn't it? Triple your debt, silence thrown in free." Stave's smile widened by a degree that should not have been possible. "We buy rumors from dock rats, Captain. Dock rats hear everything. It's why we feed them."

Pell set down her tea with exaggerated care. Juno had come forward from the wheel and stood now with her arms folded, her face doing its best impression of a locked door.

"So you know about the contract," Bram said. "Congratulations. Now say what you came to say."

"What I came to say." Stave set down his cup and clasped his hands behind his back, rocking slightly on his heels, a man about to share wonderful news. "Here's the thing about debts, Captain. Everyone thinks a debt is a rope. Something that pulls. But a debt is really a *hook*, and a hook works just as well coming toward you as it does reeling you in." He tilted his head toward the south, toward the Shoals neither of them was looking at directly, the way you don't look directly at the sun. "The Optimists have money on the Bell. Serious money. More than your debt. More than Karrow's offer, if it comes to that. And here's the part I want you to really hear, because I'm saying it as a friend, and I am, despite everything, genuinely your friend—"

"You're not my friend, Merrit."

"—the Bell will be recovered," Stave went on, unbothered, "by *someone*. The corporate fleet is three days behind you and losing ground, but they'll get there. Karrow means to take it herself—we've read her manifest, or enough of it. And you—" he opened his hands, presenting Bram to himself like a gift, "—you are sailing straight toward it with a debt around your neck and a crew you love, which makes you the most predictable man on this ocean. Everyone hunting that wreck, Captain, every single one of them, has already decided how your story ends. They're just arguing about who gets to write it."

The gulls cried somewhere overhead. The tea steamed politely in its cups. Bram became aware that his thumb was pressing against his forefinger again, counting, and this time he let it.

"And you?" he said. "What does the Optimists' ledger say happens to me?"

Stave's smile softened into something almost tender, which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet. "Our ledger says you pay. It always says that. It's a very reliable ledger." He picked up his cup again and drained it. "But ledgers only record, Captain. They don't decide. Men decide. And some men—" he tapped his own chest twice, gently, "—some men are hoping, for purely sentimental reasons, that you find a way to surprise us."

"Why?"

"Because I liked Tam," Stave said simply.

The name landed on the deck between them like something dropped from a height. Nobody moved. Pell's knuckles whitened on her wrench. Behind Bram, faintly, from below, he thought he heard Wren's pen stop scratching.

"He bought me a drink once, years ago, at the Gull's Rest," Stave went on, quieter now, the performance thinning just enough to show the man-shaped thing underneath. "Told me a joke about a harbormaster and a parrot. Terrible joke. I've remembered it for twelve years." He straightened, and the smile came back up like a shopkeeper's shutter. "So. Consider this visit a courtesy. A warning, freely given, from one professional to another: everyone believes your debt will be paid one way or another. The fleet thinks it. Karrow thinks it. My own board thinks it. The only question anyone's arguing about is *paid with what*."

He gathered his people with a small gesture, pleasant as calling for the check. At the rail he paused, one leg over the side, and looked back.

"One more thing, free of charge. Your ballast. Have your engineer check it." His eyes flicked, briefly, meaningfully, toward Pell. "Karrow's launch has been shadowing you since the point. She wants you in the Shoals, Captain—but she wants you *slow* when you get there. Think about why."

Then he dropped to his skiff, the cream sail filled, and the Optimists ran away westward into the glare, waving, three of them, cheerfully, until the sun swallowed them whole.

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The sea kept doing its gorgeous, indifferent morning. Somewhere below, Wren's pen started up again, faster than before.

"Ballast," Pell said at last. Her voice had gone flat and technical, which was how she sounded when she was frightened and refusing to admit it. "I checked the ballast two days ago. Before we cast off. It was clean."

"Check it again."

She was already moving, dropping through the hatch with a lantern, and the sounds that came up after her—metal on metal, a curse, a longer silence, a much worse curse—told the story before she climbed back up with her face gray above the grease-streak.

"Shot," she said. "Ingot molds packed with lead shot instead of ingots. Weighted right, weighted *careful*—someone wanted us riding heavy but stable. Not enough to feel wrong in calm water." She wiped her hands uselessly on her trousers. "Enough to feel very wrong in bad water. Enough to make us sluggish in a current. Somebody built us to arrive late and tired."

"Vess," Juno said.

"Or whoever Vess answers to," Bram said. "Or the fleet, if they've got divers we don't know about. Or—" He stopped. There was no point enumerating enemies; the list had gotten long enough to need its own chart.

"Stave told us," Pell said slowly. "Why would he tell us? He's *Optimist*. Everything he does serves the debt."

"Because a debtor who drowns pays nothing," Juno said. "And because—" she glanced at Bram, and finished differently, "—because he wanted us to know we're surrounded. Fear is a tool. He was sharpening it and handing it to us, handle first."

"He said he liked Tam," Wren said.

They all turned. She had come up the hatchway without anyone noticing, the log book hugged to her chest, her chin set at the angle she used when she'd decided a rule no longer applied. Bram should have sent her below again. He found he couldn't.

"You knew him?" she asked. "Tam. You knew him."

Everyone looked at Bram. The horizon stayed beautiful. The wind held soft and steady out of the southwest, filling sails that were carrying them, hour by hour, toward water that sang.

"I knew him," Bram said.

"What was he like?"

Bram looked south, where the Shoals waited under all that gold light, patient as a hook.

"Like a man who paid other people's debts," he said. "Go copy your soundings, Wren."

She went, reluctantly, and Juno moved to the wheel, and Pell went below to pull lead shot out of his ship's bones, and Bram stood alone at the rail with his cold cup and his counting hand, understanding at last the shape of the thing they were all sailing into.

Nobody expected him to come back from the Shoals with the Bell.

They expected him to come back with the bill.

Behind them, far to the east, low and fast and flying no colors at all, a second sail had risen over the horizon—and it was gaining.
