## 6. First Tide: Dead Water

Wren felt the change before anyone said a word about it.

It came up through the soles of her boots first, the way weather always did on the *Halcyon*—Pell swore the ship talked through its keel, and Wren had learned in three years aboard that he was right. The vibration of the hull changed pitch as they crossed some invisible line, like a singer shifting from chest voice to head voice, and then the wind died.

Not faded. Died. One moment the sails had been full and fat, the rigging humming its ordinary creaking song, and the next the canvas hung slack as laundry and the whole world went quiet enough that Wren could hear her own pulse in her ears.

"Dead water," Juno said, from the helm. She didn't sound alarmed. Juno never sounded alarmed; she sounded like a woman reading a grocery list that happened to contain the end of the world. "Outer ring. Everyone breathe."

Bram stood at the bow with his arms crossed, staring at the water ahead of them, and Wren watched his shoulders do the thing they did when he was counting something. Miles, probably. He was always counting miles.

The sea out here was wrong in a way Wren couldn't immediately name, and she prided herself on naming things—it was most of what she was good at. The water wasn't flat, exactly. It moved. Slow swells rolled under them from directions that made no sense, crossing each other at angles, sliding past without breaking, like a crowd of people all walking different ways who refused to bump shoulders. No whitecaps. No chop. The surface had the look of poured glass left out too long, slightly warm, slightly tired.

"It's like the currents canceled each other out," Pell said, coming up from below with grease on his hands and a frown already installed on his face. He wiped them on a rag that was itself mostly grease, which Wren had long ago stopped commenting on. "Every current running into this ring just... stops arguing. Look at the driftwood." He pointed. A branch floated off the port beam, perfectly still, not turning, not bobbing. Just sitting there like it had been nailed down. "Water doesn't do that."

"The Shoals do it," Bram said. His voice was flat in the way Wren had learned meant *don't ask*. She asked anyway, because someone had to, and because she'd noticed that Bram answered her questions more often than he answered anyone else's. Maybe because she was young. Maybe because she reminded him of somebody. She'd decided not to wonder too hard about that.

"Why does the wind stop?"

Nobody answered. Pell looked at his rag. Juno looked at her charts. Bram looked at the horizon like it owed him money, which, Wren reflected, everything seemed to these days.

She let it go. That was another thing she'd learned aboard the *Halcyon*: this crew carried its silences around like ballast, and you didn't start throwing ballast overboard just because it made the ship ride heavy.

* * *

They drifted for an hour. Then two. The sun climbed and sat there, directly overhead, as if even it had decided not to move.

Wren took the opportunity to do what she always did, which was to be quietly everywhere at once. She checked the sounding line. She coiled rope that didn't need coiling. She lay on her back against the mainmast and memorized the shapes of the clouds, which were stacked up on every horizon like an audience waiting for something to start.

It was while she was lying there, ear against the mast, that she heard it.

At first she thought it was Pell's engine room—the little auxiliary thumper he'd built himself, which ran hot and grumbled like an old man climbing stairs. But Pell's engine had a rhythm to it, a lurching two-beat, and this was nothing like that. This was smooth. This was held. A single tone, low and round, more felt than heard, rising up through the wood of the mast and into her skull where it settled behind her eyes like a hand pressed gently against the inside of her forehead.

She sat up.

The tone didn't stop. If anything, it got clearer, the way a voice gets clearer when the person speaking steps closer.

"Wren?" Juno had seen her sit up. Juno saw everything; Wren was fairly sure the woman had extra eyes stored somewhere. "What is it?"

"There's a—" Wren stopped. Her mouth had opened to say what it sounded like, and then closed again on its own, because the honest answer was sitting in her chest like a stone and she could not, for reasons she couldn't explain, bring herself to say it out loud. "There's a sound. In the hull. In the mast. It's coming from underneath us."

Pell was down the hatch and back up in thirty seconds flat, which Wren suspected was a personal record, holding a length of pipe to his ear like a physician's horn. He pressed the other end against the deck. His eyebrows climbed.

"Well," he said. "Huh."

"'Well, huh' isn't a diagnosis," Juno said.

"No, but it's an accurate one." Pell rotated slowly, pipe to ear, like a very confused sundial. "It's strongest toward—" he swung, stopped, swung a little more, stopped again, and pointed dead astern and slightly to port, out over the glass-flat water. "That way. And it's not machinery. Machinery has harmonics. This has..." He struggled. Wren knew exactly how he felt. "...this has *patience*."

"That's not a word for a sound," Bram said.

"It is now." Pell handed him the pipe. Bram took it, held it a moment, and then—Wren watched this closely—set it down on the deck without putting it to his ear at all.

"I don't need it," he said. "I know what's down there."

And the way he said it—the way his jaw went tight around the words like he was biting down on them to keep them from escaping—made Wren's stomach drop in a way that had nothing to do with the swells.

Because she knew what the sound was. She'd known since the moment she'd sat up against the mast. She knew exactly what it sounded like, the way you know the shape of your own name, and she would not say it, because saying it would make it true, and if it became true then the last twelve years of Bram Coldharbour's careful, practiced, mile-counting distance from this exact patch of ocean would collapse all at once, and she did not think he would survive it, and she was not entirely sure she would either.

The bell sounded like Tam.

Not like a memory of Tam. Not like grief wearing Tam's face. Like Tam—like the particular warm roughness of his laugh, the hum he used to make when he was working and thought nobody was listening, the note he'd hold under his breath when he was happy without noticing he was happy. Wren had never met Tam. Tam had drowned twelve years before Wren was old enough to be anyone's crew. She knew him only from stories told in fragments, from the way the whole crew went gentle and strange whenever his name surfaced, from the photograph Juno kept folded in the back of her chart case that everyone pretended not to know about.

And yet the sound in the hull was unmistakably, impossibly, *him*, and Wren was seventeen years old and holding a secret that felt like a drowning person clinging to her ankles.

"You've gone pale," Juno said to her, quietly, close beside her. When had Juno moved? "What does it sound like?"

Wren looked at her. Juno's eyes were steady and kind and absolutely unfooled, and Wren understood in that moment that Juno probably already knew—that she'd felt it too, or guessed, or simply read it off Wren's face the way she read currents off water—and that she was asking anyway, giving Wren the choice, offering her the door without pushing her through it.

"I can't say," Wren whispered.

Juno nodded once, slow, like Wren had passed some kind of test. "Then don't. Not yet. But listen to me, love." She took Wren's chin gently in one calloused hand. "Whatever it sounds like—you tell me before you tell the sea. Understood? The sea's had its turn with this crew. It doesn't get another one free."

* * *

By late afternoon the light had gone gold and syrupy, and the *Halcyon* still hadn't moved—not under sail, at least. Pell got the auxiliary engine coughing, and they crept forward at walking pace, which was somehow worse than drifting, because now they were choosing to go deeper into the ring instead of being carried.

Bram stood at the helm beside Juno, and the two of them navigated in a way Wren had never seen before.

There was no wind to steer by. There were no clean currents to read—the water here lied, crossed itself, cancelled its own promises. So Juno did something else. She had Pell kill the engine for stretches at a time, and in those silences she'd stand with her bare feet planted wide on the deck, eyes half-closed, one hand resting flat on the rail, and *listen*.

"Helm a point to starboard," she'd murmur. "There's a seam here. Feel it?"

And Bram would feel it—through the wheel, through his boots, through whatever old animal sense twelve years of storm-chasing had grown in him—and the *Halcyon* would slide along a channel of moving water invisible to every eye aboard.

"She's navigating by the wreck," Pell said to Wren, sotto voce, as they coiled lines together in the gathering dusk. "By the hum. It carries differently through different water. She's reading it like a chart."

"Is that possible?"

"Oh, absolutely not. Completely impossible. She's doing it anyway." Pell grinned, but the grin had a wobble in it. "That woman could navigate out of a locked drawer."

Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin, and Pell noticed—Pell always noticed—and his grin softened into something more honest.

"You heard it first," he said. Not a question.

She nodded.

"And you won't say what it sounds like."

She shook her head.

Pell was quiet a moment, coiling. Then: "Smart. Some things you say out loud and they get bigger. Some things you keep in your chest and they stay the size of your chest." He patted the rail. "Chest's bigger than you'd think. But it's not infinite. So when it starts getting too big—" he tapped her shoulder, twice, gently, "—you come find me or Juno. Deal?"

"Deal," Wren said, and meant it, and felt the stone in her chest shift maybe a quarter inch, which was more relief than she'd expected from a quarter inch.

* * *

Night fell over the dead water like a lid.

No wind meant no waves meant no sound of waves, and Wren discovered that she had never in her life heard true silence until now. The sea made no noise at all. The stars came out in brutal quantity, doubled in the mirror-flat water, so that the *Halcyon* seemed to hang suspended between two skies, sailing through space.

And in that silence, the hum grew louder.

Or perhaps it didn't grow louder—perhaps everything else simply stepped aside to let it through. It rose through the keel, through the deck, through the soles of Wren's feet where she sat wrapped in a blanket near the stern lamp, and it filled the hull the way water fills a cup, patiently, completely.

One by one, the others came to hear it. She watched them. Pell sat cross-legged by the engine hatch with his pipe-to-ear contraption abandoned, just listening with his whole body, his head tilted like a bird's. Juno stood at the helm with her hands folded, her face unreadable and therefore shouting volumes. Even Bram came, eventually, moving along the edges of the lamplight, and stood at the taffrail with his back to all of them, and Wren saw his knuckles whiten on the rail and saw him make no move whatsoever to leave.

He knows, she thought. He's known since we crossed the line. He knows what's singing down there and he sailed us straight toward it anyway, because Vess Karrow bought his silence and his skills and apparently his self-destruction along with them.

The hum shifted. Subtly. The way a sleeper shifts in bed.

Wren's breath caught.

For a moment—one long, golden, terrible moment—it wasn't just a tone anymore. There was texture in it. Movement. Something almost like words, almost like a melody trying to remember itself, reaching up through twelve years and forty fathoms of dark water toward the surface, toward the hull, toward—

Toward her. Toward all of them. Toward whoever was listening.

"Wren." Juno's voice, quiet, right beside her. When had she— the woman moved like weather. "You're crying, love."

Wren touched her own cheek and found it wet and had no memory of it starting.

"I'm fine," she said, which was the least true sentence ever spoken aboard the *Halcyon*, and Juno had the grace not to say so.

"What does it sound like?" Juno asked again, softer this time. "You don't have to tell me what it *is*. Just what it sounds like."

And Wren opened her mouth, and the hum rose beneath them, patient and warm and unbearably familiar, and the truth climbed her throat like something drowned finally coming up for air, and she said:

"It sounds like someone humming while they work. Someone who thinks nobody's listening. Someone who's—" her voice cracked, "—someone who's been down there a long time, and kept humming anyway, in case anybody ever came back."

The silence after that was total. Even the sea seemed to hold still.

Above them, at the taffrail, Bram Coldharbour turned around very slowly, and in the lamplight Wren saw that his face had come apart—all the practiced detachment, all the counted miles, cracked straight down the middle like ice in spring thaw—and he looked at her the way a man looks at a door he buried years ago that has just, impossibly, opened.

"How long," he said, and his voice was gravel, "has it been singing like that?"

"Since we crossed the outer ring," Wren whispered. "Maybe longer. Maybe it's been singing the whole time, and we just couldn't hear it."

Bram looked out over the black glass water, toward the place Pell's instruments said the wreck lay waiting, and when he spoke again it was barely above the silence.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we find out what it wants."

And far below them, in the drowned cathedral of the Siren Shoals, the Meridian Bell hummed on—patient, warm, and waiting—and Wren could have sworn, though she never said it aloud, that the note it held was the exact note a man makes when he sees his friends coming home.
