## 7. Second Tide: The Fleet's Wake

The atoll appeared on the second morning like a rumor made solid—a smudge of green on the horizon that Juno confirmed with the glass, then confirmed again, as if hoping a third look would change it.

"Supply station," she said. "Coriander Cay. Fresh water, coal, and—" she lowered the glass and her voice with it— "nine ships."

Bram took the glass from her. The fleet sat in the lee of the atoll like dogs waiting for their master to finish an errand: three salvage barges heavy in the water, four tenders, two escort cutters with their guns run out but not aimed at anything yet. Corporate hulls, corporate colors—the pale blue-and-white livery of the Meridian Combine, painted fresh enough to gleam even at this distance.

"Nine," Pell repeated over his shoulder, elbow-deep in the winch housing he'd been cursing since dawn. "That's not a salvage operation. That's an occupation."

"They're anchored," Wren said quietly. She was up in the rigging where she'd spent most of the last day, and she spoke the way she'd been speaking since the Shoals' outer ring—carefully, like someone carrying water across ice. "Not moving. Why aren't they moving?"

"Because they can't be," Vess said.

She was leaning against the rail by the mainmast, arms crossed, watching the fleet through her own glass with an expression Bram could only describe as satisfaction wearing a disguise. She'd been insufferable since dawn, which was to say, normal.

"The harbormaster at Coriander owes me money," she went on. "Or rather, I paid him enough that he now owes me discretion. Their charters have to be countersigned before they can weigh anchor for the Shoals. His signature is currently... unavailable."

"You bribed a port official," Bram said.

"I *invested* in a delay." She smiled at him. "There's a difference. One is a crime. The other is logistics."

"How long?"

"Three days. Maybe four if he develops a sudden crisis of conscience, which I paid him not to have." She snapped her glass shut. "We're three days behind them and exactly even, thanks to me. You're welcome, by the way. I notice nobody's said it."

Nobody said it. Pell went back to swearing at the winch. Juno turned to her charts. And Bram stood at the rail looking at those nine white-and-blue hulls and felt the old familiar arithmetic turning over in his chest—the same arithmetic that had kept him alive through twelve years of storms: *what does this cost, and who pays it?*

Because here was the thing about Vess Karrow's generosity. It always came with a receipt.

* * *

He found her that evening in the stern, eating cold rice out of the pot with the unselfconscious appetite of a woman who had never once worried what anyone thought of her table manners. The Halcyon had put distance between herself and the atoll, running dark with sails double-reefed, and the sky was doing one of its slow gold-to-violet dissolves that Bram normally would have stopped to appreciate. Tonight he barely saw it.

"We need to talk about Coriander," he said.

"Do we?" She didn't look up from the pot. "I told you everything. Bribed official, stalled fleet, three days. It's not complicated, Bram. Even you can follow it."

"Why stall them at all?"

She looked up at that. "Because every day they sit at anchor is a day we gain. That was the entire point."

"No." He crouched down so they were eye level, which with Vess meant giving up the high ground and knowing it. "Stalling them costs you nothing and gains you time—that's sense. But you did it *quietly*. No note to us, no plan shared, nothing. Juno found out because Pell heard it from a supply runner at the last island, third-hand, garbled. You let us sail into those waters thinking we were three days behind a fleet that was already moving." He paused. "You wanted us scared. Scared crews dive faster."

Vess set down the pot. Slowly. Which, from Vess, was the equivalent of another woman setting down a knife.

"And did it work?" she asked pleasantly. "Are you diving faster?"

"That's not the point."

"It's entirely the point. It's the only point you people ever have." She stood, brushing rice off her trousers, and looked at him with something that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite amusement—something older than both. "You want to know why I didn't tell you? Because you'd have argued. Because you'd have called a crew meeting and Juno would have made one of her lists and Pell would have raised six objections and the quiet girl would have stared at her boots, and we'd have lost half a day being a *democracy* while the Combine's lawyers filed paperwork. I bought us three days. I don't need your permission to buy three days."

"You needed our trust, though. And you just spent it."

"Oh, Bram." She laughed, short and genuine, which was somehow worse. "Trust. From you. The man who's been running from one thing for twelve years and calls it seamanship."

The words landed the way she'd clearly intended them to—under the ribs, angled up. Bram kept his face still. He'd had practice. Twelve years of practice.

"You don't know anything about it," he said.

"Don't I?"

"No."

"Then say so and mean it." She stepped closer. The light was nearly gone now; her face was mostly shadow and outline, and somehow that made her voice worse, because there was nothing left to watch but the words coming. "Because here's my problem, Coldharbour. I've known you twenty years. I've raced you, lost to you, beaten you once and never let you forget the other nine times. And in all that time I have never once seen you look at open water the way you look at the Siren Shoals. Like it's a door you locked from the inside and threw away the key, except the key was a person."

"Vess."

"Tam Coldharbour," she said.

The name went through him like a keel through thin ice.

The sea made a small sound against the hull. Somewhere forward, Pell dropped something metal and swore about it in the tone of a man grateful for the interruption. The gold was fully gone from the sky now, and the first stars were out, indifferent.

"What did you say?" Bram asked. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of that.

"You heard me." Vess held up a hand before he could speak again. "And before you decide how angry to be—decide instead whether you want to hear it or throw me overboard, because I'll tell you either way, but only one of those ways ends with you getting what you actually want, which is to know."

"You don't know how he died." Bram stood too. They were close enough now that he could see her eyes again, catching the lamplight from the cabin below. "Nobody knows how he died. There were four of us on the boat when the Shoals took him, and four of us came back, and none of us has said his name out loud in twelve years except Juno, once, drunk, and she apologized for a week."

"I know," Vess said, "because I've spent twelve years asking questions you people refuse to answer. Do you know what happens to a story when everyone involved agrees to stop telling it? It doesn't die. It grows teeth. People fill the holes with whatever frightens them most. Half the coast thinks the Siren Shoals ate him whole. The other half thinks you did."

The last sentence hung between them like a hook waiting for a jaw.

"I know that's not true," Vess said, softer now. "I've always known that's not true. Because I know you, Bram Coldharbour, and whatever else you are—and you are a great many irritating things—you are not a man who lets his people drown. You're a man who lets himself drown instead and calls it ballast."

For one absurd moment Bram wanted to laugh, because that was almost exactly what Juno had said to him once, years ago, at three in the morning, and he'd never figured out whether the two of them had learned to talk from the same terrible book.

"So tell me," he said. "If you know so much. How did he die?"

Vess was quiet for a moment. When she answered, she didn't perform it. No flourish, no smile. That was how Bram knew, later, that whatever she said next was at least partly true—or that she believed it was, which on the open sea amounted to the same currency.

"He died buying you time," she said. "The engine was already singing. You felt it—I know you felt it, because the Meridian Bell's resonance doesn't ask permission, it just opens you up and pours itself in. Tam understood what it was doing before any of you did. So he went down alone, into the coral, to jam the housing manually, to buy the Halcyon time to clear the ring before the song pulled you all onto the reef." She paused. "It worked. You cleared the ring. He didn't come back up. And you have spent twelve years telling yourself a gentler version, because the truth requires you to admit that a man died *for* you, specifically, by name, on purpose, and that you sailed away and let the sea keep him."

Bram discovered he was gripping the rail hard enough to hurt his hands. He made himself let go, finger by finger.

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Where did you get that?"

"A diver. Off Sable Row. Eleven years ago. He'd been part of the Combine's first survey of the wreck, before they sealed the site. He heard things. Read logs. The Bell records, Bram—it doesn't just sing, it *remembers*. Everything that happens inside its voice, it keeps." Her eyes were steady on his. "Which means somewhere down in that cathedral of coral, your brother's last minutes might still be playing. On repeat. For twelve years."

The world tilted very slightly, the way it did when a swell comes from a direction the hull isn't braced for. Bram reached for the rail again and this time let himself hold it.

Tam. Not gone. Not scattered. *Recorded.* Held in the throat of the thing that killed him, singing his last moments into the dark on an endless loop, and the Combine had known, and Vess had known for eleven years, and neither of them—

"You're lying," he said, but without force, and they both knew it.

"I might be," Vess agreed, with brutal honesty. "I might be wrong. The diver might have lied to me, or embellished, or sold me a story I wanted to buy. I'm not offering you certainty, Bram. I'm offering you a reason to keep sailing east instead of turning this ship around, and we both know you'd have found a way to do that anyway, because underneath all that weathered nobility you want to see it as badly as I do." She picked up her pot of rice again, conversationally, as though they'd been discussing wind patterns. "But here's what I'd think about tonight, if I were you. If the Bell remembers—and Pell will confirm it within a day of getting instruments on it, mark me—then this stop isn't just about an engine anymore. It's about what's *inside* the engine. And everybody racing us wants the shell. Nobody's thought about the song."

She turned to go, then paused at the cabin door.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "I'm sorry. About the way I told you. There wasn't a good way, and I chose the fast one, because that's the only kind I have." A beat. "Also, the rice needs salt. Your engineer seasons like a man afraid of flavor."

The door closed behind her.

* * *

Bram stayed at the rail a long time after that. The stars wheeled. The Shoals lay somewhere ahead in the dark, singing to themselves, holding whatever they held.

Juno found him around midnight. She didn't ask what Vess had said—she'd have gotten it out of Pell, who'd have gotten it from listening at the hatch like a man with no shame and excellent hearing. She just leaned on the rail beside him and offered him the flask, and he took it, and for a while they watched the water together.

"She's lying," Bram said finally. "Or guessing. Or telling me exactly what I need to hear to keep me pointed east."

"Probably," Juno said.

"But if she's right—"

"Then we're not salvaging an engine anymore." Juno took the flask back. In the dark her voice was very steady, the way it got when steadiness was costing her something. "Bram. Whatever's down there—whatever the Bell kept—we get in, we get the core, we get out. We don't listen. Whatever it sings, however much it sounds like—" She stopped herself. Started over. "Twelve years ago we agreed the Shoals keep what they take. That agreement is the only reason any of us can sleep near these waters. Don't let her take that from you too."

Bram looked east, toward the invisible reef, toward the drowned bell and whatever voice it might still be keeping.

"Too late," he said.

Ahead of them, faint under the wind, so faint it might have been the hull settling or might have been imagination, the sea began—very softly—to hum.
