## 8. Third Tide: The Drowned Bell

Pell had a rule about diving that she'd never told anyone: she didn't.

Twelve years of storm-chasing on the Halcyon and she'd kept her feet dry in every way that mattered. She could strip an engine blindfolded, rebuild a kite-rig in a gale, and jury-rig a bilge pump out of a soup kettle and spite, but put her under water deeper than her own height and something in her chest closed like a fist. It wasn't fear exactly. It was more the feeling of being somewhere she hadn't been invited—like walking into a stranger's house and finding all the furniture arranged just so, and knowing they were watching from another room.

So naturally, here she was, standing on the dive platform at dawn with a weighted belt around her waist and Bram checking her regulator fittings like she was made of glass.

"You don't have to go," he said. "I can take the survey lines myself."

"And leave me up here to wonder?" Pell snorted, adjusting her mask strap. "I'd rather be terrified with information than terrified without it. That's basically my whole personality."

Bram smiled, but it was one of his thin ones, the kind that did more work for his face than it actually felt. He'd been wearing those since the Shoals' outer ring. Everyone had been quieter since Wren started hearing the hum through the hull two nights ago. Quieter, and more careful, the way people get when they're walking through a house where someone died.

"Juno's got the surface watch," Bram said. "Wren's on the line."

"I know. I built the line." Pell tested the reel, then the spare reel, then the knife on her belt, because checking things three times was also basically her whole personality. "Bram. If I say I'm coming up, I'm coming up. No heroics from me. Heroics are your department. I do competence and complaining."

"Understood." He squeezed her shoulder once, brief and warm, and Pell felt the squeeze settle somewhere behind her ribs and stay there. That was the thing about Bram Coldharbour nobody who owed him money ever understood: the man was mostly armor, but what was underneath was real, and heavy, and you carried a piece of it whether you asked to or not.

She stepped off the platform into water the color of dark glass.

* * *

The first ten fathoms were ordinary. Green-gold light, drifting motes, her own bubbles ticking upward past her face like small opinions she was glad to be rid of. Pell counted her breaths the way she counted everything—in fours, engineer's rhythm—and let the weight belt pull her down the guide line hand over hand.

At twenty fathoms the light changed.

It didn't dim so much as *reorganize*. The green gave way to a deep blue that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, and Pell realized she could see the bottom—not because the water was clear, but because something down there was faintly, patiently glowing. Not firelight. Not bioluminescence, or not only. A pale shimmer along the seafloor, laid out in long regular bands, like the veins of a leaf, like circuitry, like—

"Oh," she said into her regulator, and the word came out as a silver blob that wobbled away toward the surface carrying more meaning than most sentences manage.

The coral began at thirty fathoms.

She'd seen coral before, in charts and in Juno's old stories—the branching kind, the brain kind, the kind that looked like somebody's grandmother left it in the oven too long. This was none of those. This rose in columns. Pillars forty feet tall, fluted and ribbed, spaced with a regularity that no accident of nature produced, arching overhead until Pell was swimming down the nave of something. Her mind supplied the word before she could stop it: cathedral. The Siren Shoals had built a cathedral, and it had been building for longer than there had been anyone to walk its floor, and every pillar was furred with living color—violet, rust, bone-white—that pulsed very slightly, very slowly, in time.

In time with what, Pell did not want to know. She checked her gauge anyway, because checking things was how she prayed.

And there, at the far end of the nave, hanging in the apse of coral like the heart of the whole arrangement, was the Meridian Bell.

* * *

It was bigger than the charts said. Charts always lied about size—it was one of their charms—but this was different. The Bell hung inverted from a scaffold of fused coral and old salvage iron, and it must have massed forty tons if it massed an ounce, its bronze skin gone black-green with twelve years of patient sea. Around its mouth, the coral had grown inward in delicate lacework fingers, as if the reef were trying to hold it, or feed it, or maybe just keep it company.

And it was singing.

Not sound, exactly. Sound needed ears, and Pell's ears were full of water and her own pulse. This was lower than sound—a pressure, a warmth, a slow oscillation she felt in her teeth and the backs of her knees and the fillings in her molars. One note. Held. Unchanging. The same note Wren had heard through the hull two nights ago and refused, absolutely refused, to describe.

Pell drifted closer, and the engineer in her—the part of her that had been awake since she was nine years old taking apart her uncle's clock and getting whipped for it and doing it again the next week—woke up all the way and went very quiet, the way she went quiet in front of anything truly well-made.

Because the Bell was not a bell. Or rather, the bell was the smallest part of it. Clamped along its outer curve, half-swallowed by coral but unmistakable even now, ran the machinery: resonance coils wound in triple helixes, tuning vanes folded against the bronze like sleeping wings, and at the crown, where the clapper should have hung, the engine core itself—a sphere of dark metal veined with that same pale glow as the seafloor, pulsing in time with the pillars, in time with the lacework, in time, Pell understood with a lurch, with her own heartbeat, which had apparently decided to sync up without asking her permission.

She pressed her palm flat against the housing.

The vibration traveled up her arm and settled in her chest, and for one unguarded second Pell felt what the machine felt, or thought she did, which amounted to the same thing: patience. Enormous, geological patience. Twelve years underwater and the coils still held their charge. Twelve years and the tuning hadn't drifted a hair. Whoever built this thing had built it to wait, and it had waited, and it was not finished waiting.

Her breath hitched. Bubbles scattered. She pulled her hand back and made herself run numbers instead, because numbers were safe, numbers never sang.

Output estimate. She eyeballed the coil diameter, the vane span, the core mass, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing in her head since Vess Karrow first spread those charts across the tavern table and said *resonance engine* like it was a word everyone knew. Back then Pell had guessed. Now she knew. The draw from the ambient current alone—the dead water, the canceled tides, the whole strange stillness of the Shoals—was feeding it constantly. It wasn't running down. It was *charging*.

Pell did the sum twice because the first answer frightened her and the second answer frightened her identically.

That engine, coupled to a coastal grid, could power Greywater Point for a century. All of it. Every lamp, every crane, every desalination plant and forge and fish-canner from here to the Meridian Straits, with power left over to sell. It could end the coal barges overnight. It could make some harbor baron the richest man on the coast.

Or.

Pell looked at the tuning vanes again, really looked, and her stomach turned over slow and cold. Vanes that size weren't for efficiency. Vanes that size were for *projection*. Folded down, they kept the song close, a whisper, a lure, a hum through a hull that made a young navigator go quiet and strange. But unfolded—unfolded, that note wouldn't stay in the water. It would climb. It would ride the resonant frequencies of stone and timber and bone, and every harbor within—she did the range math and wished she hadn't—every harbor within two hundred miles would hear it. Feel it. And whatever the song did to minds—and Pell had watched Wren humming a melody nobody taught her, and had watched Juno's face while she watched Wren, and had drawn her own conclusions—whatever it did, it would do it to everyone, all at once, forever, and the man holding the switch would hold them too.

"It's a battery," she said to the water, and the water didn't answer, which somehow made it worse. "It's a battery and a whip and somebody built both ends of it on purpose."

Her air gauge read a third gone. Time to go. She took one last look—engineers always took one last look; it was practically liturgical—and that was when she saw the fresh marks.

Scratches on the lower housing. Clean ones. Recent ones. Tool marks, bright metal showing through the coral crust, in a pattern she recognized because she'd invented a version of it herself: someone had been here, recently, with equipment, taking measurements. Coupling points. Someone had mapped where the engine could be detached.

Vess's advance team. Of course. The woman didn't send flowers; she sent calipers.

Pell backed away slowly, and the Bell's note followed her, patient, warm, terrible, and she thought: *twelve years*. Twelve years this thing had sat in its cathedral, charging, waiting, singing one note to an empty ocean. And she thought something else, something that arrived fully formed and unwelcome, the way true things do:

*If it's been broadcasting for twelve years, what else has it been recording?*

She kicked for the guide line.

* * *

Surfacing was like being born twice, and just as undignified. Pell spat her regulator, hauled herself onto the platform, and lay there dripping while Wren wrapped a blanket around her shoulders with the brisk tenderness of someone who'd been counting seconds on the line and hadn't liked the total.

"You were down long," Wren said.

"I was thorough."

"You were talking to it. Juno heard you on the comm. You said 'oh' and then you didn't say anything for six minutes."

"The 'oh' was doing a lot of work." Pell pulled the mask off and scrubbed a hand through her wet hair. Above them, the Halcyon's rigging creaked its familiar vocabulary, and it had never sounded so much like home. "Where's Bram?"

"Dive deck. He went down after you—eastern transect. He'll want your report."

"He'll get it." Pell stood, legs wobbling in the honest way of legs that had just met a god and walked away. "Wren. Question. When you hear the hum—when it comes through the hull—does it feel like it's *saying* something? Or does it feel like it's remembering?"

Wren went still. She was good at still, was Wren; she could out-still a heron. For a moment Pell thought she wouldn't answer, and then she said, quietly, "Why would those be different?"

And Pell, who had spent the last hour with her hand on the machine's housing, who had felt that enormous patience, that long held note, thought: *because I think it might be both*, and didn't say it, because some answers you deliver in person, with diagrams, and preferably sitting down.

* * *

Bram surfaced twenty minutes later looking like a man who'd lost an argument with the ocean and was pretending otherwise. He came up the ladder, stripped his gear with quick angry movements, and said, "Report," before he'd even gotten his mask off, which was very Bram.

"Cathedral of coral, pillars forty feet, all pulsing in sync." Pell ticked it off on her fingers. "Bell intact. Engine intact. Coils charged after twelve years—the dead water's feeding it continuously, it's not draining, it's banking. Output potential—" she named the figure, and watched Bram's face do the thing faces do when a number stops being abstract. "Coupled to a grid, that's a century of light for the whole coast. Unfolded at full projection, it's a voice that carries two hundred miles and doesn't ask permission to be heard."

Silence. Juno had come down from the wheelhouse and stood at the rail with her arms crossed, and Wren had drifted close without seeming to move, and the four of them stood in a little wet huddle on the dive deck while the Shoals glowed faintly beneath the hull like a lamp under a blanket.

"There's more," Pell said. "Fresh tool marks on the housing. Survey scratches. Somebody's already mapped the detachment points."

"Vess," Bram said.

"Vess," Pell agreed. "Advance team, probably months ago. She didn't charter us to salvage a wreck, Bram. She chartered us to unscrew a plug she's already measured."

Juno spoke for the first time, her voice level in the way that meant the opposite of level underneath. "Can it be done? Detached and moved?"

"Anything can be moved if you hate it enough." Pell rubbed her face. "Yes. It can be done. That's the problem. If it couldn't be done, we could all go home and drink about it."

Bram was staring down through the green water, at the pale banded glow below, and Pell watched him the way she watched overloaded bearings—with professional sympathy and a running estimate of time to failure.

"Power a city," he said quietly. "Or a weapon."

"Or a church," Wren said.

They all looked at her. She shrugged, small, unbothered, her eyes on the water.

"It's been down there twelve years, singing to nobody. Maybe it wasn't singing to nobody."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it *with*, and the Halcyon rocked gently at anchor over the drowned cathedral, and beneath them, patient as geology, the Bell held its single note—warm, enormous, and, Pell thought with a fresh chill she kept strictly to herself, *getting louder*.

She was almost sure it was getting louder.

She decided to check her instruments three times before dinner.
