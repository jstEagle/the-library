## 9. Fourth Tide: What Sang to Tam

Juno had a rule about counting. You counted what you could hold in your hands—knots, fathoms, coins, days—and you let the rest go uncounted, because the things you couldn't hold had a way of multiplying the moment you gave them numbers.

She was breaking her rule at dawn on the fourth tide, standing at the Halcyon's rail and counting the ways this was going to end badly.

One: Wren hadn't slept. Two: Wren hadn't spoken since the dive yesterday, except once, at midnight, when Juno found her sitting cross-legged by the mast and asked if she was all right, and Wren had said, "It's so *tidy* down there," in a voice that wasn't quite her own. Three: Bram had ordered no one in the water today, and Wren had said "yes, Captain" in that same voice, which meant nothing, because Wren agreeing with an order was like Pell agreeing with a maintenance schedule—the words came out fine and the follow-through was anyone's guess.

Four—and this was the one Juno kept circling back to, the stone in her shoe—Wren was humming.

Not tunelessly, the way sailors hum when their hands are busy and their minds are elsewhere. This was a melody. Specific. Repeating. Six notes up, four notes down, a little turn at the end like a question being asked politely. Juno had been navigating by ear since she was nine years old, and she knew every shanty, every work-song, every half-remembered lullaby that drifted through the taverns of Greywater Point, and she did not know this one.

Nobody on this ship knew this one.

That was the problem.

* * *

The morning passed in the careful, over-bright way mornings do when everyone is pretending not to watch someone. Pell rebuilt the winch brake for the third time, loudly, narrating every step as though the winch might be listening and take offense. Bram stood at the helm doing his statue impression, the one he'd perfected sometime around the year they lost Tam, jaw set, eyes on the Shoals' inner ring where the water went from green to a blue so deep it looked deliberate.

And Wren sat on the foredeck with her chart case open and her pencil untouched, humming.

Six notes up. Four notes down. The polite little question at the end.

Juno climbed to the helm beside Bram. "Talk to her."

"I did."

"You talked *at* her. There's a difference. I've watched you do both for twelve years."

Bram's jaw worked. "She's a grown woman. She signed the same articles we did."

"She's twenty-three," Juno said. "She was eleven when we lost Tam. She signed on because we were the only family that ever made room for her, and because you told her the sea was honest." She kept her voice level, professional, the way she kept everything, because the alternative—the thing underneath, the cold rising feeling she'd had since yesterday's dive—was not something she could afford to feel while standing up. "You want to talk about honesty, Captain? Start with why none of us will say her name out loud anymore."

That landed. She watched it land, saw the flinch he buried the way he buried everything, under ballast, under routine, under that flat stare he aimed at horizons.

"I'm not having this conversation," Bram said.

"No," Juno agreed. "You're having it with the ocean instead. Every day. Very quietly. It's not going well."

She left him there and went below, because there was work, and work was the other thing she did instead of feeling. But in the narrow corridor between the chart locker and the engine room, she stopped, because she heard the humming again, coming up through the deck planks—from below, from the hull itself, faint as breath.

Six notes up. Four notes down.

Except now there was an answer underneath it. A lower line, slower, patient, the way a parent answers a child who keeps asking the same question because they love the question more than any answer.

Juno put her palm flat against the hull.

The wood was warm.

The sea off the Siren Shoals in autumn ran cold enough to ache. The hull of the Halcyon should have been cool as a tombstone. It was warm as a sleeping animal, and it was vibrating—not the engine; Pell had the engine cold for maintenance; she'd watched him do it—and the vibration had *shape*. It had the melody. The whole ship was humming Wren's song back to her, note for note, like a mother bird answering from inside the egg.

Juno took her hand off the wood very carefully, the way you'd take your hand off a door you'd just realized was locked from the other side.

Then she went to find Pell, and for the first time in nineteen years of sailing with Bram Coldharbour, she gave an order before the captain did.

* * *

"Pell. Tell me about the resonance engine."

Pell looked up from the winch brake, wrench in hand, grease to the elbows. He was a small man built entirely of angles and opinions, and he had three expressions: skeptical, more skeptical, and the rare third one he saved for genuine wonder, which looked almost identical to indigestion. He was wearing the first one.

"What about it?"

"Yesterday you said it was still alive after twelve years. Faint. Dormant. Your words."

"Faintly active, yes. Like embers. Why?"

"Is it possible—" Juno chose the words the way she chose a course through reef, slow, checking each one twice, "—that it's not dormant at all? That it's broadcasting?"

Pell set the wrench down. That alone frightened her more than anything else could have; Pell only put tools down when the conversation had become more important than the work.

"Broadcasting to what? We checked. No signals on the resonant bands, nothing the fleet's gear would pick up at range. It's a whisper in a cathedral, Juno. You'd have to be underwater to hear it."

"Or touching the hull," Juno said. "Or sitting on the foredeck with salt water still drying in your hair."

Pell's face went through skepticism into something new. "Wren."

"Wren."

"The dive yesterday. She was down forty minutes. I told her forty was the ceiling, and she came up at fifty-one, and when I chewed her out about it she apologized and didn't argue, which—" he paused, recalculating, "—which isn't like her. She always argues. Arguing with me is practically her hobby."

"She's not arguing with anyone right now. She's humming a song nobody taught her, and last night the hull sang it back to me." Juno crossed her arms, mostly to keep her hands from shaking. "I need you to tell me I'm wrong, Pell. Tell me it's the wind in the rigging. Tell me I've been at sea too long."

Pell wiped his hands slowly on a rag that was already beyond saving. "I can't tell you that," he said quietly. "Because I heard it too. Around two bells this morning. I figured it was the anchor chain singing against the hawsepipe. Ships do that. But chains don't harmonize with themselves." He met her eyes. "Juno, the engine's design specs—I read them on the dive, what I could see of the housing. It wasn't built to power machines. Or—it was, but that's like saying a voice is built to ring a bell. The Bell's original manifest called it a *resonance engine*. Resonance isn't mechanical. It's sympathetic. Things that are tuned alike answer each other across any distance. Metal does it. Glass does it."

He stopped. Juno watched him arrive at the rest of it, watched him not want to say it, watched him say it anyway because Pell had never in his life left a sentence unfinished.

"Living things do it too," he said. "We're all tuned instruments, Juno. Bones, blood, the fluid in the inner ear. Everything vibrates. Everything has a frequency it answers to." He rubbed the back of his neck. "If that engine spent twelve years learning the exact pitch of this place—the currents, the coral, the wrecks, the—"

"The people," Juno said.

"The people who died here," Pell said. "Yes."

Above them, faint through the deck, six notes rose and four fell, and the polite little question turned at the end, and waited for its answer.

* * *

They convened in the galley, which was the closest thing the Halcyon had to a war room, mostly because the table was bolted down and nobody could storm out dramatically without opening the hatch first. Bram stood. Juno sat. Pell perched on the bench with his charts of the engine housing spread out like evidence at an inquest. Wren—

Wren wasn't there. That was the point of the meeting, and also the problem with it.

"You're saying my diver is compromised," Bram said.

"I'm saying my diver heard something beautiful down there and hasn't come all the way back yet," Juno said. "There's a difference, and the difference matters, because compromised people lie to you and enchanted people don't even know they're keeping secrets."

"Enchanted." Bram said the word like it was a disease name. "Twelve years ago—"

"Don't," Juno said.

"—we lost—"

"*Don't.*" Her voice cracked across the galley like a sail catching wind wrong, and both men went still, because in nineteen years Juno had raised her voice exactly twice, and both times someone had ended up dead or nearly. She breathed. She counted knots in her head until her hands stopped wanting to shake. "We don't say it in past tense in this galley. That's the rule. You made the rule, Bram. Honor it."

Bram looked at the table. "Fine. But the fact stands. Something in those waters reached into a person once before and didn't give them back. And now it's reaching again, and you're telling me it's *singing* to her."

"I'm telling you it's answering her," Juno said. "There's a difference, and I think the difference is everything. Listen to me. All those years we thought the Shoals lured people in. Sirens, songs, sweet voices pulling sailors off the rails—that's the story, that's what every survivor's tale says. But survivors' tales get it backwards, Bram. They always do. What if nothing ever called anyone *in*?" She leaned forward. "What if the Shoals simply *remembered* them, and the remembering felt like being known, and being known felt like home, and home is the most dangerous thing in the world to a person who doesn't have one?"

The silence after that had weight. Pell was staring at his charts without seeing them. Bram had gone very still, the particular stillness of a man hearing his own secret described by someone who didn't know she was describing it.

"And Wren," Juno continued, quieter, "has never had one. Not really. We found her half-starved on a dock at eleven years old, and we fed her, and we taught her stars, and we told ourselves that was enough. But a girl who's never been sung to—" her throat tightened; she pushed through it, professional, calm, calm, "—a girl like that hears a voice that knows her name, and she goes toward it. Anyone would. *I* would. Don't pretend you wouldn't."

"That's why she dives again tomorrow," Bram said flatly. "No. She doesn't. Nobody dives. We salvage the core with grapples from the surface if we have to, we cut it loose blind, and we get out."

"With respect, Captain, cutting a resonance engine loose blind is how you get a bell swinging free in a current field with its song intact and no idea where it drifts," Pell said. "Also, the grapple winch is—"

"If you say the winch brake needs another rebuild, I'm throwing you overboard myself," Bram said.

"It needed one rebuild," Pell said with dignity. "The other two were quality assurance."

Juno almost smiled. Almost. Then, through the overhead hatch, left cracked for air, the humming changed.

Everyone heard it at once. The melody—the six-up, four-down, the polite question—was still there, but now Wren was adding to it. A second line, weaving above the first, harmonizing, and beneath her voice, faint but unmistakable, the hull answered in kind, and the two lines braided together into something that sounded less like a song and more like a conversation, and less like a conversation and more like a reunion.

Bram moved first. For a man who'd spent twelve years moving away from things, he crossed that galley fast.

They found Wren on the foredeck with her arms wrapped around the base of the mast, cheek against the wood, eyes closed, singing to the ship—or the ship was singing to her, or the distinction had quietly stopped mattering. Her chart case lay open beside her. On the top chart, in her neat navigator's hand, she had written a single line of notation. Not a bearing. Not a depth sounding.

A melody. Scored. Precise. With dynamic markings.

Juno crouched beside her, slow, the way you approach a bird you don't want to startle. "Wren."

Wren opened her eyes. They were clear—clearer than yesterday, clearer than Juno liked, bright with a certainty that eleven-year-olds on docks and twenty-three-year-olds on foredecks should not have in common.

"He says he's sorry," Wren said.

The world went narrow and ringing. Somewhere behind her, Bram made a sound Juno had never heard him make, small and involuntary, like a rope parting under load.

"Who does, love?" Juno asked, and her voice held, because it had to, because Wren was looking at her now, waiting, trusting, and maternal terror and professional calm were the same muscle and Juno had been training it for nineteen years.

"The one who sings," Wren said simply. "He's been holding the song for twelve years so it wouldn't be lost. He says it isn't a lure. He says it's a *letter*." She touched the notation on the chart. "He wrote it down for us. He says we already know the tune. He says—" and here her brow furrowed, the first flicker of ordinary confusion, of ordinary Wren, "—he says Juno will remember the second verse, even though Juno thinks she's forgotten it."

Juno did not remember any second verse.

Juno remembered, with the sudden horrible clarity of a trap springing, a night twelve years ago and a campfire on the Halcyon's deck, and Tam—laughing, always laughing—hammering out a nonsense tune on a washboard while Pell objected to its meter and Bram pretended not to tap his boot, six notes up and four notes down with a silly little turn at the end like a question being asked politely, a tune they'd all sworn was Tam's own invention, a tune nobody had hummed since.

A tune nobody had taught Wren.

Because Wren hadn't been aboard yet.

Juno looked up and found Bram staring at her, and she watched twelve years of practiced detachment crack straight down the middle like ice underfoot, and she understood, with total and terrible certainty, that whatever the Meridian Bell was singing down there in its cathedral of coral—

It wasn't a siren's call.

It was a message. And it had been waiting all this time for someone in the family to finally listen.

Behind them, far out in the Shoals' inner ring, the water dimpled where no wind touched, and the hum in the hull deepened by one whole octave, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, beginning to wake.

"We're running out of tides," Pell said softly. "And I don't think it's the fleet we should be racing anymore."
