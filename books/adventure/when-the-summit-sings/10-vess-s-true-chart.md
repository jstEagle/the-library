## 10. Vess's True Chart

Bram found the chart by accident, which was how he found most things worth finding.

It was the fourth tide's slack water, the hour when the Shoals went glassy and strange, and the *Halcyon* rode at anchor in a pocket of dead calm while Pell argued with the bilge pump below decks. Bram had gone looking for a spare coil of signal line in the aft locker—the one nobody used since Tam had reorganized it twelve years ago into a system only Tam understood—and instead his fingers closed on paper. A leather folio, oilskin-wrapped, tucked behind the line coils where nobody would look unless they were desperate or snooping.

He was, as it happened, both.

He told himself he was just checking inventory. He told himself that all the way up to the deck, where the light was good, where he unwrapped the oilskin with hands that were not quite steady, and opened Vess Karrow's private manifest.

The first page was what she'd shown them at the dockside tavern: salvage contract, buyer redacted, payout figures, the pretty arithmetic of debt cleared three times over. Bram read it again anyway, out of habit, the way you reread a letter from someone you'd stopped trusting.

Then he turned the page.

The second folio was longer. Denser. Written in Vess's small, vicious handwriting—the handwriting of a woman who took notes during arguments so she could win them twice. And it wasn't a salvage manifest at all.

It was a ledger of coasts.

Bram turned pages slowly. Resonant harbors, eleven of them, from the Greywater littoral down to the Coral Reach—each one annotated with depth soundings, population figures, tide tables, and a column headed, in Vess's clipped script, *Yield*. Not yield of cargo. Yield of *leverage*. There were sketches of resonance engines—older models, half-rotted, salvaged or scuttled decades ago—with notes on why each had failed, and what the Meridian Bell's engine, still alive after twelve years underwater, could do that none of them could.

At the back, a single summary page. Bram read it three times because his mind kept sliding off it like rain off oiled canvas.

*Asset: Meridian resonance core, functional.*
*Disposition: private retention.*
*Market: dominion. Every resonant coast on the chart pays tribute or goes dark.*

She wasn't salvaging for a buyer. There was no buyer. There had never been a buyer. The redacted name on the contract was a fiction, a costume she'd dressed the job in so a crew of superstitious wreck-rats would sail into waters they'd sworn off for a payday instead of a war.

Vess Karrow meant to keep the engine herself. And every coastline that hummed—every harbor town with a resonant lighthouse, every fishing village whose bells rang true across the water—would owe her, or lose the thing that made their sea sing.

Bram stood very still at the rail. Below him, through the hull, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, the Bell sang its low note. He'd gotten used to it over four tides. That was the horror of it, he realized. You got used to anything. Even a leash, if it was quiet enough.

"Captain?" Wren's voice, from the foredeck. She was sitting cross-legged by the bow chock, doing her listening thing—eyes shut, head tilted, like a bird deciding whether a cat was asleep. "You've been standing there a long time."

"Just taking inventory," Bram said, and folded the folio shut.

Wren opened one eye. She was nineteen and had the unnerving habit of seeing straight through him, which she'd inherited from no one, because no one else on this crew was that rude. "You look like you found something that wasn't line."

"Inventory's full of surprises."

"Uh-huh." She closed the eye again. After a moment she said, quietly, not really to him: "It sounds different today. Closer. Like it knows we're deciding something."

Bram looked at her. "Deciding what?"

"That's what I'm trying to hear." She frowned. "Go argue with whoever you're about to argue with, Captain. You'll feel better."

There was no arguing with that.

* * *

He found Vess in the stern cabin she'd claimed on the first night, uninvited and unbudgeable, surrounded by her own charts like a spider who'd moved into a nicer web. She was mending a glove—actually mending it, needle and thread, which was somehow more unsettling than if she'd been counting money—and she didn't look up when he came in.

"Bram Coldharbour," she said pleasantly. "You knock like a man with a grievance. Come in, or hover? Hovering wrinkles my charts."

He dropped the folio on her table.

Her needle paused. Just for a heartbeat—a hitch so small most people would have missed it. Then she finished her stitch, bit the thread, set the glove down, and looked up at him with the smile he'd been hating since they were both nineteen and racing sloops off Greywater Point.

"You went through my things."

"You lied to my crew."

"I presented a business opportunity to your crew. Your navigator accepted it. I don't recall your signature being required." She leaned back, folding her hands. "Though I notice you're holding the real manifest, which means you've read it, which means we can skip the part where you bluster and I pretend to be wounded. Shall we skip it?"

"We'll skip it," Bram said, "if you tell me one thing plainly, for once in your life. Is any of it true? The buyer. The payout. Any of it?"

"The payout's real. Money doesn't care whose pocket it sits in." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "The buyer—" she spread her hands, an elegant little shrug, "—is me. I'm a very demanding client. I pay on time."

"Eleven coasts, Vess." He put his palm flat on the open ledger. "Eleven towns. You want them paying *you*."

"I want them paying *someone*, Bram. That's the world. It has always been the world. The only question worth asking is whether the toll-taker is some faceless insurance combine that breaks legs in boardrooms, or a person who actually knows these waters." Her voice stayed pleasant. That was the worst of her; she could say anything pleasantly. "I grew up on a resonant coast too, in case you forgot. I watched the Optimists' collectors come through my town every quarter-year, smiling, and everyone smiled back because the alternative was learning what the smile was for. I decided at fourteen that I would rather be the smile than the teeth."

"So you'd be both."

"I'd be *in charge of* both. There's a difference. Accountability, even. A face. People can negotiate with a face." She picked her needle back up, threaded it, began another stitch, watching him work. "You've met the alternative. Forty days, isn't it? Thirty-six now. How's the leg-breaking syndicate treating you? Cheerfully, I hear."

"Don't turn this around on me."

"I'm not turning anything. I'm showing you the map." She set the glove down again and stood, coming around the table, and for a moment she was close enough that he could see the gray coming in at her temples, the same wind-scouring he saw in his own mirror. Twelve years changed people. Some of them it just sharpened. "You think I'm the villain of this piece because I wrote my ambition down where you could snoop it. But look at the fleet anchored three days east of us. Look at the collectors' skiff that found us at sea. Everyone wants this engine, Bram. The combines, the syndicates, the corporate fleet with its salvage monopoly and its lawyers. The difference between me and them is that I'm honest about wanting it, and I'm *here*, and I have a crew that can actually reach it. Which is you. Reluctantly. But here."

"And when you have it? What happens to the towns that won't pay?"

She held his gaze without blinking. "They go dark. Their bells stop ringing true. Their ships misnavigate. Within a season they renegotiate." A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle, which was worse: "You're waiting for me to flinch. I'm not going to flinch. Invisible men don't get to be moral, Bram. Neither do men hiding in storm clouds chasing weather for hire because they can't stand still long enough to be blamed for anything. You've spent twelve years being nowhere. Don't lecture me about coastlines."

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them, because Vess never missed, and Bram felt the old familiar urge to walk away—to climb into whatever weather was available and let distance do his answering for him.

He didn't. That was the strange thing. He heard himself say, quietly: "Tam died keeping this engine off the water. Did you know that?"

Something crossed her face—not guilt, nothing so useful, but recognition. "I suspected."

"He died testing it. First trial run. The engine sang and the sea answered and the boat came apart, and Tam got me clear and didn't get himself clear. Twelve years I've been running from that. And you knew—or guessed—and you hired the one crew in the world with a reason to burn this thing rather than sell it, because you figured grief would keep us cheap and obedient." He shook his head slowly. "You're smarter than me, Vess. Always have been. But you made one mistake."

"Oh?"

"You gave the manifest to a man with nothing left to lose except the people on the other side of that bulkhead." He tapped the deck with his boot. "And they're not for sale either."

"They might surprise you," Vess said. "People usually do, when the price gets high enough." She sat back down and picked up her glove, serene as Sunday. "We're four tides from the wreck and two from the fleet. Think about which side of my ledger you'd rather be on. Take your time. I mend quickly."

Bram walked out. In the passageway he stopped, pressed his forehead briefly against the cool planking, and breathed.

*Invisible men don't get to be moral.* Fine. Then he'd stop being invisible. Starting now.

* * *

He gathered them in the galley because the galley was small and there was no room to pace, which he counted as a tactical advantage. Juno sat with her arms crossed. Pell perched on the bread locker, wiping engine grease onto trousers that had surrendered to grease years ago. Wren curled in the corner bunk with her knees up, watching him with those eyes that heard too much.

"Vess has been lying to us," Bram said. "Not about the money. About the buyer. There isn't one. She means to take the engine herself and hold every resonant coast on this chart for ransom." He laid the folio open on the table and let them read.

Silence, except for the Bell, humming up through the keel like a thought you couldn't shake.

Pell read fastest, because Pell read everything fast and understood most of it. He whistled, low. "Dominion. Actual dominion. That's not a payday, Captain, that's a throne."

"It's a throne built on a bell," Juno said flatly. "And we're the ones carrying her to it." She looked at Bram, and there was no anger in her face, which was somehow heavier than anger. "You're going to tell me you didn't know. I believe you. But I signed us onto this, Bram. I signed us onto it because forty days of debt was going to end with collectors snapping Pell's fingers like kindling, and I chose. So whatever we do next, I choose that too. Say it plain. What are we doing?"

Bram looked around the galley—at Juno, who'd held them together with arithmetic and stubbornness; at Pell, who talked to engines because engines listened; at Wren, who'd been hearing the Bell in her sleep for two nights and hadn't once complained.

"Twelve years ago we lost Tam in those Shoals," he said. "I've never told you how. I'm going to. But first—" he tapped the ledger, "—this doesn't reach shore. Not in her hands, not in the fleet's hands, not in the Optimists' hands. Nobody owns the song. We scuttle the engine, we take our lumps, and we figure out the debt after. That's my proposal."

"That's not a proposal," Pell said, grinning despite everything, "that's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's the family business," Wren said quietly, and everyone looked at her, and she shrugged. "Breaking things that shouldn't sing. It's what we do."

Juno exhaled through her nose—the sound she made when a bearing finally resolved. "Scuttling an engine rated to power a city," she said. "In hostile water. With a corporate fleet inbound and a bomb-happy syndicate somewhere behind us, and Vess Karrow on our own deck pretending to mend gloves."

"When you list it like that," Pell said, "it sounds *fun*."

"She'll fight us," Bram said. "The moment she thinks we mean it, she'll fight."

"Then we'd best mean it before she does," Juno said, and stood, and that was that. Twenty years of crew, settled in nine words.

Above them, through the deck planks, came footsteps—light, unhurried, crossing toward the stern. And beneath the footsteps, beneath the hull, the Bell's hum shifted. Rose a half-tone. Held.

Wren's head came up sharply. "Captain," she whispered. "It just got louder. Like it heard us decide."

Bram looked at the overhead, toward the stern, toward the woman with the needle and the ledger and the smile.

"Then let's hope," he said, "it likes our answer better than hers."
