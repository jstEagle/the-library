## 11. Fifth Tide: Mutiny Weather

Wren heard the storm before anyone else did.

That wasn't unusual—she'd been hearing things before anyone else since the Shoals got under her skin—but this was different. This wasn't the Bell's song threading up through the hull like a secret told through a floorboard. This was the sky itself, a low grumble far to the southwest, so deep she felt it in her back teeth rather than her ears.

She climbed out of her bunk and went up on deck barefoot, because shoes were for people who planned ahead and she had never once been one of them.

The horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Clouds stacked themselves in slow, patient towers, the way Pell stacked plates when he was annoyed about something and wanted everyone to know it without saying it. The wind had died entirely—the Halcyon sat becalmed on water so flat it looked poured—and Wren knew enough now, four tides into this mad errand, that dead calm before a storm was the sea taking a breath.

"Should I get Bram?" asked the voice at her elbow.

She jumped about a foot. Pell stood beside her holding two mugs of tea, which meant he'd been standing there long enough to make tea, which meant he'd been watching her stare at nothing for several minutes and hadn't said anything, because Pell was polite in the way that engineers are polite: he waited until something was actually broken.

"He's asleep," Wren said. "First time in three days."

"Then we'll let him have it." Pell handed her a mug. "Storm's still half a day out. Storms don't hurry. That's what I like about them. Very honest things, storms. They tell you exactly when they're coming and exactly how bad they'll be, and then they do precisely that."

"People should work like that," Wren said.

"People would be insufferable if they worked like that." He sipped his tea. "You've been humming again."

Wren's hand went to her mouth. She hadn't noticed. She'd been humming for two days now, on and off, a melody with no name and no origin, five notes that looped around each other like eels. She'd caught Juno listening once, head tilted, face carefully blank in the way that made Wren feel like a specimen in a jar.

"It's not doing anything to me," Wren said. "It's just... there. Like a song stuck in your head."

"A song stuck in your head has an ending. You hum it through and it lets go." Pell looked out at the bruised horizon. "This one doesn't let go?"

Wren thought about lying. She was, by her own honest assessment, a mediocre liar—her ears went red, Juno said, like signal flags. So she didn't try.

"No," she said. "It's not trying to keep me. It's more like..." She searched for it. "Like someone calling from another room. Not *come here*. More like—*I'm here*. Over and over. *I'm here, I'm here.*"

Pell was quiet for a while. The tea steamed between them.

"That's worse," he said finally. "Lures want you to come. But something that just keeps saying *I'm here*—" He shook his head. "That's lonely. And lonely things will take whatever company they can get."

* * *

By midmorning the calm broke, and so did the crew.

It started, as most disasters aboard the Halcyon did, with breakfast. Juno had laid out the day's plan on the chart table: run north of the outer ring, hold position through the storm's leading edge, dive on the wreck at slack water after it passed. Standard. Sensible. The kind of plan Juno drew up in her sleep, and possibly had.

Bram took one look at it and said, "We're not diving again."

The cabin went very quiet. Vess, who had installed herself at the far end of the table like a cat claiming the best chair, looked up with interest. Pell stopped chewing. Wren froze with her spoon halfway to her mouth.

"The engine's intact," Bram went on, in the flat voice he used when he was working very hard not to feel something. "Pell confirmed it. We confirmed the fleet's stalled. We have what we came for—knowledge of where it is and what it does. We report back to Vess, she pays us, we go home. Nobody else goes down there."

"That's not the job," Vess said pleasantly.

"The job changed."

"The job," Vess said, "is the core, Coldharbour. You agreed to salvage it. Salvage means *up*, not *looked at*."

"And I'm telling you it stays down there."

"Why?" Vess spread her hands, all reasonableness. "Because it sang to your girl? Because you've all decided a machine is haunted? It's a power source. A magnificent one. It could light every harbor from here to the Spine—"

"It could do other things too," Pell said quietly. Everyone looked at him. He set down his fork with great care. "I ran the resonance numbers again last night. If you couple that output to a coastal relay array—which, incidentally, is exactly what the Meridian Line was building when they sank—you're not lighting harbors. You're broadcasting. Into water, into hulls, into people. Every resonant coast within—" he did some arithmetic in the air with one finger, "—call it three hundred miles, hears whatever the operator wants them to hear. Forever. And can't stop hearing it."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the first gust hit the rigging and the whole ship leaned, groaning, like an old man getting out of a chair.

"So we destroy it," Juno said.

"We *what*?" Vess's voice didn't rise, which somehow made it worse. It dropped instead, into a register Wren had only heard her use once before, on the dockside, when Bram had called her something unrepeatable. "You'll forgive me. I must have misheard. Did the navigator of this vessel just propose destroying cargo worth more than everyone at this table will earn in ten lifetimes?"

"You heard fine," Juno said. She wasn't looking at Vess. She was looking at Wren, and her face had that careful blankness again, and Wren realized with a lurch that Juno wasn't arguing about money or machines at all. "Nobody goes near it again. Nobody hears it again. We scuttle the housing, deep trench, done."

"You'd owe the Optimists—"

"I know what we'd owe."

"—and me, and I do not forgive debts, Juno, it's practically my religion—"

"Then you shouldn't have hired people," Bram said, "who break things for a living."

And that was when it cracked open, the way families crack open—not with the big betrayals but with the small accumulated grievances, all of them talking at once now, Vess coolly itemizing sums, Pell insisting destruction was wasteful when containment was possible, *just seal the housing, just blind it*, Bram saying over him that sealed things get opened, and Juno saying nothing at all, which was loudest of all—

—and Wren said, "I want to answer it."

Everything stopped.

Four faces turned to her. She hadn't meant to say it out loud. It had been sitting in her chest for two days, growing alongside the melody, and apparently the argument had shaken it loose the way a storm shakes fruit off a branch.

"The song," she said, and her ears were going red, she could feel them, signal flags, damn it. "It's not a lure. I keep trying to tell you. Lures pull. This doesn't pull. It just—it keeps saying *I'm here*. Twelve years, it's been saying *I'm here*. Don't you want to know who it's been saying it to? Or—" her voice wobbled and she hated it, "—or *for*?"

"Wren." Juno's voice was very gentle. Gentle the way a hand on a hot stove is gentle. "No."

"You haven't even listened—"

"No."

"You lock the gear away and you won't listen and you won't let *me*—"

"*No.*" And then Juno did the thing she almost never did: she raised her voice, and it cracked in the middle, and underneath the crack Wren heard something twelve years old. "You are twenty years old and you dove alone into water that sang Tam Coldharbour to death, and you came up humming, and I have been awake every night since listening to you hum through the bulkhead wondering which part of you is going to come back next time and which part stays down there. So no. No, you may not answer it."

The cabin was absolutely silent. Rain began against the deck above, soft at first, testing.

Wren's eyes were burning. "That's not fair."

"No," Juno agreed, and her voice came back down, roughened. "It isn't. None of this is. Welcome to family."

* * *

Juno locked the dive gear away that afternoon, and she did it in front of everyone, which Wren understood was the point. She carried the helmets and the air regulators and the depth lines into the forward hold, padlocked the hatch, and hung the key on a cord around her own neck.

"There," she said. "Now nobody dives. Including me. Including especially me." She looked around the deck—at Bram leaning against the rail with his arms crossed, at Pell pretending to inspect a winch, at Wren sitting on a coil of rope with her knees pulled up. "If we're going to fracture, we'll fracture evenly."

"This is absurd," Vess said. "You realize I can simply order my launch's crew to—"

"Your launch," Pell said mildly, "is currently riding out the weather forty miles astern, because its captain had the sense to run for open water before the barometer fell. Which, incidentally—" he tapped the glass instrument screwed to the mast, "—it has. Considerably. In case anyone was planning to pretend the sky isn't doing what the sky is doing."

Everyone looked at the horizon. The bruise had bloomed. The cloud towers to the southwest had joined into a single wall, green-black at its base, and beneath it the sea had turned a strange luminous gray, and the wind had come back all at once, out of the wrong quarter, warm and smelling of ozone and something older, something mineral, like wet stone in a cave.

The Halcyon rode at anchor in the lee of the outer ring, and even anchored she began to strain at her line like a dog hearing a whistle.

"We ride it out here," Bram said. "Anchors down, everything lashed, hatches battened. Nobody goes anywhere."

"Agreed," said Juno.

"Obviously," said Vess.

"Pell?" Bram asked.

Pell was frowning at the barometer, tapping it, holding it to his ear as though it might confess. "Fine. Yes. Anchors. Lashing. All of it." He paused. "Although."

"Although what?"

"Although the needle's fallen further than any storm I've ever seen justify. And although—" he hesitated, and Wren watched him decide whether to say the thing, and watched him lose, "—although the ship's been pulling at the anchor line toward the wreck since noon. Not with the wind. Against it. Half a point off true, steady, like something on the seabed reeling us in. I checked the current tables twice. There's no current that does that."

The rain arrived properly then, drumming on the deck, and nobody said anything for a moment, and Wren felt the melody stir in her chest like a sleeper turning over.

*I'm here,* it said. *I'm here.*

* * *

They battened down through the evening. Wren helped Pell lash the longboat, double-lash the spare spars, chain the ballast—*my ballast*, he kept muttering, *someone tampered with my beautiful ballast and now I trust nothing*—and the whole time the storm built overhead with the patience Pell admired in it, thunder rolling closer in long unhurried statements.

Supper was salt pork and hardtack eaten in shifts, because somebody always had to mind the deck, and the conversation was the strained, over-polite conversation of people who had screamed at each other that morning and hadn't figured out how to be normal yet. Vess ate in her corner and read charts by lamplight and pretended not to watch the rest of them. Bram washed dishes, which he always did after fights; Wren had long ago worked out it was his version of apologizing to the universe without having to apologize to anyone in particular.

Wren took the middle watch.

She sat in the lee of the deckhouse with oilskins streaming, watching lightning walk along the horizon in silent white columns, and she hummed, quietly, under the wind. Five notes, looping. *I'm here. I'm here.*

And then, somewhere past midnight, the song changed.

She sat bolt upright. It was subtle—a new phrase woven into the loop, a sixth note, a turn she'd never heard before—and it undid something in her, because a pattern that changes is a pattern that's *trying to communicate*, and Pell was right, songs stuck in your head don't do that, songs stuck in your head are dead things repeating, and this was not repeating. This was composing.

She scrambled below decks, soaked, and hammered on Bram's door.

He opened it looking like a man who had known sleep was a rumor anyway. Behind him, lights were already coming on—Juno's door opening, Pell's, the smell of lamp oil.

"It changed," Wren said. "The song changed. It's never changed. It added a phrase tonight, right at—" she hummed it, six notes now, and saw Bram's face do something complicated, something old, something that looked like a man hearing a voice he'd spent twelve years not listening for.

"That's—" he started, and stopped.

"What?" Juno was in the passageway now, key cord around her neck, eyes sharp. "What is it, Bram?"

But Bram wasn't answering. He was staring at the middle distance with an expression Wren had no map for, one hand pressed flat against the bulkhead as if checking it for a pulse.

Above them, the storm arrived in earnest, and the Halcyon shuddered at her anchors, straining south, toward the Shoals, toward the drowned cathedral and the thing inside it that had finally, after twelve years, found something new to say.

And through the hull, under the thunder, clear as a bell, the whole ship heard it:

*I'm here. I'm here. I'm—*

—and then, cutting across the song from the northeast, thin and cheerful and utterly wrong, a sound that made every one of them go cold: the horn of an approaching skiff.

Three blasts. The Optimists' signature. *Coming aboard,* it said, pleasant as a dinner invitation. *Coming aboard.*
