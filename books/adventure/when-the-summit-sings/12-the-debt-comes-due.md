## 12. The Debt Comes Due

They came at slack water, which was the first thing wrong about them.

Storm chasers learn tides the way farmers learn rain. Slack water is the sea holding its breath—no current, no pull, nothing to do but wait. Nobody boards a ship at slack water unless they want you to know they can afford patience.

Bram was on deck with a mug of Pell's terrible coffee when the skiff slid out of the evening haze, painted the color of a wedding invitation. Three men aboard, all of them smiling before they were even in shouting distance. The one at the bow had a face like a festival poster and hands folded politely over his knee, as if he were arriving for dinner rather than debt collection.

"Juno," Bram called down the hatch, without raising his voice much. "Company."

"I saw." Her voice drifted up, flat as a becalmed sail. "The ugly kind or the smiling kind?"

"The smiling kind."

A pause. "Worse."

By the time the skiff bumped against the Halcyon's hull, Wren had appeared at the rail beside him, and Pell had emerged from below wiping grease on a rag that was already more grease than rag. Vess stayed in her cabin. Bram noted that. He filed it away with all the other things he'd been filing away about Vess Karrow since he'd found her real manifest, and the drawer was getting heavy.

"Captain Coldharbour!" the lead collector said, beaming up at him like the sun had personally recommended him. "What a pleasure. May we come aboard? We promise to wipe our feet."

"That's usually what people say *before* I decide," Bram said.

"Of course, of course." The man gestured, and his two companions waited with the serenity of men who had never once been told no in a way that stuck. "We'll wait on your decision. We're very good at waiting. It's practically our whole business."

Bram looked at the skiff. Looked at the smiles. Looked at Wren, whose knuckles had gone pale on the rail.

"Come aboard," he said, because the alternative was watching them circle the Halcyon all night like well-dressed sharks, and he'd rather have the shark in the boat where he could count its teeth.

* * *

Their names, if you could call them names, were Mr. Gleam, Mr. Bright, and Mr. Cordial. They introduced themselves with small bows, accepted coffee they clearly didn't drink, and complimented everything—the rigging, the chart table, the view of the Shoals through the stern windows, which was like complimenting a knife for being sharp.

"Lovely vessel," Mr. Gleam said, running a finger along the bulkhead and finding no dust, which seemed to disappoint him faintly. "We've followed your career, Captain. The Greywater runs. The Kestrel Line rescue. Very inspiring."

"You follow a lot of debtors?"

"We prefer the term *clients*." Mr. Gleam settled into the chair Juno hadn't offered and folded his hands. "And we've come with wonderful news. The Optimists have reviewed your account, and we're pleased to announce a restructuring opportunity."

Pell made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to whistle. Mr. Bright turned his smile on her, and it somehow got wider without getting warmer.

"A restructuring," Bram repeated.

"Debt forgiveness, Captain. Complete. Every coin, every interest accrual, every—forgive the term—*encouragement fee*. Gone. A clean slate." Mr. Gleam spread his hands like a man unveiling a cake. "All we ask in return is something you're already going to do anyway."

The cabin went very quiet. Somewhere above, the anchor chain ticked against the hull with the tide's small breathing.

"And what's that?" Bram asked, though he already knew. He'd known since the collector skiff had warned them off in open water, since the phrase *the Optimists have money on the Bell too* had lodged under his ribs like a splinter.

"The resonance engine," Mr. Gleam said brightly. "Delivered intact. Functional. To an address we'll provide once you've raised it. That's all. You salvage it, you hand it off, and you walk away owing nothing to anyone." He leaned forward, confiding, almost kind. "Think of it, Captain. Forty days becomes zero days. No more collectors. No more looking over your shoulder in port. You could go home."

The word *home* landed in the middle of the table like a dropped coin, spinning.

Bram thought about Greywater Point, which he'd used as an address for twelve years without ever once calling it home. He thought about the crew around this table—Juno pretending to read charts she'd memorized, Pell radiating heat like a small furious stove, Wren sitting very still with her eyes on the grain of the wood. He thought about how easily the offer would work, and hated that he could see the arithmetic of it so clearly: one engine, weighed against four lives unchained.

He also thought about coastlines. Whole ones. Enslaved by a song.

"No," he said.

Mr. Gleam's smile didn't falter, which was somehow worse than if it had. "Captain. With respect. Let me show you the alternative." From his coat he produced a ledger, bound in green leather, and opened it with the reverence of scripture. "Your principal. Your accrued interest. Your late fees—which, might I say, are creative; our accountants enjoyed those. In total..." He turned the ledger around.

Bram didn't look at the number. He'd seen the number in dreams. Instead he watched Wren look at it, and saw her go still in a way that reminded him, painfully, of someone else who used to go still right before doing something brave and stupid.

"We're not delivering the engine," Bram said. "To anyone. Not to you, not to Vess, not to a buyer, not to a temple. That thing doesn't get owned. By anybody. Ever."

Mr. Gleam sighed—not angry, just disappointed, the sigh of a music teacher whose student refuses to practice. "You know, most clients argue about the number first. You've skipped straight to principle. It's refreshing. It's also why you owe us so much." He closed the ledger gently. "Here's what happens now."

* * *

What happened now took eleven minutes, and Bram spent all of them cataloguing exits he couldn't use.

Mr. Bright went back to the skiff and returned with a case, and from the case he lifted a device about the size of a ship's lantern—dark iron, seamless, with a single glass porthole showing a slow pulse of amber light. It had no visible mechanism. It had no visible anything. It simply sat in his palms radiating the particular confidence of an object that has never needed to explain itself.

"Collateral," Mr. Bright said cheerfully. "Standard practice for clients undergoing restructuring consideration."

"It's a bomb," Pell said flatly.

"It's a *negotiation aid*," Mr. Bright corrected, delighted. "But yes. Also a bomb."

They bolted it to the mainmast step while everyone watched, because there was nothing else to do. Four bolts, driven into wood that had carried the Halcyon through eleven years of weather, securing a thing with a heartbeat of amber light and a timer Bram couldn't see but knew was there, ticking somewhere inside all that smooth iron.

"There's no fuse to cut," Pell said, circling it with her arms crossed, professional interest warring visibly with murderous intent. "No wires. What is it even—"

"Trade secret," Mr. Bright said warmly. "It listens to the ship. If the ship leaves these waters, or if the engine leaves the wreck without reaching our address, or if anyone attempts to remove, disable, or *study* the device—" he said the word *study* with genuine reproach, as though Pell had suggested reading his diary— "it becomes less of a negotiation aid."

"And if we deliver the engine?" Bram asked.

"Then we come back, we collect, and we unbolt it ourselves. With a gift basket." Mr. Gleam beamed. "The Optimists are generous partners, Captain. Ask anyone who's cooperated. Go on. Ask them." He paused, savoring it. "Oh, that's right. You can't. They don't talk anymore. Not because anything happened to them! They're just very busy, being forgiven."

Mr. Cordial spoke for the first time, from the doorway, where he'd been standing the entire visit without blinking. "The fleet arrives in two days. Corporate salvage. They'll raise the Bell themselves if you don't—and then your debt remains, and the device remains, and we'll all be very sad together." He smiled. It was the smallest smile of the three and therefore the most frightening. "Two days, Captain. The tide after next. Deliver the engine, or don't. Either way, we'll see you soon. We always see you soon."

They went over the side the way they'd come, smiling all the way down into the skiff, waving until the haze swallowed them. Mr. Gleam's last words, floated back across the water, were: "Do think about the gift basket!"

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time after the skiff vanished.

The amber light pulsed in its little glass eye, patient as a lighthouse. Pell stood in front of it with her head tilted, the way she listened to engines, except this wasn't an engine, it was a threat wearing an engine's clothes.

"Everyone breathe," Juno said finally, from the chart table, where she had not moved once during the whole visit. "Panicking uses air, and we may need it."

"I could gut it," Pell said. "Given time. There's always a seam. Everything has a seam."

"'Anyone attempting to study the device,'" Wren quoted quietly.

"I heard what they said."

"They wanted you to hear it. That's the point. It's not a bomb, it's a *story* about a bomb, and the story does the work." But Wren's voice shook slightly on the last word, and Bram noticed her eyes kept going to the amber pulse, counting its rhythm without meaning to.

"Vess heard all of that," Juno said. "Her cabin's six feet from here."

"She heard it," Bram agreed.

"So either she's planning around a bomb on our mast, or she already knew it was coming." Juno looked up, and there it was—the question they'd all been carrying since the manifest, finally set on the table between them like a fourth place setting. "Bram. How long have you known what she actually wants?"

He told them. All of it—the real chart, the resonant coasts, the plan to own the song itself. He'd meant to tell them more gently, with some lead-in, some context. Instead it came out in one breath, because the bomb was ticking and gentleness felt like a luxury from another life.

Silence again. Different silence. Wren looked at the floor. Pell looked at the bomb. Juno looked at Bram with an expression he couldn't read, and then, horribly, she almost smiled.

"You know what the worst part is?" she said. "I'm not surprised. I've been waiting twelve years for you to tell us something true about the Shoals, and it takes a bomb on the mast to do it."

"Juno—"

"Don't. Not yet. We don't have time for the full accounting." She stood, rolled the chart closed, and tapped it against her palm like a gavel. "Here's where we are. Two days until the fleet. A bomb that punishes us for leaving, for running, for thinking too hard near it. An employer who wants to own a weapon. And a debt we can't pay in coin." She looked around the table. "So. Anyone have any ideas that aren't terrible?"

"I have one idea," Pell said, "and it involves gutting the bomb anyway."

"Noted. Terrible. Anyone else?"

Wren raised her head slowly. She'd been quiet the whole evening in a way that was different from her usual quiet—this was the quiet of someone listening to something underneath the conversation. Bram had seen it before, out in the dead water, and it had scared him then too.

"The Bell's still singing," she said. "Underneath everything. All day, all night. Even now, even with—that—" she nodded at the amber light, "—even with that thing pulsing away, the song's still there, under it. Like a note held under water." Her hands curled around each other on the table. "And I think... I think it's changed. Since the first dive. Since I heard it up close. It's not singing *at* us anymore."

"What's it doing?" Juno asked, gentle.

Wren hesitated. When she answered, her voice was barely above the creak of the hull.

"It's waiting."

* * *

Bram climbed topside alone after midnight, because sleep was a country he'd lost his passport to years ago.

The Halcyon rode her anchor in the outer ring of the Shoals, and the water around her lay black and flat as poured ink. Somewhere beneath it, in a cathedral of coral, the Meridian Bell hung in the dark, singing its held note to nobody. Two days until the fleet. One bomb on the mast. One rival asleep six feet below him who'd sell a coastline to buy her grudge. And somewhere behind all of it, twelve years back, a young man named Tam who had died keeping an engine's first song from escaping—who had died, if Bram was honest, saving Bram from his own curiosity, and who deserved better than to have his death become currency in other people's auctions.

Bram leaned on the rail and looked at the amber pulse glowing softly through the companionway hatch, and did the only arithmetic left to him.

The Optimists wanted the engine delivered intact. Vess wanted it delivered to herself. The fleet wanted it delivered to a boardroom. Everyone, everywhere, wanted the same thing: the song, captured, owned, put to work.

Which meant there was exactly one outcome nobody had priced in.

"Bram?"

Wren, at the top of the companionway, wrapped in a blanket against the night chill. She came to stand beside him at the rail, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation—the kind he'd learned she preferred.

"My dad owed money once," she said eventually. "Back home. Before." She shrugged, elaborately casual. "Men used to come to the house. Smiling. My mum fed them tea and they smiled wider and the debt never got smaller. I used to think if we just paid it, they'd stop coming. Then I thought if we ran, they'd stop coming." She looked at him sideways. "They stopped coming when my mum stopped being afraid of them. Took her years. But one day she just... served the tea like it was nothing, and laughed at their jokes like they were jokes, and they got smaller. Right there in the kitchen. They got smaller."

Bram huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Your mum sounds smarter than me."

"She's a navigator too, sort of. Of kitchens." Wren pulled the blanket tighter. "Captain. Whatever you're planning—you get that look. The one where you're measuring miles instead of people. Juno says you've done it your whole life. Measured the distance instead of the arrival."

"Juno talks too much."

"Juno loves you like family, which is why she talks too much." Wren turned to face him fully, and in the dark her eyes caught the amber glow from below decks, two small points of borrowed fire. "Whatever it costs, whatever you're going to trade—I want you to know the crew isn't collateral. We're not bolts on a mast. You don't get to spend us to save us."

"I know," Bram said.

"Do you? Because the man who signed Vess's contract might not."

The old Bram—the one from forty days ago, from twelve years ago—would have bristled. Would have cited rank, experience, the weight of decisions made so others didn't have to carry them. Instead Bram looked at this young woman who'd surfaced from the dead water humming a melody no one taught her, and said, honestly:

"I don't know. But I'm going to find out. And when I do, you'll all get a vote. That's the best I've got."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way sailors acknowledge a bearing confirmed.

"Good enough," she said. "For tonight."

She went below. Bram stayed at the rail until the sky went grey at the edges, turning it over, turning it over. Below his boots, the amber light pulsed. Beneath his keel, forty fathoms down, the Bell held its waiting note. And somewhere east, beyond the dark rim of the sea, the corporate fleet lay at anchor with its cranes and its contracts, two tides away and closing.

Three parties wanted the song owned.

Bram Coldharbour straightened, rolled his shoulders until something in his back cracked like rigging in a gust, and began—at last—to plan the one delivery nobody had ordered.

The trick, he thought, walking toward Pell's cabin to wake her, would be making sure the bomb never learned what he intended until it was far too late to matter.

"Pell," he said through the hatch, and heard her groan awake. "Get up. I need you to gut a bomb."

"*I knew it,*" said Pell, with unmistakable joy.
