## 14. Riding the Singing Gale

**Bram**

The bomb sat in the hold like a guest who wouldn't leave.

Pell had covered it with a tarp, which was the most optimistic thing anyone aboard had done in days. Bram stood at the Halcyon's wheel and watched the storm come down out of the northwest like a debt collector with a smile — which, he supposed, was exactly what it was. The Optimists' collectors had rowed back to their skiff an hour ago, waving cheerfully, promising to return "once things settled." Things would settle. That was the problem. In six hours the fleet would be through the outer ring, and everything they'd found in the drowned cathedral of coral would belong to people who thought a song was inventory.

"Tell me again why we're sailing *toward* it," Pell said, coming up the companionway with grease to his elbows and terror behind his eyes.

Bram pointed his chin at the water ahead. Between them and the wreck lay the Shoals' inner ring — a maze of coral teeth and dead currents that had eaten better ships than theirs. Every chart said impassable. Every chart was written for calm weather.

"You see the gap?" Bram said.

"I see death."

"That's the gap."

Juno appeared at his shoulder, her hair whipping loose from its braid, her eyes already doing the arithmetic she did faster than anyone alive. She looked where he was looking, and Bram watched her get there — watched her face go from skepticism to calculation to something almost like delight.

"The storm surge," she said. "It'll pile water through the channel. Six fathoms over the teeth, maybe seven, if the wind holds north by west."

"If the wind holds," Pell repeated. "The wind is holding a knife to our throat, Juno."

"It's the only door," Juno said simply. "And it only opens during the storm. Afterward it closes forever — or until the fleet finds another way in, which they won't before dark."

Bram turned the wheel a point. "Then we go through while it's open."

"And Wren?"

They all three looked aft. Wren stood at the stern rail with her eyes closed and one bare hand flat against the hull, the way she'd been standing more and more since the fourth tide. Listening. Her lips moved sometimes, shaping words no one else could hear.

"She hears it," Bram said quietly. "Even now. Even through all this wind. If anyone can find the channel's voice inside the gale, it's her."

Pell wiped his hands on his trousers, leaving them worse. "I want it noted, officially, in the log, that I think this is insane."

"Noted," said Juno.

"Loudly noted."

"Noted in letters a fathom tall," Bram said, and put the Halcyon's bow into the weather.

* * *

**Wren**

The song got louder as the storm got louder, which made no sense, and Wren had stopped expecting sense.

Above her the world was screaming — rigging howling, canvas cracking like whips, the sea throwing itself against the hull in fists. But underneath the screaming, steady as a heartbeat under shouting, the Bell sang on. It had been singing for twelve years, she understood that now. It wasn't calling to her. It was *remembering*, and its memory leaked out into the water, and the water carried it, and Wren — who had spent her whole life listening to engines and tides because people were harder — could hear every note.

Including the ones that weren't the engine's.

She kept that to herself. There wasn't time, and there wasn't breath to spare, and some things you carry until you can set them down gently or not at all.

"Bear me south!" she shouted up at the deck, hand still on the hull. The vibration came through her palm and up her arm and settled in her chest, and she read it like Juno read stars. "Half a point! The deep note bends left past the coral — follow the bend!"

Juno's voice came back thin and fierce through the wind: "South half a point! Helm answering!"

The Halcyon heeled, groaned, answered. Wren braced herself against the rail and listened harder.

There. Ahead and below, threading through the roar: a low tone, round and patient, the sound the coral made when the surge poured over it — the channel singing its own name. And woven through it, fainter than anything, a melody she knew now the way you know a scar. Tam's melody. The one she'd surfaced humming on the fourth tide, the one that had made Juno go white as salt.

It wasn't a lure. It had never been a lure. It was a message, and it was waiting for them at the other end of the storm, and Wren intended to deliver them all to it.

"Left!" she screamed into the wind. "*Hard* left after the white water — trust me!"

And God help her, they did.

* * *

**Juno**

Twelve years of navigation, and Juno had never steered by someone's palm pressed against a ship's hull. She'd also never loved a crew this much, which she suspected were related facts.

"Mark!" she called, watching the lead line swing. "By the mark, five!"

"Five!" echoed Pell from the foredeck, hauling in the storm canvas with hands that moved like they'd been born to a winch. He'd trimmed the sails three times in the last minute alone, and each time the Halcyon had gone from fighting the sea to dancing with it, which was Pell's whole gift and the reason Juno had picked him off a dock in Sallow Bay fifteen years ago despite the fact that he talked too much. Especially when scared. He was talking constantly now, mostly numbers, and every number was right.

"Wind's backing!" Pell bellowed. "North by west holding but it wants to go west — if it goes west we lose the surge over the third shelf!"

"Then we're through the third shelf before it does!" Juno spread her arms against the chart table, strapped down, eyes flicking between Wren's shouted bearings and the compass and the white chaos ahead. "Bram! Third shelf in two minutes! Wren says hard left after the break!"

"I heard her!" Bram's voice, calm as a millpond, which was its own kind of miracle. Juno had known him twelve years and had watched him run from everything — debts, griefs, harbors, himself — and here he was running straight at the worst water on any coast, and sounding *happy*. Not happy. Something bigger than happy. Settled. Like a man who had finally stopped measuring the distance to somewhere else.

"Breakers dead ahead!" Pell shouted.

"I see them!"

"They're very large breakers!"

"I *see them*, Pell!"

Juno bit down a laugh that wanted to be hysterical. This. This was what she'd forgotten, in all the years of small jobs and smaller harbors and the debt sitting on their necks like weather that never broke. Not the adventure — everyone lied about missing the adventure. This. The four of them being exactly, precisely themselves at the same moment, so that the ship between them felt less like a vessel than a conversation.

"Hard left!" Wren's voice, ringing clear as a bell herself. "*Now!*"

"Hard left!" Bram spun the wheel and the world spun with it.

* * *

**Pell**

Later — much later, when his hands had stopped shaking and someone had pressed something warm into them — Pell would try to describe the third shelf, and he would fail, and he would settle for telling people it was like riding a horse down a staircase during an earthquake, except the staircase was also trying to eat you.

The surge took the Halcyon up and threw her. For two full seconds they flew — actual flight, canvas snapping taut, the whole hull airborne above a coral ridge that would have opened them like a letter — and then the sea caught them, slammed them down into the channel's deep throat, and the world became noise and green light and Bram's voice saying *hold, hold, HOLD* like a prayer with a rudder attached.

Pell held. He held the mainsheet with both hands and one boot hooked through the cleat and he held, and the Halcyon surfed down the channel's spine at a speed no kite-ship was rated for, spray hitting him sideways like thrown gravel, and somewhere in the middle of it he realized he was laughing.

Not because it was fun. Because it was *theirs*. Because twelve years ago they'd been five, and then they'd been four, and four had gotten very good at surviving and very bad at living, and here in the worst water on the coast they were suddenly, stupidly, unmistakably *alive* again, all four of them pulling the same rope toward the same impossible place.

"Wren!" he hollered aft. "What's our next madness?"

Her voice came back bright through the storm: "Straight! Straight for thirty counts — then the deep note drops and you *drop with it*!"

"Pell!" Juno, forward. "Sails! Half!"

He let the mainsheet run and the canvas luffed and the Halcyon settled just enough, and then the bottom fell out of the channel exactly as promised and they dropped — a long, sliding, screaming drop down into the inner ring's calm eye, and the storm shut behind them like a door.

Silence. Or near enough. Rain pattering on deck, their own ragged breathing, the creak of a ship that had just done something ships didn't do.

Pell sat down where he stood, which was in a puddle, and looked around. Bram at the wheel, grinning like a man half his age. Juno already re-plotting, pencil moving. Wren at the rail, hand on the hull, smiling at something beneath the water.

"We made it," Pell said. "We actually — nobody's ever —"

"Don't say it," said Juno.

"He's going to say it," Bram said.

"*Nobody's ever sailed the inner ring,*" Pell announced to the sky, to the rain, to the drowned city of coral all around them. "We are the first and possibly the last and I want that recorded, engraved, sung about—"

A sound rolled across the water from the northwest. Deep. Metallic. Followed by a second sound, uglier, that Pell's engineer's ear identified before his mind caught up: timber tearing open on coral, tons of it, the particular catastrophic music of a ship dying badly.

Everyone went still.

Through the rain curtain at the ring's edge, they could see them — lanterns swinging wildly, searchlights stabbing, the corporate salvage fleet arriving at the door just in time to watch it close. A second impact boomed across the water, closer to the channel's mouth, and a flare went up and died in the wet air, and faintly, thinly, carried on the wind, they heard men screaming orders that no order could fix.

The fleet had tried to follow them in.

Two ships were learning why no chart marked the way.

* * *

**Bram**

Bram counted the lanterns twice. Four remained upright outside the ring. Two were going down, or had gone down — he could see boats being lowered, little lights bobbing on black water, rescue crews pulling survivors off the coral. His chest did something complicated about that. He hadn't wanted anyone dead. He'd wanted them *slower*.

"They'll regroup," Juno said quietly, beside him. "Circle the Shoals, find the southern approach. Three days, maybe four, if the weather keeps them honest."

"Three days is enough."

"For what?"

Bram watched Wren at the rail. Watched her turn and look at him, and there was something in her face he'd only seen once before, twelve years ago, on a different deck, in a different kind of silence — the look of someone carrying a message they've been told they mustn't open yet.

"For whatever she's about to tell us," Bram said.

Juno followed his gaze. Her jaw tightened. "You know what she heard. Down there. You've known since the fourth tide."

"I know what *I* hope she heard." Bram stepped away from the wheel, crossed the deck, and crouched down so he and Wren were eye to eye, rain running off his nose between them. "You don't have to say it yet," he told her gently. "But when you're ready — whatever it is, whoever it's from — we'll listen. All of us. Together."

Wren's chin trembled, just once. She nodded.

Behind them, out in the storm-dark, the fleet's lanterns regrouped and began their slow, hungry circuit of the ring. And beneath the keel, beneath the coral, beneath twelve years of tide, the Meridian Bell sang on — softer now, almost tender, like something that had waited a very long time to be heard, and could hear footsteps coming down the hall at last.
