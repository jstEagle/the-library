## 15. Fifth Tide, Turned

The storm had chewed the sky down to a bruise by the time we came over the last reef shoulder, and the Meridian Bell rose out of the water like a cathedral that had lost an argument with God.

Twelve years of coral had grown up her masts in white branches. Her hull listed at a merciful angle, half-buried in the shoal's heart, and from somewhere inside her — deep, patient, almost kind — came the hum. The same hum Wren had been hearing since the first tide. The same hum I'd been hearing in my sleep for twelve years, though I'd have gutted anyone who said so.

"Starboard quarter," Juno said quietly. "Vess's launch."

I'd known she'd be here. Vess Karrow didn't chase; she arrived, the way weather arrives, the way debt arrives. Her launch cut through the dead water with that obscene smoothness of hers, and she stood at the bow in oilskins too clean for the occasion, one hand raised in greeting like we were neighbors meeting over a fence.

"Bram!" she called across the water, cheerful as a market day. "You found it! I knew you would. You always were the best finder of things you wished were lost."

"There's a sentence I'll be chewing on for a decade," Pell muttered at the winch.

We anchored. There was no point pretending otherwise — the Shoals had us now, the way they'd had Tam, and the fleet was three hours out and closing, and the only thing left to decide was who stood where when everything went loud.

Wren was already at the rail. She'd been at the rail since dawn, humming under her breath, that melody nobody taught her, and when Vess's launch bumped alongside and two of Vess's people came aboard with grappling hooks coiled over their shoulders like festival garlands, Wren didn't even turn around.

That was my first mistake. I was watching Vess.

My second mistake was believing, even for one tide, that a woman who'd tampered with our ballast, bribed a harbor official, and sold our route to the Optimists would draw a line anywhere short of a child.

* * *

It happened fast, the way bad things do — no thunderclap, just a small rearrangement of the world.

Vess came aboard smiling. She embraced Juno, who allowed it the way you allow a cold wind. She complimented Pell on the Halcyon's trim through the gale channel, and Pell said, "You'd know, wouldn't you, having nearly sunk us," and Vess laughed like it was the best joke she'd heard all week, which, knowing Vess, it might have been.

Then she walked past me — past me, deliberately, a woman who had spent thirty years measuring herself against me choosing to walk past me — and stopped behind Wren at the rail.

And the hook came off the younger man's shoulder coil, and the line whispered out, and the barbed iron settled against Wren's throat with the gentleness of a hand adjusting a collar.

The deck went very quiet. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

"Now," Vess said pleasantly, "let's talk about the engine core."

* * *

Here is what twelve years of running teaches you: it teaches you every exit. It teaches you to count boats, blades, distances, seconds. It teaches you that the moment a thing goes wrong, your body is already halfway to gone while your mind is still catching up, apologizing.

I felt all of that arrive at once, the old machinery spinning up, and then I looked at Wren's face.

She wasn't crying. She wasn't even afraid, exactly. She was looking at the water below the rail, at the white coral branches, at the place where the hum came from, and her lips were moving slightly, keeping time with a song none of us could hear. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning in reverse, that if I ran — if I did what Bram Coldharbour does, what he has always done — nobody on this deck was going to follow me into the deep after that girl.

Juno couldn't dive alone. Pell wouldn't leave the ship. And the engine's song was already in Wren like a splinter working toward the heart.

So the exits closed. All of them. At once. And what was left standing there, in the space where a runner used to be, was just a man.

"Vess." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Put the hook down."

"I will, absolutely, the moment—" she began, sunny as ever.

"The core isn't yours. It isn't the Optimists'. It isn't the fleet's. And it isn't mine either, though I've spent twelve years acting like it was." I stepped forward, and the younger man adjusted his grip, and Wren made a small sound, and I stopped. "You want a trade? Fine. I'm offering you a better one than scrap metal."

Vess's eyes narrowed. Thirty years of rivalry had taught me every expression she owned, and this one — this narrow, hungry stillness — was new. This was interest.

"You're not worth much, Coldharbour," she said. "A washed-up chaser with a debt he can't pay and a crew he can't keep."

"Maybe. But I'm worth more than the core, and I'll tell you why." I took a breath, and it hurt, the way the first breath after a long dive hurts. "Because I know why the Meridian Bell was testing its engine in these waters twelve years ago. Because I was there. Because I signed the manifest. Because Tam Row died pulling me out of the resonance chamber on the engine's first live test, and I have been running from that fact ever since — across four seas, through two bankruptcies, into the arms of the Optimists themselves — and everyone who has ever hunted me has been hunting a man who doesn't exist anymore, because he drowned in this shoal right next to Tam, and only his body walked away."

The words came out of me like ballast off a sinking ship. I hadn't planned them. I hadn't known I was carrying them. But once they started, they wouldn't stop, and I found I didn't want them to.

"Vess Karrow," I said, "you've spent your whole life trying to buy leverage. Here it is. Free. The truth about the Meridian Bell's first test, from the last living witness. Use it against the corporation. Use it against the insurers. Use it against me. It's worth ten engines, because an engine can be rebuilt and a confession can't be unsaid."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve.

Vess tilted her head. Behind her, the younger man shifted his weight, and the hook scraped lightly against Wren's collar, and I heard Juno's breath catch beside me like a snapped line.

"You're serious," Vess said at last, and there was something almost wondering in it. "You're actually offering yourself. The great Bram Coldharbour. The man who once bet me a season's earnings he could outrun a squall in a dinghy." She laughed softly. "You know what I always hated about you? Not the racing. Not the trophies. It was that you never lost anything you weren't willing to walk away from. Ships, money, people — you treated it all like ballast. Throw it overboard, sail lighter, run faster." Her smile thinned. "And now you're standing there telling me your grief is currency."

"It's the only honest coin I've got left."

"Mm." She looked at Wren — really looked, maybe for the first time — and something flickered across her face that I couldn't name and didn't trust. "And if I take the trade? If I let the girl go and take you instead? What then? You think the truth of some twelve-year-old accident stops a corporate fleet?"

"No," I said. "But it stops *you*. That's the part you haven't figured out yet, Vess. You don't actually want the engine. You want to stop being invisible. Invisible men don't get to be moral — you told me that yourself, back when we were young enough to say true things to each other. So here's your visibility. A scandal with a name on it. Yours, if you want it. Mine, if you'd rather. Either way, the whole coast hears it by winter, because I'll make sure of it, hook or no hook."

"You'd testify? Against the corporation that built the thing?" She sounded genuinely curious now, the way she used to sound over charts at midnight, twenty years ago, before everything curdled. "Knowing what it costs?"

"I've paid worse for less."

* * *

What happened next, I want to record honestly, because I will spend the rest of my life being asked about it and I refuse to lie about the parts that flatter nobody.

Vess hesitated.

Not long. Maybe three seconds. Maybe five. But she hesitated, and in that hesitation I saw the girl she'd been on the Greywater docks — quick, brilliant, furious at a world that only handed out visibility to men who owned things — and I understood that she had come here genuinely uncertain whether she was a salvager or a thief, and that my confession had tipped her, and I would never know toward which side.

Then the sea decided for all of us.

The hum changed.

It had been steady all morning, that deep patient note beneath the hull, but now it swelled — swelled the way a swell comes before a wave you can't outrun — and the water around the wreck began to move wrong. Not chop. Not current. *Purpose.* Rings spreading outward from the coral cathedral, each one wider than the last, and every ring carried the note with it, higher, clearer, closer to the melody Wren had been humming since the first tide.

Wren's head came up. Her eyes had gone wide and dark and utterly elsewhere.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it remembers—"

"Wren." Juno moved first, god bless her, moving faster than I'd seen her move in twenty years of storms. "Wren, look at me—"

The younger man with the hook stumbled. His grip slipped — not from Juno, not from any of us, but from the note itself, which passed through him like a hand through smoke — and the hook swung wide and bit into the rail instead of anything that mattered.

And Vess Karrow, standing at the center of the widening rings with her coat lifting in a wind that wasn't blowing, turned to me with an expression I had never seen on her face in thirty years of rivalry.

Fear.

"Bram," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of my name, "what did they build?"

And the bell began to sing.

* * *

I wish I could tell you I thought clearly in the next moment. I didn't. Nobody did. The note came up through the deck plates and into our teeth and behind our eyes, and it was beautiful, that's the horror of it, it was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard, and it wanted things. I could feel it wanting. It wanted us to come down to it. It wanted us to open the housing and let it breathe. It wanted, with the patience of something that had waited twelve years in the dark, to be heard by everyone, everywhere, forever.

Pell was shouting something — I'd find out later it was "the housing seals are singing loose, the whole engine's waking, we have minutes" — but at the time it was just noise under the song.

Juno had Wren by the shoulders, shaking her, calling her name, and Wren was smiling, smiling like Tam must have smiled in the last seconds I saw him alive, and that image — that image above all others — is what broke the song's hold on me.

Because I had heard this before. Twelve years ago, through a chamber door, I had heard the engine's first test begin, and I had heard it reach for Tam, and I had run — I had run the other way, down the corridor, toward air, toward light, toward the rest of my life — and Tam had gone *toward* it, into the resonance chamber, to pull the test crew out, and the door had sealed behind him, and—

No. Not this time. Not this girl. Not this crew.

"Juno!" My voice tore through the song because it had twelve years of guilt behind it, and guilt, it turns out, cuts deeper than any note. "Get her below! Pell — the charge, the flood rig, *now!*"

"And Vess?" Pell shouted back, already moving.

I looked at Vess Karrow, standing in the rings of the singing water with her hand pressed to her ear and her empire of leverage dissolving around her, and I found — to my genuine surprise — that I didn't hate her. I pitied her. She'd spent her whole life reaching for power, and the first time she touched it, it reached back.

"She's on her own," I said. "We all are. That's the whole lesson."

I went over the rail.

The water took me like a mouth. Below me, in the green failing light, the Meridian Bell's engine glowed in its cathedral of coral, pulsing with the song, waiting to be owned by someone — anyone — everyone.

I kicked down toward it, carrying nothing but a flood charge, a flooded lungful of regret, and the truth.

Behind me, faintly, through the water, I heard Vess scream my name.

And ahead of me, growing brighter, the song opened its arms.
