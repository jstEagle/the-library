## 16. The Song Takes Hold

Wren had always thought of sound as something that happened *to* water — rain on the deck, wind in the rigging, Pell's snoring through the bulkhead like a slow leak in the world. She had never considered that water might be the one making the noise, or that the noise might be making *them*.

She was thinking about it now, because Vess Karrow had just raised the core out of its housing, and the whole sea had gone quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone says your name.

They were on the wreck's upper deck, if you could call it that — a spine of coral-crusted hull breaking the surface at low tide, the Meridian Bell's tower rising behind them like a church that had decided, twelve years ago, to lie down and think about things. The storm from the night before had spent itself into a gray, rinsed morning. The collectors' skiff sat anchored fifty yards off with three Optimists aboard, smiling even in their sleep. Somewhere beyond the shoal ring, the corporate fleet's survivors were picking through their wreckage in longboats, and every one of them, Wren noticed, had stopped rowing.

Everyone was looking at Vess.

Vess stood on the tilting deck with the resonance core cradled against her chest like a stolen infant, and she was not looking at anyone. She was listening.

"Put it down," Bram said. His voice came out wrong — scraped, like he'd been using it all night, which he had. "Vess. You heard what it did to the fleet's divers. You heard what it did to—"

"To Tam?" Vess said, and smiled her coin-counting smile, but Wren saw her knuckles whiten around the core's housing. "I heard a story. Stories are cheap."

"It's not a lure," Wren said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak. Her voice still felt borrowed, like she'd left hers somewhere down in the coral cathedral on the fourth tide and this one had grown back slightly different, slightly tuned.

Wren stepped forward over the wet planking. Juno's hand shot out to catch her sleeve; Wren let it, because Juno needed to hold onto something more than Wren needed to be free.

"It's not a lure," Wren said again, steadier. "A lure wants something. This doesn't want. It *keeps*. It's been keeping everything for twelve years. Every current, every storm, every—" She stopped. Her throat closed around the word *person*.

"Every diver," Pell finished quietly, from the rail.

"No," Wren said. "It doesn't keep people. It keeps what people leave behind."

The core hummed. Not loudly. The way a cat hums when it has decided you belong to it.

* * *

Here is what Wren had never told anyone, not even Juno, especially not Juno: when she'd surfaced from her solo dive on the fourth tide, humming a melody no one had taught her, she hadn't been frightened. That was the part that scared her afterward — the retroactive kind of fear, the kind that arrives late and stays for dinner.

Down there, in the blue cathedral where the Bell hung in its cage of living coral, she had heard the song properly for the first time. And it had sounded like home.

Not Greywater Point home. Not the Halcyon's cramped galley with Pell's burnt porridge and Juno's charts curling at the corners. A different home. A home made of every sound the Shoals had ever swallowed — anchor chains, shipwreck groans, whale calls, a man laughing underwater, which is a sound Wren had not known existed until the song showed her, and which she would spend the rest of her life trying to forget.

The engine didn't sing *to* you. That was the trap everyone kept falling into, Vess included, standing there hugging it like treasure. The engine sang *as* you. It found the note you were already holding — grief, greed, loneliness, whatever you carried — and it amplified it until you couldn't hear anything else. Until your own note was the whole ocean.

That was why it sounded like a lure. Because everyone who heard it was already lured, by themselves.

And that was why, Wren realized with a cold sliding feeling in her stomach, watching Vess lift the core higher, it was going to take Vess completely. Because Vess's note was hunger, and hunger was the one thing the engine could amplify forever.

* * *

"Karrow." Bram moved between Vess and the open water, hands loose at his sides, the old storm-reader's stance. "Twelve years I've run from this place. Twelve years I let you believe whatever story got you out of bed in the morning. But I'm done running, so listen close: that thing doesn't grant power. It grants *appetite*. You raise it all the way, you won't own coastlines. You'll just be the hungriest thing in the water."

"Invisible men don't get to be moral," Vess said, but her voice had changed. There was a vibration under it now, a harmonic that wasn't hers, and Wren felt her skin prickle. The core was already singing through her. Using her throat as a sounding board. "You had your chance to buy in, Coldharbour. Everyone here did. The collectors. The fleet. We're all creditors now, one way or another, and this—" she hoisted the core, and the hum deepened, and the water around the wreck began to move wrong, spiraling gently inward like it was being poured, "—this is the payout."

"That's not how debt works," Pell muttered. "I've read contracts. Debts end. That thing doesn't."

"Pell," Juno said softly. "Not helping."

The first to fall was the nearest collector. He was a big cheerful man they'd met twice before, once boarding the Halcyon mid-squall and once at anchor with a bomb, and both times his smile had been the professional kind, wide as a dock gate and about as sincere. Now his smile changed. It stopped being aimed at anyone. It just *was*, fixed and beatific, and he stood up in his skiff and stepped over the side without bothering to take off his boots.

"Oh no," Wren breathed. "No no no—"

He didn't sink. That was the horrible part. The water held him up — held him the way the song held notes — and he began to walk across the surface toward the wreck, arms out, face turned up toward the core like a man walking into sunlight after a long winter. Behind him, the other two collectors followed. Out past the shoal ring, the longboats of the fleet's survivors turned as one, oars shipping, and their crews stood and stepped into the sea, dozens of them, walking on water that had forgotten how to drown anyone because drowning was a kind of letting go and the song permitted no release.

"They can't swim," Pell said, in a small voice. "I mean — they don't have to. That's worse. That's so much worse."

"The engine's broadcasting at full resonance," Juno said, and her navigator's calm had cracks running through it, hairline fractures she was holding together by will alone. "It's not pulling them. It's *answering* them. Everyone out there wanted something so badly they'd sell the rest of themselves for it, and the song is giving them exactly that — the wanting, forever, with nothing left over."

Wren looked at the walkers. At their faces. They weren't terrified. That was the corruption of it, the audible rot underneath the beauty: they looked *grateful*. The big collector reached the base of the wreck and pressed both palms against the coral and started to climb, singing — actually singing, a low contented drone in a key no human throat should find comfortable — and the other two joined him, and far out, the whole drowned congregation of the Shoals' history seemed to stir.

Because it wasn't just the living anymore. Wren could feel them below, the ones the song had been keeping for twelve years. Not bodies. *Notes.* Left-behind sounds, braided into the resonance: a laugh, a shout, a last word. And underneath all of it, steady as a keel, one voice she knew.

Tam's.

Her eyes went hot. She didn't let herself listen yet. Not yet. First—

"Bram!" she shouted. "Bram, look at me!"

He was staring at the walkers, and his face was doing something Wren had never seen it do. Storm-chasers learn every kind of weather-reading there is, including the kind that happens in other people's expressions, and Bram Coldharbour could read a squall line in a stranger's eyebrow. What was on his face now wasn't fear. It was recognition. He was seeing twelve years of running arrive at its destination all at once.

"Bram, it can't take you," Wren said, and her voice cracked and she didn't care. "Don't you see? It takes what you're holding onto. It amplifies the note you won't put down. The collectors want money. Vess wants power. Those sailors want rescue. But you—" she grabbed his arm, both hands, "—you already gave it up. Last night. On the deck. You said it out loud. You told us about Tam. You *grieved*. You put the note down yourself, before the song ever asked. It's got nothing to grab."

Bram looked at her. And then — this was the moment Wren would carry for the rest of her life, the moment she'd measure all other moments against — Bram Coldharbour, who had spent twelve years being a practiced detachment wearing a man's coat, began to cry.

Not prettily. Not bravely. He cried the way a hull takes water, everywhere at once, and he didn't wipe his face and he didn't turn away, and the strange thing — the thing Wren would try to explain later to anyone who'd listen, and mostly fail — was that the song *recoiled* from him. Gently. Like a wave reconsidering. Where he stood, the spiral of the water flattened out, and the humming lost its grip on the air, and for thirty feet in every direction the sea was just sea again.

"You're right," he said, and his voice was wrecked and clear at the same time. "It can't hold what isn't held." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, an almost comically ordinary gesture, and looked at Vess. "Which means I'm the only one here who can walk up to you and take that thing away. So this is me, asking nicely. One more time. Put it down."

Vess laughed.

Except it wasn't entirely Vess laughing. The sound came out doubled, harmonized, the core singing through her ribs, and her eyes had gone bright and distant and terribly, terribly satisfied. "Asking nicely," she repeated. "Oh, Bram. You sweet, ruined man. Don't you understand? I can hear it now. All of it. Every coast from here to the Meridian Strait, every harbor, every debt — it's all *owed* to someone, and I'm going to be the one who collects." She raised the core above her head, arms trembling with effort or ecstasy, hard to tell. "You surrendered your grief. Congratulations. I surrender nothing. I never have. That's why it chose—"

The song surged.

Wren felt it hit like a physical wall — a pressure change, a barometric drop, the whole ocean inhaling. The walkers on the reef began to glow faintly, or maybe that was the water, or maybe that was Wren's eyes giving up on physics. The Bell itself, hanging in its coral cathedral below, answered its runaway heart for the first time in twelve years, and the note that came out of it bent the rain-scrubbed sky.

Juno grabbed Wren and Pell and pulled them down behind the bell tower's broken parapet. Bram didn't duck. He stood in the open, tears drying salt-streaked on his weathered face, and shouted over the roar:

"VES! IT'S EATING YOU! CAN'T YOU HEAR IT? IT'S NOT YOUR SONG ANYMORE!"

And Vess Karrow — brilliant, ruthless, smiling Vess, who had bought silence and skills and harbormasters and never once in her life bought anything she couldn't resell — opened her mouth to answer.

What came out was the engine's note, perfect and total and empty.

Her knees buckled. The core stayed aloft somehow, hovering at the edge of her failing grip, drinking her, and Wren understood with terrible clarity that they had perhaps a minute, maybe less, before there was no Vess left inside the sound at all — just the most dangerous woman on the coast, hollowed into a loudspeaker.

"Pell," Bram said calmly, horribly calmly, the calm of a man who has finally stopped running and therefore has nowhere left to be afraid of. "How fast can you flood that housing?"

Pell's head came up. Wren watched the engineer do the math — housing, tide gates, the charge still wired from the collectors' visit, all of it — and watched the fear cross his face and get filed neatly under *later*.

"If somebody holds the intake lever," Pell said, "and somebody else dives the charge past the song, and nobody minds dying a little — fast enough."

"Good." Bram was already pulling off his boots. "Juno, you have the ship. Wren—"

"I know," Wren said. "I'll listen for you. Both of you. All the way down."

Below them, the congregation of the taken began to sing along.

Above them, the core rose another inch out of Vess's opening hands, hungry, patient, certain —

— and Bram Coldharbour stepped off the edge of the wreck into the singing water, and went down.
