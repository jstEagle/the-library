## 17. Scuttle the Bell

The song had teeth.

Bram felt them as he stood at the Halcyon's rail, watching the water below the wreck glow with a light that wasn't light—resonance made visible, the engine's voice given a body. It rolled up through the hull, through his boots, through the bones of his hands where they gripped the rail. Somewhere above him the storm was still building, but down there, in the drowned cathedral of coral, it was calm. That was the trick of it. The song made drowning feel like coming home.

He'd watched it take the collectors. Two of them had gone over the side of their skiff an hour ago, smiling all the way down, swimming toward the Bell like men walking into church. The fleet survivors clinging to their overturned longboat hadn't even tried to stop them. They just hummed along.

Only the crew of the Halcyon still had their own minds. Bram understood why now, in the way you understand a thing after it's already cost you everything: the song couldn't hold anyone who had already let go. Wren had surrendered her fear. Juno had surrendered the lie that she could keep this family safe by force of will alone. Pell had surrendered, somewhere around the fourth tide, any pretense that he wasn't terrified—and had kept working anyway.

And Bram. Bram had finally, on the deck of a ship in a rising storm, said Tam's name out loud for the first time in twelve years, and meant it as a wound instead of a scar.

That was the toll. That was what the song charged for passage.

"Charge is set," Pell said beside him, and Bram dragged his eyes off the glowing water. Pell held up the housing rig—a jury-built collar of flood-valves and primer charges, ugly as sin, wrapped around the engine's outer casing like a tourniquet. "One pull on this line floods the housing. One spark from this—" he tapped a smaller canister, "—cracks the core's containment shell before the pressure does it for us. I'd rather crack it clean than let the sea do it sloppy."

"Sloppy how?"

"Sloppy, it sings louder on the way down." Pell's face did something complicated. "Clean, it goes quiet first. Then deep."

Bram nodded. Twelve years ago he'd watched the engine's first test sing a man apart. He would not watch it sing again.

Above them, at the bow, Vess Karrow had Wren.

It had happened fast—the way these things always do, in the gap between one wave and the next. Vess had come out of the storm-shadow of her launch with a boarding hook in one hand and grief dressed up as purpose in the other, and now the hook's point rested against Wren's collarbone, and Wren stood very still, humming. Still humming. Even now. Even with steel at her throat, the melody came out of her steady and low, and Bram realized with a lurch that she wasn't frozen. She was holding the line. She was keeping the song's attention on *her*, so it wouldn't reach for the rest of them.

Juno was three feet away with a belaying pin in her fist and murder in her posture, and Vess knew it, and the hook stayed where it was.

"The core," Vess called across the deck. Her voice carried strangely well over the wind, or maybe the wind was carrying it for her; the whole Shoals seemed to be listening now. "You bring it up, Coldharbour, or I start being someone neither of us recognizes."

"You're already her," Bram said. "I met her twelve years ago. She signed the insurance papers on the Meridian Bell."

Something flickered across Vess's face—not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing her own name called from a long way off.

"I have debts too," she said.

"So do I. I'm paying mine differently."

He turned to Pell. "How long to dive?"

"With the current running toward the trench? Ninety seconds down, if the song lets you swim straight."

"And if it doesn't?"

Pell looked at him for a long moment. Pell, who had gutted the collectors' bomb days ago without telling anyone, gambling everything on Bram's choice, because apparently that was the kind of faith engineers kept—quiet, structural, load-bearing. "Then it doesn't," Pell said. "But it will. You're the only one left on this boat who hasn't decided yet whether he wants to live. The song likes undecided people. Gives them room."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

* * *

The water took him like a held breath finally released.

Down through the green-black, past the coral spires that rose around the Meridian Bell like the pillars of a church no god had ordered built. The light pulsed from below, slow as a heartbeat, and the song was everywhere—not sound anymore, not exactly, but pressure, meaning, a voice speaking directly into the meat of him.

*Come home,* it said. *Come home, come home, come home.*

And underneath that, threaded through it, faint as a watermark: Tam's laugh. Tam saying *watch this*. Tam, twelve years old in memory and forever, standing on the deck of a younger Halcyon while the engine's first test screamed out of its housing—

Bram swam into it.

This was the part nobody warned you about, he thought. Everyone talked about drowning like it was a fight. Nobody told you it could be a conversation. The song pressed against him and he pressed back, not with strength—with surrender, the only coin it accepted. He gave it the memory whole. Not the version he'd been carrying, the edited one, the one where he'd been below decks when the test began. The true one. He'd been standing next to Tam. Tam had seen the housing seam split a half-second before anyone else, had seen what was coming, and had put both hands on Bram's chest and shoved him through the hatch, and the door had sealed, and Bram had heard everything through four inches of hull plating.

He'd spent twelve years telling himself he'd been somewhere else. Anywhere else.

*I was there,* he said to the song, to the water, to whatever was listening. *I was right there.*

The song went quiet around him. Not silent—attentive. Like the whole ocean leaning in.

The Bell hung ahead of him, vast and dark gold, crusted in coral, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful. The engine sat in its throat, and Pell's flood-collar gleamed around it, absurdly practical, a piece of honest machinery bolted onto a miracle. The primer charge waited at its heart, a small red canister, patient as a promise.

Bram pulled the line.

He felt the housing take water more than heard it—a shudder through the structure, a change in the song's timbre, like a singer clearing her throat. The glow stuttered. In the coral cathedral, fish scattered. Far above, muffled by fathoms, something crashed—the Halcyon's deck, bodies fighting, Vess and Juno, Wren screaming, all of it happening in another country now. His country was here. His country was this bell and this choice and ninety seconds of air.

The song changed.

It stopped saying *come home.* It started saying *no.*

The pressure hit him like a hand. The current reversed—currents didn't reverse, currents didn't *do* that, except they did here, they did whatever the engine wanted—and it pushed him backward, up, away, gently at first, then not gently. The song poured into his ears and found every soft place he had. It showed him Greywater Point at sunset. It showed him a crew that stayed. It showed him Tam alive, turning around, grinning, saying *you made it out, Bram, you made it out*—

"Yeah," Bram said into his regulator, bubbles streaming, words lost to the water but not to himself. "I did. That's the problem."

He kicked against the current.

It was the hardest swimming of his life, and he had once outrun a squall off Cape Marrow with a torn jib and a broken arm. The engine was stronger than his body and always would be. But it was negotiating with a man who had finally finished paying, and there is a kind of stubbornness that isn't strength at all—it's just having nothing left to lose and knowing it. He grabbed the coral. He climbed. Hand over hand up the spires, the song shrieking now, actually shrieking, the note bending upward into a register that cracked the smaller corals off the walls and sent them drifting down like snow.

His fingers found the primer charge.

He thought about pulling the pin with a prayer. He didn't. He thought about pulling it with a speech. He didn't do that either. What he did, with the last of his air and the last of his strength, was think about Pell's face when he'd said *clean, it goes quiet first* — and he trusted that face, the way you trust good work, and he pulled.

The charge cracked the containment shell with a sound he felt rather than heard, a single enormous pulse that passed through the water and through him and through everything, and the song—

The song stopped mid-word.

All of it. Everywhere. The pressure vanished. The current died. The glow guttered out of the coral like a lamp blown out, and Bram hung in sudden ordinary darkness, in ordinary cold seawater, deafened by an absence so complete it rang.

For one heartbeat, the whole ocean held its breath.

Then the Bell began to sink.

The flooded housing did its work. Slowly, majestically, the way mountains are not in a hurry, the Meridian Bell tipped and slid from its perch of twelve years and fell toward the trench, trailing silt and light and the last ghost of itself. And as it fell, it sang one final time—one single note, pure and small and unweaponized, a note with no command in it, no hunger, no hook. Just a sound. A bell, ringing, because it was a bell.

Bram listened until the water swallowed the sound.

Then silence, real silence, settled over the Siren Shoals for the first time in twelve years, and Bram Coldharbour hung alone in the black water and wept into his mask, and the ocean held him the way oceans do—indifferently, which was somehow exactly right.

* * *

He surfaced fifty yards from the Halcyon, and the storm hit him like a slap of cold reality: rain, wind, chaos. But the deck was quiet. Quiet in the specific way of a thing that has already ended.

Juno hauled him aboard with two hands and no ceremony, and then, breaking every rule she had about harbormasters-in-waiting and professional composure, hugged him hard enough to crack a rib.

"Wren?" he managed.

"Fine. Vess is gone."

"Gone?"

"Gone." Juno jerked her chin toward the open water, where through the rain you could just make out a fast launch running south, lights swinging wild, taking the storm at full tilt with nothing in her hold. "Wren broke the hook-hold herself—somehow, don't ask me, ask her, she won't answer—Vess got to the rail, and then the song cut out, and you should have seen her face, Bram. She was standing in a storm holding a weapon and a prize, and the prize went quiet, and she just... looked at her own hands. Then she ran. Didn't say a word. Left her manifest on the deck, though." Juno's mouth twisted. "Half-burned. Enough."

Enough to prove what Vess had intended. Enough for the Optimists, maybe, someday, if it ever mattered. Bram looked at the empty water where the launch had been and felt—not triumph. Something quieter. Pity, almost. Vess Karrow had sailed into the Shoals to take a song and had left with nothing but the memory of how it felt to lose one.

"Pell!" Bram shouted over the wind.

Pell appeared at the companionway, soaked, soot-streaked, grinning like a man who had won a bet with God. "Housing flooded clean. Core's cracked. Whatever's left of it is settling into the trench about now, going down nice and quiet." He paused. "You hear it? The last note?"

"Yeah."

"Good." Pell nodded once, and that was the whole eulogy, and it was enough.

Wren came and stood next to Bram at the rail. She was pale and shaking and there was a thin line on her collarbone where the hook had kissed her, and she was looking down at the water with an expression Bram couldn't read—until he could. It was the look of someone who has lost a voice they loved and discovered their own in the same moment.

"It's still down there," she said quietly. "Not singing. But... remembering. I think it remembers everything it ever heard." She turned to him, and her eyes were wet, and fierce. "Bram. It has Tam."

The rain came down. The storm raged on around a crew standing very still at the rail of a battered kite-ship, and Bram felt the words land in his chest like a second anchor dropping.

Because he'd sunk the engine to keep its power from the world. He'd never once considered what else might be down there in the dark, held in twelve years of recorded water, waiting.

"What do you mean," he said slowly, "*it has Tam?*"
