## 18. Aftermath of Silence

Pell had always believed that silence was just a sound you hadn't learned to hear yet. An engine idling, a hull flexing, the tick of metal cooling in the dark—there was always something, if you put your ear to the right part of the world. So when the Shoals went quiet, truly quiet, for the first time in twelve years, Pell did what any sensible engineer would do.

He checked the ship for faults.

He checked all of them. Twice. He crawled into the bilge with a lantern and lay on his back in three inches of seawater listening to the Halcyon's bones. He put his palm flat against the mast and held it there until his arm went numb. He even, in a moment of desperation he would later deny under oath, pressed his ear against the ship's cat, Biscuit, who was asleep in the coil of spare line and who objected to this with a noise that was, at least, something.

Nothing. The sea around them had gone still as poured glass, and the song—the hum that had lived in the hull since the second tide, the one Wren had heard first and wouldn't talk about—was gone. Not faded. Gone, the way a tooth is gone. You kept finding the hole it used to fill.

"Stop doing that," Juno said. She was at the rail with a mug of something hot and terrible, watching the water. "You've checked the keel four times."

"Three."

"Four. I counted. You're like a man patting his pockets for keys he's already holding."

"I don't have keys," Pell said. "I have a ship that's missing a very large noise, and I'd like to know where it went."

"You know where it went." Juno didn't turn around. "You put it there."

That was the thing about Juno. She said things like *you put it there* the way other people said *pass the bread*. Pell climbed out of the bilge, wrung out his sleeve, and came to stand at the rail beside her, because his legs had decided they were done with crawling and his hands, now that he looked at them, were shaking in a way he hadn't noticed until there was a rail to hold.

The trench was off to starboard, a darker seam in the dark water. Somewhere down there, past the coral cathedral and the light that didn't reach, the Meridian Bell lay on its side with its engine flooded and dead, singing nothing to nobody. Pell had rigged the housing to flood, and Bram had dived through a storm and a song to set the charge, and now it was done, and it was morning, and nobody had died, which Pell considered a frankly suspicious level of success for a job with this many moving parts.

"You did good work," Juno said.

"I rigged a bomb to drown a bell."

"You rigged a bomb to drown a bell *correctly*." She sipped her terrible drink. "There's a difference. Most people can't tell. That's why most people aren't you."

Pell looked at his hands again. They were still shaking. He decided to let them.

* * *

The bomb was discovered at midmorning, which was late for a bomb, and that was entirely the point.

It was Wren who found it—found it *again*, technically, because she'd found it the first time too, back when the collectors had boarded at anchor and smiled their way off the ship leaving a parting gift lashed under the stern gallery. She came up from below decks at a dead run, skidded on the wet planks, caught herself on the rail, and said, "It's beeping."

"Beeping," Pell repeated.

"The collectors' thing. Under the stern. It's beeping now. It wasn't before."

Pell felt something in his chest do a slow, cold roll, like a ballast stone shifting. Because he knew what that meant. He'd known what it meant since the moment he'd first laid eyes on the thing, four days ago, when everyone else had been arguing about Vess and the engine and whether belonging to a family meant agreeing with it. He'd known, and he'd made a choice, and he'd told exactly no one, because some gambles only pay if nobody watches you place the bet.

"Well," he said. "Let's go have a look at it."

The device sat where it had sat for four days: a squat iron case bolted to the hull framing under the stern gallery, wrapped in tarred canvas, with a little brass timer face and a pressure trigger and enough explosive behind it to take the Halcyon's stern off and introduce the rest of the ship to the ocean in a manner neither party would enjoy. The timer face was blinking now. A small amber light, patient as a debt collector's smile.

Bram arrived, then Juno, then Vess's abandoned launch crew—no, not them, they'd gone; just the four of them and Biscuit, who followed at a dignified distance because cats understand that drama is best observed from slightly uphill.

"It's on a delay," Bram said. His voice was very level, the way it got when it was holding something down. "They said as much. Collateral. It arms when we're clear of the Shoals, or if we try to move it."

"It's not armed," Pell said.

Everyone looked at him.

"It's *blinking*," Wren said.

"It's blinking because I left the blinker on." Pell crouched down in front of the case, and his knees cracked, and he decided he was too tired to care about that either. "The rest of it, I gutted the night they boarded. Day before the storm run. Detonator's out. Charge's still in there, because I didn't much fancy prying out forty pounds of Optimist-grade explosive over open water with a smile like that still fresh in my memory, but it's about as dangerous as a bucket of nails now. A heavy, rude bucket of nails."

The silence that followed had a different quality than the Shoals' silence. This one was full of people not saying things.

Bram crouched down next to him. "Four days," he said. "You've known for four days."

"Five, now. Time flies."

"You let us sail into a storm run with a bomb on the stern and you didn't say—"

"I said the *bomb* didn't say anything. There's a difference, and most people can't tell, and that's why most people aren't me." Pell pulled the tarred canvas back so they could see the guts of the thing: the timer, blinking faithfully at nothing, wired to a detonator that was wired to a short length of copper that was wired, at the far end, to absolutely nowhere. "I checked the trigger, the timer, the primer. All dead. I did it while everyone was shouting at each other about the dive gear, which, for the record, was the best cover noise I've ever worked under, and I'd like it noted that Juno's shouting is structurally load-bearing on this ship."

"You gambled," Juno said. Not angry. Worse—interested. "On what?"

Pell sat back on his heels. The amber light blinked. Down the length of the ship, the sea lay flat and quiet, and the Shoals held no song, and somewhere below all of it a bell kept its own counsel in the dark.

"On Bram," he said. "On him choosing to scuttle it. Because I looked at that bomb and I thought: the Optimists built this to make sure we deliver. Which means the only way it doesn't matter is if there's nothing left to deliver. And I thought about what Bram does when someone tells him he has to do a thing." He shrugged. "Bram does the other thing. It's the most reliable system on this ship. More reliable than my engines, and I built my engines."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough at the edges. "You could have told me."

"And you'd have done what? Told me to disarm it properly, in the middle of everything, with Vess watching and the fleet inbound and Wren hearing voices in the water? You'd have carried it around in your head like you carry everything else, and it'd have made you slower, and slow is the one thing we couldn't afford." Pell stood up, knees cracking again, a full percussion section now. "Besides. If I'd been wrong, we'd all have been in very small pieces, and it would've been rude to make you worry about it in advance."

"That's not—" Bram started, and stopped, and put a hand over his face. "That's not how it works, Pell."

"It's exactly how it works. I've watched you do it for twelve years." Pell patted the dead bomb affectionately, the way he patted engines that had earned their keep. "I just finally did it back."

* * *

They spent the afternoon making the ship honest.

Pell hauled the gutted bomb up on deck and set it in the sun like a caught fish, and the four of them stood around it in a loose half-circle while he took it apart properly, piece by piece, narrating as he went, because teaching was the only way he knew how to be calm and he'd learned long ago not to fight it.

"Timer, decorative. Trigger, decorative. This little beauty here—" he held up a coiled length of fuse, "—genuine Optimist workmanship, which is to say it looks expensive and does half of what it promises. They build their bombs like they build their contracts. All smile, no teeth, until the one moment where the teeth matter." He dropped the fuse in the scrap bucket with a clank. "And the charge itself. Forty pounds, give or take. Enough to ruin a stern. Enough to ruin a morning."

"Enough to ruin a crew," Wren said quietly.

Pell looked at her. She'd been different since the fifth tide—not worse, exactly, not broken, but *carried*, the way a jar of water is carried very carefully. She'd heard the song longer than any of them. She'd answered it, in her way. And now the song was gone and she was standing in the quiet it left behind, and Pell, who understood machines and only pretended to understand people, recognized the look of someone whose favorite instrument had been sold out from under them.

"Hey," he said. "Come here. You want to see the best part?"

She came. He showed her the detonator housing, the little brass chamber where the spark would have lived. "See, this is the part I like. All that engineering, all that menace, and it comes down to one spark in one chamber. One tiny thing deciding everything." He tapped the empty chamber. "I took the spark out four days ago and I've been carrying it around in my pocket ever since."

He fished it out. A detonator cap, small as a thumbnail, sitting in his palm.

Wren stared at it. "You've had it in your *pocket*?"

"Where else? It's the only dangerous part. You don't leave the dangerous part lying about." He turned it over. "I kept waiting for the right moment to tell Bram. And then there wasn't a right moment, there was just the storm, and then the wreck, and then the song, and then—" he gestured at the flat, empty, singing-nothing sea, "—this. So. Here's the moment. Late, but here."

"You gambled," Wren said, and there was something in her voice that wasn't quite accusation, wasn't quite awe. "On all of us."

"On him, mostly. But yes. On all of you." Pell closed his fist around the cap. "And I want to be honest about what that means, because nobody else on this ship will be. It means I got lucky. That's the whole truth of it. If Bram had decided to hand the engine over—if he'd decided the debt was worth more than the deep—I'd have gutted this thing for nothing, and we'd be delivering a weapon to people who smile too much, and I'd have helped them do it. That was the bet. Me, my read on one man, and forty pounds of somebody else's explosives. Luck, all the way down." He held the cap up to the light, squinting at it. "People think engineers don't believe in luck. Engineers believe in luck more than anybody. We just like to know exactly how much of it we're using."

Bram had come up behind them without Pell noticing, which was unlike Bram, who moved like a man who'd spent twelve years practicing not being noticed and had gotten very good at it. He was looking at the gutted bomb, and then at Pell's closed fist, and his face was doing something complicated that Pell decided not to interpret, because interpreting Bram's face was Juno's job and she had a whole system for it.

"Thank you," Bram said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the version of you that's too stubborn to do what he's told."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it. That's why I'm deflecting." Pell dropped the detonator cap into the scrap bucket, where it landed on the dead fuse with a small, final click. "There. The most dangerous thing left on this ship is Juno's cooking, and even that's survivable if you aim for the middle."

Juno, from the rail, without turning around: "I heard that."

"You were meant to."

* * *

Evening came down soft, the first evening in six tides that didn't taste like a countdown.

Pell sat on the stern with his back against the dead bomb—his bomb, his trophy, his bucket-of-nails—and ate whatever Juno had made, aiming for the middle, and watched the light go gold and then grey over water that held no song and demanded nothing. Wren sat nearby with her knees drawn up, humming sometimes, little fragments of nothing-melodies, and each time she caught herself doing it she'd stop, and then a minute later she'd start again, and Pell decided this was a good sign. A person who hums is a person whose insides are moving.

Bram stood at the bow a long time, the way he did, looking at the horizon the way he did, and Pell had spent twelve years reading that silhouette and for the first time couldn't tell what it was counting. Miles, usually. Bram counted miles the way other men counted money or sins. But the Shoals were behind them now, and the debt was still out there—unpaid, unsmiled-at, a thing with teeth that hadn't bitten yet—and Bram stood at the bow not like a man measuring distance.

More like a man deciding he was done measuring.

"He's going to do something stupid," Pell said to Juno, quietly.

"He's going to do something *brave*," Juno said.

"Those are the same thing with different outcomes, and the outcome hasn't been decided yet, which means it's stupid until further notice."

She almost smiled. "What do you think he'll do?"

Pell chewed. Considered. Aimed for the middle. "He's not going to run. That's the thing everyone gets wrong about Bram, even Bram. Running's what he does when he doesn't have a better idea. And for the first time since I've known him—" Pell nodded at the flat, quiet sea, at the trench where a bell kept its silence, at the gutted bomb warming his back like a stove, "—he's got a better idea. I don't know what it is yet. But it's going to cost him something, and he's going to pay it on purpose, and then he's going to stand there afterward like a man waiting to be told he did it wrong."

"And will he have?"

Pell looked out at the water. Somewhere under all that stillness, twelve years of song had settled into the deep like sediment, and nothing down there was singing, and nothing up here owed anybody a smile.

"No," he said. "For once in his stubborn life, I don't think he will."

Behind them, below decks, Biscuit knocked something off a shelf, and the small crash of it rang through the quiet hull like a bell—Pell's bell, the ship's bell, the only one they needed—and nobody got up to see what it was, because for the first time in six tides, everyone aboard the Halcyon was exactly where they meant to be.

Except, Pell noticed, Bram had gone below. And Bram had taken the chart case with him.

Pell watched the cabin light come on, and watched Bram's shadow bend over the charts, and reached for his tea with the deep, professional calm of a man whose most reliable system was about to be tested one more time.

"There it is," he said. "The better idea."

Juno turned around at last. "What's he doing?"

"Plotting a course to the one place on this coast he's spent twelve years sailing around." Pell smiled into his cup, tired and certain and, he admitted freely to himself, lucky. "The Optimists are going to get a visitor."
