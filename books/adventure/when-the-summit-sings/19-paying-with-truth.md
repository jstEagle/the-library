## 19. Paying With Truth

The Optimists kept their offices in a converted lighthouse at the mouth of Greywater harbor, which Bram had always thought was either the funniest or the most honest thing about them. A lighthouse was supposed to warn ships away from danger. The Optimists used theirs to invite them in.

He'd walked past it a hundred times in twelve years of owing them money. He'd never once gone through the door on purpose.

Today he went through it on purpose.

The interior smelled of lemon oil and fresh bread, because of course it did. The Optimists believed that debt should feel like hospitality right up until it felt like surgery. A young woman with a sunflower pinned to her lapel greeted him from behind a desk built out of an old ship's wheel, and her smile was so warm and so genuine that Bram felt his stomach drop anyway. He'd learned that the smile meant nothing and everything. It was weather. You didn't take it personally; you just dressed for it.

"Mr. Coldharbour," she said. "We've been expecting you."

"I know," Bram said. "That's usually how it works."

She laughed like he'd said something charming, and led him up the spiral stair to the top of the lighthouse, where the lamp room had been replaced by a round office with windows on every side and a view of the whole grey harbor. And there, pouring tea from a pot that probably cost more than the Halcyon's hull, stood Master Collector Ferrow Dunmore.

Dunmore was sixty if he was a day, silver-haired, round-faced, with the build of a man who had once been strong and now simply comfortable. He looked like somebody's favorite uncle. He looked like a man who would help you carry your groceries and then break your kneecaps over a missed payment, apologizing the entire time, meaning both the apology and the breaking.

"Coldharbour." Dunmore beamed. "Sit. Tea?"

"No thank you."

"More for me, then." He poured two cups anyway, which told Bram everything about how this meeting was going to go. "You know, when your crew came back without the Bell, without Vess Karrow, without so much as a souvenir, I'll confess I was disappointed. And then I heard what happened out there, and I thought—well. That's a man who just made a very expensive decision." He handed over one cup regardless. Bram took it, mostly to have something to do with his hands. "Forty days became thirty-one while you were sailing. The arithmetic doesn't wait politely."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Everyone knows the arithmetic. It's why we're all friends here." Dunmore settled into his chair, which was positioned so the light fell exactly right on his face, warm and grandfatherly. Bram wondered if they trained that or if it was a talent you were born with, like perfect pitch. "So. Before we discuss terms—and we will discuss terms—I want to hear it from you. The engine. Where is it?"

"At the bottom of the Siren Trench," Bram said. "Under four hundred fathoms of water, inside a housing Pell flooded with concrete ballast and welded shut on three sides. Even if you found it, even if you raised it, you'd spend more getting it open than the thing is worth. And by then the fleet will have written it off, and the insurance consortium will have blacklisted anyone who touched it, and you'll own the most expensive paperweight on the coast."

Dunmore sipped his tea. "You scuttled it."

"I scuttled it."

"Why?"

It was the question Bram had been turning over for six days, since the Shoals went quiet, since Wren played them Tam's voice through a salvaged resonance coil and Juno cried into her charts and Pell pretended he wasn't crying into his wrenches. Six days of turning it over, and the answer hadn't changed.

"Because some things shouldn't belong to anybody," Bram said. "And because I've spent twelve years learning what it costs when people treat other people like salvage."

Dunmore set down his cup with a small, precise click. The warmth in his face didn't leave, but something underneath it shifted, the way the sea shifts before it changes its mind about being calm.

"That's a lovely sentiment, Coldharbour. Sentiment doesn't clear debts."

"No," Bram agreed. "But this does."

He put the oilskin packet on the table between them.

* * *

He'd spent the six days building it, and it had cost him more than any salvage run ever had—not in coin but in the particular exhaustion of deciding to stop running. Every night in the workshop Pell had opened for himself back at Greywater Point, Bram had sat with the Halcyon's logs spread out around him like a surgeon's instruments, and Juno had sat across from him with her charts, and they'd worked through it together, because that was the other thing he was learning: you didn't pay your debts alone. That was how you ended up owing twice as much.

The packet held three things.

The first was a chart. Not one of Vess's—those had burned with her launch, or sunk with it, depending on which story you believed about how she'd cleared the storm. This one was older, copied out in Juno's careful hand from the Halcyon's twelve years of accumulated soundings, and it marked three wrecks sitting in waters no salvage company had ever surveyed properly. The Coriander Queen, a merchantman out of the southern trade lanes, lost with a hold full of indigo dye worth more than the Meridian Bell's engine ever would have been. The Saint Aldous, a mail packet whose strongroom had never been recovered. And the third—the one that made Bram's chest tight every time he looked at it—a survey vessel called the Lanternfish, which had gone down in the deep channel off Cape Morrow carrying instruments that could map the seabed to a fathom's accuracy.

Three wrecks. Three fortunes. None of them singing, none of them dangerous, none of them able to enslave a coastline. Just honest, dead ships full of honest, dead valuables.

"The Optimists' salvage monopoly runs on exclusivity," Bram said. "You're the only outfit licensed to recover insured losses on this coast, which means you set the recovery rates, which means the insurance consortium pays whatever you ask, which means premiums stay high, which means every fishing family from here to the Cape pays your overhead whether they ever lose a boat or not. Am I close?"

"You're describing our business model flatteringly," Dunmore said.

"I'm describing it accurately. Here's the problem." Bram tapped the chart. "Your monopoly depends on the consortium believing there's no competition and no alternative. But the Lanternfish is carrying hydrographic instruments that would let *anyone* map these waters. If those instruments come up—if they end up in the hands of the harbor pilots' guild, say, or the navigator's school at Greywater Point—suddenly wreck locations aren't secrets anymore. Suddenly recovery isn't a licensed art. Suddenly the consortium starts asking why they're paying your rates when a cooperative with a crane and a good chart could do the same job for a tenth of the price."

Dunmore's teacup paused halfway to his mouth. It resumed. But slower.

"And the second item?" he asked.

Bram slid a folded sheaf of papers across the table. Depositions. Sworn ones, witnessed by the harbormaster-elect of Greywater Point, who turned out to have surprisingly beautiful handwriting for someone who navigated by smell and argument.

"Testimony," Bram said. "From me, from my engineer, from my navigator, regarding the events at the Siren Shoals. Including the part where an Optimist collector skiff intercepted us at sea and offered to forgive our debt in exchange for delivering a resonance weapon into your hands. Including the part where your organization left an armed explosive device aboard a civilian vessel as, and I quote your own man here, 'collateral.'" He tapped the page. "Names, dates, positions. The fleet survivors gave corroborating statements too—they were feeling chatty after losing two ships to a storm channel they didn't understand. The consortium's underwriters are going to read this and realize their exclusive salvage partner tried to acquire a prohibited weapon through deniable intermediaries. You know what underwriters do when they find that out?"

"They reassess," Dunmore said quietly.

"They reassess. Your monopoly's insurance backing evaporates. Your licenses get reviewed. Your rates get audited by people who enjoy auditing." Bram leaned back. "Or—and I'm just floating this—the testimony stays in a drawer, the chart goes to nobody, and we talk about my debt instead."

The lamp room was very quiet. Out the windows, the harbor went about its morning, gulls arguing over something, a trawler putting out past the point, ordinary life happening to people who had no idea what it cost to sit across from Ferrow Dunmore and not blink.

Dunmore laughed. It was a real laugh, which was somehow worse than the fake ones.

"Well," he said. "Well, well, well. Twelve years I've collected on you, Coldharbour. Twelve years of watching you dodge, defer, sail away, sail back, always running, always paying late, always paying scared. And now you walk into my lighthouse, sit down at my table, refuse my tea—" he gestured at the untouched cup, "—and negotiate with me like I'm the one who owes *you*." He shook his head, delighted. "What happened to you out there?"

Bram thought about it honestly, because Dunmore deserved honesty; it was the only currency either of them respected.

"I stopped counting miles," he said, "and started counting years. Turns out the math comes out different."

* * *

They talked for two hours.

Dunmore fought, because he was good at this and because a man who rolled over wouldn't have lasted thirty years in the smiling business. He argued that the chart alone was worth less than the debt. Bram pointed out that the chart plus silence about the bomb was worth considerably more than the debt, and that Dunmore knew it, and that the only reason they were still talking was that Dunmore had done his own arithmetic and didn't like the answer. He argued that the testimony could be discredited. Bram suggested he try, and watch what the fleet survivors' lawyers did with the attempt. He argued, at last, almost plaintively, that the Optimists had a reputation to maintain.

"Reputations are maintained by surviving," Bram said. "Yours survives better with three wrecks and quiet than with a scandal and a war."

"You've thought about this."

"My navigator helped. She thinks faster than I do. My engineer rigged the demonstration." He nodded at the corner of the room, where a small brass device sat on the floor, humming faintly—one of Pell's resonance coils, tuned to the exact frequency the collectors' bomb had used, currently doing nothing more menacing than making Dunmore's inkwell rattle. "She wanted you to know she could have gutted your little gift anytime she liked. She waited until after we got home, on account of manners."

"Your engineer has a terrifying idea of manners."

"She's from the Shoals coast. We all are, one way or another."

In the end, Dunmore drew up the papers himself, in his own hand, because he was old-fashioned that way and because he understood, better than most, that a contract written grudgingly gets contested and a contract written cleanly gets honored. The debt of the Coldharbour crew—principal, interest, penalties, the whole ugly sum of twelve years—was exchanged against the chart of three wrecks, the sealed testimony, and one additional condition that Bram had insisted on and Dunmore had resisted and finally, wearily, granted:

A public statement. One paragraph, posted in the harbormaster's office at Greywater Point, declaring the accounts of Bram Coldharbour and crew settled in full, with the Optimists' compliments.

"Not forgiven," Dunmore said, signing. "Traded. Let's be accurate. Forgiveness implies generosity, and I have a reputation."

"Traded," Bram agreed. "I can live with traded."

"You know," Dunmore said, capping his pen, "I half expected you to burn the testimony anyway. After the papers were signed. On principle."

"I considered it."

"What stopped you?"

Bram looked out the window at the harbor, at the grey water and the gulls and the little figure on the quay below who was unmistakably Wren, waiting for him, trying very hard to look like she wasn't waiting for him, failing completely.

"Somebody taught me that holding onto things out of spite is just another way of not letting go," he said. "I'm practicing."

* * *

Wren was seventeen now, or thereabouts—she'd never been certain of her own birthday, which she treated as a personal affront—and she had developed, in the six days since the Shoals, a new habit of listening. She listened to the harbor. She listened to the wind off the point. Once, Bram had caught her standing barefoot on the Halcyon's deck with her eyes closed, head tilted, and when he'd asked what she was hearing she'd said, "Nothing. It's wonderful," in a voice that made his throat hurt.

She fell into step beside him on the quay without a word. They walked the length of the waterfront, past the fish stalls and the rope walks and the tavern where Vess Karrow had once bought them drinks with money she intended to make back a hundredfold. The harbormaster's office door stood open, and inside, Juno was already at work at the big desk, charts everywhere, arguing cheerfully with a customs officer about mooring fees. Through the open shutter of the workshop across the lane came the sound of Pell's hammer, steady as a heartbeat, and beneath it, faintly, the hum of a hundred small projects coming back to life.

"So?" Wren said. "Did he sign?"

"He signed."

"And the debt?"

"Gone. Traded. There'll be a notice in the harbormaster's office by evening, if Juno hasn't already framed it."

Wren considered this. "You paid them with the truth."

"I paid them with three shipwrecks and a threat, and the truth was the threat."

"Hm." She walked a few more steps. "Tam would've liked that. He always said you could get further with a straight answer than a fast pair of legs. You never believed him."

"No," Bram said. "I didn't."

"And now?"

Bram stopped at the end of the quay, where the planks gave way to the shingle and the shingle gave way to the grey, patient sea. Behind him: the town, the crew, the workshop hammering and the charts unrolling and the navigator's school brochure Wren kept hidden in her jacket like contraband. Ahead of him: water, all the way to the edge of the world, holding nothing at all.

"Now," he said, "I'm going to go home, eat something Juno burns, listen to Pell complain about his hammer arm, and tomorrow morning I'm going to sleep past dawn for the first time in twelve years. And the day after that, I'm going to start learning the names of the streets I've lived beside my whole life without ever walking down."

Wren smiled—that quick, crooked smile of hers, the one that had first surfaced, changed, in the green light of the drowned cathedral. "That sounds terrible."

"It sounds terrifying," Bram agreed. "That's how I know it's right."

He turned toward the town, toward all of it, and that was when Juno came out of the harbormaster's office at a pace just short of running, a letter in her hand, her face doing something complicated.

"Bram," she called. "Bram—you'd better see this. It came on the morning packet. It's got a Karrow seal on it."

Bram looked at the letter. He looked at the sea. He took the letter.

Inside, in handwriting he recognized the way you recognize a scar, was a single line—and beneath it, folded small, a scrap of chart paper showing coordinates he knew better than his own heartbeat.

The Shoals. She was going back to the Shoals.
