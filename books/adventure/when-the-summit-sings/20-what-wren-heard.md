## 20. What Wren Heard

The Shoals had gone quiet, and Wren couldn't sleep in the quiet.

She'd spent her whole life learning to sleep through noise—the groan of the Halcyon's rigging, Pell's snoring through two bulkheads, Juno's pen scratching charts at all hours like a beetle in a wall. Noise meant the ship was alive. Noise meant everyone was where they should be. But silence, after everything, felt like a held breath.

So she sat up on deck with a blanket around her shoulders and the resonance engine's salvaged recording drum in her lap—just the drum, the brass cylinder Pell had carefully cut free from the wreck's housing before they'd flooded the rest of it into the trench. It was no bigger than a loaf of bread. It weighed almost nothing. It had nearly killed all of them.

The moon was down. The stars over Greywater Point were doing that thing stars did when you finally stopped moving—multiplying, thickening, leaning close like eavesdroppers.

Wren put her ear against the drum.

She hadn't told anyone yet. Not even Juno. Especially not Juno.

Because three nights ago, when she'd surfaced from her last dive with salt water still ringing in her ears, she hadn't just been humming a melody no one taught her. She'd been humming it *backwards*. And when she'd finally stopped fighting the sound in her head and let it play the way it wanted to play—not forward, not backward, but *inward*, the way the Bell sang, folding every note into every other note—she'd heard something underneath the song.

Not a lure. She was sure of that now, sure the way you were sure about your own heartbeat. Everyone had assumed the engine broadcast to pull people in, the way a lantern pulls moths, the way it had pulled Tam. But a lure only says one thing: *come here.* This said thousands of things. It said them all at once, layered and tangled, and most of them were just—the Shoals. Twelve years of the Shoals. Currents shifting. Coral growing. Fish passing in silver rivers. The slow patient argument between tide and stone.

It wasn't singing to them. It was *remembering out loud.*

And underneath twelve years of remembering, tucked into a fold of sound so small she'd needed Pell's best listening horn pressed to her own skull to find it—

A voice.

* * *

She waited until morning, because some things shouldn't be said in the dark, and because she wanted Pell there when she explained it. Pell would understand the mechanism. Pell could make it make sense, which would make it survivable for the others.

She found the whole crew crammed into the galley, which is what the whole crew did now, every morning, as if by unspoken agreement nobody was allowed to be alone for longer than it took to use the head. Bram was nursing tea he wasn't drinking. Juno was pretending to study a chart of waters they were done with. Pell was dismantling a toaster for reasons known only to Pell.

Wren set the drum on the table.

"Before anyone says anything," she said, "I need you to know I'm not asking permission."

Bram looked up. "That's usually how you start asking permission."

"I'm telling you what I found." Her hands were steady. She'd practiced this in the dark, saying it to the stars. "The song. Everyone thinks it was calling us. It wasn't. It doesn't work like that—it can't work like that. It's a recorder. It's been recording since Vess's people built it, or since it washed into the Shoals, or—I don't know exactly—but it records. Everything. Every current, every storm, every—" She stopped. Started again. "Every voice that ever came near it."

The galley went very still. Even the toaster stopped clinking in Pell's hands.

"Twelve years," Wren said quietly. "It's been sitting in the Shoals for twelve years. And we came near it once before."

Nobody said Tam's name. Nobody had to. Wren watched it land on each of them anyway—on Bram like a blow, on Juno like cold water, on Pell like a weight settling onto already-bent shoulders.

"There's a message," Wren said. "Buried under everything else. Folded into the harmonics so deep you'd never find it unless you already knew to listen inward instead of outward. I think—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway, because Tam had never once been embarrassed of his feelings and she wasn't going to be embarrassed of hers. "I think he left it for us. On purpose. Because he knew the engine recorded, and he knew if anything happened to him, someday someone might come back and listen."

Pell reached across the table slowly and turned the drum over, examining the housing with fingers that trembled just slightly. "The resonance lattice," Pell murmured. "If it stores acoustically, folded harmonic storage, then a voice spoken directly into the intake would... persist. Layered under ambient capture. Recoverable, theoretically, with the right—" Pell stopped. Looked up. "You've already listened to it. Haven't you."

"Yes."

"Alone?"

"Yes."

"Why?" Juno asked, and her voice was gentle, but her eyes weren't; they were fierce and wet and fixed on Wren like Wren was the only thing keeping the ship afloat.

"Because if it was bad," Wren said, "if it was him dying, or him blaming us, or him calling for help we didn't answer—I wanted to carry that part myself first. So you wouldn't have to hear it raw." She took a breath. "It's not bad. I promise you it's not bad. But it's not mine to keep."

She looked at Bram.

He hadn't moved. He was staring at the little brass drum like it was a door he'd been walking past for twelve years without knowing it was there. When he spoke, his voice came out low and scraped. "Twelve years I thought—" He shook his head. "Twelve years I thought the last thing he ever said to any of us was whatever he said on the boat that day. And I've been carrying my half of that conversation like a stone. You're telling me he talked after."

"He talked *after*," Wren said. "For almost four minutes."

Bram closed his eyes.

Juno reached over and took Wren's hand. Pell quietly reassembled the toaster without looking at it, which was how Pell cried.

"So," Wren said, when she could trust her voice again. "Do we listen?"

* * *

They did it on deck, because Tam had hated being below decks, had always said a sailor who couldn't see the sky was just cargo with opinions. Pell rigged the listening horn to the drum and ran a line to the ship's speaking-trumpet, so the sound would come out big and shared instead of small and private. Juno stood at the rail facing the water. Bram sat down heavily on the hatch cover, elbows on knees, a man bracing for weather.

Wren turned the crank.

First there was the Shoals—all of it at once, the great folded chord of twelve years, currents and coral and the long slow breathing of the sea. It should have been overwhelming. Instead it sounded, Wren thought, like coming home to a house where every room was lit. Then Pell adjusted the filter, peeling layers away the way you'd peel an onion, and the ambient roar thinned, and thinned, and thinned—

And then, out of the heart of the sea, warm and slightly amused and unmistakably alive:

"—testing, testing. Hello? Is this thing—ha! There we go."

Wren heard Juno make a sound like she'd been struck.

Tam Clearwater sounded exactly the way Wren remembered, though she'd only been nine when he died—young, bright, the kind of voice that made you believe tomorrow was going to be interesting. He was breathing a little hard, as if he'd been swimming, or running, or both.

"Okay. Okay, so. If you're hearing this, then either the engine works the way I think it does, in which case, Pell, you magnificent paranoid genius, I owe you a drink—or I'm talking to fish, in which case, hello, fish. Very nice fish."

A pause. A laugh, breathless, real.

"Right. Being serious now. I know how this looks. I know what Bram's face is doing right now—yes, Bram, I can hear your face doing it, twelve years away and I can still hear it. Stop it. Whatever story you've built about today, take it apart. It's wrong. Here's what actually happened."

The crew had gone statue-still. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

"The engine was waking up. Not because of us—we didn't wake it, it was already stirring, it's been stirring for years, that's why the Shoals sing. I got too close to the intake and it started to pull. Not drag—pull. Like it wanted company. And I felt it start to reach past me, back toward the boat, toward *you*, and I understood—it doesn't grab one person. It grabs whoever's nearest and keeps reaching until somebody answers. So I answered. I gave it what it wanted, which was attention, which was me, and while it was busy with me it let go of the rest of you. That was the trade. That was always the trade. One voice for five."

Wren watched Bram's shoulders shake once, hard, and go still.

"So nobody jump in after anybody, ever again. That's an order. I'm still senior enough to give orders, I checked. And Bram—if you're counting miles right now, stop. I know you. You've been measuring the distance between you and that day since before we lost sight of the Shoals, and I need you to hear me: there was nothing you could have done except die next to me, and then who'd have taught Wren to swear properly? Somebody had to stay topside. Somebody had to keep the family. That was always going to be you. It was never a debt. It was a job, and you did it, and look—" the voice grinned audibly, "—look at them. Still sailing. Still arguing. Still together. You *kept* us, Bram. That's the salvage. That was always the salvage."

Juno had both hands over her mouth. Pell had given up entirely on the toaster metaphor and was crying openly, laughing at the same time, the way grief does when it finally gets permission.

"Juno—navigate well. Steer by more than stars. Pell—keep building impossible things, but maybe fewer weapons, yeah? Wren—" and here the voice softened, went young and certain, "—you'll hear this someday, and by then you'll have grown up fierce and strange and wonderful, and you'll probably be the one who finds this, because you always did hear things first. Listen to the sea, little bird. It's not singing *at* you. It's keeping everything safe. Everything that ever happened out here, it keeps. Nothing in the Shoals is ever really lost. Including me. Especially me. I'm not gone. I'm just—filed."

A laugh. A long breath.

"Four minutes of air left, by my count, and I'm not going to spend them being sad. I'm going to spend them listening. The engine's showing me things—storms from before we were born, whales arguing about politics, the whole ocean's memory—and it's beautiful, you guys. It's so beautiful. I wish—"

The recording folded gently into the great chord of the Shoals, the way a wave folds back into the sea. Not cut off. Just—returned.

Silence on the deck of the Halcyon. Real silence, the good kind, the kind that comes after a held breath is finally let out.

Bram stood up. He walked to the rail beside Juno, and Wren saw him do something she had never seen him do in all her years aboard: he leaned into it, into the grief, into the family standing around him, instead of measuring the distance to the horizon. Juno put an arm through his. Pell blew their nose catastrophically.

"Well," Bram said at last, rough and quiet. "He always did talk too much."

"Runs in the crew," Juno said, wiping her eyes.

Wren picked up the drum and held it against her chest. It weighed almost nothing. It had always weighed almost nothing. But it felt different now—heavier in the way that precious things are heavy, the way a promise is heavy.

"Pell," she said. "Can you copy it? Properly? So it can't ever be lost again—even if something happens to the drum?"

Pell nodded, already sketching in the condensation on the galley window. "Folded-wax duplicate. Give me a day."

"Make it two copies," Bram said. His voice had steadied. Something in his face had settled, like a chart finally pinned flat. "One stays with the ship. And one—" he looked east, toward Greywater Point, toward the headland above the town where the sea could just barely be heard on quiet mornings, "—one goes somewhere high. Somewhere a man can stand and listen without having to chase anything."

Juno raised an eyebrow. "Since when do you want to stand still?"

"Since about four minutes ago," Bram said. "Give or take twelve years."

They were still laughing—wet, cracked, real laughter—when Wren spotted the sail.

It came around the point under full canvas, fast and clean, cutting white water, flying colors none of them had seen in years: the deep blue and silver of Karrow lines. No collectors. No fleet. Just a single swift courier sloop, and lashed to its mast, catching the morning light, a sealed letter tube with a wax stamp Wren recognized from a chart she'd once stolen a peek at—a stamp shaped like a bell.

"It's addressed to the Halcyon," the courier called across the water, holding the tube high. "From Vess Karrow. She said to tell you—" the courier squinted at his instructions, frowning, "—she said, and I quote, 'Tell Coldharbour the Shoals aren't the only place that sings.'"

Wren looked at Bram. Bram looked at the tube. And somewhere far off, beneath the hull, beneath the keel, beneath the honest ordinary silence of the sea—the faintest hum, like a memory clearing its throat.
