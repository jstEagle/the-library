## 21. Anchorage

Juno had moored the *Halcyon* at Greywater Point four hundred and eleven times, by her count, and every one of them had been a pause—a held breath between one horizon and the next. Ropes tied, lines coiled, crew scattered to their errands, and always, always, Bram at the rail with his eyes already on the weather past the breakwater, measuring miles.

This time, on the four hundred and twelfth, he was measuring nothing at all. He stood on the dock with a coil of rope in his hands like he'd forgotten what it was for, watching gulls argue over a fish head, and when Juno came down the gangplank with the harbor manifest under her arm, he didn't ask how long they were staying.

That was how she knew it was real.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm deciding," he said, "whether this dock has always been this crooked or whether I've just never looked at it before."

"It's been crooked since the flood year. Old Marta's husband built it drunk on victory ale and never got around to fixing it."

"Huh." Bram looked at the dock the way other men looked at cathedrals. Then he looked at her, and there was something in his face that made her chest ache in an unfamiliar way—not grief, not dread, none of the old familiar aches, but something newer and worse. Hope, maybe. She'd forgotten what that felt like from the inside. "You're really going to take the post?"

"The harbormaster's office is mine as of tomorrow morning," Juno said. "Assuming I survive the interview with the town council, which consists of three people who have known me since I was stealing apples off their trees, so honestly the interview is a formality wrapped around a scolding."

Bram smiled. It still startled her a little, how easily he did it now. Like a door left unlocked in a house that used to be bolted shut.

* * *

The town council turned out to be exactly what she'd predicted. Aldous Fenwick, who owned the chandlery and half the gossip; Mother Quill, who ran the lighthouse and considered herself the town's conscience on the grounds that she could see everyone's business from up there; and young Tammas Ode, third-generation fisherman, who mostly nodded along because the other two terrified him.

"So," said Aldous, folding his hands atop the table like a man settling in for a long siege. "You want to be harbormaster."

"I want to *be* harbormaster," Juno confirmed. "I don't just want it. I'm qualified. I've navigated the Siren Shoals, which no living harbormaster on this coast can say—"

"Which is precisely my concern," Aldous said. "No offense meant, Juno, but you come with a reputation. Storm chaser. Debt-runner. There's talk."

"There's always talk. Talk is free and worth what it costs." She set the manifest on the table. "Here's what I know about this harbor: where every sandbar has migrated in the last twelve years, which wrecks are hazards and which are just ugly, and how to read a squall line two hours before your fishermen can smell it. Here's what I'm offering: charts updated quarterly, posted freely. A signal system for storm warnings that doesn't rely on Mother Quill ringing the bell until her arms give out."

"I never minded the bell," Mother Quill said stiffly.

"Your arms did. I've watched you wince for a decade."

The old woman's mouth twitched, betrayed.

"And here's the part nobody else can offer," Juno went on, leaning forward. "I'm not passing through. I've spent twenty years treating this harbor as a place to resupply and run from. I'm done running. You'd be hiring someone whose whole heart is in the job, because for once, the job *is* the heart."

Silence. Tammas Ode nodded vigorously, caught himself, stopped nodding.

Aldous studied her for a long moment. "The pay's terrible."

"I've had worse offers with better pay. The worse offers came with syndicate collectors attached."

"Fair enough." He stuck out his hand. "Welcome to the worst-paying, best job on the coast, Harbormaster Voss."

Walking out, Mother Quill fell into step beside her. For a while neither spoke. Then the old woman said, quietly, "It's good, what you're doing. Staying. This town loses too many people to the sea and calls it natural."

"We lost one to the sea ourselves," Juno said. "Twelve years ago. We called it natural for a long time too. It wasn't."

Mother Quill patted her arm once, briskly, and climbed back toward her lighthouse, and Juno stood in the lane a moment, blinking at nothing, before going to find Pell.

* * *

Pell had found the workshop before Juno found Pell, which figured. The old cooperage on Anchor Lane had been empty since its owner retired inland, and it had everything: high ceilings for rigging work, a forge that needed only coaxing, and a wide door wide enough to admit a ship's boat. When Juno pushed it open, the sound of hammering rang through the rafters, and Pell emerged from behind a stack of timber with sawdust in her eyebrows and an expression of pure, unguarded joy that Juno hadn't seen since—well. Since before the Shoals. Maybe since before Tam.

"Look at this place," Pell said, spreading her arms. "Look at it! South light for fine work, a forge that just needs a new grate, and floor space enough to build a dinghy without walking around it sideways. I put coin down this morning. First property I've ever owned that isn't a tool."

"What are you calling it?"

"Pell & Daughter."

Juno blinked. "Wren's working for you?"

"If she wants. Between school terms." Pell picked up a plane, set it down again, picked it up once more—the fidget of a woman with too much happiness to hold still. "She asked me last night. Properly asked, like signing on to a crew. Said she wanted to learn how things are made, not just how they're sailed. Said—" Pell paused, and her voice went careful, the way it did around anything that mattered. "Said she wanted to build something that stays."

"That's a good reason."

"It's the best reason." Pell ran her thumb along the plane's blade. "You know, I spent twelve years keeping us afloat and telling myself that was enough. Keeping the engine turning, keeping the hull tight. But afloat isn't the same as going somewhere. I think I forgot that. Or maybe I never learned it."

"You learned it," Juno said. "You just needed somewhere to practice."

Pell laughed, short and wet, and threw a scrap of wood at her head, which Juno dodged with the ease of long friendship.

* * *

The navigator's school occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, and its headmistress was a narrow, iron-haired woman named Mistress Corvane who had taught navigation to three generations of Greywater Point's children and remembered all of their failures individually.

"She interviewed me," Wren reported that evening, perched on the *Halcyon*'s rail with her knees drawn up. "Not the other way around. She asked why I wanted to study navigation when I clearly already knew how to navigate."

"What did you say?"

"I said knowing how and knowing why aren't the same thing." Wren turned a shell over in her fingers—one of the small spiral ones from the Shoals beach, though she'd stopped flinching at them weeks ago. "She liked that. She said I start at the bottom anyway, because pride sinks more ships than rocks do."

"Mistress Corvane always did have a way with words," Juno said.

"Bram says she failed him twice."

"Bram earned it both times. He treated navigation like a puzzle to beat instead of a language to learn." Juno smiled at the memory. "He's better now. Slower. He actually reads the water instead of racing it."

Wren was quiet a moment. Out over the harbor, the evening light lay gold on the water, and fishing boats were coming home in ones and twos, their lamps beginning to glow.

"Do you think we'll actually stay?" she asked. Not anxious—just asking, the way she asked everything now, having learned that questions were allowed. "Really stay. Not just until the next thing pulls him out."

Juno followed her gaze to the dock, where Bram was talking with the fishmonger's boy, gesturing at the crooked planking, apparently explaining—at length, from the boy's expression—everything wrong with it and how to fix it.

"He'll get restless," Juno said honestly. "Restless is in his bones; we don't cut that out, we just give it somewhere to live. But restless and leaving aren't the same animal anymore. He found out on the Shoals that running was never taking him anywhere. Now he knows what it costs to stop. He paid for that knowledge." She reached over and tucked a windblown strand of hair behind Wren's ear. "We all did."

Wren leaned into the touch briefly, then hopped down from the rail. "I told Corvane about the song," she said, at the gangplank. "Not all of it. Just—that the sea sings, if you listen right. She said she'd been a navigator forty years and never heard it, and I said that was because she was listening for directions." Wren grinned, quick and bright. "She wrote it down."

Of course she did, Juno thought. That's what teachers do. They catch the strange things children say and hand them to the future.

* * *

The letter came eleven days after they moored, which was fast even for Vess Karrow, and it arrived the way Vess did everything: with style and a faint suggestion of menace. A courier sloop out of the eastern trade lanes brought it, sealed in grey wax stamped with a compass rose, addressed in a hand so elegant it was practically taunting.

Juno took it from the courier at the harbor office—her office, she was still getting used to the phrase—and carried it up the hill to Bram without opening it, because some things weren't hers to open anymore. He was on the headland path above town, doing his dawn walk, which had become a ritual in the eleven days since they'd arrived: up the hill before sunrise, down before the town fully woke, as if he were practicing belonging in private before performing it in public.

He read the letter standing in the wind with the sea below them, and his face did several complicated things.

"Well?" Juno asked.

He handed it over. One line, in that beautiful hand:

*Next time, Coldharbour.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in different ink:

*Tell the girl she listens well.*

Juno read it twice. "That's either a threat or a compliment."

"With Vess it's always both." Bram took the letter back, looked at it a moment longer, and then—this was the part that made Juno's chest do the unfamiliar aching thing again—he folded it away into his coat pocket instead of tearing it up or feeding it to the wind. Kept it. Filed it under next time, whatever that meant, whenever that came.

"Are you worried?" she asked.

He considered it honestly, which was another new habit of his. "A little. She's out there with nothing and a grudge, and nothing plus a grudge is the most dangerous cargo afloat." He turned to look at the harbor—the *Halcyon* at her berth, the fishing fleet, the crooked dock, the smoke rising from Pell's chimney on Anchor Lane, the customs house where Wren was presumably being sternly educated at this very moment. "But she's across the sea, and we're here. And I'm done letting the next thing own the now. If she comes, she comes. We'll be here to meet her."

"*We*," Juno repeated.

Bram had the grace to look slightly embarrassed. "If that's—"

"It is," Juno said. "It absolutely is. But you're saying it out loud at least once a week until it stops sounding surprised."

"Deal."

They walked down together, unhurried, and the wind came up the hill smelling of salt and woodsmoke and the particular green smell of the headland grass in autumn, and below them Greywater Point went about its morning: nets mended, ovens lit, children chased off the breakwater by a harbormaster who was still learning to shout with authority and mostly just sounded delighted.

Roots, Juno thought. Not dropped by accident, not grown wild from whatever soil you happened to land in—but put down deliberately, one at a time, the way you'd drive pilings for a pier you intended to stand on for the rest of your life.

It was, she decided, the finest piece of navigation any of them had ever done.

And if, far across the water, a woman with a compass rose seal was already drawing new charts—well. The harbor would be ready.
