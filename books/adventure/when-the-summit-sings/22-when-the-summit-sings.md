## 22. When the Summit Sings

Bram Coldharbour had spent thirty years learning to read weather, and the sky over Greywater Point on the morning he finally climbed the headland was the kind of sky he'd have chased in his younger days—clean and high, with a long gold seam along the east where the sun hadn't quite committed yet. The kind of morning that promised nothing dramatic. The kind of morning, he was only now learning to notice, that promised everything else.

The path up was worse than he remembered. Of course, he'd never actually taken it. He'd looked at it from the deck of a dozen ships, from the tavern window, from his bunk at odd hours when sleep wouldn't hold. *That's the summit there,* he'd say to whoever was new aboard, pointing with his chin the way old sailors do, as if pointing were the same as going. *Good vantage. Never had cause.*

Never had cause. That was the lie he'd told himself for three decades, and like most lies a man tells himself, it had held up beautifully right up until it collapsed under its own weight.

He climbed anyway.

His knees registered formal complaints by the first switchback. His breath went ragged on the second. By the third he'd stopped pretending this was anything but an argument between his body and his stubbornness, and for once—for the first time in longer than he could calculate—his stubbornness wasn't running from anything. It was just walking uphill because the hill was there and the morning was fine and a man owed himself one honest view before the light changed.

* * *

He reached the top as the sun cleared the water, and Greywater Point unrolled below him like something out of Juno's charts—which, he supposed, it literally was. He could see the harbor, small and brown-green, the breakwater with its paint peeling in exactly the pattern Pell had been complaining about since before Wren was born. He could see the Halcyon at her mooring, her kite-rig furled, looking smaller from up here than she ever had at sea. Ships always did that. They shrank when you got far enough away from them, which was probably why sailors kept leaving port, just to remember how big they were.

And he could see the town itself. Not passing through it. In it.

Juno's flag was already up over the harbormaster's office—the new one, blue with a white compass rose that Wren had painted slightly crooked because, as Wren put it, "a perfectly centered compass is a compass that's never been anywhere." Juno had pretended to be annoyed about it for two days and then quietly had it varnished so it would never fade.

Pell's workshop sat where the old chandlery used to be, smoke curling from its chimney even at this hour, because Pell had never in his life met a morning he trusted enough to sleep through. Bram could just make out the shape of him in the yard, bent over something, probably talking to it. Pell talked to machinery the way other men talked to horses—with respect, suspicion, and a running commentary neither party admitted to enjoying.

And there, on the seawall, a small figure with a satchel, waiting for the ferry that ran to the navigator's school on the far side of the bay. Wren. Even at this distance Bram knew the way she stood—weight on her heels, face into the wind, listening. She still listened more than she spoke, though lately what she said, when she said it, landed like a keel: steady, deep, holding the rest of them true.

Twelve years ago he'd lost a man in the Siren Shoals and spent every day since running from the sound of it. Six tides ago he'd sailed back in, scuttled a song that wanted to own the world, and paid a debt with truth instead of gold. And now here he stood on a hill he'd pointed at for thirty years, watching the people who'd dragged him through all of it go about their mornings below.

It occurred to him, standing there with his heart still thudding from the climb, that he had no idea what a man did with a life that wasn't being run from.

He found a flat rock, sat down on it like it had been expecting him, and decided to find out.

* * *

The hours passed the way hours pass when you stop trying to spend them.

Midmorning, he watched the ferry take Wren across the bay, and he watched the little boat heel in a gust, and he caught himself leaning forward on the rock with both hands gripping stone, as if he could steady her from two miles off. She didn't need steadying. She'd proven that in the Shoals, diving alone against orders, coming back changed, coming back carrying Tam's last message inside a melody no one had taught her. If anyone on that water knew what they were doing, it was the girl who'd learned to hear a dead man's voice in the hum of a drowned engine.

But he leaned forward anyway. That, he was discovering, was what family was—not the absence of fear, but the willingness to keep feeling it, daily, on behalf of people who didn't require your assistance.

Around noon, Pell emerged from the workshop, spotted the figure on the headland through his spyglass, and—Bram could see it plainly, even at this distance—stood in his yard for a full minute, hand shading his eyes, before raising one arm in a slow, deliberate wave. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment. *I see you up there. I see you stayed.* Bram raised his arm back and felt something in his chest come loose that he hadn't known was bolted down.

Early afternoon, Juno came out of the harbormaster's office, walked to the end of the breakwater, and stood looking up at the summit for a long time. Then she turned, went back inside, and reappeared a few minutes later climbing the path herself, moving with the efficient irritation of a woman who navigated storms for a living and considered hills a personal affront.

She arrived at the top breathing hard, dropped onto the rock beside him without asking, and handed him a flask.

"You climbed my summit," she said.

"Your summit?"

"I've been charting approaches to it for six years. It's mine now. Harbormaster's prerogative." She squinted out at the water. "You look terrible."

"I'm sixty-one years old and I just climbed four hundred feet of loose shale."

"And yet somehow you look worse than that accounts for." She took the flask back, drank, passed it again. For a while neither of them said anything, because they were the kind of friends who'd learned that silence shared was worth more than talk rationed.

Finally she said, quietly, "You're not leaving."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it like one. "No."

"The debt's settled. Vess is a rumor on the far side of the sea. You could take the Halcyon anywhere. I know you've thought about it. You've got the look."

"I've always got the look, Juno. That's the problem. The look was the whole architecture." He turned the flask in his hands. "You know what I figured out up here? I spent thirty years measuring distance. Miles to the next storm. Miles to the next port. Miles between me and the Shoals, miles between me and Tam. And I got very good at it. World-class. And the whole time—" he shook his head— "the whole time I thought I was chasing weather. I was just counting the space around a hole."

Juno watched the water. "And now?"

"Now I'm trying to count years instead." He laughed, short and rough. "Turns out I'm bad at it. I don't know how many I've got left. There's no chart for it. No bearing. It's the first navigation I've ever done without instruments and I'm terrified of it, and I'm doing it anyway."

She bumped her shoulder against his, gently, the way you'd nudge a ship off a sandbar. "You'll manage. You managed the singing gale."

"The singing gale had a channel."

"So does this. You just can't see it from the helm." She stood, brushed off her coat, and looked down at him with the expression she reserved for charts she intended to correct. "Supper's at seven. Pell's cooking, which means bring your own seasoning and low expectations. Wren gets back on the evening ferry. She's got something she wants to show you—she won't say what, but she's been humming it all week, and I'd bet the Halcyon herself it's new."

Bram looked up at her. "New?"

"There are more songs in the world than the ones we sank, Bram. Some of them haven't been written yet." She started down the path, then paused. "Oh—and the Optimists sent a letter. It's on your bunk. Before you make that face: it's not a bill."

"What is it?"

"No idea. But it came sealed in green wax, and Pell says nobody uses green wax except people who want something. Supper at seven."

She descended, sure-footed, cursing the shale affectionately, and Bram sat back down on his rock and let the afternoon happen to him.

* * *

The light went long and honey-colored, the way it does in the last hour before evening, when the sun stops showing off and starts telling the truth. The harbor filled and emptied. Fishing boats came home with their catches and their complaints. Somewhere below, Pell's hammer rang against something in a rhythm that was either work or music, and knowing Pell, both.

And the sea—

Bram heard it before he understood he was hearing it. A faint sound rising off the water, threading up the headland, settling over the summit like a hand laid lightly on the shoulder. Twelve years ago, a sound like that would have sent him running for open water. Six months ago, it would have stopped his heart.

He listened.

There was nothing in it. No melody. No pull. No hunger wearing the mask of beauty. Just the sea, doing what the sea has always done—breathing against the shore, saying nothing, meaning everything. The Siren Shoals were quiet, truly quiet, for the first time since the Bell went down, and what remained was only water. Water and wind and the small human noises of a town getting ready for night.

He'd spent half his life afraid of a song. It turned out the silence after it was not emptiness. It was room.

Bram Coldharbour sat on his summit until the sun touched the water, and he did not count the miles to anything. He counted, instead—and slowly, clumsily, like a man learning a language late in life—the years. The ones behind him, with their weight and their wreckage and their rare, stubborn grace. The ones ahead, unwritten, unhurried, holding supper at seven and a letter in green wax and whatever melody Wren had been carrying around all week, humming, keeping safe, waiting to give.

The last light slid off the water. Below, lamps began to bloom in the windows of Greywater Point, one by one, like a harbor answering a signal.

Bram stood, stretched knees that had earned their complaints, and started down the path toward the lights—toward the town he'd moored in, the crew he'd chosen, the years he meant, at last, to keep.

Behind him, the sea breathed easy, holding no song at all.

Ahead, faintly, from the direction of Pell's workshop, someone was already humming something new.
