## 1. Nineteen Days of Good Weather

The storm was still forty miles offshore when Oscar Delacroix first smelled it in the market — a green, metallic tang riding under the salt and the diesel and the char of the fish stalls, the way the air went sweet-sour before a system made up its mind. Most people at the Saltrow Market wouldn't have noticed. They were too busy haggling over mackerel and arguing about the price of rope. But Oscar had spent eleven years reading weather the way priests read scripture, and he knew what that smell meant: something big was building out past the shelf, turning slowly, deciding whether to be a nuisance or a sermon.

He bought two mangoes from a woman with hennaed hands and didn't haggle, because haggling cost time and time was the only currency he had less of than money.

"Nineteen days," he said to her, mostly to himself.

"What?"

"Good weather. Nineteen days of it, maybe." He tucked the mangoes into his jacket. "Then it won't matter."

She looked at him the way market women look at men who talk to themselves — with pity and a quick calculation of how far away he was standing — and turned to her next customer.

Oscar moved on through the market with the loose, unhurried gait he'd perfected over years of being followed. That was the trick of it. A man who runs looks like a man who's guilty. A man who strolls looks like a man who belongs wherever he happens to be. So Oscar strolled, past the fish stalls with their silver piles of catch gleaming like spilled coins, past the tarpaulin awnings snapping in the strengthening wind, past the boy selling batteries and the old man selling nothing at all, just sitting on an upturned crate watching the harbor with eyes gone the color of fog.

The wind was coming around to the northeast. That was wrong for this time of year. Wrong was interesting. Wrong was also dangerous, and Oscar filed both facts away in the part of his mind where he kept everything that might later save his life.

He was counting awnings when he saw the reflection.

That was how you spotted Vantor men — not by looking at them but by looking at what they disturbed. The crowd in front of the fruit stall bent slightly, the way grass bends before a walker nobody's seen yet. And there, in the polished chrome trim of a parked delivery scooter, a shape: broad shoulders, a pale suit, a smile you could see even in reflection because Vantor men never stopped smiling. It was policy. It was practically doctrine.

Dario Vantor. The youngest of them. The one they sent when they wanted to seem friendly.

Oscar adjusted his course without appearing to adjust anything at all, drifting left toward the alley between the fishmonger and the chandlery, the one that cut through to the harbor road. He ate a mango as he walked, because a man eating fruit is a man with nowhere urgent to be, and spat the stringy fibers into the gutter with the casualness of long practice.

Behind him, unhurried, the crowd bent again.

*Never stop,* his brother Eli used to say, back when Eli was alive and Oscar was someone else entirely. *Never stop, and never owe twice. You can owe a man money or you can owe him a favor, but if you owe him both, you're not a debtor anymore. You're furniture.*

Eli had been dead six years, and his rules were the only inheritance Oscar had kept.

The alley smelled of brine and cat and the ghost of ten thousand fish. Oscar walked it at market-stroll speed while his heart ran at sprint speed, and the gap between the two was where he lived now, most days. He came out onto the harbor road with the wind full in his face, strong enough now to shove, and looked out at the water.

The horizon had changed since morning. It had thickened. There was a bruise-colored band sitting on the edge of the world where there had been clean blue at breakfast, and above it the clouds were doing that thing he loved and hated equally — stacking themselves into towers, white and terrible, like architecture built by something with more ambition than mercy. A monsoon system, maybe. Maybe just a squall line passing through. The forecasters on the radio couldn't agree, which meant it was real weather, the kind that hadn't been decided yet, the kind that could go anywhere and do anything.

Oscar loved weather like that the way some men loved horses they couldn't afford to keep.

"Delacroix."

The voice came from behind and to the left, warm as bathwater. Oscar didn't turn around. He finished chewing, swallowed, wiped his hands on his trousers, and then turned around like a man who had all the time ever minted.

Dario Vantor stood perhaps fifteen feet away, hands in the pockets of a suit that probably cost more than the Kestrel's engine, smiling his family's smile. He was younger than Oscar by five years and better fed by a lifetime. Two of Dario's cousins lounged near the scooter, pretending to admire the harbor, which was funny, because everyone in the Vantor family pretended constantly and none of them were good at it.

"Dario," Oscar said. "You're a long way from the restaurant."

"We opened a new one. Harbor district. You should come. The snapper is—" Dario kissed his fingertips, an absurd little gesture, "—a religious experience."

"I'll put it on my calendar."

"You should. You should put a lot of things on your calendar." Dario drifted closer, and up close the smile had its machinery showing, the way smiles do when they've been rehearsed in mirrors. "My mother asks after you. She says, *that Oscar, he works so hard, he never rests.* She worries. You know how she worries."

"She's a saint."

"She is. She's a saint, and she counts, Oscar. She counts everything. Receipts, seasons, promises." Dario's eyes went briefly, almost tenderly, to the sky. "Nineteen days. That's what she counted for you. Nineteen days of good weather, she said. After that, we'll need to talk about arrangements. Isn't that a nice word? *Arrangements.* Very civilized."

The wind shoved again, harder, and somewhere down the harbor a halyard began slapping a mast with a sound like slow applause.

"Dario." Oscar stepped closer, close enough that the smile flickered — not faded, Vantor smiles never faded, but recalibrated. "Tell your mother I said thank you. Tell her I don't waste days."

"Oh, I know you don't." Dario spread his hands, magnanimous, delighted. "That's what she likes about you. You're a runner. Runners always pay eventually — either with money or with something worth more than money. Either way, the family eats." He clapped Oscar on the shoulder, twice, the way relatives do at weddings and funerals. "Enjoy your mangoes."

Then he turned and strolled back toward his cousins, whistling something cheerful and unplaceable, and the crowd closed behind him like water behind a boat.

Oscar stood on the harbor road with the wind tearing at his collar and let himself feel, for exactly three seconds, the thing he never let himself feel in front of Vantors: the cold arithmetic of it. Fourteen thousand owed, compounding monthly, held by people who genuinely, sincerely, with warmth and laughter and home-cooked meals, would ruin him. That was the horror of the Vantors. Cruel men you could hate. The Vantors you could only survive.

Three seconds. Then he folded the feeling away, flat and small, into the same drawer where he kept his brother's name, and started walking.

* *

The Kestrel sat at berth nine looking, as always, like a boat that had lost an argument with the sea and was pretending otherwise. Forty-one feet of salvage trawler, hull patched in seven places with eight different materials, deck scarred, wheelhouse windows fogged from the inside no matter the season. She leaked. Everyone knew she leaked. Tomas called it *breathing* and threatened violence against anyone who called it anything else.

Tomas was under her right now, actually inside the engine compartment to his waist, and his voice floated up through the deck plates accompanied by the percussion of a wrench applied hopefully to something stubborn.

"If you're standing there judging my engine," the voice said, "I can hear you thinking."

"My thoughts are silent and respectful."

"Your thoughts are loud enough to strip threads." Tomas emerged, gray-haired, oil-blackened to the elbows, holding a gasket that had clearly died in service. "We have a situation."

"We always have a situation. It's why you're paid."

"I'm not paid. That's the situation." He slapped the dead gasket against the hull. "Cooling pump seal. I can nurse it, but nursing costs parts, and parts cost money, and our account at Chandlery Row has entered what the chandler calls a *creative phase.*"

Oscar looked at the gasket. He looked at the sky, where the towers of cloud had grown another thousand feet in the last hour and were beginning to lean, the whole formation tilting northeast like a crowd craning toward something. Nineteen days. Sixteen of them usable, maybe, if the system offshore stayed polite. Less if it didn't.

"I'll get money," he said.

"You said that Tuesday."

"It's still true on Thursday. That's the remarkable thing about it."

Tomas studied him with the flat, patient look of a man who had been lied to by professionals and could taste the difference. Whatever he saw, he decided not to charge for it. He dropped back into the engine compartment, and the wrenching resumed, and Oscar climbed the ladder to the wheelhouse to find Priya already there, because Priya was always already everywhere, seated at the chart table with three sources of weather data open and a fourth contradicted.

"Northeast turn," she said, without looking up. "You felt it at the market."

"You felt it here?"

"I felt it at four this morning. You felt it when it hit you in the face." She tapped the chart. "System forming off the shelf edge. Models disagree on track. The European model takes it south of us. The American model takes it directly through the harbor in nine days. The local fishermen's model, which is a knot tied in a length of seaweed, agrees with the Americans, which frightens me more than the Americans do."

"Nine days."

"Nine days of warning, if it comes. Which leaves us ten days of work window before the season closes down anyway." She finally looked up. Her eyes were the color of the water over sandbar — light, clear, and impossible to see the bottom of. "So whatever job you're going to take to pay the Vantors, you need to take it in the next seventy-two hours, or the weather takes the decision away from you."

"I haven't decided to take any job."

"No," Priya agreed mildly. "You've decided to stand in the wheelhouse staring at the horizon like it owes you an apology. Very different thing."

Oscar opened his mouth to answer, and that was when Bo came up the dock at a run, which Bo never did, because Bo moved through the world at the speed of honey. He hit the gangway hard enough to rock it, took the ladder in two grabs, and burst into the wheelhouse with his cap half off and his face lit up like a kid at a firework stand.

"There's a woman at the dock office," he said. "Asking for passage to Sable Shoals."

Everything in Oscar went very quiet, the way the air goes quiet in the middle of a storm, in the eye, when the sea flattens and the birds vanish and every instinct you own starts screaming that the walls are still moving.

"Sable Shoals," Oscar repeated.

"Yeah! Cash up front, she says. Real cash, not transfer, not script. Cash cash." Bo grinned. "Isn't that great? We could get the pump seal. Maybe two seals. Maybe—"

"Bo." Oscar's voice came out level, which cost him something. "Nobody runs to Sable Shoals. Not this season. Not any season. Who is she?"

"Name's Voss. Ilsa Voss." Bo shifted his weight, and his grin dimmed by degrees, the way it did when he sensed a room cooling. "She asked for you specifically, Oscar. Said she heard you were the best salvage captain on the coast." He paused. "Said she heard you'd been out there before. Six years ago."

The wind chose that moment to come around the headland and hit the Kestrel broadside, rocking her against the fenders, and out past the breakwater the cloud towers leaned a little further northeast, and Oscar Delacroix stood in his leaking wheelhouse and counted — nineteen days, fourteen thousand owed, one name he hadn't spoken aloud in six years — the way a man counts exits from a room that hasn't caught fire yet.

"Show her the harbor bench," he said finally. "Tell her I'll be along."

"And will you?" Priya asked quietly.

Oscar looked at the chart, at the small inked cross six years old that marked where the sea had taken his brother, and found that he already knew the answer, and hated it, and picked it up anyway.

"Yes," he said. "Get me the file on the Shoals concession. All of it."

* * *

He read the file until the light failed, sitting alone in the wheelhouse while the first outer bands of the system came ashore as fat, warm rain that ticked against the windows like fingers. Somewhere in those pages was a buyer's name, a permit window, a fee schedule — and somewhere underneath all of it, printed in the header of a document dated six years back, a name he'd have known blindfolded, in the dark, underwater.

*E. Delacroix.*

Oscar closed the file. Out past the breakwater, the lightning started, silent and distant, illuminating the cloud towers from within like lanterns in a cathedral built by something that had never once answered a prayer.

Nineteen days, he thought. Plenty of time.

He almost believed it.
