## 2. The Vantors Smile

The Vantor house sat on the last high ground before the harbor gave up pretending to be land, a long white building with green shutters and a veranda that wrapped around it like an arm offering to hold you. Oscar had been inside twice before. Both times he'd left owing more than when he arrived.

He stood at the gate for a moment in the failing light, working the salt off his hands with the hem of his shirt. The monsoon was still three days out by his read of the sky—a bruised band along the southern horizon, patient as a creditor—but the air already had that thick, expectant weight, the whole coast holding its breath. Somewhere behind him, down in the market streets, lamps were coming on one by one, and somewhere ahead of him, through those green shutters, lamplight was already warm and generous.

That was the thing about the Vantors. They were never dark. You could always see exactly where you were going.

He let himself in. The gate didn't latch; it never did. A house that locked its doors, Papa Vantor liked to say, was a house ashamed of what it kept inside.

* * *

They were all there, arranged around the long table on the veranda as though dinner were a portrait being painted and they'd held the pose for years. Papa Vantor—Aurel, though nobody called him that but his wife—sat at the head with a glass of something amber and a face like a holiday postcard: tanned, creased into permanent good humor, eyes bright as new coins. Mama Vess was beside him, small and silver-haired, shelling peas into a bowl with quick economical fingers, as if even hospitality were a form of bookkeeping. And scattered down the table, their children—the ones who mattered, anyway. Dario, the eldest, broad and easy, carving bread. Little Sefa, seventeen and sharp-eyed, pouring wine with the concentration of someone defusing something. And at the far end, half in shadow, quiet Corin, who did the family's arithmetic and almost never smiled, which in that house made him the most frightening person in the room.

"Oscar." Aurel Vantor's voice carried across the veranda the way warmth carries across cold water. "There he is. There's our storm man. Vess, look—he came. I told you he'd come."

"I never doubted it," Mama Vess said, not looking up from her peas. "Sit down, Oscar, before the fish goes cold. Dario caught it this morning. Out past the point, alone, in that little boat of his. He thinks he's brave."

"I am brave," Dario said comfortably, and pulled out a chair for Oscar two seats down from the head. Close enough to be family. Close enough to be watched.

Oscar sat. His chair scraped, and the sound seemed very loud against the soft percussion of the sea below the veranda wall.

"You look thin," Vess said, finally raising her eyes to him. They were pale gray, almost colorless, and kind in the way that deep water is calm. "Are you eating?"

"Every day," Oscar said.

"But well?" She tipped her head. "There's a difference. A man can eat every day and still be starving. I see it in sailors all the time. They eat standing up, half of it wind." She pushed the bowl of peas toward him, an offering, a small kindness with no visible hook in it, which was somehow worse than if there had been one.

The food came in waves after that—fish with green sauce, rice cooked with coconut, bread, a salad of things Oscar couldn't name that tasted of lime and heat. The Vantors ate the way they did everything: generously, loudly, with real pleasure. Aurel told a story about a young debt collector he'd once employed, back before the family moved into salvage financing, a boy who'd chased a fisherman halfway up a mountain over eleven coins and come back with nothing but a sprained ankle and the fisherman's gratitude, because the fisherman had fed him lunch at the top. Everyone laughed. Oscar laughed too, because not laughing was a data point, and the Vantors collected data points the way other families collected photographs.

"And do you know what my father said to me?" Aurel said, leaning toward Oscar with his glass, delighted with his own history. "He said, *Aurel, you learned more at that lunch than you would have with the money.* Because the boy understood something. That the fisherman wasn't running from the debt. He was running from the *conversation* about the debt. People will pay anything to avoid a conversation." He beamed. "Isn't that true? Isn't that the truest thing you ever heard?"

"It's true," Oscar said.

"So we're having the conversation." Aurel spread his hands, and the table went quiet around him—not silent, because the sea was still there under everything, but the human noise dropped away like a tide going out. "And look how painless. Look how nice the fish is."

* * *

Corin did the arithmetic.

He did it without malice, without theater, sliding a leather folio from beneath his napkin and opening it flat the way a surgeon opens a case. He was maybe twenty-six, narrow, with his mother's colorless eyes and none of her warmth, and when he spoke his voice was so mild it took a moment to register that the numbers he was reading aloud were a sentence.

"Principal, four thousand two hundred. Interest accrued since the spring restructuring, six hundred eighty. Salvage equipment lien, honored, no additional charge—we appreciate that, Oscar, that you kept the winch insured. It shows respect for collateral." He turned a page. "Outstanding against the Kestrel's hull valuation, given current condition—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel finding the edge of something, "—the position is not comfortable. But it is workable. That's why we're having dinner instead of doing something else."

"What else would you do?" Sefa asked, sweetly, from the wine bottle. She wasn't looking at Oscar. She was looking at her glass, smiling at it.

"Sefa," Vess said mildly, and the girl subsided, and everyone understood that the question had been asked anyway.

"The position is workable," Corin repeated, "because of the season. Monsoon salvage pays triple rates. Wrecks are frequent, insurance is nervous, buyers are desperate. A man with your boat and your crew should clear the principal in a single strong run. Two runs to be safe. Three if the weather is generous." He looked up, and for the first time all evening he smiled, a small precise expression, like a signature. "So. Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days," Oscar said.

"Permits expire in nineteen days," Aurel said, spreading jam of some kind on bread he didn't seem to be eating. "After that, the concession windows close until next season, and next season, Oscar, is a long way away, and interest is not. We're not monsters. We're giving you the whole window. Every day of good weather the sky gives you, we give you too." He raised his glass to the horizon, where the first bruise of the system was just visible above the waterline, purple-black, patient. "Even the bad days. Consider them yours as well. Free of charge."

"That's generous," Oscar said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"It is generous," Aurel agreed pleasantly. "Vess, tell him about the Rennick brothers."

Mama Vess set down her pea pod. She folded her hands. When she spoke, her voice was gentle, almost apologetic, the voice of a woman describing a recipe.

"The Rennicks owed us less than you do, dear. Half, nearly. Good boys, hard workers, but unlucky—engine trouble, then a bad partner, then pride. We waited eighteen months. We ate dinner with them four times, just like this. Lovely family. Their mother made a custard I still think about." She sighed, genuinely, and the genuineness was the worst part. "In the end we took their boat, and then their mooring rights, and then their father's shop, and the boys went north to work the freight lines. And here is the thing, Oscar, and please believe I say it with love: they were *happier*. Truly. No more weight. Dario saw one in Port Callas last year, hauling cargo, laughing in a bar. He said the Rennick boy looked ten years younger than the last time we'd seen him, when he was sitting where you're sitting, eating our fish."

"Debt is heavy," Dario said sagely. "I carry crates all day. I know about heavy."

"We don't want your boat, Oscar," Aurel said, and now his voice changed—dropped its performance, or rather revealed that the performance was the sincerity, both running together like double rigging. "We like you. You're careful, you're honest in your fashion, you don't drink what you owe and you don't gamble what you've salvaged. You're the best risk on this coast, which is why we let the position run as long as we have. But nineteen days is nineteen days. After that, Corin stops being family and starts being business, and Corin in business mode"—he glanced down the table with paternal fondness—"well. He's very tidy."

"Tidy," Corin confirmed, closing the folio.

"So." Aurel lifted his glass again, and the whole table lifted theirs, Sefa and Dario and Vess and even Corin, a synchronized movement, rehearsed or instinctive, impossible to tell. "Nineteen days. To the season. May it be a monster."

"To the season," the table echoed.

Oscar lifted his glass. The wine was good—of course it was good—and it tasted like nothing at all.

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and Sefa had gone inside and Dario had gone to check on something at the dock, Oscar found himself alone on the veranda's end with Corin, who was smoking a thin cigarette and watching the water with the unblinking attention other people reserved for fire.

"You flinched," Corin said, without turning. "When Papa said nineteen days. Just now, at the toast. Small flinch. Left eye."

"I didn't flinch."

"You did. It's fine. Flinches are information, and information isn't an insult." He tapped ash into the sea wind. "You have a plan. I can always tell. Men without plans eat like it's the last meal. You ate like a man keeping his strength up."

Oscar said nothing. Below them, the swell was already changing character—longer intervals, deeper troughs, the ocean clearing its throat.

"For what it's worth," Corin said, "I hope your plan is good. I like it better when it works out for people. The collections are the part of the family business I'd give away, if anyone asked me, which they don't." He flicked the cigarette into the dark. "But I'll tell you something for free, storm man, since you counted my numbers all night like I wouldn't notice. Whatever run you're thinking of—whatever job fell into your lap this week that smells like salvation and pays triple—I'd look at who's offering it. Twice. Because jobs that arrive exactly when a man needs them aren't luck. They're bait. Somebody knows your position. Somebody always knows your position. That's not cynicism." He finally turned those colorless eyes on Oscar, and smiled his small signed smile. "It's arithmetic."

* * *

The walk back down to the harbor took twenty minutes, and Oscar spent all of them replaying the dinner like a man checking a lock he'd already checked. The warmth was real—that was the trap of the Vantors, the thing nobody believed until they'd felt it. Aurel genuinely liked him. Vess genuinely worried about whether he was eating. If they'd been cruel, he could have hated them, and hatred was fuel, hatred kept a man moving. But they weren't cruel. They were *kind*, precisely, surgically kind, and their kindness left marks the same as knives, only cleaner.

Nineteen days.

At the bottom of the hill, the harbor lights wobbled on black water, and the Kestrel sat at her mooring riding the new swell, and beyond the breakwater the southern sky had organized itself overnight into something with structure—bands, a lean, intent. The system was coming early. He could feel it in his teeth, in the pressure behind his eyes, the old animal knowledge that had made him rich twice and wrecked him once.

He was halfway down the dock when he saw the light burning in the Kestrel's cabin, though he'd doused it himself that morning, and stopped.

Someone was aboard. Someone was sitting in the dark of his boat, waiting for him, and when he came close enough to see through the salt-hazed portlight, the shape resolved into a woman he hadn't seen in six years—a woman with a scar through one eyebrow and a canvas roll under her arm, who had stood on a different dock on the worst night of his life and watched him walk away from the water.

Maren Kade looked up at the window as if she felt his eyes, and did not smile.

"Nineteen days, I hear," she called through the glass. "We should talk, Delacroix. About Sable Shoals."
