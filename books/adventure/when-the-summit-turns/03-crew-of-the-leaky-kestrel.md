## 3. Crew of the Leaky Kestrel

The Kestrel sat at her berth in Cutter's Reach like an old dog that knew it had been bought cheap and intended to make its new owner regret it.

Oscar came down the dock at first light with coffee going cold in one hand and a list of repairs going longer in his head, and before he'd cleared the fuel shed he could hear Tomas already swearing at her bilge. That was how every morning began now: not with weather reports or tide tables but with the sound of a man arguing with a boat that argued back.

"You're up early," Oscar said, stepping over the coiled hose on the deck plates.

"I never went down." Tomas's voice floated up from the engine bay, muffled by steel and grievance. He emerged a minute later on his back on a mechanic's creeper, a smear of grease across his jaw like war paint applied by someone else, holding a length of pipe he regarded with the expression of a man who had been betrayed by family. "The raw water intake is weeping again. I told you last month it was weeping. You said patch it. I patched it. It has decided to weep somewhere else, out of what I can only assume is spite."

"Boats don't have spite."

"This one does. This one has a whole personality and I hate her."

Oscar crouched at the edge of the hatch and looked down into the dark where his engineer lived half his life. Forty-one years old, forearms like dock rope, a face that had stopped trying to be handsome around the same time he'd stopped caring — Tomas Ferro had come aboard four years ago with a duffel bag, a toolbox, and a story he'd never told, and in four years he hadn't told it once. He'd fixed everything else on the boat twice over, though. Everything except the leak.

"How bad?" Oscar asked.

"She takes on maybe eight liters an hour at rest. Twenty if we're running hard in a chop. The pump handles it. The pump handles it *now*." He rolled out from under the engine and sat up, wiping his hands on a rag that was more rag than cloth. "But you're not asking me to run coastal runs anymore, are you. You're asking me to take her out past the shelf."

Oscar didn't answer right away. Down the dock, the market was waking — gulls fighting over fish heads, a woman hosing blood off her slab, the smell of diesel and salt and fried dough all braided together into the single smell of this coast, which was the smell of every place he'd ever been able to afford to sleep. Somewhere out beyond the breakwater the sea lay flat and gray-green and patient, and he thought about nineteen days, and about men who smiled when they counted.

"I'm asking you to keep her alive," he said finally. "Whatever I ask her to do."

Tomas looked at him for a long moment. He had that quality, when he chose to use it, of being able to look through a man to the thing behind him — the debt, the fear, the name written on a paper in a drawer. Then he shrugged and went back under the engine.

"Then bring me parts," he said. "Real ones. Not salvage-yard prayers."

"We'll see what the job pays."

"That," said Tomas, from the dark, "is what worries me."

* * *

Priya arrived at nine, which was late for her, which meant she'd been up since five working on something she didn't want anyone to see yet.

She came down the dock with her chart case under one arm and a paper bag of buns in the other hand, and she handed the bag to Bo without looking, the way you feed a stray that's always there, and climbed aboard. She was small and precise and moved like a woman who had already calculated the distance between every two points on the dock. Her hair was cut short enough to be practical and long enough to be stubborn about it.

"You look like a woman with news," Oscar said.

"I look like a woman who slept four hours." She dropped into the wheelhouse, spread her charts on the table, and weighted the corners with coffee cups. "Because I did. Because somebody asked me yesterday to plot a route to Sable Shoals and I stayed up doing it instead of sleeping, because apparently I work for a man who makes requests at midnight and expects miracles by morning."

"I expect competence by morning. Miracles are bonus."

"Mm." She tapped the chart with one finger. "Here's your miracle. Sable Shoals concession sits forty nautical miles off the monsoon coast, dead center in the corridor every system this season wants to walk down. There are two approaches. The northern line runs wide of the shoals but crosses the storm track — you'd be sailing perpendicular to anything coming off the ocean, which means taking weather on the beam, which means, given our vessel—" she paused, with delicacy, "—our vessel's well-documented relationship with water, that we would very likely die."

"And the southern line?"

"The southern line hugs the coast and cuts in behind the shoal field through the Narrows. Sheltered most of the way. But the Narrows haven't been surveyed since the last concession folded, the charts are eleven years old, and the sandbars move out there like they're paid to." She leaned back and looked at him. "So. Northern line and weather, or southern line and guesswork. Pick your poison, captain."

Oscar studied the chart. The shoals were inked in as a scatter of hazard symbols, a constellation of things that had sunk better boats than his. Eleven-year-old charts. A leaking hull. Nineteen days.

"What's the weather window look like?"

"For the southern approach? Four days, maybe five, before the next system organizes. After that—" she made a small gesture with her hand, opening it, the way you'd describe a flower blooming or a door closing. "After that the whole corridor lights up."

"Southern, then. We thread the Narrows while they're quiet."

"I'd have made the same call." She gathered her charts with a snap. "That's why I'm telling you now so you can't pretend later that it was my idea."

"Priya."

"Yes."

"Why'd you really stay up? You've plotted worse routes asleep."

She was quiet a moment, folding the chart along its old creases, each fold worn soft as cloth. Priya had been with him three years, hired off a container ship where she'd been the only navigator who could find a port in fog, and like Tomas she carried her reasons below the waterline where nobody could sound them. He knew pieces. Everyone on the Kestrel knew pieces of everyone else; that was what a crew was, a mosaic with the grout missing. He knew she sent money somewhere every month, in amounts too regular to be gambling and too large to be comfort. He'd never asked where. She'd never offered.

"Because if we're doing this," she said, "I want to know exactly how we're doing it. I want no surprises. I've had enough surprises in my life to last—" She stopped herself, the way she always did, closing the sentence like a door on a room with the light still on. "Four days, Oscar. Don't waste them."

* * *

Bo found him at noon on the foredeck, where Oscar was pretending to inspect the anchor chain and actually watching the horizon.

Bo Nkemelu was twenty-three and built like the answer to a question nobody had asked yet — all shoulders and grin, a deckhand who could carry a winch drum under each arm and who sang, badly and constantly, whenever the work was hardest. He'd shown up two years ago at a fuel dock in the rain, seventeen years old, asking for work with the specific desperation of a boy who needed the work to be far from wherever he'd come from. Oscar had hired him because he was strong and because he reminded Oscar uncomfortably of Eli, and then had spent two years failing to stop reminding himself of that.

"Captain." Bo dropped onto the deck beside him, cross-legged, producing from nowhere two of the buns Priya had given him and offering one. "Tomas says the boat's dying. Priya says the route's dying. You're staring at the water like it owes you money. So I'm thinking — good day. Very good day. Everybody's cheerful."

"Your definition of cheerful needs work."

"My definition of cheerful is fine. My definition of *this crew* needs work." He bit into the bun. "We're running to Sable Shoals. In the Kestrel. During monsoon season. With a leak."

"With a *managed* leak."

"Managed." Bo laughed, delighted. "That's a good word. I'm going to use that word when we're bailing. 'Don't worry, boys, this is a managed sinking.'"

Oscar almost smiled. It was impossible not to, mostly. Bo was the only person aboard who treated catastrophe as a genre of entertainment, and some mornings that was the only thing keeping the rest of them honest about how afraid they were. But underneath the grin — Oscar had learned to see it, the way you learn to read water color over a reef — Bo worked like a man paying something off. He never talked about home. He sent nothing anywhere. Whatever he'd run from, he ran from it still, every day, cheerfully, the way Oscar ran from the Vantors, and Oscar recognized in the kid the same rule he lived by: never stop, never owe twice. Some debts weren't money.

"When do we sail?" Bo asked.

"Soon. Waiting on permits and a buyer's confirmation."

"Maren Kade's buyer." Bo watched him carefully when he said the name, which was unlike him. Bo noticed more than he let on; that was the trap of him. "The harbor talks, captain. People say Maren Kade is back in town and people say she hired you and people say those two things like they're a firework waiting for a match."

"People say a lot of things."

"People say you two used to run together. Before." He didn't say *before the wreck*. Nobody on the crew said *the wreck* out loud; it was the one subject the mosaic protected completely. "I'm just saying, if there's history, I'd rather hear it from you than from the harbor."

Oscar turned the bun over in his hand without eating it. Out past the breakwater, the flat sea shimmered, and far south — so far it might have been imagination — a smudge of cloud stood up against the sky like a man rising from a chair.

"There's history," he said. "It stays in the past where I put it. That's all you need."

"Sure." Bo nodded, easy, agreeable, and then ruined it entirely by being sincere. "You know you can tell us anything, right? Any of us. Tomas pretends he doesn't care but he'd take a pipe wrench to a hurricane for you. Priya would navigate us into hell if you asked nicely. And I'd—" he shrugged, enormous and simple, "—I'd bail. That's my whole skill set, but I'd bail until my arms fell off. Just saying. In case someday you need somebody to know a thing."

For a moment Oscar couldn't speak. He looked at this kid who owed him nothing and offered everything, and felt the old familiar ache, the one shaped exactly like his brother, and stood up abruptly and pretended the anchor chain required him.

"Eat your bun," he said. "And check the fenders. We may have a visitor."

* * *

The visitor came at dusk, and she was not the visitor Oscar expected.

He was in the wheelhouse reconciling accounts that refused to reconcile when Bo's voice came down from the deck, pitched wrong — not alarm, but curiosity sharpened into caution. "Captain. Got a woman at the gate asking for you. Says she wants passage."

Oscar came out. At the head of the dock, where the security lamp threw its cone of yellow, stood a woman he didn't know — mid-thirties, travel-worn coat, a single bag at her feet, standing with the stillness of someone who had learned that stillness got you further than hurry. Even at that distance something about her registered wrong, the way a chart registers wrong when one depth number doesn't match the others around it.

"Passage where?" Oscar called.

"Sable Shoals," the woman said. Her voice carried clean across the water. "I understand you're sailing there. I pay cash. And I don't ask for the cabin, just a bunk and a corner."

Behind him, Oscar heard Bo shift his weight, already softening — Bo softened toward strays the way the shore softened toward waves, inevitably, expensively. He heard Tomas's hatch bang open below, the engineer surfacing at the scent of a stranger the way a dog surfaces at a knock. From the wheelhouse, Priya appeared in the doorway, arms crossed, eyes already working.

Three reasons to stay. Three loyalties, each with a hairline crack running through it, each carrying weight it shouldn't, all of it held together by a hull that leaked and a captain who ran. And now a stranger at the gate, asking for the exact destination, in the exact window, with cash in hand and questions Oscar couldn't see yet but could feel coming like pressure ahead of a front.

"How much cash?" he called back.

The woman smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of someone holding a card she hadn't played.

"Enough," she said, "to fix your leak."

Oscar stood very still. He hadn't told anyone at the gate about the leak.
