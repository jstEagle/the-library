## 4. Maren Kade Returns

She was standing at the end of the pier when Oscar came down the harbor road, and for one stupid heartbeat he thought the storm had finally caught him—because that was what Maren Kade had always been to him: weather with a face.

Ten years since he'd seen her last, and she hadn't changed in any of the ways that mattered. Same cropped gray-streaked hair plastered flat by the drizzle. Same canvas jacket gone soft as cloth at the elbows, salt-bleached at the shoulders where a pack rode. She stood with her boots apart on the wet planks like she owned the pier, or like she was daring it to move under her, and behind her the harbor lay gray and shivering under the building pressure of the system offshore—the same system Oscar had been counting like a debt all week.

He considered turning around. He actually did it, half a step, weight shifting back toward the market road—and then he stopped, because running from Maren Kade had never once worked, and pretending otherwise was an insult to them both.

"You look terrible," she said when he got close enough.

"You look exactly the same."

"That's the tragedy of me." She didn't smile. Maren rationed smiles the way other people rationed water in a drought. "You're limping."

"I'm not limping."

"Your left knee. You favor it when you're tired. You've done it since—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Oscar felt the old wound open along its whole length, clean and painless at first, the way deep cuts are.

Since Eli, she'd been about to say. Since the night your brother went into the water off Sable Shoals and I watched the flare go out.

"I need to talk to you," she said instead. "Not here."

"There's nothing we have to talk about that isn't here."

"Then let's do it fast, before you start lying to me again." She glanced past his shoulder, up the harbor road, and something in her expression shifted—not softer, but more careful, the way a helmsman's face changes when the wind backs without warning. "The man in the yellow slicker who's been following you since the fish market. He's still there. Third stall back, pretending to read a price board."

Oscar didn't turn around. Ten years of running taught him that much; you never confirmed you'd seen them. "Vantor man," he said quietly. "They're not violent. That's the worst part. They just *arrive*."

"I know who the Vantors are, Oscar."

And there it was. Not the name dropped casually, not gossip, but said flat and certain, the way you'd say the name of a reef you'd charted yourself. He looked at her properly then, rain beading on her jacket, eyes the color of the water before a squall, and understood that this meeting was not an accident, had probably never once in his life been an accident where Maren Kade was concerned.

"How long have you been in port?"

"Two days."

"Two days," he repeated. "And you waited two days to ambush me on a pier in the rain."

"I wasn't ambushing. I was watching how you moved." She shrugged. "A man being hunted moves differently than a man hunting. You've been hunted for a while now. Months, I'd guess. And you're losing."

The rain thickened, ticking on the planks, on the tarped masts of the fishing boats, on the roof of the chandlery behind them where a lamp burned yellow through fogged glass. Offshore, the horizon had gone the color of a bruise going bad. The barometer in Oscar's chest—he'd carried one so long he'd stopped thinking of it as a metaphor—had been falling for three days straight.

"The Anchor Room," he said finally. "Twenty minutes. If you're not there, I leave."

"I'll be there. I'm always there. That's my whole problem." 

* * *

The Anchor Room smelled of diesel, fried onions, and forty years of spilled beer soaked so deep into the floorboards no tide could ever lift it out. The lunch crowd had thinned to a handful of riggers playing cards by the window, and the woman behind the bar—Sefa, who had poured for Oscar's father before him—took one look at Maren walking in and set down two glasses without asking.

"Some things don't change," Oscar said.

"Whiskey's changed. It's worse. Sit down."

He sat. They faced each other across a table scarred with initials, and for a long moment neither of them drank, neither spoke, and the silence between them did what silences between old rivals always do—it filled itself with everything they weren't saying, until the room felt too small for both of them and their dead.

"You knew about the Vantors," Oscar said at last. "How?"

"Because everyone on this coast knows about the Vantors. Cheerful people. Generous. They smile when they take your boat, smile when they take your house, smile when they take years off your life. My cousin borrowed from them to refit a trawler." She turned her glass a quarter-turn on the wood. "He's hauling nets for them now. Indefinitely. They told his wife it was an honor."

"They told me nineteen days."

Maren's eyes came up sharp. "Nineteen days for what?"

"To make good. Or make arrangements." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's the word they used. *Arrangements.* Like flowers for a funeral. Like seating at a wedding."

"And how much?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters because it tells me how desperate you are, and how desperate you are tells me whether you're going to say yes to what I came here to offer."

The rain hammered harder against the window, and somewhere out in the harbor a halyard slapped a mast in a rhythm too fast for the wind—a small thing, but Oscar heard it the way he heard everything now, every sound filed under *storm coming*, because for six years filing sounds under *storm coming* had been the only discipline that kept him alive and moving.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever it is. Say it fast."

"Sable Shoals."

The name landed in him like a hook set deep. He kept his face still—he'd gotten good at that—but inside, everything tore loose at once. The concession platform rising black out of green water. The permit office with its rubber stamps and its coffee rings. Eli's handwriting on the manifest, looping and careless, *back by Thursday, promise*. The storm that was supposed to pass north. The report that said it would pass north.

"No," he said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"No." He pushed his chair back. "I swore off that run. We both did. We shook hands on it over Eli's grave, Maren, if you can call what we did shaking hands—you were so drunk you could barely stand and I was so sober I wanted to die, and we agreed, both of us, that neither of us would ever take money to run that concession again. So whatever you're selling, sell it to someone whose brother isn't at the bottom of that channel."

"He's not at the bottom of the channel."

Oscar stopped with one hand on the table. The room's noise—the card game, the fryer, the rain—all of it receded, the way sound does underwater.

"What did you say?"

Maren held his stare, and he saw now what he'd missed on the pier, what ten years and one glance hadn't shown him: she was afraid. Not of him. Of the thing she'd come to say. Maren Kade, who'd once steered a crippled tender through a squall line laughing, was afraid, and that frightened him more than anything the Vantors could send walking up a harbor road.

"Eli's not in the channel," she said. "I've known that for six years. I dove it myself, twice, the first year. Every meter of it. He's not there, Oscar. Neither is the boat."

* * *

The whiskey sat untouched between them, going warm. Outside, the light had failed early, the storm pressing its shoulder against the coast, and the window had become a gray mirror in which Oscar could see himself sitting across from her—older, thinner, a man built entirely out of forward motion, and behind his own reflection, faintly, hers.

"Start from the beginning," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"The beginning is the report." Maren leaned forward, forearms on the table, and lowered her voice though there was no one near enough to hear. "The weather bulletin that cleared the run. You remember it. Storm passing north, window of thirty-six hours, seas moderate. We all read it. Eli read it out loud in the wheelhouse, made a joke about it—I remember the joke, Oscar, I've had six years to memorize every second of that day."

"I don't want—"

"You asked for the beginning. This is it." Her hand flattened on the table, steadying itself or steadying him. "Three years ago I started pulling threads. Small ones. A clerk at the regional forecast office who'd been paid off and couldn't live with it. A routing order that was amended after the fact. The concession buyer—Corvane Maritime Services—they falsified the bulletin, Oscar. They knew the system was turning south. They sent that boat out anyway because the permit window was closing and the cargo was worth more than the crew. Four men, Oscar. Four men for a shipment of survey equipment and bonded goods."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Oscar gripped the edge of the table.

"That's not possible. The inquiry—"

"The inquiry was bought. You know how these things work. Corvane wrote the finding, the finding said *act of God*, and everyone signed it and went home." She sat back. "Everyone except me."

"Why?" The question came out raw. "Why dig? Why now? What do you get?"

For the first time since the pier, Maren looked away from him. She looked at the window, at the storm-dark coming down over the harbor, and when she answered, her voice had lost its edge and become something quieter and worse.

"Because I was on the water that night. Because I saw the flare go out and I spent ten years telling myself it was the sea, just the sea, bad luck and bad timing, and then a drunk clerk in a bar in Port Meridian told me it wasn't, and I couldn't unknow it." She turned back. "And because the concession permit comes open again in nineteen days. Same waters. Same buyer. Corvane holds the option, and the option expires, and whoever runs salvage and resupply on that concession in the next three weeks will have access to Corvane's field records. The original logs. The routing orders. The real bulletin, if it still exists. Proof."

Oscar stared at her. Nineteen days. The number rang in him like a struck bell, the same number the Vantors had pronounced over dessert with their bright terrible smiles, and he understood—with the cold, dropping feeling of a keel leaving the water—that this was not coincidence. Maren Kade did not traffic in coincidence.

"You knew," he said slowly. "You knew about my debt. You knew the timeline. You timed this."

"I timed it because you're the best salvage runner on this coast and the only person alive who has a reason to burn Corvane down besides me." She spread her hands. "Yes, Oscar. I timed it. I'm not proud. I'm also not sorry."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire another crew and I do it alone, and I probably die, and Corvane keeps its secret, and Eli stays a lie on a piece of paper." She picked up her whiskey finally and drank, grimacing at it. "But you won't say no. Not because of the debt. Because of what I just told you about the channel."

He should say no. Every instinct honed over six years of running said no—no new waters, no old wounds, no job with a ghost's name on the manifest. The rule was simple and he'd built his whole life on it: never stop, never owe twice. Taking Maren's job meant owing the past. It meant sailing back into the one place on earth he'd sworn never to return, with the one person who'd watched his brother die, chasing proof of a crime committed by men rich enough to buy the weather itself.

It was the worst idea he'd ever heard.

It was also, he realized, looking at her across the table—at the fear she'd finally stopped hiding, at the ten years of guilt worn smooth as sea glass into her face—the first idea in six years that pointed anywhere except away.

"The crew," he said. "Tomas, Priya, Bo. They don't know anything about Eli. About any of it."

"Then you'll have to tell them."

"They'll ask why. Everyone always asks why, eventually. Bo asks why about everything, it's his whole personality." He rubbed his face. "And there's a leak in the Kestrel I haven't fixed, and a monsoon sitting offshore that the forecasts keep calling *passing north*—"

He stopped. Maren went very still.

"—and I just heard myself say that," he finished quietly. "*Passing north.*"

"Yeah," she said. "It gets under your skin. Everything does, once you know."

Outside, the first real gust of the leading edge hit the harbor, and the boats at anchor swung together on their moorings with a groan of chain, and the lamp in the Anchor Room flickered and held. Nineteen days. One debt to the smiling family, one debt to the dead, and only one boat to pay either.

"When do you need an answer?" he asked.

"You already gave it." Maren stood, dropping bills on the table, and pulled her collar up against the wind coming through the door as she opened it. "Permit office opens at eight tomorrow. Bring your crew list."

She paused in the doorway, rain streaming past her into the light, and for a moment she looked back at him with an expression he hadn't seen on her in ten years—not rival, not witness, just a tired woman standing at the edge of the same wreck they'd both been circling for a decade.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I'm glad it's you. I hate that it's you. But I'm glad."

Then she was gone into the storm, and Oscar sat alone with two untouched whiskeys and the sound of the sea getting louder, trying to remember the exact words of the oath he'd just broken, and finding—as the wind rose and the lights of the harbor swayed—that he couldn't.
