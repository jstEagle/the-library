## 5. What Was Sworn Off

Priya mapped water the way other people prayed.

She sat in the Kestrel's cramped chart room with the door latched against the harbor noise, a pencil behind her ear and three charts of the Sable Shoals approach spread across the table, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug, a coil of copper wire, and Bo's lucky fishing lure, which he'd left there like an offering. Outside, the afternoon light came through the salt-hazed porthole in a color she had no name for—gray trying to be gold and giving up halfway.

The route was simple on paper. Everything was simple on paper. That was the first lie of navigation and the last one too: you drew a line from where you were to where you wanted to be, and the sea agreed to none of it.

Sable Shoals sat ninety miles down-coast, past the Teeth—a scatter of drowned granite that had eaten better boats than theirs—and then forty miles of open fetch across the Vantor Bank shallows, where the bottom rose up lazy and shallow and turned any swell into something mean. The concession itself was a salvage yard on a spit of coral and rust, licensed for wreck recovery, storm debris, whatever the monsoon season shook loose and left behind. Nineteen days of permit. Weather window, if you could call it that—the tail edge of the dry season bleeding into the first breath of the monsoon, which meant wind out of the southwest building by degrees, which meant the crossing would get worse every day they waited and no better any day they ran.

So they'd run soon or not at all. Priya knew that. She also knew that everyone aboard knew it, and that nobody had said it out loud yet, because saying it made it real, and the Kestrel's whole culture ran on the gentle fiction that decisions hadn't been made until they were spoken.

She penciled in the first leg anyway. Harbor mouth to the outer buoy, buoy to the Teeth' northern gap, gap to the shoals. Clean lines. Confident little arrows. She drew them the way you'd draw a lie you intended to keep.

* * *

They gathered in the main cabin at dusk, because Tomas cooked on Tuesdays and Tuesdays meant rice and smoked fish and everyone pretending the galley wasn't a biohazard. Bo set the table without being asked—he always did, four plates even when it was five of them, one plate kept clean and upside-down at the end of the table that nobody mentioned and nobody moved. Eli's plate. Six years of Eli's plate. It was the kind of ritual that looked like grief from the outside and functioned like ballast from the inside, and Priya had long since stopped wondering whether it helped or hurt. It just was.

Oscar came in last, wet to the shoulders, smelling of rain and diesel. He'd been at the fuel dock negotiating credit again, and his face had the particular flatness it got when someone had said *no* to him politely. He dropped into his chair, said grace in two mumbled words that weren't really grace, and started eating.

Maren's offer sat on the table between them like a fifth person.

"So," Bo said finally, because Bo always broke first. "Are we actually talking about this?"

"We're eating," Oscar said.

"We're eating *and* talking about this."

Tomas ladled rice without looking up. "The boat leaks. I've told him. He's decided not to hear me." He said it in the tone of a man reporting weather—mild, factual, and underneath it, a current strong enough to drown you. "New packing on the shaft seal, maybe. If I can find the parts. Which costs money. Which we don't have."

"The job pays," Bo said.

"The job pays *well*," Priya corrected quietly, and felt all three of them look at her. She kept her eyes on her plate. "Advance enough to fix the seal, provision properly, and still clear debt. Real debt. Not dock-credit debt."

Nobody asked whose debt. They all knew about the Vantors. You didn't survive on this coast without knowing about the Vantors, the way you didn't swim these waters without knowing about the sharks—it wasn't a moral question, it was geography.

"Say the number," Oscar said.

Priya said the number. It hung in the cabin air, absurd and beautiful, roughly triple what the Kestrel grossed in a good quarter, and a good quarter hadn't happened in two years.

Bo whistled low. Tomas put down his spoon.

"And what does Maren want for it?" Tomas asked. "Because Maren doesn't give money away. Maren invests money. There's a difference, and the difference is she owns you after."

"She wants the concession run done," Oscar said. "Clean, on permit, before the monsoon sets in for real. Her buyer wants the salvage rights documented and the heavy equipment pulled off the shoal. That's it."

"That's not it," Tomas said. "That's never it."

"It's the same concession," Priya said.

The words came out of her before she'd decided to say them, and once they were out, the cabin went very quiet in the way a room goes quiet when a floorboard creaks somewhere upstairs. Four people not looking at each other. The pressure lamp hissed. Somewhere below the waterline, the bilge pump kicked on with its tired mechanical cough, ran its fifteen seconds, and died.

"You know that," Oscar said. Not a question.

"I charted the original run," Priya said. "Six years ago. I was the one who plotted the return leg." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for numbers. Numbers were safe. Numbers had never drowned anyone; people drowned ignoring them. "Same concession block. Same buyer, near as I can tell from the permit codes. Same water."

Eli's plate sat upside-down at the end of the table, white and clean and silent.

* * *

She remembered fragments, the way you remember anything from a bad night—pieces, not scenes.

The radio room of the old boat, the *Marlin*, six years ago, her headphones on, the storm coming in off the bank like a wall being pushed by God. Oscar's voice on the deck above, arguing with someone—she'd never known who, the words shredded by wind, only the shape of the argument reaching her through the hull: *we turn back now or we don't turn back at all.* And then the order coming down the ladder, and the order being wrong, and her plotting the course she was told to plot because she was twenty-three years old and the man signing the orders owned the boat and the permits and, apparently, the truth.

She remembered the last transmission from the *Marlin*'s sister vessel—the workboat that went out second, the one Eli was aboard. Static, then Eli's voice, young and laughing even then, even in that, saying something about the swell being big enough to surf. Then static. Then nothing, forever, in the specific way the sea goes nothing.

She remembered Oscar afterward, standing on the dock in the rain while the coast guard boats came back empty, and how he didn't cry, and how she'd understood even then, at twenty-three, that the absence of tears was not the absence of drowning. Some men flood inward.

And she remembered—this was the fragment she kept locked deepest, the one she took out only at 3 a.m. when the boat rocked wrong—that the order to send the second boat out had come with a signature on it. A work order. She'd seen it later, in the insurance filings, when she'd gone looking the way grieving crews sometimes go looking, hunting for a reason the way a compass hunts north. The signature at the bottom had been smudged, photocopied twice, illegible. She had told herself it was illegible. She had worked very hard at telling herself that.

Everyone knew it was a bad idea, this job. Everyone knew, the way you know a storm is building offshore—you feel it in your ears before you see it in the sky. And everyone was going to do it anyway, because the alternative had a name and the name was the Vantors, and because nineteen days was nineteen days, and because hope, on the Kestrel, had always been less a feeling than a financial instrument.

* * *

After dinner, while Bo washed up and Tomas disappeared into the engine room to commune with the leak, Priya stayed at the chart table and did the thing she did instead of sleeping.

She built the route honestly.

No confident arrows this time. She laid out the approach with every hazard marked in red: the Teeth, with their tide rips timed against the moon; the bank shallows with their fetch-amplification noted in her small precise hand; the fuel margin calculated twice and then a third time assuming headwinds, because assuming tailwinds was how navigators ended up famous posthumously. She wrote the numbers for each day of delay beside the route—day one, day three, day five—and watched the margins shrink like ice melting. By day seven the crossing became a gamble. By day ten it became a suicide note with waypoints.

Nineteen days of permit. But the real clock was the weather, and the weather gave them maybe five days of survivable water, and those five days had already started burning.

She should say no. That was the correct answer, the answer she'd give any client, any crew, any younger version of herself sitting across this table: don't take the job, don't sail the same water that killed your brother, don't sign your name next to the ghost. The correct answer sat right there, obvious as a reef at low tide.

But correctness didn't service debt. Correctness didn't buy a shaft seal. Correctness hadn't stood in the Vantors' dining room—she'd heard about the dinner from Bo, who heard about everything—and watched cheerful dangerous people smile at Oscar across the candles and give him nineteen days like a gift.

Priya had her own arithmetic, the private kind. Two years of shrinking shares. A mother in Colombo whose house needed a roof before the rains, real rains, not metaphorical ones. A savings account that was a rounding error. She was thirty-one years old and the best navigator on this stretch of coast and she was broke, and the job paid triple, and the water was the same water, and the water didn't care.

That was the thing nobody said about complicity: it never arrived wearing a villain's face. It arrived as a spreadsheet. It arrived as a sick mother, a leaking boat, a smiling creditor. It arrived asking only for a signature, a plotted course, a silence—and it offered, in exchange, exactly enough.

She picked up the pencil. Her hand was steady. That surprised her; she'd expected it to shake.

She drew the route. Harbor mouth to the buoy, buoy to the Teeth, Teeth to the shoals. Clean lines, confident arrows, the whole beautiful lie of it. Then, beneath the chart, in the corner where only a navigator would look, she wrote the true figure—the honest fuel margin, the honest weather window, the honest odds—and dated it.

If they went, they'd go on her numbers. Whatever happened out there would be, in some ledger somewhere, partly hers. She signed her work the way she always did, small initials in the lower corner, P.K., and understood, as the pencil lifted, that she had just done the thing she'd spent six years telling herself she'd never do again: she'd plotted a course into the same water, for the same buyer, knowing everything she knew.

The bilge pump coughed on below her feet. Fifteen seconds. Died.

Bo appeared in the doorway, drying his hands on a rag, and he looked at the chart, and he looked at her face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, the way you nod at a funeral.

"He's going to say yes tomorrow," Bo said. "Isn't he."

Priya looked at the route she'd drawn, the line running straight and certain toward the Teeth, toward the shoals, toward the place where the sea had already taken one plate off their table.

"He's going to say yes tonight," she said. "He's up on deck right now, talking to Maren."

And as if the words had summoned it, they both heard it—footsteps on the companionway ladder, two sets, one heavy and familiar, one light and deliberate, descending together out of the dark.
