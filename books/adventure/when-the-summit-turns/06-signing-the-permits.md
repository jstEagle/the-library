## 6. Signing the Permits

The harbor office smelled of mildew and old ink, the way all government buildings smell when they sit too close to salt water—as if the sea were slowly claiming them from the inside out. Oscar Delacroix stood at the counter with his cap in his hands and read the same line for the fourth time.

*Concession holder of record: Delacroix, O.*

Below it, in smaller type, a line he had not expected:

*Prior concession history: Delacroix, E. — revoked 6 years prior. Status: deceased.*

He had known it would be there. He had signed these papers before, six years ago, standing in this same office with rain hammering the tin roof and his brother's handwriting still wet on the crew manifest. But knowing a thing and reading it are different animals. Knowing is a scar; reading is the knife again.

"You can take your time," said the clerk, a narrow woman named Odile with reading glasses on a chain and the patience of someone paid by the hour regardless of outcome. "The permits don't expire until you sign them."

"They expire either way," Oscar said. "Nineteen days."

"Eighteen, after midnight." Odile smiled without warmth. "Time's honest that way. It doesn't negotiate."

Outside the fogged window, Maren Kade leaned against the harbor office wall with her arms crossed, watching him through the glass the way she watched weather systems build—patiently, professionally, with an eye already calculating where the damage would land. She'd insisted on coming. *You'll flinch,* she'd said on the walk over. *I want to see it.* He hadn't asked what she meant by that, because he already knew.

He picked up the pen. The nib was brass, worn to one side by ten thousand signatures from men and women who had all, presumably, told themselves the same thing he was telling himself now: that paper was just paper, that a name on a line was just a name, that the past stayed past if you didn't invite it forward.

But he was inviting it forward. That was the whole job.

The Sable Shoals concession ran forty nautical miles of drowned coastline that no sane operator wanted—shifting bars, unmarked wrecks, a monsoon season that arrived early and left late—and the salvage rights were worth real money only because the buyer was willing to pay for what everyone else refused to touch. Maren hadn't told him who the buyer was. She'd slid the contract across Priya's chart table two nights ago and said, *Same terms as before,* and the words *as before* had landed in the cabin like something dropped from a height.

Now here it was in official type. *Buyer of record:* a company name he didn't recognize, registered through three holding shells, which meant it didn't want to be known, which meant Maren either didn't know herself or knew exactly and was keeping it from him until he was too committed to walk away. Both were possible. Both were very much her style.

His hand hovered over the signature line.

Six years ago he had signed a different document in this same office—a storm-order authorization, the kind of paperwork salvage crews filed so they could run ahead of weather instead of behind it. Eli had been supposed to countersign it. Eli had been asleep on the Kestrel, dead tired from double shifts, and Oscar had signed both lines himself, forging his brother's initials in a hand so close to Eli's own that even their mother couldn't have told the difference. *Delacroix, E.* Two letters and a loop. He had put his brother's name on a run into a building storm because the Vantors' interest was compounding and the salvage was sitting there like fruit going soft, and Eli would have said yes anyway, Eli always said yes, and so Oscar had simply skipped the part where he asked.

The boat went out. The storm turned. Eli did not come back.

That was the whole story. That was the story he'd been running from down every coast between here and the trade winds, outrunning collectors and creditors and his own reflection in harbor windows, because as long as he never stopped moving, the story stayed a rumor instead of a fact. Motion was anesthesia. Debt was a schedule. A man with nineteen days left doesn't have time to remember anything older than that.

"Mr. Delacroix?" Odile said gently.

"I'm reading it."

"You've read it four times."

"Then I'll read it a fifth."

She withdrew behind her ledger with the practiced discretion of harbor clerks everywhere, and Oscar looked at the line again. *Delacroix, E. — deceased.* Six years, and the registry still carried the name, because registries are honest in the way Odile said time was honest: they don't negotiate, they don't forget, they don't accept the version of events you've built your life around. Somewhere in the archive below this floor was the old authorization with his forged initials on it, filed and stamped and sleeping. If anyone ever pulled it—if anyone ever compared the ink—

He thought of Maren outside the window and understood, finally and completely, why she'd wanted to watch him sign. Not cruelty. Accounting. She was checking whether the man she was about to sail with could hold a pen steady while his ghosts sat on his shoulders, because out at Sable Shoals there would be no counter to lean on, no market crowd, no Odile to retreat behind. There would only be water and weather and whatever was true between them.

He signed. *Oscar Delacroix*, full name, no flourish, the letters slightly too large the way his always were when his hand wanted to shake and he wouldn't let it.

Odile took the page, blotted it, stamped it twice—once in blue, once in the red wax that meant bonded and binding—and slid the duplicate across to him. "Concession rights transfer on completion of delivery," she recited. "Permits void if the run isn't logged within eighteen days. Penalties revert to the bondholder." She looked up over her glasses. "Who's your bondholder?"

"A family friend," Oscar said.

"Mm." Odile had processed enough harbor paper to know what *family friend* meant in this economy. "Then I'd log the run early."

* * *

Maren fell into step beside him on the ramp without being asked, and they walked the length of the commercial pier in a silence that had texture to it—the silence of two people who had once talked all night about everything except the one thing that mattered.

"Nineteen days," she said eventually.

"Eighteen, after midnight."

"You counted."

"I count everything now. It's a professional habit." He kept his eyes on the masts ahead, the forest of them swaying against a sky the color of wet slate. The monsoon edge was visible offshore, a gray wall sitting on the horizon like something waiting to be introduced. "You watched me flinch. Satisfied?"

"I watched you almost not sign." Maren's voice was level, unhurried, the voice she used on crews in bad water. "There's a difference. A man who flinches still signs. A man who almost doesn't is telling you something."

"What's it telling you?"

"That you know what's on those papers better than the registry does." She stopped at the pier's rail, where the planks were furred green with damp, and looked out at the gray wall of weather. "Eli's name is still in the system. You saw it."

It wasn't a question. Oscar gripped the rail. Below them, the harbor water moved in slow brown coils, carrying leaves and bottle caps and one lost sandal, indifferent as everything else.

"The registry keeps everything," he said.

"It does."

"And anyone who pulls the old files—"

"—would find a storm authorization with both Delacroix signatures on it, dated the day before the wreck." Maren turned to look at him, and up close her face had the fine weathering of someone who'd spent a decade squinting into horizons: crow's feet like chart lines, a small white scar through one eyebrow he'd never asked about. "I know. I helped file it."

The words went through him colder than any squall. "You what?"

"I filed the paperwork, Oscar. After. When the inspectors came through asking why a boat went out under a building system with no valid order, someone had to produce an order." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Maren; nothing about her ever rose, she just got denser, like water going deep. "So I produced one. Backdated. Your initials on Eli's line, your signature on yours. I made it clean. I made it *boring*. Boring paperwork doesn't get pulled."

"Why?" The question came out rawer than he intended, loud enough that a fisherman two berths down glanced over. Oscar lowered his voice. "Why would you cover for me? You hated me before the wreck. You had every reason to let them hang it on me."

Maren looked back out at the horizon. For a long moment the only sound was halyards clinking against masts, that harbor music that never stops, and somewhere beneath it the low grumble of surf on the outer bar.

"Because I read the order wrong," she said. "That night. I was on the water—I told you that. What I haven't told you is that I was the one who relayed the weather update to your boat. The forecast said the system would hold south. I passed it along word for word. It didn't hold." Her jaw worked once. "So when the inspectors came, I wasn't covering for you. I was covering for me. You signed a bad order based on my bad report, and if they dug into mine, they'd have found I'd taken the buyer's routing fee and cut the forecast short to save transmission costs. Three minutes of weather data. That's what Eli's life came down to in the file. Three minutes I didn't send."

Oscar stared at her. Ten years of hatred rearranged itself in his chest, not dissolving—nothing between them would ever dissolve—but shifting, the way a wreck shifts on a shoal, settling into some new configuration that was somehow worse and more true than the old one.

"You never said."

"You never asked. You were too busy running." She pushed off the rail. "Here's what matters now. The buyer on this concession is the same shell structure as the buyer six years ago. Same registration pattern, same holding-company fingerprints. I checked. Whoever bought our silence then is buying this route now, and they're paying premium for a stretch of water nobody wants—which means they want something on it, or under it, or they need the delivery record to make an old liability disappear." She met his eyes. "Either way, we're sailing into the middle of the thing we've both been running from. And I needed to know, before we cast off, whether you'd sign your name next to a dead man's twice."

The gray wall offshore seemed closer. Or maybe that was just the light going.

"And if I hadn't?"

"Then I'd have signed alone, and you'd have gone back to outrunning the Vantors until they caught you, and we'd both have kept our stories." Maren started up the pier toward the Kestrel, then paused. "You signed. So the stories end out there, one way or another. Nineteen days, Oscar. Try to spend them honestly."

* * *

The Kestrel sat at her berth looking, as always, like a boat apologizing for herself—patched sails, rust blooms along the toe rail, a hull that Tomas swore was sound and the bilge pump swore otherwise. Priya was aboard already, charts spread across the cabin table, redrawing the route to Sable Shoals for the third time. Bo was topside coiling lines that didn't need coiling, which was how Bo handled nerves: by giving his hands work his head couldn't argue with.

Oscar stepped aboard and felt the deck shift under him, familiar as a heartbeat, and for one moment—one treacherous, quiet moment—he almost told them. All of it. The forged initials, the order, the three missing minutes of weather data, the whole architecture of guilt that six years of motion had built walls around. They were his crew. They deserved a captain whose cargo wasn't a lie.

But Priya looked up from her charts with the flat competence of a woman who had already decided the risk was worth it and didn't need her captain unraveling on the eve of departure, and Bo gave him that open, uncomplicated grin that Oscar could not bear to stain, and the moment closed like water over a dropped coin.

"Papers?" Priya asked.

Oscar set the bonded duplicate on the chart table, next to her neat pencil lines. "Signed. Eighteen days."

"Route's ready. I've marked three weather windows." She tapped the chart. "Though I'll say what I said last night—this buyer's routing takes us through the Molay Deep approach, and there's no reason for it. It adds two days. Unless they want us passing something specific."

"Or avoiding something specific," Maren said from the companionway.

Priya's pencil stilled. "Which?"

"That's what we're going to find out." Maren looked at Oscar, and something passed between them—not forgiveness, not trust, but a kind of armed truce, two people who now held pieces of each other's worst truth and knew the other knew. "Nobody signs anything else until we understand who we're really working for."

Bo appeared at the rail, wiping his hands on his trousers, wearing the particular expression he wore when he'd done something kind and knew it might be received as inconvenient. "Cap? There's someone on the dock asking for you. Well—for passage, but she asked for the Kestrel by name." He scratched his jaw. "Woman. Cash money. Says she needs to reach Sable Shoals and won't say why. Name of Voss. Ilsa Voss."

Oscar felt the name land somewhere below his ribs, in the place where old wrecks sink.

"How does a stranger know this boat runs to Sable Shoals?" Priya said quietly. "We signed the permits an hour ago."

Above them the first fat drops of the monsoon's leading edge began to tick against the deck, slow and deliberate, like fingers counting.

"She's waiting on the dock," Bo said. "Says she'll pay double for a yes tonight."
