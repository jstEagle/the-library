## 7. The Stranger at the Dock

Bo Ferreira counted money the way other people counted sheep—slowly, gratefully, one bill at a time. It wasn't that he'd ever had much of it. It was that he'd learned, in the years since his mother's hands had gone too shaky to gut fish and his own had become the only ones in the family that worked, that money was a kind of weather. It came in systems. It left in systems. And if you were very, very careful, you could read the sky of a man's pockets and know what was coming.

The stranger who came down the pier at half past seven in the evening, while the light went the color of old pewter and the Kestrel knocked her fenders against the pilings like a horse shifting in a stall, had pockets Bo could not read at all.

He was sitting on the dock with his legs hanging over the black water, mending a net he didn't need to mend, because mending nets was what his hands did when his head was full. And his head was full. It had been full since Maren Kade had walked into their lives three days ago with a job that tasted like a grave. *Sable Shoals.* The name alone made Tomas go quiet in a way that was worse than shouting, made Priya's pen stop moving on the chart, made Oscar look at the middle distance like a man reading a letter he already knew was bad news.

Bo was the only one who hadn't argued. Not because he wanted the money—well, not *only* because he wanted the money, though God knew he did, with his sister's school fees sitting on his conscience like a stone. But because he'd learned something about bad ideas in his twenty-six years, most of them his own: the ones you run from chase you anyway. Better to face them with a full crew and a mended net.

The woman's footsteps on the planks were the first thing he noticed. Not heels—nobody wore heels on a working pier—but not the flat, rolling gait of a sailor either. Something between. Deliberate. A walk that had been practiced, or chosen, the way a person chooses a coat.

"Good evening." She stopped three meters off, close enough to talk, far enough to be polite. "Is this the Kestrel?"

Bo looked up. The woman was maybe forty, maybe fifty—it was impossible to say. Gray threaded her dark hair at the temples like frost on a window. She wore a traveling coat that had been good five years ago and a pair of boots that had been good yesterday, which was an interesting combination, and she carried a canvas bag with a strap worn shiny at the shoulder. Her face was the kind of face you trusted immediately and then, later, when you were alone in your bunk, worried about.

"That's her," Bo said. "Though she's more of a suggestion of a boat than a boat, some days."

The woman smiled. It reached her eyes, or very nearly did. "I've sailed on worse suggestions."

"We're not running passengers, I'm afraid. Not this trip." He said it kindly, because he said everything kindly, because his mother had taught him that kindness cost nothing at the moment you spent it and everything at the moment you didn't. "Charter work. Concession run."

"To Sable Shoals."

It wasn't a question, but Bo heard the question inside it, the way you hear the sea inside a shell. He set down the net. "News travels."

"News travels," she agreed. "Ilsa Voss." She didn't offer a hand, and Bo found that he was grateful, because he didn't know yet whether he'd have taken it. "I need passage to the Shoals. I understand you're the only boat going."

"The only boat fool enough," Bo said, and then wished he hadn't, because a man who told a stranger the crew's own jokes was a man who'd already opened the door. "We're—look, I'd help you if I could, but it's not my call. And the skipper's not—" He gestured vaguely at the town, at the dark, at everything Oscar Delacroix was not, which at this hour was anywhere Bo could point.

"I'll wait." She said it simply, the way you'd say *it's raining.* She set her bag down on the planks and, to Bo's astonishment, sat down beside him, a meter of respectful dock between them, and looked out at the water like she had all the time that had ever been minted.

Bo looked at her. She looked at the harbor.

"You're Bo," she said.

"How'd you—"

"The deckhand mending a net at dusk on a boat that's leaving in three days. The others are inside arguing. I watched the big one come out for cigarettes twenty minutes ago and go back in without smoking them." She nodded toward the harbor office where the crew had been holed up with charts and cold rice. "That leaves the one who sits on docks. Bo."

"That's a little frightening," Bo said honestly.

"It's a little frightening," she agreed, "how much a person can learn just by sitting still and watching. Most people never try it. They think watching is the same as waiting, and it isn't. Waiting is passive. Watching is—" she searched for it, found it— "*hungry.*"

Bo laughed, because he couldn't help it, and because laughing was the other thing his hands couldn't do, so his mouth did it instead. "And what are you hungry for, Ms. Voss?"

The pause was small. A half-second, maybe less. But Bo had grown up reading weather, and weather was nothing but pauses—the stillness before the squall line, the held breath between lightning and thunder—and he heard this one the way a fisherman hears a change in the wind's voice.

"Passage," she said. "And answers."

"To questions you haven't asked yet."

"To questions I've been asking for six years," she said, "and nobody would answer."

The water knocked against the pilings. Somewhere across the harbor a gull complained about the century. Bo found that his hands had gone still on the net, and he didn't like that, because his hands were the honest part of him and when they stopped working it meant the rest of him was listening to something his mouth hadn't agreed to yet.

* * *

She paid in cash.

That was the second thing, after the walk and before the questions, and Bo would think about it later, lying in his bunk with the Kestrel's familiar night-sounds around him, how the cash had been the moment he should have known. Not because it was cash—half the coast ran on cash, and paper was paper—but because of *how* she paid. She didn't count it out. She didn't hesitate. She peeled bills off a roll the way a woman peels an orange she bought that morning: without ceremony, without attachment, without the small involuntary tightening of the fingers that even honest people did when money left them.

The amount was generous. The amount was *more* than generous. It was the kind of generous that wasn't a fare at all but a purchase, and Bo, who had grown up poor enough to know the difference between a gift and a transaction dressed in a gift's clothing, felt the unease settle into him like cold water into a boot.

They were sitting in the harbor office now, the four of them plus the stranger, and the lamplight made everything look like a painting of itself. Oscar sat at the head of the table with the roll of bills in front of him, not touching it, the way a man sits across from a dog he's been told bites. Maren leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, watching Ilsa Voss with the flat, total attention she gave to approaching weather. Tomas stood at the window with his back to the room, which was what Tomas did instead of speaking. Priya had the chart open in front of her and was not looking at it.

"Passage to Sable Shoals," Oscar said. "One way?"

"One way," Ilsa said. "And back, if you're coming back."

"If the boat's coming back," Maren said from the door, "which is a different question."

Ilsa turned her head and looked at Maren, and Bo watched something pass between them that he didn't have a name for. Recognition, maybe, but not of faces—of *kinds*. Two women who had both learned to stand very still in bad conditions.

"You've been to the Shoals before," Ilsa said.

"Everyone at this table has been to the Shoals before," Maren said. "That's the problem with this table."

"Then you know what it costs to get there."

"I know what it *cost*," Maren said, and the word landed in the room like a dropped tool, and Bo saw Oscar's jaw tighten and Priya's pen finally, deliberately, begin to move.

Oscar pushed the bills back an inch, not returning them, just—measuring them. "What's in it for you, Ms. Voss? Nobody pays this kind of money for a ride. People pay this kind of money to make something happen. What's happening at Sable Shoals?"

And here was the thing Bo would remember, the thing that kept him awake: she didn't flinch, didn't deflect, didn't do any of the things a liar does. She looked Oscar Delacroix straight in his tired, haunted face and said, "Answers. I told your deckhand. I'm going to the Shoals to ask questions, and I'm paying you to carry me there while I ask them. That's the whole of it. You don't have to like it. You don't even have to believe it. You just have to sail."

"Questions about what?" Priya asked. Her voice was light, conversational, and Bo, who had shared a wheelhouse with Priya through two seasons of weather, heard the steel under it.

Ilsa Voss looked at each of them in turn—Oscar, Maren, Tomas's back, Priya, and last, lingering half a heartbeat longer, Bo—and Bo felt that look go through him like a sounding line going through dark water, measuring depth, finding bottom.

"A wreck," she said. "Six years ago. Out past the Shoals. A boat called the *Meridian's Daughter*."

The silence that followed had texture. Bo felt it on his skin like a change in pressure. Tomas turned from the window—not all the way, just his head, the profile of him carved out of the lamplight. Priya's pen stopped. Maren didn't move at all, which from Maren was the loudest sound in the room.

And Oscar—Oscar did the thing that Bo had only seen him do once before, the night a Vantor cousin had smiled at him across a dinner table and said the word *nineteen*—he went very still, and very pleasant, and said, "Old news. Boats go down. That one went down."

"Four men went down with it," Ilsa said. Her voice hadn't changed at all. It was still level, still reasonable, still the voice of a woman asking about a fare. "Two bodies were recovered. Two weren't." She let that sit. "Eli Delacroix was one of the two."

Bo heard the sound Oscar made. It wasn't a word. It was the sound a hull makes when it takes water over the rail—sudden, structural, involuntary—and then it was gone, swallowed, and Oscar's face was a closed door with a fresh coat of paint on it.

"You've done your research," Maren said quietly. It wasn't a question either, and it wasn't an accusation, but it stood in the room holding both of them like weapons it hadn't decided between.

"I've had six years," Ilsa said, "and nothing else to do with them."

* * *

They argued after she left. Bo stood at the sink washing cups that were already clean, which was what he did instead of arguing, because in his family the person who washed the dishes was the person who didn't have to choose sides, and Bo had made a religion of that position.

"She knows too much," Priya said, pacing the length of the office with the chart rolled in her fist like a baton. "The boat's name. The date, she knew the date, did anyone catch that? She said *six years ago in the October window*—nobody outside this crew knows we ran that concession in the October window. Nobody."

"The harbor master knows," Tomas said. "The permit office knows. Half the coast knows."

"Half the coast doesn't pay cash like that."

"Half the coast doesn't have her questions either," Maren said. She was still by the door. She hadn't moved since Ilsa left, and Bo wondered, watching her, whether Maren Kade was afraid of the woman or afraid *for* her, and whether there was any difference left, after six years, in Maren's private arithmetic.

"She's grieving," Bo said, into the middle of it.

They all looked at him. Even Tomas.

"She lost somebody," Bo said, setting down a cup. "You can see it. It's not—she's not a spy, she's not a creditor, she's not one of the Vantors' people, she's not any of the things we keep inventing so we have a reason to say no. She's a woman who lost somebody and the sea never gave her back a body, and she's spent six years walking toward the place where it happened. I know that walk. My father did it for eleven years after the *Almira* went down. He walked to every harbor that had ever heard her name. It's not a scheme. It's a wound with legs."

"That's poetry, Bo," Priya said, not unkindly. "Poetry doesn't pay for hull repairs."

"No," Bo agreed. "But cash does. And she's paying cash. And we're nineteen days from—" he stopped, because he'd seen the paper, everyone had seen the paper, the Vantors' terms sat in Oscar's jacket pocket like a stone in a hull. "We're nineteen days from a conversation none of us wants to have. She's the only money on the table that isn't Maren's, and Maren's money is tied to the job we swore off, and the job we swore off is the only job there is. Do the arithmetic yourself. I just wash the dishes."

The silence came back, but it was a different silence now, a considering one.

"She vouched for herself too easily," Tomas said at last, from the window. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in two days. "People who tell you exactly what they are, exactly when you need to know it—they've usually rehearsed."

"Or they're just honest," Bo said, "and we've been out here so long we've forgotten what it looks like."

Tomas looked at him. There was something in the engineer's face that Bo couldn't read—something old and tired and almost tender. "That's the problem with you, little brother," Tomas said. "You see the best in everybody. Someday it's going to be the truest thing about you and the last thing."

* * *

Bo went back down to the dock after midnight, because he couldn't sleep and because the Kestrel knocked against the pilings in a rhythm he found honest, and because a part of him—the part that watched instead of waited—wanted to see whether the harbor held anything else it hadn't told him.

She was there.

Ilsa Voss stood at the end of the pier, at the very tip, where the planks gave out and the black water began, with her coat collar up and the wind pulling her hair sideways, looking east toward the open sea. Toward the Shoals. Toward the weather that was still three days out and building, a long gray bruise on the horizon that the whole coast had been pretending not to see.

Bo walked down the planks slow, so she'd hear him coming, because that was courtesy and because he wanted to see what she did with the warning. She didn't turn. She just said, "Your crew is deciding whether to take my money," in the same level voice, "and your engineer is right about me. I have rehearsed."

Bo stopped a few paces off. "That's not what a rehearsed person says."

"No," she agreed. "It's what an honest one says when she's tired of the rehearsal." She turned then, and the harbor light caught her face, and Bo saw what he'd been seeing all evening without letting himself see it: not suspicion, not scheming, but the specific exhausted ferocity of a person who has been carrying something heavy for so long that the weight has become her posture, her walk, her way of standing at the end of a pier at midnight. "I'll tell you the truth, Bo Ferreira, since you're the only one who asked me a question instead of a challenge. I'm not going to Sable Shoals for passage. I'm going for names. There are two men who signed a weather report six years ago that was a lie, and that lie put a boat out into a storm that every honest forecast said would kill her, and I have spent six years getting close enough to those names to touch them. The concession is the door. Your boat is the key. That's all of it."

The wind came off the water and Bo felt it go through his jacket and into the place where he kept his own dead, his own father and the *Almira* and eleven years of harbors.

"And the crew?" Bo asked quietly. "The ones who were on that boat? The ones who survived her?"

Something moved behind Ilsa Voss's eyes—something that had been waiting all evening, patient as deep water.

"That," she said, "depends entirely on what they tell me when I ask."

Bo stood on the dark dock with the Kestrel knocking her honest rhythm behind him, and he thought: *I just vouched for her. I stood in that room and called her a wound with legs, and they listened to me, because I'm the one they listen to about hearts.* And he thought: *and she just told me, kindly, plainly, with all the courtesy of a paying passenger, that she is sailing with us straight into the middle of the thing we buried.*

The gray bruise on the horizon had come a little closer in the last hour. Bo could almost swear to it.

He walked her back up the pier, because that was the kind of man he was, and at the top of the planks she paused and said, "You have a kind face. It's why I told you." And then she was gone into the town's dark streets, and Bo stood alone with the wind, knowing that in the morning he would have to stand in front of Oscar Delacroix and say the words *I vouched for her* all over again—except that now, this time, he would have to say them knowing what they cost.
