## 8. Departure in Gray Light

The Kestrel left the dock at 4:40 in the morning, which was to say she left it in that hour when the harbor couldn't decide what color it wanted to be—black water going pewter, sky a bruised gray with a thin seam of rust along the east where the sun hadn't committed yet. Oscar stood at the wheel and let the town slide away behind them without looking back. Looking back was a habit he'd trained out of himself years ago, the way you train yourself out of checking your wallet in certain neighborhoods. You didn't look because looking made you count what you had, and counting what you had made you afraid of losing it, and fear slowed a man down at exactly the moments he needed speed.

Nineteen days. Seventeen now, if you counted from the signing. He counted anyway. It was like pressing a bruise—pointless, compulsive, weirdly comforting.

"Course is laid in," Priya said beside him, tapping the chart table where her pencil lines ran out past the headlands into open water. "We'll skirt the system's western edge. If the forecasts hold."

"If they hold," Oscar said.

"They won't." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, folding her glasses closed. "But they'll hold long enough."

Long enough. That was the whole religion of salvage work distilled to two words. Long enough to load, long enough to run, long enough to get paid before the sea or the Vantors collected first. Oscar had built a life on long enough, and the life had held the way a dam holds—not because it was strong but because so far the water hadn't gotten any higher.

He felt it getting higher this morning. Not the debt—that was a constant, a tide you stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. This was something else. Maren Kade stood at the bow rail with her hands folded on the top rail like a woman posing for a portrait nobody was painting, watching the monsoon's edge build itself on the horizon. The clouds out there weren't clouds yet, not really. They were an intention. A wall of gray stacked up ten miles high and forty wide, lit from underneath by lightning that flickered like something breathing behind a curtain. Beautiful the way a knife is beautiful. He'd spent half his life chasing that beauty and the other half paying for it.

"You're staring," Maren said, without turning around.

"So are you."

"I'm allowed. I'm a passenger."

"You're a client. Clients stay below decks and don't touch anything."

She turned then, and the gray light did something unkind to her face—hollowed the cheeks, silvered the short hair she'd started wearing cropped since the last time he'd seen her, three years ago in a customs office in Port Anselm where they'd nodded at each other across a room full of strangers and neither of them had crossed it. Ten years since the wreck. Ten years since the last time they'd stood on a deck together, and the deck had been burning oil-slicked water and a life raft with one person too few in it.

"Ten years," she said, as if she'd heard him thinking it, "and you still pilot like a man being chased."

"I am a man being chased."

"Not today. Today you're just sailing away from things. Different animal." She came aft along the rail, unhurried, testing her balance against the Kestrel's early-morning roll the way only someone who'd grown up on working boats could. "Chased men make decisions. Men running away make excuses. I've watched you do both, and I can always tell."

Oscar adjusted the wheel a degree he didn't need to adjust it. "You didn't come all this way to psychoanalyze my helming."

"No. I came because Sable Shoals is the only thing I've ever wanted that I couldn't take by force, and you're the only captain stupid enough and good enough to get me there before those permits die." She leaned her forearms on the rail beside him, close enough that he could smell coffee and rain on her jacket. "But while I'm here, I might as well psychoanalyze."

"Tomas has the engine warm," he said, ignoring her. "If we make the outer channel before six we'll have the tide with us the whole way to the shelf."

"Mm." She looked at him sideways. "You do that."

"Do what."

"Change the subject with logistics. You've been doing it for ten years. Every time anyone gets within a mile of Eli's name, suddenly there's a fuel calculation or a weather window or a hull inspection." Her voice wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It was patient, almost gentle, the voice of someone who had rehearsed this conversation alone for a decade and finally found herself trapped on a forty-foot boat with no exit. "I used to think it was grief. Now I think it's inventory management. You keep the feelings in separate crates and you never, ever open two at once."

Ahead of them the outer channel markers blinked red-green-red through the last of the dark, and beyond them the sea opened up flat and oily calm, the kind of calm that monsoon sailors called the lie. The storm's edge was still hours off, but you could feel it already, a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the air like the whole atmosphere holding its breath.

"Eli's name hasn't come up," Oscar said.

"No," Maren agreed. "It hasn't. Funny how that works."

* * *

By seven the wind had arrived ahead of the rain, a steady southeast push that put a following sea under the Kestrel's stern and let Tomas shut down to half throttle, saving fuel they'd need later. The boat settled into the rhythm she knew best—running hard with bad weather coming—and Oscar felt some knot between his shoulder blades loosen by a single degree. There was a version of himself that only existed underway, and it was the version he liked best: hands busy, horizon fixed, nothing required of him except the next hundred yards of water. Momentum as anesthesia. His brother used to say he was addicted to departure. Eli had said it laughing, the way he said everything, standing on a dock somewhere with his arms spread like the whole coast belonged to him. *You don't love boats, Oz. You love leaving.*

Oscar had laughed too, back then. He didn't laugh now.

Bo appeared with coffee in dented tin mugs, four of them balanced along one forearm with the gravity-defying ease of a boy who'd grown up carrying trays through a dockside canteen. He handed one to Oscar, one to Priya at the chart table, carried the third forward to Maren without being asked, which earned him a raised eyebrow from Oscar and a grin from Bo that said *what, she's crew-adjacent.* The fourth mug he kept, dropping onto the engine housing beside Tomas's hatch to drink it with his elbows on his knees, watching the water go by with the simple uncomplicated pleasure of a nineteen-year-old who had never once been chased by anything worse than a bar tab.

Oscar envied him the way you envy a dog its ignorance of mortality.

"How long to the shoals if the weather holds?" Bo asked, loud enough to carry forward.

"Four days at this pace," Priya answered. "Five if we have to run wide of the squall line. Six if the leak—" She stopped. Just stopped, mid-word, smooth as a hand closing over a card, and picked up her pencil and went back to the chart.

Oscar caught it. So did Tomas, whose head came up out of the engine hatch like a fox scenting something. Their eyes met across the deck for half a second, and Oscar filed it away in the crate marked LATER, because opening it now meant opening it in front of Maren, and there were only so many crates a man could have open at once.

Six days out, five days back, worst case. Eleven days against seventeen. It fit, barely, the way everything fit lately—barely, and only if nothing moved. But things always moved. Weather moved. Debts compounded. Leaks—

Leaks.

He drank his coffee and watched the horizon eat the distance and told himself the unease in his gut was just the pressure drop.

Maren came aft again around eight, when the first real rain bands began sweeping across the deck in cold gray curtains that lasted ninety seconds each and passed, each one a little heavier than the last. She stood under the dodger shaking water off her sleeves and watched him steer for a while in silence, and Oscar understood that the silence was tactical. She was letting him feel comfortable. It was the oldest trick in interrogation and she'd learned it from the same people he had.

"The buyer," she said eventually. "For the concession. Did I tell you who it is?"

"You told me it was a company."

"It is. Corvid Marine Resources." She watched his face when she said it, and he gave her nothing, because giving people nothing was the other thing he'd trained out of himself backwards—he'd trained himself *into* it. "You know them."

"Everyone knows them. They buy concessions all over the basin."

"They bought the concession six years ago too. The one Eli died running." She said it plainly, no blade in it, which somehow made the blade worse. "Same permit office. Same survey team. Same name on the counter-signature, even. I checked."

The wheel hummed under Oscar's hands. A rain band hit them broadside, cold enough to sting, and passed.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because on the dock you wouldn't stand still long enough to hear it. Because out here you can't walk away—you can only sail in circles, and I can follow you in circles for days." She took a sip of her coffee. "And because I want you to hear it from me before you hear it from whoever else is on this boat who knows more than they're saying."

His eyes went to the bow, where Ilsa Voss stood at the rail with her hood up, motionless, facing the storm edge like a woman waiting for a train. She'd been there an hour. She'd been at the rail most of the night, Bo said, watching the lightning.

"And who else would that be," Oscar said, not making it a question.

"You tell me. It's your boat." Maren set down her mug. "Ten years ago I promised you something, Oscar. On the water, that night. I promised I'd never say what I saw, and I haven't. Not to the board of inquiry, not to the insurance men, not to myself in the mirror at three in the morning. I have kept that promise so long it's grown roots." Her voice stayed level, but her knuckles were white around the rail. "I'm not breaking it. I want you to know that. Whatever happens out here, whatever she asks you, whatever comes up out of that water—I'm not breaking it."

"But?"

"But promises rot, same as everything else. And I need you to understand that when it breaks, it isn't going to be me swinging the hammer." She pushed off the rail and headed for the cabin door, then paused with her hand on it. "Get some sleep, Captain. You look like a man counting days."

"I'm not counting days."

"Liar," she said, almost fondly, and went below.

* * *

Noon came gray and windless under the storm's outer apron, the light gone strange and sourceless, shadows falling in every direction at once. They ate standing up—rice and salt fish out of the galley pot, the crew scattered along the leeward rail. Even Ilsa came in from the bow for it, pulling back her hood, and Oscar watched her across the deck while pretending not to. She ate neatly, quickly, like someone refueling rather than dining. When Bo offered her seconds she accepted with a smile that reached her mouth and stopped well short of her eyes.

Priya spread the afternoon watch rotation on the chart table and pinned it with a brass casing. Oscar took first, Priya second, Tomas third, Bo on call. Nobody assigned Maren or Ilsa a watch, and nobody discussed it, and the omission sat on the deck between them all like a third passenger.

At 12:40 Priya touched his elbow. "Come look at this."

She had the chart out and her finger on a position fix, the little cross where their dead-reckoned track crossed their last confirmed sight. "Barometer's been falling all morning. Faster than the forecast band should account for." She tapped the numbers she'd penciled down the margin, a column of readings like a pulse rate accelerating. "This system isn't tracking west-northwest anymore. It's turned."

"Turned how far?"

"South. Maybe south by southwest." She looked up at him, glasses back on, eyes flat and serious. "Which means it isn't flanking us, Oscar. We're not skirting its edge. We're sailing parallel to it, close aboard, and if it turns another twenty degrees we're inside it before we ever see the shoals."

Oscar studied the chart. The penciled track ran clean and confident toward Sable Shoals, and above it the storm symbol sat where the forecast had put it yesterday, fat and spiral-armed, and he understood suddenly that the map was already a historical document. The sea had voted. Maps were just the minority report.

"We watch it," he said. "Six-hour fixes. If it bends south of southwest, we run wide and lose a day."

"A day we don't have."

"A day we don't have," he agreed. "So let's hope the weather reads the same permits we do."

Priya almost smiled. Almost. She rolled the chart, and as she did, quietly, not looking at him: "Tomas wants to talk to you. About the pump."

The word went through Oscar like a fishhook. "What about the pump?"

"He'll tell you." She tucked the chart under her arm. "Soon."

She walked aft toward the engine hatch, and Oscar stood alone at the wheel with the gray light thickening around him and the barometer's ghost ticking in his ears, and he thought about crates. Feelings in crates, Maren had said, and she was right, she'd always been right, that was the curse of her. One crate for the debt. One for Eli. One for the promise Maren had kept for ten years and was now, unmistakably, beginning to loosen its lid. One for the stranger at the bow who paid in cash and questions. One for a pump that an engineer wanted to talk about *soon*, soon being a word that on a leaking boat meant the same thing it meant everywhere else: after it's too late to matter.

The first heavy rain band of the afternoon came across the water like a moving wall, and Oscar steered into it without slowing, because slowing was stopping and stopping was thinking and thinking was the one cargo the Kestrel could not afford to carry.

Behind them, low on the southern horizon, the storm's edge flexed and turned, patient as interest compounding, and began, degree by degree, to come about.
