## 9. First Squall

Tomas Reyes had a theory about leaks, and the theory was this: a boat never lies to you all at once. It tells you the truth in installments, a drip at a time, the way a guilty man confesses—first the small sins, then, when it's too late to turn back, the whole rotten ledger. The Kestrel had been confessing to him for three years. He'd learned her confessions by heart. He knew which seams wept in cold water and sealed themselves in warm, knew the way the stuffing box dripped twice a minute when she ran flat and went silent when she labored, knew the soft tick of water finding the limber holes behind the engine mounts. He could stand in the engine room with his eyes shut and read her like a man reading a lover's pulse.

Which was why, on the third morning out from harbor, with the monsoon's gray edge still a day off and the sky the color of old pewter, he did not like what she was telling him now.

He'd gone down at dawn for the morning check, same as every morning—oil level, belt tension, bilge soundings—and he'd found the water higher than it should have been. Not by much. Two inches over the mark he kept chalked inside the stringer, maybe three. Three inches in twelve hours was nothing, on most boats. On most boats you'd wipe it up and forget it. But the Kestrel wasn't most boats, and Tomas had spent three years learning exactly how much nothing she could hold.

He crouched at the stringer and touched the water. Cold. Colder than the sea outside, which meant it wasn't coming through the raw-water system, which meant it was coming through the hull itself. He put his palm flat against the planking below the waterline and held it there, eyes closed, listening with his skin the way other men listened with their ears.

There. Aft of the freshwater tank, where the hull curved up toward the transom knee—a seam that had been re-caulked twice before he ever owned a wrench on this boat. It was weeping. Not dripping. Weeping, the way a wall weeps after a flood, a fine continuous bead of water pushing through the caulking under pressure, and when he pressed harder he felt something no caulking should ever let him feel: give. A flex, small as a heartbeat, the hull breathing inward and back under the long rhythmic shove of the following swell.

The Kestrel was working herself apart, one heartbeat at a time, somewhere aft of the freshwater tank.

* * *

He didn't tell anyone. Not right away. That was the part he would think about later, in the nights that came after, when there was time enough for thinking—the way he'd stood there in the dim rocking light of the engine room with his hand on the seam and made a decision in about four seconds flat, the way a man steps over a sleeping dog rather than wake it.

Because here was what Tomas knew that the others didn't: they couldn't turn around. Nineteen days of permits, sixteen left now, and Oscar carrying debt the way other men carried shrapnel. Priya had spent two days plotting this route down to the tenth of a mile. Bo had stowed Ilsa Voss's cash payment in the chart drawer like it was a holy relic. And Maren Kade stood watches at the helm like a woman who had something to prove to the ocean itself. If Tomas walked up on deck and said the word *leak* with the word *structural* anywhere near it, Oscar Delacroix would turn the Kestrel around so fast the wake would slap them in the face, and everything—all of it, the debts, the permits, the concession, whatever ghost was riding this boat that nobody talked about—would come apart right there in open water.

So instead of saying anything, Tomas did what engineers do. He measured.

He chalked a new line on the stringer, lower than the old one, and wrote the time beside it in pencil: 0640. Then he sat down on his stool with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the water come, and it came the way it always comes, which is to say slowly and then not slowly at all. By 0900 the new line was submerged by half an inch. By noon, an inch. He did the arithmetic in his head while he pretended to service the raw-water impeller, and the arithmetic said this: at the current rate, with the manual bilge pump running twenty minutes out of every four hours—which was what he'd been doing anyway, disguised as routine maintenance—they stayed even. Barely. Exactly. The kind of even that lasts until anything goes wrong.

And things, on the Kestrel, in monsoon season, were constitutionally inclined to go wrong.

* * *

The squall came up from the southwest at midafternoon, and it came the way squalls do in these waters—out of a clear-ish sky, or clear-ish for the leading edge of a monsoon, a black bruise of cloud walking across the water on legs of rain. Priya spotted it first and called it down the companionway in her flat navigator's voice, the voice she used for facts: "Squall line, bearing two-four-zero, moving fast, maybe forty minutes out."

Oscar took the wheel himself. Of course he did. Tomas watched him come alive the way he always came alive when the weather turned—the set of his shoulders changing, some animal part of him standing up inside the tired man. Storm chaser. That was the word people used, and it fit, but Tomas had a private theory about that too: that Oscar didn't chase storms so much as outrun them, and that the thing he was really running from had been sitting quietly in his chest for six years wearing a brother's name.

"Reef the main," Oscar called. "Bo, take the jib down to the storm canvas. Tomas—" and here he paused, just a beat, looking down the companionway at him, and Tomas felt the look go through him like a spike, because Oscar Delacroix missed nothing on a boat, absolutely nothing except, God willing, chalk lines and pencil times, "—batten everything. I want her tight."

"She's tight," Tomas called back, and heard his own voice come out steady, and marveled a little at the craftsmanship of the lie.

They got the sails down with twenty minutes to spare, and then the squall hit them, and the world went white.

It wasn't the monsoon yet—that was still a day out, a vast slow animal turning on the horizon—but this was its herald, its outrider, and it hit the Kestrel with thirty-five knots of wind and a solid wall of rain that erased the ocean entirely. The boat heeled, recovered, heeled again. Bo laughed into the wind—he was the only human being Tomas had ever met who laughed into wind—and hauled the last line home. Maren stood at the mast without being asked, feet planted, doing the work of two men, and Tomas thought, not for the first time, that whatever history lived between her and Oscar had been built out of storms exactly like this one.

Down below, the Kestrel began to tell him more truth.

He felt it first through the soles of his boots—a change in the rhythm of the hull's working, the flex aft of the freshwater tank opening wider with each slam, each recovery, the boat twisting along her length the way boats twist in a quartering sea. Then he heard it: the weep becoming a seep becoming, briefly, during the worst slam, a gurgle. He swung the flashlight onto the stringer.

The water was above his noon line. Above it by four inches, in ninety minutes of weather.

His stomach did something cold and slow. He knelt, put his hand on the seam, and felt the give again—wider now, a flex he could see with his eyes if he watched the caulking line, a hair-thin gap opening and closing, opening and closing, like a mouth trying to speak. Somewhere under six layers of old paint and thirty years of repairs, a frame was cracked, or a garboard strake was letting go, or both, and every hour of pounding sea was asking the question *how much longer* and getting an answer he didn't want translated.

He started the manual pump.

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding a leak on a small boat: the pump is loud. The diaphragm clacks, the water slaps through the hose, and any crew worth its saltwater knows the sound of their own bilge pump the way they know their own name. So Tomas rationed it. Twenty minutes on, two hours off, timed for when the deck noise covered him—when Bo was hauling lines, when the wind filled everyone's ears, when Oscar was at the wheel with his whole soul in the compass. He pumped the level back down to his original chalk line, killed the pump, wiped the housing dry, and sat in the dark listening to the squall spend itself overhead, doing new arithmetic.

New numbers: in sustained weather, the leak gained on them. In calm, they held even. Which meant the entire run to Sable Shoals now depended on a forecast, and the forecast was a monsoon, and the monsoon was a day out and the size of a country.

* * *

The squall passed in under an hour, the way squalls do, leaving behind it a washed green light and a sea still heaving from the argument. Oscar held course. Nobody suggested otherwise; the permits were burning whether they ran or hid.

At dusk Tomas came up on deck with the excuse of checking the rigging, and found Bo coiling line in the fading light, humming. Bo always hummed. It was unnerving and endearing in equal measure, the hum of a man with no secrets, and Tomas felt the weight of his own secret shift in his chest like ballast.

"How's she sounding down there?" Bo asked, cheerful. "She always talks more in weather."

"She talks," Tomas said. "Nothing she hasn't said before."

Bo grinned at him, and for one horrible second Tomas thought the boy saw straight through him—Bo had that quality, an openness that worked like sonar on other people's concealments—but the grin was only a grin, and Bo went back to his coils, and Tomas stood at the rail watching the western sky.

The monsoon was there. You could see it now even in the dark, a blackness deeper than night, a wall of cloud from the water to heaven with lightning flickering inside it like thoughts in a closed mind. It was beautiful. Tomas had grown up on this coast, and he knew the local word for that kind of beauty, a word his grandmother used for the sea when it gave and took in the same motion—a word that translated roughly to *the thing you cannot argue with.*

Sixteen days of permits. Five days of running, best case, if the weather cooperated. And a hull that had just told him, in the plain language of chalk and water, that it would not survive sustained weather without help.

There were options. He inventoried them honestly, engineer-fashion. One: tell Oscar, turn back, lose everything, watch the Vantors collect whatever the Vantors collected. Two: tell Oscar, propose pushing on with round-the-clock pumping, and pray the monsoon's edges were softer than they looked. Three: say nothing, manage the leak himself, keep the boat even until Sable Shoals, and carry the weight alone.

He chose three. He told himself it was one and two combined, really, a deferred version, a tactical silence. He told himself he'd speak up if it worsened past a line he drew in his head—a real line, quantified, an inch per hour, that was the threshold, engineering had thresholds. He told himself that a secret managed was not a secret kept.

All of which was true, and none of which changed the fact that when he went below that night and chalked a fresh line at 2200 hours, the water was already lapping at it, patient as interest accruing, and the monsoon on the horizon flickered its private lightning, and somewhere aft of the freshwater tank the Kestrel's cracked heart opened and closed, opened and closed, keeping time.

* * *

He almost told Maren.

That was the strange part, thinking back. Not Oscar—Oscar would have turned the boat, and turning the boat was death by another name, slower but just as certain. Not Priya, who would have recalculated the entire voyage around the flaw and found the flaw won. Not Bo, who would have carried the knowledge the way he carried everything, openly, and it would have shown on his face within the hour. But Maren—Maren Kade, who had stood a decade on the wrong side of a secret and knew exactly what a held truth cost, who might understand a rationed confession better than anyone alive.

He found her on the midnight watch, alone at the helm, the self-steering disengaged because she trusted her own hands more than any vane. The cockpit light was off. She steered by the loom of the storm ahead and the faint stars behind it, and she looked, in the dark, like a figurehead that had climbed down off the bow and taken the wheel.

Tomas handed her a mug of coffee and stood beside her for a long time without speaking. The Kestrel drove on. Below his feet, faintly, he could hear the water in the bilge sloshing at its chalk line, and he willed it silent, absurdly, as though the boat could be embarrassed into holding still.

"You've been down there a lot," Maren said at last. Not accusing. Observing, in the flat tone of a woman who had spent ten years observing things she never reported.

"Pump maintenance," Tomas said. "Pre-storm."

"Mm," said Maren.

The wind shifted a point. She corrected the helm without looking at it, small movements, economical, and Tomas understood suddenly why Oscar had never beaten her to a finish line, back in the years when they raced. She didn't fight the sea. She negotiated with it.

"The monsoon turns tomorrow," she said. "Maybe the day after. When it turns, it'll either miss us by eighty miles or swallow us whole, and I genuinely don't know which." She glanced at him sideways. "So whatever you're rationing down there, Tomas—ration it well."

His blood went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

And that was all. She faced forward again, and the Kestrel ran on into the dark toward a wall of storm that was deciding, hour by hour, whether to love them or eat them, and Tomas went below with his coffee untouched and lay in his bunk listening to the drip, drip, drip of the only honest member of the crew, and drew his line in his head one final time:

An inch an hour. Past that, I tell them. Past that, whatever happens happens.

At 0400, checking by flashlight, careful as a thief, he found the water two inches past the line.

And far to the west, silent as a held breath, the monsoon began to turn.
