## 10. Ilsa's Questions

The Kestrel smelled of diesel, salt, and old arguments, and Ilsa Voss catalogued all three the way she had learned to catalogue everything: as evidence.

She stood at the rail on the second morning out, watching the gray water unspool behind them, and let the crew forget she was there. That was the trick of it. People forgot you if you were quiet enough, useful enough, unremarkable enough. Her father had taught her that before he taught her anything else about the sea. *A passenger is furniture until she asks a question,* he used to say, laughing, and then he had gone out one night in weather he should have refused, on a boat that should have stayed tied, because a man with a signature told him the storm would hold off until morning.

Six years now. Six years of learning to be furniture.

Bo found her first, the way Bo found everyone. The deckhand had a way of appearing at your elbow with coffee or a rag or a question of his own, as if kindness were a job he'd been assigned and took seriously. He handed her a mug without asking if she wanted one.

"You're up early," he said.

"I don't sleep well on boats."

"Liar. You sleep like a stone. I checked on you last night when I came off watch—you were out cold in that bunk like the sea was your own bed."

Ilsa smiled at him over the rim of the mug. Bo was the soft place in this crew's armor, and she was not so proud that she didn't feel a flicker of shame at how she meant to use it. She let the shame sit, the way she let everything sit. Feeling it and doing it anyway had become, over six years, the same gesture.

"My brother used to say that about me," she said. "That I could sleep through anything."

"Yeah? Older or younger?"

"Younger. He was the responsible one." She watched the horizon, where the monsoon's edge had begun to assemble itself, a bruise-colored wall that hadn't been there yesterday. "He worked the water. Salvage, mostly. The dangerous kind."

Bo leaned on the rail beside her, close enough that she could smell the coffee on him and the engine grease under the coffee. "Dangerous how?"

"Storm work. You know the type. The crews that go out when everyone else is running in." She let a pause open up, the length of a breath, the length of a wave. "He died that way. Six years ago. A boat went down off the monsoon coast with a crew of four. He was one of them."

Bo went still. She felt it happen, the small seismic event of a man recalculating. "That's—there were a lot of wrecks that year. The season was bad."

"This one was in August. A boat called the *Cormorant*."

The name landed the way she'd known it would. Bo set his mug down on the rail with exaggerated care, as if his hands had suddenly become unreliable equipment. "I know that name," he said. "Everybody on this coast knows that name. That was—"

"Oscar Delacroix's brother," Ilsa said, gently, as if offering him the fact rather than taking it from him. "Eli Delacroix. Yes. I know."

* * *

The thing about grief, Ilsa had learned, was that it made you a better liar. Not because grief taught you to lie—because grief taught you what truth cost, and once you knew the price of a thing you could spend counterfeits without flinching. She had spent six years learning the shape of the *Cormorant*'s last night the way other people learned a language: the weather report filed at 16:40 that promised the system would track south; the order signed at 17:15 sending the boat out anyway; the report's amendment, quietly filed three weeks later, quietly buried deeper, that corrected the storm's track to north. The company that filed it. The man who signed. She had the paper trail down to the ink.

What she didn't have was the water. The human part. The part that lived in the mouths of the people who had been there, and who had spent six years not saying it.

She found Priya at the nav table that afternoon, bent over charts with a pencil behind her ear, and asked her question the way a surgeon asks *does this hurt*—lightly, watching the eyes.

"You've run this coast a long time," Ilsa said, standing in the doorway of the nav shack with two cups of tea she didn't intend to drink. "You'd remember a bad season."

"Remember it?" Priya didn't look up. "I navigate around it. Every August I reroute like it's a reef." She took the tea anyway, which Ilsa noted: Priya accepted offerings, catalogued them, gave nothing back for free. "Why?"

"The *Cormorant*," Ilsa said. "August, six years back. You'd have been running then."

Priya's pencil stopped.

It stopped for exactly one second—long enough for a woman who had trained herself to count seconds to count it—and then it resumed, tracing a coastline that didn't need tracing. "Everyone remembers the *Cormorant*," Priya said. "It's not a secret. It's a story people tell in bars to make each other shut up about the weather."

"What's the story they tell?"

"That the storm turned." Priya looked up then, and her eyes were flat and careful, a navigator's eyes reading Ilsa the way they'd read a squall line. "That the report said south and the storm went north, and the boat was in the wrong place at the wrong time, and the sea doesn't care whose fault anything is. That's the story. It's even mostly true."

"Mostly."

"The sea part is true. The sea doesn't care." Priya set the tea down untouched and went back to her chart. "The rest of it you'd have to ask someone who was there. I wasn't."

"You were on this coast."

"I was on this coast." The pencil pressed a little harder. "And I'll tell you what I told every reporter and every insurance investigator and every drunk in every bar for a year afterward: the crew that went out that night were good sailors, and good sailors don't go out into a north-tracking system because a piece of paper told them it was going south. Either they didn't know, or they were told wrong, or—" she paused, the pencil hovering, "—or they were told it didn't matter. And I don't know which, because I wasn't there, and the people who were there don't talk about it. Including the man who owns this boat. So if you're asking what I think you're asking, you should know that this crew runs because we don't dig. We leak, we owe money, we chase weather, but we don't dig. Digging is how boats sink twice."

Ilsa stood in the doorway with her two cups of tea and felt the past close around the little shack like weather. *The people who were there don't talk about it.* But they talked around it. That was the thing about the truth—it leaked. This whole boat leaked. Tomas pumped it every hour and said nothing. Priya charted around the subject and said nothing. And Oscar Delacroix, who had hired her passage with barely a glance, who hadn't asked her a single question about herself in two days at sea—Oscar said nothing so completely, so elaborately, that it was practically a confession with the words removed.

* * *

Tomas was harder. Tomas was an engine room, and engine rooms didn't run on conversation.

She went down on the pretext of a rattle in her bunk's bracket, which was even true, and found him elbow-deep in the bilge pump with his sleeves rolled and a wrench in his teeth, and she watched him work for a while because watching was free and Tomas forgot her when he worked. The pump was running. She noticed that it was always running, or nearly always, and that Tomas checked it the way a man checks a wound he's hiding from his family.

"That pump works hard," she said.

"It's a boat. Boats leak." He said it without looking up, in the tone of a man reciting scripture.

"Even this one?"

"Especially this one." He spat the wrench into his hand and finally looked at her, and his eyes had the flat defensive shine of a man who had already decided what she was. "You want to ask me something, ask it. Bo says you've been asking questions. Priya says you've been asking questions. I don't like questions I can hear through the hull."

Ilsa sat down on an upturned crate, making herself small, making herself harmless. "My brother died on a boat called the *Cormorant*," she said. "Six years ago. I'm not a reporter. I'm not insurance. I just want to know what happened to him, and everyone on this boat was on this coast when it happened, and nobody will say more than three words about it."

Tomas was quiet for a long time. The pump chugged. Somewhere above them, Bo's footsteps crossed the deck, light and unhurried, a man with no secrets in his stride.

"Three words," Tomas said finally. "Here's your three words. The report lied." He went back to the pump. "That's not a secret either. The whole coast knew the report lied. What nobody knows—what the investigators never got—is *who made it lie*. Company filed the correction three weeks late, took a fine, buried it. The man who signed the sailing order that night, the one that put them out with the storm already turned—" his jaw worked, "—that name never surfaced. Paper trail goes into the company and stops. Like the signature was a ghost."

"Do you have a theory?"

"I have a rule." Tomas wiped his hands on a rag that was more oil than rag. "My rule is the same as Priya's. We don't dig. You know why? Because six years ago a man on this coast started asking who signed that order, a salvage broker, decent fellow, asked too loudly in too many places. Lost every contract he had inside a month. Left the coast. Nobody's asked since." He looked at her, and for the first time there was something in his face besides the flat defense—something that might, in a better light, have been pity. "You're asking. I can hear it through the hull. So I'll tell you the last thing for free: whatever you think you're going to find on this boat, understand that the man who owns it loses more than contracts if you find it. He loses the story he's been living in. And men defend their stories harder than they defend their lives."

* * *

Evening came down gray and thick, the monsoon's edge now a definite wall to the south, and the Kestrel ran north of it with the deliberate, strained calm of a herd animal that has smelled the cat. Ilsa stood at the stern rail and watched Oscar Delacroix come up from the cabin.

He moved like a man who had measured every route off a boat and never taken one. She had watched him for two days now and the watching had confirmed what the file had suggested: this was not a man at peace with the sea. This was a man using the sea the way a runner uses a road—something to be fast on, something to be gone along. He checked the horizon the way other men checked a clock. He never looked astern. She had tested it. Twice she had stood where he'd have to look past her to see the wake, and both times he had found something urgent to do with the rigging.

"Mr. Delacroix," she said, when he passed close enough.

He stopped. Up close he was younger than his reputation and older than his face, and his eyes had the specific exhaustion of a man counting days. "Miss Voss."

"My brother was on the *Cormorant*," she said.

The sea made its small sounds against the hull. Somewhere forward, Bo laughed at something Priya said, the sound carrying across the water like a light in a window.

"I know," Oscar said.

"You know."

"Bo told me this morning." He said it evenly, but she saw his hand close on the rail, knuckles going pale, and she thought: *there*. There was the seam in the hull. Six years of pumping and the water still came through. "I'm sorry. It was a bad night. He was a good man—Eli. Everyone said so."

"You knew him?"

"Everyone knew everyone, on that coast, back then." He turned to face the bow. The wall of weather was off the starboard quarter now, and he looked at it the way a man looks at an old dog that once bit him. "I'm sorry for your loss. It was a long time ago."

"Six years." She kept her voice level, almost gentle. "The report said the storm would track south. It went north. The boat went out anyway. Somebody signed the order. The company corrected the report three weeks later and paid a fine the size of a rounding error. That's the part that's a long time ago, Mr. Delacroix. The part where it stopped mattering to everyone whose brother wasn't on the boat."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful in a way that told her more than shouting would have. "What is it you want, Miss Voss? Exactly. Passage, you said. But people who buy passage to Sable Shoals in cash and ask questions like these—they want something else. So say it. I'd rather know."

*There it is,* she thought. *The direct question, at last. The one thing he can't outrun.*

"I want the name of the man who signed the order," she said. "That's all. That's the whole cargo."

Something moved behind his face—something fast and drowned and gone before she could hold it—and then he was only a tired man on a leaking boat with a storm on his quarter and nineteen days' worth of trouble in his pocket.

"Then you should hope you never find it," Oscar Delacroix said, and walked forward into the gray, not looking back, not once, and Ilsa stood at the rail and understood with a certainty that felt like cold water rising that the name she had crossed an ocean for was standing forty feet away from her, pumping it down every hour, and calling it a leak.

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone, the storm's wall had begun to bend—curving north, off its filed track, the way weather does when it has its own signature and no one aboard has thought to check the charts since noon.
