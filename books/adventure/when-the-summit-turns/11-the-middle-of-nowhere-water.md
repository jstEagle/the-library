## 11. The Middle of Nowhere Water

The wind died on the third day out from the Gray Light anchorage, and it died the way Priya's mother used to say bad news arrived—not all at once, but in a slow draining, a going-out like breath held too long.

She felt it first in her feet. Ten years on the water had taught her body to read the Kestrel before her instruments did: the faint tremor through the deck plates when the swell organized itself into long rollers, the way the halyards went slack against the mast with a sound like someone sighing. By the time she climbed into the cockpit at dawn, mug of cold tea in hand, the sea had gone flat as poured tin and the color of an old bruise, green-black at the troughs, oily where the light caught it.

"Dead calm," Bo said from the rail, stating the obvious with the reverence he gave everything.

"I can see that."

"No, I mean—" He gestured at the horizon, at the enormous nothing of it. "It's beautiful."

Priya looked at the horizon and saw four days of fuel burned for nothing, a chart plotter full of waypoints she no longer trusted, and a schedule built on the assumption that they would outrun the monsoon rather than sit beneath it like a target. She did not see beautiful. But she said, "Sure," because Bo was the kind of person who found beauty in things that were trying to kill them, and someone on this boat had to be wrong about something cheerful.

* * *

By noon the heat was a physical presence, pressing down through the cockpit canopy, turning the cabin into a low oven that smelled of diesel, mildew, and Tomas's engine grease. The Kestrel rolled on a swell that came from nowhere and went nowhere, a lazy, sick-making motion, and Priya sat below at the nav station with the charts spread around her like the entrails of some sacrificed animal, checking work she had already checked twice.

That was the thing about dead calm. It gave you time. And time, on this boat, with this cargo of secrets, was not a neutral substance. Time was a solvent. It dissolved the varnish off things.

She ran the numbers again.

Departure: 0540, day one. Course to Sable Shoals: 214 true, then a dogleg east around the Corvane Shelf shallows, then south-southeast along the shelf edge to the concession marker buoy. Total distance: 610 nautical miles. At their planned average of seven knots—optimistic for the Kestrel, but Maren had insisted the weather window made it possible—they should have been two hundred miles further along by now.

Instead, the GPS showed 118 miles made good. And the track—

Priya pulled up the electronic track and stared at it the way you'd stare at your own handwriting on a confession you didn't remember signing.

The track bent. Not much. On the plotter's scale, with the whole route displayed, the deviation was almost invisible—a shallow sag to the west, a few degrees held too long, corrected late, corrected again. Anyone glancing at it would call it leeway, current set, the ordinary slop of a small boat beating through confused seas during the squall on day two.

But Priya did not glance at tracks. She read them the way other people read faces.

Current didn't do this. Current pushed you sideways uniformly; it didn't bend your course and then let it straighten. Leeway didn't do this; leeway was consistent for a given wind and hull. And she had hand-steered through the worst of the squall herself, Priya had, both watches, because Oscar had been aft arguing with Maren in voices too low to carry and someone had to keep the bow pointed somewhere sensible. She knew what course she'd held. She'd held 218 through the gusts and 206 in the lulls, and the average was exactly what the plan called for.

The track said otherwise. The track said that for nearly three hours on the second night—the hours when she'd been off watch, asleep in the forepeak with the hatch shut against the spray—the boat had been steered, or navigated, or logged, along a line that did not match any line she had chosen.

She sat very still and listened to the sounds of the boat: Tomas in the engine room, swearing softly at something in Tagalog and English both; Bo on deck, humming; the tick of the plotter; the creak of the hull rolling on its nowhere swell. Five people aboard. Six counting the stranger.

Ilsa Voss was up top, reading. Priya could see her through the companionway, folded onto the leeward bench with a paperback she'd brought aboard wrapped in plastic, her pale face tilted down, her stillness so complete she might have been part of the boat's fittings. She paid cash. She asked questions. She knew things about wrecks six years old that she shouldn't know.

Priya closed her eyes and made herself think it plainly, without the cushioning language she usually wrapped around ugly thoughts: *Someone fed us a false waypoint.*

Not necessarily Ilsa. That was the trap her mind wanted to spring—to point at the stranger, the outsider, the woman who appeared at the dock with cash and questions, and let the suspicion settle there like silt. Easy. Comforting, even, in its way, because if it was Ilsa then it wasn't family, and if it wasn't family then the boat hadn't rotted from inside.

But Priya had loaded the route herself. She remembered doing it, back at the dock, the night before departure—remembered the dockside lamp flickering, remembered Bo bringing coffee, remembered Maren standing at the foot of the gangway talking to Oscar in that voice she used, low and level, like a blade being drawn slowly. She remembered entering the waypoints one by one, cross-checking each against the paper chart, initialing the log.

Did she remember? Or did she remember remembering? Three nights of broken sleep stood between her and that moment now, and memory under sleep debt was a chart drawn from hearsay.

She got up, took the paper chart folio from the shelf above the berth, and spread the Sable Shoals approach sheet on the table, weighting the corners with mugs.

The plotted route on the paper matched the plotter. Of course it did. She'd drawn it herself, same night, same tired hands. A false waypoint entered once would have shown here as a kink, a jog, a line that touched water it shouldn't. There was nothing. The pencil line ran clean from departure to the shelf dogleg to the buoy.

Which meant either the route had always been clean and something else had bent their track—or the route had been altered after she drew it, and altered on the paper too, which meant a steadier hand than hers and a colder nerve.

Or—which was the thought she kept circling like a bird over a fish shoal, unable to strike—there was no alteration at all, and the deviation was real, and innocent, and she was a tired woman in a hot box seeing patterns in noise because fear draws patterns the way water draws channels: inevitably, and toward the lowest ground.

She heard footsteps on the companionway ladder and didn't turn around fast enough to arrange her face.

Maren Kade filled the doorway, a mug in each hand, gray-eyed and unhurried as ever. "You've been down here six hours," she said. "Even cartographers eat."

"I'm not hungry." Priya took the mug anyway, because refusing Maren's coffee was a longer conversation than accepting it. The coffee was terrible, burnt, made with water from the tank that tasted of the tank. She drank it.

Maren leaned against the bulkhead and looked at the charts spread out, at the plotter screen still glowing with the bent track. She looked for perhaps two seconds too long.

"Problem?"

"Drift," Priya said. "Checking our drift against current tables."

"Mm." Maren's gaze stayed on the screen a moment more, then lifted. "Find anything?"

"Not yet."

There it was again—that fractional pause, that half-second where Maren's face did something Priya couldn't parse, some adjustment behind the eyes like a helmsman correcting for a gust no one else had felt yet. Maren Kade had been on the water the night Eli Delacroix died. Everyone on this crew knew it in the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked: nobody talks about it, everybody routes around it. And now Maren was standing over the navigation records asking gentle questions with a face like a locked drawer.

"You've been quiet since the squall," Maren said.

"It's a quiet ocean."

"PRIYA!" Bo's voice from the deck, delighted, urgent. "Come look at this!"

* * *

What Bo wanted her to look at was a waterspout, far off to the northeast, a white rope connecting sea to cloud, wandering drunkenly across the middle distance like a drunk walking home. It was genuinely spectacular. Priya gave it thirty seconds of professional appreciation, confirmed it was ten miles off and moving away, and then used the climb into the cockpit to study her crew.

Oscar stood at the helm even though there was nothing to steer, hands on the wheel the way a man holds a railing over a long drop. He hadn't slept well; she could see it in the grit at the corners of his eyes, in the way he flinched when the rigging ticked. Since the squall he'd been watching Maren, and since the squall Maren had been watching him, and the two of them conducted entire conversations in the language of not-looking-at-each-other, fluent as eels.

Tomas surfaced from the engine room wiping his hands on a rag that had started life white sometime last year. He smiled at the spout, said something about how his grandmother believed waterspouts were the sea fishing for the sky, and went back below. His smile reached his eyes. It always did. That was the problem with Tomas—you couldn't read anything in him because he gave everything away equally.

Bo was Bo, radiant over the spout, guileless, open as a window. If Bo were the traitor, he'd have to be the best actor alive or the most oblivious, and Priya genuinely could not decide which possibility frightened her more.

And Ilsa Voss sat on the leeward bench with her paperback, and when Priya's gaze crossed hers, Ilsa looked up—calmly, immediately, the way a person looks up when they've been aware of being watched the whole time—and said, "Is it dangerous?"

"The spout? No. Too far."

"That's not what I asked."

Priya held her gaze. Ilsa Voss had a face built for waiting rooms and border crossings, a face that gave nothing and requested nothing, and behind it moved a mind Priya could feel working like something swimming under a boat at night—you never saw it, only the wake.

"No," Priya said. "Nothing's dangerous. Yet."

Isla nodded slowly, as if Priya had confirmed something on a list. "In my experience," she said, "the calm is worse. In the calm, you find out what the storm already knows about you."

Then she went back to her book, and Priya stood in the killing heat with sweat running down her spine and thought: *that is not a sentence a passenger says.* That is a sentence a person says who has been in the calm before, on the other side of it, looking for something the storm buried.

* * *

They burned the fourth day. Then the fifth.

The monsoon system they'd been flanking sat on the horizon to the west like a siege army, building by afternoon, towering, lightning stitching inside the cloud in silent silver veins, and every hour of calm was another hour it grew while they sat becalmed above a fuel supply Tomas rationed with the grim tenderness of a man feeding a sick animal. They motored at minimum revs to hold position and make a knot or two of southing. The Kestrel rolled. The heat pressed. The permits in Oscar's strongbox aged like milk.

On the fifth night, unable to sleep in the forepeak's oven, Priya came back to the nav station and did what she should have done on the first day: she stopped trusting the electronics entirely and went back to first principles. Celestial. Dead reckoning. The old arithmetic of speed, time, and heading written in pencil in the log book, page after page of her own handwriting, the most honest record aboard because she'd written every entry herself, at the end of every watch, from her own eyes and instruments.

She worked backward through the log entries from the second night. Her own entry at midnight: position, course, distance run—all consistent. The entry at 0400, end of the middle watch—

She frowned.

The 0400 entry was in her handwriting. Of course it was; she wrote all the log entries, that was the arrangement, that had been the arrangement for three years. But the figures were wrong. Not dramatically—wrong the way a forged signature is wrong, right enough to pass a glance, wrong to anyone who knew the hand. The slant of the numerals was a degree steeper. The 4s were closed at the top; she left hers open. Someone had written a log entry in her hand, in her book, at a position forty miles west of where the boat actually was, and had then carried that false position forward into the plotter, waypoint by waypoint, building a route to a place that was not the place.

Her mouth went dry in a way the heat couldn't account for.

Forty miles west of the direct approach to Sable Shoals lay the Corvane Shelf's northern fingers—uncharted shoal water, the drowned ribs of an old volcanic ridge, the kind of ground that opened hulls like cans. A boat following the falsified track, trusting the falsified log, would arrive at the dogleg believing she had forty miles more sea room than she had, and would stand on into water that went from forty fathoms to four fathoms in the length of a song.

Someone aboard was not steering them wrong by accident. Someone aboard was steering them onto the reef.

Priya sat in the lamplight with the log book open and felt the boat around her—the creak, the roll, Tomas's snore through the engine room bulkhead, Bo's breathing from his bunk, the small honest sounds of sleeping people—and understood that one of those sounds belonged to something else entirely.

She thought of Ilsa's precise questions. She thought of Maren's locked-drawer face. She thought of Oscar, who owed men who broke legs cheerfully, and of what a man drowning in debt might do for a payoff—or what might be done to him. She thought of the wreck six years ago, the one nobody talked about, and of the fact that the only two people who knew what happened that night were both on this boat, circling each other like weather fronts.

She closed the log book quietly.

Above her, through the deck, she heard footsteps cross the cockpit—soft, deliberate, barefoot—and stop directly over the nav station. They stayed there a long moment. Then they moved away, toward the stern, and a match rasped, and through the companionway she saw, briefly, the orange bloom of a cigarette cupped in two hands, and the silhouette of the smoker turned not toward the sea but back toward the cabin, toward the dark square of the companionway, as if checking whether anyone was awake.

The figure was too slight to be Oscar. Too tall to be Ilsa.

The glow rose and fell, rose and fell, patient as a lighthouse, and Priya sat motionless in the dark with her hand flat on the falsified log and did not breathe until the match died.

* * *

At dawn the wind returned.

It came out of the southeast, warm and wet and wrong—a monsoon feeder, the leading edge of the system they'd spent five days failing to outrun, filling in behind the calm like water filling a footprint. The barometer, which had hung at 1008 for two days, began to fall: 1005 by breakfast, 1002 by midmorning, steady, purposeful, a countdown.

Priya came on deck with the log book under her arm and the truth of it settled in her chest like ballast. She watched the crew take in the returning wind—Oscar's face lifting into it with something like relief, because motion meant hope to a man built like him; Maren studying the western sky with a navigator's respect; Bo laughing as the first real breeze hit the sweat on his neck; Tomas already at the throttle, ready to spend fuel they didn't have.

Five people. One of them had written lies in her handwriting. One of them wanted the Kestrel on the Corvane Shoals, and had come within a night's steaming of getting her there.

"Course two-one-four," Priya said aloud, loud enough for all of them, her voice level, her eyes moving from face to face. "Direct approach. No dogleg. I'm re-plotting everything from today forward, and I'll be hand-steering the night watches myself."

A pause. The wind filled it.

"Problem?" Oscar asked.

"No," Priya said, meeting his eyes, then Maren's, then Ilsa's, then the others'. "Just making sure everyone's using the same map."

She turned for the companionway, and behind her she heard—Isla's voice, soft, almost pleasant, pitched only for her:

"Smart. The last navigator who trusted the wrong chart on this run didn't get to finish the trip."

Priya stopped with her hand on the ladder rail, the log book heavy under her arm, and did not turn around, because whatever Ilsa Voss saw in her face next, she wasn't ready to show it yet.
