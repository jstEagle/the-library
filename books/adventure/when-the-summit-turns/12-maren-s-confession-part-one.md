## 12. Maren's Confession, Part One

Maren Kade had learned a long time ago that guilt was not a weight you carried. It was a debt you serviced. You paid it down in installments, small and regular, never the whole sum at once, because the whole sum would crush you flat, and a crushed woman could not steer a boat through the Sable Shoals approach in monsoon season.

She had been paying for ten years. Tonight she would make another payment.

She found Oscar at the stern rail after midnight, when the sea had gone strange and glassy under a sky the color of wet slate. The heat was wrong for this latitude, this hour—thick, breathless, the kind of stillness that Priya kept calling *the calm before the middle of nowhere* and that Tomas refused to name at all. The Kestrel groaned beneath them, her patched hull ticking as the metal cooled, and somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat, the bilge pump cycled on. Maren counted the seconds of its run without meaning to. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Too long. She filed that away with everything else she'd been filing away since they'd cleared the harbor, a growing folder of things she'd deal with later, or never.

Oscar didn't turn when her boots hit the deck. He'd known it was her. Ten years hadn't dulled that animal awareness between them—the way two people who had once shared a wheelhouse could read each other's footsteps over engine noise and wind.

"Can't sleep either," he said. Not a question. He kept his eyes on the horizon, where lightning flickered inside a cloud bank like something trapped and trying to get out.

"I sleep fine." She came to stand beside him, close enough to share the rail but not the silence. "I just don't."

A huff of almost-laughter from him. It died fast. That was how they talked now—half-sentences, jokes with no floor under them, conversations that ended before they began because neither of them trusted where the other one would take them.

Below deck, Bo was snoring with the untroubled rhythm of a man who vouched for strangers and slept well afterward. Tomas was up in the engine room; she could hear him tinkering, the small metallic percussion of a wrench on a fitting, working on something at one in the morning that he should have fixed three days ago. And Ilsa Voss was in the guest berth with her lamp off, and Maren did not know if she was asleep, and that uncertainty sat in Maren's chest like a swallowed hook.

Because Maren knew who Ilsa Voss was. Had known since the dock, since the cash and the questions, since the moment the woman said her name and Maren watched her face for the flicker—and found none, which was worse. A woman who could say a false name without flinching was either a liar or someone who had practiced being someone else so long the practice had become the person.

*Not false,* Maren thought. *Just incomplete.* There was a difference, and the difference was going to tear this crew apart before they reached the Shoals, unless Maren got ahead of it.

Unless Maren paid another installment.

"You've been watching me," Oscar said. Still not looking at her. "Since the dock. Since Voss came aboard. You've been watching me like you're waiting for me to do something."

"I'm always watching you. Occupational habit."

"Maren."

The way he said her name—not angry, not pleading, just *tired*, tired all the way down to the keel—did more damage than anger would have. She gripped the rail. The paint was blistered and peeling there, little curls of gray flaking off under her palm, and she thought about how many coats of paint covered the original steel, how many layers of repair stood between the sea and the people above it, and how nobody ever really knew how thin the hull had gotten until the water told them.

"Eli used to stand right here," she said.

Oscar went rigid. She felt it through the rail, through the deck, the whole boat registering his stillness.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"He liked the stern. Said you could feel the engines better back here. Said a boat tells you the truth at the stern and lies everywhere else." She heard her own voice doing what voices did when they carried cargo they'd held too long—too even, too careful, each word set down like a glass on a saucer. "He'd have hated this boat. Too much rust. He'd have made Tomas strip her to bare metal."

"Tomas isn't Eli."

"No. Nobody is Eli. That's rather the point."

Lightning pulsed inside the cloud bank again, closer now, or maybe just brighter. The air pressure had that taste to it, metallic and sweet, the taste the sky got before it stopped pretending. Priya had plotted their position twice that afternoon and frowned at both plots, and Maren had seen the frown and said nothing, because saying something would have meant explaining why she already knew these waters were lying, why dead-calm heat in this corridor meant the system offshore wasn't building the way the models said—it was *reorganizing*, pulling itself apart and stitching itself back together into something the forecasters wouldn't have a name for until it was on top of them.

She knew because she'd been running this coast for twenty years. And she knew something else, too, something she'd been carrying since the harbor, since before the harbor, since a night six years ago when the rain came down like the sky had a grudge and a boat called the *Meridian* went out anyway.

"Oscar." She turned to face him fully now, and he finally turned to her, and in the low light his face looked older than ten years should have made it. "There are things you've decided I know, and things you've decided I don't know, and you've been managing me according to those decisions for a decade. And I have let you. Because letting you believe your version was easier than telling you mine."

His jaw worked. "My version."

"The version where you weren't there. Where you were ashore. Where the *Meridian* went out on Corven's order and you spent the night drinking yourself stupid at the Saltline Bar and woke up to the news like everyone else." She held his gaze. "That version has a hole in it the size of a man, Oscar, and we've both been standing in front of the hole for ten years pretending it's a wall."

The sea made no sound. That was the worst part—that vast, swollen quiet, the whole ocean holding its breath along with them.

"You were there," he said. Flat. Not a question either. The words came out of him like something he'd been keeping in a locked drawer and had finally, carelessly, left open.

"I was on the water that night." Her hands tightened on the rail. Here it was, then. The installment. She paid it out slowly, carefully, the way you fed line to a winch under load—one controlled length at a time, because let it run free and it would take your arm. "Corven called me an hour before he called the *Meridian* out. Wanted a second opinion on the window. I told him it was garbage. I told him the barometer was falling faster than any model could track and that anyone who put a boat on that water deserved what the water gave them. He thanked me very politely and sent the *Meridian* out anyway, forty minutes later."

Oscar's breathing had changed. Shallow. Controlled. A man rationing himself.

"Why?" His voice cracked on the single word. "Why would he—if you told him—"

"Because the buyer wanted the concession surveyed before the season closed, and the buyer pays Corven's company, and Corven does what buyers want. Same as it's always been on this coast. Weather reports aren't science out here, Oscar. They're invoices." She paused. "You know that. You've always known that. You just haven't let yourself know *why* you know it."

*Careful,* she told herself. *Not yet. Not all of it.*

Because here was the part she could not say, not tonight, maybe not ever: that she hadn't only given Corven her opinion. That she'd been offered money to confirm the window instead of condemning it—a number with enough zeros behind it to buy her own boat outright, no more chartering, no more scraping—and that she had refused the money and told the truth and it hadn't mattered, and that some nights the arithmetic of that still kept her awake, because refusing the bribe hadn't saved anyone, and taking it wouldn't have changed anything either, and there was no version of that night where her hands came out clean. Only versions where they came out less dirty. She had built ten years of self-respect on the difference between dirty and less dirty, and she guarded that difference like a hull guards against the sea: knowing full well the sea gets in eventually.

"And the job," Oscar said. His voice had gone distant, the voice of a man reading his own autopsy. "This job. The Sable Shoals concession."

"Same concession." She made herself say it plainly. No anesthesia. "Same coordinates. Same survey parameters, updated for six years of shoal drift. And the same buyer—or the same buyer's successor, which amounts to the same thing, because companies like that don't die, they restructure. Meridian Salvage and Survey. You remember the name?"

"I named my—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Eli named our boat after the company that trained us. Before we knew what they were."

"Yes."

"So this whole run—" He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd heard in years, dry and hollow, a laugh with nothing alive in it. "We're sailing back into the exact job that killed him. On purpose. With permits. I signed permits."

"You signed permits," she agreed gently. "And I brought you the contract. So whatever this is, we're both standing in it."

The cloud bank had eaten half the sky while they talked. She could see the lightning properly now—not flickers anymore but long silent discharges that lit the storm's internal architecture, shelf after shelf of cloud stacked up like a cathedral collapsing in slow motion. The pressure in her ears had shifted. Somewhere aft, the bilge pump kicked on again, and this time she counted fourteen seconds before it shut off, and she saw Oscar count too, and saw him decide not to mention it, and understood that the boat was leaking in more ways than one tonight and everyone aboard was choosing which leaks to acknowledge.

"Why tell me now?" he asked.

Because Ilsa Voss was sleeping thirty feet away with a folder of evidence that would end careers and reopen graves. Because the permits burned down day by day and the storm was closing and there would be no better time than this strange glassy midnight, no privacy more complete than the privacy of a crew too exhausted to eavesdrop. Because guilt was a debt and debts compounded and hers had been accruing interest for six years and the payment plan was coming due whether she serviced it voluntarily or not.

But she said none of that. Instead she said: "Because you're going to hear it soon regardless. And I'd rather you hear it from me, in pieces you can survive, than all at once from someone who wants something from you."

"Voss." His eyes sharpened. "You know who she is."

"I know who she says she isn't."

"That's not an answer, Maren."

"It's the only answer I can afford tonight." She turned back to the horizon, to the storm assembling itself out there with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. "Ask me again tomorrow. Or the day after. Ask me every day if you need to. I'll pay it out as long as I can, Oscar. But I'm telling you now, as your rival, as your—whatever I am to you—as the last person besides you who was close to that night: there is a version of the truth about Eli that is worse than anything you've imagined. And when it comes, it won't come from me first."

"What does that mean?" He grabbed her arm, spun her toward him, and his grip hurt, and she let it hurt. "Maren. What does that mean?"

She looked at his hand on her arm. At his face, stripped of everything but the question. Six years of running had carved him hollow, and she understood that hollow better than he'd ever suspect, because she'd carved her own hollow in the same wreck, just on the other side of it.

"It means," she said, "that the storm is going to make us honest before it's finished with us. All of us. Every secret on this boat is going to come up like bilgewater, and we're going to bail together or we're going to sink separately. Those are the only options left. I've done the math."

Above them, the first fat drops of rain struck the deck—single, deliberate, spaced like gunshots. Then another. Then a handful more, and the glassy sea shivered, and far off in the dark the thunder finally arrived, rolling across the water toward them like something answering a summons.

Oscar released her arm. He stared at the storm building on the horizon, and she watched him do the same math she'd done, watch him arrive at the same terrible sum.

"The *Meridian* went out in weather like this," he said softly.

"Worse," Maren said. "It was worse than this."

And below their feet, faint and steady and far too often, the bilge pump began to cycle again.

* * *

She stayed at the rail after he went below. That was her penance and her habit both—to stay with the weather while others slept, as if her vigilance could retroactively cover the nights she'd failed to be vigilant, as if standing watch now could reach backward through six years and stand watch then.

The rain thickened. Not the true monsoon yet—Priya would call this the leading edge, the scout—but enough to soak her jacket and streak the deck and turn the world into sound. She listened to the Kestrel work underneath her, hull and pump and engine, the honest noises of a dishonest boat, and she let herself think about the part she hadn't paid tonight. The installment still owed.

The order. The paper with the signature on it. The reason she'd stopped short, the reason her confession had a floor in it, the reason she'd told Oscar that the truth wouldn't come from her first.

Because it was true. It wouldn't. When the whole of it surfaced—and it would surface, storms had a way of pumping out secrets the way Tomas's pump pumped out the sea—it would come from Ilsa Voss, or from the documents in whatever bag the woman kept locked in the guest berth, or from Oscar himself, in some black hour, finally saying aloud the thing he'd buried so deep he'd built a whole life of running on top of it.

And Maren would be there when it happened. And she would have to decide, in that moment, whether to add her final installment—the one about what she'd seen on the water that night, the lights, the flare, the choice she'd made at the helm of her own boat, the choice that had kept her alive and haunted and that she had never once spoken of in six years—or whether to let Oscar carry it alone.

*Not alone,* she corrected herself. Never alone again. That was the whole point of tonight's payment. She was moving herself into position, the way you moved a boat into position before weather—close enough to help, far enough not to be caught by the worst of it.

Thunder rolled closer. The rain came harder. In the chart room below, she could see lamplight moving—Priya, awake, replotting. Good. One of them should be.

Maren wiped the water from her face and made herself a promise, there in the dark, with the storm's leading edge hissing across the water toward them: one more installment before the Shoals. Just one. The one about the lights.

She owed Eli that much. She owed them all that much.

Behind her, a cabin door opened, and soft footsteps crossed the wet deck—light ones, deliberate ones, footsteps she'd catalogued at the dock and never forgotten.

Ilsa Voss stopped beside her at the rail, hood up, face shadowed, and said quietly, over the rising rain:

"You told him some of it. I heard." A pause. "Now tell me the rest, Ms. Kade. Before the storm does."
