## 13. The Monsoon Closes

Oscar had a theory about storms, one he'd never said out loud because saying it would make it true in the way that naming a thing makes it yours. The theory was this: a monsoon is not an object moving through the world. It's a decision. The whole system—two thousand kilometers of cloud stacked like bad news, the pressure falling the way a man's shoulders fall when he's decided something he can't take back—is not traveling anywhere so much as it is *choosing*, over and over, hour by hour, where to be. And for six years Oscar Delacroix had made his living on the belief that he could read the choosing faster than the weather could make it.

For five days out of Port Calloway, the belief held.

He stood at the Kestrel's rail with Priya's charts flapping under his elbow and watched the system breathe on the southern horizon, that vast gray-green wall of it, lightning flickering inside the cloud deck like thoughts behind a closed face. They were running the flank—the safe side, the western edge, the corridor of ugly-but-navigable water between the storm's outer bands and the dead calm at its core. It was the kind of sailing he was born for. Close enough to feel the weather's weight, far enough to stay unbaptized.

"Pressure's down another four millibars since noon," Priya said, coming up beside him with her tablet sleeved in plastic against the spray. She didn't say what four millibars meant. She didn't have to. Four millibars in six hours was the atmosphere clearing its throat.

"It'll turn north before we do," Oscar said. "It always does."

"You keep saying that like it's scripture."

"It has been, so far."

Priya looked at him the way she looked at a chart with two conflicting soundings—one line trusted, one line doubted, no way yet to know which was which. "Maren says the same thing," she said, and went below.

That was the other thing about the last five days: Maren Kade had stopped circling. Somewhere around the second night, after Ilsa Voss's questions had gone quiet and Tomas's engine had developed a rhythm that was almost but not quite right, Maren had simply started standing watches beside him without being asked. She read the sky the way he did—by smell first, instruments second—and when they disagreed about a cloud shelf off the port bow on day three, they'd argued for twenty minutes in the pilothouse and then both been wrong together, which was somehow more companionable than either of them being right alone.

Ten years of silence, and now they shared a thermos. Oscar didn't trust it. He didn't trust anything that arrived easily; easy things were how the Vantors got you.

* * *

The turn came at 0400 on the sixth day, and it came the way all the worst things in his life had come—not as a surprise but as a confirmation.

Oscar was asleep in the narrow bunk off the pilothouse when the motion of the boat changed. Not rougher. *Wronger.* Every sailor knew the difference: sea state had a grammar, and the Kestrel had just begun speaking a sentence he'd heard once before, six years ago, in the last hour of his brother's life. He was upright and barefoot on the companionway ladder before his mind caught up with his body, and by the time he reached the pilothouse Priya was already there, tablet glowing, face lit from below like something rising out of deep water.

"Tell me," he said.

"The feeder band collapsed." Her voice was flat, which was how Priya sounded when she was frightened—flatter than calm, flat as a coastline seen from very high up. "The whole western flank. It didn't weaken. It *folded*. The circulation reorganized around a new center forty klicks south-southeast of where every model put it, and the new track—" She turned the tablet toward him.

The track arrow bent like a finger crooking. Not north. Not away. East-northeast, curving up and around, a hook, a shepherd's crook, closing on the exact patch of ocean where a hundred-and-forty-foot steel trawler conversion named Kestrel was currently making seven knots into seas that had, in the last hour, doubled.

"When?" Oscar asked.

"Six hours. Maybe eight if we're lucky." She swallowed. "We're not going to outrun it, Oscar. There's no corridor anymore. The flank we were riding is going to be the front wall."

Behind them, the pilothouse door opened and Maren came in pulling a jacket over her sleep shirt, hair still wet from the spray that had found its way below. She looked at the tablet for three seconds. Then she looked at Oscar, and whatever she saw in his face made her do the thing he hated most, which was to be gentle with him.

"I've seen this turn before," she said quietly.

"I know you have."

"Eli's last run. Same season. Same shape." She said it plainly, no knife in it, and somehow that was worse. "The system hooked east and nobody believed the barometer until the wall was on top of them."

"We're not on the old boat."

"No. You're on a boat with a hidden leak and a passenger who knows too much and nineteen-day permits that are now on day eleven." Maren crossed to the chart table and pinned the corner with her palm. "But the storm doesn't care about any of that. So what do we do?"

What they did, for the next six hours, was everything. Oscar ran the crew through it like a man conducting an orchestra whose music was burning: double-lash the deck cargo, strip the tender down to its frame and lock it, seal the hatches with gasket tape and then seal the tape with canvas, pump the bilge on a schedule Tomas set with a face like a man confessing in advance. Bo worked the foredeck in harness, singing some half-remembered shanty to keep his own fear company, and Oscar let him sing because Bo's voice was the only thing aboard that wasn't counting hours.

Ilsa Voss appeared on deck at dawn and did not go back below. She stood at the rail with her hands folded, watching the southern horizon build, and when Oscar passed her she said, without turning, "You're not running anymore."

"I'm maneuvering."

"That's what my brother called it too." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were calm in a way that had nothing to do with peace. "Right up until the water came through the wheelhouse windows."

* * *

By noon the horizon was gone.

Not lowered—*removed*, erased, replaced edge to edge with a single continuous surface of cloud so vast and so uniformly dark that it stopped reading as weather and started reading as geography, as terrain, as a country they were about to enter whether or not their papers were in order. The light went the color of old pewter. The wind backed around in slow quarter-hour increments, each shift a few degrees more northerly, the atmosphere rotating its grip like a wrestler settling in.

And Oscar—who had loved weather his whole life, who had chased it across three oceans, who had stood on open decks in conditions that sent grown men below to pray, who had felt in the approach of a great storm the same vertiginous joy other men felt looking at a lover—stood at the helm and understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning, that the love had always been a misunderstanding.

He had thought the storm saw him. That was the truth of it, the embarrassing center of the whole obsession: somewhere in the boy he'd been, in the young man running salvage skiffs along the monsoon coast with Eli laughing in the bow, lived the conviction that the weather noticed him—that when he threaded a squall's edge or rode a system's shoulder, the storm and he were engaged in something mutual, a conversation, even a courtship. Respect between large forces. He had built a life on that feeling the way other men built lives on God.

Now, watching the wall come on, he felt what the wall actually contained, which was nothing. No recognition. No malice, even—malice would have been a kind of attention. Just mass, and moisture, and heat doing what heat does, indifferent as arithmetic. The storm was not coming for him. The storm was not coming for anyone. It was simply *arriving*, the way a landslide arrives, the way debt arrives, and everything in its path—Kestrel, crew, concession permits, the whole fragile architecture of Oscar's second chances—was equally weightless to it.

"Oscar." Priya's hand on his arm. "Radio's gone."

He tested it himself, though her face already told him. HF: static shaped like breathing, no carriers. VHF: the same, plus a faint rhythmic click that might have been lightning hitting the antenna farm ashore or might have been nothing. He tried the emergency frequencies anyway, keying the mic and speaking his vessel name and position into the noise, and heard his own voice come back at him distorted, thin, like a message from a man who had already drowned.

"Satellite phone?" Maren asked.

"Died with the charging bank when the inverter tripped this morning." Tomas stood in the pilothouse doorway, dripping, hollow-eyed. He didn't apologize again. He'd apologized twice during the night watch and Oscar had told him to stop spending breath on the past and spend it on the pumps. "We're dark, Captain. Completely dark."

Dark. The word sat in the pilothouse like a third person. No radio, no satellite, no AIS handshake with any ship or shore station, no voice reaching out of this gray cathedral of air to tell anyone where they were. If the Kestrel went down here, she would go down unwitnessed, and the sea would close over the site of her the way it had closed over the site of Eli's boat, and someone—Ilsa, maybe, or a Vantor with a smile full of sympathy—would eventually stand at a harbor rail and say her name aloud to no one.

Permits: day eleven of nineteen. Eight days left, and the nearest safe harbor was now behind the storm.

"Options," Oscar said, because a captain says options even when the list has one item on it.

"Run east ahead of the curve," Priya said. "Buy maybe ninety minutes before the circulation wraps us."

"Heave to and let it pass over," Maren said. "Ride it out hove-to, storm sails only. We've got the hull for it—if the leak cooperates."

Everyone looked at Tomas. Tomas looked at the deck.

"She'll cooperate," he said. "I'll see to it personally. In shifts. All shifts."

"There's a third option," Ilsa said from the companionway. She'd come up silently, the way she did, and now she stood with one hand on the frame, watching Oscar with that terrible patience of hers. "There's a cove on the northeast shoulder of Sable Shoals. Deep enough for the Kestrel, sheltered from anything but a direct easterly. My brother's charts marked it. The company used it for staging."

"Sable Shoals is two hundred miles inside the storm's projected path," Priya said.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "Which means no one will be there. Which means when we arrive, the concession office, the records room, and the buyer's local representative will all be exactly where I need them to be, unguarded, while every other boat in the region runs the other way." She paused. "I paid for passage, Captain. I'm only now telling you the destination."

The wind chose that moment to arrive in earnest—a long low moan through the rigging, rising, the sound of the atmosphere drawing its first real breath—and the Kestrel leaned, and the coffee mug slid off the chart table and broke against the bulkhead, and nobody moved to pick it up.

Oscar looked at the wall of cloud where the horizon used to be. He looked at his crew: Priya with her jaw set, doing arithmetic on lives; Maren with ten years of covered sins standing open on her face; Tomas already halfway to the engine room in his heart; Bo coming up the companionway behind Ilsa with his big honest eyes going from face to face, waiting to be told the world was still a place where people took care of each other.

Eight days of permits. A storm with no corridor around it. A passenger who had just revealed that the job was never passage at all.

"Get below and strap in, all of you," Oscar said. "Priya, bring her east and hold her there as long as holding means anything. We decide about the cove when we can see the water we'd be deciding in."

"And if we can't see it?" Bo asked softly.

Oscar put his hands on the wheel and felt the first true gust hit the glass, hard enough to flex it, hard enough to make the whole boat flinch like a struck animal.

"Then the storm decides," he said, "and we find out what it thinks of us."

The rain came then—all of it at once, the entire sky's worth, a white roaring curtain that swallowed the pilothouse windows whole—and the world outside ceased to exist, and the Kestrel sailed on into the country of the storm, carrying every secret aboard her like ballast.
