## 14. Bailing by Hand

Bo had grown up believing that water was patient. His grandmother used to say it on the porch of their stilt house in the delta, watching the river eat another meter of bank: *Water doesn't hate you, mija. It just never gets tired.* He'd thought about that a lot in the last hour, because the Kestrel's bilge was filling like a cup held under a tap, and no matter how many times they emptied it, the sea kept pouring.

The monsoon had them now. Not grazing them, not flanking them—the way Oscar liked to talk about weather, like it was an animal you could circle and outwait—but holding them. The wind came in long sustained screams from the northeast, and the rain didn't fall so much as travel sideways, hard as gravel, and every wave that broke over the bow found its way below through whatever wound the old boat was hiding in her hull.

"Bucket line!" Priya's voice cut through the roar from the companionway. "Two up, two down. Move!"

Bo moved. That was the one thing he'd always been good at—moving when someone said move, without asking why, without hesitating to calculate what it would cost him later. The crew teased him about it sometimes, called him the simplest man on the water, but Bo knew what they didn't say out loud: simple wasn't stupid. Simple was knowing exactly what you were for. He was for hauling. For lifting. For standing between a thing that needed doing and the reason nobody else could do it.

He dropped down the ladder into the engine compartment, where the air was already knee-deep with sloshing gray water and diesel smell and the electric tang of fear. Tomas was crouched at the pump, working the handle in short furious strokes, his forearms shaking.

"It's not keeping up," Tomas said. He didn't look up. His voice had a strange flatness Bo had never heard from him before, like a man reading his own autopsy report.

"How bad?"

Tomas finally turned. In the swinging light of the emergency lamp his face looked carved out of something pale and soft, wood before it dries. "Worse than I told you. Worse than I told anyone."

* * *

They formed the line anyway. There wasn't time for anything else.

Priya rigged it in ninety seconds flat—she was like that, Priya, turning chaos into procedure the way other people turned flour into bread—and suddenly there was a rhythm: bucket filled in the engine room, passed hand to hand up the ladder, heaved over the rail, empty bucket passed back down. Fill, pass, lift, throw. Fill, pass, lift, throw. Bo took the position at the top of the ladder because he could lift the full buckets higher than anyone, and because at the top you got the worst of the weather, rain like thrown nails and the deck pitching under your boots, and he preferred it that way. When his hands were full he couldn't think.

Above them the sky was a moving ceiling of black wool, torn open in places to show nothing behind it, just more dark. Lightning walked along the horizon in long silent strides, and each flash printed the waves onto Bo's eyes—a mountain range in motion, coming for them from every direction at once.

Oscar was at the helm and had been for six hours. Through the wheelhouse glass Bo could see him hunched into the wheel like a man carrying it on his back, hair plastered to his skull, saying nothing. Maren was on the winch and the storm sails, reefed down to rags, and she moved across the deck with a certainty that made Bo's chest ache to watch—one hand always for the boat, one for herself, never confusing the two.

And Ilsa Voss—Bo had half expected her to be below, sick or hiding or both, because she'd come aboard as a passenger and passengers had a way of discovering they were cargo in weather like this. But she was on deck, clipped to the jackline, bailing with the rest of them. Her hands were split and bleeding and she didn't complain, and every few minutes she looked at Bo with an expression he couldn't read, an expression like she was memorizing him.

Fill, pass, lift, throw.

His shoulders burned. His palms had gone past burning into a hot numbness, and he could feel the skin of them gone soft as paper, ready to tear. Somewhere around the second hour Priya appeared beside him with tape and wrapped his hands without a word, without stopping the line, and then she was gone again, back to the nav station where she fought the chart plotter like a woman wrestling a liar.

It was near midnight—Bo measured time now only in the rhythm, in the count of buckets, four hundred, five hundred, the numbers dissolving as fast as he made them—when the pump died.

Not slowed. Died. A clunk, a shudder through the deck plates, and then the sound Bo had been dreading all night: silence from below, except for the water, which did not go silent, which never went silent, which rose.

"TOMAS!" Priya's voice, raw now.

"I know!" And then Tomas was coming up the ladder against the flow of buckets, forcing his way down the line, and Bo caught his arm as he passed and felt the tremor in it, deeper than exhaustion.

"The impeller's gone," Tomas shouted over the wind. "Shaft seal's weeping too—I can pack it by hand but I need twenty minutes and I need the water below knee level or I'm working underwater!"

Twenty minutes. Bo looked at the ladder, at the black water rising in the throat of the boat, and did the arithmetic every sailor does and hates: five people, one bucket line, and a hole in the world that didn't get tired.

Then Oscar was there. Bo hadn't seen him leave the helm—Maren must have taken it, and there was a sentence Bo never thought he'd live inside—and Oscar stood at the head of the ladder with the lightning printing his face white, black, white.

"Nobody sleeps tonight," he said. "Nobody stops. We bail until Tomas says stop, and if he doesn't say stop, we bail until the sea gets bored." He almost smiled. It was the thing about Oscar that Bo had followed him for, years ago, half his life ago: even now, even drowning, some part of him treated the ocean like an opponent who could be outlasted, out-charmed, out-stubborned. "Bo. You're anchor of the line. Set the pace."

Bo set the pace.

* * *

The hours after that lived in Bo's memory afterward as a single unbroken sensation rather than a sequence of events: cold, weight, rhythm. Rain hammering the deck until it sounded like applause, enormous and dead. Buckets passing up through the dark like organs being handed along a chain of gravediggers. Once, a wave came over the rail whole—a ton of ocean delivered personally—and knocked two buckets and nearly Priya down the ladder, and Bo caught her harness strap one-handed and held her against the rail while she got her feet under her, and neither of them said anything about it because there was nothing to say that the wave hadn't already said.

Somewhere in the small hours, his mind began to float free of his body, the way it did on long hauls, and he started thinking about his mother's kitchen, and the smell of cardamom, and the year the flood took the ground floor of their house and the family stood upstairs watching the water rise and his father said, calmly, *Well. Now we know where the floor is.* Everyone had laughed. It had been that kind of laugh, the kind you make when the alternative is screaming, and Bo understood for the first time in his life that his father had been bailing too, in his way, all those years, and that everyone Bo had ever loved was in a bucket line of one kind or another, passing the weight along, throwing it overboard, going back for more.

That was when Tomas came up.

He came up slowly, both hands on the ladder rails, and stood on the deck streaming with bilgewater and sweat, and he didn't rejoin the line. He stood apart, near the mast, and the crew kept bailing around him for maybe a minute before Priya saw him and stopped, and then Maren, and then the whole line stuttered and halted, and the only sounds left were the storm and the water below, still rising, patient as ever.

"I have to tell you something," Tomas said.

Bo's stomach dropped. He'd known this moment was coming—not this moment specifically, but this species of moment. He'd watched men carry secrets through storms before, and the weight always told eventually; the sea just squeezed it out of you.

"The hull damage from the first squall," Tomas said. "It wasn't new. The planking around the shaft log was rotten when we left harbor. I found it during the refit survey. I epoxy-pumped it and glassed over it and I logged it as sound." His voice cracked and he pushed through the crack. "I hid it. From all of you. From Oscar."

The wind filled the silence, shrieking through the rigging like something being flayed.

"Why?" Priya said. Just that. One word, flat as a depth sounding.

"Because I owe money." Tomas laughed, and it was the worst sound Bo had heard all night, worse than the pump dying. "Not Vantor money. Small money. Ugly money. To men who don't smile when they collect. If the Kestrel failed survey, she'd be condemned, and I'd lose my share, my tooling, everything I've got is in this boat's engines—" He stopped. Wiped his face with a hand that left a gray streak. "I told myself it would hold. I told myself the patch would hold through one season. I'm a good engineer. I believed my own work. That's the truth of it—I trusted my own lie."

Bo watched Oscar come forward out of the wheelhouse glow. He'd been expecting—everyone had been expecting—the explosion. Six years Bo had worked for Oscar Delacroix and he'd seen him tear a man's contract in half over a falsified fuel log, seen him put a dockworker through a fish table for cheating Bo himself on a payout. Oscar's anger was famous. It was load-bearing. The whole crew's sense of fairness was built on the foundation of Oscar's fury.

Oscar stood in front of Tomas, rain running off his chin, and for a long moment he said nothing at all.

"You lied to this crew," Oscar said finally. "In the middle of the ocean. In a monsoon."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me now because the pump died and you ran out of places to hide it."

"Yes."

Another silence. Below them, the water climbed. Bo could feel it through the deck, the boat sitting lower, heavier, the waves hitting her hull with a duller note, and he wanted to scream *bail, bail, we're losing the race*—but he understood, dimly, the way he understood tides, that this moment was also load-bearing, that what Oscar did in the next thirty seconds would hold up the rest of the voyage or sink it faster than any hole in the hull.

Oscar looked at Tomas for a long time. Then he looked at each of them—Priya dripping at the rail, Maren with her arms crossed against the wind, Bo with his taped hands, Ilsa Voss with her unreadable eyes—and something in his face changed. Something let go.

"Get back on the pump," Oscar said. "Pack the seal. Bail with us after. And when we make harbor, you and I are going to sit down and you're going to tell me every number you owe and to whom, and we're going to fix it the honest way, which is the slow way, which is the way that doesn't kill anybody." He put his hand on Tomas's shoulder, once, hard. "You don't get to drown us. But you don't get to drown alone either. That's the deal on this boat. Always has been."

Tomas made a sound Bo had never heard a grown man make—not a sob, something before sobbing, something older—and went down the ladder.

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then Priya picked up her bucket, and Maren picked up hers, and Bo set the pace again, fill-pass-lift-throw, and the line closed around the gap Tomas had left like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Near dawn—or what Bo decided was dawn, since the dark stopped getting darker—the storm eased just enough to be a cruelty instead of a death sentence. The wind dropped from scream to growl. The rain thinned to a steady hiss. They could hear each other speak without shouting, and somehow that was almost worse, because now there was room for everything they'd been too busy to feel.

They bailed in exhausted silence, five figures swaying on a deck awash with gray light, and Bo's arms had stopped being arms and become two ropes of pain attached to a will that refused, on principle, to end.

It was Ilsa who finally spoke.

"He'll fix it," she said. She was looking down the ladder, toward the engine room where Tomas knelt in the rising water with his hands inside the boat's wound. "Your engineer. He'll fix the seal. But Bo—" She turned to him, and in the gray light her face was stripped of everything he'd taken for friendliness, everything he'd vouched for at the dock, and what was underneath was so bare and so fierce that Bo physically stepped back. "When he opens that space up to reach the shaft log, he's going to find something else down there. Something that isn't damage."

"What do you mean?" Bo said. "What isn't damage?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, and the wind pulled strands of her wet hair across her face, and when she spoke again her voice was quiet enough that only he could hear it, quiet as a secret being paid out hand to hand down a line.

"I didn't come aboard for passage, Bo. I came aboard because six years ago a boat went down off Sable Shoals with my brother on it, and somebody signed the order that sent her out, and the proof of who and why is in the hull of this boat—in the paperwork Tomas glassed over, in the survey records, in the name on the concession permit." Her eyes went past Bo, up to the wheelhouse, to where Oscar stood at the wheel with the gray dawn breaking behind him. "By morning, one way or another, this whole crew is going to know what happened to Eli Delacroix. And your captain is going to have to decide whether he tells it first—or last."

Bo stood with his empty bucket in his ruined hands, and below him the Kestrel groaned like a living thing, and the water rose, patient, tireless, waiting for morning the same as everyone else.
