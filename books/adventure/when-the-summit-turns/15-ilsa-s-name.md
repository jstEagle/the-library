## 15. Ilsa's Name

The storm had stopped pretending to be weather. It was a country now, with borders and laws, and the *Kestrel* was an illegal immigrant rattling inside it.

Ilsa Voss stood in the galley doorway with a mug of cold coffee she had no intention of drinking, watching the crew move around each other in the exhausted choreography of people who had bailed all night. Bo slept sitting up against the bulkhead, his hands still curled as if around a bucket handle. Tomas had not left the engine compartment in six hours. Priya was at the chart table with her head in her hands, and Oscar Delacroix stood at the forward window watching the rain come at the glass sideways, the way he had been watching it for what Ilsa guessed was most of his life.

And Maren Kade sat at the corner table, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, because sharpening was what Maren did instead of talking.

Six days. Ilsa had been aboard six days, and she had spent them the way she had spent the last two years: listening, waiting, filing. She was good at it. She had learned patience in a hospital corridor in Kalversund, learning it from the same teachers who had taught her grief — the long fluorescent hours, the doctors who spoke in the passive voice, the paperwork that reduced her brother to a date and a vessel name. Patience was just grief that had learned to hold still.

But the storm had changed the arithmetic. The *Kestrel* was leaking in a way that could not be hidden much longer, the monsoon had them in its fist, and the permits in Oscar's cabin had — she had seen them, folded into his oilskin, because she had looked, because she looked at everything — nineteen days written on them like a sentence. Nineteen days, and they were six into it, and the sea between here and Sable Shoals had turned into a wall.

It was time to stop filing. It was time to use the blade.

* * *

Her brother's name had been Anselm Voss, and he had died on the fourteenth of October, six years ago, on a boat called the *Cormorant Maid*, in weather that no competent man would have taken a dinghy into, let alone a salvage run to the Sable Shoals concession.

She had been twenty-three. She had learned of it by telephone, from a woman at the maritime authority who pronounced his name wrong — *An-sell-um*, flat as a coin — and Ilsa had stood in her kitchen holding the phone away from her ear as if distance could change the words. Anselm had been twenty-nine. He had crewed salvage for six years and sent money home and called every Sunday, and on the Sunday after the fourteenth of October she had sat by the phone from morning until dark, telling herself that storms delayed calls, that harbors had bad reception, that the sea was large and phones were small.

The official report had come in a gray folder. *Weather event of unusual severity. Vessel lost with all hands. No evidence of negligence.*

She had believed it for eleven months. That was the part she could never forgive herself, the eleven months. She had believed the gray folder because believing it was easier than the alternative, and because grief is lazy — it wants a story it can put down. *The sea took him.* The sea, that vast impersonal thing, that god with no face. You could curse the sea. You could not sue the sea.

Then, in the second winter, a letter had come. No return address. Typewritten, one page, and the sentence that had reorganized her entire life sat in the middle of it like a shrapnel fragment:

*The forecast was falsified. The concession office filed a fair-weather report on the fourteenth. Check the archive at the Port Authority annex, file 114-K, and ask who signed the routing order.*

She had checked. File 114-K existed. The forecast filed by the Sable Shoals concession office on the morning of the fourteenth — the forecast Anselm's captain would have seen before committing — described a system tracking *east and away from the shoals*. The actual system, per the meteorological bureau's own records, had turned west and sat on the shoals like a cat on a mouse hole. Someone had known. Someone had filed a fiction anyway, because the concession contract carried a penalty clause for delayed delivery, because the buyer was waiting, because money.

And the routing order — the document that put the *Cormorant Maid* out into that specific water on that specific day — bore a signature at the bottom that Ilsa had stared at until the letters stopped meaning anything.

She had spent two years learning everything. She had learned that the concession had changed hands twice since. She had learned that the buyer on the fourteenth of October — the company that had needed its cargo so badly it had swallowed a falsified forecast like a pill — was a firm called Halloway & Struan, and that Halloway & Struan was, by a chain of acquisitions no one had bothered to disguise because no one had ever expected to be asked, the same firm holding the Sable Shoals concession today. The same buyer. The same water. The same paperwork, six years fatter.

And she had learned something else, something the typewritten letter had not told her: that the man who signed the routing order had not been some faceless administrator. He had been a salvage man, young then, hungry, drowning in the particular debt that drowns coastal men. He had lost a brother of his own in the same wreck. And he had spent the six years since running — from harbor to harbor, debt to debt, storm to storm — as if the sea itself might be outrun if only he never stopped moving.

Oscar Delacroix.

She had his file. She had his brother's name — Eli — from the casualty list, the two names printed together the way the dead are printed together, alphabetical, indifferent: *E. Delacroix. A. Voss.* She had a photograph taken at a harbor memorial two years ago, a man at the back of the crowd with his collar up, who left before the reading of the names was finished. She had a debt ledger, purchased at a price she did not let herself think about, showing what the Vantor family was owed and what the Vantor family did to men who ran out of road.

She had not come aboard the *Kestrel* for passage. Passage was the coin; the truth was the purchase.

* * *

The galley smelled of bilge and burned coffee and the particular salt-sweet rot of a boat taking water. Ilsa set her untouched mug down and crossed to the chart table, where Priya sat surrounded by the wreckage of their route.

"You knew," Ilsa said.

Priya looked up. She had the gray face of a woman who had spent the night doing arithmetic with lives. "Knew what?"

"The waypoint." Ilsa pulled out the chair opposite her and sat, deliberately, the way you sit across from someone when the conversation has stopped being casual. "The false one. You figured it out yesterday — I watched you figure it out. You've been turning it over since, trying to decide whether it was Maren, or me, or a mistake in your own work. It wasn't a mistake. And it wasn't me."

Priya's eyes went flat and careful. "You've been aboard six days. You don't know what I know."

"I know you haven't slept. I know you re-plotted the route three times and burned the second one in the galley stove because you didn't want anyone to see it." She said it gently. That was the craft of it — the blade wrapped in kindness, because she had learned that people did not confess to accusation; they confessed to being understood. "I'm not here to hurt you, Priya. I'm here because I've spent two years learning how to read a crew, and this one is carrying more than cargo."

Priya said nothing. Outside, the wind hit the hull and the whole boat flinched.

"Here's what I'll give you for free," Ilsa said. "The waypoint wasn't fed to us by anyone aboard. I checked the harbor office at our last port, before I ever asked Bo for passage. A clerk was paid to alter the filed route — paid by a man I can name, an agent for Halloway & Struan. They didn't want the *Kestrel* at Sable Shoals. They wanted it delayed. They wanted the permits to expire with the job undone, so the concession reverts and no inconvenient crew is standing on their shoals asking old questions."

Priya's hands had gone still on the chart. "Why would you check the harbor office? Passengers don't check harbor offices."

"Passengers want rides." Ilsa met her eyes. "I want a grave."

* * *

She found Oscar in the wheelhouse an hour later, because she had decided the order of operations the way you decide anything surgical: Priya first, to stop the bleeding of paranoia; Bo never, because Bo's kindness was the one clean thing aboard and she would not spend it; Maren last, because Maren was the hardest bone to set.

Oscar stood at the glass with the storm's gray light carving his face into planes. He did not turn when she came in. She suspected he always knew who was behind him — a man hunted for years developed that — and that not turning was its own kind of speech.

"You should be below," he said. "It's going to get worse before it gets worse."

"I know what's at Sable Shoals."

Now he turned. Slowly, the way men turn when a floor they trusted makes a sound.

"The concession," she said. "The buyer. Halloway & Struan. You've heard the name."

"Everyone's heard the name. They buy up half the coast."

"They bought more than that." She took the folded paper from her jacket — she had carried it for two years, soft at the creases now, translucent with handling — and held it out. "Six years ago, in October, Halloway & Struan needed a delivery from the shoals inside a penalty window. The forecast that morning said the system was tracking east. It was tracking west. Someone in that office wrote a fair-weather report over a killing storm, and a routing order went out, and a boat called the *Cormorant Maid* sailed into it with five men aboard."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Under the wind, under the rain, under the groan of the hull, she could hear the pump running in the bilge — Tomas's secret, still pretending to be routine.

"Two of those men," Ilsa said, "came back as names on a list. Eli Delacroix. Anselm Voss." She watched the name land. She had imagined this moment a thousand times, rehearsed it in kitchens and bus stations and hospital waiting rooms, and in none of the rehearsals had Oscar Delacroix's face done what it did now — which was nothing. Which was the terrible practiced blankness of a man who had been hit there before.

"My brother," she said, "was twenty-nine. He called every Sunday. He was saving to buy our mother a house with a roof that didn't leak, because he said a roof was the only promise worth keeping." Her voice held. She had practiced that too, and hated that she'd had to. "The report in the gray folder said no evidence of negligence. The report was wrong. The evidence exists. I've spent two years collecting it, and it's in a waterproof bag in my bunk, and it is worth more than everything on this boat."

Oscar looked at the paper in her hand and did not take it. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to Sable Shoals."

"The storm—"

"The storm is the only reason they'll be honest," Ilsa said. "Nobody guards a lie in a monsoon. And because you owe the Vantors, and the Vantors don't wait, and Halloway & Struan's falsified forecast is worth exactly one debt, one concession, and one truth — if a man is brave enough to spend it." She finally set the paper down on the chart table, between them, like a card played face-up. "I didn't come for passage, Captain. Passage is what I told Bo. What I came for is the file. The original routing order. The signed forecast. Halloway & Struan keeps its concession records at the shoals station — six years of them, including the ones they thought sank with the *Cormorant Maid*. I need to stand in that office. I need thirty minutes and a man with a permit that gets us through the gate."

"And if I say no?"

She had an answer for that. She had answers for everything; two years of answers, sharpened nightly like Maren's knife. But standing there, with the storm leaning on the glass and his brother's name hanging unspoken in the air between them — *Eli*, the name on the old documents she had seen him flinch from, the name she now understood he could not say aloud — she put the blade down.

"Then I'll ask again," she said. "At the shoals. When you see the office. When you see what they kept." She paused. "You've been running six years, Captain. I've watched you do it — you run beautifully, I'll give you that, better than anyone I've ever tracked. But the thing you're running from signed its name. It's waiting at Sable Shoals with my brother's blood on the paperwork. It doesn't get tired. Only you do."

She left him there with the storm and the paper and the name she had not quite said — *your brother died because of a signature, and I think you know whose* — and went below to check on Bo, because Bo was the one person aboard she had not yet had to hurt, and she found she wanted, very badly, to keep it that way a little longer.

* * *

Maren found her at dusk.

Ilsa was in the aft hold with her waterproof bag open, doing what she did every evening — checking the documents, the copies, the photograph of the memorial crowd — when the hatch opened and Maren Kade came down the ladder with the knife still on her belt and something in her face that Ilsa had not seen there in six days of watching.

"You knew my name before you came aboard," Maren said. Not a question.

"I know everyone's name aboard. It's the first thing I learn."

"Voss." Maren said it the way you set down something heavy. "Anselm Voss. He was a good deckhand. Better than good. He used to sing on the night watch, badly, and nobody ever told him to stop." She stood at the bottom of the ladder, not coming closer, rainwater dripping off her oilskin onto the boards. "I was on the water that night."

"I know."

"You don't know what I was doing there." Maren's jaw worked. "And you don't know what I saw. And I'm not going to tell you tonight, because tonight the boat is leaking and the storm has us and a confession made in a monsoon is worth nothing at the shoals. But I'll tell you this much for free, since you seem to deal in it." She took a breath, and for one unguarded second Ilsa saw not the rival, not the survivor, but a woman who had been carrying a specific weight for six years and knew the exact shape of it. "The forecast was false. I've known it was false since the morning it was filed. And the man who signed the order that put that boat out there—" she stopped, and Ilsa watched her stop, watched the sentence get swallowed whole, "—has hated himself longer and better than you could ever hate him. So when you use your proof at the shoals, use it on the company. Use it where it cuts the ones who deserve the cutting."

"And the one who signed?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. The rain hammered the deck above them like a fist on a door.

"Ask him yourself," Maren said. "He's about to tell you. I can see it in him — it's coming up in him like water in a bilge. All you have to do is be standing there when it does." She turned toward the ladder, then paused. "And Voss — when he tells you, whatever he tells you — remember that the company filed the forecast. Whatever a drowning man signed, the company wrote the storm."

She climbed. The hatch closed. Ilsa sat alone in the hold with her brother's documents spread around her like a shrine, and outside the wind rose another register, and she understood that she had come aboard hunting a killer and was instead standing in the middle of a wreck of a different kind — a wreck made of guilt and love and six years of silence, with everyone still aboard it, still bailing.

She closed the bag. She touched the photograph once, the crowd at the memorial, the man with his collar up slipping out the back.

"Almost, Anselm," she said to him, quietly, in the language of their childhood. "Almost. Hold on."

Above her, boots crossed the deck toward the wheelhouse. Oscar Delacroix, going to find his courage or lose it, and either way she would be there when it surfaced — because grief was patience, and patience was a blade, and hers had waited two years to be drawn.
