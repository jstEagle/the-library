## 16. The Truth About Eli

The storm had stopped being weather and become a place.

Oscar felt it in the way the Kestrel moved—not the rolling of open water but the shuddering, trapped motion of something held. The monsoon had closed over them like a fist around a coin, and for six hours they had been shaken inside it, the boat groaning through its seams, the bilge pumps running on Tomas's rationed schedule because the rationing was all that kept them honest now. Every man and woman aboard knew the number of minutes between pump cycles. It had become their clock, their liturgy, the thing they counted instead of days.

He stood at the wheel under the canvas dodger, hands wrapped so tight around the spokes that his knuckles had gone the color of the sea—gray-green, bloodless. Rain came sideways with enough force to sting through oilskins. Ahead there was no horizon, only a wall of water that rose and fell like something breathing, and somewhere beyond that wall, if Priya's dead-reckoning held, lay the Sable Shoals concession and nineteen days that were now eleven.

Eleven days. He'd been counting exits for so long he'd forgotten what it was to count toward something.

The hatch banged open behind him and Maren came through it sideways, one hand on the frame, her hair plastered flat, her face pale in the red glow of the running lights. She didn't ask to take the wheel. She just took it, shouldering him gently aside the way she had ten years ago, as if no time had passed at all, as if the last decade hadn't been built entirely out of the thing they never said.

"Bo's asleep," she said over the wind. "Finally. Kid bailed until his hands split."

"Tomas?"

"Pumping. Priya's got the chart."

They stood together then, the two of them, wedged against the wheel while the Kestrel climbed another gray mountain and slid down its far side with a shudder that Oscar felt in his teeth. Neither spoke. There was nothing left to say about the storm. The storm had said everything already: you are small, you are late, you are here.

It was Ilsa Voss who broke the silence—or rather, the memory of her voice did. *My brother's name was Anders. Anders Voss. He died with Eli Delacroix on a boat that should never have left the dock.* She'd said it three nights ago in the galley, lamplight swinging, her face calm the way water is calm right before it goes over the edge. And then she'd asked the question that had been circling the boat ever since like a bird that wouldn't land:

*Who signed the order?*

Oscar had watched Maren's face when Ilsa asked it. He'd watched Maren not look at him. And he had understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching a wave build past the height where waves can be survived, that the answer was coming whether he gave it or not.

"Maren." His voice came out rougher than he intended, half-swallowed by wind. "I need to tell you something."

She glanced at him. Rain ran off the brim of her hood between them like a curtain.

"You don't," she said.

"I do. Because she's going to ask again, and next time you're going to answer, and I'm not—" He stopped. Swallowed. The old reflex rose in him, the beautiful reflex, the one that had kept him alive for ten years: *run*. Turn the bow downwind, put distance between himself and the sentence, let the horizon do what horizons do. He had outrun debt collectors through markets. He had outrun the Vantors across three seasons of monsoon coast. He had outrun his brother's name into every port that would have him.

But the storm had closed. There was nowhere to run that wasn't here.

"I signed it," Oscar said.

The words came out smaller than the truth inside them. Smaller than ten years of nights. He heard them land between himself and Maren and felt the boat keep moving, indifferent, climbing and falling, and some part of him had expected the world to react—the sky to split, the sea to rise up and take its payment—and instead there was only rain and the groan of the hull and Maren's hands steady on the wheel.

"The order," she said quietly. Not a question. A confirmation, the way you confirm a bearing you've known for years.

"The order that put the *Marta Cee* out that night." His throat worked. "The forecast said the system would hold south. I read it wrong—I didn't read it wrong, I *chose* to read it wrong, because the buyer's window was closing and the bonus was double and I was twenty-six years old and stupid with wanting out. One run, I told myself. One more run and we're clear, Eli and me, we buy our own boat, we're done chasing other people's weather." He laughed, and it came out broken, a terrible sound torn away by wind. "One more run."

Maren said nothing. Her hands stayed on the wheel. That was the thing about Maren Kade—he'd spent a decade hating her for her silence, and now, hearing his own confession dissolve into the storm, he understood the silence had never been hers to break. It had been a wall she stood in front of, all these years, taking the weather.

"You knew," he said. "That night. You were on the water. You told me the system was turning before I signed."

"I told you it might turn."

"You told me it *was* turning. You had the barometer in your hand. You said, *Oscar, don't be an idiot.*" The memory arrived whole, undimmed, the way the worst ones do: Maren standing on the dock of the Sable Shoals staging yard, rain just starting, holding her aneroid like a verdict. Him laughing. Signing anyway. Eli below decks in the *Marta Cee*, checking fuel lines, waving up at him through the hatch with that grin, that stupid bright grin, trusting him the way brothers trust, which is to say completely, which is to say without any defense at all.

"And then it went down," Oscar said, "and I couldn't breathe, and the inquiry came, and everyone wanted to know who signed the dispatch order, and I stood there in that room with my hands shaking and I opened my mouth to say *me*—"

"And I said it first." Maren's voice was flat, steady, the voice she used for helm work in heavy seas. "I said I'd signed it. I said the copy in the log was mine."

"Why?" The question tore out of him after ten years, raw as a wound reopened. "Why would you do that? We weren't—we hated each other. We'd been rivals since we were kids. You should have let them hang it on me. God knows I earned it."

The Kestrel reached the top of a wave and hung there, a long suspended moment, the whole gray world tilted, and in that moment Maren turned and looked at him fully for the first time since the confession began. Her eyes were wet, and it was impossible to tell rain from anything else, and maybe that was mercy, maybe that was why sailors wept only in storms.

"Because I'd seen you sign it," she said. "And I knew what it would make you. A man who loses his brother to his own signature—that man doesn't come back from it, Oscar. That man drowns standing up. I'd watched it happen to other men. I thought—if I carry it, he gets to keep something. Gets to keep *hating me* instead of himself. Hate keeps you moving." She looked back at the sea. "And God help me, it worked. You've been running for ten years. Fastest man on the coast. Never stopped once."

"That's not living, Maren. That's just drowning slower."

"No," she agreed softly. "It isn't. But it was the only lifeboat I had to give you, and I gave it, and I've regretted it every single day since, because carrying your sin meant you never had to face it, and a man who never faces it never—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "Never stops. Never anchors. Never becomes anyone worth the trade."

* * *

Below, the cabin was a chaos of swinging lamps and wet wool and the smell of diesel and salt. Tomas was asleep sitting up against the engine housing, one hand still resting on the pump handle like a faithful dog. Bo lay in his bunk with his bandaged hands curled against his chest, breathing the deep breaths of the young and exhausted. Priya sat at the chart table, hood down, hair drying in wild curls, marking their position with a pencil worn to a stub.

Oscar came down the ladder and stood dripping in the middle of it all, and Priya looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her set the pencil down.

"Captain?"

"Ilsa awake?"

"In the forward berth." Priya hesitated. "Oscar. Whatever you're about to do—"

"It's overdue by ten years."

He crossed the cabin and knocked on the forward bulkhead, once, twice. The berth curtain drew back and Ilsa Voss looked out at him, awake—she was always awake; he suspected she slept the way cats do, in shallow armed intervals—with her brother's photograph in one hand and her eyes already reading him like a chart.

"You know," she said.

"I signed the order." He said it again, and it was easier this time, or at least faster, the way a second plunge into cold water is faster. "Six years ago. The night the *Marta Cee* went down with Eli and Anders aboard. I read a forecast I knew was optimistic because the money was good and I was greedy and young. Your brother died because of my signature. Maren took the blame so I could keep walking, and I walked for ten years, and I am done."

Ilsa studied him for a long time. The lamp swung; shadows wheeled across her face. He braced for rage—he had rehearsed rage, imagined it, almost welcomed it, because rage would be clean, rage would be a payment he understood. What he had never budgeted for was what actually happened, which was that she looked down at the photograph in her hand, and her thumb moved over the glass covering Anders's face, once, gently, the way you touch something fragile.

"Eli sent letters," she said. "Did you know that? Before the last run. He wrote to Anders—they'd served together, before the coast. He wrote, *my brother signs the orders but I check the weather myself, always.*" She looked up. "Your brother trusted you absolutely, Oscar Delacroix. And he checked the weather himself. And the report he checked was false. Someone at the buyer's office falsified the forecast feed. That's what I've spent six years finding out. So yes—you signed. Yes, you were greedy. But the storm that killed them was sold to you as safe, and the man who sold it is sitting at Sable Shoals right now waiting for this concession, and he has done it before, and he will do it again."

The floor of Oscar's world, the rotten floor he had been running across for a decade, gave way—and beneath it was not abyss but ground. Solid, terrible, true ground. He had built his whole flight on the story that he alone had killed his brother, and the story had been load-bearing, and now it came down, and what remained was worse and better: guilt without the excuse of totality. He had signed. He was guilty. And he was also, for the first time, a witness rather than only a murderer in his own court.

Above them, the wind changed pitch—a new note, higher, thinner, threading through the roar. Priya's head came up sharply. Tomas stirred against the engine housing.

"What is that?" Bo mumbled from his bunk, surfacing.

Priya was already moving, scrambling up the ladder two rungs at a time. Oscar followed her into the cockpit, where Maren stood motionless at the wheel, her face turned upward, rain falling on it straight down for the first time in hours.

The barometer needle, visible through the little brass window, had begun to climb.

"The eye," Maren said. "We're in the eye. We've got maybe forty minutes of this." She turned to Oscar, and in the strange silver half-light of the storm's hollow center her face was stripped of everything but the essential. "Forty minutes, Oscar. Whatever we decide—turn back, push through, turn them in—it gets decided in the quiet. All of it. Right now."

Oscar looked up at the walls of the storm surrounding them, towering black columns of cloud slowly revolving, and for the first time in ten years he did not feel like a man counting exits.

He felt like a man standing still, at the top of everything, with a choice to make.
