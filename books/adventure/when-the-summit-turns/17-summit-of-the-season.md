## 17. Summit of the Season

*Oscar*

The wind stopped.

Not eased—stopped, the way a heart stops, all at once and without apology. One moment the Kestrel was buried to her rails in gray violence, the next she rose on a swell into air so still Oscar could hear his own pulse ticking in his ears, could hear water dripping from the shrouds onto the deck, each drop a small bright bell.

He stood at the wheel with his hands locked around it, knuckles white, and looked up.

The eye of the monsoon was a cathedral. A hundred feet above them—no, five hundred—the clouds had peeled back into a ringed wall, slate and violet and a strange bruised gold at the top where the light came down like something spilled. Stars showed through a tear in the center. Actual stars, cold and indifferent, watching from a sky that had no business existing over this sea.

"Jesus," Bo whispered somewhere behind him.

Oscar didn't answer. He was doing the thing he always did with weather, the thing that had made him famous once and broke him twice: he read it. The wall to the south was higher, denser, rotating with a slow terrible patience. The back half of the system. Behind it, if the barometer's stutter meant anything, more of the same—a second landfall of wind stacked behind the first, the monsoon's whole weight swinging around like a door closing.

They were inside it. Inside the one place on this coast where the ocean forgot, briefly, what it was trying to do.

He became aware that the crew had gathered in the cockpit without anyone calling them. Tomas, soaked through, hands bleeding from the pump. Priya with her charts wrapped in oilcloth clutched to her chest like a child. Bo, gray-faced, holding the bail bucket he hadn't been able to put down for six hours. Maren at the companionway, hair plastered flat, looking older than her years and harder than her reputation. And Ilsa Voss, standing slightly apart, as she always did, as if she'd boarded from some other ship entirely.

Nobody spoke. In the eye there was nothing to say against it. The silence was bigger than language.

Oscar looked at each of them in turn and understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning in reverse, that whatever they decided in the next few minutes would be the last decision any of them made lightly again. The Kestrel was taking water. The permits had eleven days left. Somewhere behind them lay the Vantors' smiling patience, and ahead lay Sable Shoals and a buyer who had signed his brother's death warrant with a weather report.

And here, in the middle of everything, was stillness. The false kind. The kind you got at the top of a wave, just before gravity remembered you.

"All right," he said, and his voice came out rough, scraped raw by salt. "We've got maybe twenty minutes of this. Use them."

*Tomas*

Tomas went below first, because someone had to, and because he couldn't stand to look at the sky a second longer. Beautiful things had always made him nervous. Beautiful things lied.

In the engine room the bilge told him the truth the silence upstairs couldn't touch. Six inches. Maybe seven. The pump had gained ground during the calm but not enough, never enough, because the leak wasn't a leak anymore—it was a seam letting go by degrees, a slow unzipping along a weld he'd patched twice since Port Calloway. He put his palm flat against the hull and felt the water pressing back, patient as a creditor.

He thought about the night he'd finally confessed, bailing alongside Bo while the deck pitched, saying *I hid it, I hid the damage, I thought I could outrun it*—and the strange mercy of nobody hitting him, nobody even shouting. Bo had just handed him the bucket and said, *Well, now we both know.* That was the crew. They forgave you and then they held you to it forever, which was worse and better than punishment.

Twenty minutes of calm. He ran the numbers the way he ran everything, obsessively, miserly. Pump duty cycle, intake rate, the weight of water they'd shipped in the last six hours against what four people bailing could move. It came out to a margin so thin it was almost a joke. Almost.

But not quite.

He climbed back up into the impossible light and found them all waiting, and he said what he'd come up to say: "She'll hold. If we don't stop moving and if we don't take another beating like the last one. She'll hold long enough to matter."

Priya looked at him. "Long enough for what?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Tomas wiped his hands on his trousers and left it hanging, because he'd spent enough of this voyage keeping secrets, and this one belonged to all of them.

*Priya*

Priya spread the chart on the cockpit sole and weighted the corners with shackles, and they all crouched around it like conspirators, which, she supposed, was exactly what they were about to become—one way or another.

"Here." Her finger found the position fix she'd taken forty minutes before the calm, dead reckoning corrected by the last glimpse of the lighthouse on Vane Rock before the rain ate it. "Here is us. Here—" the finger moved southeast, across two hundred miles of open water marked with the little symbols she'd learned to hate, "—is Sable Shoals. Eleven days of permits. At our best speed, six days, if the sea gives us anything."

"And behind us?" Maren asked quietly.

Priya turned her hand. The wall of cloud to the north was visible even from the deck, a mountain range built out of weather. "Behind us is the rest of the season. This system doesn't break for a week, maybe ten days. Nobody sails through that. Nobody sails around it either—not with a leaking hull."

"So we're committed," Bo said. "Whatever we choose, we're choosing it here."

Priya sat back on her heels. She had fed them the route, or someone had fed it to her—she still didn't know which, and the not-knowing had been eating her alive for three days—and every mile of wrong water they'd burned was a mile of permits, a mile of margin, a mile of Tomas's patch holding. She had lain awake in the dead-calm heat running the false waypoint over and over, asking herself who aboard wanted them lost, and she had never once landed on an answer she could live with.

But here was the thing she'd realized somewhere in the worst of the squalls, bailing beside people who should have hated her for the navigation and didn't: it didn't matter anymore who had set them adrift. What mattered was that being adrift had shown her the shape of the choice. Turn back and lose everything—the permits, the concession, the money she needed so badly she could taste it. Run for Sable Shoals and collect the prize, and let the buyer who killed Eli Delacroix keep counting his profits. Or—

Or the third thing. The thing nobody had said out loud yet, because saying it made it real.

She looked up at the ringed sky, at the gold light bleeding down the cloud walls, and kept the third thing behind her teeth a little longer.

*Ilsa*

Ilsa Voss had imagined this moment for six years, and in every version of it she had been alone.

She had rehearsed it in her brother Daniel's room, which she still kept, which she would keep until she died. She had rehearsed it on trains, in ferry terminals, in the rented room above the chandler's in Port Calloway where she'd paid cash and answered no questions. In every rehearsal there was a moment of perfect quiet—she'd known even then it would come inside the storm, though she couldn't have said how she knew—and in that quiet she would finally hold the proof in her hands, the falsified forecast, the signature, the order, and the world would crack open along the fault line and give her brother back.

The quiet had come. The proof was folded in oilcloth inside her coat, dry, intact, more fragile than paper had any right to be. And the world had not cracked open. Daniel was still dead. Eli Delacroix was still dead. The man who had signed the order was still alive, still rich, still waiting at Sable Shoals with his concession and his clean hands.

What she had not rehearsed was the crew.

She watched them crouch around the chart—Delacroix with his ruined face, Maren Kade with her rationed confessions, the engineer with his bandaged hands, the navigator with her guilt worn like a wet coat, the boy Bo with his terrible open heart—and she understood that she had spent six years sharpening herself into a blade and had forgotten that blades are useless alone. You need a hand to hold them. She had come aboard for passage. She had stayed for something she refused to name.

"The buyer's name is Corven," she said, and every head turned. "Aldous Corven, Meridian Salvage and Concessions. Six years ago his company filed a weather advisory clearing the northern approaches for salvage work on a night when every other service in the basin had flagged the system red. Two boats went out on his advisory. One came back." She let that sit. The drip of water from the rigging counted the seconds. "I have the filing. I have the amendment log. I have the name of the forecaster who was paid to write it. What I don't have is anyone willing to stand in a room with Corven and say it to his face."

"Why not?" Bo asked, and his voice cracked on it, young and furious. "Why wouldn't anyone say it?"

"Because saying it costs everything," Maren said. "That's why."

Ilsa looked at her. "Then it's fortunate," she said, "that we're already in the storm."

*Maren*

Maren had spent ten years believing the truth was a debt you could pay in installments. A confession here, a withheld detail there, interest accruing quietly in the dark. She'd told herself it was kindness—protecting Oscar from the thing he couldn't survive knowing about himself. She'd told herself it was cowardice too, on the honest nights, and there had been fewer honest nights every year.

Now, under the impossible sky, with the whole crew's eyes on her, she felt the last installment coming due.

"I was on the water that night," she said. She'd said it before, three nights ago, but saying it again in front of all of them made it heavier, made it true in a new register. "I was running the same concession, for the same buyer, out of the same office. I heard the advisory go out. I heard it and I knew it was wrong—I could feel the pressure falling the way you feel a floor going soft—and I went anyway, because Corven's bonus was half my year's pay and I was twenty-six and stupid with want." Her voice didn't shake. She'd practiced that too, God help her. "I came about two miles short of the shoals when the front hit. I turned back. Eli and Daniel didn't turn back. They trusted the advisory."

"Maren." Oscar's voice was very quiet. "You told me you weren't sure the report was falsified."

"I told you what let us both sleep." She met his eyes and did not look away, and it cost her more than the whole voyage had. "I'm done telling you that."

The silence after it was different from the silence of the eye. Smaller. Human. Bo had put his hand flat on the chart, over the little inked symbol marking Sable Shoals, as if he could cover it, smother it, keep it from meaning what it meant.

*Bo*

Bo had grown up in a house where feelings were weather—you didn't argue with them, you dressed for them—and right now the feeling in the cockpit was a pressure change he could taste, metallic, like the air before lightning. Everyone was deciding something. He could see it happening behind their faces, six private storms, and it made him crazy, all this silence, when the answer seemed so simple it was almost embarrassing.

"We go," he said.

They looked at him. Even the sky seemed to lean in.

"We go to Sable Shoals, and we don't go for the money." His voice was steadier than he felt; his hands were shaking around the bail bucket, so he set it down. "Ilsa's got the proof. Maren saw it happen. Oscar—" he faltered, because there was something in the captain's face he'd never seen before, something like a man standing at the edge of his own reflection, "—Oscar knows things none of us know yet. But the point is, we've got everything Corven's been hiding from for six years, and we've got it on a boat that barely floats, in the biggest storm of the season, with eleven days left. If that's not a sign, then signs aren't real."

"That's not how signs work," Priya said gently.

"It's exactly how signs work. They're just bad news wearing a costume until somebody decides otherwise."

Tomas laughed—a short, cracked, exhausted sound, the first laugh in days—and something loosened in the circle. Not peace. Nothing so kind as peace. But a settling, like ballast finding its place.

*Oscar*

Oscar listened to them, all of them, and felt the story he had built his life on come apart with almost no sound at all.

For ten years the story had been this: the sea took Eli. The sea, the storm, the bad luck, the advisory he'd never checked closely enough. The sea, which he loved and chased and fled, which owed him nothing and therefore could be forgiven nothing. He had built his running on that story. Every debt dodged, every harbor abandoned at dawn, every rule—never stop, never owe twice—all of it load-bearing on one beam: that the wreck was the weather's doing and not his own.

And the truth, which had been sitting in him like swallowed ballast for a decade, was that he had signed the order. That he'd read the advisory, felt the same softness in the pressure Maren had felt, and signed anyway, because the bonus was Eli's medical debts and Eli had smiled at him across the chart table and said *we'll beat it down, little brother, we always do.* That Maren had seen the signature afterward and chosen, for reasons Oscar had never dared to ask, to carry it for him.

He looked up at the eye. The stars were still there, still cold, still watching. The southern wall was building, black and patient, the monsoon gathering itself for the second act. Twenty minutes of grace, maybe fifteen now. The barometer needle had begun its slow climb, which meant the calm was already dying.

Turn back, and the story survived, and he drowned in it slowly for the rest of his life. Run—just run, abandon the concession, vanish the way he always vanished—and the story survived that too, wore him smooth as sea glass, left nothing.

Or he could tell it. All of it. In a room with Aldous Corven in it, with Ilsa's papers on the table, with Maren's testimony beside his own, and let the truth do what truth does, which is cost everything and buy back a little more than it costs.

"Priya," he said. "Lay me a course for Sable Shoals. Through the back wall."

Every head snapped toward the southern horizon, where the cloud stood like a cliff.

"You're serious," Tomas said. "Through that."

"The system's rotating northeast. If we run the back edge on the quarter, it pushes us the whole way—we make six days' distance in four, maybe less, and no boat in the basin will be able to follow us, and no word will reach Corven before we do." He heard his own voice and marveled at it: steady, certain, the voice of a man who had stopped counting exits. "It's the fastest road there is. It's also the worst one. I'm not going to pretend it isn't."

"The Kestrel," Tomas began.

"Will hold," Bo said, "because you'll make her hold, and we'll bail, and that's the whole job."

Priya was already unrolling a fresh chart, pencil moving, lips counting degrees. Ilsa had gone very still, the way people go still when the thing they've hunted for six years comes into view. Maren caught Oscar's eye across the cockpit and gave him a single nod—no absolution in it, no forgiveness, just recognition, one survivor acknowledging another's choice.

Oscar took the wheel. Below his hands, the hull groaned once, deep, like something turning over in its sleep.

The eye was closing. He could see it—the walls leaning in, the star-tear narrowing, the gold light draining upward as the monsoon remembered what it was. The stillness at the top of everything, already ending.

"Hold on," Oscar said, and turned the bow toward the wall.
