## 18. The Back Wall

Tomas felt the eye before he saw it — a change in the Kestrel's voice, the way the hull stopped groaning in two keys and settled into one. For six hours the ship had been screaming. Now she was only singing, low and ragged, like a woman who had cried herself hoarse and found something underneath the crying that was almost calm.

He stood in the engine bay with his ear against the housing block, one hand flat on the bulkhead, reading the vibration the way other men read weather off the sky. The port shaft bearing was hot. Not catastrophic-hot, not yet, but running at a temperature it would not forgive twice. He'd been rationing everything for three days — pump time, fuel, his own sleep — and now, with the wind gone soft as a held breath around them, he allowed himself thirty seconds of standing still.

Through the open hatch above him, the eye of the monsoon was a cathedral. Gray-green light fell straight down through a hole in the clouds half a mile wide, and the sea inside it lay flattened, oiled, moving in long slow swells like something breathing in its sleep. Rain still hung in the air but no longer fell; it drifted, weightless, catching the light so the whole column of atmosphere glittered faintly, like dust in an abandoned church. On the rim of the world in every direction, the storm's walls rose black and boiling, taller than any building Tomas had ever seen, taller than anything built by hands.

They were inside the summit of the season. They were alive inside it.

"Tomas." Priya's voice came down the hatch, stripped of its usual dryness. "You need to see this."

He climbed the ladder into a silence so complete he could hear Bo crying quietly at the bow rail and pretending he wasn't, and Ilsa Voss sitting very still on the deck locker with her arms wrapped around her knees, staring up into the light like a woman looking at a face she'd forgotten. Oscar stood at the helm without touching it. Maren was beside him, chart case open on the binnacle, both of them watching the wall to the east with the expressions of people doing arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

Priya had the radar display angled toward him. The return showed the eye as a clean circle, maybe four miles across, drifting northeast at walking pace. And behind it — behind, meaning south and west, meaning where they'd come from — the back wall was already rebuilding itself, convection stacking up in towers that the radar rendered in furious reds and magentas, a cliff face of weather eight miles high.

"We've got maybe forty minutes of this," Priya said. Her finger traced the circle's edge. "Then the eye moves on or collapses, and either way we're back inside it. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless we stop trying to get out from under it." She looked up at him, and Tomas saw that she'd already done the numbers twice, three times, and hated every version of them. "The whole way here we've been flanking. Running the edges, dodging cells, burning fuel we don't have making way the sea keeps taking back. Look at our position. Look at where the current runs."

Tomas looked. The Sable Shoals concession sat northeast, ninety miles as the gull flew, and between them and it the monsoon's forward quadrant was a solid wall of headwinds they could never punch through on a leaking hull with a hot bearing. But the eye was tracking northeast too. And on the far side of the eye, in the storm's rear quadrant, the winds reversed — blew *with* them, hard, sustained, the kind of following sea salvage crews spent their whole lives waiting for and rarely survived.

"You want to ride the back wall," Tomas said.

"I want to ride the eye until the last possible minute," Priya said, "and then let the storm throw us at Sable Shoals like it's angry at us. Because it will be. And because it's the only thing out here strong enough to carry us."

* * *

Tomas went below and did things to the port bearing that he would describe later, if he described them at all, as prayer with tools.

The problem was simple and unsolvable: the bearing needed six hours of cooling and a machine shop, and it had neither. What it had was a can of heavy grease, a hose clamped to the raw-water intake to force more flow past the housing, and Tomas's hands, which had been keeping dying engines alive since he was fourteen years old in a boatyard outside Puerto Cabello where the scrap steel came in on barges and nobody asked where it had been. He packed the grease, tightened the clamp, wired the temperature gauge needle back so it wouldn't bounce and lie to him, and then he sat on the deck plates with his back against the block and talked to the engine the way you talk to a horse you're about to ask for one more river crossing.

"I know," he told her. "I know. I hid the leak and I'm sorry, and I'll spend whatever's left of me paying for it, but not today. Today I need you to be the thing you were built to be. One more run. One more, and I'll pull you apart bolt by bolt and rebuild you on a beach somewhere with my own money, if I ever have any again."

The engine ticked and settled, ticking and settling, the sound of metal deciding.

Above him the hatch opened and Bo's boots came down the ladder, and then Bo was crouching next to him in the bilge-smelling dark, offering a tin cup of coffee that was mostly condensed milk and salt, because everything aboard was mostly something else by now.

"Priya says we're going to sail into the worst part of the storm on purpose," Bo said.

"She's right."

"And you say that's fine."

"I say it's the only direction left that's downhill." Tomas drank the terrible coffee. "Bo. When you bail water, do you throw it against the wind or with it?"

Bo frowned. "With it. Obviously. Against the wind it comes back on you."

"So does everything else." He set down the cup. "We've spent six days fighting this storm. Fighting costs double. It's time to spend some of what it's offering instead."

Bo looked at him for a long moment, and then he grinned — that wide, unguarded grin that had made Tomas trust him from the first day, because a man who smiled like that in a leaky engine room either had nothing left to lose or had already decided what mattered, and both kinds made good crew. "Okay," Bo said. "I'll tell the others the engineer has finally lost his mind completely. That'll calm everyone down."

* * *

Priya ran the numbers one last time at the chart table while the light outside began, very slowly, to fail.

The eye was not going to hold. Eyes never held; they were a hole the storm punched in itself and immediately regretted. The satellite feed — such fragments of it as the antenna could still pull through the cloud deck — showed the system tightening, the eyewall contracting, which meant the winds behind them were building even as the calm lasted. Forty minutes became thirty-five. She marked the drift, corrected for current, and drew the line she'd been avoiding drawing all morning: a course that hugged the eastern edge of the eye and then, when the eyewall overtook them, turned northeast and *ran*, full throttle, sails if the rig would take them, straight down the throat of the storm's trailing winds.

It was the kind of route you didn't draw unless you trusted the boat, the crew, and God, in ascending order of doubt.

Oscar came and stood over her shoulder. She felt him reading the chart upside down, felt him arrive at the same arithmetic.

"The bearing won't take full throttle for ninety miles," he said.

"No. It'll take it for maybe sixty. Then Tomas throttles back and we surf the swells on momentum and prayer."

"And if the eyewall closes before we clear it—"

"Then we're inside the worst weather on this coast with a leaking boat and a hot engine, which is exactly where we are right now, except currently we're stationary." Priya capped her pen. "Oscar. I've been navigating since I was nineteen. Every bad position I've ever been in, I got there by being careful. This is me being careful. This is the careful option."

He looked at her for a moment, and something moved through his face that she'd never quite seen there before — not the restless counting-days look, not the flinch he wore whenever Eli Delacroix's name surfaced. Something quieter. He put his hand flat on the chart, over the line she'd drawn, as if testing whether it would hold weight.

"Then we go," he said. "All hands. Tell them what you told me. Nobody steers into this blind."

* * *

The eye began to close at 1540 by Priya's watch, and she knew it not from instruments but from the birds.

There had been birds all through the calm — terns and a single exhausted frigatebird riding the still air above the mast, fallen into the eye the way ships fall into it, sheltering in the storm's hollow hand. Now they lifted all at once, wheeled once against the graying light, and flew east, fast and low, out ahead of the collapsing calm. The frigatebird went last, climbing hard into the wind that was beginning to whisper at the top of the mast.

"Animals always know first," Maren said quietly.

"Then we follow the birds," Priya said, and rang the changes.

The transition was nothing like the movies. There was no dramatic wall of black. There was a deepening of the gray, a thickening of the air, rain starting again in fat slow drops, and then — within the space of four minutes — the wind arriving like a tide, twenty knots, thirty, forty, veering around onto their stern quarter exactly as the models promised, and the sea beneath them rising up in long glassy-gray hills and beginning, gloriously, terribly, to push.

Tomas gave the engine everything. The port bearing screamed a note only he could hear and held. The Kestrel gathered herself, shipped green water over the stern, and *went*.

* * *

What happened over the next five hours, Tomas would spend years failing to explain to people who hadn't been there.

The monsoon's rear quadrant carried them. That was the plain truth of it, and it sounded simple, and it was not simple at all. The swells came from astern at fifteen-second intervals, each one a moving hill of water taller than the mast, and the Kestrel rode them the way a hawk rides thermals — climbing the faces, hanging a weightless heartbeat at each crest with the whole gray chaos of the ocean visible below, then sliding down the backsides with the wind howling in the shrouds and the hull humming like a struck bell. Surfing. There was no better word. A hundred-ton salvage trawler, patched and leaking and overloaded with grief and secrets, surfing down the faces of the largest seas Tomas had ever been on, making nine knots, eleven, once a sustained fourteen that he confirmed three times on three different instruments because he didn't believe it.

The storm was carrying them. The thing that had hunted them for six days, drowned their plans, flooded their bilges, killed their radio and nearly their nerve — it had turned, all at once, into the strongest ally any crew ever had. All it had required was that they stop fighting it. All it had required was surrender shaped precisely like courage.

He stood his trick at the engine controls with the throttle wide and his palm flat on the housing, feeling the heat climb and plateau, climb and plateau, the bearing living at the absolute edge of what metal forgives. Twice he eased it back ten percent. Twice the speed fell and Priya's voice came down the tube — "Tomas, I need everything you have" — and twice he opened it back up, because the math was merciless: every knot they made was a mile of storm crossed before dark, and every mile was a mile the sea couldn't kill them in.

At the height of it, near dusk, the whole sky aft lit up. Not lightning — or not only lightning. The back wall of the monsoon, towering behind them, was strobing internally, sheet after sheet of silent white fire rolling through the cloud towers like thoughts passing through a vast mind, and the light came down the swell faces ahead of them in long silver runners, so that for a few seconds at a time they were sailing on a road of light with the darkness chasing.

Bo whooped. Actually whooped, hanging on the stern rail with spray hammering him, laughing into the wind like a man at a carnival. And Tomas, soaked, deafened, running an engine past every limit he'd sworn by, found himself laughing too — a helpless, cracked-bell laugh that hurt his ribs — because it was absurd, all of it, the debt and the permits and the dead brother and the stranger with her blade of grief, and here at the bottom of the world's worst weather the answer was simply to *go faster*, and the sea, for once, agreed.

* * *

Priya stood her trick at the helm beside Oscar and navigated by feel, because the radar was painting nothing useful inside the echo chamber of the eyewall's remains and the GPS drifted and lied.

She navigated by the wind on her neck, by the period of the swells, by the sound the hull made in the troughs versus the crests, by the compass card trembling in its bowl and by the shape of the darkness ahead, which her eyes insisted on organizing into texture — harder black to starboard where a cell still stood, a bruised and thinning black ahead where the storm was spending itself against the coast. Ninety miles. Sixty. The bearing note changed pitch under their feet and Tomas throttled back and the Kestrel settled into the swells alone, unpowered, running on the storm's own breath, and Priya discovered that she wasn't afraid.

That was the strange gift of it. Six days of fear had burned something away, and what remained was a navigator's pure attention, cold and bright, a mind with nothing in it but the next heading. She thought of her mother's house in Colombo, of the money she owed and the reasons she owed it, and those thoughts passed through her like the rain — present, real, and unable to touch her. Later there would be time to be afraid again. Right now there was only the helm, the wind, the line she had drawn on the chart becoming real mile by mile beneath the keel.

Once, in the third hour, Oscar spoke without turning his head. "My brother would have loved this."

Priya kept her eyes on the darkness. "Yes," she said. "I think he would."

"He used to talk about riding a storm's back side. Said it was the only honest speed there is. Everything else is fighting. That's just falling with style."

"Then we're falling with style," Priya said, "at thirteen knots."

And Oscar laughed — short, startled out of him, the first laugh she'd heard from him since Maren Kade came back aboard — and the sound lasted exactly as long as the crest they were riding, and then the bow dropped into the trough and the night took them both again.

* * *

They cleared the storm's outer band at 2230, and the change was physical, like stepping out of a crowd.

The wind fell to twenty knots and then fifteen, still astern, still pushing, but sane now, ordinary, a wind you could speak in. The swells lengthened and gentled. Stars appeared in ragged holes overhead — actual stars, hard and cold, and Priya took a fix off them with hands that shook only afterward, once it was safe to shake, confirming what the dead-reckoning had claimed: they were forty-one miles from Sable Shoals, ahead of schedule, ahead of *any* schedule, having crossed ground in one night that should have cost them two days and most of their fuel.

Forty-one miles. At their battered cruising speed, dawn.

She wrote the position in the log with a pencil stub, and her hand was steady until she wrote the time, and then it wasn't, and she sat for a moment in the chart house letting the shaking happen, because the alternative was letting it happen at the helm.

Below, she could hear Tomas talking to the engine, low and continuous, a man keeping a promise aloud. She could hear Bo singing something tuneless while he rigged lines. She could hear, fainter, Oscar and Maren's voices at the bow, low, urgent, stopping and starting — the conversation they'd been circling for a decade finally catching fire, now, at the end of the world's worst week, with land coming.

And she heard something else. Faint, from the radio bench, where the set had been dead for two days: a click. Then another. Then, threading up out of the static like a body rising through dark water, a voice — clipped, cheerful, unmistakably *pleased* — saying words that froze the blood in Priya's wrists:

*"—Kestrel, Kestrel, this is Vantor Maritime, do you copy? We see you coming, Oscar. We see you coming beautifully. Be sure to hurry — the buyer's representatives arrived at Sable Shoals this evening, and they are asking such interesting questions—"*

Priya's hand closed on the transmit key.

Behind her, footsteps on the companionway ladder.
