## 19. Confrontation at the Concession

The storm let them go the way a fist opens—slowly, finger by finger, leaving the shape of itself pressed into everything it had held. By the time the Kestrel came around the last headland of Sable Shoals, the sky was the color of scoured tin and the sea still carried a long, angry swell, but the wind had dropped to something almost conversational. Almost kind. Oscar didn't trust it. He didn't trust anything anymore that arrived wearing calm.

He stood at the wheel with his hands aching from two days of holding on, and he watched the concession come up out of the haze.

Sable Shoals wasn't a port so much as an argument between land and water that the water was slowly winning. A crescent of black sand. A breakwater built from concrete tetrapods gone gray and furred with salt. Behind them, a compound of prefabricated buildings on stilts, a crane, a fuel farm, a radio mast with its guy-wires singing in the leftover wind—and above it all, the flag of Meridian Extraction Services snapping wet and heavy, like a bedsheet someone had drowned.

Six years since he'd seen it. It looked smaller. Everything looked smaller when you finally stopped running toward or away from it.

"You're going to want to tie off on the seaward bollards," Priya said beside him, her chart case under her arm like a shield. "The inner ones are rotted through. I can see it from here."

"Then we'll use the seaward ones."

"Oscar." She lowered her voice. Bo was forward, coiling line; Tomas was below, listening to the engine with a stethoscope and the expression of a man who'd made peace with a dying relative. Maren stood at the rail, arms crossed, watching the shore the way you watch a door you're not sure you want to walk through. Ilsa Voss sat on the deck locker with the waterproof case in her lap, both hands flat on top of it, perfectly still, the way people are still when stillness is all they have left to hold onto.

"I know what you're going to say," Oscar said.

"Then say it to yourself first, so I don't have to."

He almost smiled. "We're past the part where this is smart."

"We were past that part six days ago." Priya looked out at the compound. "I ran every approach. There's one channel in, one channel out, and the tide turns against us at fourteen hundred. If we're not back aboard by then, we're staying whether we like it or not."

"So we're back by then."

"That's what I'm telling myself." She adjusted the chart case. "For what it's worth—I've decided I believe her. Voss. The report was falsified. I pulled the archived pressure data off the relay buoy before the storm ate the signal. Somebody wrote a forecast for a system that didn't exist, and buried the one that did."

Oscar felt that land somewhere behind his sternum, in the old bruise. "You could have told me sooner."

"You could have asked." She said it without heat, which made it worse. Then she went forward to supervise Bo, because that was how Priya loved people: by giving them work to do together.

* * *

They tied off at 11:40. The tide would turn at fourteen hundred. Three hours and twenty minutes to spend the only currency Oscar Delacroix had left, and he had spent most of his life learning exactly how little that currency was worth.

Meridian's compound smelled of diesel, mildew, and coffee that had been kept warm too long as an act of hostility. A junior man in a company jacket met them at the gate with a clipboard and the particular blankness of someone paid not to be curious, and led them down a corridor of prefab to a room with a long table, eight chairs, and a window that looked out on nothing but the gray sea—the sea that had taken Eli, presented here as a view, as if it were an amenity.

The buyer was already there.

His name was Corren Hale, and Oscar remembered him from six years ago—a younger man then, a regional operations manager with a firm handshake and eyes that never quite landed on you, the way a gull never lands on water it doesn't intend to take something from. Now he was older, silvered at the temples, better tailored, and he rose from his chair with the warmth of a man greeting clients rather than ghosts.

"Delacroix." The handshake was dry and precise. "And Kade. My God. It's been—it must be six years." His gaze moved across them, cataloguing: Maren, weathered and watchful; Tomas with his oil-stained hands; Bo, who looked like a friendly dog brought into a vet's office; Priya with her charts; and last, longest, Ilsa Voss, whose name meant nothing to him. Oscar watched him decide she meant nothing. He watched him be wrong.

"You knew we were coming," Oscar said.

"I hoped you were coming. The permits expire in—" Hale consulted nothing, because men like Hale never needed to consult anything, "—four days now, isn't it? Nineteen-day window, monsoon season, and yet here you are. That's seamanship, Mr. Delacroix. That's why Meridian pays what it pays for reliable crews." He gestured to the chairs. "Please. Sit. Coffee?"

"No," said Ilsa.

It was the first word she'd spoken since the dock, and it landed in the room like a cleat dropping onto a deck. Hale blinked at her, recalibrating, filing her under difficult vendor, and smiled wider.

"Of course. Straight to business. I respect that." He opened a leather folder on the table. "The Sable Shoals concession covers salvage rights across eleven square kilometers of shoal field, plus priority moorage and refueling. Six-year term, renewable. Your crew would operate under our weather desk—we provide routing advisories, storm windows, the full forecasting package. As you know, our meteorological service is—"

"Is excellent," Oscar said. "That's what everyone says."

"It is excellent," Hale agreed pleasantly. "State-of-the-art buoy network. Redundant telemetry. We've never lost a vessel operating under Meridian advisories."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the leftover swell hit the breakwater in a slow, heavy rhythm, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Never," Oscar repeated.

"Never." Hale's smile held. Whatever he saw in Oscar's face, he read it as fatigue. Storm crews were always tired; tired men signed things. "Now, if we can get to terms—"

"Before terms," Oscar said, "there's a matter of record-keeping."

"A matter—"

"Six years ago," Oscar said, "a boat called the *Cormorant* went out of this concession under a Meridian advisory. Two men aboard. The advisory said the system would track north-northeast, well clear of the shoals. It tracked south-southwest instead. Directly over them. Both men died." He laid it out flatly, the way Priya laid out charts, because that was the only way he could lay it out at all without his voice breaking apart in his hands. "I've spent six years believing that was weather. Bad luck. The sea doing what the sea does. I've recently been given reason to believe otherwise."

Hale's face did something small and fast—not guilt, not quite; more like a man catching a card slipping from his sleeve and pressing it flat with his thumb. "Mr. Delacroix. Any loss at sea is a tragedy. But I'd caution you—second-guessing archived forecasts serves no one. The data was reviewed. Internally. Thoroughly."

"By whom?"

"By the people qualified to review it."

"Show us," Ilsa said.

Hale turned to her with the patience of a man humoring weather. "I'm sorry, Ms.—"

"Voss. Ilsa Voss." She set the waterproof case on the table. Her hands were steady. Oscar thought: she has practiced this. Not the speech—the steadiness. She has practiced being steady the way I practiced running. "My brother was Daniel Voss. Deckhand, age twenty-six, aboard the *Cormorant* the night of October ninth, six years ago. You may not remember him. I remember everything about him, including the fact that your company sent him to sea on a forecast that your own lead forecaster had flagged as internally inconsistent forty hours before departure. The flag was overridden. I'd like to see the override authorization."

The silence that followed had texture. Bo shifted his weight. Tomas stopped looking at the wall and started looking at Hale.

Hale laughed—a short, controlled sound, the laugh of a man buying time. "Ms. Voss, I understand grief makes people search for—"

"Don't," she said quietly. "Don't do the thing where you explain my grief to me. I brought my own documents."

She opened the case. Inside, sealed in plastic sleeves against the damp that had tried to destroy them for six years, were papers: printouts of buoy telemetry with timestamps; a copy of the internal forecast bulletin, its correction flag visible even in a bad photocopy; a chain-of-custody log; and a single sheet bearing a signature block that Oscar recognized, because he had signed documents with that same block, in this same building, six years ago.

"The original bulletin," Ilsa said, laying the pages out one by one, "forecast a southward track. Someone revised it to north-northeast. The revision cites a secondary model run that does not exist in the archive. I checked. For three years, I checked. The archive has no such run. What it does have—" she tapped the signature sheet, "—is an authorization for the revision, signed by the regional operations manager on duty. Who was promoted eighteen months later. Who is sitting in front of me."

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way wrecks happen—fast, then slow, then fast again.

Hale reached for the papers. Ilsa took them back. Hale said something about forgery, about legal exposure, about how she had no standing in these waters, and Priya said, evenly, "She has copies. Certified, timestamped, lodged with a maritime attorney in Port Callas and with the coastal authority's accident review board. Filing occurred four days ago. Destroying anything in this room changes nothing." She said it the way she reported a tide turn: factual, unhurried, final. Hale looked at her and understood, visibly, that the navigator had already charted every exit from this conversation and found them all closed.

So he changed tactics, the way drowning men do. He turned to Oscar.

"Delacroix." His voice dropped into something warmer, confidential, the register of men who do business in back rooms. "Whatever grievance this woman has, it isn't yours. You came here for a concession. Four days left on your permits—you think anyone else will process you in four days? In monsoon season? I'm your only buyer, and we both know it. Sign today, and whatever this is—" a gesture at the papers, at Ilsa, at the dead, "—stays outside this room. Meridian takes care of its crews. You know that. You knew that six years ago."

And there it was. The hook, baited with the same thing it had always been baited with: survival. Keep moving, keep owing, keep the truth filed under tragedy and move along. Oscar felt the old pull of it in his chest, the familiar current, six years strong. Sign the paper. Take the money. Stay ahead of the Vantors, stay ahead of the memory, stay ahead of the day when someone asks him directly—

Maren stood up.

She hadn't said a word since the gate. She'd stood at the back of the room with her arms crossed like a woman waiting out weather, and Oscar had told himself that was mercy, or cowardice, or both. Now she walked to the table, put both hands flat on it, and looked at Corren Hale the way she looked at a squall line she intended to sail straight through.

"You want testimony?" she said. "Here's testimony. I was on the water that night. Eleven miles southeast of the shoals, running the same concession route you're selling right now. I heard the *Cormorant*'s last transmission on channel sixteen. Daniel Voss, reporting his position, asking for updated tracking on the system because his barometer had fallen ninety points in an hour and the advisory didn't match his sky." Her voice stayed level, which cost her; Oscar could see what it cost, in the tendons of her neck, in the white of her knuckles. "Your desk told him the system was tracking away. I heard it. I logged it. I've kept that log for six years because I kept waiting for someone else to say it first, and nobody did, because everybody who could say it was either bought or broken."

"Maren." Hale's smile was gone now. What was underneath was younger and uglier. "Think carefully about what you're—"

"I've thought about nothing else for six years." She pulled a folded, oil-stained notebook from inside her jacket and set it on the table next to Ilsa's papers. "Radio log, October ninth, timestamps included. It's been in a waterproof bag in my seabag ever since. I told myself I was keeping it safe. I was keeping it buried. Those aren't the same thing, and I'm done pretending they are."

Oscar's throat closed. Because he knew what wasn't in that notebook. He knew the other half of it, the half Maren had carried alone so he wouldn't have to—the order, the signature, the nineteen-year-old desperation of a man who'd signed the *Cormorant* out into a closing sky because the schedule said so and the money said so and his brother had grinned and said *we've outrun worse*. Maren had covered for him for ten years—no, six, though it felt geological—and she was standing here spending the truth she'd hoarded, and she was still, even now, protecting him. Leaving his name out of it. Spending herself instead.

He couldn't let her pay alone. Not this time. Not when paying alone was the whole disease.

"There's more," Oscar said.

Every head turned. Hale's eyes narrowed; Ilsa's went wide and sharp; Maren closed her own for half a second, and when she opened them there was something in them that might have been relief.

"The concession contract that year," Oscar said. "The dispatch authorization for the *Cormorant*. The skipper's signature on the departure order." He put his hand flat on the table, next to Maren's notebook, because his hands needed something true to rest on. "It's mine. I signed the boat out. I read the advisory, and my gut said wrong, and I signed anyway, because the schedule paid double and I owed money and my brother wanted the run. The falsified forecast didn't kill them on its own. It killed them because I believed it—or told myself I believed it—and I signed. That's the whole of it. That's what I've been running from for six years, and running hasn't worked, so I'm done."

The room held still. Outside, the swell boomed against the tetrapods. Somewhere below, the tide was beginning to think about turning.

Hale recovered first, because men like Hale always recover first; it was practically the job description. "An emotional confession, Mr. Delacroix. Touching. Legally meaningless, of course. A grieving skipper's self-flagellation proves nothing about Meridian's—"

"It proves the timeline," Priya said. "Signed departure order, timestamped against the flagged bulletin and the false revision. Three documents, three witnesses, one attorney, one review board. Your forecaster flagged it. Your office overrode it. Your manager signed the override and got promoted." She gathered her chart case. "We didn't come here to negotiate, Mr. Hale. We came here to hand you the bill."

"And here's the arithmetic," Ilsa said, and her voice finally cracked—not with weakness, but with the effort of six years held at a controlled temperature, thawing all at once. "Two men. Daniel Voss and Elias Delacroix. One falsified report. One promotion. One company that has since sold concessions on the strength of a safety record built on their graves." She leaned across the table, close enough that Hale leaned back. "The review board convenes in Port Callas in three weeks. The coastal authority has subpoena power over Meridian's charter licenses—all of them, every concession in the basin. You can testify first and keep something, or testify last and keep nothing. That's not a threat. That's a weather report. And unlike yours, mine is accurate."

* * *

They walked out at 13:50, ten minutes inside Priya's deadline, into air that tasted of salt and ending.

Hale had not signed anything. He had not admitted anything. He had sat very still while they placed the truth on the table like ballast, and then he had said, in a voice sanded smooth, "You'll hear from Meridian's counsel," which was what guilty men said instead of *no*, and everyone in the room had known it.

But as they passed through the gate, Hale caught Oscar's arm—one grip, brief, the handshake finally honest because it was finally hostile.

"You understand what you've done," Hale said quietly. "Three weeks. Do you know what happens to a crew with no concession, no permits, and a reputation? The whole coast will hear about this by Friday."

"I'm counting on it," Oscar said.

He meant it. That was the strange part. Six years of never stopping, never owing twice, never letting the truth catch up—and now he'd handed the truth a head start and a fair wind, and he felt lighter than he had since the *Cormorant* went down. Not happy. Lighter. The difference mattered.

Behind them, the Meridian flag snapped on its mast. Ahead, the Kestrel rode her lines in the turning tide, patched, leaking a little, holding. Bo waved from the rail like a man greeting family coming home from church. Tomas was already on deck, and Priya had the tide tables open, and Maren fell into step beside Oscar without a word, because some things didn't need words yet—they needed distance first, and time, and maybe never.

Ilsa stopped at the gangway. She looked back once at the compound, at the flag, at the gray water beyond the breakwater where her brother's boat and Oscar's brother's boat were the same boat, in the way that mattered.

"Three weeks," she said. "Port Callas."

"Three weeks," Oscar agreed. "We'll be there."

She nodded and went aboard, and Oscar stood a moment longer on the dock, watching the tide begin to turn against them, watching the first new clouds build far out over the open water—because there were always more clouds; that was the one forecast he'd never needed help with—and he realized he was smiling, tiredly, at the horizon like a man who had finally stopped running from weather and learned, at last, the harder trick:

reading it.

Then Priya called from the wheelhouse that they had twelve minutes of favorable tide and a hundred miles of coast between them and Port Callas, and that a Vantor cutter had been sighted anchored off the headland since dawn—waiting, patient, cheerful—and Oscar stepped aboard, and said, "Cast off."
