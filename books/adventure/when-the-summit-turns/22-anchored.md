## 22. Anchored

The Kestrel came out of the water on a Tuesday, hauled up the marine railway at Port Anselm by a winch that groaned like an old man being asked to dance. Oscar stood at the base of the slip with his hands in his pockets and watched her settle into the cradle, and for the first time in eleven years of owning her he looked at her hull without a schedule burning in his chest.

She was worse than he'd let himself believe. The surveyor, a squat woman named Ferro with sawdust in her hair, walked the length of the keel and clicked her tongue the way other people said *I told you so*.

"You ran this boat through a monsoon," she said.

"I did."

"On purpose."

"Mostly on purpose." He crouched beside her, touched the seam aft of the engine bed where the leak had lived all those years, patient as a tenant. The caulking had gone to powder. There was a hairline fracture running from the seam toward the garboard strake, thin enough to miss if you weren't looking, wide enough to drink the sea one swallow at a time. "How long?"

Ferro shrugged. "Years. She's been telling you about it every time you pumped. You just never listened slow enough to hear what she was saying."

Oscar thought about that. Tomas had hidden the damage in the storm, rationed pump time like a man rationing water in a lifeboat, and the crew had bailed by hand through the black hours rather than say aloud what they all knew: that the Kestrel was dying and they were asking her to carry them anyway. He'd forgiven Tomas before the words were even out of his mouth. It was hard to be angry at a man for keeping a secret you'd been keeping too.

"How much to do it right?" he asked.

Ferro named a number. It wasn't small. It also wasn't the Vantors, and that made it feel almost like nothing—a debt with an end date, a debt you could look in the eye.

"Do it right," Oscar said.

* * *

The harbor at Port Anselm filled and emptied around him while the Kestrel sat in the yard. He learned its rhythms the way he'd once learned weather systems—by standing still long enough for them to come to him. The fish boats went out before dawn with their running lights doubled, came back mid-morning riding low and smelling of brine and diesel. The concession barges tied up at the government pier on Thursdays. A woman sold coffee from a cart near the chandlery and remembered everyone's order after two days, which was one day faster than most harbors managed.

He took a job the third week, mostly because Priya told him to.

"You're haunting my chart table," she said. She'd rented a room above the chandlery and spread her charts across every flat surface, replotting the Sable Shoals run over and over, not because she needed to but because her hands wanted work. "Either get a job or get a hobby. And don't say weather. You've done your time with weather."

The job found him before he could argue. A salvage outfit run by a broad-shouldered man called Okafor needed someone who knew how to read a barometer and wasn't afraid of open water in shoulder season. Local runs. Day trips, mostly. Wreck assessment, buoy tending, the occasional tow when a pleasure boat died ten miles out and started drifting toward the shoals. The pay was honest and unremarkable. Nobody smiled at him the way the Vantors smiled. Nobody mentioned interest or patience or family.

His first week, Okafor watched him check the forecast four times in an hour and finally said, "You know the sky's still going to be there tomorrow, right? You don't have to catch it before it changes."

Oscar laughed, and it surprised him coming out. "Habit."

"It's a bad habit. Out here we wait for weather. We don't chase it." Okafor handed him a thermos. "Chasing's for men running from something."

Oscar drank the coffee and didn't argue, because the man was right, and because for the first time the thing he'd been running from was sitting still behind him instead of gaining.

* * *

Tomas stayed through the refit. He couldn't help himself; the Kestrel was on the hard twenty meters from his rented bunk and he visited her every day like a relative in hospital. He crawled the bilge with a flashlight clamped in his teeth. He argued with Ferro about fasteners. He rebuilt the raw-water pump twice because the first rebuild didn't satisfy him, and the second time he brought it to Oscar's little room above the harbor office and set it on the table between them like evidence.

"She'll hold now," Tomas said. "Not sort of hold. Hold."

"You could've told me about the leak years ago."

Tomas looked at the pump, then at him. "You'd have stopped running."

"Yeah," Oscar said. "That was the idea, I think. Yours, I mean."

Tomas had a reason he couldn't leave, same as all of them—two daughters in a school three towns inland, an ex-wife who answered his calls now, a pension that required him to be findable. But he'd run with Oscar for six years anyway, pumping the bilge and saying nothing, and they both understood without discussing it that the silence had been a kind of loyalty and also a kind of cowardice, and that both things could live in the same man at the same time.

"I'm taking the ferry Friday," Tomas said. "To see the girls. Then back Monday for the sea trial."

"Take Tuesday too."

Tomas grinned. "Look at you. Giving away days like a rich man."

* * *

Bo got a job on a fish boat inside the week and loved it with his whole uncomplicated heart. He came by the yard in the evenings smelling of mackerel and salt, sat on an upturned dinghy, and talked while Oscar scraped old antifouling off the Kestrel's belly. Bo talked the way weather moved—everywhere at once, no particular direction, but somehow covering everything.

"—and Marta says the skipper's superstitious, won't whistle aboard, so obviously I whistled, just once, just to see, and nothing happened, which proves either there's no curse or I'm immune, and honestly either way it's good news—"

"Bo."

"—also Ilsa sent a letter, did I tell you? From upcoast. She's got a lawyer now, a real one, and the inquiry's official. They're subpoenaing the weather service records. Six years of them. She says—" Bo paused, and for once his voice came down out of the wind. "She says thank you. For staying for the hearing. She says nobody ever stayed before."

Oscar kept scraping. The scraper whispered along the hull. "I'm not sure I stayed. I think I just ran out of places that made sense."

"That's what staying is," Bo said. "For people like us."

* * *

Maren left first and came back last.

She'd stood at the concession office in Sable Shoals and testified in a flat, unadorned voice about what she'd seen the night of the wreck—the lights of the *Corvina* going out, the falsified storm bulletin, the order signed in Oscar Delacroix's hand sending a boat with a cracked hull into a system every honest forecaster had flagged red. She'd given the company the truth like a bill presented for payment, and the company had folded, because companies always fold when the arithmetic turns against them. The concession rights had become leverage overnight. Oscar had traded them to the Vantors before the ink dried on the inquiry's findings, and the Vantors had accepted with the delight of people who appreciated a good joke even when the punchline cost them.

Old Man Vantor had shaken his hand at the settlement. "You paid us with something that wasn't yours yet," he'd said, warm as brandy. "That's the best kind of payment. We'll make it ours. That's what we do." And he'd meant it kindly, which was the frightening part, and Oscar had walked out of that office lighter than he'd felt since he was twenty-two years old.

Now Maren's boat appeared on the horizon on a gray morning, and Oscar watched it come in from the harbor wall with his heart doing something complicated and unwelcome.

She tied up at the fuel dock. She climbed down onto the quay. She looked older than she had at the start of all this, or maybe just less armored, the way a coastline looks different once you've seen it from behind.

"Delacroix," she said.

"Kade."

They stood there. Ten years of unsaid things had been mostly said by now—in the storm, in the inquiry room, in the long night afterward when they'd read the names of the dead aloud at the harbor rail and neither of them had flinched from their own part in the list. What was left unsaid was smaller now. Smaller things are harder to say.

"The yard tells me she floats dry," Maren said, nodding toward the railway where the Kestrel waited.

"Sea trial Monday. You're welcome aboard."

"I heard. Tomas has been broadcasting it on channel sixteen like it's a christening." She put her hands in her jacket pockets. "I'm not staying, Oscar. I want you to know that up front. I've got a contract, a boat, a route. I'm leaving Thursday."

"Okay."

"But I'll be back. Seasonally. The way migratory things come back." She looked at him sideways. "If the harbor's still here."

"The harbor's not going anywhere," Oscar said. "Neither am I. That's the whole point."

Maren studied him for a long moment, looking for the flinch, the exit, the old restless animal looking past her shoulder at the horizon. She didn't find it. Whatever she found instead made her mouth soften by a degree.

"Huh," she said. "You're actually going to be somewhere."

"Don't sound so disappointed."

"I'm not disappointed. I'm recalibrating." She stepped closer, briefly, and pressed her forehead against his—the old salute from their racing days, bow to bow, before anything had broken between them. Then she stepped back, and it was over, and it wasn't, and both of those were fine. "Thursday, Delacroix. Watch me leave. It's good practice."

"For what?"

"For watching me come back."

* * *

Monday dawned clear with a light offshore breeze, the kind of day that would have made the old Oscar itch, a day too calm to outrun anything, a day with nowhere to hide. The crew gathered at the railway at seven: Tomas back from his weekend with sand still on his boots, Priya with her charts rolled under her arm out of pure habit, Bo off the fish boat early with permission and a bag of pastries, Ilsa upcoast but present in the form of a letter Bo read aloud while they worked the cradle controls, lowering the Kestrel toward the water she'd been leaking into for a decade.

The letter ended: *Tell the boat I said thank you too. She carried you all further than she should have. Some of us know what that's like.*

The Kestrel touched the water and sat there. No gurgle from the bilge. No whisper of the sea coming home through the seams. Tomas went below with a flashlight and stayed for ten minutes and came up with tears he pretended were bilgewater, and nobody corrected him.

They took her out past the breakwater. Priya gave Oscar a heading that went nowhere in particular—a lazy loop around the bay, past the fish grounds, out to the bell buoy and back. Bo ate pastries at the helm and fed pieces to nobody and hummed. Tomas listened to the engine like a doctor listening to a heart. And Oscar stood at the wheel with the wind on his cheek and realized, with the mild shock of a man finding money in an old coat, that he wasn't counting anything. Not days. Not exits. Not the distance between the boat and the horizon. Just the boat, and the water, and the people on it.

At the buoy they stopped. Engines idling. Gulls working the current line. Priya leaned on the rail beside him.

"So," she said. "Crew meeting. Informal. Everyone decides their own course—that was the deal, right? You said it at the inquiry, you said it on the dock. No more signing people onto sinking ships."

"That was the deal."

"Tomas is back Monday. Bo's got his fish boat but he wants Sundays. I've got charter work lined up through the season, and I'd—" she paused, and Priya pausing was rarer than a fair wind in monsoon season, "—I'd like to keep the chart table. If there's still a chart table."

"There's a chart table."

"And you? What's your course, Skipper?"

Oscar looked at the harbor. At the yard where the Kestrel would tie up in an hour. At the coffee cart, the chandlery, the quay where Maren would cast off Thursday and where, some months from now, a gray boat would round the breakwater again. At the whole small, fixed, reachable world of it.

"Right here," he said. "I'm taking the local job year-round. Fixing what breaks. Being findable." He turned the wheel and the Kestrel came about, slow and sure, her hull dry as a promise kept. "Turns out stopping doesn't cost more than running. Turns out it never did. I just had to stay in one place long enough to get the invoice."

Priya laughed. Bo raised a pastry in salute. Ahead of them the harbor opened its arms, and the Kestrel went home under a sky that wasn't going anywhere either—and on the fuel dock, already tying up, earlier than she'd said she would, a gray boat with a familiar name on her transom waited like an answer to a question Oscar hadn't asked out loud yet.

He eased the throttle forward.

Some arrivals, he was learning, you sail to meet.
