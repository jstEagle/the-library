## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning the world was still ordinary, Hazel Oyelaran woke at four-thirty to the sound of his own breath fogging in the cold, and for a long moment he lay in the bunk of his cabin listening to the silence the way other men listened to music.

It was a good silence. The kind you could trust. No wind in the wires, no sleet ticking against the tin roof, no radio crackling with somebody's emergency. Just the mountain holding its breath, and Hazel holding his, and between them an understanding that had taken eleven years to negotiate: *you leave me alone, I'll leave you alone.*

He got up, lit the stove, and put the kettle on. Outside the window, dawn was still only a rumor — a gray suggestion behind the black teeth of the range — but he'd flown enough dawns to know it would arrive on schedule. Dawn always did. It was one of the few things in his life that never required him to make allowances.

The kettle sang. He made coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in, which was how his brother used to make it, which was why he made it that way, though he'd have bitten off his own tongue before admitting it.

There was a list pinned to the door of the icebox, written last night in pencil, because pencil forgave and ink didn't:

- Insulin — Kettle Creek (Mrs. Amos)
- Mail, general — Kettle Creek
- Vet supplies — Kettle Creek (the Hendersons' mare, foaling)
- Flour, nails, lamp oil — Two Rivers
- One (1) harmonica — Two Rivers (Sammy Flett, age 9, payment pending)

Hazel read the list twice, then folded it into the breast pocket of his flight jacket, where it joined the other folded things that lived there: a spare fuel chit, a photograph he never looked at, and a small brass compass his father had given him with the instruction *this points north; so should you.* His father had been a deacon. Hazel had become a pilot. Between those two callings there was, in his experience, roughly the same amount of faith required and considerably better views.

* * *

By six o'clock the Otter was loaded and the frost on her wings was scraped back to bare aluminum, and Hazel stood in the doorway of the hangar doing what he did every morning before every flight, which was nothing. He simply stood. He looked at the airplane, and the airplane looked back at him with all the patience of a machine that had never once lied to him about anything.

Her name was *Josephine*, painted on the nose in letters that needed repainting, and she was older than most of the people he served and younger than none of his regrets. She had carried him through weather that the manuals said no aircraft should survive, and she had done it without complaint, which was more than could be said for any woman he'd ever courted, any partner he'd ever flown with, or any government official he'd ever had the misfortune to sit across from.

"Morning, Jo," he said.

She didn't answer. That was another thing he liked about her.

He ran the preflight by touch more than sight — control surfaces, fuel caps, pitot cover off, tires, struts — and somewhere in the middle of it his hands stopped being his hands and became the ritual's hands, moving through the sequence the way a priest moves through a liturgy. Eleven years of mornings. You didn't count them. Counting was how you noticed they were going by.

The engine caught on the third blade, coughed, thought about it, and settled into that deep uneven rumble that Hazel privately considered the truest voice in the territory. Everything else lied. The radio lied. The newspapers lied. Men in offices with maps and red pencils lied professionally, as a matter of employment. But an engine either ran or it didn't, and when it ran, it told you exactly what it was every second you let it speak.

He taxied out onto the frozen lake that served as his strip and took off into the gray, and the mountains came up around him like a congregation rising.

* * *

Kettle Creek was ninety miles into the interior, which meant forty minutes in the Otter and four days by dogsled, and this arithmetic explained everything about Hazel's standing in the district. He was not loved, exactly. Love required presence, and Hazel was present the way weather was present — regularly, usefully, and without any intention of staying. But he was *relied upon*, which is its own kind of affection, and in the interior reliance runs deeper than love anyway.

Mrs. Amos met him at the creek landing with her coat buttoned wrong, which she did every time, and which Hazel had learned not to correct, because correcting it seemed to embarrass her more than wearing it wrong did.

"Insulin," she said, taking the box from him with both hands, the way you take something alive.

"Fresh as of Tuesday."

"You're a good man, Hazel Oyelaran."

"I'm a delivery man, Mrs. Amos."

"Same thing out here." She squinted up at him. She was seventy if she was a day, and she had the eyes of a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived both their opinions. "You eating? You look thin."

"I look aerodynamic."

"You look like your mother's cooking went to waste." She tucked the insulin inside her coat, against her heart, which was where she kept everything that mattered. "There's a man came through on the radio last night asking after pilots. Government fella. Wanted names."

Hazel kept his face pleasant and empty, which was a skill, and one of the few his father's church had actually taught him. "What kind of government?"

"The kind that asks questions it already knows the answers to." She patted his arm. "I gave him yours. Only name worth giving."

"That was generous of you."

"It was efficient of me." And she turned and crunched away over the snow toward her cabin, coat still buttoned wrong, leaving Hazel standing on the ice with the mail sack over his shoulder and a small cold stone settling into his stomach that he couldn't have named if you'd asked him to.

Government men didn't ask after bush pilots. Bush pilots asked after government men, usually to find out why their permits were late.

He shook it off. There was mail to deliver, and a foaling mare, and a harmonica owed to a nine-year-old who had been paying for it since October in the currency of arrowheads, agates, and one live frog that Hazel had declined to accept on grounds of aviation safety.

* * *

The Hendersons' mare delivered a leggy brown filly at some point during the night, and by the time Hazel arrived the whole family was standing around the stall looking stunned and delighted, the way people do at births, as though the process hadn't been going on since the Pleistocene and still managed to surprise everyone.

"Vet says keep her warm," Hazel said, handing over the kit. "And don't let the dog in here."

"The dog's terrified of the horse," said Sammy Flett, who was nine, freckled, and had followed Hazel from the strip like a small devoted satellite.

"Then we're agreed."

"Hazel. Hey. Hazel." Sammy tugged his sleeve. "Did you bring it?"

Hazel looked down at him with the gravity the moment deserved. "Brought what?"

"You *know* what."

"I know a lot of whats. I'm a man of many whats."

"*The harmonica.*"

Hazel reached slowly into the jacket pocket, past the fuel chit, past the photograph, past the compass, and produced the harmonica, and the look on that boy's face was the entire reason Hazel flew medicine and mail into country that could kill you in forty different ways and mostly didn't care which one it picked.

Sammy held it like it was made of eggshell and scripture. "How much do I owe?"

"Let's see the goods."

From his own pockets Sammy produced three arrowheads, a piece of rose quartz, and — with visible pain — a fourth arrowhead that was clearly his best one, chipped just so, the kind of thing a boy finds once in a lifetime along a creek bank and keeps in a matchbox under his bed.

Hazel looked at it a long moment. Then he took the first three and pushed the fourth back into the boy's mittened hand.

"Keep that one," he said. "You'll want it later, when you remember being nine."

"What happens when I'm nine that I'll want to remember?"

"That's the trick, isn't it," Hazel said, and swung himself back up into the plane before the boy could ask anything else, because Hazel Oyelaran was very good at leaving conversations at exactly the moment they started becoming about something.

* * *

Two Rivers by noon. Flour, nails, lamp oil, and old Tom Brandt waiting at the store porch with his hands in his pockets and the particular expression of a man preparing to discuss someone else's business.

"Hazel." Tom nodded at the sky, at the south, at nothing in particular. "You hear about the survey outfit?"

"The government lot, up past the divide?" Hazel lashed down the last crate. "What about them?"

"Nobody's heard from them. Going on two days now." Tom spat, expertly, into the snow. "Radio's been quiet as a church. And a fella come through yesterday — smooth coat, city boots, asking questions about the high passes. About the melt timing."

"Spring melt's a ways off yet."

"Fella didn't seem to think so." Tom looked at him sideways. "Fella seemed to think it was coming *early*."

Hazel tied off the knot, tested it, and found his hands were steady and his mind was not entirely. "Maybe he knows something the almanac doesn't."

"Maybe." Tom shrugged, the shrug of a man who had learned that the interior tells you its secrets on its own schedule and resents being hurried. "Or maybe there's something up in that ice somebody wants bad enough to come early for."

They stood a moment in the cold, two men watching the mountains the way farmers watch a sky that's gone the wrong color.

"Well," Hazel said finally. "Not my survey. Not my melt."

"No," Tom agreed. "Course not."

But neither of them moved, and the silence between them had a shape to it now, and Hazel recognized the shape because he'd felt it once before — the year his brother's plane went missing in the same range, and everyone in every settlement had stood exactly like this, looking at exactly those peaks, saying exactly nothing, while the mountain kept whatever it had taken.

He climbed back into the Otter. He flew home into the falling afternoon light with the peaks burning orange and then pink and then that blue that isn't a color so much as a temperature, and he landed on the lake ice in perfect stillness, and shut her down, and sat in the cockpit a minute longer than usual with his hands loose in his lap.

Eleven years. He didn't count them. Miles, not years — that was the rule. Miles you could measure, log, total up at the end of a season and feel like the number meant something solid. Years just drained away while you weren't watching, taking people with them.

He walked back to the cabin, lit the stove, and was reaching for the coffee tin when the radio on the shelf hissed, popped, and spoke.

At first it was only static — the deep, rolling static of the high bands at dusk. Then, underneath it, threading through it like wire through cloth, a voice. Thin. Broken. A callsign he knew — the survey team's frequency, the one he'd logged a dozen times without ever thinking twice about it.

"—mayday, mayday—" A burst of static tore the words apart. "—if anyone reads— we found—" Static, long and grinding. Then, fainter, almost swallowed: "—it's not natural, it's *in* the—"

And then nothing. Not fading, not trailing off. Cut. Clean as a switch thrown.

Hazel stood with the coffee tin in his hand, the cabin cold around him, staring at the little green eye of the radio as though it might blink.

Outside, the mountain held its breath.

So did he.
