## 2. The Quiet Frequency

The trouble with living alone at the edge of the map, Hazel Oyelaran had learned, was that you started talking to your airplane.

Not full conversations — he wasn't that far gone yet — but a running commentary, the way some men hummed. *Easy now, old girl,* as the Cessna bucked over the ridge line. *You and me both.* The mechanics in Copper Landing thought it was funny until they realized he did it with everything: the coffee percolator, the temperamental woodstove, the radio that crackled like a bag of chips whenever the aurora came out to dance. A man who lived alone, Hazel figured, kept house with whatever company he could get.

It was the radio he was talking to on the night it happened.

March still owned the interior. The days had stretched long enough to be useful but not long enough to be kind, and the cold came down off the summits every evening like a landlord collecting rent. Hazel was in his shack above the strip, boots propped on the stove rail, working through a bowl of stew that had gone slightly wrong in an interesting way, when the set spat static and then — impossibly, unmistakably — a voice.

"—any station, any station, this is Survey Six—"

Hazel's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

He knew the survey's schedule the way he knew the tide tables. The government team had been up in the high interior since January, poking at the glacier country with instruments and polite official optimism. He'd flown a resupply for them in February — four crates of tinned food, a replacement drill head, and a man named Okonkwo who'd talked the whole flight about his daughter's piano recital and how the mountains made him feel small in a way he'd decided was good for him. Hazel had liked him. He'd liked him the way he liked most people: briefly, from a safe altitude, without letting it cost anything.

"—Survey Six, we have—" The signal dissolved into a wash of static, then fought its way back, thinner. "—not weather, I say again, this is not—" A sound Hazel couldn't name. Not a crash. Not wind. Something between a shout and a door closing. Then, faintly, someone saying a word that might have been *Mara* or might have been nothing at all, and then—

Nothing. Just the long hiss of empty frequency, which sounded, in the quiet shack, exactly like breathing.

Hazel sat very still for a moment. Then he put down the spoon, wiped his hands on his trousers out of habit rather than need, and keyed the mic.

"Station calling Survey Six, this is Oyelaran, Copper Landing strip. Say again your last." He waited. Tried twice more on the guard channel, then swept the band the way a man pats his pockets for keys he knows are gone. Nothing. Whatever had come through had come through once, the way lightning does.

He looked at the clock. Nine-forty. The survey's base camp sat better than two hundred miles into the high country, past ridges that ate radio signals for breakfast, and at this hour there'd be no one airborne between here and there except himself and God, and God didn't fly at night in March.

So Hazel did the sensible thing. He wrote down the time, the frequency, and every word he could remember — *not weather* — underlined it twice, and got on the phone to the district office in Copper Landing, where he reached a man named Pell who answered on the eleventh ring sounding like he'd been personally insulted by the ringing.

"Pell. It's Oyelaran. I just caught a fragment off the survey team. Distress traffic."

A pause long enough to pour a drink through. "At this hour?"

"That's generally when things go wrong, Pell."

Another pause. When Pell spoke again, the sleepiness had been replaced by something smoother, something administrative. "You're certain it was them? Signal could've been anything. Skip off the ionosphere, bounce from a fishing boat two thousand miles south. Happens all the time this season."

"It said Survey Six. It said their callsign twice."

"Huh." Papers rustled, or were made to rustle. "Look, Oyelaran, I'll log it. First light, somebody'll look at the schedule, see when they're due to check in next. These survey outfits miss check-ins all the time. Batteries die. Antennas ice up. Fellas get busy playing cards and forget the radio exists. You know how it is."

Hazel did know how it was. That was what bothered him. "They checked in Tuesday. Regular as church. And the transmission I caught wasn't a missed check-in, Pell. It was a mayday that got cut off mid-word."

"And I'm sure it was very dramatic," Pell said, with the gentle patience of a man soothing a dog that had barked at the moon. "But I can't scramble anything at ten o'clock at night based on half a sentence and a feeling. You want to fly up there yourself in the dark, be my guest, but I'd have to write down that I advised against it, and neither of us wants the paperwork."

Hazel looked out the window at the black wall of the range, where the stars stopped abruptly, as if the mountains had drawn a curtain. Flying that country at night, alone, in March, looking for a camp he couldn't see — even he wasn't foolish enough to call that rescue. It would just be a second emergency with a witness.

"Log it," he said finally. "Time, frequency, content. Word for word."

"Word for word," Pell agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing to anything so long as it ended the call.

Hazel hung up and sat with his cooling stew and the hiss of the radio, and found that the unease hadn't left with the conversation. It had settled in instead, the way cold settles into a cabin overnight — not dramatic, just present, in the corners, waiting for morning.

He told himself it was nothing. He told himself Pell was probably right. He washed the bowl, banked the stove, and lay down, and lay awake listening to the wind work at the eaves, and somewhere in the small hours he slept, and dreamed of a door closing on a word he couldn't quite hear.

* * *

Morning made it worse by making it ordinary.

By eight, the sky was that hard, clean blue that pilots trust the way they trust a rope bridge — completely, and with one eye open. By nine, Hazel had the Cessna fueled and loaded with the week's mail run: letters, a crate of eggs wrapped in wool, a box of parts for the trapper at Kettle Lake who treated his snowmobile the way some men treat racehorses. Routine. The kind of flying he could do half-asleep, which was fortunate, because he was running on about four hours of it.

Before he took off, he called the district office again. This time he got a different voice — a woman named Brandt, brisk and competent, who actually pulled the file while he waited.

"Survey Six," she read. "Scheduled check-in yesterday, sixteen hundred. Missed. Standard procedure is a twenty-four-hour grace period before we flag it." She paused. "There's a note on the file from last night. Your report?"

"My report. And they missed another window this morning at oh-six-hundred, if the schedule I saw holds. That's two."

"That's one missed and one pending," Brandt corrected, not unkindly. "Mr. Oyelaran, I understand you know that country better than anyone on our list, and I understand you caught actual traffic, which counts for something. But the response protocol comes from further up than me, and further up has classified this as a communications fault pending review."

"A what?"

"A communications fault. Pending review." He could hear her choosing her words like footsteps on river ice. "There's... interest in this survey, Mr. Oyelaran. From above my floor. The equipment they're carrying is expensive and the data is sensitive, and nobody upstairs wants a rescue operation blundering around a site until they know what's actually needed. A review team is being assembled. It'll move when it moves."

"When it moves," Hazel repeated. "Brandt, those are eleven people. Okonkwo's got a daughter who plays piano. If they're sitting in the dark with a broken antenna, every hour we spend 'reviewing' is—"

"I know," she said, and for a second the briskness cracked and he heard the person underneath, tired and honest. "I know. But I count files, Mr. Oyelaran, and you count hours, and neither of us decides. Fly your mail run. Keep your ears open. If you catch anything else, call me direct — extension's on the pad. Not the main line."

"Not the main line," Hazel echoed. "Why not the main line?"

The pause was brief, but it had weight, like a stone dropped in a well that takes longer to hit bottom than it should. "Because the main line gets answered by whoever's nearest the phone," Brandt said, "and lately that's been Mr. Pell, and Mr. Pell has instructions I don't fully understand regarding survey matters. Good day, Mr. Oyelaran. Safe flying."

She hung up. Hazel stood in the shack with the receiver in his hand and the engine warming outside, and let the shape of it settle over him. Instructions. Regarding survey matters. On a file that was supposed to be a routine government job, the kind of thing that moved through offices like water through a culvert — unnoticed, unremarkable.

Somebody wanted the silence to last. He didn't know who, and he didn't know why, and he had no evidence beyond a smooth-voiced clerk and a well-timed phrase. But he'd spent fifteen years reading weather off ridgelines and intentions off voices, and the reading was the same either way: something was moving behind the front, and it wasn't clouds.

He flew the mail run.

That was the discipline of it, the thing his brother Tom had never understood about him back when Tom was alive to misunderstand it — that a man could carry a worry the size of a mountain in one pocket and a crate of eggs in the other, and give each its proper attention. Hazel flew low along the river because the light was flat, waved at the Kettle Lake trapper who came out shaking his fist at the snowmobile parts as though they were late on purpose, delivered a letter to a widow who pressed a jar of spruce-tip jelly into his hands and refused to take no, and all the while, beneath the drone of the engine, he kept one ear turned toward the sky like a dog toward a door.

Nothing. The frequencies stayed empty. The mountains kept their own counsel, white and enormous and indifferent, and by mid-afternoon Hazel was back on his own strip with the jelly and the uneasy conviction that the emptiness itself was information. Eleven people didn't go quiet all at once. Radios failed one at a time, batteries died one at a time, men forgot check-ins one at a time. Silence this complete wasn't an accident. It was a condition. Somebody had imposed it.

Or the mountain had. That was the other possibility, the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Avalanches happened. Whiteouts happened. Eleven people could vanish into the high country between one check-in and the next, leaving tents standing and no tracks, and the mountain wouldn't even consider it rude.

He was putting the plane to bed when the idea arrived, fully formed, the way the best and worst ideas do.

Nobody upstairs wanted a rescue operation blundering around the site. Fine. But Hazel Oyelaran wasn't a rescue operation. He was a bush pilot with a mail contract, a fuel allowance, and a longstanding habit of taking the long way home when the light was good. Tomorrow's run put him within forty miles of the survey's last coordinates anyway — a detour of twenty minutes, maybe thirty, if the pass was behaving. He could climb to altitude, swing a leg across the high country, have a look. Tents standing or tents flattened. Tracks or no tracks. Smoke or no smoke. One pair of eyes doing what the review team apparently couldn't bring itself to start.

And if he saw nothing? Then he'd have spent thirty minutes of fuel buying himself a night's sleep, and he'd log it as a sightseeing trip, and Pell could write down that he'd advised against that too.

Hazel stood in the failing light with his hand on the propeller, the metal biting cold through his glove, and had a short, honest argument with himself. The sensible man — the man he'd built, deliberately, over fifteen careful years, the man with rules against attachments and debts and reasons to land where he shouldn't — that man said *report it again tomorrow, let the machinery grind, keep your nose clean and your tail dry.*

But the other voice, the one that sounded a little like Tom and a little like the mountains themselves, pointed out that machinery grinds slowest precisely when someone is paying it to grind slow. And that Okonkwo had a daughter. And that *this is not weather* was a strange thing for a man to say unless he'd looked at the weather first and found it innocent.

"You and me both, old girl," Hazel murmured to the plane, and went inside to pack a survival kit he'd hoped not to need until spring.

* * *

He was up before dawn, which was not unusual, and in the air as the sun cleared the eastern ridges and set the whole white world briefly on fire, which was only unusual in that he wasn't carrying mail. He'd filed a flight plan — sightseeing, high country, returning via Kettle Lake — and the man at the flight service desk had raised one eyebrow and said "rough country for sightseeing," and Hazel had said "the light's good," and that had been the end of the scrutiny, because bush pilots were allowed their eccentricities right up until they weren't.

The pass was behaving. Better than behaving — the air was glass, the kind of stillness that made the engine sound intrusive, and the peaks stood up out of the morning haze like the backs of sleeping animals. Hazel climbed until the Cessna's wings felt thin and the world below resolved into the geography he carried in his head: the river braiding silver, the moraines like frozen grey rivers beside the living white ones, and there, ahead, the broad glacial valley where Survey Six had pitched its camp three months ago, close enough to the ice face to matter to the geologists and far enough to matter to everyone else.

Forty miles out, he tried the radio. "Survey Six, Survey Six, this is aircraft November Charlie Oyelaran, if you read, come back." Static. He tried again at twenty miles, and again at ten, feeling increasingly like a man knocking on a door in a house where all the curtains are drawn.

Then he was over the valley, and he throttled back, and looked down, and the unease that had been riding with him since nine-forty the night before finally put a name to itself.

The camp was there. Three tents, standing. The supply cache tarped and weighted, undisturbed. The radio mast up, guy wires taut, antenna pointing at the sky like a raised hand. Fresh snow had fallen — he could see it in the drift patterns, a clean two inches, maybe three, laid down sometime in the last day — and across that fresh snow there were no tracks at all. No footprints between the tents. No trail to the latrine trench. No path to the cache. No sign anywhere of the comings and goings of eleven human beings, as though the camp had been set down complete, like a model, and abandoned mid-sentence.

His eyes went to the ice face at the head of the valley, blue-white and ancient, and he caught himself thinking — absurdly, uselessly — *what did you find?*

Hazel circled once. Twice. On the third pass he saw the thing that finished the argument with the sensible man forever: the tent flap of the largest tent, the mess tent, hanging open a hand's width, and beneath it, half in shadow, the corner of something that might have been a table and might have been a body, and no way on earth to tell from five hundred feet.

He should climb. He should go home, call Brandt direct, describe exactly what he'd seen, and let the machinery grind — slowly, expensively, and on someone else's schedule.

Instead, Hazel Oyelaran, who had rules about this sort of thing, looked at the long smooth runway of wind-packed snow beside the frozen river, judged it in the way he could judge a strip in his sleep, and began his descent.

"Just a look," he told the plane. "In and out. Ten minutes."

The wheels touched snow with a soft crunch that sounded, in all that silence, like a question being asked.
