## 3. Gear and No Tracks

The morning of the third day after the transmission, the weather broke like a promise kept too late.

Hazel stood on the dock at Ternwater with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the ceiling lift off the peaks the way a blanket comes off a sleeping dog — slowly, then all at once. By seven the pass was open. By half past he had the mail bags loaded, the medicine crate strapped down where it wouldn't shift, and the *Kestrel* ticking as her engine cooled from the preheat.

He was halfway through his preflight when he made himself say it out loud, because saying things out loud was how you found out whether they were true.

"You're going to fly over their coordinates first."

The *Kestrel*, being an airplane and therefore honest, said nothing. But she didn't argue either, which was more than most people managed.

It wasn't sentiment. He told himself that twice, once while checking the fuel caps and once while taxiing out. It was efficiency. The survey camp sat eleven miles off his mail line to Kettle Inlet — a dogleg any sensible pilot would fly anyway if the wind favored it, which today it did. Eleven miles. Four minutes. A man would be a fool not to look.

A man would also be a fool to look, find nothing, and spend the rest of his life wondering what he'd missed. Hazel had learned that particular arithmetic from his brother, though the lesson had cost more than tuition should ever cost. You looked. That was the whole job. You looked, and then you decided what to do about what you saw.

He took off into air so clean it felt newly minted, climbed to nine thousand, and turned northeast toward the coordinates he'd copied off the survey's last scheduled position report — 61 degrees and change, tucked under the shoulder of a massif the old trappers called the Sleeping Priest because its summit profile suggested a man in a hooded cassock, face turned permanently away from the world below.

The mountains did what mountains do, which is pretend to be eternal and then quietly rearrange themselves. But Hazel had been reading this range for nine years, the way other men read newspapers, and he knew its grammar. He knew where the cornices built up and where the avalanches ran and which valleys held fog like a cupped hand holds water. He knew, without needing the map, exactly where a six-man government survey team would have pitched camp: on the broad gravel bench below the Priest's eastern shoulder, close to fresh water, sheltered from the north wind, with a long enough approach for a ski plane to get in and — this was the part amateurs forgot — *out* again.

At 7:52 by his wristwatch, he saw the tents.

Three of them, orange and black, standing neat as teeth in a jaw. He throttled back and let the *Kestrel* drift down along the bench, banking gently so he could read the ground the way you'd read a page.

Here is what he expected to see, if weather had taken the team: tents collapsed or shredded, gear scattered, maybe a cache dug into the snow, signs of struggle against the elements. Men caught by a storm dig in. They anchor everything. They leave a mess, because weather fights dirty and mess is what fighting looks like.

Here is what he actually saw:

The tents stood taut and squared, guy lines dressed properly, snow skirts banked around them with the tidy competence of men who expected to come back to them. The cook tent's stove pipe still rose straight, unbroken. Near the center of camp, a sled load of core boxes sat strapped and covered under a tarp, corners tied down with the kind of knots you learn before you're allowed near expensive equipment. A fuel drum stood upright beside the landing strip, cap on. Somebody had even propped a shovel against the drum, blade down, the way you lean a shovel when you mean to pick it up again in ten minutes.

No bodies. No tracks.

That second part was the one that put a cold wire down Hazel's spine, and he made himself look three times to be sure. Fresh snow had fallen — two inches, maybe three, light and dry, the kind that records everything. It lay smooth across the whole bench, unbroken from the tree line to the glacier toe, except for one set of ski-plane tracks leading in to the camp and none leading out. And no footprints anywhere. Not to the water. Not to the latrine trench, which he could see had been freshly dug. Not between the tents. Nothing.

Six men did not walk out of a camp without leaving prints in three inches of fresh snow. Six men did not *vanish* without leaving prints. Snow like that held a record the way wax holds a seal — every boot, every drag of a sled rope, every stumble written plain for anyone who came looking.

And nobody had come looking. That was the part that kept snagging, like a burr in a sock. The authorities had his report. The authorities had, in Hazel's professional opinion, reacted to a missing government survey team with roughly the urgency of a man responding to a neighbor's complaint about fence posts. Nine days since the garbled transmission. Nine days of "we're coordinating resources" and "conditions permitting." Conditions permitted now. The sky was clean as a bell. And Hazel was, as far as he could tell, the only man in the territory looking down at this camp.

Which meant either the government was incompetent — possible; he'd met governments — or the government was in no hurry for anyone to arrive.

He circled twice more. On the second pass he noticed something he'd missed: the survey's own plane wasn't there. They'd flown in — the ski tracks proved it — but their aircraft was gone, and there was no wreck on the bench, no scatter of aluminum on the glacier below, nothing in the trees. A plane doesn't evaporate. Either it had left under its own power, which raised the question of why the camp was abandoned mid-operation with the stove pipe still standing, or someone had removed it, which raised considerably worse questions.

Hazel looked at the fuel gauge, which said he had plenty. He looked at the camp, which said nothing at all, loudly. He looked at the sky, which was clear now but wouldn't stay clear — this time of year the weather came through the pass like trains through a station, and the next one was due by evening.

His better judgment, a small dry voice that sounded remarkably like his brother, said: *Land at Kettle Inlet, deliver the mail, file your report, let the professionals handle it.*

His better judgment had never spent a winter alone in the interior. His better judgment didn't know that "the professionals" meant a search party arriving four days late with a checklist and a form to fill out, and that forms don't answer questions like *where did six men go without their boots?*

Because that was the detail that finally decided him. He'd seen it on the last circle and hadn't understood it until now: the tents' flaps were all zipped shut from the outside. Every one. Men leaving a camp in a hurry — a storm, an injury, a bear — don't zip the tents behind them. Men who expect to return in ten minutes might. But those same men don't take the plane and leave the stove pipe standing.

Somebody had closed this camp the way you close a book you've finished reading. Carefully. Deliberately. From the outside.

"That's not weather," Hazel said aloud. The *Kestrel* shivered agreeably in the slipstream, which he chose to take as agreement. "Weather doesn't zip up."

He brought her around, lined up on the ski tracks, and landed against every prudent instinct he owned.

* * *

The snow squeaked under his boots, dry as salt. He tied the *Kestrel* down anyway — habit, and because a man who skips tie-downs on a calm day meets a windstorm by lunch — and stood for a moment just breathing the altitude and listening.

Silence. Not mountain silence, which is never really silent — it creaks and settles and drips, it breathes. This was a different quality of quiet. An emptied-house quiet. The bench felt like a room people had stepped out of.

He went to the nearest tent first and worked the zipper with a gloved hand, half expecting it to resist. It slid smoothly. Inside: four sleeping bags rolled and strapped, kit stowed, a paperback novel facedown on somebody's cot with a pencil marking the page. A man doesn't mark his page if he's fleeing for his life. A man marks his page if he thinks he'll be back tonight.

The cook tent held the real story. On the folding table, breakfast things sat washed and stacked — washed, Hazel noted, not abandoned crusted in a panic. The stove was cold but clean. On a clipboard hanging by the door, the day's work plan was filled out in a careful engineer's hand: *Core runs 4–6, upper bench. Radio check 0800.* Dated nine days ago. Below it, in different ink, someone had written a single word and then scratched it out so thoroughly the paper had thinned.

Hazel tilted the clipboard toward the light coming through the tent fabric. He couldn't make out the word. But the pressure of the pen had been hard enough to emboss it, and he ran his thumb across the grooves like a blind man reading.

Four letters. Maybe five. Starting with what felt like an *N*.

"Not now," he murmured, and pocketed the thought along with a growing certainty.

Outside, he walked the perimeter the way he'd want a man to walk it if it were his camp and his people missing. The latrine trench was fresh-dug and unused. The water hole at the creek had been chopped that morning — the ice skin over it was thin and new. Everything here said *we'll be back by supper.*

Except the snow. He made himself follow the full length of the bench, boots crunching, looking for anything — a track, a disturbance, a melt pattern where something had lain. At the far end, near where the glacier began its slow gray grind down from the Priest's shoulder, he found the only anomaly in a quarter mile of blank white: a patch of snow perhaps ten feet across that had been disturbed and then smoothed over. Deliberately smoothed — dragged flat with something wide, a toboggan or a shovel board, the surface left with faint parallel strokes like a bed made in a hurry.

Under the smoothing, the snow was compressed. Something heavy had been there. Something heavy had been *taken* from there, and someone had tried to erase the taking.

Hazel crouched and pressed his bare hand flat against the packed surface. Cold soaked up his palm. He thought about six men, or however many, loading something heavy onto a sled in the dark, dragging it out to a plane that had already gone, and then — what? Walking where? There were no tracks leading anywhere. The smoothed patch simply ended, and beyond it the snow lay innocent as a fresh sheet.

Unless they hadn't walked. Unless whatever had lifted them out had landed *there*, on the glacier ice itself, where tracks in snow could be explained away or blown clean by rotor wash—

He stood up too fast and his knee cracked in protest. Nine years of flying had taught him to trust the specific over the general, the detail over the theory. The theory was getting ugly fast. The details were uglier. Zipped tents. Washed dishes. A scratched-out word starting with N. A smoothed patch of snow and no plane.

This wasn't weather. Weather was honest. Weather killed you in the open and left you where you fell, and everyone shook their heads and said *the mountains, the mountains,* and meant it as consolation.

This had been done by people. People who tidied up.

Hazel walked back to his airplane with his heart going harder than the walk justified. He untied her, then stopped, hands on the strut, and had the argument he'd been postponing.

*Fly to Kettle Inlet. File the report. Let it become someone else's problem.*

*You already know what filing the report accomplishes. You watched them sit on a distress call for nine days.*

*Then what — you're going to investigate? You're a bush pilot. Your qualifications are mail, morphine, and knowing where the fog lives.*

*My qualification,* Hazel answered the voice, *is that I'm here, and I'm looking, and nobody else is.*

There it was. Underneath all the reasonable scaffolding — the efficient dogleg, the good-faith site check — the plain true thing: leaving people unaccounted for was a debt, and Hazel Oyelaran did not fly away from debts. He'd learned that the hard way, at a price he paid in full and never discussed, and the learning had never worn off. Somewhere under this ice or beyond this ridge or in the bottom of some crevasse were six men whose last radio words had been cut off mid-syllable, and the world's response had been paperwork.

He climbed back into the *Kestrel*, and instead of turning south for Kettle Inlet, he swung her nose north, toward the glacier, and opened the throttle.

If the survey team had been hauling something heavy off that bench, they'd hauled it up-ice. Heavy loads and thin ice meant low routes, and low routes meant the plane had stayed within sight of the walls. And planes that fly low into the high interior leave traces — a scorch mark on a ridge, a smear of oil on a landing spot, a wheel pant glinting in a moraine. Hazel had spent nine years learning to see those traces. It was, he sometimes thought, the only prayer he knew: *show me what's really there.*

The glacier ran north between walls of blue-shadowed ice, widening as it climbed, and twenty minutes later, wedged in a side valley beneath the Priest's hooded face, he found what the snow had refused to show him on the bench.

A second camp. Smaller. Newer. Two tents, a cache of drums, and a rough landing strip stamped into the glacier's accumulation zone — and scattered around it, catching the sun like dropped coins, dozens of pieces of bright metal that no geological survey had any business producing.

Hazel pulled up and over, circling wide, and the cold wire in his spine became a bar.

Because the second camp wasn't empty either. It was occupied. Smoke rose thin and straight from a stove pipe, and parked at the edge of the strip, half-loaded, sat a heavy transport ski-plane wearing fresh paint and no markings at all — the aviation equivalent of a face deliberately turned away.

Men moved below, small and purposeful, loading crates.

Hazel eased the *Kestrel* over the ridge and out of sight before anyone below thought to look up, and flew south very fast, his mind doing arithmetic it didn't like. Missing survey team. Unmarked aircraft. Armed silence from the authorities. A lode worth whatever it was that made grown men scratch words off clipboards and smooth over snow.

He needed fuel, a radio, and a decision. He had exactly enough of the first two to reach Kettle Inlet.

What he didn't yet know — what he couldn't have known, banking hard over the white divide with the sun in his eyes — was that the decision had already been made for him, three hundred miles south, by a man named Crane who was at that moment reading Hazel's name off a list of licensed pilots and drawing a slow circle around it.
