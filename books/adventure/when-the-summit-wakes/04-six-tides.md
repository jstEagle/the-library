## 4. Six Tides

The government man arrived on the Thursday mail plane, which was fitting, because everything about him arrived late and slightly damp.

Hazel was in the hangar re-lacing a tire when the Cessna bumped down on the gravel strip, throwing up a rooster tail of meltwater that caught the afternoon sun like someone had shaken a jar of coins. He watched it taxi with the particular interest of a man who knew every plane on this route by its engine's cough, and this one he didn't know. New paint, government gray going green at the seams. A pilot he'd never seen before — young, nervous, hands too busy on the yoke — and in the passenger seat a man in a wool coat that had been expensive approximately fifteen years ago.

The man climbed out without waiting for help, which Hazel approved of, and then stood in the middle of the strip looking around at the hangar, the fuel drums, the wind sock hanging limp as a wet sock itself, as though all of it were an exhibit he'd already read the placard for.

"Hazel Oyelaran," the man said, extending a hand before Hazel had come within shaking distance of it.

"You've got me at a disadvantage," Hazel said. "You know my name, I know your coat."

"Aldous Crane." The handshake was firm, brief, calibrated. "Department of Northern Affairs."

"Department of Northern Affairs," Hazel repeated. In eleven years of flying medicine and mail into the interior, he had met exactly two men from Northern Affairs, and both had been lost within twenty minutes of leaving his company. The interior did that to department men. It filed them somewhere.

"I'll need your aircraft," Crane said.

And there it was. No hello, no how's the weather up here, no offer of coffee from the thermos Hazel kept out of habit though he drank his coffee black and scalding at home like a civilized person. Just *I'll need your aircraft*, delivered the way a man asks to borrow a pen.

"Will you," Hazel said. He went back to the tire.

Crane followed him into the hangar, ducking under the wing strut with the gracelessness of a man who had not spent much time under wings. "Three days ago you reported a distress transmission from the Meridian survey team. Coordinates logged. You then flew the coordinates yourself against explicit advice to wait."

"Nobody gave me advice," Hazel said. "I don't get much radio reception past the ridge, so mostly I just get weather and my own opinions."

"And what did you find?"

"Tents standing. Equipment stowed neat as a Sunday table. Fresh snow with no tracks in it. No people." Hazel pulled the lacing tight, bit the cord off with his teeth. "I told all this to your dispatcher. Twice. He thanked me both times, which I found suspicious on its own merits."

"The matter is being handled."

"Handled." Hazel stood up, finally, and looked at Crane straight on. He was a head shorter than the man and had learned long ago that height was mostly theater. "Mr. Crane, six people walked into those mountains in September. Six people stopped talking three days ago mid-sentence, and the sentence wasn't finished. That's not handled. That's *pending*."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — not guilt, Hazel thought, more like a man recalculating a ledger. "Which is why I'm here," Crane said, and his voice changed register, dropped half an octave, took on the warmth of a stove in a cold room. "I'm not going to insult you, Mr. Oyelaran. You're the only pilot who knows that pass well enough to fly it in spring conditions, and you're the only one close enough to matter. There is a recovery operation assembling at Fort Callum. Equipment, personnel. They'll be ready in nine days."

"Nine days." Hazel wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since the previous administration. "The pass starts melting in six."

"Yes."

"You want me to fly a recovery team through a pass that will be shedding ice by the ton, ahead of a schedule built by people who've clearly never watched a glacier think."

"I want you to fly *me*," Crane said. "One aircraft. One observer. We go in ahead of the fleet, we locate the site, we secure whatever's recoverable, and we're gone before the melt makes the pass impassable. Six tides, Mr. Oyelaran. Six high-water marks between here and the glacier face. After the sixth, nobody goes in or comes out until August."

* * *

Hazel made coffee, because it was either that or say something he'd regret, and the kettle gave him time to do neither. Crane sat on the workbench stool like a man visiting a dentist he trusted, which was to say: completely still, entirely prepared for pain.

"Why the hurry?" Hazel asked, pouring. "If the site's been sitting there since September, another week of proper planning—"

"The melt won't wait for proper planning."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I'm authorized to give." Crane accepted the coffee without looking at it. "I understand how this sounds. A man arrives unannounced, asks for your plane, tells you nothing. But I'll tell you what I can. The survey team was carrying instruments worth more than every building in this settlement combined. Scientific instruments. If they fall into the wrong hands — if they simply *melt into the ice* — the loss is permanent. My job is to make sure it isn't."

"Instruments," Hazel said.

"Instruments."

Hazel leaned against the bench and drank his own coffee and thought about the camp. He'd circled it four times before landing, and he could still see it when he closed his eyes, which was most of the time lately. Tents guyed out properly, snow walls cut in blocks the way experienced men do it, the generator sled parked square to the wind. And the snow — fresh, deep, undisturbed in every direction, a page with nothing written on it. People didn't leave camps like that. Camps like that left *people*.

"Your dispatcher wasn't surprised," Hazel said. "When I reported the transmission cutting out. I've reported emergencies before. Dispatchers get loud. This one got quiet. Quiet like a man checking whether anyone else heard."

Crane set his cup down with a small precise click. "You have a suspicious mind, Mr. Oyelaran."

"I have a survival mind. Up here they're the same thing." He looked out the hangar doors at the light going long and gold across the strip, the mountains behind it standing up white and blue into a sky scrubbed clean. Beautiful country. Country that ate people politely and left their tents standing. "Six people, Mr. Crane. Are any of them alive?"

The pause lasted two seconds. Hazel counted them the way he counted seconds between lightning and thunder, measuring distance to something dangerous.

"I don't know," Crane said, and it was the first thing he'd said that sounded true. "That's the honest answer. If any are alive, they're sheltered, and sheltered men survive on supplies, and supplies run out on schedules. Every day we plan is a day off their clock."

"And if they're not alive?"

"Then we recover what they carried and account for them properly. Their families deserve names and dates, not a file marked *pending*."

It was a good answer. It was almost exactly the answer Hazel would have given, which was how he knew to distrust it. Men who lied well always borrowed from the truth first.

* * *

He didn't sleep that night, or rather he slept the way he flew in bad weather — in short corrected bursts. He lay in the bunk above his workbench listening to the wind test the hangar roof, and he ran the arithmetic he always ran, the only arithmetic that had never once failed him.

Eleven years. Forty thousand miles a year, give or take. Medicine to Kettle Narrows, mail to Fort Callum, parts and flour and once — he smiled at the ceiling remembering it — a bride's wedding dress wrapped in oilcloth and treated with more care than anything he'd ever carried, including the nitroglycerin tablets for old Mrs. Adeyemi, which he'd flown through hail. He measured his life in miles because miles added up honestly. Years didn't. Years took things. His brother, for one. His brother had taken a boat out on gray water nineteen years ago and the water had kept him, and Hazel had learned that attachments were just debts you agreed to owe in advance.

So: no wife, no dog even, though the husky at the trading post had made a strong case for himself last winter. A plane, a strip, a route. Miles, not years.

Except.

Except six people had walked into his mountains and stopped mid-word, and he had seen their tents standing tidy as gravestones, and some part of him — the part that had landed against his better judgment, the part that had reported it twice and been thanked into silence — had already signed something. Leaving people unaccounted for was a debt, and Hazel Oyelaran did not fly away from debts. It was the one rule he'd kept that had ever kept him.

He got up at four, which was not dawn here so much as a slightly different shade of dark, and put the kettle on, and by the time Crane emerged from the bunkhouse looking rumpled and human for the first time, Hazel had the flight board out and the charts weighted flat with socket wrenches.

"Conditions window," Hazel said, tapping the chart. "High pressure sits over the divide for the next five days, maybe six if it's feeling generous. After that, a low comes up from the coast and the melt goes from a trickle to a river. So we fly tomorrow, first light. Not nine days. Not your fleet's schedule. Tomorrow."

Crane blinked. "The recovery operation—"

"Is nine days of paperwork away from being an obituary. You said it yourself: supplies run out on schedules. Well, so does weather." Hazel rolled the chart and tapped it against his palm. "Here's my price. Not money. Three things. One: my plane, my decisions. You ride shotgun and you keep your hands off the controls and off the radio unless I hand it to you. Two: full fuel at every stop, paid in advance, cash, because I've been stiffed by departments before. Three—" he paused, and found the words came harder than he expected, "—whatever we find out there, whoever we find, they come back with us. All of them. Alive or otherwise. Nobody gets left to simplify anybody's paperwork."

For a moment something flickered across Crane's face that might have been respect, or might have been the look of a man discovering his instrument was more complicated than advertised.

"Agreed," Crane said. "All three."

"One more thing." Hazel poured the last of the coffee and handed it over. "You called it a wreck site. I never said wreck. I said camp. Tents standing, no damage. Somebody used the word *wreck* before I did, Mr. Crane, and it wasn't me, and it wasn't the mountain. So before we're wheels-up tomorrow, you're going to tell me what your fleet is really recovering."

Crane looked at the coffee, then at Hazel, and for a long moment the stove-warmth was gone from his voice entirely, and what was underneath was colder and older and much more interesting.

"Get us through the pass," Crane said quietly, "and I promise you'll wish I hadn't."

Outside, the wind shifted, and far up the valley the ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like the mountain clearing its throat before it spoke.

* * *

They were wheels-up at 5:40, into a sky the color of hammered tin, the peaks ahead still holding the night in their folds. Hazel ran the engine up and watched the gauges settle into their honest numbers, and beside him Crane sat rigid, gripping the armrest as though it had personally offended him.

"Relax," Hazel said, easing the yoke forward. "She's flown this pass two hundred times. She knows it better than either of us."

"And the sixth tide?"

Hazel looked north, where the glacier face waited somewhere beyond the blue distance, holding whatever it held — six people, or six silences, or something else entirely, something a department man wanted badly enough to lie about.

"We'll beat it," he said. "We have to."

Below them, the rivers were already running brown with the first melt, and the mountains had begun, very quietly, to let go of their winter.
