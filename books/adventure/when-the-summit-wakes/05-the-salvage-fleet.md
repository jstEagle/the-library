## 5. The Salvage Fleet

The trouble with a lie, Hazel's father used to say, is that it has to be fed. A truth will sit in the corner all day and ask for nothing. But a lie gets hungry, and it gets hungry at the worst hours, and eventually it eats the hand that feeds it.

Hazel had thought about that saying more than once since Aldous Crane climbed into his Cessna with a briefcase, a government smile, and a story about recovering equipment. He was thinking about it now, three days into the run north, as he watched the sun come up over the Serrateds and turn the icefields the color of old brass.

Crane sat in the right seat doing what he always did: reading. Not charts — Hazel had noticed that early on. Charts were for pilots, and Crane never touched them, never asked about headings or fuel or the weather stacking up over the pass like dishes nobody wanted to wash. He read documents. Typed pages in manila folders, which he reviewed with a fountain pen and then locked back in the briefcase, which stayed cuffed to his wrist.

"Fuel stop at Kettle," Hazel said. "Twenty minutes."

"Mm." Crane didn't look up.

"You want coffee?"

"I don't drink coffee."

"Everybody drinks coffee."

"Then I'm everybody's exception." Crane turned a page. The pen scratched. Somewhere behind that mild face, Hazel was fairly sure, a lie was asking to be fed.

He put the Cessna down at Kettle mostly because he liked the place and partly because he wanted to see what Crane did when left alone with other people. Kettle was a fuel cache and a trading post run by a widow named Aggie Prosser, who was seventy-one years old, weighed perhaps ninety pounds, and had buried two husbands and any number of fools in the permafrost behind her store. She greeted Hazel with the warmth of long acquaintance and Crane with the flat appraisal of a woman who had survived the interior by knowing exactly what kind of animal had walked into her yard.

"Your friend's got government hands," she said, pumping fuel while Hazel checked the tanks. "Soft ones. Never done a day's work that left a mark."

"He says he's here to recover equipment."

Aggie snorted, a sound like air brakes. "Equipment." She said it the way you'd say *ghost*. "Man flies north in April with a briefcase full of paper and no cold-weather gear worth the name, no bedroll, no rifle, and a wristwatch that costs more than my whole operation. That's not an equipment man, Hazel Oyelaran. I've sold gas to equipment men for forty years. They smell like two-stroke and regret. This one smells like cologne and lawyers."

Hazel glanced through the window at the plane. Crane stood beside it, briefcase in hand, looking out across the strip with the patient expression of a man waiting for a bus in a city where buses ran on time. He wasn't looking at the mountains. He was looking south, down the valley they'd come up, as if expecting something.

Or following something.

* * *

It was two hours later, crossing the divide above the Talus River, that Hazel saw the convoy.

He saw it first as a smudge — a brown-gray smear against the white of the far slopes, moving. He'd flown this country long enough to know what moved and what didn't. Weather moved. Rockslides moved, once, catastrophically. Caribou moved in threads and loops. But nothing else down there moved in a line, and this was a line: a procession strung along the frozen riverbed, heading north, heading exactly where Hazel was headed.

He banked to get a better look, and Crane looked up from his papers for the first time in an hour.

"What is it?"

"Company," Hazel said, and dropped lower.

At four hundred feet the smudge resolved itself, and what resolved made the hair on his forearms stand up under his flight jacket.

There were six vehicles. Not sleds, not dog teams, not the battered snowcats a survey outfit would rent from the coast and nurse along with baling wire and prayer. These were purpose-built machines — low, wide-tracked transports with enclosed cabs, the kind Hazel had seen exactly once before, in a magazine article about polar logistics contracts, the kind that cost more than his airplane, his hangar, and probably his life insurance policy combined. They moved in echelon, kicking up rooster tails of spindrift, eating ground at a pace that made Hazel do the arithmetic twice because he didn't like the first answer.

They were going to make the pass in five tides. Maybe four.

Behind the lead vehicles came sledges under tarps, and one of the tarps had slipped, and what showed underneath was not equipment. Equipment doesn't come in crates stenciled with serial numbers. Equipment doesn't get lashed down by men wearing matching parkas. And equipment — this was the detail that lodged in Hazel's mind like a splinter — equipment does not include a mounted gun.

He wouldn't have believed it if he hadn't seen it. But there it was, on the third sledge: a weapon on a pintle mount, and a man beside it in a white parka, and the man was not riding with the loose slouch of a hired hand killing time. He rode the way soldiers ride, knees loose, hands ready, scanning the ridgelines.

"Hazel." Crane's voice was still mild. It was remarkable how mild he could keep it. "I'd suggest we not linger."

"Recovering equipment," Hazel said. He pulled up and away, climbing back toward cruise altitude, his heart going harder than the climb alone explained. "That's what you told me. Back at the coast. You said a survey team went missing, their gear was government property, and my job was to beat the melt to the site so a recovery crew could come in after."

"That's correct."

"Then whose crew is that?"

Below them, the convoy crawled on, six dark stitches closing a wound in the white.

"A private contractor," Crane said. "Engaged to assist."

"Assist with what? The recovery crew hasn't mustered yet. You told me that yourself. You said we had until the sixth tide before anyone else could be in position." Hazel kept his eyes on the instruments, on the horizon, anywhere but on the man beside him, because he'd learned in thirty years of flying that you can say hard things easier when you're not watching someone's face while you say them. "Those machines are ahead of schedule. Those machines aren't assisting anybody. Those machines are racing us, and they're winning."

For a long moment Crane said nothing. The engine filled the cockpit with its steady roar, and the mountains slid past, indifferent, beautiful, keeping whatever counsel they'd kept since before there were men to keep secrets from.

"The syndicate—" Crane began, and stopped himself. Hazel heard it. He heard the word die in the man's mouth like a bird hitting a window.

"The what?"

"The contractors," Crane said smoothly, too smoothly, the sentence re-laid like fresh gravel over a washout. "Are funded through a consortium of interested parties. Their presence accelerates our timeline. It doesn't change your mission."

"My mission," Hazel said, "was rescue."

"Your mission," Crane said, "is navigation. You fly. I'll worry about what the flying is for."

And there it was. Hazel had been circling it for three days the way you circle a thunderhead, looking for the gap, and now Crane had gone and shown him the shape of it plain as a lightning scar. This was never about equipment. Equipment gets recovered by men with wrenches. Whatever was down at that glacier, whatever the survey team had found or seen or died for, it was valuable enough to buy machines like those, and armed enough to need men like the one on the gun, and important enough that a man like Aldous Crane would cuff a briefcase to his own wrist and lie about it with a fountain pen.

"Turn us around," Hazel said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"We're going back to the coast. Right now. Today. Because I signed on to find missing people, Mr. Crane, and every mile we fly north I'm more convinced the finding part ended a long time ago, and what's left is the covering-up part, and I don't fly cover-ups."

Crane turned in his seat, and for the first time Hazel saw what lived underneath the mildness. It wasn't anger. Anger would have been almost a relief. It was patience — the deep, bottomless patience of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because there was always something else he could raise instead.

"Mr. Oyelaran. Do you know what happens to a bush pilot who refuses a government charter mid-contract?" Crane asked pleasantly. "His license goes under review. His insurance becomes a matter of correspondence. His fuel accounts freeze while committees deliberate — and committees, as you know, deliberate longest in winter. By spring, when the flying season opens, some other young man with a shiny new license is flying your mail route, and your customers have forgotten your name, and the mountains don't care, because the mountains never cared."

The words landed the way he'd meant them to land, each one placed like a stone in a wall. Hazel flew in silence for a while, jaw working, watching the convoy shrink behind them and hating it, hating that it shrank, hating that every minute he spent arguing was a minute the machines gained.

Because that was the trap, and Crane knew it, and Crane knew that Hazel knew it. He could turn back and lose everything he had, slowly, bureaucratically, in triplicate. Or he could keep flying north toward people who might still be alive — the survey team, or what remained of them — and tell himself the compromise was temporary, just until the missing were accounted for, just until the debt was paid.

Leaving people unaccounted for was a debt Hazel couldn't fly away from. Crane had read that about him inside of a day. Men like Crane always could.

"Kettle again on the return leg," Hazel said finally. "Aggie sells coffee. You might try some. It builds character."

"I have sufficient character."

"Nobody does. That's why they keep making coffee."

Crane almost smiled. Almost. Then he unlocked the briefcase, took out a fresh folder, and began to read, and the cockpit closed around its silence like a fist.

* * *

They overnighted at a survey cabin on the flank of Mount Corvair, a tin-roofed shelter stocked with stove wood and tinned everything, built back when the government still pretended to care about the small parties it sent into the white. While Crane slept — actually slept, soundly, the sleep of a man with no debts — Hazel sat by the window with a mug of tea and watched the moon come up over the icefield, and did the thing he did instead of praying: he counted.

Not years. Miles.

Two hundred and ten miles to the last known coordinates. Sixty miles of that over open icefield where a plane on floats-skis could set down in an emergency and a convoy could not follow fast. Forty miles of canyon country where the river narrowed and the walls came in and anything on the ground would be funneled, visible, predictable — his country, flyer's country, the one advantage altitude gave him over tracks.

And somewhere ahead, past all of it, the glacier. The camp with its tents standing. The snow with no tracks in it. The quiet that had come through the radio in fragments and then stopped coming altogether.

He thought about the man on the gun, riding easy, scanning the ridgelines. He thought about Aggie's voice: *cologne and lawyers.* He thought about the word Crane had swallowed — *syndicate* — and how a government man only misspeaks when the truth is bigger than his cover story, and how the truth, in Hazel's experience, was almost always about money, and money at that scale was almost always about something somebody else had found first and couldn't defend.

The tea went cold in his hands. Outside, the moonlight lay on the glacier fields like a sheet over furniture in a house where everyone had left.

One question kept turning over in him, stubborn as a rock in a tire tread. If the convoy was already north, already ahead, already armed — then what exactly was Hazel for? Why pay a bush pilot to race a race that was already lost?

Unless it wasn't lost. Unless there was something at that glacier the machines couldn't reach without a man who knew the mountain — or something, or someone, the syndicate wanted found by a friendly pair of eyes before it could be found by anyone else.

Or unless the pilot was the errand. Unless Hazel himself was the last piece of equipment being delivered to the site.

He looked over at Crane, asleep in the bunk, briefcase cuffed to his wrist even now, even in dreams. The moonlight caught the case's brass fittings and made them shine, small and bright and locked.

Tomorrow they'd fly the canyon country. Tomorrow the pass would lose another tide to the melt, and the convoy would gain another day on them, and the questions would keep multiplying faster than the answers.

Hazel finished the cold tea, banked the stove coals, and lay down on the floor by the door, between Crane and the exit, with his boots on.

Just in case.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and windless, the kind of sky that couldn't decide. Hazel was loading the plane when he heard it — a thin thread of sound from the south, wrong for weather, wrong for birds. He stood in the shadow of the wing and listened, and the sound resolved into engines. More than one. High.

He shaded his eyes. Two specks against the eastern haze, flying line abreast, closing fast — twin-engine aircraft, painted dull and anonymous, moving with the unhurried confidence of machines that did not need to worry about fuel caches or weather holds or the price of anything.

They weren't following Crane's flight plan. Hazel knew every registered route in this sky the way he knew the lines of his own palm, and these planes were flying none of them. They were cutting straight across the restricted zone — the zone the authorities had been so oddly unhurried about, the zone where a distress call had died mid-word three weeks ago.

The same zone Hazel was being paid to enter.

The planes passed far to the east, high and steady, and did not deviate, and did not acknowledge him, and were gone behind the shoulder of Corvair like a secret sliding back under a door.

Behind him, Crane zipped his jacket with small precise movements. "Weather window's closing," he said. "We should be airborne within the hour."

Hazel didn't move. He was watching the empty sky where the planes had been, and the arithmetic in his head had changed, and none of the new numbers were good.

"Mr. Crane," he said quietly. "How many aircraft does your consortium operate?"

Crane paused, one hand on the cabin door, and smiled the smile of a man feeding a very large, very patient lie.

"All of them we need," he said.
