## 6. The Woman in the Ice Cave

The weather turned on Hazel the way a friend turns on you at a card table — suddenly, completely, and with every intention of taking everything he had.

One minute he was threading the upper valley with the sun on his wings, following the survey's old route markers toward the glacier face. The next, the sky to the west went the color of wet slate and began rolling toward him like something with an appetite. He'd flown this range long enough to know that mountain weather didn't negotiate. It presented terms.

"Right," he told the plane, because talking to her kept him honest. "Terms."

He ran the arithmetic the way he always did: fuel against distance, distance against the wall of cloud, the wall of cloud against the fact that he did not particularly want to die today. The numbers came back ugly. There was a strip on the far side of the pass he might reach if the melt hadn't taken it, but *might* was not a word Hazel Oyelaran liked to bet his life on. He'd seen what it did to men who used it as currency.

What he needed was rock overhead and a floor that wasn't moving.

He knew a place. Everyone who flew the interior knew a place or two like it — the kind of knowledge you traded over coffee and never wrote down, because written things got found. Three miles up the valley, where the river bent hard around a shoulder of granite, there was a cave mouth maybe forty feet above the waterline, wide enough for a man and narrow enough that the wind mostly forgot about it. He'd sheltered there once, years ago, waiting out a storm with a crate of vaccine and a thermos of terrible coffee, and he'd promised himself he'd remember it.

He remembered it now, banking low along the river bend while the first gusts began shoving the tail of the plane like a rude hand between the shoulders. No hope of landing on water — the river was running high with melt, white-capped and furious. But above the cave, where the slope gentled before rising into scree, there was an old gravel fan, bare of trees, angled downhill just enough to be interesting.

Interesting was the best he could do. He set her down on the uphill run, standing on the brakes harder than any instructor would have approved of, and rolled to a stop twenty feet from a boulder the size of a church, with the storm arriving behind him like a debt collector.

* * *

Hazel spent the first hour doing what pilots do: securing the plane, double-tying the tie-downs to rocks because there were no anchors, dragging brush over the wings as if a few sticks would argue with a hundred-mile wind. Then he carried what mattered up the slope to the cave — sleeping bag, stove, food, the rifle he'd started carrying since Crane's people had entered his life, and the radio, which crackled and spat and gave him nothing but static shaped vaguely like weather warnings.

The storm settled in for the night. It howled down the valley and found the cave mouth and played it like a bottle, a low moaning note that would have unnerved a man with less practice being alone. Hazel boiled snow for coffee, ate tinned beans cold because heating them felt like effort without reward, and sat looking out at the grey chaos, thinking about six tides and a woman named Voss who was either dead or the best liar Crane had ever hired.

That was the thing that kept snagging in him like a burr in a sock. Crane had shown him photographs. Mara Voss, thirty-four, geologist, presumed lost with the rest of the survey team. And Crane had said *presumed* in a voice that was just slightly too smooth, the way a man says *presumed* when he already knows the answer and is telling you the version he wants in the record.

Hazel had filed it away. Pilots learn to file things away; the cockpit is small and there's no room for unexamined suspicions rattling around loose.

He was on his second cup of terrible cave coffee, listening to the storm try to take the mountain apart, when he heard the sound.

At first he took it for the wind. The wind in that cave made a dozen noises, most of them unhappy. But underneath the moan there was something rhythmic. A scrape. A pause. A scrape. Like someone dragging something heavy across stone, very slowly, the way you move when moving fast has stopped being possible.

Hazel put down the cup and picked up the rifle, and felt foolish immediately, and kept hold of it anyway, because feeling foolish was cheaper than being wrong.

"Hello," he called toward the deeper dark of the cave, where the daylight gave out. His voice came back off the walls, flattened by the storm-noise. "I've got a stove and coffee. If you're a bear, I should tell you the coffee's not worth dying for."

Silence. Long enough that he almost convinced himself he'd imagined it.

Then, from the black: a woman's voice, cracked and thin and furious all at once.

"If you're one of Crane's, I'll warn you now — I have a rock hammer and no reason left to be polite."

Hazel lowered the rifle and let out a breath he felt in his knees.

"Ma'am," he said, "I promise you I am nobody's. I'm the mail pilot. Hazel Oyelaran." A beat. "And I'd take a rock hammer over Crane's lot any day of the week."

Another silence, longer. He could practically hear her thinking, weighing him the way she'd weigh a core sample — is this real, is it worth anything, will it break.

"The mail pilot," she said at last, and there was something in it that wasn't quite disbelief and wasn't quite laughter. "They sent the *mail pilot*."

"They conscripted me," Hazel said. "Which I'll admit doesn't reflect well on their judgment, or mine."

A scraping sound again, closer now. Then hands on stone, and a shape resolved out of the dark — a woman, gaunt, wrapped in a survey parka gone grey with grime, one hand braced against the wall, favoring her left leg. Her face was frost-nipped at the cheekbones, her lips cracked, her eyes — her eyes were the sharpest thing in that cave, sharper than his rifle, and they went through him like a drill through shale.

"Mara Voss," she said. Not offering a handshake. Offering a name, the way you offer evidence.

"I know," Hazel said. "You're supposed to be dead."

"And you're supposed to be flying medicine to Kettle Narrows." She swayed, caught herself, and glared at her own body as if it had betrayed her in front of company. "I listened to your frequency for two days from the ridge. You report weather every morning at seven. You're very punctual."

"It's the only virtue anyone's ever accused me of."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, surfacing through the frostbite like a vein of ore through rock. Then her knee buckled, and Hazel moved without deciding to, catching her under the arm before she hit the stone.

She stiffened at his touch. He felt it — the whole-body flinch of someone who had learned, recently and thoroughly, that other people were weather to be survived.

"Easy," he said. "I'm setting you down by the stove. That's the whole plan. Stove, blanket, beans. In that order."

"I don't eat beans," she managed.

"Nobody eats beans," Hazel agreed, lowering her onto his bedroll. "We endure them. It builds character." He got the stove lit and the pot on, and when he glanced back she was watching him with those drill-bit eyes, cataloguing everything — the rifle within his reach, the radio, the way he moved, the make of his boots.

"You're trying to figure out if I'm going to sell you," he said.

"Are you?"

"No."

"People say that."

"People say a lot of things. Crane said 'recovering equipment.'" Hazel stirred the pot. "I've been flying supplies to that man's operation for two weeks and I haven't seen a single piece of equipment worth the word 'recovery.' I've seen armed men and fuel manifests that don't add up. So." He looked at her straight. "You want to tell me why a government geologist is living in a hole in a mountain, eating — I'm sorry, enduring — my beans, instead of letting her own people find her?"

Mara Voss held his gaze for a long moment. Outside, the storm threw itself against the mountain and lost, the way it would lose all night.

"Your coffee smells criminal," she said finally.

"It is. It's the worst coffee in the territory. Possibly the hemisphere."

"Give me some anyway." She took the tin cup he offered in both hands, fingers too stiff to trust themselves alone, and drank, and grimaced, and drank again. Warmth came back into her face by degrees, like dawn working its way down a peak. "Ask me again tomorrow," she said. "When I can feel my feet. If you still want the answer."

"I'll want it tomorrow," Hazel said. "I'll want it at three in the morning, probably, because that's when I do my worrying. But it keeps."

* * *

*Mara slept in pieces.*

That was the truth of the last nineteen days — sleep came in fragments, never whole, because whole sleep belonged to people who weren't hiding. She surfaced every hour or so to check the facts: cave, fire, stranger. Cave, fire, stranger. The facts held. The stranger sat propped against the far wall with his rifle across his knees, not aiming it anywhere, occasionally feeding the stove, humming something tuneless under his breath like a man who had lived alone so long that silence had become a room too big and he'd furnished it with noise.

She should have run. That was the discipline, the rule that had kept her alive since she'd crawled out of the ice with Wren's map case strapped to her chest: never be found, never be predictable, never trust anyone whose survival depends on reporting yours. She'd watched four good people die or disappear because someone higher up had decided the mountain's arithmetic worked better without witnesses.

But her left foot had gone past pain into a hot, wrong numbness two days ago, and her food had run out yesterday, and the storm had boxed her in, and then a plane had landed on the gravel fan below with the particular careful competence of a man who knew exactly how much runway he had and respected it.

And he'd brought beans. And the worst coffee she had ever tasted, which was somehow reassuring, because liars with resources bought good coffee.

Morning came grey and quieter, the storm spending itself in long exhausted gusts. Mara woke properly for the first time in days to the smell of woodsmoke and the sight of the pilot crouched at the cave mouth, studying the sky with the squinting seriousness of a farmer reading clouds.

"How's the foot?" he asked without turning around.

"You were supposed to think I was asleep."

"I was hoping you were asleep. Hoping and thinking are different departments." He came back and crouched by her, and nodded at her boot. "May I? I flew a season of medevac before the mail run. I know what frostbite looks like coming and going."

She hesitated — the flinch again, automatic — then nodded. He handled the boot like it was made of eggshell, easing it off, peeling back the wrappings she'd improvised from shirt sleeves. He was quiet a long time.

"Well?" she said.

"Well, it's ugly. But it's not black, and the toes answer when I press, which is more than they'd do yesterday, I'd guess. You've been warming it right. Whoever taught you field medicine taught you well."

"Wren did. Our medic." Something moved through her chest, sharp and brief, and she looked away at the cave wall. "Wren's dead. Or gone. They're the same thing out here."

Hazel rewrapped the foot without comment, gently, and when he spoke his voice had changed — lower, plainer, the friendly patter set aside like a tool he'd finished with. "Dr. Voss. Yesterday you said ask me tomorrow. It's tomorrow."

She looked at him. He waited. That was the thing she noticed, the thing that started the crack in the wall she'd built: he didn't push. He just waited, steady as a fixed-wing on final approach, and let the silence do its work.

So she told him.

Not all of it — not yet, not the part that lived lowest in her, the part about the money and the signature and the lie she'd told herself on the flight in. But the shape of it. The survey that wasn't a survey. The readings that had come back so far beyond anything on record that the team's sponsor had gone quiet, then strange, then absent. The radio orders that stopped making sense. The night the second plane arrived unannounced with men who weren't scientists, and Aldous Crane stepping out of it with a smile like a ledger, and Wren pressing the map case into her hands and saying *run* in a voice Mara would hear until she died.

"They took the others," she said. Her voice stayed level, mostly. "Okafor, Lindqvist, the twins. They loaded them on the plane. I don't know if they're alive. I think the ones who saw the full assay aren't meant to be." She pulled the blanket tighter. "And Crane sent you — a bush pilot with a good reputation and no family anyone knows of — to fly ahead of his salvage fleet and mark the route. Because a mail plane doesn't frighten anyone. Because you're the kind of man whose word people believe."

The words landed and she watched them land, watched for the flinch, the defensiveness, the guilt. Instead Hazel Oyelaran sat back on his heels and rubbed his jaw and said, quietly:

"That explains the fuel caches."

"What?"

"The caches. Crane had me stage fuel along this route — 'for the recovery teams.' Except the quantities were wrong. Too much for equipment recovery, not enough for a proper expedition. Enough for aircraft." He shook his head slowly. "I keep books on everything. It's how I stay honest. And his books haven't balanced since the day I met him."

"So now you know," Mara said. "Your employer is a murderer, or close enough, and you're sitting in a cave with the loose end he's been trying to tie off." She lifted her chin. "For what it's worth, I'm sorry. I know what I cost you. Your contract, your license, probably your life. You could still walk out of here and report finding me, and Crane would call it loyalty, and you'd be paid."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Outside, the last gusts of the storm dragged themselves over the ridge and away, and the light in the cave mouth turned from slate to silver.

"My brother Tomás used to say I collect debts," he said at last. "Not money debts. The other kind. Somebody pulls me out of a river, I spend ten years looking for a chance to pull them out of something. It's a character flaw. My accountant despairs of me." He looked at her, and there was no pity in his face, which she hadn't realized she'd been braced against until its absence undid her a little. "Four people got loaded onto a plane, Dr. Voss. Whether they're breathing or not, somebody owes them an accounting. And I don't mean Crane's accounting."

"That's not duty," Mara said. "That's suicide with extra steps. There are nine armed men in that fleet and one of you."

"One of us," Hazel corrected mildly, and rose to feed the stove, and the correction hung in the air between them, small and enormous.

* * *

They spent the day waiting out the storm's tail, and it went easier than either of them expected, which surprised them both in opposite directions. Mara discovered that Hazel Oyelaran talked to his airplane — she heard him outside doing his pre-flight walk-around, murmuring to the tail strut, "I know, I know, we ask a lot of each other, don't we" — and when he came back inside she raised an eyebrow, and he had the grace to look faintly caught.

"She listens better than most people," he said.

"Does she talk back?"

"Only in maintenance bills."

And Mara laughed — actually laughed, a rusty, startled sound that hurt her cracked lips and she didn't care — and Hazel grinned at her like he'd just gotten a difficult engine to catch on the first crank, and something shifted in the cave's cold air, some small thawing that had nothing to do with the stove.

Later, she showed him Wren's map case. He read the notes in silence for a long time, his lips moving slightly, doing sums. When he looked up, his face had gone carefully blank in the way of a man standing very close to an edge.

"This lode," he said. "If even half these numbers are real—"

"They're real. I ran the assays myself. Twice."

"—then there isn't enough money in the territory to buy what happens next. Not for Crane. Not for whoever's behind him." He closed the case gently, like shutting a door on a lit room. "Which means the fleet isn't coming for equipment."

"No," Mara said. "It isn't."

Hazel stood and walked to the cave mouth, where the sky was clearing in the west, a long clean band of blue opening above the pass like a door unlocking. Six tides, Crane had said. The melt was coming whether they liked it or not, and somewhere south of here, nine armed men were counting on it too.

"We can't outrun them the obvious way," he said, half to himself, eyes tracing the ridgelines, reading the mountains the way she read strata. "But there's an old route through the Serrateds that isn't on any government chart. Nobody flies it because it eats planes." A pause, and then, with the ghost of a grin: "Fortunately, I've never much liked what charts think of my flying."

Mara leaned back against the stone, foot throbbing, ribs aching, nineteen days of fear sitting in her chest like ballast — and found, to her considerable irritation, that she trusted him. Not completely. Trust was a core sample; you took it in increments and tested each one. But the first increment held.

"Hazel Oyelaran," she said, "you are either the bravest man in this territory or the worst judge of trouble I have ever met."

"Both, probably," he said, and started packing the stove. "Tide's turning. We'd better be in the air before it does."
