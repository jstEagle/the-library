## 7. What the Survey Found

The fever broke sometime in the hours when the cave went from grey to black, and Mara Voss woke to the smell of coffee and the sound of a man humming badly.

She knew the tune. Everyone in the interior knew it — some old mail-run ballad about a pilot who flew through weather no sane man would walk through, and died doing it, and was remembered mostly because of the rhyme scheme. Hazel Oyelaran was butchering it with real commitment, off-key in two directions at once, while he crouched over the little camp stove with his back to her.

Mara lay still and took inventory. Toes: present, painful, hers. Fingers: same. The frostbite on her left cheek had gone from white to an angry pink that meant blood was moving again, which meant she'd probably keep the face she had, more or less. Her pack was open beside her — she watched herself notice this, watched the spike of alarm go through her body like a nail — and everything was still in it. The notebook. The duplicate notes. The core samples wrapped in wax paper like somebody's grandmother's Christmas candy.

He hadn't looked. Or he'd looked and put everything back exactly as he'd found it, which would be worse, or better, or both.

"You snore," Hazel said, without turning around. "I want you to know that for the record. Like a small engine with something caught in it."

"I was hypothermic."

"You were hypothermic *and* snoring. Impressive, really. Most people can only manage one catastrophe at a time." He poured coffee into a tin cup and held it out behind him without looking, the way a man offers a treat to a horse he hasn't decided to trust yet. "Drink. Slowly. Your stomach's been on strike for three days and I'd rather not negotiate with it."

She took the cup. Their fingers didn't touch; he made sure of it, pulling his hand back a half-second early, and she thought: *careful man.* She'd been careful too, once. Careful men were either the safest people alive or the most dangerous, depending on what they were being careful about.

"Thank you," she said. It came out rougher than she intended. Three days of ice and silence will do that to a voice. "For the fire. For — all of it."

"Don't thank me yet." He finally turned, settling cross-legged across the stove from her, and up close she could see what the cockpit swagger had hidden: a tiredness around his eyes that wasn't from lack of sleep. It was older than that. Structural. "You've got a story, Ms. Voss. Everybody who hides in a hole in a glacier has a story. And I've spent the last four days flying around this mountain telling myself yours is none of my business."

"And now?"

"Now I'm out of coffee grounds and patience, roughly simultaneously." He nodded at her pack. "I didn't go through your things. I want that understood. But I did see the survey stencil on your kit before you buried it under a rock, and I know what a government-issue sample case looks like, and I know there are six people unaccounted for from that camp, and one of them is thawing out in my cave drinking my coffee." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "So. Story."

*Tell him nothing,* said the part of her brain that had kept her alive for eleven days. *He's a stranger. He might already be theirs. Crane found you once; he can find you twice.*

But Crane hadn't found her. A bush pilot with a bad singing voice and a rule against attachments had found her, by accident, by stubbornness, by flying a canyon pass in weather that would have grounded anyone with sense. And if Hazel Oyelaran were working for the syndicate, she'd be dead already, or zip-tied in the back of a plane headed somewhere with no name. Instead he'd carried her three hundred meters over scree with her arm around his neck, muttering the whole way about how much she weighed, which she'd been too far gone to be insulted by and now rather wished she could take back.

"Voss," she said. "Mara Voss. Structural geologist. Late of the Halvard Institute, formerly of the Meridian Consortium, currently of nowhere in particular." She pulled the notebook from her pack and held it on her knees, not opening it. "And you're right. There's a story. You're not going to like it."

"I never like stories," Hazel said. "That's why I read the endings first."

* * *

She started, as she always did in her head, with the money.

"That survey had seven names on the manifest," she said. "Six at the camp, plus me running the mobile lab out of the second plane. Officially we were doing a glacial stability assessment for the Interior Ministry — ice depth, melt rates, hazard mapping. Boring work. Important work. The kind of work nobody lies about."

"But?"

"But the funding didn't come from the Interior Ministry." She turned the notebook over in her hands. "It came through three shell companies and a foundation with a name so bland I've actually forgotten it. Something with 'Heritage' in it. When I chased the money backward — and I did chase it, because that's the kind of person I am, the kind who reads contracts at parties — every trail dead-ended in the same office in the capital. A man named Aldous Crane."

Hazel's jaw tightened. Just slightly. Enough.

"You know him," she said.

"He knows me now." Hazel fed a stick of wood to the stove. "Showed up at my strip four days ago with a government letterhead and a smile like a loan officer. Wants me to fly him into the interior before the melt. Says the survey team needs 'recovery.'" He pronounced the word the way you'd pronounce a diagnosis. "Six tides. That's how long he gave us before the pass goes soft."

"Then you should know what you're recovering." Mara opened the notebook to the page she'd marked with a pencil stub, the page she'd rewritten four times in her head during eleven days alone in the dark, trying to find a version of it that wouldn't sound insane. There wasn't one. She'd settled on just saying it fast, like diving into cold water. "We weren't mapping hazards. We were hunting ore. And we found it."

She told him.

She told him about the aeromagnetic anomaly that showed up in the baseline data in week two — a reading so clean it looked like instrument error, until they ran it three times and it came back identical each time. About the ground-penetrating radar that drew a shape under four hundred meters of ice that no geological process she knew of could explain: a lens of dense, continuous material, two kilometers long, folded into the root of the mountain like a coin slipped into a pocket. About the day she and Okonkwo rappelled into a crevasse system near the glacier face and came out into a pocket where the ice had melted back from the rock — a chamber the size of a chapel, its walls veined with something that caught their headlamps and threw it back wrong. Not gold. Not silver. Something denser, rarer, with properties that made her hands shake when she ran the first assay and made them shake worse when she ran it again.

"The numbers don't make sense," she said. "That's the honest answer. If the deposit is even half the size the radar suggests, it isn't a mine. It's a reordering. Of markets, of supply chains, of — " she gestured vaguely at the cave, the mountain, the world outside it — "of who owns what. Countries have gone to war over deposits a thousandth of this size."

Hazel had gone very still. Not skeptical-still. Pilot-still — the stillness of a man recalculating weight and balance with new cargo on board.

"And the rest of the team?" he asked quietly. "Okonkwo. Reyes. The others."

The cold water again. Dive.

"They figured it out the same week I did," Mara said. "Maybe faster. Reyes was the one who found the second set of orders — the real ones — encrypted in the satellite uplink schedule. The syndicate wasn't planning to report the discovery. They were planning to sit on it. File a false negative, pull the team out quietly, and come back later with private crews and no paperwork. Except the false negative required everyone to sign off on it, and Reyes wouldn't, and then Okonkwo wouldn't, and then it stopped being a question of signatures." Her voice stayed flat. She'd practiced flat. Flat was load-bearing. "Crane's people arrived on the ninth. Not government. Private security, well equipped, very polite. They took the data cores first. Then they took the men."

"Took them where?"

"I don't know. I was at the secondary camp when it happened — we'd split up to run transects. By the time I got back there was a helicopter pad churned into the snow, boot prints going one direction, and the survey plane's fuselage burned down to the spar. Deliberately. Thermite on the engine block; I found the residue." She looked at her hands. "I ran. Eleven days. I ate my emergency rations in two and then I ate things I'm not proud of. I was heading for the coast on foot when the weather pinned me, and then — " she glanced up at him — "then a lunatic landed a taildragger on a glacier shelf to check on a distress call nobody answered."

"Somebody answered it," Hazel said. "Badly. But somebody answered."

Something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there. She drank her coffee to cover it. It was terrible coffee, burnt and bitter, and it was the best thing she'd ever tasted.

* * *

"So let me make sure I have this straight." Hazel stood, which in the low cave meant hunching, and paced the four steps available to him, which took him approximately nowhere. "A private syndicate used a government survey as a fig leaf. Found the largest mineral deposit in recorded history under a glacier. Decided the law shouldn't get a vote. Made six people disappear to keep it quiet. And now the same government that signed off on the survey has sent the syndicate's errand boy to recruit me — me, a man whose total net worth is one airplane and half a fuel cache — to fly into the site ahead of the melt, ostensibly to rescue the very people his employers vanished."

"When you say it like that, it sounds implausible."

"It sounds *insane*, Ms. Voss."

"Mara."

"It sounds insane, Mara." He stopped pacing. "Why tell me? Genuinely. Why does a woman who's spent eleven days hiding from helicopters hand her whole story to the first stranger who lands?"

It was a fair question, and she respected him for asking it instead of simply taking what she offered. She considered lying — a partial truth, the geology without the syndicate, the science without the bodies. But she was done with partial truths. Partial truths were what had gotten six people killed, herself included in a sense; she'd signed the contract, taken the money, told herself the mountain wouldn't miss what they took, and every hour since had been interest accruing on that lie.

"Because you're the only aircraft on this side of the range," she said. "Because Crane will offer you money, and when that doesn't work he'll offer you something worse, and you deserve to know what you're refusing. Because those six people have families who think they're missing, not murdered, and someone has to make that not true anymore." She paused. "And because you carried me three hundred meters over scree without dropping me, and I've decided to gamble that a man like that is worth betting on."

"A man like what?"

"Stubborn," she said. "Bad singer. Keeps his word apparently even when it's inconvenient." She nodded at the stove. "You told me you'd stay until I could walk. You could have flown out this morning."

"I could have," Hazel agreed. "The weather's turning, though. Front coming down the valley — you can smell it." He crouched again, forearms on his knees, and studied her with an expression she couldn't fully read. Underneath the wariness there was something else. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man finding another survivor of the same shipwreck. "Here's my problem, Mara. Everything you've just told me makes Crane a monster and makes me a fool for shaking his hand. But everything you've just told me also makes you either the bravest witness in this territory or the best liar I've ever met, and I've known some genuinely gifted liars. Customs officers. Ex-wives. One bishop."

Mara laughed — a short, rusty sound that surprised them both. Then she reached into the pack, past the notebook, and drew out the wax-paper bundle. She unwrapped it on the floor between them: three core samples, dull grey-green, unremarkable to look at, heavy as sin.

"One of these assayed at a purity the lab equipment couldn't finish measuring," she said. "There are perhaps four instruments on the continent sensitive enough to confirm what's in this rock, and I destroyed our field unit myself so Crane's people couldn't recover it. This is the only physical evidence that exists. I've slept with it under my head for eleven days." She pushed it toward him. "Take it. Bite it. Lick it, if that's your method. I don't care. But understand that I handed it to you freely, and that I don't do that, and that if you fly out of here tomorrow and sell me to Crane, at least you'll die rich knowing you beat a dying woman at trust."

Hazel didn't touch the samples. He looked at them for a long moment, then at her, and whatever calculation he'd been running behind his eyes finished and cleared.

"I believe you," he said.

"Just like that?"

"No. Not just like that." He picked up one of the cores, turned it in the firelight, set it down with exaggerated gentleness, like it might go off. "Like a man who's spent four days watching a government man lie to him with excellent posture. Your story fits the holes in his. That's usually how you know a story's true — it explains things the official version leaves conveniently dark." He exhaled. "Also, nobody fakes frostbite that ugly. I've seen frostbite. Yours has *character*."

"Flatterer."

"I contain multitudes." He was quiet a moment, feeding the stove, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the performance. "My brother flew survey work once. Similar outfit, different mountains. Came home talking about contracts he wasn't allowed to show anyone and money that didn't add up. Six months later his plane went into a lake and the inquiry called it weather." He looked up. "I've spent nine years believing that. You've just made me wonder if I should have been asking harder questions."

Mara reached across the space between them and put her hand on his forearm — the first deliberate touch either of them had allowed. He didn't pull away.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Yeah." He cleared his throat. "Well. So here's where we are. Crane expects me at the mouth of the pass in two days with a full tank and a cooperative attitude. The melt gives us six tides, maybe less with that front coming in. And somewhere south of here there's a convoy of armed men moving toward this mountain faster than any rescue in history." He rubbed his jaw. "I have fuel for one leg, a passenger who can barely walk, and a hold full of evidence that gets us both killed."

"That's the situation, yes."

"Good." He nodded slowly, and something in his face settled — the tiredness rearranging itself into purpose, the way ice settles into a bridge. "I hate flying with unanswered questions. Now at least I know what's chasing us." He glanced at her, and the corner of his mouth moved. "Fair warning, Mara Voss: I'm going to need you to navigate. My plan for the next six days is to break every regulation I've spent nine years obeying, and I'd like at least one of us to know where we are while I do it."

Mara pulled the notebook onto her lap and opened it to the route maps, and felt — for the first time in eleven days, in eleven months, maybe longer — something that was not fear.

"Get your pencil, pilot," she said. "The pass melts on the fourth tide. After that, we fly the river."
