## 8. First Tide: The Pass Holds

The melt began the way bad news always does in the interior — quietly, and then all at once.

Hazel heard it before he saw it. A sound like a giant clearing its throat somewhere up-valley, a deep wet groan of ice shifting on stone, and then the first trickle running under the wing of the plane as it sat tied down at the survey's forward camp. By dawn there was water talking to itself in every gully, and by mid-morning the pass they'd have to fly through would start doing what passes do in spring: closing.

"Six tides," Mara said, crouched at the edge of the glacier runoff, watching it like it owed her money. "That's what Crane said. Six tides before the melt closes the low route and opens the high one — and the high one belongs to whoever has the most fuel."

"And we have how much?"

"Enough to be interesting."

"That's not a unit of measurement."

"It is where I'm from." She stood, brushed snow off her knees, and looked at him with that flat geological patience she wore like a field jacket. "It means we can't afford to be wrong about anything. Not weather, not weight, not route."

"I'm never wrong about weather."

"You told me yesterday the cloud was 'nothing but scenery.'"

"And I stand by that. It was very pretty while it tried to kill us."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth then — not quite a smile, but the survey stake marking where one might eventually be driven. Hazel had learned to read her face the way he read gauges: slowly, at first, and then all at once, until he wondered how he'd ever flown without it.

They loaded the plane in the gray half-light, working around each other without speaking, which was its own kind of conversation. She handed; he stowed. She knew where everything needed to sit for balance; he knew how much everything weighed by the sound it made hitting the floor. Somewhere over the past two days — since the ice cave, since the firelight, since she'd told him what the survey had really found and who really wanted it — their hands had stopped needing instructions.

"Charts?" he asked.

"Up here." She tapped her temple. "The survey burned its paper maps when we left the last camp. Standard procedure."

"For a legitimate survey."

"For any survey." Her voice went carefully level. "Ours just had more reasons than most."

He didn't push. There'd be time for the rest of her confessions later, if the mountain allowed them a later. He climbed in, ran the checks by feel, and brought the engine up into a cough, a grumble, and finally that steady roar that had been the only voice he trusted for eleven years.

The wheels left the glacier shelf at 6:14 in the morning, and the pass took its first look at them.

* * *

Flying the interior in melt season was like threading a needle that kept moving. The valleys filled with fog off the warming ice; the air went soft and treacherous over the bare slopes, lifting on one side of a ridge and dropping you like a bad habit on the other. Hazel flew the way his brother used to say he did everything — late, fast, and somehow exactly on time.

Mara sat in the right seat with her eyes moving constantly, reading the country the way other people read newspapers.

"There," she said, twenty minutes in, pointing at a notch between two shoulders of rock that looked, to Hazel, identical to the forty notches they'd already passed. "The survey came through there. I remember because Okafor complained the whole way. He said the mountain was personally insulting him."

"Was it?"

"Everything insulted Okafor. That's how we knew he was happy."

Hazel banked toward the notch. It fit them with room to spare — barely. On the far side the valley opened into a long green-brown corridor running northwest, and Mara nodded once, satisfied, a woman confirming the world still matched her memory of it.

"How far ahead of them are we?"

"The convoy?" She did arithmetic in her head, lips moving slightly. "If they left the coast when I think they did, and they're flying the high route — twelve hours. Maybe ten if they're pushing."

"They'll be pushing."

"They'll be pushing," she agreed. "But the high route has weather of its own. And men who are paid by the hour don't push as hard as men who are paid by the result."

"That's either very comforting or very depressing."

"Yes," she said, and this time the smile arrived in full, brief and crooked, gone before he could enjoy it properly. He decided to make it his business to collect more of those. A man needed cargo worth hauling.

* * *

They put down at noon on a lake still wearing its winter lid, six inches of blue ice holding them up out of pure stubbornness. Hazel taxied to the lee shore, shut down, and got out to stretch while Mara walked the perimeter with binoculars, checking the ridgelines out of habit neither of them commented on anymore.

"Fuel first," she said, coming back. "Then food. Then you can tell me why you keep touching the strut."

"I'm not touching the strut."

"You've touched it four times since we landed."

He looked down. His hand was on the strut. "Checking for ice," he said with dignity. "A pilot's habits are sacred objects. You wouldn't understand."

"I understand ritual. I've spent my life around people who tap rocks twice and call it science." She sat down on the pontoon beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and pulled two cans of beans from her coat. Cold beans, no fire, no smoke to give them away. She passed him a spoon. "Here. Gourmet."

They ate in companionable silence, watching the lake breathe under its ice — long slow swells traveling shore to shore like something turning over in its sleep.

"My brother hated cold beans," Hazel said, before he quite decided to say it.

Mara didn't turn her head. She had a way of receiving things without making them heavier. "Tell me about him."

"Ezra. Younger by two years. Louder by about forty." Hazel turned the can in his hands. "He wanted to fly charter with me. I kept telling him to finish school first, get his hours somewhere safe. Somewhere flat." He shrugged. "He got his hours somewhere flat. Died on a mail run in clear weather. Engine let go over open ground, plenty of room to set down, and he still hit hard enough that—" He stopped. Started again. "The board called it survivable conditions. I've thought about that phrase every day for nine years. Survivable conditions. As if the conditions were the whole story."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then: "My last job ended with a report I wrote being used to strip a hillside that I'd recommended protecting. They edited it. Cut the recommendation section entirely, kept the ore grades." She laughed, short and without humor. "Survivable conditions. My career survived. Nothing else did."

"So we're both ghosts."

"We're both ghosts," she agreed. "Though you're a better-restored model."

"I wax regularly."

She bumped her shoulder against his, and he found, to his mild alarm, that the touch stayed with him longer than the beans did. Eleven years of flying alone, and it had taken one frostbitten geologist in an ice cave to remind him that a cockpit was a small place to keep a whole life.

"First tide's ours," she said finally, standing, brushing off her coat. "Let's go spend it."

* * *

The afternoon gave them the long valley run — ninety miles of river corridor with the sun behind them and the melt swelling below, the water going from whisper to argument as the day warmed. Hazel flew low enough to see the current, because Mara wanted to see the current.

"Faster water means faster melt means the pass closes sooner," she explained, leaning toward the window. "And look — that gravel bar wasn't an island yesterday. It's an island now. The river's rising ahead of schedule."

"You've been here twice."

"Once, and once was enough to leave marks on me." She pointed ahead. "Bear the left wall after the bend. There's a canyon cut that saves us eight miles of dogleg, and the survey used it because Okafor—"

"—was insulted by doglegs?"

"Deeply. Professionally."

The canyon took them in with walls close enough to count the strata, and Mara named the layers as they slid past — old seafloors, ash beds, a band of white quartzite she called "the good stuff" with the reverence other people saved for cathedrals. Hazel listened with half an ear and all of his attention, because listening to her talk about rock was rapidly becoming his favorite way to burn fuel.

And then, over the engine's drone, thin and far away, he heard the other engine.

He knew engines the way Mara knew strata. This one was big, twin, turbocharged — a heavy hauler built for range, not for landing on lakes. It was behind them and above them, riding the ridge line where the light made it hard to see, and it was matching their speed.

"Mara."

"I hear it." All the warmth had gone out of her voice, packed away like instruments at day's end. "Don't climb. Don't turn. Fly stupid."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Fly like a bush pilot with nothing to hide. Wander. Look for a campsite. If we act like prey, we confirm we're worth catching." Her eyes were on the mirror now. "There. Ridge line, our seven o'clock. Silver fuselage."

He saw it then — a glint sliding along the crest, patient as a shark finning. Not closing. Counting.

"They're logging our heading," Hazel said.

"They're logging everything. Every mile we fly tells them where we think the value is." She settled back in her seat, arms crossed, jaw set. "So we give them a boring afternoon."

For the next hour Hazel flew the most deliberately unremarkable aviation of his career. He circled a likely-looking meadow and pretended to consider it. He descended to buzz a frozen waterfall, the kind of thing a bored pilot does to amuse himself. He even, at Mara's deadpan suggestion, waggled his wings at a herd of caribou, who were unimpressed in the way of animals who have seen pilots before.

Behind them, the silver shape held its distance, held its altitude, held its silence.

"He's writing us down," Hazel muttered. "I can feel him taking notes."

"Good. Let him write *bush pilot, erratic, alone.*" A pause. "Is it alone? Can he see me?"

"Not from above with the canopy glare. You're just a duffel bag with opinions."

"Flattering."

"Honesty is the foundation of partnership."

The shadow followed them to the mouth of the next valley and then, as the light went long and gold, peeled away northward without ceremony — climbing back over the divide toward the high route, toward the fleet, carrying its notes home.

Mara exhaled slowly. "They know our line now."

"They know a line. We'll fly another one tonight." Hazel checked his watch, then the sky, then the fuel gauge, in descending order of comfort. "Which brings up the arithmetic problem."

"Say it."

"If we run the direct line to the next cache, we arrive with thirty minutes of reserve and no margin for weather. If we divert south to Kettle Creek — the ghost town, the old mine strip — we lose half a tide but we sleep behind walls with fuel on the ground."

Mara was quiet long enough that he glanced over. She was looking at the western horizon, where clouds were stacking up in slow gray shelves, the mountains building tomorrow's weather tonight.

"Kettle Creek," she said. "Crane will expect the direct line. He'll have men at the cache."

"Then Kettle Creek it is." He rolled the plane into a gentle turn, southbound, the evening sun flaring across the windscreen and filling the cockpit with amber. For a moment they were flying inside a piece of honey, and Mara's face was lit gold, and Hazel thought — not for the first time, but for the first time without flinching — that some cargoes change the pilot.

"You're smiling," she observed.

"I'm navigating."

"You're smiling *and* navigating. That's against regulations."

"Most of the best things are."

She shook her head, but she was fighting the smile again, losing, and the valley opened ahead of them, dark and generous, and somewhere behind the divide the salvage fleet was burning fuel and midnight oil to catch a ghost that had already changed course.

Half a tide spent. Five and a half to run. Behind them the summit caught the last of the light and held it, the way the mountain held everything — patiently, and never for free.
