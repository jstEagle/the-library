## 9. The Fuel Ledger

Hazel had a rule about fuel caches: you counted them twice, and you never trusted the second count to be the same as the first. Ice heaved drums around. Meltwater found seams. Once, a wolverine had chewed through a gasket and drunk himself stupid on avgas — Hazel had found him the next morning, sprawled in the snow like a small furry corpse at a wake, and had carried him to the treeline to sleep it off.

So when he knelt beside the cache at Kettle Creek that morning, breath smoking in the gray half-light, and saw the numbers come up wrong, his first thought was not sabotage. His first thought was *wolverine*.

The second drum was light. Not empty — light. He rocked it on its base and felt the slosh sit low, maybe forty gallons where there should have been ninety. The third drum was worse. The bung had been backed off a quarter turn, neat as you please, and a rag stuffed into the threads — packed tight enough to let vapor weep out slow over weeks but not enough to smell wrong unless you put your nose right to it, which nobody did, because nobody visited Kettle Creek except Hazel Oyelaran and the occasional bear with poor taste.

He sat back on his heels in the snow and looked at it for a long time.

"Wolverines don't use rags," Mara said behind him.

She'd come up the bank with the fuel gauge from the plane tucked under her arm, her frost-scarred fingers wrapped in fingerless gloves she'd improvised from a mail sack. She crouched beside the drum and studied the rag without touching it. In the flat morning light her face looked carved out of something patient and hard.

"No," Hazel said. "They don't."

"Crane's people came through here."

"Crane's people came through here." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it went when the numbers stopped working, and he hated it, because Mara deserved better than flat. "Or somebody who wants us walking."

She didn't say anything for a moment. Then: "How bad?"

Hazel pulled the ledger out of his jacket — an actual paper ledger, soft-cornered, its spine held together with friction tape and stubbornness. Every pilot he knew had gone digital years ago. Hazel kept the book because ink didn't lose signal, didn't need charging, and couldn't be remotely edited by a government man with soft hands and a harder agenda. He licked his thumb, turned past the mail runs and the medicine flights and the page where he tracked, in careful columns, exactly how much of his life he'd spent burning other people's money across the sky, and found the cache page.

"Kettle Creek should hold two hundred ten gallons," he said. "Usable, I've got maybe one-twenty. The strip at Coldfoot's another hundred, if it hasn't been touched." He did the arithmetic the way he did everything else, silently and twice. "Coldfoot puts us at the pass with a margin of eleven gallons. Eleven gallons is not a margin. Eleven gallons is a prayer with a receipt."

"And if Coldfoot's been touched too?"

"Then we're a very well-documented pair of corpses."

Mara laughed. It surprised them both — a short, cracked sound, more surprise than humor, but real. She pressed her glove against her mouth like she could push it back in, and failed, and laughed again.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's just — I spent six weeks doing geology under an assumed name for a criminal syndicate, and I never once laughed. And now we're stranded in the tundra with a sabotaged fuel cache and I can't stop."

"Don't apologize for it." Hazel closed the ledger. "Laughing's free. It's the only thing left around here that is."

* * *

They flew the short hop to Coldfoot with the tank needle already leaning toward empty like a tired man against a doorframe. Hazel flew it high and smooth, no flourishes, trading speed for every drop, and Mara rode copilot with the map open on her knees, calling terrain in a low, even voice that he was beginning to rely on more than he wanted to admit.

"Creek bends north in two miles," she said. "There's a shelf of rock past it — the survey marked it as a landmark. Reddish stone."

"I know the rock."

"You know everything out here."

"No," Hazel said. "I know what I know. Out here that's usually enough. Today it isn't."

Coldfoot's strip appeared out of the murk looking exactly as it always did: a scar of gravel some mining company had graded in 1961 and then abandoned along with its dignity. The cache there was buried under a cairn of stones, and Hazel set the plane down with the particular gentleness of a man who knows his landing gear is the only thing between him and a long walk, and taxied to the cairn, and got out, and stood very still.

The cairn had been rebuilt. Rebuilt *well*. The stones were stacked with a care that almost looked respectful, and the ground around it had been swept with a pine bough so the footprints were gone, all but one partial print near the drum — a boot heel, deep-treaded, military or pretending to be.

"They're tidy," Mara said, coming up beside him.

"They're thorough." Hazel started pulling stones. "Tidy and thorough are different things. Tidy means they care how it looks. Thorough means they care that it works. These people only care about the second one."

The drum under the cairn told the story without any help. Bung backed off. Rag in the threads. Fuel down by evaporation to a spiteful sixty gallons. Somebody had flown in here — a ski plane or a big-wheeled Cessna, nothing bigger would've made this strip — sometime in the last week, and they hadn't rushed, and they hadn't worried about being seen, because they knew exactly who would find their work and exactly how far from anywhere he'd be standing when he did.

Hazel stood over the drum with his hands on his hips and let the cold work its way through his coat while he thought about Aldous Crane. Crane, who had shown up at Hazel's strip in a pressed coat with a folder full of photographs and a voice like a man reading terms aloud. Crane, who had said *reach the wreck site before the salvage fleet does* as though rescue were a matter of punctuality. Crane, who had shaken Hazel's hand with a grip calibrated to communicate nothing at all.

"He never intended for us to make it," Mara said quietly. "Not both of us. Maybe not either of us. We're useful until we're not, and then the mountains do the paperwork."

"That's the part that gets me." Hazel kicked snow over the rag, pointlessly. "He didn't steal the fuel. Stealing's honest. He let it leak. Slow. Quiet. So that when we run dry somewhere over the pass, the report writes itself: experienced bush pilot, poor planning, tragic loss. Nobody audits a tragedy."

Mara looked at him sideways. "You've thought about this a lot."

"I've thought about being blamed for things my whole life. Gets faster with practice."

* * *

Sixty gallons at Coldfoot plus whatever Kettle Creek still held gave them — he ran it three times, hoping the numbers would get embarrassed and change — a range that fell short of the pass by a distance he could measure in hours of walking, if walking were an option, which over that country it was not. Not with the melt coming. Not with the rivers waking up under the ice like something stretching after a long sleep.

So Hazel sat on the tailwheel of his own airplane, ledger open on his knee, and did the thing he hated most in the world, which was arithmetic in front of an audience.

"There's Anvil," he said. "Ghost town. Old copper camp, up the Moraine River. There was a drum cache there from the survey resupply — Mara, your outfit staged through it?"

"We did." She'd gone still, the way she did when memory cost something. "Two drums, maybe three. It's thirty miles off our line. Detour costs us—" she glanced at the sky, reading it the way he read ledgers, "—most of a day. A full tide."

"A full tide," Hazel agreed. Six tides of melting pass, Crane had called it, and now one of those tides belonged to a man with a rag and a quarter-turn wrench. "And if Anvil's been touched too, we'll have spent the day learning it."

"It won't have been." Mara said it with a certainty that made him look up. "Anvil's not on any current chart. The syndicate's route sheets — the ones I saw — went Kettle Creek, Coldfoot, the pass. They planned around the maps everyone uses. But the survey flew Anvil once, early, because I asked. I wanted the copper-stained rock for reference." She hesitated, and then gave him the rest of it straight, which was becoming, he noticed, her way. "Nobody knows about Anvil because nobody bothered to ask me anything. That was the whole culture there. Questions were a kind of weakness."

Hazel closed the ledger. "Then Anvil it is. Thirty miles sideways and a day behind, chasing fuel that might not exist, based on the memory of a woman the syndicate thinks is dead."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounded insane the first four ways too. This was just the politest."

* * *

The Moraine River valley took them in the way valleys do — gradually, then completely. The walls rose brown and gray, streaked with old slide paths like scars on a knuckle, and the river below ran dark in its channels, braiding and rebraiding, still mostly frozen but talking to itself in places, little voices of open water that hadn't spoken since last fall. Hazel flew low enough to read the ice and high enough to live, and Mara leaned toward her window with her breath fogging the glass, watching the rock go by with an expression he'd learned to recognize: hunger. The good kind. The kind that had gotten her into geology in the first place, before money and men with folders got their hands on it.

"There," she said suddenly, pointing. "See the stain? Orange band in the cutbank. That's why I asked to fly Anvil. Iron oxide — means sulfides underneath, means the whole district's mineralized. I stood in that town for twenty minutes and I swear I forgot the survey existed. Just walked around touching rocks like a child."

"What happened to the town?"

"Copper died in '29. Everybody walked out. They say the last man left the schoolhouse clock running." She smiled faintly. "Probably not true. But it's the kind of true a place like that earns."

Anvil came around the bend of the valley all at once, the way ghosts do. A headframe leaning like a drunk against a lamppost. A row of company houses with their windows gone dark and blind, snow drifted against the doors. A schoolhouse with its bell tower cocked at an angle, patient as a question. And a strip — rutted, heaved by frost, but a strip — running along the river terrace like a sentence somebody had started and never finished.

Hazel brought the plane in slow, feeling every rut through the controls like a handshake from an old acquaintance, and rolled to a stop in the middle of the ghost town's one street, if a street could be said to exist where no wheels had turned in fifty years.

The silence after shutdown was enormous. Not empty silence — inhabited silence. The kind a place keeps up out of habit.

"Well," Mara said softly. "Hello, Anvil."

* * *

The cache was where the survey notes said it would be, in the root cellar of the third company house, under a floorboard that Mara found on the first try because of course she did; she'd helped bury it herself, back when she'd still believed the survey was a survey. Three drums. Hazel opened the first with his heart going like a kid's, and the smell that came up out of it was clean and honest and sweet as anything he'd ever smelled: avgas, untouched, full to within a hand's width of the bung.

The second drum matched. The third was short by ten gallons — mice, weather, God's small taxes — but it counted.

Hazel sat down on the cellar steps with the ledger and wrote the numbers in, and his hand wasn't quite steady, and he decided not to have feelings about that. One hundred eighty gallons usable. Enough to reach the pass with a margin. Not a comfortable margin — Crane had killed comfortable somewhere back at Kettle Creek — but a margin. A number you could bet a life on, which was, when he thought about it honestly, the only kind of number he'd ever bet anything on.

Mara came down the steps with an armful of scavenged treasure: a coil of copper wire, a tin of matches gone soft but serviceable, and a kerosene lantern that she lit with the care of a woman handling a relic. Its light filled the cellar the way light fills a church, yellow and forgiving, catching the frost on the joists overhead.

"There's a stove in the assay office," she said. "Chimney looks sound. If we're spending the night—"

"We're spending the night." Hazel clicked his pen closed. "Sun's too low to make the pass approach and land it in one piece, and I don't fly that canyon tired. Tomorrow, first light, we're gone. Tonight we're rich in gallons and poor in everything else, and I've been poorer."

They carried the lantern up through the town, boots crunching on the crusted snow, past houses whose doors hung open on rooms where somebody's life had simply stopped mid-sentence — a chair pushed back from a table, a calendar on a wall, its pages furred with mold, stuck forever on a month neither of them had been born in. Mara touched the doorframe of the schoolhouse as they passed, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder.

"The clock?" Hazel asked.

She pointed. Above the blackboard, bolted to the wall, hung a schoolhouse clock, its face yellowed, its hands stopped at nine minutes past three.

"Somebody wound it on their way out," she said. "Ran till the spring, probably. Then just — stopped. Nine minutes past three, forever." She was quiet a moment. "You measure your life in miles, you said. I think this town measured its life in minutes, and then it decided to stop counting."

Hazel looked at the clock longer than he meant to. Nine minutes past three. Some afternoon fifty years gone, some last man winding the spring because leaving it dead felt like murder, and then the long white quiet. He thought about his own ledger, the columns of miles, the careful refusal to add up anything else. He thought about a woman standing next to him in a dead town, reading its mind.

"Yeah," he said. "Well. Some of us take longer to stop counting."

In the assay office, the stove drew clean and the lantern light was warm, and they ate beans and tinned bread and drank coffee brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in, sitting on assay stools in a room where men had once weighed rock against the future. Outside, the wind moved through the headframe cables with a low ringing note, like the town humming to itself in its sleep.

Mara spread the survey maps on the desk — her duplicate notes, the ones that had gotten them this far — and weighted the corners with bolts, and began tracing tomorrow's line to the pass with one scarred fingertip. Hazel watched her do it and understood, with the simple finality of a fuel gauge falling, that the detour had cost him more than a tide. It had cost him the fiction that this was duty. Somewhere between Kettle Creek and a stopped clock, the race had stopped being about the wreck site, or Crane, or the debt of the missing. It was about the woman at the desk, and getting her through the pass alive, and what came after, which he had never once let himself plan for and was now, helplessly, planning.

"Hazel." She'd looked up. "You're staring."

"Doing arithmetic," he said.

"On what?"

"Whether we make the pass tomorrow."

"And?"

He looked at the map, at her finger resting on the blue line of the glacier, at the small pencil cross marking the survey's last camp. "We make it," he said. "But Mara — if Crane's people were tidy at Coldfoot, they'll be tidy at the pass too. Whatever's waiting for us on that ice, it isn't an accident."

Outside, the wind rang the headframe cables once, long and clear, like a bell counting out the hour — though no hour, in Anvil, had counted for fifty years.
