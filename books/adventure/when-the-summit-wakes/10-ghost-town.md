## 10. Ghost Town

The town announced itself the way ghosts do — not all at once, but in pieces. First a chimney standing alone in the alder scrub like a man who'd forgotten why he came into the room. Then a water tower on three legs, listing eastward as if it had been listening to a long story and finally lost interest. Then the street.

Hazel brought the plane down on what had once been the main road, gravel and frost-heave and a decade of alder seedlings punching through, and rolled it out under the leaning arm of the water tower. The engine ticked itself quiet. Somewhere down the valley a raven said something unkind and flew off.

"Charming," Mara said from the right seat. Her voice was still rough from the cave, from the frostbite, from everything, but there was a dryness in it now that Hazel was beginning to look forward to the way other men looked forward to mail.

"It's got potential," he said. "Fixable up. Good light. Very quiet neighbors."

"What's it called?"

He squinted at the faded board over what had been the general store: KETTLE RIVER — POP. 214. "Kettle River," he read. "Population two hundred and fourteen."

Mara looked out at the empty street, at the snow drifting through the doorway of the hotel like it paid rent there. "I count fewer than that."

"They're shy this time of year."

* * *

The fuel was the reason they'd come, and the fuel was where it always is in towns like this — wherever somebody thought the boom would last forever. In Kettle River's case that meant the assay office, because whoever built it had been so certain of gold that they'd installed a generator, a pump, and four steel drums of aviation gasoline in a concrete vault behind the safe, presumably so the flying machines of the glorious future could refuel without troubling the shareholders.

The future had arrived forty years late, cold, and short one survey team, but the drums were there. Two were full by the sound of them when Hazel knocked — a deep, honest slosh. One was half gone to rust and weeping rust-colored tears onto the floor. The fourth had been tapped by some prospector or trapper years ago and recapped with a cork and optimism.

"Two full drums," Hazel reported, wiping his hands on his trousers. "Call it eighty gallons usable if my siphon doesn't betray me."

"And you need how much to make the pass?"

"On a good day, sixty-five." He didn't say *and today isn't a good day*, but the arithmetic sat between them anyway, doing its cold little dance. Sixty-five gallons of fuel against six tides of melting pass, a convoy with better planes and worse intentions, and a detour that had already cost them most of a day.

Mara crouched beside the drums, turned her bandaged hands carefully, and studied the pump fittings the way she'd probably studied core samples. "Then we take both drums, we top off here until the tanks won't hold another drop, and we carry the rest in cans."

"In what cans?"

She looked at him.

"I'm just asking," Hazel said, "because I've flown with optimists before, and they all weigh more on the way back."

* * *

They found the cans in the hotel, which was the kind of building that keeps its secrets badly. Snow had gotten in through the roof and made a small private glacier of its own down the staircase, and the rooms stood open like mouths mid-sentence — a boot here, a playing card there, a calendar frozen on a month neither of them bothered to check. In the kitchen, behind the stove, someone had left eleven five-gallon cans, rinsed and stoppered, waiting for a trip that never happened.

"Somebody meant to come back," Mara said.

"Somebody always does." Hazel hefted a can. It was clean inside, dry, sound. He set it by the door with the gentleness people use for other people's unfinished business. "We'll bring them back empty. That's more than most ghosts get."

* * *

By the time the light went copper through the west windows, the plane was topped off, seven cans were lashed and ready, and Hazel's shoulders had filed a formal complaint. They carried the last drum up to the hotel kitchen — the only room with a stove, a table, and a door that closed — and Mara got a fire going in the old range while Hazel did the thing he pretended wasn't checking the weather: standing at the window, looking north, saying nothing.

"You do that a lot," she said.

"Do what?"

"The window thing. Like the mountain's going to move if you don't watch it."

"It has been known to." He came away from the glass and sat across the table from her. The stove ticked and settled. She'd found a kettle somewhere and set it on, and the smell of hot metal and old dust slowly giving way to steam was, Hazel thought, about the finest perfume ever manufactured. "Weather's holding. Wind's supposed to lay down after midnight. If we're wheels-up at first light, we buy back some of the tide we spent getting here."

"And if we're not?"

"Then Crane's fleet gets a day closer, and I spend the whole flight doing arithmetic instead of flying."

Mara turned her mug slowly on the table. Her hands were still wrapped — the frostbite on her fingers had been second-degree at worst, the doctor in her would say, though the doctor in her also would have said *you should have come in out of the cold nine days ago, Dr. Voss*. "You know," she said, "we've been sharing a plane, a cave, and a running gun-adjacent situation for going on four days, and I realize I don't actually know anything about you except that you fly well, siphon fuel competently, and talk to your engine like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money."

"There it is. Deflection. Classic."

Hazel laughed — surprised out of him, rough at the edges from disuse. Outside, the wind moved down the empty street and rattled something loose in the assay office, and for a moment the town sounded almost inhabited.

"All right," he said. "What do you want to know?"

"Why a man who flies like you do lives alone at the end of a mail route, measures his life in miles, and flinches every time anybody asks him a question with a year in it."

"That's three questions."

"I'm thorough. It's why I got disgraced."

* * *

**Mara**

She hadn't meant to say that last part out loud. But the stove was warm and the tea was hot and the man across the table had a face that made honesty feel less like confession and more like conversation, and Mara Voss had been alone with her own company for eleven days in an ice cave, and her own company was terrible.

"Go on," Hazel said. Not pushing. Just leaving the door open the way he seemed to leave everything open — the cockpit window, the question, the silence.

So she told him.

"I had a career," she said. "A real one. Glaciology, structural geology. At thirty-one I had a chair at a university and a grant line and graduate students who spelled my name right on their applications. And then I published a paper."

"Papers are usually the point."

"This one said that a formation everybody in the field had built twenty years of careers on was misdated. Wrong by forty million years. Which meant their models were wrong, which meant their funding arguments were wrong, which meant a lot of very comfortable men had to choose between admitting I was right and making sure nobody ever heard from me again." She turned the mug again. "You can guess how that vote went. It wasn't even close. There was a committee. Committees are where careers go to be buried politely, with nice words at the funeral. 'Rigorous,' they called me. 'Prolific.' 'Perhaps better suited to applied work.'"

"And then a government survey offered you applied work."

"Offered me a paycheck and a helicopter ride into ice nobody had ever cored." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did around the part of the story she hadn't finished telling anyone, including herself. "I took it because I needed the money and because I told myself the mountains wouldn't miss what we took out of them. Rocks don't vote. That was my whole ethics, Hazel. Rocks don't vote."

The kettle sighed. Out in the dark, snow ticked against the window like fingers testing the glass.

"And then we found the lode," she said, "and I learned that rocks do have opinions, and mine were being counted by men with guns. And the rest of the team—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The rest of the team trusted the wrong frequency too."

Hazel reached across the table and put his hand — big, scarred, grease still in the creases — lightly over her bandaged ones. He didn't say anything. That was the thing about him she was having trouble filing: he knew when the kindest instrument was silence.

"So," she said, blinking hard and pretending it was the woodsmoke. "That's my disgrace. Your turn, pilot. Why does a man live at the end of a mail route counting miles instead of years?"

* * *

**Hazel**

He should have known the question would circle back. Everything circled back; that was the trouble with questions, they had the homing instinct of birds.

"My brother Thomas wanted to be a pilot," Hazel said. "I wanted to be whatever Thomas wanted to be, which is how it goes when you're younger and he's louder. He got his license first. Flew floatplanes up the coast, then bush work in the interior. I followed him up two years later and spent the whole time trying to land smoother than him, which was impossible, because Thomas landed like the airplane was setting down on his mother's good linoleum."

Mara smiled. "And you?"

"I landed like I was paying by the bounce."

Her laugh cracked out of her, quick and real, and Hazel found he liked the sound of it more than was strictly convenient.

"He died eight years ago," Hazel said. "Not flying. That's the joke everyone expects and it isn't true. A truck, on the coast road, in the rain. Some kid fell asleep at the wheel. Thomas never even got to be reckless in the air. All those hours, all that skill, and the mountain never touched him. A highway did." He looked at his hands. "After that I couldn't— Everyone kept saying things. *He's in a better place. Time heals.* Time doesn't heal anything, it just moves you further from the site of the accident. So I stopped counting time and started counting miles. Miles are honest. A mile is a mile no matter who's waiting at either end of it. You log enough of them and the years get embarrassed and stop introducing themselves."

"And the crash?" Mara asked gently. "You mentioned a crash taught you to keep flying. That's usually the other way around."

"Yeah, well." He huffed. "Three years ago. Engine failure over the Kettle, as it happens — this river, right down there. Put it in the water hard. Swam out, stood on the bank watching it sink, and I remember thinking, very clearly: *well, now you can quit.* Nobody would've blamed me. Insurance would've covered it. I could've taken a job in a hangar, indoors, where nothing tries to kill you and nothing matters."

"But you didn't."

"I ordered a new engine before I'd finished drying off." He shook his head at himself. "Because I understood, standing on that bank, that quitting wouldn't be quitting flying. It'd be quitting the only place I ever felt close to him. Up there, at altitude, with the range laid out below — that's the closest thing to hearing his voice I've got left. He's in the way the air behaves. He's in the lee side of every ridge. I keep flying because up there I'm not the brother who lived. I'm just a pilot, and pilots don't have time for that particular grief. It's selfish, probably."

"It's human," Mara said. "Probably both. Most true things are."

* * *

They sat with that awhile, the fire working, the snow ticking, the ghost town keeping its distance around them like a crowd that had agreed to give the two of them the floor.

Then Mara said, "Your rule. Against attachments. Where'd that come from? Because I've noticed you follow it the way I used to follow my grant deadlines — technically, resentfully, and with a great deal of evidence to the contrary."

Hazel opened his mouth to deflect and found, to his mild alarm, that he didn't want to. "Attachments are weight," he said. "Weight is performance. Every pound you carry costs you altitude, range, options. I learned that in the water down there. A man alone is always within glide of somewhere he can land."

"And a man with attachments?"

"Is always carrying fuel he can't spare for a place he might not reach." He met her eyes across the stove-light. "That's the arithmetic. That's the whole ugly ledger. And I've been running it for eight years, and it balances perfectly, and it has bought me exactly nothing."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then, with the particular courage of a woman who had already lost one career and therefore had less left to lose: "Maybe the ledger's wrong."

"Geologist's opinion?"

"Structural. The fault line runs through the whole model. You've been measuring the wrong axis." She smiled, tired and crooked. "It happens to the best formations."

Hazel laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, and he let it, because the stove was warm and the town was dead and the convoy was a day south and none of it, none of it, mattered as much as the fact that she was laughing too, and that her hand had turned palm-up under his without either of them deciding it.

* * *

**Mara**

Later — much later, the fire burned to orange coals, the two of them wrapped in horse blankets from the hotel that smelled of cedar and decades — Mara lay listening to the building settle and did the thing she did with data: reviewed it honestly.

Four days ago she'd pulled a stranger out of a blizzard's aftermath with a pistol she barely knew how to hold and a plan that consisted entirely of *don't die yet*. The stranger had turned out to be a man who talked to engines, grieved in nautical miles, and held her bandaged hands like they were instruments he was afraid of breaking. She had signed on to a lie for money and told herself the mountain wouldn't mind, and the mountain had minded enormously, and the only thing between her and the syndicate's version of events was this man's stubborn, competent, infuriating refusal to leave people unaccounted for.

She was going to get him killed. Or he was going to get her free. The data supported both hypotheses, and she hated data that refused to resolve.

"Hazel," she said into the dark. "Are you awake?"

A pause. "Define awake."

"If we get out of this. After. What happens to us?"

Another pause, longer. When he spoke, his voice came low and unhurried, the way he read weather. "I've been asking myself that since the cave. Best answer I've got is: I don't fly alone anymore. Whatever that costs."

Mara stared at the ceiling, where the firelight made moving shapes like aurora trapped indoors, and felt the cold arithmetic of the last week quietly lose an argument it had been winning her whole life.

"That's the worst flight plan I've ever heard," she said. "No alternate, no reserve, no margin."

"Best ones never have any. You just commit and read the ground."

She was still smiling at that when she slept.

* * *

**Hazel**

He woke to grey light and a sound that didn't belong to Kettle River's population of zero: a low drone, far off to the south, riding under the wind like a note held on a bad radio.

He was at the window with binoculars before his boots were fully on. Down the valley, above the tree line, a speck resolved into two — aircraft, running the river corridor north, tight formation, disciplined. Not mail planes. Mail planes wandered. These flew like they were being paid by the mile and shot by the mistake.

"Mara." His voice came out flat and calm, the cockpit voice, the one that meant the numbers had stopped being friendly. "Get dressed. We're leaving now."

She was up instantly, frostbite and all, pulling her boots on one-handed. "Convoy?"

"Convoy. They're not following our track — they're ahead of schedule, and they're cutting straight up the Kettle." He swung the binoculars south again, jaw tightening. Which meant they weren't searching. Which meant they knew where he and Mara had gone, or where they were going, or both. Which meant Crane had a source Hazel hadn't found yet, and the six-tide race had just quietly become something else.

Behind him, Mara lashed the last fuel can with quick, sure knots, and when she spoke, her voice matched his — flat, calm, wide awake.

"How long until they pass over the town?"

Hazel watched the specks grow, did the math, and didn't like any of the answers.

"Less than that," he said, and reached for the propeller.
