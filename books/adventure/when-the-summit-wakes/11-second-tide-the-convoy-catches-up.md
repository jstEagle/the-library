## 11. Second Tide: The Convoy Catches Up

The ghost town's strip was a rumor of a runway — a thousand feet of frost-heaved gravel that some optimistic soul had graded in 1938 and nobody had touched since. Hazel walked it twice at first light, boots crunching, kicking aside a drift of snow that had crept across the middle third like a slow gray tide. Mara followed with the fuel hose over her shoulder, breath steaming, hair escaping from under her cap in copper wisps.

"Opinion?" she asked.

"It'll fly off it," Hazel said. "It just won't enjoy it."

"Neither will I."

"That makes two of us. Misery loves company, and so does takeoff roll."

They'd spent the night in the old assay office, which had a stove that smoked like a guilty conscience and a floor that listed to port. They'd scavenged forty gallons from a rusted drum behind the mine head — aviation gas, miraculously, left over from some survey crew thirty years dead, still good enough after Hazel had filtered it through chamois and prayer. The ledger in his head had finally balanced: enough to reach the pass, enough to cross the second tide before the melt swallowed it, not enough for mistakes.

He'd told her about Tom last night. She'd told him about the hearing in Vancouver where they'd taken her license and her name and handed back only her name. They'd sat close on one bench because the other bench had collapsed years ago, and at some point her shoulder had settled against his arm as naturally as weather, and neither of them had moved it.

Now she stowed the drum straps and looked up at the sky the way she'd been doing all morning — a quick, flat glance east, then west, then east again.

"You keep checking the ceiling," he said.

"I keep checking the *east*."

"The convoy?"

"I heard engines in my sleep. Or I dreamed them. With those people it's hard to know the difference."

Hazel climbed into the cockpit and ran the preflight by touch, every switch and lever an old friend with a known handshake. The engine caught on the second try, coughed out its overnight grievances, and settled into that steady four-beat rhythm he trusted more than most people. He watched Mara swing up onto the wing and drop into the seat beside him, and he felt the small, unaccustomed weight of someone else's life riding on his hands, and found — to his own surprise — that it didn't frighten him. It focused him. Like adding ballast to a plane that had been flying too light for too long.

"Second tide," he said, and pushed the throttle forward.

* * *

The strip gave them ninety feet of gravel and then a long fall into the river valley, and Hazel flew off it the way you'd step off a curb — wheels leaving while the tail was still arguing about it. The old girl sank, wallowed, found the cold morning air down in the valley bottom, and began to climb in patient spirals, trading airspeed for altitude the way a careful man trades dollars.

Mara had the map folded to the canyon section, her finger tracing the river. "If we stay low through the narrows we're under their sight line until the fork," she said. "After that—"

She didn't finish, because the sound arrived.

It came down the valley from the east, rolling ahead of itself off the canyon walls — not one engine but several, deep-throated and wrong, the sound of big radial engines run rich and mean. Hazel knew every honest engine sound in the north the way a shepherd knows sheep, and this wasn't sheep. This was wolves with money.

"They're here." Mara's voice had gone very quiet and very level, which he was learning was how she got when things were worst.

"How far?"

"Behind the eastern ridge. Minutes."

Hazel did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. They were at eight hundred feet and climbing at two hundred a minute. The pass was eleven miles west. A loaded bush plane does not outrun anything built in the last decade. His eyes went to the canyon below them — the narrows, the slot where the river pinched between walls of black granite, the route Mara had been pointing at when the world interrupted.

"There," he said.

"Hazel, that canyon is—"

"I know what it is. I've flown it twice in twenty years, both times going the other direction, both times swearing never again." He rolled into a descending turn, and the horizon tilted, and the river came up at them like something alive. "But nobody who values their paint job follows us in there."

"And if they don't value their paint job?"

"Then we find out what this airplane and I are made of."

The walls rose on either side. He leveled off fifty feet above the water, close enough that the prop wash bent the river into silver feathers, close enough to see the individual stones on the bars, the ice shelves clinging to the rock like dirty lace. The canyon narrowed. The walls leaned in. Somewhere above them the sky went on existing without their permission.

Mara had gone silent in a way he recognized — not fear exactly, or not only fear, but the fierce attention of a person watching someone else do the dangerous thing. Her hand had found the edge of her seat and gripped it, knuckles pale, and her eyes weren't shut. They were open, tracking the river ahead, reading the water the way she read strata, calling the bends half a second before he needed them.

"Bend right, then the shelf on your left, then it opens—" 

"I see it."

"Then stop talking and fly, Oyelaran."

He almost laughed. Terror and something else, something bright and electric, running together in his chest like two wires stripped and twisted — and he realized, with the river flashing beneath him and the granite blurring past his wingtips, that this was why he flew. Not the mail runs, not the medicine drops, not the quiet competent miles he'd used to wallpaper over the years. This. The whole animal attention of it. The moment when there was nothing in the world but the machine and the air and the next three seconds.

And flying for himself alone had never once felt like this. Flying for her — that was different. That was the difference between swimming and being pulled out of the water.

The shelf came at his left wing and he lifted it, just enough, feeling the wind shear shove the tail like a rude hand. The canyon kinked right around a fallen spire and he banked steep, the old girl groaning, and for one heartbeat the wall filled the entire windshield and Mara made a small sound that was absolutely not a scream, and then they were through, and the canyon opened like a held breath released.

"Still with me?" he called.

"Ask me when we land." But her voice had color in it now. "There's a fork ahead. Left goes down to the flats — open ground, no cover. Right climbs toward the pass."

"Right."

"Hazel." Her hand left the seat and landed on his forearm, brief and warm. "Right."

* * *

They came out of the narrows climbing, hugging the western wall, and Hazel risked one look back down the canyon.

The lead plane was at the mouth.

It hung there against the bright morning like a wasp at a window — low-winged, twin-engined, painted the dull gray of government paperwork, with the kind of clean aggressive lines that said *built for speed, armed for argument*. Even at this distance he could see the shape of the gun mount where a survey aircraft would have carried cameras.

"He's not coming in," Mara said.

"No."

"Why not? You said he wouldn't follow."

"He won't follow *me*." Hazel's mouth had gone dry. "But he doesn't have to. Look where he's pointed."

The gray plane wasn't entering the canyon. It was climbing along the outside of the eastern wall, gaining altitude in wide impatient circles, and its nose kept swinging west — toward the pass. Toward the high country where the canyon ended and the cover ended and the second tide lay waiting under a skin of meltwater.

"He's going over the top," Mara said. "He's going to be waiting for us at the far end."

"Or he's going to radio ahead and let somebody else do the waiting." Hazel fed in throttle and the engine answered with everything it had, which today was nearly enough. "Either way, we've bought ourselves maybe ten minutes of being alive and unobserved. Let's spend them well."

They climbed out of the valley into the high country, and the world changed the way it always did up here — trees giving way to scree, scree to snow, snow to the blue-white emptiness where the mountains stopped pretending to be scenery and became the whole of existence. The pass opened ahead of them, the second tide, and even from five miles out Hazel could see what the warmth was doing to it. The snowfields wept. Meltwater braided across the old ice in silver threads, and along the southern edge a lake had formed overnight where no lake belonged, lying in the pass like spilled mercury.

"We can't land on the south side anymore," Mara said quietly. "Look at it. By tomorrow that whole shelf goes."

"Then we don't land on the south side. We cross high and put down on the moraine past the shoulder." He glanced at her. "You said the survey route crossed at the headwall."

"I said we *tried* to cross at the headwall. There's a difference between a route and a success." She was leaning forward now, scanning the pass, and her voice dropped into the register she used for geology — flat, precise, certain. "Stay north of the medial moraine. The ice under it is fifty feet thick. South of it, it's twenty, and it's been rotting since April."

"Fifty feet of ice. Comforting."

"It's not there to comfort you. It's there to hold you up. Try to be grateful."

The gray plane was a speck now, far ahead and high, dropping over the far shoulder of the pass like a stone into a well. Going to ground somewhere beyond. Waiting.

Hazel checked his fuel. Checked it again, as though the needle might have repented. Enough to cross. Enough to reach the third campsite on Mara's route. Nothing spare. The ledger balanced the way tightropes balance.

"Mara."

"Yes."

"When we're through the pass — if they're on the ground waiting, I can't fight them. I need you to know that. I have a rifle, a flare gun, and a strong opinion. That's the arsenal."

She turned and looked at him, and there was frostbite scarring still healing on her cheekbone, and her eyes were the clear hard gray of glacier ice, and she smiled — actually smiled, tired and crooked and entirely without comfort.

"Hazel. I spent six weeks hiding in a hole in a glacier eating melted snow and my own pride. Whatever's waiting on the other side of that pass, I promise you it has never met anyone as motivated as I am."

He laughed out loud, alone in the sky at nine thousand feet with a wolf plane somewhere ahead and a melting mountain underneath, and the laugh felt like fuel.

"All right," he said. "Headwall crossing. North of the moraine. Hold on to something you love."

"What if I'm already holding it?"

He didn't answer. He just flew — into the pass, into the second tide, into the white roaring silence where the summit stood watching with its old cold patience, and the meltwater sang everywhere beneath them, thinning, weakening, counting down.

The far shoulder rose to meet them. And on the gravel fan below it, small and neat and unmistakable, sat the gray plane — parked, empty, its crew already gone to ground among the rocks.

Waiting for them to land.
