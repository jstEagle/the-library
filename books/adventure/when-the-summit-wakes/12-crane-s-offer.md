## 12. Crane's Offer

The canyon had a voice, and for two hours it had been telling Hazel everything he needed to know.

It spoke in echoes off the granite walls, in the way the wind funneled through the narrows and flattened out again where the river widened, in the small tremors of the stick that meant the air was thinking about turning ugly. He'd flown this stretch of country maybe forty times over nine years, hauling mail and medicine and once — memorably — a crate of live chickens that had gotten loose somewhere over the divide and turned his cabin into a feathered riot. He knew the canyon the way you know a friend's breathing. Right now it was breathing easy, and that was worth more than any instrument on his panel.

Mara sat in the right-hand seat with the survey's route map spread across her knees, one frost-scarred hand pinning it flat against the vibration of the floorboards. She'd been quiet since dawn. Not the bad kind of quiet — not the silence of someone deciding whether to lie to you — but the working kind, the silence of a geologist running numbers behind her eyes.

"You're doing the thing," Hazel said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at the fuel gauge like you can guilt it into reading higher."

"It's at a quarter," she said. "A quarter is not a number. A quarter is a mood."

"A quarter's an hour of flying and change," he said. "And there's a strip at Kettle Bend, if the melt hasn't taken the south end of it. We fuel there, we're through the pass before the third tide closes it."

"And if the melt *has* taken the south end?"

"Then we land on the north end and walk south." He grinned at her sideways. "I've done worse walks."

"I believe you," Mara said. "That's what worries me about you generally."

The radio crackled then — static first, the long dry hiss of the high country, and then a voice that made Hazel's hands go still on the yoke.

"Hazel Oyelaran. This is Crane. Come in, Oyelaran."

Hazel didn't answer. His thumb hovered over the transmit switch and then moved away from it, deliberately, the way you move your hand away from a stove you've already learned about once.

"He knows our frequency," Mara said quietly.

"He's known our frequency since the fuel cache," Hazel said. "Maybe since before that. Maybe he's been listening since the day I reported that distress call and he decided I was either useful or inconvenient." He glanced at her. "Question is which."

The voice came again, patient as weather. "I can hear your engine, Oyelaran. I don't need you to talk. But I'd rather you did. What I have to say goes better with conversation."

Hazel looked at the sky ahead — clean blue over the divide, the pass still holding its snow, six tides of meltwater roaring somewhere below the ice — and he looked at Mara, who was watching him with an expression he was starting to know by heart: level, unflinching, already braced for whatever came next because she'd been bracing since the ice cave.

He keyed the mic. "I'm listening. That's all I'm promising."

"That's a start." Crane's voice was smooth as poured concrete. "You're heading for Kettle Bend. Don't bother denying it; there are three ways into this range and you've chosen the only one with fuel left in it. I want you to know I respect that. You fly well. You've flown better than my people expected, and they don't expect much from bush pilots."

"Flattery. From a government man. My mother warned me about exactly this."

"Here's the offer." No pause, no pretense of warmth now — Crane simply laid it out, the way a man lays cards on a table when he's already counted the deck. "You put down at Kettle Bend. You leave the woman and the survey data with my people when they arrive — they'll be there within the hour, so there's no drama required. And you walk away. Clean record, license intact, no inquiry, no questions about why a mail pilot spent six tides flying government business without authorization. Plus a sum I think you'll find changes your arithmetic considerably. Fifty thousand. Cash, wired anywhere you name."

Hazel flew in silence for a long moment. The canyon walls slid past, orange rock striped with old water stains, and somewhere below the river ran brown and fast with the melt.

"Fifty thousand," he said finally. "That's a lot of money for a man whose story is 'recovering equipment.'"

"The equipment is valuable."

"So valuable you offered me fifty grand to stop looking for survivors of a missing survey team." He shook his head, though Crane couldn't see it. "You know what's funny, Crane? Back when you conscripted me, I almost believed you. The worried-official act. The families waiting for word. I've got a soft spot for debts owed to the dead — always have. You found it and you used it."

"Grief is a lever," Crane said, without shame. "Everyone has one. Mine happens to be efficiency."

"And hers?" Hazel said. "Voss? What's her lever?"

For the first time, Crane hesitated. It was a small hesitation — half a second of dead air — but Hazel had spent nine years listening to radios, and he knew what a man sounded like when a question landed somewhere he hadn't armored.

"Dr. Voss signed a contract," Crane said. "She knew what the survey was. Whatever she told you in whatever cave she's been hiding in, understand that she chose this. She's not your rescue, Oyelaran. She's your cargo. And cargo that fights its manifest ends up costing everyone."

Hazel felt something go cold and steady in his chest — not anger, exactly. Anger burns hot and spends itself. This was the other thing, the thing that comes after, when a line gets drawn somewhere inside you and you feel it set like concrete.

"There it is," he said. "There's the real Crane. I was wondering how long the government voice would hold."

"Oyelaran—"

"Let me tell you how I see it." Hazel kept his voice level, conversational, the way he talked to nervous passengers. "You sent men into the interior to take a survey team off a glacier, and the team didn't come back. Five people. Somebody's brother, somebody's daughter. You've spent every tide since trying to make sure nobody finds out what happened to them, and now you're offering me fifty thousand dollars to help you finish the job. So here's my counteroffer."

"I'm listening."

"Nothing. You get nothing. Not her, not the data, not my cooperation, not even my good manners. You get me flying this plane to Kettle Bend with her in the seat beside me, and after that you get me being a problem for you for as long as it takes for somebody with a badge and a conscience to start asking your employers the questions you won't answer."

Another pause. Longer this time. When Crane spoke again, the smoothness was gone, and what was underneath was worse — not rage but arithmetic, cold and patient, a man recalculating.

"You should understand what you're choosing, Oyelaran. I don't need you alive for this anymore. That window closed when you refused the cache job. The only reason you're still breathing is that dead pilots draw inquiries and live ones draw nothing. But that cuts both ways. Two more names on a mountain that's already eaten five — who investigates that? Who even looks? The interior takes people every spring. It took your brother, didn't it?"

The cabin went very quiet. Mara's head came up. Outside, the canyon went on speaking its long slow language, indifferent.

"My brother," Hazel said carefully, "died because the weather lied to him and the mountains are honest about being cruel. That's nature. What you're describing is just cruelty wearing nature's coat. And here's the difference, Crane, and you can write it down: the mountain never pretends to be anything but what it is. You do. Every time you open your mouth."

"You're romanticizing a fuel gauge and a compass."

"I'm drawing a line," Hazel said. "You crossed it when you started pricing people. Fifty thousand for her. What's the going rate for me — or do I come free with the plane?"

Crane's laugh was short and entirely without humor. "You come expensive. Everyone does. That's the lesson of this whole country, pilot — everything costs, and the ones who pretend otherwise die surprised." The radio hissed, and when the voice returned it had gone flat, final, a door closing. "Kettle Bend, one hour. My people will be on the strip. Put down, hand her over, take your money, keep your life. Or keep flying, and I'll bury you both under a story so clean that in ten years nobody will remember either of your names. Your choice. You have until the strip."

The channel clicked dead.

* * *

For a while neither of them said anything. The engine filled the cabin with its steady roar, and the canyon gave way to open country — grey scree slopes, a frozen lake holding the sky like a mirror, the white shoulder of the divide rising ahead with the pass cut into it like a scar.

Mara reached over and touched his arm. Just briefly. Her fingers were cold even through his jacket sleeve.

"He mentioned Tom," she said.

"He fishes," Hazel said. "Men like Crane fish. They cast out a hook and wait to see what bites." He rolled his shoulders, working the stiffness out. "It bit a little. I'll admit it bit a little."

"Hazel."

"He's wrong, is the thing." Hazel's voice came out rougher than he intended. "Tom wasn't taken by the interior. Tom was taken by weather and a bad decision and a plane that was older than both of us. There was nothing to investigate, nothing buried, nobody covering it up. It was just loss. Clean, stupid loss." He shook his head. "What Crane's selling is different. He wants me to believe the world works the way he says it does — everything priced, everyone bought — because if I believe that, then taking the fifty thousand isn't betrayal. It's just sense."

"And you don't believe it?"

Hazel was quiet for a moment, watching the divide grow. "Nine years ago I would've taken the money," he said. "Not because I'm crooked. Because I was tired, and money buys distance, and distance was the whole religion I practiced. Miles, not years. Keep moving, keep nothing, owe nobody." He glanced at her. "Then some fool government survey goes missing, and I report it like a decent citizen, and instead of a thank-you I get conscripted into the ugliest errand of my life. And somewhere between the fuel cache and the ice cave, I stopped flying away from things."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at the map on her knees, and her mouth did something complicated that might have been a smile fighting a storm front.

"For the record," she said, "you could have taken the money. Nobody would have blamed you. I wouldn't have blamed you. I signed on with these people, remember. I knew what the survey was before you ever heard the word 'survey.' If anyone on this plane has earned a clean exit, it's you, and I'm the reason you don't get one."

"Well," Hazel said, "that's the trouble with cargo that argues."

"I'm serious."

"I know you're serious. You're always serious. It's one of your best features and one of the most exhausting." He reached over and tapped the fuel gauge, quarter full, stubbornly itself. "Here's what I know, Voss. Fifty thousand dollars buys a lot of miles. It doesn't buy back five people off a glacier, and it doesn't buy one minute of looking at myself in a mirror over a morning coffee. I tried the other arithmetic for thirty-eight years. It balanced on paper and it never balanced anywhere else." He shrugged, awkward, the words coming harder than any landing he'd ever made. "So the line's drawn. You're on this side of it. That's just where things stand."

Mara didn't say anything. But she folded the map with great care, and she didn't move her hand from near his arm, and when the divide finally rose up to meet them — white and vast and glittering, the pass a dark thread stitched across its shoulder — she was the one who spotted the smoke first.

"There," she said. "Ten o'clock. Coming up the valley toward Kettle Bend."

Hazel saw it: a thin dark thread against the snowfields, too straight and too steady for a campfire. Engine exhaust. More than one engine.

"They're early," Mara said.

"No," Hazel said, and pushed the nose down toward the strip, feeling the old familiar calm settle over him like a coat — the calm that arrived precisely when things went wrong, the gift his brother had never lived long enough to use. "They're on schedule. We're the ones who changed the plan."

He scanned the strip below as it grew — gravel and old snow, the ghost town's rusted headframe standing sentinel at its north end, the south end soft and dark where the melt had begun its work. Three vehicles at the tree line. Men beside them. Small at this distance, patient as stones.

And parked square in the middle of the strip, wings gleaming, sat a plane he recognized from the salvage convoy — big, new, armed, planted there like a cork in a bottle.

"They've blocked the runway," Mara said.

"Yeah." Hazel's eyes were already moving, measuring, the pilot's arithmetic running faster than fear: wind out of the northwest, strip length, the soft ground at the south end, the trees, the headframe, the gap between the wrecked hangar and the water tower. "They've blocked the runway."

"Tell me you have a plan."

Hazel grinned, and it wasn't entirely a sane grin, and he was honest enough with himself to know it.

"I have a canyon," he said, "and twenty minutes of fuel, and a co-pilot who knows this range better than the men trying to kill us. Buckle up, Dr. Voss. We're not landing."
