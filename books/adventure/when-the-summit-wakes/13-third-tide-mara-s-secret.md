## 13. Third Tide: Mara's Secret

The third tide came in on a wind that smelled of iron and old water.

Mara felt it before she saw it — a change in the pressure behind her ears, the way the glacier's meltwater had begun to speak in a lower, more urgent voice beneath the ice shelf where they'd made camp. Six tides, Crane had said, though he'd meant it as arithmetic. The mountain kept its own calendar, and it was running fast this year.

She sat at the mouth of the overhang with the survey's route map spread across her knees, weighting the corners with stones, and watched Hazel work on the plane by the light of a lantern hung from the wing strut. He'd been under there an hour, coaxing at something near the oil line, swearing with great creativity and no real anger — the way he swore at weather, at engines, at the general stubbornness of the world. It was, she had come to realize, how he talked to things he intended to keep.

*Things he intended to keep.* She turned the phrase over and it cut her, gently, like a paper edge.

Because there was a thing she hadn't told him. A thing she'd been carrying since the cave, since the firelight and the frostbite and his hands wrapping hers around the tin cup, and every day she didn't say it, it got heavier — not because it changed, but because he did. Because every mile they flew together, every bad joke about fuel gauges, every time he checked her bandages without being asked, he was building something out of the version of her that was true only halfway.

The lantern light shifted. Hazel backed out from under the fuselage, wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last week, and looked over at her.

"You're doing the face," he said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're reading a map but you're actually reading your own funeral." He came over, ducking under the wing, and crouched beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him — engine warmth and human warmth, all of it borrowed from somewhere. "Third tide's rising. We've got maybe ten hours of good light tomorrow, then the pass starts weeping again. If you've got something to say, Voss, now's the cheap hour to say it."

He knew. Of course he knew. He was a man who read weather off the color of clouds and trouble off the length of a radio silence; he'd probably known for days and been waiting, patient as bedrock, for her to find her way down to it.

Mara folded the map. Unfolded it. Folded it again, which accomplished nothing except giving her hands a job.

"When I signed onto the survey," she said, "I knew what it was."

Hazel didn't move. Didn't nod, didn't frown. Just stayed there, crouched, listening the way he listened to engines — completely, without interrupting.

"Not the whole of it," she went on. "I want to be fair to myself, even if I don't deserve it. I didn't know about Crane. I didn't know men would die. But I knew the money was wrong." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's the part nobody ever believes. Not that I missed the signs. That I *read* them."

* * *

It had started, as these things always did, with a letter on expensive paper.

Her career had been dead two years by then. Disgraced was the polite word — drummed out was the honest one. She'd published a paper arguing that the government's seismic surveys of the northern range were systematically understating the mineral potential of the deep glacial formations, and worse, that the omissions followed a pattern too consistent to be incompetence. She'd named names. She'd been right, which was the unforgivable part, and she'd been young, which was the fatal one. Three review boards, one lawsuit, and a settlement with a gag clause later, she was teaching introductory geology to freshmen who called her "the crazy ice lady" behind her back, and eating rice in a basement apartment where the ceiling stain had developed what she privately considered a personality.

Then the letter. No letterhead. A private post box. An offer: join a resource assessment team, six months' contract, a figure attached that would have paid her rent for eleven years.

"I ran the numbers," she told Hazel. "Not the geology numbers — I mean the *money*. A legitimate survey pays per diem plus hazard. This offer was triple the top of any legitimate scale, and the contract had clauses in it I still can't talk about without wanting a drink. Equipment insurance against seizure. Data custody provisions. A confidentiality schedule longer than my dissertation." She looked at him. "You know what a data custody clause is for? It's for when the data isn't yours anymore. Legitimate science doesn't need it. Nobody steals public knowledge."

"And you signed anyway."

"I signed anyway." She said it flatly, because dressing it up would have been worse than the crime itself. "I told myself a story. It was a good story. I'd like you to hear it, because I told it to myself so many times I almost believed it, and I think you should know exactly what kind of liar you're flying with."

Hazel said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere under the ice, the meltwater rose half a tone.

"The story was this," Mara said. "The mountain doesn't care. That was the whole moral architecture of it — the load-bearing wall. Minerals aren't alive. A lode of ore under three hundred meters of glacier has been sitting there for forty million years, indifferent. It isn't a grave. It isn't a forest. It isn't anything that suffers when you take it. So when I told myself *the mountain won't miss it*, I could almost make it true. Almost. The way you can almost make a bad landing good if you commit hard enough."

"Almost," Hazel said quietly, "is the most expensive word in aviation."

"Is in geology too." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "Here's what I actually knew, itemized, so you can weigh it properly. One: the client wasn't government, whatever the paperwork said. Two: the survey design was built to locate, not to study — drill targets, extraction access, tonnage estimates. You don't do science with a survey like that. You do inventory. Three: the team roster. Half those people weren't scientists. They were logistics men, security contractors with geology degrees bought mail-order, and one communications specialist whose job, I realized much too late, was making sure our transmissions home said exactly what the client wanted them to say."

She stopped. The next part was the part she'd been circling for days, the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"And four," she said. "I noticed Okafor was scared."

* * *

Emeka Okafor had been the team's actual scientist — a glaciologist, soft-spoken, brilliant, the kind of man who apologized to equipment. He'd been the one who found the first anomaly, the conductivity reading that made him go quiet for a full day and then start checking his instruments with the wariness of a man who suspected his own eyes.

"He came to me on the fourth night," Mara said. "Out on the ice, away from camp. He showed me the readings. Hazel — the lode under that glacier isn't a deposit. It's a *province*. What we were standing on was worth more than the annual budget of most countries, and it wasn't just one class of mineral. It was a suite. Rare earths, platinum group, things I'm not going to name because naming them is how this whole mess started." Her voice cracked and she let it. "And Emeka looked at me and said, 'Mara, why does a private syndicate already have extraction schedules for a site that officially doesn't exist?'"

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd ask questions." She closed her eyes. "And then I didn't. That's the sin, Hazel. Not the signature — signatures are cheap, everyone signs things they shouldn't. The sin is that Emeka handed me the truth and asked for help, and I calculated. I stood there on the ice and I *calculated*, the way I'd been calculating since the letter. If I pushed, I lost the contract, the money, the chance to buy back my name. If I stayed quiet, I got paid and told myself the mountain wouldn't miss it. Emeka was asking me to be brave and I gave him a cost-benefit analysis instead."

The lantern guttered in a gust off the ice. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"Three days later the comms man filed a false position report, and the day after that, the 'resupply flight' arrived early with men on it instead of food, and Emeka—" Her breath caught. She rode through it. "Emeka tried to raise an emergency frequency on a handheld unit he'd hidden in his instrument case. They caught him doing it. And I ran. I took the duplicate field notes I'd been keeping — not out of conscience, at first, just professional habit, cover-your-own-tail habit — and I ran into the whiteout like a coward, and I lived, and he didn't, and four other people didn't, and I have spent every hour since the cave trying to work out whether surviving counts for anything if the reason you survived is that you were already halfway to being one of them."

* * *

The silence after that lasted long enough for the lantern to burn low and for Hazel to get up, wordlessly, and adjust the wick. That small domestic act — him tending the light while she sat in the wreckage of her own confession — undid her more than any reaction could have.

When he came back, he didn't crouch this time. He sat down properly beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and looked out at the dark shape of the glacier, and was quiet for a while.

"Can I tell you something unflattering about myself?" he said finally.

"If it makes you feel better."

"It doesn't. That's what makes it unflattering." He rubbed the back of his neck. "My brother Theo died because I did a calculation. Different math than yours, same discipline. He wanted to fly the mail run in weather I'd have waited out. I let him take the plane because I'd run the numbers — his hours, the front's speed, the margin — and the numbers said he'd make it. The numbers were right, technically. He made it to within nine miles of the strip." Hazel's jaw worked. "For years afterward I told myself a story too. Mine was: *the sky doesn't care either*. Same load-bearing wall as yours. Weather isn't evil, mountains aren't cruel, the numbers are just numbers. It's a comforting philosophy because it means nothing is anybody's fault. You can live inside it for decades. I did."

"But?"

"But here's what I learned, and it cost me everything I had to learn it: the story isn't wrong, exactly. The sky really doesn't care. The mountain really won't miss its ore. That's all true." He turned and looked at her, and in the lantern light his eyes were steady and very tired and very kind. "What's a lie is the conclusion people build on top of it. Because *the mountain doesn't care* doesn't mean *you* don't have to. That's the trick. That's the whole con, and Crane's syndicate runs it on everybody — pilots, geologists, governments. They hand you a truth that lets you stop carrying your own weight, and then they charge you for the ride."

Mara found she was crying, quietly, without drama — just leaking, the way the glacier leaked, pressure finding its level. "I keep waiting for you to be angry."

"Oh, I'm angry." He said it easily, honestly. "I'm furious. Mostly at Crane, some at myself, a respectable amount at the entire concept of private resource syndicates. But at you?" He shook his head slowly. "Voss, you signed a bad contract and then you kept duplicate notes and hid a handheld radio's worth of conscience in your kit and ran into a whiteout rather than become fully one of them. That's not innocence. I'm not going to insult you by pretending it is. But it's not guilt either. It's the space in between, and the space in between is where everybody I've ever respected actually lives."

"That's generous."

"That's accurate. Generous would be pretending you didn't calculate. Accurate is knowing you did, and knowing you've hated yourself for it ever since, and knowing the difference between you and the people hunting us is that their calculations never once included a human being as anything but a line item." He nudged her knee with his. "You put Emeka in your ledger every night. I've watched you do it. People like Crane sleep fine."

"How would you know how Crane sleeps?"

"Nobody who sleeps well wears cologne at eight thousand feet."

Despite everything — despite the stone in her chest and the tide rising under the ice — Mara laughed. It came out wet and broken and real, and Hazel grinned at her, briefly, like the sun through a gap in cloud, and then the gravity settled back over both of them.

"There's more," she said. "The part that matters for tomorrow."

"The duplicate notes."

"You knew?"

"I hoped. You've been guarding that satchel like it holds the last eggs in Christendom." He nodded toward her pack. "How complete?"

"Every reading. Every position report, including the falsified ones, with dates and the original transmissions logged against them. Drill core descriptions. The lode's extent, roughly mapped. Enough to hang Crane and enough to prove the survey team found what they found and were silenced for it." She hesitated. "And enough to make the lode findable again. By anyone. That's the problem I haven't solved. The evidence that convicts Crane is the same evidence that draws the next syndicate, and the one after that. As long as that mountain holds what it holds, there will always be another letter on expensive paper."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the pilot's voice, the one he used when the weather ahead was worse than the weather behind.

"Keep that thought," he said. "Don't finish it yet. Just — hold it."

"Why?"

"Because I've been looking at the fuel drums and the spare charges in the back of the plane, and doing my own calculating." He stood, offered her his hand, and pulled her up out of the cold. "Come on. Tide's turning, and I think we need to talk about what we're carrying out of here — and what we leave behind."

Far below the overhang, deep in the dark under the glacier, the meltwater rose another tone, like an instrument tuning itself for something enormous.
