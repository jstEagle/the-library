## 14. The Wreck

The glacier face came up on us like a wall that had been waiting all morning to be rude about it.

I'd been flying the last hour with my jaw set and my hands doing their work without much help from me, which was how I preferred it — hands first, feelings later, preferably never. Mara sat in the right seat with the survey's route map spread across her knees, one frost-scarred finger tracing the line they'd flown that final winter.

"There." She pointed through the windscreen. "The shelf below the seracs. That's where they made the last camp. I can see —" She stopped. Her finger stayed where it was, steady as a surveyor's instrument, but her voice went thin. "Hazel. The plane."

I saw it before she finished saying it. A pale smear against the blue-white of the ice shelf, half-drifted over, wings at angles wings don't come to honestly. Even from two miles out and climbing air, something about it looked wrong. Wrecks have a shape. This one had a *posture*.

"That's not a crash," I said.

"No," Mara said. "That's an execution."

I brought us around in a long, wide circle, the way you do when part of you is looking for a reason not to land. No movement on the ice. No smoke. No second set of tracks leading anywhere hopeful. Just the wreck sitting in its drift like a thing the mountain had been asked to keep.

"You're sure you want to see this up close?" I asked.

"I've been wanting to see it for four months," Mara said. "From inside a cave. With frostbite. So yes, Hazel. Land the plane."

There wasn't much argument left in me after that. There rarely is, when she uses that voice. I've heard her use it on glaciers, on government men, and once — memorably — on a stove that refused to light in the ghost town, and the stove gave in first.

* * *

The snow was knee-deep on the shelf and lying in that treacherous way snow does over glacier ice, soft on top, honest nowhere beneath. We roped together — her idea, her rope, her knots, because apparently geologists learn knots the way pilots learn checklists, and hers were better than mine, which I elected not to mention aloud since she'd only gotten smug about it.

We were fifty yards from the wreck when we found the first thing that wasn't ice.

A boot. Just the boot, upright, planted sole-down in the drift as if its owner had stepped out of it and walked on. Mara stopped so suddenly the rope went taut between us.

"Hazel."

"I see it."

"That's Okafor's. He had them custom-made in the capital, he complained about the price for six weeks, he —" She pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice had been sanded flat. "Somebody took his boots off him."

People say things like that when the truth is too large to hold whole, so they hold a piece of it instead. Boots instead of bodies. I understood. I was doing the same arithmetic myself, counting what I could carry.

We found the rest of it scattered along a hundred yards of shelf, and none of it made sense until all of it did. A parka, empty, folded — *folded*, not torn, not frozen into a sprawl. A mess tin, clean. A radio handset lying thirty feet from any radio. And then, near the wreck itself, a scatter of brass casings, dull with age, in a pattern I knew the way I know weather.

"Not a firefight," I said. I crouched by the casings without touching them. "They stood here. In a line, or close to it. Somebody else did the shooting."

Mara said nothing. She had gone very still, the stillness of a woman doing sums she doesn't want the total of. Then she walked past me toward the plane, and I let the rope run through my gloves and followed.

* * *

Up close, the wreck told its story plainly, the way honest wreckage does, and this wreckage was not honest.

The Twin Otter — the survey's bird, the one they'd gone missing in — sat on its belly with both props bent back, and every mark on it was wrong for a forced landing. A pilot sets a plane down dead-stick nose-low, wings level, gear up if he's lucky, and the damage runs front to back. This damage ran everywhere. The tail had been broken deliberately, axed or pried, snapped like a wishbone. Both fuel tanks had been opened with tools — clean cuts, squared edges, not tears. And the engine cowls had been removed and set neatly beside the fuselage, side by side, the way a careful mechanic lays out his work.

"Somebody landed it," Mara said quietly. She'd climbed onto the wing root and was looking down into the cabin through the shattered door. "Or somebody flew it here and then destroyed it."

"And then somebody shot the crew," I said, "and walked away with everything worth carrying, and left the boots."

She came down off the wing slowly. Her face in the flat glacier light had gone the color of the ice itself.

"The instruments are gone," she said. "The core samples. The field notes. Everything from the lode — Crane has it all already, doesn't he? He sent us racing across the pass for data he's been holding since the day my team disappeared."

"Maybe." I said it carefully, because there was a splinter in my mind working its way deeper, and I wanted it out where I could look at it. "Or maybe he doesn't. Maybe some of it's still missing, and that's why he needs us. Why he needed *you*."

"He thinks I have duplicate notes." Mara laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all week, and I'd heard her describe frostbite in clinical detail over dinner. "He thinks — Hazel, if he thought that from the beginning, then this whole rescue, this race, the six tides, all of it —"

"Was never a rescue," I said.

The words came out of me level and cold, and once they were out I couldn't take them back in, and I didn't want to. They just kept going.

"It was a search. Not for survivors. For paper. For anything she might have hidden." I looked at Mara, and the splinter came all the way out, and what was under it was fury, white and clean and better than grief, because grief just sits there and fury at least points somewhere. "Crane sent me out here because I'm the only pilot fool enough to fly this pass in melt season, and because if you were alive, I was the one who'd find you. He used my rules against me. Leave people unaccounted for — I told him I couldn't do it. I *told* him. And he smiled and said thank God for men like me."

Mara put her hand on my arm. Through two layers of wool I felt it anyway.

"The others," she said. "Okafor. Lindqvist. The Torres twins. Petit. They weren't lost, Hazel. They were never lost. They were —"

She couldn't finish it. I finished it for her, inside my own head, where nobody had to hear it: *they were tidied up.* Five people who spent their lives reading ice, erased from it in an afternoon, and the mountain asked no questions because mountains never do. That job falls to people.

"We should mark them," I said. "However many there are. However much the snow's taken."

"There'll be nothing to find. Four months of accumulation, and Crane's men would have —" She stopped herself. Nodded once, hard, like a woman setting her own bone. "Yes. We mark it anyway."

So we did. It took most of an hour, walking the shelf in widening arcs, and what we found we won't speak of easily even now, and some of it I will not write down at all. We built a cairn from shelf ice and the stones the glacier had coughed up, and Mara produced a pencil stub from her kit — geologists, I've learned, are never more than arm's reach from a pencil stub — and wrote on a strip of chart paper, in handwriting that shook only at the very end:

*Here lie the survey party of the Meridian Ice Expedition. They read the mountain honestly. Their country did not read them at all.*

Then she weighted it with a stone, and we stood with our heads bare in a wind that had no business being that cold in spring, and I said the only kind of words I know for the dead, which are short ones, and Mara said longer ones, in three languages, and the mountain took all of them and gave nothing back, which is the arrangement.

* * *

It was on the walk back to my plane that we found the thing that changed the shape of everything.

It was tucked under the wreck's port wing root, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged where only someone who knew aircraft would think to look — or someone who'd been shown by someone who knew aircraft. Inside the oilcloth: a canvas document case, water-stained but sound, and inside that, a notebook with a rubber band around it and a single sheet of paper folded on top.

The paper was addressed in block capitals, the writing of a man expecting not to survive his penmanship:

**TO WHOEVER FINDS THIS — IF IT IS THE PILOT OYELARAN, TELL MY SISTER IN KOWLOON I KEPT MY PROMISES. IF IT IS ANYONE ELSE, WE FOUND THE VEIN AND THEY FOUND US AND THE ORDER CAME FROM COASTAL COMMAND RELAY, CALLSIGN MERIDIAN ACTUAL. CHECK THE FUEL LEDGER. HE COUNTED OUR GALLONS BEFORE HE COUNTED OUR GRAVES.**

Mara read it twice. Then she sat down right there in the snow, which frightened me more than the casings had, more than the boot, more than all of it, because Mara Voss does not sit down in snow; Mara Voss stands up in snow and tells the snow to move.

"Meridian Actual," she whispered. "That's — Hazel, that's Crane's relay callsign. It was Crane's relay the whole time. He was running the resupply. He counted our fuel. He knew exactly how far we could go and exactly when we'd be stranded, because he *planned the stranding*."

"And the fuel ledger," I said slowly, and my own voice sounded far away to me. "The cache at Mile Forty-One. The numbers that didn't work. The diversion that cost us a tide."

We looked at each other, and the same conclusion landed on us both at the same instant, the way bad news does — not gradually, but all at once, like a load shifting in the cargo bay.

"He's been thinning our margins since the day we took off," Mara said. "Every cache, every calculation. He doesn't need to shoot us down. He's arranged for the pass to do it. Six tides, Hazel — he told us six tides because he knows to the gallon how many we'll have left on tide five."

I stood there with a dead man's notebook in my mitts and the wind coming down off the summit like a debt collector, and I thought about my plane sitting a quarter mile away — my father's engine hours in her logbooks, my brother's name scratched inside the cockpit door where nobody was ever supposed to see it — and I thought about Crane's smooth voice on the radio saying *bring me the data and I'll bring you home rich.*

"He was never going to let us land," I said. "Not anywhere he'd have to answer questions."

"No," Mara agreed. She got up out of the snow, brushing it off with brisk, furious little motions, and when she looked up at me her eyes had gone bright and hard as the ice we stood on. "So here's what we do. We take his six tides and we spend them better than he budgeted. We get off this glacier, we get to a transmitter, and we put that ledger and that notebook in front of every family, every newspaper, every inquiry board on the coast. He counted gallons." She held up the notebook. "Petit counted graves. Let's see whose arithmetic holds."

And that was when we heard it — faint, from the south, threading down the valley under the wind.

Engines. More than one. Flying low, and flying fast, and coming up the pass with the melt at their backs.

I checked my watch, though I already knew what it would say. Fourth tide, and the ice giving early.

"They're a tide ahead of schedule," I said.

Mara was already moving toward the plane. "Then so are we."
