## 15. Fourth Tide: The Ice Gives

The mountain woke them at four in the morning by screaming.

Not wind — Hazel had heard every kind of wind the interior could produce, and he'd catalogued them all the way a sailor names squalls. This was deeper. A groan that came up through the gravel of their camp and into his spine, followed by a crack like a rifle shot fired somewhere under the world.

He was out of the tent before Mara finished sitting up, boots half-laced, breath smoking in the gray pre-dawn light. Across the valley floor, at the face of the glacier where the survey's final camp had been — where the wrecked plane still lay like a picked-over carcass — the ice was moving.

Not flowing. Ice always flowed; that was the polite fiction geologists told you over coffee. This was *giving*. A seam had opened along the glacier's lower lip, a black mouth in the blue-white, and meltwater was pouring out of it in a brown rope thick as a man's leg, steaming faintly where it hit the cold air.

"That's it," Mara said beside him. She'd come up silent, parka over her shoulders, binoculars already at her eyes. Her voice had the flat calm she saved for things that frightened her most. "That's the entrance. The melt's eaten through the ice lens that sealed it."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I spent eleven days inside it." She lowered the glasses. "I know its teeth."

The seam widened as they watched — slowly, the way a door opens when nobody's in a hurry behind it. Water sheeted down the glacier face in a dozen new silver threads. Somewhere above, another deep crack rolled across the valley like distant artillery.

Hazel felt something settle in his chest, heavy and familiar. It was the same feeling he got reading a fuel gauge that didn't match the flight plan. The arithmetic had changed overnight, and the new numbers were worse.

"We're not getting out ahead of this," he said.

"No," Mara agreed. "We're not."

* * *

They'd made camp two miles below the glacier face, tucked into a fold of moraine that hid the tents from the air — or so they'd hoped. By full light, Hazel had the camp struck and everything essential crammed into the Otter: fuel drums lashed, the survey data case wedged between the seats where a passenger would sit, Mara's duplicate field notes sewn into the lining of her jacket because she trusted no container more than her own body.

"Options," he said, standing at the tail of the plane with the map spread on the fuselage. Wind kept trying to take the corners, and Mara weighed them down with rocks without being asked, which was one of about four hundred things he'd learned to love about her in nine days. "One: we fly now, low, up the river valley, and hope the fleet's lead plane is still nursing fuel at their forward strip."

"They're not. I watched their pattern yesterday. They stage at first light."

"Two: we stay hidden until dark and fly out at midnight."

"By midnight the pass will be running meltwater and rockfall. You said yourself — six tides, and we're standing in tide five."

"Three." He tapped the map at the glacier face. "We go up there. Into the entrance. We see what Crane is willing to burn an entire salvage fleet to reach, and we decide what to do about it before he gets here."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. The glacier boomed again, farther off, like a whale sounding in deep water.

"You want to walk into the thing that killed my team," she said.

"I want to stop being chased by it. There's a difference." He looked up at her. "Crane's men land today or tomorrow. When they do, they'll come straight for that hole in the ice. If we're not standing between them and it, we're just loose ends they sweep up after. If we are—" He shrugged. "Then we're a problem they have to solve while we solve them right back."

"That's not a plan, Hazel. That's a bar fight with glaciers."

"I've won bar fights with less."

She almost smiled. It was thin, but it was real, and he counted it the way he counted gallons — carefully, and grateful for every one.

"All right," she said. "But we go armed, and we go now, while the melt's still doing our work for us. Every hour that hole gets bigger, and every hour Crane's pilots have worse landing options on that ice. We're not the only ones racing the water."

* * *

MARA

The entrance had been a crawl space when she'd left it in February, a slot you entered on your belly with your pack held ahead of you like an offering. Now it was a cathedral door.

She stood at the edge of it with her headlamp off, letting her eyes adjust, listening to the glacier breathe. Meltwater ran everywhere — underfoot, overhead, down channels carved overnight in walls that had been solid for ten thousand years. The air coming out of the dark was cold and mineral and strangely sweet, the smell of the lode itself, of the ore body the instruments had screamed about until the needles pinned.

Eleven days she'd spent in here. Eleven days taking core samples and logging assays and telling herself the same lie every night: *the mountain won't miss it.* She had needed the money. That was the whole of it, the plain ugly truth she'd finally given Hazel on the third tide, expecting him to turn away and finding instead that he'd only nodded, the way you nod at weather. Everyone flew through storms. What mattered was whether you told your copilot what was in the clouds.

"Hazel," she said quietly. "Stay near the wall. The floor's rotten in the middle — there's a melt channel under the ice, maybe six feet down. In February you could hear it. Now—"

Now the floor *was* the channel, mostly. They picked their way in along the left wall, boots crunching on refrozen slush, her hand trailing the ice like a blind woman's cane. Forty meters in, the passage opened into the first chamber, and she stopped.

Her headlamp found the vein.

It ran diagonally across the far wall like a lightning strike frozen mid-bolt — a hand-wide ribbon of ore glittering with the dense metallic luster that had ended four careers and, if Crane had his way, four lives. Even knowing what it was, even hating what it had cost, some animal part of her brain went *oh* — the way it did at the northern lights. Beauty and money wearing the same dress.

"There it is," Hazel said behind her. His voice had gone careful. "That little stripe is worth more than every plane in the territory."

"That little stripe is worth more than every plane in the country." She swept the lamp along the vein. "And it doesn't stop. It goes back into the ice for two kilometers, maybe three. The syndicate's own geologists put the number at—" She shook her head. "The number isn't real. Numbers that big aren't real. That's why they kill people. Once the figure passes a certain size, people stop being people. They become line items."

"Which one were you?"

"The cheap one." She meant it as a joke. It came out true, and they both heard it.

From deep in the glacier came a sound she'd been dreading since dawn — not a crack this time but a long, tearing roar, like fabric ripped by a giant, followed by the crash and tumble of ice calving somewhere in the upper chambers. Dust breathed out of the passage behind them, glittering in the lamplight.

The glacier was opening its own mine faster than any syndicate could.

"We should log what we need and get out," she said. "If the roof decides—"

Hazel's hand closed on her arm.

Outside, faint but unmistakable, carried down the valley on the morning air: engines. More than one. Deep-throated, heavily loaded, the sound of aircraft built to haul weight and not care what.

"They're early," she whispered.

"They're motivated." He clicked off his lamp, and the dark rushed in around them, close and total, broken only by the blue ghost-glow of the entrance behind. "How fast can we get to cover?"

"Define cover."

"Anything Crane's bullets bounce off."

She thought of the chamber map in her head, drawn from memory, refined over nine nights of drawing it in the dirt for Hazel. "There's a side gallery, sixty meters back, that dog-legs down. Solid ice, no sightline from the entrance. If they shoot into the main chamber, we're behind the corner."

"Then we're going to spend the day," Hazel said, "listening to men with guns stand in front of the most valuable hole in the world, and we're going to learn exactly how many of them there are."

"And then?"

In the dark, she heard him smile. She was learning to hear it — a small change in the air, a warmth arriving before the words did.

"Then we ruin their whole week."

* * *

HAZEL

They heard the planes land before they saw anything — the long whine of wheels touching ice, transmitted through the glacier itself, so the sound seemed to come from inside Hazel's own skull. Then voices, distorted by the ice into something hollow and choral, and the crunch-crunch-crunch of many boots on snow.

He counted. It was a pilot's habit, counting — souls on board, gallons remaining, minutes of daylight. He counted footsteps until the rhythm blurred, gave it up at thirty-odd, and pressed his eye to the hairline gap where the side gallery met the main chamber.

Through the entrance, washed in flat white daylight, came the advance party of the salvage fleet.

Eight men came down onto the chamber floor first, roped together like climbers, carrying coils of cable and cases marked with hazard diamonds. Behind them, at the mouth of the cave, silhouetted against the glare, stood a man Hazel knew by posture alone. Aldous Crane didn't walk so much as arrive, the way bad news arrives — unhurried, inevitable, already assuming the room belonged to him.

Even at eighty meters, through a slit in the ice, Hazel could feel the man's certainty. It radiated off him like heat off tarmac.

"Drill points here and here," Crane was saying, his voice bouncing nicely off the chamber walls, pitched for his crew but reaching Hazel's hiding place perfectly. The glacier, it turned out, was an excellent accomplice. "Core the vein at twenty-meter intervals. I want assay confirmation within the hour. And somebody get me a radio mast up outside — I want the home office hearing my voice by noon."

A younger man, clipboard under his arm, said something Hazel couldn't catch.

"Because the pilot and the doctor are still in the field," Crane said, and Hazel felt Mara go rigid against his shoulder. "And because dead men don't contest mineral claims, Mr. Ferro. Their absence *is* the claim. Every hour they stay missing, our position improves. Which is why nobody shoots at anything until I say so. A bullet hole in a witness is evidence. A missing person is a rounding error."

*Witness*, Hazel noted. Not fugitive. Not target. Witness. Somewhere in the last nine days, Crane had stopped pretending they might be alive.

Mara's breathing had gone shallow and fast beside him. He found her hand in the dark and squeezed it once — *here, still here* — and felt her fingers answer, trembling and then steady.

On the chamber floor, the drill crew was setting up with the brisk competence of men who'd done this in worse places. A generator coughed to life. Floodlights bloomed, and the chamber lit up like a stage, the vein blazing gold-white under the lamps, and eight strangers stood in the light of ten thousand years of ice and talked about rounding errors.

Hazel had flown medicine into villages where the whole year's cash economy fit in a coffee tin. He had watched old men cry over a delivered crate of antibiotics and mean it more than any boardroom ever meant anything. And he thought: *this is what power looks like up close. It looks like floodlights on something beautiful, and men with clipboards deciding who counts.*

He leaned close to Mara's ear.

"Eight in the chamber. Call it fifteen, twenty tops at the surface, plus flight crews. Two big transports on the ice — I heard both landings."

"You have a thought," she whispered. "You get a particular tone."

"I have a thought about the generator."

She turned her head. In the spill of reflected floodlight he could just see one eyebrow rise.

"It's sitting on the ice," he said, "right next to their fuel bladder. And this glacier has been looking for an excuse to collapse all morning."

Understanding moved across her face slowly, and then all at once, and it wasn't entirely approval.

"You want to set off an avalanche on top of sixteen men."

"I want to set off an avalanche on top of their *equipment*. Men run. Generators don't. No fuel, no drills, no radio mast, and Crane spends the next day explaining to his home office why the most valuable hole in the world is currently a swimming pool." He paused. "Also, yes, possibly some avalanching of men. I'm not going to pretend the option doesn't appeal."

"Hazel." Her whisper was very quiet. "If we bring that ceiling down, we might bring the whole entrance down with it. We'd be sealing ourselves in with them."

"Only if the charge is too big. So we make it small. A nudge, not a hammer. Enough vibration to drop the rotten cornice above the entrance — the one that's been hanging there since dawn begging for permission." He tapped his temple. "I've been listening to this ice for two hours, Mara. It wants to fall. All we have to do is introduce it to the generator."

For a moment she said nothing. Then, softly, in the tone of a woman filing something away to worry about later:

"When this is over, you and I are going to have a long conversation about the company I keep."

"Looking forward to it."

She slipped back into the gallery to fetch the flare pistol from their packs — their one piece of ordnance, carried for bears and regretted for nothing. Hazel stayed at the gap, watching Crane stride the chamber floor, watching the fuel bladder gleam fifty feet beyond the generator, watching the cornice of rotten ice above the entrance shed its first small threads of meltwater in bright, patient strings.

Below, Crane checked his watch and raised his voice for the benefit of the ice, the valley, and anyone hidden inside it.

"Dr. Voss!" he called, pleasant as a man calling across a dinner table. "I know you're here. The tracks were two days old and headed *in*. Come out, and we'll discuss your future like professionals. Stay, and the mountain will settle your account for us — and I promise you, the mountain keeps worse books than I do."

Hazel glanced back into the dark. Mara was already returning, flare pistol in her fist, her jaw set in the expression he'd privately started calling *the assay face* — the look she wore when the numbers were bad and she'd decided to read them anyway.

"He knows," she breathed.

"He suspects. There's a difference, and in about ninety seconds it's going to matter very much." He took the pistol from her, checked the cartridge, and settled his grip. Above the entrance, the cornice shivered, shedding another thread of water.

"Mara," he said quietly. "When I fire — whatever happens, whatever comes down — you run for the gallery floor and you don't stop until the dog-leg. Promise me."

She looked at him for a long second. Outside, Crane called her name again, silkier this time, offering her everything she'd ever lost.

"Promise me," Hazel said again.

"No," said Mara Voss, and took his free hand. "Whatever comes down — we run *together*."

Above the entrance, the cornice waited, patient as arithmetic, for someone to do the math.

Hazel raised the flare pistol, aimed at the fuel bladder beside the generator, and exhaled.
