## 16. Fifth Tide: What Power Wants

Mara Voss had spent eleven days learning the sound of Hazel Oyelaran's breathing.

Not on purpose. It was simply that in a plane the size of a shoebox with wings, there was nowhere else for sound to go. His breathing when he scanned the sky — slow, deliberate, a man reading a page. His breathing when he laughed at his own jokes, which was most of them, because he'd long ago stopped expecting an audience. And his breathing now, as they crouched behind the wrecked survey plane at the glacier's face, watching a white flag come down out of the sky like a handkerchief dropped by God: steady. Annoyingly steady.

"Is he serious," she whispered.

"He flew all this way with a bedsheet," Hazel said. "I'd say he's committed to the bit."

The plane that had delivered Aldous Crane sat down on the ice fifty yards out, engines ticking, propeller slowing with the smugness of a man who knew exactly how much fuel he had left and how little anyone else did. The white flag was lashed to the strut, snapping in the wind off the glacier. Behind it, Crane himself emerged in a coat that probably cost more than Hazel's entire aircraft, and stood in the open with his hands visible, palms out, the universal posture of *I am not going to shoot you yet*.

"You don't have to go," Hazel said quietly.

"Yes, I do." She checked the pistol she'd taken from the wreck site three tides ago, mostly for the comfort of its weight. "He came under a flag. If I don't hear what he has to say, it'll rattle around my head forever. You know how I am about loose ends."

"I do," Hazel said. "You alphabetized the canned goods in the ghost town."

"It was a long night."

"And the beans were filed under B."

"*All* the cans should be filed under B, Hazel, that's the entire point of alphabetical—"

"Mara." He caught her sleeve. His hand was warm through two layers of canvas, and his eyes had that look she'd been carefully not naming for six days, the one that made her want to both stay and run, preferably at the same time. "Whatever he offers you — and it'll be big, men like him lead with big — remember what he did to your team."

She remembered. She remembered everything. That was her curse and her profession.

"I'll remember," she said. "Watch the ridge. If anyone else comes down that slope who isn't wearing a bedsheet, you fly. Don't wait for me."

"Don't be stupid."

"Hazel." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real. "For once in your life, let someone else be the stubborn one. It's my turn. I've been practicing."

* * *

Crane waited for her at a respectful distance, which was itself a performance. Everything about Aldous Crane was a performance — the rumpled decency of the coat, the tired fatherly lines around his eyes, the way he said her name like it was a small apology.

"Dr. Voss. You look remarkably well, considering."

"Frostbite on two fingers and a diet of tinned beans, Mr. Crane. But thank you for noticing."

He gestured toward a folding stool one of his men had set beside a camp stove, because of course Crane traveled with furniture. Power, Mara thought, is mostly the ability to make other people carry things. She stayed standing. So, after a moment, did he.

"I'll be brief," he said. "You're a scientist. You appreciate data over rhetoric."

"I appreciate honesty over rhetoric. Data I can work with either way."

Something flickered across his face — respect, maybe, or the recalibration of a salesman meeting a customer who'd read the fine print. He reached into his coat and produced a folder, real paper, heavy stock, and held it out.

"The syndicate's offer. In writing. I was told to deliver it personally, and frankly, given the alternative methods available to us, I volunteered."

"The alternative being the men on the ridge with the rifles."

"The alternative being the men on the ridge," Crane agreed mildly, "who are under strict orders not to use them while I'm talking. After that, Dr. Voss, my influence over events becomes considerably more theoretical."

She took the folder. It was warm from his pocket, which felt obscene somehow, paper warmed by a human body carrying orders to bury people.

She read.

It was elegant. That was the thing that frightened her — not the money, though the money was there, a number with so many zeros it looked less like a salary than a phone number for another planet. It was the architecture of it. Full partnership. Her name on the recovery operation. Legal counsel retained on her behalf. Immunity structured through three shell companies so clean they gleamed. And beneath the financials, the part that made her breath go shallow:

*A public narrative restoration. The survey team will be commemorated as heroes of northern science, lost in service to their nation. Monuments. Named fellowships. Their families provided for in perpetuity.*

She read that paragraph twice. Three times.

"You'd tell their families they died heroes," she said.

"They *did* die heroes," Crane said. "That's the tragedy of it, Doctor. They died doing difficult work in a terrible place. The only question is whether their deaths mean something or whether they mean nothing. Right now they mean nothing — a bureaucratic accident, bodies never recovered, insurance disputes, widows fighting a government office for years. I'm offering you the power to make their deaths *mean* something. A lode worth more than the GDP of several nations, developed responsibly, funding hospitals and universities—" he waved a hand at the glacier, "—and their names carved in stone above the door."

"And you get the mountain."

"We get the mountain," he said, without shame. "Everyone gets something. That's what a deal is. What are *you* getting right now, Dr. Voss? Let's take inventory. You have a pilot with a bullet-scarred airplane and no fuel reserves. You have notes that no court will ever see, because the syndicate owns the courts. You have frostbite and six days of beans. And in approximately forty hours, you have nothing at all, because we will be here, and you will not."

The wind moved across the ice. Somewhere up on the ridge, a rifleman shifted, metal whispering against metal.

"You killed them," she said. "Okafor. Lindqvist. The Reyes twins. You took them off that glacier and—"

"I didn't," Crane said, and here his voice changed, dropped its polish, and she hated herself for listening closely enough to hear that it might be true. "I want to be precise with you, because precision is the only currency I have left that you might accept. The extraction went wrong. Men panicked. Decisions were made above my authority and below my standards. I have carried that error for four months." He looked at the glacier, and for one unguarded second he looked old. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to decide what the next forty hours purchase."

* * *

Here was the thing Mara had never told anyone, not even Hazel, especially not Hazel:

She wanted to say yes.

Not for the money. Money had never been her weakness; she'd walked away from a tenure track and a trust fund in the same year, to her mother's lasting despair. No — it was the paragraph about the monuments. The named fellowships. Okafor's daughter learning that her father hadn't vanished into a bureaucratic void but had died *for something*, with his name on a building, with strangers saying it kindly.

Power, she understood now, standing on the ice with the folder trembling slightly in her hands, doesn't tempt you with what you lack. It tempts you with absolution. It finds the guilt you carry and offers to launder it. Every bad choice she'd ever made — signing on with the syndicate's cover program, telling herself the mountain wouldn't miss what they took, staying silent while the survey became something else — every one of those choices could be rewritten. Not erased. *Rewritten.* Into heroism. Into meaning. Into a story where Mara Voss was not the woman who'd sold her principles at market rate, but the woman who'd survived to honor the dead.

That was the seduction. Not wealth. *Forgiveness, retail.*

"You've done this before," she said. "Offered people their own redemption at a discount."

"Twice," Crane said. "Both accepted. One of them sends me a card at Christmas." He almost smiled. "People believe what they need to believe, Doctor. I merely provide better documentation."

She thought of Hazel back at the wreck, watching the ridge, refusing — on principle, on some deep geological fault of character — to let her face this alone. She thought of him filing canned goods wrong and flying canyons on nerve and telling her, at the ghost town, in the orange light of a scavenged stove, about his brother, about the crash, about why a man measures his life in miles. He'd told her the truth without any monuments attached. Just the truth, offered plain, like bread.

And she thought of the number in the folder. God help her, she thought of the number.

"How much of it is real?" she asked. "The immunity structure. If I signed. Would it hold?"

Crane studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him answer honestly, which was perhaps the cruelest thing he could have done.

"For a while," he said. "Long enough for you to be rich. Long enough for the monuments to be built. Nothing holds forever, Dr. Voss. Not empires, not glaciers, not marriages. The question is never whether a thing will end. The question is what you'll have been, while it lasted."

"That's a terrible sales pitch."

"It's the only honest one I have."

She looked down at the folder. The paper was very white against the ice. Somewhere under her feet, three hundred meters down, lay the lode — the discovery of the century, the thing she'd mapped with her own instruments, her own careful hands, the find that should have made her career and had instead made her a fugitive. All she had to do was say yes. Say yes, and the past four months reorganized themselves into a story she could live inside. Say yes, and Okafor's daughter got a scholarship with her father's name on it. Say yes, and Mara Voss stopped running.

Her mouth opened.

What came out was: "Who signs the monument dedication?"

"What?"

"The plaque. The dedication. Whose name goes on it — mine? Yours? The syndicate's?" She heard her own voice go quiet and cold, the field voice, the one she used on rock samples and liars. "Because I've read your documentation, Mr. Crane, and I notice the monuments clause has no enforcement mechanism. No trustee. No schedule. It's a promise written in weather. You'd build the hospital and lose the plaque the same way you lost my team — through some error made above your authority and below your standards."

Crane's composure held, but barely, and Mara realized with a strange, sad clarity that she wasn't looking at a monster. She was looking at a middle manager of evil, a man who genuinely believed he was the reasonable one, the brake line on the machine, and who would keep believing it right up until the machine ran over him too.

"You're smarter than the last one," he said.

"The last one accepted?"

"The last one is currently building a marina in Patagonia and has never once returned my calls." He extended his hand for the folder. She didn't give it back. "Dr. Voss. Last chance, and I say this with genuine regard: walk away with me now, and in five years none of this will have touched you. Stay, and I cannot protect you from what happens in the next forty hours. I am not threatening you. I am *describing* you."

"No," Mara said. "You're describing yourself. I've been doing that since the cave. It's called differential diagnosis."

She tore the folder in half. Then, because the moment seemed to call for thoroughness and she was, after all, a scientist, she tore it in half again, and let the pieces fall on the ice, where the wind took them skittering away toward the crevasse field like pale, dying birds.

Crane watched them go. When he turned back, all the warmth had drained out of his face, and what remained was worse than anger. It was administration.

"All right," he said. "Forty hours."

He walked back toward his plane without hurrying, coat flapping, a man with a schedule. At the cabin door he paused, half-turned, and said, not unkindly:

"For what it's worth, Doctor — the last one cried. You tear paper. I respect that."

The plane lifted. The white flag, still lashed to the strut, streamed behind it like a surrender in reverse.

* * *

Mara walked back to the wreck on legs that felt borrowed. Hazel met her halfway, scanning the ridge over her shoulder, then her face, then the ridge again.

"Well?" he said. "How much was the farm?"

"The farm was considerable."

"And?"

"And I tore it up." She held up her hands, still shaking, and hated that they were shaking, and decided to be honest about it because apparently that was the kind of person she was becoming. "Hazel, I want you to understand something. I *almost*. There was a paragraph about monuments. About their families. About making the deaths mean something. And I stood there doing arithmetic with dead men's names, and I nearly — I was *close*, Hazel."

"Yeah," he said.

"'Yeah'? That's it? I confess to nearly selling out everyone we've lost, and you say 'yeah'?"

He shrugged, that maddening, gentle shrug, wind pulling at his collar. "Mara, I watched you alphabetize beans under life-or-death conditions. I know exactly how close you can get to a bad idea." He took her shaking hands in his, warming them without ceremony, as if it were the most ordinary task in the world. "You came back. That's the whole test. Everybody's tempted. Coming back is the test."

She wanted to kiss him. She also wanted to point out that his theory of moral testing was scientifically unsound and had a sample size of one. She settled for squeezing his hands and saying, "The forty hours start now. He wasn't bluffing about the timeline — the melt's ahead of schedule, and those men on the ridge aren't a negotiating position anymore. They're a cleanup crew."

"Then we burn the data tonight and blow the entrance tomorrow, before their next supply flight."

"Hazel—"

"I know." He was already turning toward the wreck, already moving, that terrible competence engaging gear by gear. "I know what it costs. Come on. We've got charges to build and a doctor's fingers to keep warm, and if we're going to die on this glacier, I'd rather do it on schedule."

They made it ten steps toward the plane before the ridge spoke — a single shot, high and deliberate, kicking ice-dust off the wreck's tail a foot from Hazel's head. A warning. Or an opening bid.

Then a second shot, lower, truer, and Hazel folded sideways into her arms with a sound she would spend the rest of her life trying not to hear again.
