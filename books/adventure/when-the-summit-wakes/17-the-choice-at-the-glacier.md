## 17. The Choice at the Glacier

The trouble with being shot at, Hazel had long ago decided, was that it ruined your concentration.

He'd been thinking about fuel mixture ratios when the first man came around the ice ridge — a big fellow in a gray parka with a rifle slung casual as a fishing rod — and Hazel had been thinking about Mara's laugh when the second one grabbed him from behind. A man could not run a proper scuffle and a proper daydream at the same time. Something had to give.

What gave was his left side, against a knee of blue ice, hard enough to make the whole glacier ring like a struck bell. Then a fist found his jaw, and then the world did a slow, dignified rotation, and then he was on his back looking up at a sky so clean and indifferent it felt almost personal.

"Stay down," said the big one, not unkindly. There was something almost apologetic about him. Hazel appreciated that. If you were going to be beaten by professionals, it was nice if they were polite about it.

"I was actually planning to get up," Hazel said through bloodied lips.

"Don't."

Mara's voice came from somewhere behind them, high and cracking: "Hazel—"

"He's fine," the big one said. "Mr. Crane wants everybody breathing. It's the paperwork he hates."

Hazel lay there and took inventory, the way you do after a rough landing. Jaw: functional. Ribs: complaining but present. Left side: going to be a spectacular bruise, the kind Mara would poke later just to watch him flinch. Pride: total loss. All things considered, better than some landings he'd walked away from.

Across the ice, two more of Crane's men had Mara by the arms, walking her toward the white flag fluttering on its bamboo pole like a flag of surrender planted by nobody who intended to surrender anything. She wasn't struggling. That frightened him more than the fists had. Mara Voss fought when there was something to fight for, and her stillness now meant she was calculating, and her calculating meant she was listening to whatever Crane was saying to her, low and reasonable, twenty yards off near the yawning mouth of the lode.

The melt had opened the glacier overnight. What had been a seam of dirty ice yesterday was now an archway tall enough to drive a truck through, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and deep time. Somewhere inside it lay the lode — the veins of pale metal Mara had described by stove-light in the ghost town, her voice dropping the way people drop their voices in churches. Enough wealth under this ice, she'd said, to buy governments. Enough to make the survey team's disappearance worth arranging.

And Crane wanted to arrange Mara right into it.

* * *

Mara let them walk her because struggling was for people without options, and she had spent three weeks in a cave learning the difference between options and pride.

Crane stood at the lip of the archway with his hands clasped behind him, watching the meltwater pour off the ice in silver ropes. He looked, as always, like a man waiting for a train that would certainly be punctual. Up close she could see the age in him — the fine cracks around his eyes, the government-issue patience worn smooth as river stone.

"Dr. Voss," he said, without turning. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other geologist."

That earned her half a smile. "I did see the other geologist." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; it was how men like him got other men to dig their own graves. "I want to show you something before we discuss terms."

He nodded, and one of his men unrolled a canvas satchel on the ice: documents, photographs, a ledger bound in green cloth that Mara recognized with a lurch in her stomach. The survey's master log. Her own handwriting on the spine label. Taken from the wrecked plane, or from the camp, or from one of the men whose names she still said to herself at night like penance.

"The syndicate has prepared a version of events," Crane said. "Clean, generous, heroic. Your colleagues died in a crevasse collapse during a routine survey. Posthumous commendations. Pensions to the families — real ones, Dr. Voss, indexed properly. And you, the sole survivor, the woman who kept the science alive against impossible odds. There will be a medal. There may be a building."

"And the lode?"

"Developed responsibly. Which is to say, quietly." He finally turned to face her, and his eyes were not cruel. That was the worst part. They were tired, and reasonable, and utterly without exit. "You've seen what's down there. You know what happens if this gets loose into the open market — every claim-jumper, every militia, every desperate government within a thousand miles comes to this range with dynamite. The mountain dies either way, Mara. The only question is whether anyone is compensated."

It was a good argument. That was what terrified her. Crane didn't lie, mostly; he just built small honest rooms and locked the doors.

"And Hazel?" she asked.

"Hazel Oyelaran is a bush pilot with a suspended license pending inquiry and a plane worth less than the fuel in it. He walks away. Rich, if he's sensible. Alive, if he's not." Crane said it mildly, the way you'd discuss weather. "You're the asset. He's the vehicle. Vehicles are replaceable."

Something went very cold and very clear in Mara's chest, colder than the cave had ever made her, clearer than three weeks of frost and fear. Because she had heard this speech before — not from Crane, from herself, years ago, telling herself the mountain wouldn't miss what they took, telling herself she was the scientist and the money was incidental, telling herself all the comfortable arithmetic that let clever people sleep beside ugly ledgers.

She had signed on knowing. She had confessed that to Hazel in the ghost town with the stove ticking between them, and he hadn't forgiven her exactly, but he had stayed, which was harder and worth more.

"Here's my counteroffer," Mara said.

"Mara—"

"No. You'll want to hear it." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "You keep your medal. You keep your building. I take the master log, I take my duplicate field notes — yes, Crane, duplicates, I'm a geologist, we believe in redundancy — and Hazel and I fly out of here with nothing else. No evidence reaches a market. No evidence reaches a newspaper. The lode stays buried, and I mean buried — Hazel's already rigged the charges. You get your quiet. The families get the truth eventually, because I mailed the notes from the ghost town ten days ago and you can't intercept a letter that's already crossed six tides of country."

Crane's expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, tectonic and slow. "There were no mail runs through Kestrel Flats. I checked."

"Then you checked badly," said Mara Voss, who had spent one long feverish night in an abandoned depot with a stamp pad and a lie about a supply flight, "which must be embarrassing for a government man."

* * *

They put Hazel back in the plane — not gently, but not brutally either, because Crane's orders were explicit and his men were the sort who followed explicit orders even when they disagreed with them. The big one, whose name turned out to be Ferro, cinched the cargo strap across Hazel's lap and muttered, "Sorry about the ribs," which Hazel chose to find funny rather than insulting, since laughing hurt too much for either.

Through the windshield he watched them bring Mara out of earshot and back toward the plane, and he read her walk the way he read weather. Shoulders level. Chin up. Stride unhurried. That was Mara deciding something, not Mara defeated. He'd seen defeated once, in the cave, in the first hour, when she'd thought he might be one of them. This wasn't it.

Ferro leaned in the door. "For what it's worth, pilot — most of us figured the survey crew for dead months ago. Nobody sleeps great."

"That's a shame," Hazel said. "Sleep's important."

Ferro almost smiled, then didn't, and left.

The camp settled into a strange truce. Crane's men tended a stove on the ice. The melt roared softly in the archway. Somewhere above, a cornice groaned and shed a curtain of powder that drifted down the glacier face like flour through a sieve. Hazel sat in his seat with his ribs strapped and his jaw swelling and did the only thing he knew how to do with fear, which was to convert it into arithmetic. Fuel: eleven hundred pounds, minus what the leak in number two tank had cost them coming in. Distance to the pass: one hundred forty miles, over terrain that ate planes for breakfast. Wind: building out of the northwest, which was bad, unless you were clever, in which case it was free altitude.

And Mara. Mara was the variable no table could hold.

She climbed in twenty minutes later, folded herself into the seat beside him, and stared straight ahead through the frost-furred glass.

"Well?" Hazel asked.

"He offered me everything."

"And?"

"And I told him where he could file it." She turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "He's going to let us taxi, Hazel. He's going to stand there with his white flag and watch us line up, and then the moment we're airborne his lead plane is going to come off that ridge where it's been sitting since dawn, and we both know it."

Hazel looked. Sure enough, on the eastern ridge, half-hidden in the shadow of the moraine, sat a shape he'd been pretending was a rock. Low wing. Twin engines. The salvage fleet's lead hunter, patient as a spider.

"Yeah," he said. "I saw it."

"So what's the play? Because I have spent three weeks surviving on hope and glacier water, and I am fresh out of clever."

Hazel worked his jaw, winced, and found that underneath the fear and the cracked ribs and the certain knowledge that he was about to do something unforgivably stupid, there was a strange calm. The same calm that arrived sometimes on short final in crosswinds, when the decision was already made and all that remained was execution.

"The play," he said, "is that I'm not leaving here with nothing."

Mara frowned. "Hazel—"

"Listen to me." He turned in the straps, and his side screamed, and he kept talking anyway. "Crane thinks you're the asset and I'm the vehicle. Fine. But here's what he doesn't have in his ledger. I don't need the evidence. I don't need the fortune. I don't need the medal or the building or any of it. Six days ago I would have told you my whole life was that plane and these mountains, and I'd have been lying, because I measured my life in miles so I'd never have to count the years. And then a garbled distress call came through on a quiet frequency, and I flew into it, and everything I actually care about turned out to fit in one seat next to mine."

Mara had gone very still.

"So here's my offer," Hazel said. "No terms. No counteroffer. I fly us out of here with nothing — no log, no notes, no proof, no payday. Just you. And if the pass closes, I put her down on a gravel bar and we walk, and if the fleet catches us, well." He shrugged, and paid for it. "I've been shot at worse. Once. By a goose. Long story."

A sound escaped Mara that was half laugh, half sob, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth.

"You idiot," she whispered. "You beautiful, reckless idiot. Do you know what I gave up in that tent back there? Everything. I handed him the only future I had left, and it cost me — God, it cost me, Hazel, I sat there and I *wanted* it—" Her voice broke. "And you're telling me you'd trade yours for a passenger."

"Not a passenger," Hazel said.

"No," she agreed, wet-eyed and fierce. "Not a passenger."

She reached over and took his hand, the one resting on the throttle, and closed her fingers around it, and neither of them said anything else for a moment because there was nothing left that needed saying. Outside, the melt thundered. Inside, two people who had spent their whole lives keeping accounts decided, together and forever, to go bankrupt.

"Sixth tide tomorrow," Mara said finally, voice thick. "The melt peaks. After that, the archway closes for another year and Crane loses his window, and he knows it. He won't wait us out. Whatever he does, he does tonight or at first light."

"Then we don't wait either." Hazel's thumb moved over her knuckles, once. "Tonight, while they're sleeping off their victory. We torch the survey data in the wreck, we blow the entrance, and we go out low down the river valley with the wind behind us and the dark on top of us."

"They'll hear the charges."

"They'll hear them," Hazel agreed. "But they'll be hearing them from a glacier that's coming down on their heads, and I promise you, Mara Voss, nobody shoots straight while running from an avalanche."

She laughed then, really laughed, the sound cracking open in the little cabin like sunlight through cloud, and shook her head at him.

"When this is over," she said, "you're grounded. Permanently. I'm putting you somewhere flat with a garden."

"Deal," said Hazel, who meant it, who had never meant anything more. "But I get to pick the garden."

* * *

Dusk came down the glacier like a held breath slowly released. The light went from gold to violet to a blue so deep it seemed to come from under the world, and the archway in the ice glowed faintly from within, lit by refraction, like a doorway standing open onto somewhere older than maps.

Hazel ran the preflight by feel in the dark, fingers reading switches, ears reading the engine's cough and catch. His ribs throbbed in time with the propeller. Across the ice, Crane's camp had gone quiet except for one sentry pacing the perimeter, breath smoking, rifle carried with the weary indifference of a man on his last watch before relief.

Mara slid into her seat and buckled in. In her lap, wrapped in a wool sock, sat the fuel charges — three of them, scavenged from the wreck, wired crude and honest. In her coat pocket, the master log. She'd taken it back off Crane's table during the parley, plain as daylight, and nobody had stopped her, because Crane had believed — needed to believe — that she was already his.

"Ready?" Hazel asked.

Mara looked out at the glowing archway, at the mountain that had taken her career and her team and nearly her life, and that had also, in its cold and roundabout way, given her everything.

"No," she said. "Let's go anyway."

Hazel fed the throttle forward, and the old plane rolled out onto the ice under a sky full of hard stars, toward the wreck and the fire and the sixth tide, while behind them on the ridge, unseen in the dark, the lead hunter's engines began, very softly, to turn.
