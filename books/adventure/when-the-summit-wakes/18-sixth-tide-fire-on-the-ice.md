## 18. Sixth Tide: Fire on the Ice

The melt had a sound to it, Hazel decided. Not the crack and boom the books talked about — that was the glacier's voice, deep and theatrical. This was smaller. A hiss. A thousand small hisses, water finding its way through ice that had held its breath for ten thousand years and was now, all at once, letting it out.

He stood at the mouth of the lode with a jerrican in each hand and the taste of copper in his mouth, and he listened to the mountain exhale.

Behind him, the ice cavern yawned — wider now than it had been two days ago, the melt peeling back the glacier's face like skin off a fruit. Inside, the vein Mara had described ran through the dark in a ribbon of grey-blue gleam, the mineral catching what little light reached it and throwing it back wrong, too bright, like something that had been told to hold still and was furious about it. The syndicate's geologists would have wept. Hazel intended to make sure they never got the chance.

Ahead of him, down the valley, the salvage fleet's planes were a smudge of dark against the white — three of them now, maybe four, coming up the river corridor in the loose staggered formation of men who had stopped pretending this was a rescue.

"Two minutes," Mara called from the wreck of the survey plane. She was on her knees in the snow with the survey's data cases open in front of her, sorting with the terrible efficiency of a woman who had spent her career deciding what mattered. "I need two minutes. Some of this is real work, Hazel. Ten years of it. Somebody's cores, somebody's field notes from before all this went rotten."

"We don't have two minutes."

"Then give me one and a half and I'll only burn the good parts."

He almost laughed. That was the thing about her — the thing he'd been discovering tide by tide, the thing that had gotten him into this in more ways than one: she could stand in the mouth of the end of everything and still argue about the allocation of seconds. It wasn't bravery, exactly. It was more like a refusal to let the situation dictate the terms of the conversation.

He carried the jerricans to the cavern mouth and set them down where the vein ran closest to the surface. The fuel charges were crude — jerricans, primers, a length of survey fuse Mara had found in the wreck, the kind of improvised demolition that would make an actual explosives man wince and then leave the country. But the physics were simple. Fuel, fire, and a glacier already halfway to letting go of itself. He didn't need to be precise. He just needed to be persuasive.

The mountain would do the rest.

* * *

They had argued about it the night before, in the lee of the wreck, under a sky that wouldn't quite go dark.

"You could still take the data out," Mara had said. "Some of it. Enough to prove what they did to my team. Names, dates, the manifest Crane signed. The mineral can stay buried and the evidence can walk."

"And the syndicate reads the evidence in a courtroom they own, and the lode sits under the ice like a coin in a mattress, waiting for the next Crane. Or the one after him. The melt's already opened it, Mara. In five years some survey crew stumbles into it honest as you please, and we've bought the world a decade and called it a victory." He'd turned the jerrican in his hands. "I'm tired of buying decades."

"So you'd bury it all. The vein and the proof together."

"I'd give the mountain back its own." He'd looked at her then, and the words had come out plainer than he'd planned, the way true things do. "Your team didn't die for a mineral. They died because some men looked at that ice and saw money where other men saw a mountain. I can't bring them back. But I can make sure the money stays under the ice. That's the only honest inheritance anybody's getting off this glacier."

She'd been quiet a long time. The melt had ticked and whispered around them.

"My grandmother had a saying," she'd said finally. "When the flood comes, you don't argue with the river. You just make sure you're standing on the right hill." A pause. "Burn it. But I keep my duplicate notes. The ones I cached at Third Tide, in the cairn by the moraine. The families get their truth, Hazel. The syndicate keeps its rock. That's the trade."

"That's the trade," he'd agreed.

And now it was the morning of the Sixth Tide, and the river was coming, and it was time to pay.

* * *

The first plane put down on the ice flat at the valley's mouth at 0940, by Hazel's watch — a big converted freighter on skis, white-painted, no markings that a man could swear to in court. It taxied to a stop and did not shut down. Doors opened. Men got out. Men with rifles, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had been told, correctly, that no one was coming to stop them.

"Four on the ice," Hazel said, flat on his belly at the lip of the rise above the wreck, binoculars up. "Two holding at the plane. Two moving up-valley. There'll be a second wave — Crane won't spend his shooters all at once."

"Crane's with them?"

"He'll be with the second wave. Crane leads from behind. It's the one consistent thing about him." He passed her the binoculars. "How's the fuse?"

"Short." Mara lay beside him, close enough that he could feel the frost on her sleeve against his arm, and he found, absurdly, that this was the thing he noticed — not the rifles, not the freighter, but the small cold pressure of her shoulder against his. "Thirty seconds, maybe forty, if the wind doesn't eat it. You light it and you run, and you don't look back to admire your work, because I have known you for eleven days and I already know you'll want to."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would. You're a man who talks to his engine."

"My engine answers."

"Hazel." She lowered the binoculars. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and beneath the frostburn and the exhaustion she looked — there was no other word for it — resolved. "The vein runs under the whole face. When that goes, the ice above it goes with it. You understand we're not closing a door. We're bringing down the whole wall."

"I understand."

"And the wreck — the team's plane —"

"Is already a wreck." He said it gently. He knew what that plane was to her. It was the last place her people had been people. "The mountain will keep them better than a courtroom would. Under the ice, nobody rewrites them."

She looked at him a moment longer, and then she did something he hadn't expected: she kissed him, quick and hard, tasting of frost and kerosene and the end of arguments. "For luck," she said. "Not because we're going to discuss what it means. We'll discuss it when we're not being shot at."

"Best terms I've been offered all week."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had three parts, and Hazel had learned in eleven days that any plan with more than three parts was a plan for people with better luck.

Part one: Mara fired the fuse and they ran for the plane, which was fueled and warmed and pointed down-valley like a dog pointing at a door.

Part two: the charges brought the glacier face down across the lode, and the ice flat with it, which meant the freighter at the valley mouth would be sitting on ground that was, in the technical sense, no longer ground.

Part three: there was no part three. Part three was whatever happened next, and Hazel had learned that part three was always where the plan went to die and the flying began.

The two men coming up-valley made it easy. They were fifty yards out and closing at a walk, rifles slung, when Mara slipped down the slope toward the fuse with the casual gait of a woman out for a stroll on a glacier, and one of them shouted something — a warning, a question — and the other one raised his rifle, and that was when Hazel stood up on the rise, big as life, arms wide, and bellowed:

"Gentlemen! You're standing on a melting glacier above a fuel charge, and I am a licensed pilot with nothing left to lose! I suggest a conversation!"

It was, he would think later, the single most ridiculous moment of his life, and it worked for exactly four seconds, which was four seconds more than he'd budgeted. The two men stared. Mara crouched at the fuse. And then the nearer one — young, quick, not stupid — swung his rifle up and fired, and the bullet cracked past Hazel's ear with the flat slap of a door slamming in a house he used to live in, and everything got very simple and very fast.

"Light it!" Hazel roared, already running.

Mara struck the match. The fuse caught with a hiss, a bright line crawling toward the cavern mouth, and she turned and ran, and Hazel ran to meet her, and the world resolved into snow and breath and the two of them converging like the two halves of a sentence.

"Thirty seconds," she gasped.

"We need twenty-five of them. Run anyway."

Behind them, the shooting started in earnest — not aimed, not yet, the two men on the ice firing at figures, at movement, at the general affront of being defied. A round kicked snow up between Hazel's boots. Another whined off the wreck of the survey plane with a sound like a struck tuning fork, and something in his chest clenched — that plane had carried his brother's kind of men, honest men, dead men — and then they were at the Otter, and Mara was up and into the right seat, and Hazel was hauling himself in, and the engine was already turning over because he'd left it running, because he was a man who talked to his engine and his engine knew him.

"Twenty seconds," Mara said. "Hazel. Twenty."

"I know."

He shoved the throttle forward and the Otter lurched across the snow, skis chattering, tail swinging, and the freighter down at the valley mouth was turning, men spilling from it, someone on a radio, and the glacier behind them hissed and whispered and held its breath.

"Fifteen."

The tail came around. The nose came down the valley. The ice flat stretched ahead of them, a mile of it, and the freighter sat on it like a chess piece on a table someone was about to flip.

"Ten."

"Hold on to something," Hazel said, "and also hold on to me."

"Both hands are busy."

"Then hold on with your opinions."

The first charge went with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard — a deep, structural *whump*, the mountain's own bass note — and the glacier face shrugged. Snow and ice lifted off the slope in a white sheet, and the second charge followed it, and the third, and the vein that had gleamed in the dark for ten thousand years went under a hundred thousand tons of its own mountain, and the ice flat began, gently at first and then not gently at all, to come apart.

"GO," Mara said, though he was already going, throttle against the stop, the Otter accelerating down a runway that was cracking behind them like a plate in a slow fist. The freighter ahead was trying to turn, trying to lift, men running for it, and the ice under it groaned with a sound like a ship dying.

The tail of the Otter lifted. The skis sang. And then they were airborne — barely, wheels-of-the-soul barely, the glacier's breath lifting them and shoving them all at once — and Hazel hauled the yoke back and left, banking hard over the valley wall as the world behind them turned white.

* * *

He looked back. He knew she'd said not to, and he looked back anyway, because some things a man owes his eyes.

The glacier face was gone. Where the lode's mouth had yawned, there was a fresh scar of blue ice and rubble, already sloughing, already smoothing itself over with new snow, the mountain pulling its blanket back up over its chest. The freighter sat canted at an angle on broken ice, one ski through the surface, its crew standing around it in a small dark cluster like mourners at a wedding that had been called off.

And on the ice flat, at the edge of the ruin, a single figure in a good coat, standing very still, watching them go.

Crane.

Hazel couldn't see his face at this distance. He didn't need to. He tipped the Otter's wing — a small, insolent, unmistakable gesture — and held it there for two seconds, and then he leveled off and put the mountain behind him.

"You're a petty, petty man," Mara said. There was something in her voice that might have been a laugh trying to get out through the shaking.

"I contain multitudes. Mostly pettiness."

And that was when the second wave arrived.

* * *

They came out of the sun — two planes, fast, low, the kind of aircraft that had no business being this far from any legitimate airfield — and the first burst of fire walked across the sky ahead of them like a man testing ice.

"Hold on," Hazel said, and put the Otter down into the valley, down where the walls rose on either side and the river ran brown with melt, down into the only country where a slow old bush plane and its two exhausted passengers could disappear.

The river valley swallowed them. The gunfire stuttered and fell behind, and the Otter flew on into the long white throat of the north, engine steady, fuel gauge — Hazel glanced at it, and glanced again, and felt the day reach into his chest and close its hand — the fuel gauge sitting lower than any arithmetic could forgive.

"Hazel," Mara said quietly. She'd seen it too. She'd seen everything; that was the trouble with her. "How far to the pass?"

He looked at the mountains ahead, and the miles between, and the needle that didn't care about either.

"Not far enough," he said.
