## 19. The Long Glide

The engine quit the way old men quit arguments — with a shudder, a cough, and a silence that felt like surrender.

Hazel's hands were already moving. Carb heat. Mixture rich. Best glide speed, seventy-two, and he trimmed the nose down until the horizon sat where it belonged. Below them the river unspooled through the valley like a silver thread someone had dropped from a great height, and the pass — the pass they needed, the pass that would take them over the wall and down to the coast — sat behind and above, a notch in the granite they would not reach.

"Talk to me," Mara said. Her voice was steady. It had been steady since the gunfire, which Hazel found either admirable or slightly alarming.

"Fuel line's hit. Maybe two places. I've got pressure dropping and a fuel pressure light that's having the time of its life." He checked the gauge again, as if it might have reconsidered. It hadn't. "We're not making the pass."

"How far to the coast if we did?"

"Two hundred miles. We've got maybe forty of flying in the tanks, if the tanks agree, and they're leaking, so they won't." He glanced at her. "I'm sorry. I got us up. I should have—"

"Hazel." She put her hand flat on the instrument panel, over the fuel gauge, as if she could hold the numbers still through sheer will. "You flew us through gunfire. Whatever happens next, that part's done, and it was done well. Now tell me what you need."

What he needed was altitude, and the mountain had stopped lending it. The nose dropped a degree, then another, and the valley floor came up to meet them with the patience of something that had been waiting ten thousand years and could wait ten thousand more.

"River," he said. "I'm going down the river."

"Rivers have bends."

"Rivers have gravel bars, too. Somewhere in between those two facts, we're going to land."

* * *

Mara had read water her whole life, though not the way pilots did. A geologist reads a river the way a doctor reads a face — for history, for pressure, for what the water was doing last spring and what it intended to do next. She leaned over the window now, the cold glass against her forehead, and watched the valley come up at them.

The river ran wide and shallow here, braided into a dozen silver channels that split and married and split again around islands of gray stone. Good, she thought. Braided rivers are honest. They tell you where their gravel is.

"Hazel. Left fork, past the big island. The water goes pale there — that's shallow, that's gravel."

"Pale means shallow?"

"Pale means the water's running over its own bed instead of carving one. Dark water is deep water. It's the same with people, if you think about it."

"I'll think about that when we're not falling out of the sky."

The plane sank through the valley air in long, patient arcs. Hazel flew it the way he did everything — with an economy that looked like calm and wasn't. She'd watched him for two weeks now, through passes and storms and the wreckage of everything they'd believed, and she had learned that the steadiness was a discipline, not a temperament. He chose to be calm the way other men chose to be brave, hourly, against evidence.

"There," she said. A gravel bar lay along the inside of the next bend, long and pale and mercifully flat, its upstream end trailing a fan of shallow water. "That one. It's the best-looking thing I've seen all day, and I've been looking at you."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in a cockpit." His eyes never left the river. "Flaps when I call them. And Mara — when we touch, put your hands in your lap and let go of everything. Let the plane do what it's going to do."

"Let go of everything," she repeated. It occurred to her, with the strange clarity of the nearly-crashed, that she'd been trying to do that for two years and had only managed it now, at seventy miles an hour, with no engine.

"Flaps."

She pulled. The plane ballooned, sank, steadied. The gravel bar grew from a suggestion into a fact. The river hissed past on both sides, close enough that she could see the individual stones through the shallows, and then the wheels touched — a hard, bounding slap, a second slap, a long grinding roar as the plane plowed a furrow through loose stone and slewed sideways and stopped, all at once, the way a held breath lets go.

Silence. Real silence, the kind the mountains make when they're not being interrupted.

Hazel sat with both hands on the yoke, head down, breathing. Then he laughed — one short, disbelieving bark of a laugh.

"We're down," he said. "We're actually down."

Mara looked at her hands, folded in her lap, exactly where he'd told her to put them. "I let go of everything," she reported. "It worked."

* * *

The plane was dying and they both knew it. Fuel wept from a punctured line and pooled under the wing in a rainbow-colored puddle that grew even as they watched, and the bullet holes along the fuselage — seven of them, Hazel counted, because counting was how he kept his hands from shaking — let the daylight through in little neat circles, as if the plane had developed a sudden interest in astronomy.

"She's done," Hazel said. "Even if I had fuel, she's done. She won't hold pressure. She's leaking from places that don't have names."

They stood in the shallows and looked at her, at the little yellow bush plane that had carried Hazel's whole life — mail and medicine and miles and miles and miles — and Mara watched him look, and understood that he was not looking at an airplane. He was looking at a version of himself that was ending.

"We could stay with her," she offered. "Wait. Someone comes eventually."

"Crane's someone comes eventually. That's the problem." He crouched and put his hand flat on the gravel, the way she'd seen him do a dozen times, reading the ground like a page. "No. We walk. There's a fish camp at the mouth of the Clearwater — old man Tengbe's place. Thirty miles, maybe thirty-five, down the river. He's got a radio, if it works, and he's got a boat, if it floats, and he's got coffee, if God is kind."

"Thirty-five miles." Mara looked down the valley, where the river ran away between walls of spruce and shadow. Her feet, inside her boots, made a small, weary objection that she chose to overrule. "I once walked eleven miles to a roadhead with a broken ski and a grudge. I can do thirty-five."

"What was the grudge?"

"A man who said women couldn't read ice." She started walking. "He was wrong, by the way. I read it fine. The ice was the problem."

* * *

They walked the river the way the river wanted to be walked — on the bars, in the shallows, along the gray shoulders of stone where the water couldn't argue. Hazel carried the survival kit and the axe; Mara carried the survey notes, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her chest, and neither of them suggested leaving them behind, because the notes were the only reason any of the dying had meant anything.

The afternoon went long and gold. The valley smelled of spruce and cold water and, faintly, of the melt — that green mineral breath that came off the glacier country and followed them like a memory. Mara found herself cataloguing it automatically, the way she catalogued everything: valley floor, glacial till, recent slump on the east bank, the river eating its way toward some new argument with the land. The mountain was waking, just as she'd told Hazel it would. Somewhere behind them the lode's entrance was yawning wider by the hour, and Crane's men were converging on it, and none of that could be helped by her feet, so she pointed her feet downriver and let the rest go.

"You're quiet," Hazel said.

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Steps. It keeps the thinking quiet." She glanced at him. "You count miles. I count steps. We're the same disease, you and me."

"We're not the same disease." He smiled, tired and crooked. "You count steps to stop thinking. I count miles to stop feeling. Completely different diagnoses."

"Ah. My mistake." She stepped over a runnel of snowmelt, cold biting through her boot, and thought about what she was going to say next, and said it anyway, because they had passed the point where they could afford unsaid things. "Hazel. The plane."

"I know."

"Your license. The inquiry. Everything."

"I know, Mara." He walked a few paces in silence, boots crunching the flat gray stones. "You know what's strange? I've been counting miles since I was nineteen years old. Mail runs, medevacs, freight — I could tell you to the mile how far I've flown, every year, like rings in a tree. And this morning, going through that canyon with bullets coming through the windows, I wasn't counting anything. First time in twenty years. And it was the best flying of my life." He looked at her, and the gold light was on his face, and she forgot, briefly, how to count. "So the plane's done. Maybe the counting's done too."

"That's either very wise or very concussed."

"Could be both. Check my pupils when we stop."

* * *

They stopped when the light went from gold to copper, on a wide bar where the river split around an island crowned with a single wind-bent spruce. Hazel got a fire going from the kit's dry tinder and some deadfall, and they ate the survey team's abandoned rations — beans and hard biscuits that tasted like punishment — and drank river water boiled in the kit's battered pot, and it was, Mara thought, the finest meal of her life, which said less about the beans than about everything else.

The fire cracked and spat. Up the valley, the peaks had gone pink, then ash, and the first stars were pricking through. It was beautiful in the way that only places that had tried to kill you could be beautiful — you earned the view, and the view knew it.

"Hazel. Tell me about your brother."

He fed a stick to the fire instead of answering, and she thought he wouldn't, and then he did.

"Tomás. Younger by two years. The pilot in the family, officially — he had the license, the airline job, the uniform. I was the one who just flew." He turned the stick. "We went up together one October. Ice on the pass that shouldn't have been there. He was flying. I was in the right seat, and I saw it before he did, and I said it too late, or he heard it too late, or the mountain just decided — I've never known which, and I've stopped needing to know." The firelight moved on his face. "I walked away from that wreck. He didn't. And I learned two things in the hospital. One: never fly with anyone you can't stand to lose. Two: that rule is a way of being alone and calling it safety." He looked over at her. "I broke rule one about six tides ago. I'm told that's called progress."

Mara reached over and took his hand. His fingers were cold and cracked from the river water, and she held them anyway, and neither of them said anything for a while, because the fire was saying it for them.

"My turn," she said finally. "The man who said women couldn't read ice. His name was Dr. Ellery, and he cost me my career, and I have spent six years being angry at him, and I realized today, walking, that I can't remember his face. I can remember being angry. I can't remember him." She laughed, small and surprised. "Anger's a terrible pack animal. It eats everything you carry and gives nothing back."

"What are you carrying now?"

She considered. The fire. His hand. The oilcloth bundle against her chest, with its duplicate notes that might, if they lived, bring the whole rotten thing down. "Not much," she said. "It's nice."

* * *

They walked out at first light, and the valley gave them the day like a gift — hard, bright, and paid for. By midmorning the river had widened and slowed, gathering itself, and the spruce gave way to alder, and then, around a bend, to the ramshackle geometry of Tengbe's fish camp: three cabins, a drying rack, a stovepipe smoking cheerfully into the blue, and an old man mending a net who looked up at the two of them — mud to the knees, frost-nipped, singed, bullet-grazed, alive — and said, without any surprise at all:

"Radio's inside. Coffee's on. You look like a story."

Mara laughed until she had to sit down on a fish crate.

Hazel sent the message — coast station, then the aviation office, then, after a moment's thought, a name in the capital he'd sworn he'd never trust again, a journalist who owed him from a medevac two winters back and paid her debts in print. *Survivors of the Voss survey, two, alive. Requesting transport to the coast. And get someone asking questions about Aldous Crane before he gets there first.*

The key clicked and clicked under his hand, and down the river the water ran silver toward the sea, carrying, for once, nothing but itself.

Mara stood in the doorway of the cabin, the duplicate notes heavy against her chest, and watched the river go. "Thirty-five miles," she said. "We made it."

"We made it," Hazel agreed. Then, because the old habit died hard, he caught himself starting to count — the miles still between them and the coast, the hours, the tides — and stopped, and let the number go, and put his arm around her instead.

The coffee was terrible. It was the best coffee either of them had ever tasted. And somewhere downriver, in the world they'd walked out of, Aldous Crane was about to discover what the mountain had decided to keep — and what it had decided, at last, to give back.
