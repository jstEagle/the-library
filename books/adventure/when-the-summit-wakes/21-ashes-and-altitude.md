## 21. Ashes and Altitude

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Governments did their damage on Tuesdays, when the week was too young to fight back.

Hazel read it standing in the doorway of the mail shed at Corvair Field, the envelope still smelling faintly of the coastal office it had been stamped in — ink and damp paper and somebody's cold coffee. The language was the usual beautiful nothing: *pending completion of inquiry*, *temporary suspension of certificate*, *aircraft assessed as constructive total loss*. Constructive total loss. He turned the phrase over like a stone he'd found on the strip. Somebody in an office had sat down and constructed those three words on purpose, and they meant, in plain speech, that his plane was ash and his wings were paper and neither was coming back.

The Otter had been written off before, technically speaking, but this was different. This time there were photographs in the file. He'd seen them. The wreck on the gravel bar downriver, one wing folded like a bird settling to sleep, the fuselage scored with bullet channels you could have laid a finger in. The assessors had counted forty-one holes. Hazel had stopped counting at twenty because after that it stopped being arithmetic and started being a eulogy.

"You're doing the face," Mara said from behind him.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like a man reading his own obituary and taking notes."

He handed her the letter. She read it faster than he had, the way she read everything, her eyes going down the page like water finding cracks. She'd been out at the garden plot behind the shack they'd rented — rented, not leased by the season, because leases implied futures and futures were suddenly things people talked about having — with dirt to her elbows and a smear of it across one cheekbone that she hadn't noticed and he wasn't going to tell her about, because some things you kept just for yourself.

"Constructive total loss," she said. "That's almost poetry."

"It's forty-one bullet holes is what it is."

"It's a government deciding your airplane died of its wounds." She folded the letter along its original creases, precise as everything she did. "Which, to be fair, it did."

He grunted. It was easier than agreeing, and agreement would have opened a door onto a room he wasn't ready to walk through yet. Because here was the thing he'd been circling for eleven days now, since the inquiry board had finished asking him the same question nine different ways — *Mr. Oyelaran, can you account for the discharge of fuel charges on the glacier* — and he'd answered, every time, with the same flat truth: *I burned it. All of it. Ask me why and I'll tell you again.* Here was the thing: without the miles, he didn't entirely know who he was.

Twenty-two years. Mail runs and medevacs and freight nobody else would take, up valleys that didn't appear on maps so much as apologize for being left off them. He'd measured his life in miles the way other men measured it in years, because miles were honest. A mile was a mile whether you flew it alone or not. Years just piled up quietly behind you while you weren't looking, and then one day a man in a gray suit handed you a letter on a Tuesday and you discovered you'd been counting wrong the whole time.

"Hazel." Mara touched his arm. "You went somewhere."

"Just tallying."

"How many?"

"Forty-one holes," he said. "Give or take a life."

* * *

The inquiry closed on a Friday with no ceremony whatsoever, which was somehow worse than if they'd held one. Crane's conviction came through the same wire service feed that carried the fishing reports: *former government liaison Aldous Crane sentenced on charges including conspiracy, obstruction, and the deaths of four members of the Kestrel Range survey team.* Four names. Hazel read them twice each, the way you'd read names carved into something. Okonkwo. Lindqvist. Perrault. Adeyemi. The families had forced the inquiry — Mara's duplicate field notes, mailed from three different towns under three different names while she'd been hiding in the ice, had done what no single survivor's testimony could. The syndicate itself never surfaced. Somewhere behind Crane, bigger shapes moved in deeper water, and the nets the families could throw weren't built for that depth. But Crane fell, and the four names got said out loud in a courtroom, and Mara stood in the back of the public gallery in her one good coat and listened to every word of it without blinking.

Hazel's own verdict arrived as a paragraph: license suspended pending review, review scheduled for the spring, no findings of misconduct, several findings of *irregular judgment under exigent circumstances*. He'd laughed out loud at that last part, alone in the shack, until it turned into something else and he had to sit down on the edge of the bed and put his head between his knees like a man coming out of a spin.

Irregular judgment. Twenty-two years of flying weather that grounded men half his age, and the sum of it was *irregular*.

Mara found him there. She didn't ask what was wrong, because she knew exactly what was wrong; she'd been watching him circle it for days the way he circled a strip in crosswind, checking the wind sock, never quite committing. Instead she sat down next to him on the bed, which complained under their combined weight, and said:

"Tell me about the first time you flew alone."

"Why?"

"Because you're mourning," she said, "and I've learned you don't mourn forward. You go back and walk the whole road once more, and then you're done. So we'll walk it. Start at the beginning."

So he told her. About being nineteen, and the Cessna 140 with the door that wouldn't latch right, and old Tom Beaudry who owned it saying *if the door pops open, don't panic, just fly the plane and shut it on the ground.* About the first solo circuit, how the airplane had gone light when Tom stepped out, lighter than anything, and how Hazel had lifted off and the whole valley had opened beneath him like a book to its first page. About landing and realizing his hands were shaking and his mouth wouldn't stop grinning and that he had, in the space of twenty minutes, become a person who could not be anything else.

"And your brother?" Mara asked, gently, because she always asked gently even when the question had edges.

"Jonah wanted to fly too. Never got the license. Said he'd get around to it." Hazel looked at his hands. "He rode along instead. That day in the pass — the downdraft, the load, my fault more than the weather's, though I spent ten years telling myself it was the weather. After that I decided attachments were cargo. Cargo shifts. Cargo kills. You fly light or you don't land." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Turns out I was carrying the heaviest thing in the airplane the whole time. Just couldn't see it on the weight-and-balance sheet."

Mara leaned her head against his shoulder. Dirt from the garden transferred to his shirt and he decided it could stay there indefinitely.

"The mountain took your price," she said. "It doesn't get to take the flying too. That part comes back in the spring."

"And if the review board says no?"

"Then you teach me to fly," she said, "and we'll be the worst two pilots in the territory together."

* * *

Winter settled in the way it did on the coast — not falling so much as arriving, gray and patient, moving into the house like a relative with no stated departure date. Hazel discovered what a man did with his hands when they weren't on a yoke, and the answer was: badly, at first.

He rebuilt the woodstove's flue because it needed it, and then rebuilt it again because the first attempt smoked the kitchen like a kipper house and Mara had stood in the doorway fanning herself with a newspaper, coughing, saying, "This is either very cozy or a fire hazard and I genuinely cannot tell." He split wood until the pile behind the shack looked like a fortress. He fixed Mrs. Calloway's roof two doors down because she fed him stew and asked no questions, which after that autumn was the finest currency he knew. He walked the beach below the field at low tide, all the way to the point and back, and caught himself doing the thing he'd always done — converting distance into flight time. Three miles. Two minutes in the Otter. Then he'd stop himself, and make himself walk it, and feel the ground do what ground does, which is arrive honestly, footfall by footfall.

Some nights the dreams came. The glacier yawning open. The sound the lode made when it collapsed — not a roar, really, more a long exhalation, like the mountain finally breathing out after holding its breath since the ice age. Forty-one holes. Crane's voice on the radio, smooth as varnish: *walk away rich and clean, Oyelaran.* In the dream he always answered the same way he'd answered in life — *I'm already walking, and I'm not clean, and none of it's yours* — and then the bullets came and he woke with Mara's hand already on his chest, because she'd learned the rhythm of his nights the way she'd learned the survey routes, by heart, in the dark.

"They buried it," she murmured one such night, half asleep herself, her forehead against his shoulder blade. "The mountain has it back. Whatever it was, it was the mountain's before it was anyone's. We just returned it."

"Expensive return postage."

"The best kind," she said. "The kind you pay once."

In December the first real storm came down off the range and the coast town pulled in behind its breakwater like a boat at anchor. Power failed for two days. They played cards by oil lamp — she cheated, flagrantly and without shame, and he pretended not to notice because calling her on it ended the game and the game was the best part of the evening. She told him stories about the survey team that she'd been keeping close, the living ones now, the ones that hurt good: Perrault teaching everyone a card game from Marseille and losing spectacularly; Adeyemi singing in the tent with a voice that shouldn't have come from a man that quiet; Lindqvist's terrible instant coffee, drunk anyway, ceremonially, every morning at six. Hazel listened and understood she was doing what he'd done with Jonah — walking the road once more, all the way to the end, so it could be a road and not a wound.

On New Year's Eve the fog lifted off the harbor at midnight and for about four minutes the sky cleared enough to show stars, hard and close, the winter kind. They stood on the porch with blankets around their shoulders and breath smoking, and somewhere down the bay someone set off flares — fishermen, probably, celebrating — red sparks climbing and dying over the black water.

"That's cheating," Mara said. "Those aren't fireworks, those are distress signals."

"Best use I ever saw made of them."

She laughed, and the laugh went up into the cold air, and Hazel thought: *there it is.* Not the miles. Not the years. This. The thing the mountain couldn't take, standing beside him wearing his spare blanket, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and engine grease because she'd been helping him tinker with the generator that afternoon and had gotten into it with both hands the way she got into everything.

* * *

In late January, a letter came that wasn't from the government. It was from the air service in Tern Harbor, sixty miles south — a outfit called Brannick & Sons, two beat-up Cubs and a Beaver older than both sons combined. Old Brannick wrote in pencil, in handwriting like fence wire: *Heard what you did. Heard what it cost. We need a pilot come thaw. Pay's poor, planes are worse, weather is weather. No inquiries pending. Interested?*

Hazel read it three times. Then he set it on the table under the lamp and stared at it for a long while, the way you stare at a door you thought was painted shut.

Mara came in with an armload of firewood, saw his face, saw the letter, and read it upside down across the table without missing a beat.

"Tern Harbor," she said. "That's mail runs."

"That's mail runs."

"That's the same job you had."

"It's a different valley," he said. "Different mountains. Worse coffee, if Brannick's reputation holds." He rubbed his jaw. "It's starting over, is what it is. At fifty-one. With a suspended ticket and a airplane-shaped hole in my bank account."

Mara put the wood down, came around the table, and stood looking at him with that expression she had — the geologist's expression, the one that read strata, that saw the long record underneath the surface weather.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" she said. "The pass. Six tides. Everyone said it couldn't be flown before the melt — Crane said it, the fleet bet on it, the whole arithmetic of that race said we'd be too late. And you flew it anyway, because you'd read that valley your whole life and you knew something about it the numbers didn't." She tapped the letter with one finger. "This is a tide too. It goes out. It always goes out. The question isn't whether the water leaves, Hazel. It's whether you're ready when it does."

He looked at the letter a long moment more. Outside, the January light was failing early the way it did, blue shadows pooling between the houses, and somewhere down the street Mrs. Calloway was beating a rug with the metronomic violence of a woman who had outlived two husbands and intended to outlive the rug besides. Ordinary sounds. Ground-level sounds. Sounds a man heard when he wasn't a thousand feet above everything, and which, he was slowly discovering, were not a demotion. They were just a different altitude.

"I'd want to see the Beaver first," he said. "Brannick's planes are legendary in the worst way."

"Naturally."

"And the pay's poor."

"Noted."

"And if the review board clears me in the spring, I'll want my own ticket back eventually. My own ship, someday. I'm not pretending otherwise."

"I'd worry about you if you did pretend otherwise," Mara said. She sat down across from him, took the pencil stub lying by the lamp — Brannick's pencil, shipped with the letter like a man who planned for everything except ink — and slid it across the table to him. "Well, pilot. Paper's right there."

Hazel picked up the pencil. His hand, he noticed, was perfectly steady — steadier, in fact, than it had been in years, as if some tremor he'd been carrying since the pass, since the crash, since Jonah, had finally worked its way out through his fingers and gone.

He wrote: *Interested. When does the ice go out?*

And below it, after a moment, because honesty had gotten to be a habit he couldn't shake: *Two of us.*

Mara read it, and smiled — not the careful smile she wore for boards of inquiry and men in gray suits, but the other one, the one from the ghost town stove, the one that had gotten him through six tides of melting pass — and she reached over and added a postscript in her quick surveyor's hand:

*She navigates.*

Outside, far to the north, past the harbor lights and the darkening water, the range stood white against the last of the sky, and the summit caught the fading sun and held it a moment longer than anywhere else — the way it always had, the way it would tomorrow, keeping its own counsel, keeping its price, letting the two of them keep everything else.
