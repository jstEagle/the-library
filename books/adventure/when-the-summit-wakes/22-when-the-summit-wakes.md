## 22. When the Summit Wakes

The mail plane was a rental, and it showed.

Hazel ran his hand along the door frame of the old Cessna 180 and felt the paint gone rough where someone had sanded out a repair instead of doing it right. The seat springs had opinions about his spine. There was a coffee stain on the headliner shaped like a country that didn't exist, and the radio crackled with a static that sounded less like weather than like gossip.

"You're judging her," Mara said from the strip's edge, two mail sacks over one shoulder like they weighed nothing, which they did not.

"I'm *assessing* her."

"You're holding a grudge against every airplane that isn't yours."

He let that sit for a second, because it was true, and because a year was long enough now that he could hold the truth of it without it cutting. "She'll do," he said. "She'll do."

That was the whole review. Mara grinned and heaved the sacks through the cargo door, and Hazel climbed in after them and went through the preflight the way he always had — slow, deliberate, touching each thing as if introducing himself to it. Fuel caps. Control surfaces. Tires. A year ago this ritual had been grief dressed up as procedure; flying other people's airplanes on other people's schedules while the inquiry ground through its paperwork and some clerk stamped SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW across the only identity he'd ever wanted. Now it was just what he did before dawn with a woman who read water better than most men read maps, and if that wasn't a life, he didn't know what the word was for.

The strip at Corvane Landing was still soft from spring thaw, frost steaming off the gravel in the first gray light. He taxied out, ran up the engine, listened to it argue with itself and settle. Behind him Mara had the mail manifest open on her knee, pencil tucked behind her ear, boots up on the baggage compartment like she owned the place. She'd started doing that about three months in, and he'd stopped minding about two weeks later.

"Fourteen stops," she said. "Two of them are just a cabin and a dog."

"Those are my favorite customers."

"The dogs?"

"The cabins. The dogs bite."

"Only when you flirt with their owners."

"I have never once flirted with an owner."

"You winked at Mrs. Aldous at Miller Bend."

"That was a squint. I had sun in my eyes. It was November."

"It was snowing." She marked something on the manifest with great ceremony. "Noted. Flirtation, confirmed, witnessed by one witness."

He laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone in a cockpit the way he used to be alone everywhere — and pushed the throttle forward, and the Cessna gathered herself up off the gravel and into the pale air, and the range came up to meet them.

* * *

They flew the same corridor he'd flown for eleven years, but nothing above ten thousand feet looked the same to him anymore. That was the thing nobody warned you about: you can survive a mountain, do everything right, walk away with your skin and your license eventually restored and your name cleared enough for government work — and the mountains will still keep the receipt. Every peak past the divide had a memory stapled to it now. That saddle was where the convoy's lead plane had come out of the sun. That canyon floor was where he'd flown so low the willows combed his wheels. And there, dead ahead, white and patient under the climbing light: the glacier.

It looked quiet. That was the trick of it. Glaciers always look quiet, the way sleeping men look harmless.

Mara had gone still beside him. He felt it more than saw it — the particular silence she made when the past stood up in front of the windshield. He didn't say anything. Neither did she. They flew toward it together because the mail route went that way, and because neither of them had ever once suggested going around, and because going around would have been its own kind of confession.

The summit caught the dawn while they were still twenty miles out.

It happened the way it always happened up there, sudden and total — the sun clearing the eastern wall and striking the high face of the peak, and the whole mountain going from gray to gold in the space of a breath, like something under the ice opening one eye. Hazel had seen it a thousand times. He'd built a whole philosophy on it once, back when his philosophy fit inside a cockpit: miles, not years; keep moving; never look too long at anything beautiful, because looking is how you start counting what you'd lose.

"Look at that," Mara said softly. Not a request. Not quite a command. Just a fact she was handing him.

And neither of them looked away.

That was the whole difference, he thought. A year ago he'd have glanced, logged it, moved on. Now he let it hit him full in the face and kept flying straight through the middle of the light, and the mountain took its look at him right back, and neither party flinched.

"There it is," he said. "Still showing off."

"She has reason to."

Below them, the glacier lay smooth and unbroken, its secret sealed under forty feet of collapsed ice and burned survey paper and the honest arithmetic of fuel charges. No lode. No syndicate. No Crane — Crane was eighteen months into a federal sentence and would be for years, though the name above his had never surfaced, and Mara said that was how it always worked, the big fish swimming away while the little ones flopped on the dock. The families of the survey team had their answers, or most of them. The mountain had its price paid in full. And the ice, indifferent as arithmetic, had healed over everything like skin.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Mara said.

"That you should have taken Crane's offer and we'd both be rich?"

"That too, daily." She turned in her seat to look at him, and the dawn was in her eyes, and he had to consciously return his attention to the instruments, which she noticed, and enjoyed. "I keep thinking about the others. Okafor. Lindqvist. The boys. How they'd feel about all this — the glacier shut, the company broken open, me drinking bad coffee in a rented plane delivering catalogues to trappers."

"What do you think they'd feel?"

She considered it honestly, the way she considered everything, turning it over like a core sample. "I think they'd want to know we flew the mail," she said finally. "I think that's the part they'd understand. Everything else got complicated. But somebody's medicine getting somewhere by morning — that part stays simple no matter what else happens."

Hazel nodded and didn't trust his voice for a moment. Eleven years he'd told himself the flying was simple, and it hadn't been — it had been armor. Now it actually was simple, and it turned out simple was heavier and better than armor ever was.

* * *

The first stop was Miller Bend, which was indeed a cabin and a dog, and the dog was named Admiral and bit Hazel on the second visit of every year without fail, punctually, like a tide table. This year Hazel crouched down and offered his forearm with the resignation of long experience, and Admiral considered it at length and then, unprecedentedly, licked it instead.

"He's getting old," Mara said.

"He's getting *soft*."

"He's getting fed by Mrs. Aldous, who you definitely don't wink at."

The cabin's owner came out with the mail sack already waiting and news already loaded: the river road washed out again at the narrows, the Hendersons' new baby weighed nine pounds, and the government man who'd been asking questions around the coast last month had moved on to questions somewhere else. That last item landed quietly between them, the way such items did. Not every thread had been cut clean. Some names had survived the inquiry the way seeds survive winter. But the questions were elsewhere now, and elsewhere was a direction, and directions were flyable.

"Anything for us?" Mara asked lightly.

"Nothing with your names on it," the trapper said, and handed over a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and that was the whole exchange, and it was enough.

Back in the air, Mara opened the parcel out of pure professional curiosity and found it contained, of all things, a jar of wild cranberry preserves with a note: *For the pilot and his geologist. Heard you two finally got the same address.* 

"We have an address," Hazel said, testing the words.

"We have a lease. With a woodstove that smokes and a roof that sings."

"And a neighbor who leaves us preserves. That's an address. That's practically a nation."

She laughed and put the jar in the map case, official government storage, and for a while they just flew. Fourteen stops became twelve became eight. The sacks lightened. The day warmed. Somewhere over the third valley Hazel caught himself humming, tunelessly, and Mara joined in worse, and the radio static gossiped, and the mountains rolled by beneath them wearing their year of new snow like nothing had ever been buried anywhere at all.

* * *

It was on the leg home, low over the river valley with the evening coming on gold, that the engine coughed.

Once. Twice. A third time with a metallic rasp underneath that Hazel felt in his teeth before he heard it in his ears.

Both of them went silent in the way of people who had been here before — not panic, exactly, but the body remembering. His hands were already moving: mixture, gauges, ears cocked. Oil pressure fine. Fuel fine. Temperatures fine. The engine settled back into its rhythm like a man clearing his throat mid-sentence.

"Rented airplane," Mara said carefully.

"Rented airplane."

"That sounded expensive."

"That sounded like a rod bearing thinking about its future." He watched the gauges another full minute. Steady. The Cessna droned on, faithful as anything rented could be. "We'll put her down at Corvane and I'm writing the owner a letter that will singe his eyebrows."

But his heart was going harder than the situation warranted, and he knew why, and he suspected she knew why, and somewhere over the bend in the river she reached across and rested her hand on his knee and left it there, and neither of them said anything about the Long Glide, because they didn't need to. Some landings you carry with you. Some silences in an engine you hear forever after.

Then the engine coughed again — longer this time, a stuttering loss and catch — and the oil pressure needle began, gently, horribly, to lean.

"Hazel."

"I see it."

There was a gravel bar ahead. Of course there was; the river had been laying them out every half mile since the divide, flat and gray and patient, the same kind of bar where a different plane had set them down a year ago and given them back their lives on foot. He didn't even have to think about it. That was the strange grace of it — the decision arrived already made, the way the best ones always did. Carb heat. Mixture. Flaps on schedule. Mara reading the water below, calling the glassy stretch, calling the wind off the far bank, her voice level as a spirit bubble.

"Little left," she said. "Now."

The wheels touched gravel with a sound like applause, and the Cessna rolled out fifty yards and stopped, ticking, steaming slightly, dead as a museum piece on the bar where the river bent.

Silence. Water sounds. A raven somewhere upstream making its opinion known.

Hazel sat with his hands on the wheel and breathed, and then laughed — a short, cracked, entirely involuntary laugh — and Mara laughed too, helplessly, the two of them sitting in a dying rented airplane on the exact species of gravel bar that had become, apparently, their tradition.

"One year," she said. "One year to the week."

"I checked the calendar. It's four days early."

"Close enough. We should mark it. Annual Gravel Bar Day."

"Annual Engine Failure Day, you mean."

"Same thing, apparently. It's our anniversary either way." She said it lightly, and then it hung there in the cockpit, not lightly at all, and he turned and looked at her and she looked back and neither of them blinked, the way they hadn't blinked at the summit that morning, the way they'd stopped blinking at each other somewhere around the ghost town stove and never started again.

"Mara Voss," Hazel said, "will you marry me on a gravel bar, because I refuse to spend money on a venue."

She stared at him. Then she looked around the cockpit — the stained headliner, the dead gauges, the oil pressure needle lying flat in surrender — and back at him, and her eyes were bright and furious with laughing.

"You absolute cheapskate," she said. "You couldn't wait until we walked out? You couldn't even get me to a *cabin*?"

"The mountain keeps its price," Hazel said. "I'm keeping you. I figured we'd do the paperwork where we do everything else."

And Mara Voss, disgraced geologist, survivor of the ice, keeper of buried secrets and duplicate notes and one very good recipe for bad coffee, leaned across the dead instrument panel and kissed him while the river went on talking and the raven complained and the last light came down the valley and lit the peaks behind them gold, one more time, like the summit waking — like it had been watching the whole year, and approved.

They walked out the next morning, hand in hand, carrying the mail.

Because somebody's medicine had to get somewhere by morning. That part stayed simple.
