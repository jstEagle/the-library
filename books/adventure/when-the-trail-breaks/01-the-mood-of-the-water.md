## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Verrin ran low and green that morning, the way it did in late summer when the mountain snow had given up everything it meant to give, and Freya Pemberton stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with one boot hooked on the rail and a cup of chicory going cold in her hand. Twelve years she'd run this river. She knew its moods the way other women knew their husbands' — which is to say better than they'd admit, and with more forgiveness than either deserved.

"Captain." Tomas came up from the cargo deck wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white and was now the color of every job he'd ever done. "We got a problem."

"We always have a problem. That's what a boat is. A problem that floats."

"This one's a good problem." He leaned against the rail beside her, a big man gone soft at the edges in the way that made him look comfortable rather than old. "The Halloran sisters sent down a crate of preserved plums with the last shipment. Said to tell you it's for the crew. Said it plain, too, like they thought you might hoard it."

"I would have."

"You see? Honesty. The river respects it." He plucked the cold cup from her hand, drank, made a face at the chicory, drank again anyway. "Plums for the crew. And Pell's been below since dawn pretending he isn't starving, which means we feed him before he starts eating the caulking again."

"He didn't eat the caulking."

"He ate *some* caulking. I counted the seams."

Freya smiled despite herself, which was Tomas's particular talent — not making jokes exactly, but making the space where jokes could live, so that even a morning of freight manifests and low water felt like something worth waking for. She took her cup back. Below them, the Kestrel's engine turned over with its familiar uneven heartbeat, a sound she trusted more than most people.

The Kestrel was forty years old if she was a day, a stern-wheeler built for hauling timber and now hauling whatever paid: salt pork, lamp oil, bolts of cloth, coffins occasionally, though nobody liked to say so out loud. She was patched in six places and beautiful in none of them, and Freya loved her the way you love a thing that has carried you through grief without asking questions.

"Ines up yet?"

"Since four. She's redrawing the bend at Cutter's Shoal. Says the charts are wrong."

"The charts are always wrong. The river moves."

"She says the river doesn't move, Captain. She says the *charts* moved. There's a difference, apparently, and she explained it to me for an hour while I was trying to sleep, so now you get to hear about it too."

* * *

Ines worked at the little table in the pilothouse, her husband's drafting tools laid out in their worn leather roll with the reverence other people gave to saints' bones. She was small and precise and had the kind of face that looked like it was listening to something far away, which in a way it always was. Aldous had been dead three years now, drowned in a survey accident upriver, and Ines had taken his unfinished atlas of the Verrin and made it her own — correcting, extending, arguing with his margins in pencil.

"The shoal's shifted forty yards east," she said without looking up when Freya came in. "Aldous charted it here—" she tapped a fine ink line, "—but the sediment tells another story. Look at the willow growth on the point. New. Five years, maybe less. The current's cutting a new channel behind it."

"So we go wide."

"So you go wide, and then you write to the Survey Office and tell them their map would drown a man, and they'll write back that Aldous Delacroix—"

"Marrow," Freya said gently. "Ines Marrow. Different name entirely."

"—that the widow's corrections are unsolicited and unverified." Ines set down her pen. Her hands were steady; her jaw was not. "I know what they write back, Freya. I've gotten eleven letters."

"And you've corrected eleven maps anyway."

"Somebody has to know where the water actually goes." She said it lightly, but Freya heard the weight under it, the same weight she carried herself: the dead leave work behind, and the living do it, and that is the only monument anybody gets.

From the corner of the pilothouse came a rustle, followed by a silence that was trying very hard to be innocent.

"Pell," Freya said, not turning around.

Nothing.

"Pell, I can see your reflection in the brass."

A boy unfolded himself from behind the chart cabinet — fourteen maybe, all elbows and hollow cheeks, hair the color of river mud, wearing a coat two sizes too big that had clearly been someone's funeral coat once. He had the specific stillness of a creature that has learned to survive by not being noticed.

"I wasn't stealing anything," he said.

"No. You were admiring the cabinet."

"It's a nice cabinet."

"It is." Freya crossed to the shelf under the wheel, where a covered dish sat, and lifted the cloth. Bread, hard cheese, half a pear going soft. She set it on the table in front of him. "Tomas says you've been below since dawn."

"I was working."

"Eating the caulking isn't work."

Pell looked at the food the way some men look at cards they're afraid to bet on. Then hunger won, as it always did, and he ate with the desperate efficiency of somebody who believed every meal might be his last negotiation. Freya watched him and felt the old ache shift in her chest, the one shaped like a boy who wasn't this boy, who had never gotten the chance to eat anything ever again.

She didn't say any of that. She said, "You want work, there's work. Cargo lines need coiling on the foredeck. Tomas will show you the bowline knot for the ninth time."

"He showed me eight times."

"Then today makes nine, and by winter you'll pretend you always knew it, and he'll pretend to believe you. That's how families work."

Pell stopped chewing. It was the smallest hitch, barely a breath, but Freya saw it land — the word *families*, dropped carelessly into the room like a coin into deep water, still sinking. He ducked his head and ate faster, and said nothing, and she let it go. Twelve years of running a river had taught her that some things you catch and some things you let drift past, and knowing which was which was the whole art of it.

* * *

They made Cutter's Shoal by midafternoon, wide around the new channel just as Ines promised, and tied up at Marlow's Landing with the sun still high enough to count as respectable. The landing smelled of tar and frying onions and the particular sweetness of a river town that knows it exists only because the water permits it.

The Company man was waiting on the dock.

Freya spotted him before he spoke, because Company men dressed differently — cleaner, harder, with boots that had never known honest mud. This one was young, polite in the way of a knife in a drawer, and he held a leather folder against his chest like a hymnal.

"Captain Pemberton?" He smiled. "Merrick Corven, Verrin Consolidated Mining and Navigation. I understand you run regular freight downstream."

"I run whatever's legal and some things that are merely tolerated."

"Then you'll want to hear this." He opened the folder. "The Company's offering a standing contract. Priority cargo, sealed crates, no questions asked. Triple your current rates. Monthly runs, guaranteed tonnage, and a bonus at year's end that would buy you a new boat outright."

Around them the dock went quiet in the way docks do — not silent, but attentive. Tomas had materialized at her shoulder. Ines stood in the pilothouse window, watching.

"Sealed crates," Freya said.

"Company property. Sensitive equipment."

"Going downstream."

"Downstream."

"Nothing comes *up* from the mines worth sealing," Freya said. "So these crates are going to somebody downstream who pays for silence. Which means the contents are either stolen or dangerous, and either way the triple rate is buying my discretion, not my labor."

Corven's smile held. "Captain, the Company employs half this valley. Half the valley eats because we dig."

"And the other half eats because I haul their goods honestly. Funny how the halves balance." She stepped back toward her gangplank. "I'll pass. Tell your office the Kestrel doesn't carry sealed crates, and tell them I said why."

"They'll remember that."

"They usually do." She met his eyes. "That's rather the point."

He closed the folder without hurry, and something in the unhurriedness of it frightened her more than a threat would have. "Enjoy your river, Captain. It's a long one."

He walked off down the dock, boots too clean, and the dock exhaled and went back to being itself.

"Tomas," Freya said quietly.

"Yeah?"

"When we cast off, take the long way round the mooring buoys. I want to see if anyone follows."

"You think he'll mark us?"

"I think men like that don't ask twice. They file." She looked upriver, where the water came down green and patient out of the high country, out of places no charter boat went, out of the country above the falls where her father had gone thirty years ago and never come back, and where the Company's maps went blank as deliberately as a man closing his eyes.

"Ache anywhere?" Tomas asked, meaning the refusal, meaning the money, meaning all of it.

"Everywhere. It always does." She turned for the gangplank. "Feed the boy. Coil the lines. We run at first light."

* * *

Evening came down gold on the water, and the Kestrel sat easy at her moorings while the crew ate plums on the foredeck out of the Halloran sisters' crate, passing the jar around like communion. Pell ate three and pretended he hadn't. Ines sketched the landing lights in the margin of her atlas, where Aldous's handwriting still filled the edges with notes in a dead man's careful hand. Tomas told a story about a customs inspector and a goat that Freya had heard nine times and would hear nine more, gladly.

This, she thought. This is the whole of it. A boat, a river, these three. Everything else is weather.

She was almost asleep when Pell appeared at the foot of the stairs to the pilothouse, barefoot, holding something behind his back with the guilty geometry of a boy who has found a thing he shouldn't have.

"Captain?" His voice was strange. Too careful. "Can I ask you something about the tide?"

Freya sat up. Downriver, the water against the hull had gone quiet — quieter than quiet, the Kestrel settling on her lines in a way she only did when the river beneath her was doing something it had no business doing.

"What about the tide, Pell?"

He brought his hand out from behind his back. In his palm lay a small brass key, green with age, stamped with a name she hadn't heard spoken aloud in thirty years.

It read **PEMBERTON**.
