## 2. What the Tide Gave Back

The river woke Pell before the sun did.

That was the thing about sleeping aboard the Kestrel — she talked all night. Water against the hull, rope creaking in its cleats, Tomas snoring in the fo'c'sle like a man arguing with God and losing on points. Pell had learned to read all of it the way other boys read letters. So when the river went silent in the grey hour before dawn, he sat straight up in his nest of cargo netting behind the wheelhouse and knew, before he knew why, that something was wrong.

Then the world shrugged.

Not a sound so much as a feeling — a long, low grumble from under the earth, like a giant rolling over in bed and deciding it wasn't done sleeping. The lantern swung on its hook. A tin cup skittered across the deck and went over the side with a plink. Somewhere downstream, a dog started barking and would not stop.

Pell scrambled to the rail and looked over.

The Verrin River was leaving.

He'd never seen water move backwards before, not really — not the little sucking of a wave pulling out, but the whole river, wide and brown and certain, sliding away from the bank like a tablecloth drawn from under the dishes. Mudflats appeared where there had been fathoms. A sunken skiff he'd never known existed rose up dripping, its ribs bare as a fish skeleton. The pilings of the old ferry landing stood a man's height taller than they had at supper, wearing skirts of black weed.

"Captain!" he yelled, and then remembered nobody else was awake, and felt the word sit warm in his chest anyway. *Captain.* He'd been calling her that for three weeks now, ever since she'd caught him in the coal bunker outside Vell's Landing and hadn't thrown him off. "Captain, the river's going!"

No answer. Freya slept like a stone with a grudge. Tomas slept like two stones. Only Ines was ever up early, and her lamp in the map room was dark.

Pell stood at the rail with his heart going like a paddle wheel and watched the river give back its secrets.

Because that's what it was doing. He understood that much, the way you understand a story without knowing how. The Verrin was old, older than the Company, older than the towns, and she'd swallowed a hundred years of things — boats, tools, coins, men — and now, for whatever reason, she'd decided to open her mouth and show them.

And there, where the current had always run hardest against the north bluff, where the water had always boiled and hissed like something being cooked —

There was a door.

* * *

Pell had heard the legends the way he'd heard everything since coming aboard: secondhand, half-believed, delivered over card games and dishwater. Old Marta at the Vell's Landing dock had told it best, with her pipe going and her eyes going somewhere else.

"The Door of Salt," she'd said. "In the north bluff, where the river runs backwards twice a day. The old folk said the river kept a door and charged admission. You want to go in, you pay. And the price was never money."

"What was it, then?" Tomas had asked, because Tomas always asked, mostly to be contrary.

"Something you'd miss," Marta said, and tapped her pipe out, and would say no more.

Now Pell stood at the rail and looked at it. A cave mouth, black and high as a barn, set in the bluff where no cave had ever been marked on any chart Ines owned — and Pell had looked through every chart she owned, twice, out of boredom and hunger for the world. The water had drained away from it in a long, smooth tongue, leaving a floor of wet gravel running right up to the dark.

The legends said the river charged admission.

Pell was an orphan with empty pockets. He'd been paying admission to things his whole life — paying with hunger, mostly, and with the particular loneliness of a boy nobody claimed. He figured the river couldn't charge him anything he hadn't already paid.

He told himself that was why he went.

He told himself a lot of things, climbing down the ladder at first light, his bare feet finding the cold gravel. He told himself he'd just look. He told himself he'd be back before Freya woke, and she'd never know, and if she did know, well — she hadn't thrown him off at Vell's Landing, had she? There was a soft spot in that hard woman. He'd seen it. He intended to keep finding it.

The mud sucked at his heels. The morning smelled of river rot and old stone, a smell like the inside of a coin purse. Gulls — where had gulls come from? — wheeled and screamed over the exposed flats, feasting on whatever the river had kept.

At the mouth of the cave he stopped. The dark inside didn't look like an absence. It looked like a thing with weight, sitting there, patient. Water dripped somewhere deep, keeping time.

"Hello," Pell said, because his mother had raised him polite for the six years she'd had him, and because it seemed the sort of door you ought to knock at.

The dark said nothing, which Pell took as an invitation.

* * *

Inside, the world changed.

The cave floor was gravel at first, then stone, worn smooth and slick, and the walls opened out above him faster than he'd expected, so that after fifty paces he was walking through a hall rather than a tunnel. Light came in behind him in a grey wedge and died about ten paces in, and beyond that he was feeling his way with his fingertips, along walls that were not cold the way stone should be cold. They were cool, and faintly damp, and under his palm they felt — he couldn't think of a better word — *attentive*.

The salt smell grew. That was the name of it, then. The whole place breathed salt, old and mineral, like tears kept in a jar for a hundred years.

His fingers found a groove in the wall. Then another. Regular, deliberate, cut by tools. He followed them, and the grooves became letters, and the letters were old and half-filled with mineral crust, and he couldn't read them in the dark, but he could feel the care in them. Somebody had wanted this wall to say something. Somebody had spent hours saying it.

Pell had a match, one match, hoarded from Vell's Landing in a twist of oilcloth. He stood in the dark a long time, weighing it. One match was a fortune. One match was light, was warmth, was a small piece of the world you could hold.

He struck it.

The flame stood up straight and gold, and the cave leaned in around it, and Pell forgot to breathe.

The walls were *marked*. Not graffiti, not the crude scratchings of bored boatmen — survey marks, fine and exact, lines and figures and little careful symbols, running in ranks up the stone like a ledger written on the world. Numbers. Dates. He could read some of the dates: they went back forty years and more. And among them, repeated in a fine, disciplined hand, a set of initials he didn't know, and a word he did:

*Pemberton.*

The match burned down toward his fingers. He cupped it, watched the letters swim, watched the name stand out of the stone the way a face stands out of a crowd.

Pemberton. Like the captain. Freya Pemberton, who never talked about her father except once, late at night, when she thought Pell was asleep and she was talking to the wheel, telling it the river was high and her father would have known what to do about it.

The match bit his fingertips and he hissed and dropped it, and the dark came back down like a lid.

He stood there blind, heart hammering, and thought: *the river charges admission, and I've paid, and now it's showing me what I came for.*

His fingers found the wall again and followed the marks. There was a niche at knee height, cut square and deliberate, the kind of place a workman leaves something he means to come back for. Pell's hand went in. His fingers touched dust, and grit, and then — metal. Small. Cold. Shaped.

He drew it out and held it up to the grey wedge of light from the cave mouth, turning it.

A key. Brass, heavy for its size, green with age at the teeth but sound, sound as the day it was cut. And stamped into the bow, in letters small and sure:

*E. PEMBERTON.*

Pell sat down right there on the wet stone, because his legs had opinions of their own, and held the key in both hands like a bird he was afraid of crushing. E. Pemberton. Elias Pemberton, he'd heard Ines say once, very quietly, when she thought no one was listening. The captain's father, who'd gone up the river twenty years ago and never come down, and whom the towns called a deserter and the captain called nothing at all, ever, out loud, to anyone.

And here was his name, cut into the Door of Salt, and here was his key, waiting in a niche like a letter in a slot, like something left for whoever was meant to find it.

*Me,* Pell thought, and then felt foolish, and then didn't. Somebody had left this. Somebody was meant to find it. The river had drained itself dry and shown a stowaway boy a door, and maybe that wasn't fate, maybe it was just tide and earthquake and luck, but sitting in the dark with the brass warm now from his hands, Pell believed it was meant, and being meant was the finest thing that had ever happened to him.

He put the key on the cord around his neck, under his shirt, against his skin. It was cold and then it wasn't.

* * *

He was halfway back to the light when he heard the water.

It started as a sound like a distant crowd — a long, low murmur from the deep of the cave, behind him, below him, in the walls themselves. Then it became a rush. Then it became a roar, and the floor under Pell's bare feet began to hum.

He ran.

The grey wedge of daylight at the cave mouth had changed while he was inside — he saw that at once, and his stomach dropped through the gravel and kept going. It was higher. The tongue of gravel that had led him up to the door was shorter, drowning inch by inch, and beyond it the river was coming *back*, sliding in over the flats in long silver sheets, fast as a horse at a canter, filling the channel it had emptied.

The tide had turned.

Only it hadn't. The Verrin wasn't tidal, not this far up — Ines had said so, had said it like a law of the world, *no tide above the Split, boy, the sea gave up its claim at the Split* — and yet the water was rising, and rising fast, and the legends said the river charged admission and the price was something you'd miss, and Pell thought with a cold, clear horror: *it's charging me now. I looked. I took. It wants its price.*

He hit the gravel at a run and the water hit him at the same moment, ankle-deep, then knee-deep, shockingly cold, dragging at his legs like hands. The flats that had been dry at dawn were a moving lake. The sunken skiff was gone again, swallowed whole. The Kestrel floated fifty yards out, riding higher now as the river climbed her hull, and nobody on her deck, nobody awake, nobody knowing where he was.

Pell fought for the ladder, gasping, the current shoving him sideways, the key a small hard heart against his chest. He got a hand on the lowest rung, then a foot, and the water tore at him and he clung, and climbed, one rung and another, the river rising up the ladder after him like something climbing too.

He hauled himself over the rail and lay on the deck, soaked and shaking, and looked back at the bluff.

The Door of Salt was closing. Not all at once — the water rose up its mouth in a smooth black sheet, inch by inch, patient as a shopkeeper sweeping a step — until the last grey light of the cave went out under the river, and the current ran hard against the north bluff again, boiling and hissing, as though nothing had ever been shown at all.

Pell knelt at the rail, dripping, and pulled the cord from around his neck.

The key lay in his palm, brass and certain, stamped with a dead man's name. E. Pemberton. The captain's father. The river had given it to him, and the river had tried to keep him for the price, and he didn't know which of those frightened him more.

Behind him, the wheelhouse door banged open. Freya Pemberton stood in the frame in her shirtsleeves, hair unbound, one hand already on the wheel, her eyes going from the flooded deck to the boy sprawled at her rail to the north bluff where the current boiled over a door that was not on any chart she owned.

"What," she said, in the voice she used on storms and Company men, "did you do?"

Pell closed his fist around the key and thought: *I paid the admission. Now I have to tell her what it bought.*

"The river showed me something, Captain," he said. "It showed me your name."
