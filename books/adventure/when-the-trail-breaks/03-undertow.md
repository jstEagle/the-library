## 3. Undertow

The river had been misbehaving all morning, and Freya Pemberton did not like it one bit.

She'd said as much to Tomas over breakfast — a plate of fried eggs gone rubbery at the edges because the galley stove had developed opinions about heat distribution — and he'd laughed at her through a mouthful of toast and told her rivers were allowed moods same as anybody.

"Rivers are allowed moods," she agreed. "I just prefer to know which mood before I'm standing in it."

Now she stood at the wheel with both hands wrapped around spokes worn smooth by twelve years of her own palms, watching the Verrin do something she had never once seen it do.

It was running backward.

Not all of it — not a tide reversal, nothing so explainable. The surface still slid downstream in its usual gray-green way, lazy and broad, but underneath, where the current should have pressed steady against the hull, there was a pull. A slow, patient tug, like something below had opened its hand and was drawing the Kestrel toward it finger by finger. The lead line confirmed what her boots already knew: the depth soundings were wrong. All of them. The bottom of the river had dropped away overnight, and the water above it had grown deep and cold and strangely quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

"Freya." Ines stood at the rail with her charts spread on the cargo hatch, weighted down with a tin cup, a spare belaying pin, and Pell's left boot, which he'd surrendered under protest that morning. "The soundings."

"I know."

"The soundings from yesterday put us over forty feet of gravel bar. Today I can't find the bottom." Ines's voice was level, the way it always was, the careful evenness of a woman who had spent years married to a man who measured things for a living and had learned that panic is just arithmetic done too fast. "And the banks have dropped six feet since dawn. Look at the mud lines."

Freya looked. The exposed banks glistened pale and raw, striped like a cut onion, roots hanging out of them in wet tangles. Whole trees leaned drunkenly over water they hadn't touched yesterday. Somewhere upstream, the river had lost a great deal of itself very quickly, and it was all coming down here now, gathering, looking for somewhere to go.

"Pell!" she called. "Get off the bow rail. If you go in after your boot I'm not fishing you out."

"I'm not *on* the rail," said Pell, who was absolutely on the rail, a skinny silhouette against the morning fog with his arms out for balance. He was eleven, or possibly thirteen; he'd stowed aboard eight months ago claiming to be fourteen, and nobody had corrected him because correcting him would mean admitting they'd known all along. He dropped down and came aft with his particular walk, half swagger, half apology. "Captain. Is the river supposed to look like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like it's *thinking*."

Freya opened her mouth to tell him rivers don't think, and then closed it again, because the water ahead of them had begun to move in a way that looked exactly like thinking. A long smooth seam had appeared across the channel, running bank to bank, and along that seam the current turned — visibly turned, the whole surface swinging like a weathervane in a gust — until it pointed not downstream but upriver, toward the cliffs, toward the place the old-timers called the Door of Salt and charged no admission because nobody had ever come back to pay.

"Tomas," Freya said quietly. "Engine."

"Already lit her," Tomas called back from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black so long it might have been born that way. He was a big man built like a bollard, with a laugh that carried across water and hands that could fix anything except, apparently, his own collar, which stood crooked every day of his life. "But Captain — I'll be honest with you, because I'm always honest with you about engines — if this pull gets any stronger, she's going to need more than me and a boiler."

"How much more?"

"A miracle and a longer towline."

The seam in the water reached them.

There was no drama to it, no roar, none of the violence Freya had braced for. That was the worst part, she thought later. It took hold of the Kestrel the way sleep takes hold of a tired child: gently, completely, without asking. One moment the boat answered her wheel; the next, the wheel answered the boat, spinning loose in her hands like a toy, and the Kestrel swung her head around and began to run upstream at a speed no steamboat of her tonnage had ever managed against the Verrin's current.

"Anchor!" she shouted. "Tomas, drop everything we own!"

Tomas didn't argue, which told her how bad it was. He threw the stern anchor first, then the spare, then — with a kind of gallows flourish — the galley's iron stove grate, and the Kestrel dragged all three of them across the water like a cat dragging a ribbon. The chains sang out and went taut and then, horribly, kept paying out, the winch brake smoking, the deck shuddering under their feet.

"She won't hold!"

"Then let go before it takes your arm off!"

He let go. The anchors vanished astern, taking forty fathoms of chain and Freya's composure with them, and the Kestrel ran on, upstream, into the fog, toward the cliffs.

"Ines!" Freya gripped the wheel uselessly, out of some stubborn belief that a captain should hold something. "How far?"

Ines had her chart pinned flat against the pilot house wall with both forearms, hair whipping loose from its knot, reading it the way other people read scripture. "Half a mile to the cliff face. The Door sits maybe ten feet above the old waterline — but the waterline's dropped, so—" She stopped. Recalculated. Her jaw tightened. "We're going to fit."

"We're going to *what*?"

"Fit. Through it." Ines looked up at her, and behind the fear in her face there was something else, something almost apologetic, as though the geography had failed her personally. "Freya, the mouth is thirty feet wide. We're fifty-two feet of boat. But the water's carrying us at the top of a swell, and if the swell holds—"

"Boats don't fit through doors, Ines."

"This one's going to."

Pell had wedged himself into the corner of the pilot house, both fists knotted in his shirt, and Freya saw him mouth the word *door* like a boy tasting a story. She wanted to send him below decks. There wasn't time, and besides, she suspected he wouldn't go, and she refused to spend what might be her last minutes as a captain ordering a stowaway about.

"Tomas! Get everyone away from the starboard side. If we clip the rock, it'll peel us like an orange."

"Aye, Captain." And then, because he was Tomas, and because fear made him funnier instead of quieter: "For the record, I'd like it noted I suggested turning back at breakfast."

"You suggested second helpings at breakfast."

"Same instinct. Self-preservation."

The cliffs rose out of the fog ahead, black and dripping, taller than anything had a right to be this far from the sea. And there — exactly where the legends put it, exactly where the drowned men of three generations had sworn it wasn't — the Door of Salt stood open. White mineral streaks ran down from its arch like old tears. The cave mouth breathed out cold air that smelled of stone and something older, something like the inside of the earth holding its breath, and the river poured into it in a smooth green hill of water, silent as grain into a chute.

Fifty-two feet of boat. Thirty feet of door.

"Hold on," Freya said, to all of them, to no one, to herself. "Hold on, hold on, hold on—"

The Kestrel hit the swell and climbed it. For one impossible moment they were airborne — Freya felt her stomach float free, heard Pell whoop despite himself, heard Ines say something in her late husband's language that was probably a prayer and possibly not — and then the world narrowed to white limestone on either side, close enough to touch, close enough to scrape the paint and the planking and everything they owned into the dark, and the Kestrel went through the Door of Salt with six inches to spare on each side and a sound like a held note played on a saw.

Then darkness. Then silence. Then the river let go.

* * *

They drifted.

That was all it was, afterward — drifting, in a cavern so vast their lantern light couldn't find its ceiling or its walls, the Kestrel turning slowly on black water so still it reflected the lantern flames back at them like a second sky. Somewhere ahead, faintly, the water moved. Somewhere behind—

Freya turned and looked back the way they'd come, and her chest went tight.

The Door of Salt was closing. Not slamming — settling, the way a jaw settles shut. The pale arch of it shrank against the last gray thread of daylight, and the water beneath it sank inch by inch, and then the light was a coin, and then a slit, and then nothing at all. The river behind them was gone. The river ahead was a whisper in the dark.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Even Pell, whose gift was filling silences the way other boys filled pockets, said nothing.

"Well," Tomas said finally, into the black. "On the bright side, no one can say we didn't make good time."

"Tomas."

"Yes, Captain."

"If you make one more joke in the next five minutes, I'm putting you ashore."

"Captain, respectfully, I don't believe there is an ashore anymore."

It was Ines who broke the spell — Ines, who had been standing at the rail with the lantern held high, turning slowly, reading the walls. Freya watched her go still in a new way. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of recognition.

"Bring the light here," Ines said. "Please."

Freya steered the Kestrel — such steering as still meant anything — toward the near wall, and the lantern found it: pale stone scored with lines. Straight lines. Measured lines. Numbers beside the lines, in a cramped, precise hand, marching off into the dark like a column of ants.

"What is it?" Pell whispered.

"It's a survey," Ines said. Her voice had changed. It had gone somewhere private. "Chain measurements. Elevation marks. This is professional work — this is *good* work." Her fingers hovered over the carved numbers without touching them, the way you hover over a sleeping face. "I know this hand."

Freya looked at her. "Ines."

"My husband surveyed the upper Verrin for six years." Ines's breath fogged in the cold air. "They told me he drowned doing it. They gave me his hat." She lifted the lantern higher, and the light climbed the wall, and the numbers climbed with it, and far above them, at the edge of the dark, another mark caught the flame — a small, deliberate symbol, drawn twice, crossed once, the signature of a man who signed his work twice because he believed maps were promises.

"He was here," Ines said. "Aldous was *here*. And whatever he found in this cave, Freya — someone went to a great deal of trouble to make sure the rest of us never would."

Ahead, in the black, the river whispered on — and beneath the whisper, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of oars.
