## 4. The Company's Men

Tomas had a theory about trouble, and the theory was this: trouble always arrived dressed better than you.

He was below decks, elbow-deep in the bilge pump's guts, when he heard the skiff. Not the engine — the *silence* after the engine. The Kestrel drifted in the long green throat of the river with the current gone strange and sluggish around them, and every bird that had been arguing in the willows since dawn shut its mouth at once. Birds knew things. Tomas had learned to listen to birds the way other men listened to their mothers-in-law: with respect and a certain dread.

He wiped his hands on a rag that had once been white, back in some innocent era of the world, and climbed the ladder to the deck.

The skiff came out of the side channel like a knife coming out of a drawer. Black hull, grey canopy, two men at the oars rowing in perfect unison, which told Tomas they were paid by the hour rather than by the catch. A third man stood at the bow with his hands folded behind him like a schoolmaster waiting for an excuse.

"Company," Pell said from behind the cargo netting, where he'd wedged himself with the instinct of a cat who has been kicked before and intends to be difficult about it next time too.

"I see it," Tomas said quietly. "Stay there. Stay small."

Freya was already at the wheel, arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore for bad weather and worse paperwork. Ines stood at the rail with her charts rolled under one arm like a weapon she hadn't decided on yet.

The skiff swung alongside, graceful as a tax notice. The man at the bow looked up. He was dry in a way that seemed deliberate — pressed shirt, polished boots, hair combed flat as if even his head disapproved of disorder. His eyes did a slow inventory of the Kestrel: the patched gunwale, the stacked crates, the crew. Tomas watched him count them. One, two, three — and then his gaze snagged on the netting where Pell was hiding, and lingered half a heartbeat too long, and moved on.

That linger was going to be a problem. Tomas filed it away.

"Morning," the man called up. His voice was pleasant the way ice is smooth. "Captain Pemberton?"

"No comment," Freya said.

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. "I'm Corven. Verrin River Company, Survey and Salvage Division." He produced a leather folio and opened it with the reverence of a priest. "I'll be brief, because I know how you working folk value your time." The courtesy had edges on it. "There's been seismic activity upstream. The river above this point is quarantined. Hazardous. You'll want to turn about."

"The river doesn't look quarantined," Freya said. "It looks like a river."

"The river looks however we say it looks, Captain. That's what a quarantine is." Corven turned a page in his folio. "Now. There was an earthquake last night. Earthquakes shake things loose. Things wash downstream. If your vessel has recovered any such items — debris, wreckage, Company property — you're required by charter law to declare it. Salvage belongs to the Company."

"We've got crates of salt pork," Freya said. "Some rope. A card table with a wobbly leg that Ines swears she can fix."

"A card table," Corven repeated.

"It's mahogany," Ines called down, with dignity. "It merely lacks confidence."

Something almost human crossed Corven's face and was escorted off the premises. "I mean artifacts, Captain. Metalwork. Documents. Anything bearing Company marks or — " he paused, delicately, like a man stepping over a puddle — "*names*."

Tomas felt the word go through the deck under his feet like a fish under a boat. *Names.* And there was a brass key sitting in Pell's pocket at that very moment with PEMBERTON stamped into it in letters deep enough to read by touch, and there was a boy in the netting doing a magnificent job of looking like cargo, and there was Tomas standing between all of it and this dry, patient man with his folio full of other people's secrets.

"Well," Tomas said brightly, leaning on the rail, "you've come to the right boat for junk. We're practically a floating museum of things the river didn't want. Come aboard and inspect, if you like. Mind the third step, it bites."

Corven considered him. It was the look of a man deciding whether a dog was worth kicking. "And you are?"

"Tomas. Deckhand, cook, moral compass. The position pays poorly but the hours are flexible."

"Charming." Corven didn't smile. "We'll inspect from here, thank you. Bring your crates to the rail."

For the next twenty minutes Tomas performed the finest theatrical work of his career. He hauled up crates of salt pork and knocked the lids loose with a thump calculated to sound honest. He held up rope coils. He displayed the wobbly card table at length, turning it so the morning light caught every scar in the wood, while Ines narrated its repair history with the gravity of a museum curator. Corven watched all of it with those pale, unhurried eyes, and twice he asked a question that wasn't really a question — *how long have you worked this stretch, how far down did the water drop last night* — and Tomas answered each one cheerfully and truthfully, because the best lies are built on a foundation of true things, and everything true he gave Corven pointed nowhere near a cave mouth or a key.

Pell never moved. Tomas could feel the boy's stillness through the deck boards, a held breath the size of a child.

At last Corven closed his folio. "Clean ship," he said, in the tone of a man who did not believe in clean ships. "You may proceed downstream, Captain. The quarantine line is marked at Gull Rock. Cross it and your charter's void, and we void charters with fire, not paperwork."

"Noted," Freya said. "We were headed downstream anyway."

"Were you." Corven put the folio away. Then, softly, almost kindly, which was somehow the worst thing he'd done all morning: "Strange tide last night, Captain. Rivers don't usually run backwards. When they do, it means something underneath has broken. Something old." He looked up at her, and then — deliberately, unmistakably — at the Kestrel herself, stem to stern, the way a man looks at a horse he intends to buy or a house he intends to burn. "We'll be seeing you."

The skiff peeled away into the side channel and was gone, and the birds started up again all at once, furious, as if filing complaints.

* * *

Nobody spoke until the channel was empty and had been empty for a good five minutes. Freya killed the engine to a low idle and turned.

"Pell. Out."

The netting rustled and the boy emerged, trying to look casual and achieving instead the look of a boy who has recently been extremely casual. His hand went to his pocket. Tomas saw it happen — saw the small involuntary gesture, saw Freya see it too.

"What's in your pocket?" Freya asked.

"Nothing," said Pell, in the voice of every child since the invention of pockets.

"Pell."

"He found something at dawn," Tomas said. "Before the skiff came. Down on the flats when the water dropped." He kept his voice easy, because everyone on this boat was strung tight as rigging and someone had to be the slack. "Show her, lad. She's scary but she's fair."

Pell hesitated. Then he brought it out and held it up on his open palm: the brass key, river-polished, stamped clean.

Freya went very still. Tomas had known her twelve years and had catalogued perhaps six of her stillnesses, and this was a new one. Her thumb came out and touched the stamp, tracing the letters. PEMBERTON. Her own name, worn by water older than her grief.

"That's my father's mark," she said finally. "Company survey keys. He carried one." Her jaw tightened. "Where exactly."

"The Door of Salt," Pell whispered. "The cave. It was just — lying there. Like it wanted finding."

"The Door of Salt," Ines repeated softly, from the rail. She had gone the color of old paper. "My husband surveyed that stretch. Before the flood. He drew maps of passages the Company swore didn't exist." She looked at the key, then at Freya, and something passed between the two women that Tomas couldn't follow and wisely didn't try to.

"So Corven's not hunting salvage," Freya said slowly. "He's hunting *this*. Or whatever it opens."

"Or whoever carries it," Tomas said.

Silence again, except the idle of the engine and the river doing its strange, sluggish business around the hull.

Then Freya held out her hand. "Give it here. I'll keep it safe."

And there it was. The moment. Loyalty comes down to moments like that — a hand held out, a pocket within reach, three seconds to decide who you are.

Tomas looked at the key. He looked at Pell, whose fingers had closed around it, whose whole thin body had gone taut with the same question. Because Tomas had done the arithmetic already, quick and cold beneath the warmth: if the key rode in Freya's coat, and Corven stopped the Kestrel again — and he would, that lingering glance at the netting had promised as much — then it would be the captain they searched, the captain they took it from, the captain whose name was already stamped on the damn thing. But a key in a boy's pocket, hidden in a coil of spare line in the hold, wrapped in oilcloth among forty identical lumps of nothing — that was a needle in a haystack, and Tomas was very good at haystacks.

Also — and he'd deny this under torture — there was the look on Pell's face. The boy had walked into a legend at dawn and come out holding proof that stories were real and that names mattered, and taking it from him now would be like taking a candle from a man in a tunnel.

"Actually," Tomas said, "I think it should stay with me."

Freya's eyebrows went up. "Excuse me?"

"You're the captain. If Corven boards us again, he searches the captain first — that's protocol, I've seen it done. Nobody searches the deckhand. Nobody searches the deckhand because nobody wants to know what the deckhand knows." He grinned his best idiot grin. "Ignorance is camouflage, Captain. Let me be camouflaged."

"That's absurd."

"It's the smartest thing I've ever said, which admittedly sets a low bar, but still."

Freya stared at him. Behind her, Ines made a small sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole. And Pell — Pell looked at Tomas with an expression that undid him more than he cared to admit, the expression of a boy being trusted with something for maybe the first time in his remembered life.

"Fine," Freya said at last. "But if we sink, you tell me where it is before the water reaches your chin."

"Deal. Though knowing me I'll swallow it accidentally first."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she turned to the wheel and rang the engine up to working speed, and the Kestrel leaned into the current and ran.

But Tomas caught it — later, hauling line, watching the banks slide past — the way Corven's parting words sat in the air like smoke. *We'll be seeing you.* And he caught something else too, something that raised the hair on his forearms despite the warm morning: far astern, at the edge of sight, a black shape holding station against the current. Not following. *Waiting.* Keeping pace with them the way a debt keeps pace with a debtor.

He touched the oilcloth bundle in his coat pocket, where the brass key lay against his ribs like a second heartbeat, warm from Pell's hand.

Downstream, then. Gull Rock, the quarantine line, the wide safe mouth of the river, and the authorities who might — *might* — care what a dead cartographer and a dead whistleblower had left behind.

Except the river, Tomas noticed, had other ideas. The current that had been dragging them gently along all morning had begun, very quietly, to pull the other way.

Upstream.

And the Kestrel's rudder, for the first time in twelve years, was starting to disagree with her captain.
