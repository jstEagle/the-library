## 5. Ines's Charts

Ines had learned long ago that grief does not leave a house; it simply rearranges the furniture.

For three years after Aldous drowned — or was lost, or was taken, the river never sent back so much as a boot to say which — she had kept his charts rolled in oilcloth at the bottom of her trunk, the way other widows keep a wedding dress. She did not unroll them. Unrolling them meant reading them, and reading them meant hearing his voice again, and hearing his voice again meant weeping into the Kestrel's galley kettle, which Tomas used for soup and would not forgive.

But tonight the Kestrel sat beached in a cavern that should not exist, lit by two lanterns and one nervous boy holding a third, and Ines had run out of reasons to keep the trunk shut.

"Those are old," Freya said from the doorway of the aft cabin, arms crossed, in the way she crossed them when she wanted very much to look like a captain and not like a woman asking for help.

"They are mine," Ines said, "and they are forty years newer than anything else on this boat." She smoothed the first sheet across the chart table and weighted its corners with the salt cellar, the pepper cellar, and Pell's tin mug, which he surrendered with the expression of a boy donating a kidney.

The lamplight found Aldous's hand immediately. She had forgotten how distinctive it was — the way he lettered depth soundings like a man signing his name over and over, *Pemberton survey, by commission of the Verrin Navigation Company*, each sheet stamped and countersigned. Her husband, who had once measured the width of their kitchen table to settle an argument and then written up the results in triplicate.

"He was proud of this work," she said. "At the start."

"And at the end?" Freya asked.

Ines did not answer, because the answer was on the table, and she had not yet let herself look at it properly. Instead she began where all honest work begins: with the dates.

* * *

Aldous's final commission had lasted eleven months. She knew this because he wrote to her every week, and she had kept every letter, and re-reading them now — spread beside the charts like testimony beside a confession — she could see what she had been too much in love to notice then. The early letters were full of birds and weather and the excellent trout above Harrow Falls. The later letters were shorter. The last three were written in pencil instead of ink, as though he could no longer afford certainty.

She matched the letters to the sheets. Sheet fourteen showed the river narrowing toward its source, and along the eastern bank Aldous had drawn a town — streets, a mill, a church with a steeple tall enough to be vain about — labeled in his careful capitals: **MARROW BEND. POP. 312.**

There was no Marrow Bend. There had never been a Marrow Bend, not on any map Ines owned, not in any song the deckhands sang. She said so aloud.

"There's a song about it," Pell offered. "Sort of. Old Tomas sings it wrong."

"I sing it *efficiently*," came Tomas's voice through the bulkhead, followed by no further defense of his artistry.

"It's a drowning song," Pell said, quieter. "My gran said it was about a place that went under when she was little. Forty souls, she always said. Forty souls and the church bell still rings under the water if you're wicked enough to listen."

Ines looked down at sheet fourteen, at the steeple Aldous had drawn with such affection, and felt the cabin tilt though the Kestrel was aground and steady.

Sheet fifteen was the last one. It was different. The soundings stopped being neat. The margins, which on every previous sheet held only compass corrections, here held writing — small, crowded, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, the handwriting of a man who had stopped caring who read over his shoulder.

Ines bent close. The lantern flickered. She did not blink.

*They tell me the dam is sound. I have measured it myself and it is not. The spillway cut was filled with slag and gravel and signed off by men who have never stood in the water. When the spring melt comes heavy, Marrow Bend will drown. I have told Corven's office. I have told the magistrate. They have told me my contract ends in March.*

Below that, harder still:

*I will take the evidence downstream myself. If I do not return, the ledger is where the bell can hear it.*

Freya made a sound — small, involuntary, the kind of sound a person makes when a floorboard gives way beneath them. Ines looked up. The captain had gone the color of the paper.

"My father," Freya said. "He worked the source. He went up the same season. He never came down, and the Company wrote him off as a deserter, and my mother believed them until the day she died because believing them was easier than—" She stopped. Captains do not finish sentences like that. She straightened. "Read it again."

Ines read it again. And then, because grief is a kind of literacy, she read everything else — the marginal arithmetic, the spillway figures, the list of names Aldous had copied from the workers' roll, forty-one names in a column, and there, ninth from the top, in her husband's unmistakable hand: **E. PEMBERTON.**

* * *

Later — how much later she could not have said — the lanterns had been refilled twice and Pell had fallen asleep sitting up, cheek on the chart table an inch from E. Pemberton's name, the way children sleep anywhere grief is happening, trusting the adults to manage it.

Tomas sat sharpening a knife he did not need sharpened. Freya stood at the little stern window, looking out at black water and blacker stone.

"You knew him," Freya said at last. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I married him," Ines said. "I knew him the way you know weather. He was stubborn in the direction of truth, which is the most exhausting possible direction." She gathered the letters into their stack, squared their edges, and found her hands were not entirely steady. "When they sent me his effects — one trunk, no body — these charts were missing. I assumed the river took them. I assumed the river took everything."

"And now they're in your trunk."

"Now they're in my trunk," Ines agreed, "because a dying cartographer on the Harrow dock pressed an oilcloth bundle into my hands and said, *for the widow, she'll know what to do,* and then died before I could ask him what, precisely, he thought I'd know." She had spent three years telling herself the man had been confused. Grief keeps a tidy house only if you sweep daily.

"So the Company didn't just lose your husband's survey," Tomas said slowly, laying the knife down. "They lost it *on purpose*. And it came home anyway. Like a cat."

"Like a cat," Ines said, and despite everything — despite the forty-one names, despite the girl at the window who had just learned her father was murdered by paperwork rather than faithless — she laughed, briefly, wetly, because Tomas was Tomas, and some things hold.

Freya turned from the window. "Corven's skiff is upstream. Corven wants salvage. Corven's office killed forty people and called it weather." Her voice was level in the way ice is level. "If he learns what's on this table—"

"He already suspects," Ines said. "Why else quarantine a river? You don't quarantine water, Captain. You quarantine *witnesses*."

The silence that followed had weight. Pell slept through it, which Ines privately considered the boy's finest quality.

* * *

She worked alone after that, because someone had to, and because working was the only prayer she trusted.

By the second lantern she had assembled the whole shape of it. Aldous had hidden his ledger — the original measurements, the correspondence, the signed false certifications — in Marrow Bend itself, in the church, *where the bell can hear it.* A joke between them, that phrase. On their wedding day the vicar's bell had cracked mid-peal, and Aldous had whispered, *even the bells eavesdrop here.* He had known, when he wrote those last pencil lines, that he might not come back down the river. He had known she would someday follow the charts. He had known her that well, and he had still gone, and she loved him and was furious at him in the same breath, which she suspected was simply what marriage was.

The dam would fail. Aldous's figures said spring melt; the earthquake said sooner. The Door of Salt had opened like a wound because the mountain itself was shifting, and somewhere above them a wall of slag and gravel and lies was deciding how long it wished to remain standing.

Which left the choice, the one she had been avoiding since the salt cellar came off the first chart.

Downstream lay the magistrate, the newspapers, the authorities — the whole slow machinery that had failed Aldous once already but was the only machinery there was. The evidence belonged there. Duty said carry it down, deliver it, let the law do what laws do, even if the law had eaten her husband and called it a career.

Upstream lay Marrow Bend, and the ledger itself, and a boy asleep on a dead man's name, and a captain who had just discovered her father in a column of forty-one.

And behind them, gaining, a skiff full of men paid to make sure none of it ever surfaced.

Ines thought of the widow on the Harrow dock, three years ago, holding a stranger's bundle and knowing nothing. She thought of herself now, holding everything, knowing almost as much.

"Aldous," she murmured to the empty cabin, to the careful capitals, to the man who had measured their kitchen table in triplicate, "you absolute maddening saint. You couldn't have just written it in a letter?"

But of course he couldn't have. Letters get read. Charts get filed. Truth, her husband had understood, must be hidden where only the desperate go looking — and then trusted to send someone desperate enough.

She heard boots on the deck above, quick ones, and then Freya's voice cutting through the dark, sharp with an alarm Ines had hoped not to hear until morning:

"Ines! Get your charts. All of them. There's a light coming down the channel — and it ain't Corven's skiff."

Ines folded sheet fifteen last, E. Pemberton's name inward against her heart, and reached for the oilcloth.
