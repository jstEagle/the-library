## 6. Rapids at Nightfall

The Kestrel ran the dark water like a woman who knew she was being chased and refused to look over her shoulder.

Freya stood at the wheel with the lamp hooded to a coin of light, enough to read the foam and nothing more. Behind her, the side channel where Corven's skiff had vanished was a black seam in the blacker hills. She didn't trust the dark to keep them. Company men carried lanterns that cost more than her boat and didn't mind spending the oil.

"Tomas," she said quietly. "How's the river ahead?"

"Angry," Tomas called back from the bow, where he'd been reading the water by feel and by ear, the way he'd done it for twelve years. "She's dropping fast. That quake shook something loose upstream — I can hear her chewing on rocks she used to glide over."

"Can we run it?"

"Can we run it *at night* is the real question." He turned, and even in the coin of lamplight she could see the grin he wore the way other men wore armor. "But I've seen you dock this barge in a hailstorm while arguing with a customs man, so I'd say our odds are insulting to the river."

Ines appeared at the companionway with the lamp turned low, charts rolled under one arm like a woman carrying firewood that happened to be worth more than the boat. Pell trailed behind her, wide awake and pretending he wasn't thrilled about it.

"Freya." Ines spread the chart on the deck box, weighting the corners with a belaying pin and a biscuit. "If we're going upstream past the Narrows, there's no easy water. My husband marked three chutes here, here, and here. He wrote *'bad in spring, worse in drought'* in the margin, which is the sort of thing a man writes when he wants his widow to stay home."

"Did you stay home?"

"I became a cartographer's widow," Ines said dryly. "So no."

Freya looked at the chart a long moment. The lines of her dead friend's hand were steady and sure, the soundings dense as stitches. She had known Aldric Vane only in passing — a quiet man who measured rivers the way priests measured souls — but his work was a kind of voice, and right now it was saying: *you can make it, if you don't hesitate.*

"Then we don't hesitate," she said. "Tomas to the bow. Ines, you're my eyes on the chart — call the turns before I need them. Pell—"

"I can steer," Pell said, too fast.

"You can hold a lantern and not drop it. That's a job for a steady man, and I'm promoting you."

The boy's chest swelled. He took the lantern like a knighthood.

Freya put the wheel over and the Kestrel turned her nose upstream, into the teeth of the Narrows.

* * *

The first chute took them like a hand closing.

The river had been drained by the quake and then fed by some hidden fury upstream, and the result was water that came in standing waves, brown and muscular, slamming the hull with a rhythm like a fist on a door. Freya drove into them at the angle Ines called — *starboard of the seam, NOW* — and the Kestrel climbed a wave the height of a chapel and slid down the far side with the whole crew holding their breath like passengers at a funeral.

"Second chute!" Ines shouted. "Aldric marked a rock mid-channel — *left of it, hard left!*"

"I see it!" She did see it, a black tooth gleaming wet in Pell's lantern light, and she gave the river everything the wheel had. The Kestrel heeled, cargo shifted below with a groan, and for one long second Freya was sure they'd broach. Then the current took pity, or took interest, and swung the stern through, and they were through, wallowing, alive.

"Third one's the worst," Ines said. Her voice had gone thin. "He wrote one word here. *'Avoid.'*"

"We can't avoid it. There's no bank to tie to and Corven's behind us."

"Then I'm glad he's not here to say *I told you so*," Ines murmured, and put her finger on the chart. "The channel bends right, then the water folds back on itself. There's a reversal — a hole. Keep your speed *up* through it. If you slow down, it'll take the stern and spin you."

Freya nodded and looked forward to where Tomas stood braced at the bow rail, calling depth and danger in that easy voice of his, as though they were gossiping on a porch. *Rocks port, two boat-lengths. Drop away to starboard, good water, good girl.* He talked to the boat like that. He'd talked to her like that for twelve years, and she had only just, in the last few days, let herself hear it as what it was.

She had spent twelve years being careful with her heart the way she was careful with her cargo — lashed down, tarped over, never opened in weather. Grief had taught her that. Her father went up this same river and never came down, and she had decided somewhere in the years after that loving people was a contract with a bad clause hidden in the fine print. So she kept the crew close and kept them at arm's length, and told herself that was the same thing.

It was not the same thing. She knew it now, standing at the wheel with her knuckles white, listening to Tomas's voice come back to her over the roar like a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

The third chute opened its mouth.

* * *

It went wrong at the fold, the way Aldric must have known it could.

The Kestrel hit the reversal at full steam and the river simply *stood up* — a wall of backwash that caught the stern and lifted it, spun it, slammed it down. Freya heard the wheel shudder in her hands and then the sound every captain dreads: the wheel going loose, the steering cables parting with a crack like a rifle shot.

"Wheel's gone!" she shouted. "I've got nothing!"

The Kestrel swung broadside. The current took her beam-on and began to roll her toward the rocks, and in the lantern light Freya saw Tomas already moving — not away from the danger but *toward* it, down the side deck to the broken steering gear aft, where the parted cable whipped like a live thing in the wash.

"Tomas, *no*—"

He didn't hear her, or he heard her and went anyway. That was the thing about Tomas. He went anyway. He got the spare cable off its cleat, looped it around his forearm, and leaned out over the churning water to splice the broken end to the quadrant — bare-handed, in the dark, in white water, while the boat rolled under him.

"Give me steerage!" he bellowed. "Freya, the moment it bites — *hard to starboard!*"

She hauled the dead wheel uselessly, watching him, watching the rock come. He made the splice. She felt it bite — a shudder through the hull, the rudder answering — and she threw her whole weight against the wheel, and the Kestrel *lurched*, and the rock scraped past with a sound like a coffin lid, and then they were through, in the long dark eddy below the chute, wallowing but whole.

Tomas wasn't on deck.

"TOMAS!"

The lantern light swung — Pell had dropped it, was scrambling — and then Ines was shouting and Freya was already moving, over the rail, into water cold enough to stop her heart, grabbing a fistful of shirt as Tomas came up gasping in the eddy. He'd been swept off the side deck by the backwash. His hand found her wrist, iron-strong even half-drowned, and between them and Pell hauling the heaving line and Ines steering by brute force on the emergency tiller, they got him over the rail and onto the deck, coughing river water, laughing.

*Laughing.*

"You should see your face," he wheezed.

"You—" Freya's voice broke. She knelt over him, soaked to the skin, hands shaking so badly she pressed them flat against the deck to hide it. "You absolute *idiot*. You could have—"

"But I didn't."

"You went anyway. You always go anyway."

"Someone has to," Tomas said softly. "You're busy driving."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted to hold him. She did neither, because Pell was there suddenly with a blanket, and Ines with brandy, and the moment folded itself away like a chart — but not gone. She felt it settle somewhere under her ribs, permanent as ballast. Twelve years of careful lashing-down, and it had taken one man leaning into black water to cut the lines.

*I love him,* she thought, with the simple horror of a woman discovering a leak she can't patch. *Well. That's that, then.*

* * *

They tied up below the chute in a willow stand, engines cold, one lamp burning. Ines worked by its light, and her face as she read had gone strange.

"Freya." She beckoned them all close. "Aldric's survey. I've been reading it in pieces, but tonight — tonight it makes sense. The quake didn't just open the Door of Salt. Look." Her finger traced the lines. "The source of the Verrin isn't a spring. It's a *dam*. Company-built, thirty years ago, to flood the valley above — Marrow Bend. They drowned a town to make a reservoir, and they built it cheap, and Aldric found the fault lines in the rock. He was coming back to document it when he died." Her voice caught and steadied. "The quake cracked it. His soundings, the water levels, the seepage marks — it gives us two days. Maybe less. When it goes, everything from the source to the Door of Salt comes down that valley at once. Every town on the river. Everyone who ever laughed at me for keeping his charts."

Silence. The willows hissed. Somewhere far upstream, the river made a sound Freya had never heard it make — a deep, patient groan, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Two days," Pell whispered. "To do what?"

"To get the proof out," Ines said. "The ledger's in Marrow Bend. Under the water. In the steeple." She looked at Freya. "I know what I'm asking."

Freya looked upstream, into the dark, toward the drowned town and her father's ghost and a wall of water with two days left in it. Behind her, Tomas wrung out his shirt and said nothing, because he knew she'd already decided, and Ines knew it, and even Pell knew it.

"We'll need the dinghy," Freya said. "And rope. And every ounce of luck this crew has ever wasted."

Tomas grinned through his bruises. "Then we're rich."
