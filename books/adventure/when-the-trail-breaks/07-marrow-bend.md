## 7. Marrow Bend

Pell had heard a hundred stories about the Verrin's source, and every one of them ended with somebody drowning. So when the river finally went quiet — so quiet he could hear his own heart knocking in his ears like a fist on a door — he decided that was probably worse.

The Kestrel drifted into a bowl of hills where the water lay flat as poured tin. Mist hung over it in ropes. And rising out of the middle of that stillness, black and crooked, was the top of a church steeple.

"Saints above," Tomas whispered. He'd been quiet since the rapids, one arm still wrapped where the wheel had nearly taken him under, but now he leaned over the rail and squinted. "That's a church."

"That's *half* a church," Ines said.

She stood at the bow with her charts spread across a crate, her gray hair whipping loose from its braid, and Pell watched her face do something complicated — grief and hunger fighting for the same chair. Her husband Aldric had drawn these waters before anyone knew they needed drawing. Before the flood took Marrow Bend and forty souls with it, including, as of two nights ago, whatever illusions Freya still carried about her father.

"Water's maybe forty feet deep here," Freya said. She sounded steady, but Pell had learned to hear the seams in her voice, the places where she was holding something shut with both hands. "The steeple's the only thing tall enough to break surface. The rest of the town's down there."

"The ledger's in the steeple," Ines said. "Aldric always said if a survey was worth killing for, you'd find it in the bell loft. He thought nobody robs a church." A small, crooked smile. "He was wrong about most things. I'm hoping he was right about that."

Pell looked at the steeple. Then at the water. Then at Freya.

"I can dive," he said.

Three heads turned toward him at once, which was more attention than he'd gotten in his whole life before the Kestrel, and he felt his ears go hot.

"You're twelve," Freya said.

"Thirteen in the spring. And I'm the smallest, and the bell loft hatch'll be small, and Tomas is half-drowned already, and Ines can't swim, and you—" He stopped, because he'd been about to say *you have to stay with the boat*, and he wasn't sure that was true anymore, and he wasn't sure he wanted it to be true either. "I've been swimming since before I could talk proper," he finished. "River kids all do."

Freya looked at him a long moment. Pell had catalogued her looks by now: the look that meant trouble, the look that meant supper, the look that meant she'd caught him stealing bread and couldn't decide whether to be angry or impressed. This was none of those. This was new. This was the look of somebody seeing a stowaway turn into something else.

"Tethered," she said finally. "Rope around your waist, other end to me. You feel anything pull wrong, anything at all, you kick up and we haul. No heroics. No poking around. Ledger, and out."

"Yes, Captain."

"And Pell." She crouched to his level, which she almost never did, and the mist beaded in her hair. "If it's bad down there — if there's — people —"

"There won't be," Pell said, with the terrible confidence of a boy who hadn't yet thought it through. "It's been years. Fish would've — "

"*Pell.*"

"No people," he said quietly. "I promise."

* * *

They weighted him with a stone from the ballast and tied the rope in a bowline around his waist, and Tomas taught him the hand signals in about ninety seconds — *up, down, trouble, found it* — while Ines pressed a folded scrap of chart into his palm, waterproofed in wax, showing exactly where Aldric had marked the loft hatch.

"Third course of stones below the louvers," she said. "There should be an iron ring. Aldric never trusted hinges."

"He sounds like he didn't trust anything," Pell said.

"He trusted me," Ines said. "Everything else, no."

The water took him like a mouth.

Cold first — cold that squeezed his chest and made his thoughts go small and white — then dark, then the strange floating hush of a drowned world. He'd expected ruin, wreckage, horror. What he found instead was a town, sleeping. Streets below him, soft with silt. A wagon, its wheels furred green. A doorway standing open with nothing behind it. Laundry lines gone to lace. It looked less like a disaster than like everyone had simply stepped out one afternoon and forgotten to come back, and somehow that was worse. His chest hurt, and not only from the cold.

*Down,* he signaled, though nobody could see him, and kicked for the steeple.

The tower rose past him out of the gloom, and the higher he climbed along it — hand over hand up the lightning rod's old mounting bracket, boots finding cracks in the mortar — the more the light came back, gray-green shafts slanting through the louvered windows. Fish scattered from the belfry like startled coins. He found the iron ring exactly where Aldric's chart promised, third course below the louvers, and the hatch beneath it swung open with a groan of bubbles that scared him half to death.

Inside, the bell loft was dry.

Not dry dry — everything wore a skin of slime and the air was dead and mineral — but above the waterline, sealed in its own little pocket of the long ago, and Pell hung on the sill gasping and laughing at the same time until he remembered the rope pulling taut against his waist and Freya's voice in his head saying *no heroics.*

The bell itself hung above him, huge and green with age, tilted off its yoke like a drunk leaning on a friend. And beneath it, wedged in the corner where a man would have put something he wanted found by someone clever enough to look, was a lead-lined document case, strapped shut, stamped with a surveyor's mark.

*Found it,* he signaled through the hatch, and hauled the case to the sill, and paused.

Because on the wall beside the hatch, scratched into the plaster with something sharp, someone had left words. Water had eaten most of them. But the last line survived:

*...if the Company reads this, know the dam was cut, not failed. Forty names attached. Ask Pemberton.*

Pell stared at it until the letters swam. Then he stuffed the case inside his shirt, filled his lungs with the foul little pocket of air, and dove.

* * *

He came up coughing into a wall of hands. They hauled him over the rail and Tomas pounded his back and Ines wrapped him in a blanket and Freya held the rope like she'd been holding it the entire time without once letting slack into it, and Pell realized, dimly, through the shivering, that nobody had ever held the other end of anything for him before.

"Well?" Freya said.

Pell got the straps open with numb fingers. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a ledger — fat, swollen, its pages soft as cloth but legible, Aldric's neat surveyor's hand marching across them alongside columns of figures and, tucked in the back, a roll of workers' names in somebody else's cruder script. Payroll. Marrow Bend mine, final season.

Ines made a sound like she'd been struck. She touched the pages with two fingers, feather-light, reading her husband's handwriting the way other people read letters from home.

"It's all here," she whispered. "The blasting charges. The dates. The order came down from Corven's own office — they cut the dam early to flood the lower galleries and hide the silver seam, and they did it during shift change so the paperwork would drown with the men." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Aldric knew. He copied everything. He was bringing it downstream when — " She stopped. Closed the ledger gently, like closing a door on a sleeping child.

Freya hadn't moved. "Pell," she said. "You saw writing on the wall. Say it again. All of it."

"*Ask Pemberton,*" Pell recited. "It said ask Pemberton."

"Ask him what?"

And that was when Pell, who had spent his whole life reading people the way other folks read weather — orphan kids get good at it or they don't eat — understood that Freya already knew. That some part of her had known since Ines first unrolled the charts. That she'd been waiting for someone else to say it out loud so she wouldn't have to carry it alone.

He opened the payroll roll to the last page, because it seemed right that it should be him, somehow. Because he'd brought it up out of the dark.

There, between *Oakes, H.* and *Parra, S.*, in the same crude hand:

**PEMBERTON, E. — shift boss. Flagged: refused orders.**

"Not a deserter," Pell read aloud, and his voice wobbled and he let it. "Shift boss. He stayed. He *refused orders.*" He looked up. "Captain. Your dad tried to stop it."

For a moment Freya Pemberton, who had run the Verrin for twelve years and out-stubborned floods and Company men alike, sat down very slowly on a cargo crate and put her face in her hands. Tomas put a hand on her shoulder and left it there. Ines held the ledger to her chest like a second heartbeat.

Pell stood among them, dripping, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of tar and Tomas's pipe, and felt something crack open in his chest that wasn't grief. Or wasn't only grief. It was the strange, terrifying warmth of standing inside a family on purpose, chosen, in the wreck-light of everybody's worst day, and knowing — actually knowing — that nobody was going to tell him to get off at the next landing.

"We should go," Ines said at last, softly. "Downstream. To the authorities, before Corven —"

But Freya's head came up, sharp, and Pell followed her gaze to the mist behind them, where the still water had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to tremble.

From upstream, low and getting louder, came a sound like the whole river clearing its throat.

"The dam," Ines breathed. "It's not two days away. It's *now.*"
