## 8. Corven's Bargain

The source of the Verrin was not what Freya had imagined in twelve years of wondering. She had pictured a spring, maybe — something modest and holy, a trickle out of rock that you'd cup in your hands. What she found instead was a wound.

The river came out of the mountain through a gap in a dam face, a black seam forty feet wide where water screamed through under pressure, and above it rose the dam itself, old masonry furred with moss, holding back a lake that had no business existing this far into the high country. The air smelled of wet stone and old iron. Behind them lay Marrow Bend, drowned and dark. Ahead lay the seam, and beyond the seam, if Ines's charts were right, forty-eight hours before the whole rotten thing let go.

"Anchor here," Freya said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Tomas, take us in close to the east wall. There's an eddy behind that spur."

"You see an eddy," Tomas said, wrestling the wheel, "and I see a whirlpool wearing a hat."

"That's the spirit."

They slid into the slack water behind the rock spur, and the Kestrel rocked once, twice, and went still — as still as anything could be with the mountain groaning overhead like an old man getting out of a chair. Pell appeared at the rail without being called, which was his particular talent, and Ines came up from below with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart.

Freya looked at all three of them and felt something she hadn't let herself feel since she was nineteen years old: the specific terror of having something to lose.

* * *

Corven's skiff came around the spur at dusk, running no lights, which told Freya everything about how he meant to conduct himself. It bumped against the Kestrel's hull with a courtesy that fooled nobody. Four men came aboard, and Corven came last, stepping over the rail like a man visiting a neighbor he intended to ruin.

"Captain Pemberton." He took off his hat, which was somehow worse than keeping it on. "You've had an eventful week."

"I've had a quiet week," Freya said. "Cargo run. You know how it is."

"I do." He smiled. He had the kind of face that had been handsome once and had gotten tired of it. "Quiet enough to run the Door of Salt, survive white water at night, and dive a drowned church steeple. Very quiet. Very restful."

Behind him, one of his men shifted, and Freya saw the lantern light catch on the ledger-shaped bulge of nothing at all — they didn't know yet whether she had it. That was the game then. That was why he'd come talking instead of shooting. He needed to know where the truth of Marrow Bend was sleeping before the mountain woke up and buried the question forever.

"We're leaving," Freya said. "You can have the river back. It's been terrible."

"I'm sure it has." Corven walked the deck slowly, trailing a hand along the rail like a buyer at horse fair. "Here's my trouble, Captain. In two days — call it forty hours now, the seismographs are chatty — that dam comes down. When it does, everything upstream of the narrows goes to the bottom or over it. Including, potentially, a certain survey ledger written by a certain dead cartographer, which names certain men at the Company who opened a spillway gate they knew was cracked, because closing the mine for repairs cost money, and forty people downstream cost less." He stopped. "You see my trouble."

"I see you confessing to murder on my deck."

"I see *arithmetic*," Corven said mildly. "So. The ledger for safe passage. My skiff escorts you down past the quarantine line, past every checkpoint, no questions, no bullets. You keep your boat, your boy, your crew, your lives. All four of you float home fat and happy." He spread his hands. "Or I take the ledger off this boat myself, and then we find out how many of you the river wants anyway. I'd rather not. I'm told I'm sentimental."

Ines made a sound beside Freya — small, involuntary, the sound of a woman hearing her husband's life priced in ferry passage.

"And if I say yes," Freya said carefully, "and the flood takes the ledger anyway? Forty people stay dead and nameless, and you sleep fine."

"The flood takes everything eventually, Captain. I'm just choosing what it takes first."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Twelve years she'd run this river dodging exactly this kind of man, and she'd always thought the trick was never letting them see what you loved. But Corven had already seen it. He'd counted her crew like coins.

"My father worked that mine," she said. "Aldous Pemberton. He's on the workers' roll. He died in the flood your Company made, and the Company spent twenty years telling everyone he ran, that he abandoned his post, that he was a coward and a thief. My mother believed it until the day she died. I grew up on it. Every winter, every hard year, some part of me thought — well, blood tells, doesn't it." Her voice did not shake, which cost her more than shaking would have. "That ledger is the first true thing anyone has ever handed me about him. You're asking me to drown it to save myself."

"Yes," said Corven. "I am. And I think you're smart enough to do it. Sentiment sinks boats, Captain. Ask any wreck."

"He's right, you know."

It was Tomas who said it. Freya turned, and Tomas stepped forward out of the shadow of the wheelhouse with his hands loose at his sides and his jaw set in the way it set when he was about to do something magnificent and unforgivable.

"Tomas—"

"He's right," Tomas said again, louder, for Corven's benefit. "Sentiment sinks boats. So don't be sentimental, Captain. Be arithmetic." He looked at Corven. "You want somebody to blame, somebody to hold, somebody to make your story tidy. Take me. I'm the one who dove the steeple. I'm the one who carried the ledger up. Put whatever you were going to put on them on me instead — obstruction, theft, piracy, I'll sign my name to all of it in your pretty book. They go downstream clean, escorted, safe passage, sworn. The ledger stays with the crew." He shrugged. "I've been meaning to learn to swim in calmer water anyway. Prison has rivers, I hear."

"No," said Freya.

"It's a good offer," Tomas said, still looking at Corven.

"It's a *stupid* offer, and I'm the captain, and it's refused." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it and kept going. "Tomas Reyes, you have steered my boat through ice, fire, the Verrin at spring flood, and one memorable evening involving a wedding party and a stolen barge, and if you think I will trade you — trade *any* of you — for a piece of paper, then you have spent six years on my deck learning nothing about me whatsoever."

"Six years next month," Tomas said quietly. "I remember the date."

"Of course you remember the date. You remember everything. That's the problem with you." She turned back to Corven before either of them could make it worse, or better. "Your bargain is refused. Both versions of it. No ledger, no Tomas, no deal."

Corven sighed, and the sigh had real regret in it, which frightened her more than the guns had. "Captain. In thirty-six hours that dam fails and this whole valley becomes a chute. My men and I have horses on the ridge. You have a boat and a prayer. Give me the paper and live."

"Then we'll pray in company," Freya said. "Get off my boat."

For a heartbeat she thought he'd simply take it — four armed men, one narrow deck, and the ledger sitting ten feet away in Ines's arms. She saw him weigh it. She saw him find the same thing she had found in the eddy behind the spur: that the Kestrel's people had already decided to die badly rather than hand it over, and dying men shoot straighter than scared ones.

But it was Pell who broke the balance. The boy stepped out of the shadows by the coal hatch — thin, soaked to the elbows, brass key hanging at his throat where he'd worn it since the Door of Salt — and looked Corven dead in the eye.

"She won't trade us," Pell said. "Nobody trades anybody. That's the whole point of her."

Something moved across Corven's face too fast to name. He put his hat back on.

"Thirty-six hours," he said. "When the water comes, Captain, remember I offered you the rope." He turned to his men. "Off. We'll watch from the ridge. If the ledger floats out of here, we'll be there to net it. If the crew floats out—" he paused at the rail, and for one unguarded second he looked almost young, almost sorry, "—I'll net that too. God help me."

The skiff pulled away into the dusk, oars wrapped in cloth, silent as a rumor.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The mountain groaned. Somewhere inside the dam, deep down, something went *clank*, like a door trying to lock.

"Well," said Ines at last, hugging the ledger. "That went nearly as well as could be expected."

"He offered to arrest me," Tomas said. "Me. As if I'd look good in stripes."

"You'd look devastating in stripes," Freya said, and then her voice gave out entirely, and she sat down hard on the coil of rope by the wheelhouse, and Tomas sat down next to her, shoulder against hers, warm and solid and infuriatingly alive.

"You shouldn't have done that," she said.

"Offered myself?"

"Remembered the date."

He laughed — low, rough, real — and after a moment she laughed too, there on the deck of the Kestrel with the end of the world ticking away inside the mountain, and it was the truest sound either of them had made in years.

Pell climbed onto the rope coil beside them, and Ines stood over the four of them like a lighthouse, and Freya looked up at the black seam in the dam where the river screamed through, doing its arithmetic.

Thirty-six hours. One boat. One ridge full of watchers waiting to see what floated out.

"Right," Freya said, standing, captain again. "Tomas, check the boiler seals. Ines, mark me every channel off this pool — there has to be a way down that isn't the main chute. Pell—" she hesitated, then put her hand on the boy's damp head, "—Pell, you're with me. We're going to need every miracle on this boat, and you seem to collect them."

Above them, deep in the masonry, the dam answered with another long *clank* — closer now, and louder, like something counting down.
