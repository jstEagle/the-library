## 9. The Source Gives Way

The dam did not wait for its appointed hour. Dams, in Tomas's experience, were like women from Harlow's Landing — they told you when they'd had enough, and never at a convenient time.

He heard it first through the soles of his boots, a groan in the stone that no man's throat could make. Then the sound arrived properly: a deep, grinding complaint from upriver, where the source narrowed between black cliffs and the old Company works squatted like a toad that had died mid-sentence.

"Freya!" he shouted, and then again, louder, because she was already at the wheel and Freya Pemberton heard weather before she heard people. "She's going! The dam's going *now*!"

The Kestrel lay tied off against a shelf of rock below the source pool, engines idling warm, Pell sent up the scree an hour ago with Ines to find the high survey line and a way out that didn't involve drowning. Forty-eight hours, Corven had said. They had perhaps forty seconds.

"Cut us loose," Freya said. Her voice was calm in the way of a woman standing in a burning house discussing the wallpaper. "Tomas. Cut us loose."

He cut them loose. The bowline parted under his knife and the Kestrel swung her nose downstream just as the world behind them turned white.

There was no wall of water, not at first. There was something worse — a *sucking*, the river pulling backward toward the breach like a breath drawn before a scream. The water level dropped three feet in as many heartbeats, and the Kestrel's hull kissed mud with a scrape Tomas felt in his teeth. Then the scream came. The flood came down the gorge like a freight train made entirely of the Verrin River, forty years of held-back water remembering what it was for.

"Hold her into it!" Freya yelled. "Bow-on! We ride it or we broach!"

Tomas threw himself at the engine throttle and gave her everything the old girl had. The Kestrel shuddered, rose, and met the flood head-on.

Riding a breaking dam is not like riding rapids. Rapids have rhythm; you read the water like Ines reads her charts, and you dance with it. A dam failure has no rhythm. It is one long, furious note, and your only job is to stay pointed upstream of your own death and pray the boat loves you back. The Kestrel loved him back. She climbed the first wave like a gull taking wind, hung there a moment with her screws churning air, and slammed down the far side hard enough to throw Tomas across the engine housing. Something cracked — he'd learn later it was two ribs — and he got up anyway, because the wheel was Freya's problem and the engine was his, and a man holds his own end of a boat or he's no use to anybody.

"IN!" Freya screamed over the roar.

He looked up. That was his mistake, looking up instead of looking at his gauges, because what he saw was the scree slope on the north bank, and on the scree slope, two small figures — Ines, clinging to the survey stakes, and Pell, halfway between the high line and the waterline, frozen, the boy's face white as salt.

Pell had gone back down. God knew why — for the ledger, maybe, or for the boat, or simply because twelve-year-olds are built with more courage than sense — but the flood was coming around the bend and the boy was on the low rocks and there was no time, no time at all.

"Freya." Tomas grabbed her arm. "Pell."

"I see him."

"We can't—"

"I *see him*, Tomas."

And then she did the thing that Tomas would carry in his chest for the rest of his life, the thing he'd tell badly and often in riverside taverns until people paid him to stop. She put the Kestrel's bow *into* the flood. Not away from it, not broadside to survive it — into it, turning upstream, driving against a wall of water that had already killed a town once, to come alongside a scrap of rock where a stowaway boy stood alone.

"You're mad," Tomas said, meaning it as a compliment.

"He came aboard my boat without permission," Freya said, hands steady on the wheel, eyes on the rocks. "That makes him mine. Get to the rail."

* * *

Getting Pell off those rocks took ninety seconds and cost Tomas most of his remaining faith in physics. He went out on the foredeck with a line around his waist, the other end made fast to the bollard by Ines — who had, impossibly, come down from the high line and appeared at the stern rail with her skirts soaked to the knee and her husband's ledger strapped to her chest like a breastplate. The Kestrel bucked and yawed beside the rocks, close enough that spray off the flood hit Tomas like thrown gravel, and he lay flat on the deck and reached out into the chaos with both arms.

"Jump, boy!"

"I can't swim!"

"That's fine! Neither can this boat, right now! JUMP!"

Pell jumped. He jumped the way boys jump — too late, too short, all heart and no arithmetic — and caught Tomas's wrists, and for one long moment they were both suspended over the maelstrom, four hands and a prayer, while the river tried to pry them apart like a lock trying a key. Tomas's ribs screamed. His shoulders screamed. Somewhere beneath him Freya was holding the Kestrel against the current with nothing but willpower and forty years of someone else's engineering, and he thought, absurdly, *I love her, I have loved her since the day she fished me out of the shallows at Tern Quay and called me useless, and if we live through this I am going to tell her so at tremendous volume.*

Then his boots found the deck coaming and he rolled inward with Pell under him, and the boy was sobbing and laughing at once, and Tomas was doing neither because breathing had become a full-time occupation.

"Get below!" he rasped. "Both of you! Ines, take the wheel with her!"

But Freya wouldn't leave the wheel, and the flood wouldn't leave the gorge, and the gorge bent hard to starboard half a mile down where the cliffs pinched together — the Narrows, which at normal water was a thrill and at this water was a coffin. The Kestrel came around the bend sideways.

They didn't broach. What saved them was uglier than skill: a floating pine, whole, root-ball first, came out of the white and struck their port quarter, spinning the boat true just long enough for Freya to catch the current and thread the Narrows with a hand's width to spare on either side. Tomas watched the cliff slide past close enough to touch and understood that the river had decided, today, to be generous, and that generosity of rivers is a loan, not a gift.

Then the water began to spread. Below the Narrows the valley opened, and the flood did what floods do — it stopped being a fist and became a hand, flattening out, slowing, spreading wide across the low country. The Kestrel rode the shoulder of it now, rolling but answering her helm, and Tomas allowed himself one full breath.

Which was when he saw the skiff.

Corven's skiff, the Company gray hull he'd lied to not three days ago, was in the flood's path ahead of them, swept sideways, swamped to the gunwales. Three men clung to it. One was Corven — Tomas knew the set of those shoulders, the way the man held his chin even while drowning, as though the water ought to apologize.

Freya saw them too. Tomas watched her face do the arithmetic every captain dreads: risk, distance, obligation, hate.

"They left us to Corven's mercy," Tomas said quietly. "We don't owe—"

"I know what we don't owe." She spun the wheel. "Come about. Get a line ready."

* * *

What happened next happened fast and would be retold slow. The Kestrel came alongside the sinking skiff in the flood's slackening edge, and Tomas threw a heaving line, and one Company man caught it and was dragged gasping aboard. The second missed and went under and came up thirty yards on, clawing driftwood, and Pell — Pell, half-drowned himself an hour ago — lay flat on the deck and fished the man out with a boathook, hauling him in like a catfish, swearing in a voice still cracking.

Corven did not reach for the line.

He clung to his skiff's sternpost, gray water to his chest, and looked up at Freya Pemberton across six feet of boiling river, and whatever passed between them in that moment, Tomas never fully learned. He saw Corven's mouth move — *the ledger, give me the ledger and I'll see you safe-passage, I swear it* — the same bargain, offered even now, even here, to a woman standing on a boat he'd been sent to sink.

Freya looked at him. She did not shout. She said, in a voice the flood almost let pass unheard:

"You can drown holding your secret, Mr. Corven, or you can live losing it. Those are the only terms on this river."

And Corven — Corven, whose whole life had been the Company's arithmetic, men weighed against silver — let go of the skiff.

Tomas hauled him in. The man came over the rail coughing, ruined, stripped of everything except the fact of being alive, and he knelt on the Kestrel's wet deck among the people he'd been sent to destroy, and he said nothing at all. But twenty minutes later, when the flood had gentled to a brown, roaring sea and the Kestrel labored downstream under a jury-rigged staysail, it was Corven who spotted the snag threatening their rudder and shouted the warning, and it was Corven who took a turn of the towline when Ines's arms gave out.

The river carried them down through the drowned valley, past rooftops and fence posts and a church bell ringing underwater somewhere, and Tomas sat on the deck with his broken ribs and Pell asleep against his shoulder, and watched Freya at the wheel with the flood light gold behind her.

"Duty," Ines said, sitting down beside him, the ledger safe in its oilcloth. "Everyone says duty is the heavy thing. It isn't. Duty is easy — it's a list. Love is the thing that costs."

"Aye," Tomas said. "And she paid twice today. Once for the boy. Once for him."

Below them, far downstream, the flood was already carrying more than boats. It was carrying paper — pages torn from offices and strongrooms in Marrow Bend, documents the water had pried loose from the Company's drowned vaults, evidence set loose upon the Verrin like seeds. Ines touched the oilcloth over her husband's ledger and smiled the smile of a woman who knows a secret the river is about to stop keeping.

"Tomas," she said. "By tomorrow morning, every landing on this river is going to know what the Company did. The flood's delivering it door to door."

Tomas looked at the water rushing past, brown and triumphant, bearing its cargo of truth toward every town that had ever bought Company silver and wondered why the price tasted bitter.

"Well," he said, and winced, and laughed, and winced again. "Let's hope they read their post."
