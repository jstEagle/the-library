## 10. Downstream

The river took three days to forgive them.

Freya knew this because she spent those three days apologizing to it — or rather, the Kestrel did, limping down the Verrin with a cracked rail on her port side, a wheel that groaned like an old man climbing stairs, and a hull full of water that Tomas bailed with the grim devotion of a man who had nearly drowned and intended never to do it properly.

"River's not angry," he said on the second morning, wringing out his shirt over the side. "Rivers don't get angry. Rivers just *are*. It's people who take it personal."

"You took it personal," Freya said. "You screamed at it."

"I screamed at it in a friendly way."

From the bow, where Pell sat mending a net with more enthusiasm than skill, came the sound of a boy trying very hard not to laugh. Ines, folded into her corner of the deck with the ledger open on her knees for perhaps the hundredth time, looked up and smiled — the small, private smile she'd been wearing since Marrow Bend, as though grief had finally agreed to sit beside her instead of on top of her.

They were all of them marked by the flood. Pell had stopped flinching at loud noises somewhere around Graywater Landing, which everyone pretended not to notice. Ines slept without candles now. And Freya — Freya found herself standing at the wheel at odd hours just to feel the current answer her hand again, obedient and ordinary, the most beautiful thing in the world.

The ledger was the other cargo they carried, and it was heavier than anything the Kestrel had ever hauled.

* * *

It happened at Tallow's Reach, four days downstream, because Ines insisted they stop and because Ines usually insisted on the right things.

The town had flood-marks on its doors still drying — brown tidelines at knee height, the river's signature left on every building like a debt collector's chalk. But nobody in Tallow's Reach was mourning. They were reading.

Ines carried the ledger up the landing steps herself, refusing all offers, and laid it open on the fish-market table while the whole town gathered around like gulls. She read aloud the entries her husband Aldric had copied from the Company's own survey books before they drowned him in paperwork and silence: the dam at Marrow Bend built thin to save silver, the warnings filed and buried, the forty names of the dead read out one by one into the morning air.

When she reached the name *Josiah Pemberton*, the crowd went quiet in a way crowds rarely do — completely, the way water goes still before it decides what to become.

"He wasn't a deserter," Ines said simply. "He was the only man in that company who told the truth on paper. The Company called him a coward so no one would look for his body. He died trying to open the sluice gates himself."

An old woman near the front began to cry without any noise at all. A man took off his hat. And Freya stood at the back of the crowd with Tomas's hand finding hers without either of them arranging it, and felt something in her chest unlock that had been rusted shut for twelve years.

Her father had not run. Her father had stayed. He had stayed when staying meant dying, and the river had kept his secret the way rivers keep everything — patiently, and then all at once.

That evening the town fed them until they could barely row back to the boat, and someone's grandmother pressed a jar of plum brandy into Pell's arms "for the captain," and someone else's son asked if he could sign on as deckhand when he was older, and Freya said yes before she remembered to be careful about promising things.

By morning, word was moving downriver faster than the Kestrel could. By the time they reached Verrinmouth, the Company's offices there had been emptied by their own clerks, who walked out carrying armfuls of documents and deposited them, with great ceremony, on the courthouse steps. The magistrate read for two days straight. The Company men scattered like minnows when the shadow of a heron crosses the water.

Corven, they heard later, had been seen in Graywater buying passage east, alone, paying with coin he didn't try to hide. Some said shame. Tomas said hunger. Ines said men like Corven were always running toward something, even if they only ever ran in circles. Freya decided she didn't need to know which it was. The towline he'd thrown them in the maelstrom had said whatever needed saying, in the only language they'd ever shared.

* * *

Spring came up the valley the way spring does on a river — gradually, then all at once, then gradually again, as if it couldn't quite commit.

The Kestrel got new rail boards, a wheel that only groaned on cold mornings now, and a coat of green paint that Pell chose and everyone else pretended was acceptable. They ran cargo again: salt, timber, seed grain, letters. Ordinary freight for ordinary days, and Freya loved every dull mile of it with a fierceness that would have embarrassed her once.

She measured the river differently now. Twelve years she'd counted miles — miles to the next landing, miles from the last bad memory, miles between herself and anything that might ask something of her heart. Now she caught herself counting years. Pell would be tall enough to work the winch by next season. Ines was talking about drawing new charts, proper ones, of the whole upper Verrin, "so no one else has to find the truth by drowning." Tomas had begun leaving his boots outside the pilothouse door at night, which on a boat is as good as saying *I live here*, and everyone understood it, and nobody made a joke of it except Tomas, constantly.

"You know," he said one evening in June, leaning on the rail beside her while Pell and Ines argued cheerfully about star charts below, "you never actually thanked me for saving your wheel."

"I fed you for a year. That's thanks."

"That's wages. Thanks would be—"

"Tomas." She turned and looked at him, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop performing, which was rare enough that he went quiet entirely. "Thank you," she said. "For the wheel. For the key you hid from me. For holding us in the flood when every sane part of you wanted to let go." She paused. "For staying."

He was quiet a moment longer than usual. "Well," he said finally, gruff as gravel, "somebody has to bail."

She laughed, and he laughed, and below them Pell shouted that the North Star was *right there, Ines, honestly*, and Ines replied that the North Star was exactly where it always was and it was Pell who had moved, and the argument dissolved into the kind of laughter that has no bottom to it.

Freya leaned on the rail and watched the banks slide past — willows, cattle, a church steeple, children waving from a rope swing. Somewhere upstream, behind the rockfall that had sealed the Door of Salt forever, the drowned town of Marrow Bend lay quiet at last, its ledger out in the world doing the work her father had died trying to start. Somewhere downstream, magistrates were still reading. The truth, it turned out, traveled well by water. You only had to give it a current.

The Kestrel rounded the bend at Miller's Light, and the evening opened ahead of them — gold water, long shadows, the smell of someone's supper drifting off a porch.

"Pell," Freya called down. "What's our heading?"

"Downstream, Captain!" he called back, grinning. "Same as always!"

"No," Freya said, and put her hand on the wheel, and felt the whole river answer. "Not the same as always. Better."

And the Kestrel ran on, light in the water, heavy with everything that mattered, into the long gold dark of a summer that promised nothing but offered everything — years, all of it years now, and every one of them theirs.
