## 1. Miles, Not Years

The chainage marker at Kilometer 112 said *112.4*, and that was the whole of what Isandro Zhou knew about the day. Not the date—dates had a way of stacking up into years, and years into the kind of arithmetic he refused to do. But the marker was honest. It sat where it had been planted, galvanized steel gone soft with rust at the base, its stenciled numbers fading to the color of old bone, and it told him exactly how far he was from the last benchmark. That was enough. That had been enough for three years.

He crouched beside it with his level on its tripod, thumb resting on the focusing knob without turning it, and let the mountain talk to him in the only language he trusted anymore: grade, bearing, offset. The morning fog was lifting off the valley in slow rags, and where it thinned he could see the old line below him—a single thread of rusted rail stitched along the contour of the slope, disappearing north into the black slot of a tunnel mouth half a kilometer distant. The Kettle River Line. Closed eleven years ago, officially for landslides, unofficially for reasons that appeared in no document Isandro had ever been permitted to read. The company paid him to walk closed lines like this one twice a year and confirm that they remained closed—that the culverts were clear, the rock sheds intact, the fences uncut. Confirming absence. It was, he had come to understand, the perfect work for a man whose chief ambition was to be nowhere in particular.

"Zhou." Odile Brant's voice came down the slope ahead of his thoughts, gravel over gravel. She was standing at the next marker with her hands jammed in her coat pockets, a woman built like something the railroad itself might have forged—square-shouldered, weather-darkened, her gray braid pinned up under a cap two sizes too practical. She didn't look back at him when she spoke. She never did. "You're drifting again."

"I'm leveling."

"You're leveling at a fence post you've leveled four times." She turned then, and her eyes did the thing they always did—the quick inventory, the tally of him, as if she were checking a manifest. "Fog's burning off. We'll make the tunnel by noon if you stop measuring the same meter of earth until it confesses to something."

Isandro straightened and folded the tripod. "Everything confesses eventually," he said. "That's why I keep asking."

Odile snorted. It was the closest thing to affection their working relationship contained, and after three years of sharing survey camps, cook fires, and the particular intimacy of people who sleep within earshot of one another's breathing, he had learned to read her snorts the way other men read letters from home. This one meant: *I see you, and I'm not going to say what I see, because we'd both hate it.*

They walked. The old line took them north along the contour, ballast long since surrendered to grass and alder scrub, ties soft as bread underfoot. Isandro counted markers because counting markers was what he did instead of counting anything else. 113.0. 113.2. The river ran somewhere below the fog line, audible but unseen, a sound like continuous applause for nothing.

Three years. He knew it was three years the way you know the depth of water you've decided never to swim in—by refusing to look directly at it. Three years since the Cordillera crossing, since the span that had stood for nine months of construction and then, on an ordinary Tuesday in ordinary weather, had not. Fourteen men. He had signed the survey. His figures, his benchmarks, his neat columns of elevation data in a hand so careful his instructors had once used it as an example. The bridge had trusted those numbers, and the numbers had lied—or rather, the man who made them had lied to himself about what they meant, and fourteen men had stepped out onto that lie at seven-forty in the morning carrying toolbags and thermoses and, in the case of young Ferro Delacruz, a photograph of a girl he intended to marry in the spring.

Afterward there had been inquiries, and findings, and a settlement, and a verdict of *contributory factors*—a phrase engineered, Isandro had come to believe, by people who understood load-bearing language better than any engineer alive. Contributory factors. As if the truth were a weight distributed across many shoulders so that no single spine would break. His hadn't broken. That was the obscenity of it. He had walked away upright, licensed, employable, and he had discovered that the only way to live inside a body that had done what his body did was to keep it moving—chainage to chainage, camp to camp, contract to contract—so that time never accumulated enough to have a shape. Miles, not years. A man who measured in miles never had to arrive anywhere, and a man who never arrived never had to be found.

"You're doing it again," Odile said.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where your face goes to the river." She nodded downslope, toward the sound of water. "Every time we walk this stretch. Every inspection, same spot, same look. You know what I think?"

"I suspect you'll tell me regardless."

"I think you're listening for it." She said it flatly, without cruelty, which was Odile's way—she wielded kindness like a pry bar, blunt and effective. "Fourteen men went off a bridge three years back, and every crew man in this province knows it, and half of them have some look they get near water. Yours is the listening look." She shrugged. "For what it's worth, I don't think you're listening for screaming. I think you're listening for footsteps. Like maybe if you stand still long enough, they'll walk back up out of the current and want to know what took you so long."

Isandro said nothing. The fog was thinning around them now, and the tunnel mouth had come fully into view ahead—a dark semicircle punched into the mountainside, masonry portal furred with moss, the number *17* carved above it in stone by hands dead before either of them was born. Tunnel Seventeen. Two hundred and forty meters through the shoulder of Mount Cerenne, and beyond it, according to the maps, another eleven kilometers of sealed line climbing toward the high country where the ore bodies had supposedly run out, though Isandro had always privately doubted that geology had anything to do with the closure. Geology was what companies blamed when the real reason wore a face.

He stopped walking.

It wasn't decision that stopped him. It was the light—or rather, something wrong with the light at the tunnel mouth. The portal faced east, and the morning sun, clearing the ridge now, struck it full, and where the sun struck there should have been nothing but wet stone and shadow. Instead there was a shape. Small, angular, hunched against the left jamb of the arch like a bird with a broken wing propped upright by sheer refusal.

"What is that," Odile said. Not a question. Her hand was already moving to the radio on her belt, then stopping, because radios were useless here—the closed lines sat in dead zones the company had never bothered to remedy, on the theory that nothing living had business on them.

"A tarp?" Isandro said. "Debris off the rock shed?"

"It's got a hat."

It had a hat. As they came closer—fifty meters, thirty—the shape resolved into a man. An old man, seated on the ground with his back against the portal masonry, legs extended into the tunnel mouth's shade, wearing a canvas coat gone the color of the hillside and a wide-brimmed felt hat that had been reshaped by decades of rain into something like a folded leaf. A prospector's kit lay scattered around him: a cracked leather satchel, a short-handled pick, a brass compass in a wooden case, a canvas roll that clinked faintly when the breeze touched it. And under one hand, pressed flat against the stone as if he'd been holding it down against the wind, a folded sheet of paper gone soft and brown as cloth.

"Hello!" Odile called, already moving faster, her long stride eating the ballast. "Sir! Can you hear me?"

The man's head lifted. Under the hat brim his face was all planes and stubble, sun-cured and cracked, with eyes the pale washed-out blue of glacial ice—and even at twenty meters Isandro could see that those eyes were doing something eyes shouldn't do. They were trying to focus and losing the argument. The man's lips moved. No sound came.

Then he saw the survey gear on Isandro's shoulder—the level, the range pole, the orange vest with its reflective chevrons—and something happened in his face that Isandro would spend the rest of this day and many days after trying to describe. Relief, yes. But underneath the relief, something fiercer: recognition, and beneath the recognition, urgency, the kind that burns through exhaustion like a flare through fog. The man's hand came off the paper and reached—not toward Odile, who was nearest, but past her, toward Isandro, toward the vest, the instruments, the whole visible apparatus of the railroad.

"You're—" The voice was a ruin, gravel dragged over gravel, Odile's voice aged forty years. "Railroad. You're railroad."

"Contract survey," Isandro said, kneeling. Up close the man smelled of woodsmoke and sweat and the sweetish edge of something wrong in his blood. His lips were gray. His breathing came in shallow pulls, each one costing more than the last. "We're doing an inspection. Are you hurt? What happened?"

"Fell." The old man almost laughed; it came out as a cough that bent him double, and Odile steadied his shoulder with a hand that was gentle in a way Isandro had rarely seen her. When the coughing passed, the man sagged back against the stone and looked at Isandro with those ice-pale eyes, and said, with terrible clarity: "Been walking six days. Knew I wouldn't... knew the map needed to get out before the rains." His fingers found Isandro's sleeve and gripped it with a strength that surprised them both. "Before the monsoon. You hear me? Before the rains close everything."

"Sir, we need to get you down to the valley. There's a medical post at Harker's Junction, we can—"

"No." The grip tightened. "No time. Listen. Listen to me." He fumbled for the paper under his hand, dragged it up between them. It was a map—Isandro could see that much, could see the fine ink linework under the stains, the confident drafting of someone who had spent a lifetime reading terrain. "Mateo Reyes. My name is Mateo Reyes, and I need you to take this where it goes. Not to the company." His mouth twisted. "Never the company. They sealed these lines to bury what's in them, and I'm the only one left who knows, and my heart is—" He stopped. Looked down at his own chest with mild curiosity, as if consulting an instrument whose readings had become unreliable. "My heart is finishing its survey."

"Mateo." Odile had her coat off and was bundling it behind his shoulders. "Mateo, we can carry you. Between us we can get you down this line today."

"You can't." He said it without drama, the way a man states a grade or a distance. "Six days walking on a heart like mine. I'm done walking. But the map isn't done." He pushed it against Isandro's chest, hard, and Isandro's hands came up to catch it without his having decided to. "Forty years I carried this. Forty years I told myself somebody would come. Somebody honest. Somebody who'd know what the numbers mean." His eyes flicked to the level on its tripod, the range pole, the vest. "A man who measures things. That's what I prayed for. God sends strange angels."

Isandro looked down at the map in his hands. Even stained, even softened, the linework was beautiful—contours feathered in by hand, elevations annotated in a cramped sure script, and running along the alignment of the high line, the eleven kilometers beyond Tunnel Seventeen that existed on no current company drawing, a chain of small marks like stitches closing a wound. And beside the marks, in that same cramped script, words he couldn't yet read at this angle, in a language of abbreviations and symbols that was half surveyor's shorthand and half something else entirely.

He knew this terrain. That was the first cold drop of it, falling into him. He had helped close this line—he had been on the final inspection crews, three years ago, his second contract after the bridge, back when he still believed work could be work. He had walked these very kilometers with a company escort and strict instructions not to photograph, not to note deviations, not to ask. He had told himself then that the unease he felt was just the ordinary ghost-smell of abandoned track. Now, holding a dying stranger's map of the same ground, he understood that the unease had been information, and he had declined to read it.

"Who else knows about this?" Isandro asked quietly. "Who else has come looking?"

Something moved across the old man's face—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Men have come," he said. "Men who pay well. Men with clean boots." His breath caught. "That's why it has to go out before the rains. After the rains, the passes open, and they'll..." The sentence lost its footing. His head tipped forward, caught, tipped again.

"He's going into shock," Odile said, all business now, her hands at the man's throat counting pulse. "Thready. Zhou, we move him now or we lose him here. Help me get him onto the tarp."

But Mateo Reyes had found one more reserve somewhere, the way failing machinery will find one more revolution, and his hand shot out and closed on the map in Isandro's hands, holding it there, pinning it against Isandro's heart as if he could staple the promise to the man himself.

"Promise me," he said. "Not the company. Not the men with clean boots. Somewhere safe. Somewhere it gets *seen*." His voice dropped to a thread. "There are names. In the high line. There are names that have waited forty years, and I am the last man who—" The sentence broke apart like a cut string. His eyes stayed fixed on Isandro's, enormous and pale and absolutely lucid, and Isandro, who had spent three years perfecting the art of promising nothing to anyone, heard himself say:

"I promise. I'll take it where it needs to go."

The grip loosened. Something in the old man's face let go, some tension held so long it had become architecture, and he sank back against the masonry with Odile's coat around his shoulders and began, incredibly, to hum—a few bars of something tuneless and old, a walking song, the kind of thing a man sings to keep his feet company on a long trail.

They worked on him for an hour. Odile had a camp stove and sugar and a foil blanket and the brisk competence of a woman who had pulled men out of worse places, and for a little while it seemed possible—possible that the heart would hold, possible that they could rig a litter from range poles and get him down to Harker's Junction by dark. Isandro sat with the old man's head pillowed on his own pack and fed him water a spoonful at a time and listened to him talk, in fragments, between dozes, about a country Isandro had only ever seen through instruments: a hanging valley above the high line where the snow never fully melted, a spring that ran warm in January, a seam of quartz like a vein of frozen lightning. Talk that wandered. Talk that circled. And twice—only twice—talk that stopped dead, and the pale eyes opened, and found Isandro's, and checked. Still there. Still holding the map. Still promised.

Near dusk, the humming stopped.

Odile checked for a long time, longer than she needed to, because Odile was thorough even about the obvious. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at the tunnel mouth, at the dark arch with its carved stone seventeen, and said nothing at all, which from Odile was a eulogy.

Isandro stayed kneeling beside the body while the light failed. The map lay in his lap, soft as cloth, heavy as ballast. He should feel grief, he thought distantly. He should feel something. But the numbness was old and deep and well-maintained, a tunnel of its own, and all he could register was the arithmetic: one more death on a closed line, witnessed by a man who confirmed absences for a living. One more set of coordinates. One more mile.

Except.

His thumb had found the margin of the map without his noticing, and there, in the cramped sure script, half worn away, was a line of writing he could now read in the last of the light. Not survey notes. Not elevations. Just five words, inked decades ago and traced over so many times the paper had nearly failed:

*Suri — if you are reading this.*

Below it, the writing continued. Below it, the map kept talking.

Isandro sat in the dark at the mouth of Tunnel Seventeen, beside a dead man, holding a promise he had no idea how to keep, and for the first time in three years he forgot to count the miles.
