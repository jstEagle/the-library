## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The fever came on fast after dark.

Isandro had seen it before—men who walked into the high country whole and came down the mountain hollowed out by something too small to see. He'd carried two of them down himself in his first year with the survey crews, back when he still believed that speed and competence could outrun most things. Back when he still believed in *outrunning* at all.

He built the fire up higher than prudence allowed. The closed line was supposed to be empty; a signal fire at the tunnel mouth of Kilometer 31 was the kind of thing that got a man written up, or worse, asked questions he had no interest in answering. But the prospector's skin had gone gray as slate, his lips cracked and blue-tinged, and Isandro found that some old instinct—the one that made him check a bridge twice, that made him count heads coming off a span—refused to be buried along with everything else.

"Water," the man rasped.

Isandro held the canteen to his lips and watched him drink in small, disciplined sips, the way a man drinks who has learned that gulping is what kills you in the dry country. Up close, in the firelight, the prospector was older than Isandro had first guessed—not sixty, maybe seventy, weathered down to sinew and leather. His hands were a topography all their own: knuckles swollen like river stones, scars crossing the backs of them in pale seams. A miner's hands. A rope-and-dynamite man's hands.

"You're railroad," the man said. Not a question. His eyes, fever-bright, went to Isandro's boots, his coat, the surveyor's transit case standing open against the rock. "Company issue. All of it."

"I'm doing an inspection."

"On a line that's been dead eleven years." The man smiled, and it cost him something; Isandro watched the effort drag across his face like a winch over rough ground. "I've been walking this grade for three days waiting for somebody official enough to matter and foolish enough to come."

* * *

His name was Mateo Reyes. He gave it without ceremony, the way men give names they've worn so long the name has become just another tool. He'd been a prospector, he said, though the word sat strangely on him—prospectors chased ore, and whatever Mateo Reyes had been chasing, it wasn't silver. He talked in fragments between coughs that bent him double, and Isandro listened because there was nothing else to do until morning, and because the fragments were wrong in a way that snagged his attention like a burr in a sock.

Reyes knew the closed lines. Not the way a poacher knows them—for water holes and game trails—but the way an engineer knows them. He spoke of culverts by their chainage numbers. He mentioned the drainage gallery above the second viaduct on the old north fork alignment, a structure so minor Isandro had only ever seen it on drawings, and he described its weep-holes being choked with limestone bloom as if he'd run his fingers through them last week.

"How do you know that gallery?" Isandro asked.

Reyes looked at him for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere below the tunnel mouth, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a freight train that never arrived.

"Because I helped build it," he said. "Nineteen years old. Fitted stone for the abutments on the north fork. I was there when they sealed it." His voice dropped, not for drama but because the words themselves seemed heavy, required lifting. "I've been carrying something ever since."

* * *

He brought it out near midnight, when the fire had settled to a red heart and the cold pressed close around their circle of light.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord gone brittle as old cheese rind. Inside the oilcloth was leather, and inside the leather was a map—or rather, a sheaf of maps, hand-drawn on drafting linen that had yellowed to the color of old teeth, stitched together at the folds with thread. Isandro's breath caught before he understood why. He knew drafting linen. He knew the weight of it, the way it took ink, the particular smell of the fixative. Someone had made these with care, with training, over years.

The top sheet showed the mountain lines. All of them. The alignments the company had closed in a single season and never spoken of again—the north fork, the high traverse, the great bore at Kilometer 44 where the tunnel went into the massif and never came out the other side. Isandro had helped draw the closure orders. He had stood in a warm office with a ruling pen and drawn neat X's across spans he'd once inspected, and the work had felt like closing a book no one wanted to finish reading.

But these maps showed more than alignments. They showed annotations in a cramped, patient hand: dates, elevations, notes on rockfall and seepage, marks that might have been campsites or graves—he couldn't tell which—and running along the margins, a sequence of small numbered circles connected by dotted lines, like a rosary threaded through the mountains.

"What is this?" Isandro asked, though his hands already knew, holding the linen the way you hold something living.

"A witness," Reyes said.

* * *

The fever broke its banks after that. Isandro did what he could—willow bark from his kit, water, the fire fed through the small hours—but he could read the signs the way he read a stress fracture in a girder: the structure was failing, and no shoring would hold. Around two in the morning, Reyes surfaced from a bad stretch and fixed his eyes on Isandro with terrible clarity, the clarity of a man spending his last coins.

"Listen to me now," he said. "While I can still choose my words."

Isandro set down the canteen. "I'm listening."

"This goes somewhere safe. Not to the company—they'll burn it. Not to the government archive—it'll sit in a box until everyone who remembers is dead, and then it'll be misfiled and lost, and that's the same as burning." Each sentence cost him breath; he spent it anyway, deliberate as a man laying track. "There are people. Journalists, in the valley city. A woman named Corazón Ibáñez at the *Meridian Courier*. She's printed small things before. She'll print this if it arrives whole." He pressed a scrap of paper into Isandro's palm—an address, written in the same cramped hand, the ink faded but legible. "Before the monsoon. The passes close. And if the rains take me before—" a cough tore through him, long and wet, "—before someone else comes looking for it. Because they will come looking. They always come looking."

"Who comes looking?"

"The company sends men. Polite men, well-paid men." Reyes smiled again, and this time there was iron under it. "I've outrun them for forty years. Forty years, engineer. Do you know what that is? It's a life spent looking over your shoulder while carrying a stone you cannot put down." His hand found Isandro's wrist—dry, hot, shockingly strong. "I am putting it down. In your hands. That is the bargain."

"I didn't agree to any bargain."

"You will." The old man's eyes didn't waver. "You're the kind that agrees. I watched you carry me off the ballast when you could have logged me as trespasser and caught the afternoon motorcar back down. You counted me. I saw you do it—one head, accounted for. Men who count heads don't leave them behind."

Isandro said nothing, because it was true, and because the truth of it landed in a place he'd kept boarded shut for three years.

"I'm not asking you to understand what's on those sheets," Reyes went on, softer now. "Not yet. Understanding comes later, and it comes hard—that I promise you too. I'm asking you to carry. Can a carrier carry?"

The fire ticked. Above the tunnel mouth, the massif stood black against the stars, its sealed portal a darker darkness, breathing cold air out of the mountain like something sleeping.

"I can carry," Isandro said.

It was, he would think later, the first promise he'd made anyone in three years, and he made it to a dying stranger at the mouth of a dead tunnel, and it felt less like a choice than like a door swinging open onto a room he'd forgotten his house contained.

Reyes nodded, satisfied, and let go of his wrist. "Then sleep," he said. "One of us should."

* * *

He died an hour past dawn.

It was quiet, the way a lamp goes out—no drama, no last words. Isandro had stepped away to refill the canteen at the snowmelt seep fifty meters down the grade, and when he came back the silence had changed key. He knelt, checked, and found what he already knew: no pulse, breath gone, the body already beginning its long surrender to gravity and cold.

He sat beside Mateo Reyes for a while. The morning light came down the valley in long gold shafts, lighting the frost on the ballast stones, gilding the rusted rail that ran into the tunnel mouth and stopped at the steel gate and the company seal, eleven years unbroken. Somewhere upslope a bird was working through its repertoire, indifferent and bright.

Isandro realized he didn't know the man's age, or whether there was anyone to notify, or what rites he'd have wanted. He knew only the name, the trade, the promise, and the weight of the oilcloth bundle now sitting against his own pack like a sleeping animal.

He dug the grave himself. The ground at the tunnel mouth was broken rock and thin soil, and it fought him every stroke, and he let it fight him, because the work felt right—hard, honest, measured in shovel-blows instead of miles. He laid Reyes in facing the mountains he'd walked for forty years, stacked stones over him in a cairn, and drove a length of scrap rebar for a marker, tying to it the man's own kerchief so the color would show travelers that someone lay here who had been somebody.

Then he shouldered his pack, picked up the bundle, and stood a long time looking at the sealed gate.

* * *

That evening, camped in the lee of the old maintenance shed at Kilometer 29, he finally opened the maps all the way.

He told himself it was due diligence—a carrier ought to know what he carries, if only to protect it. But diligence was a thin disguise over the plainer truth: he needed to know what he had promised to deliver, the way a bridge man needs to know what's on the deck before he takes it across.

He spread the linen sheets on the floor of the shed, weighting the corners with stones, and worked through them by lantern light while the wind worried the loose roofing tin overhead.

The drafting was superb. Whoever had drawn these had trained properly—line weights confident, lettering disciplined, hachures rendered with an artist's patience. But the content was what stopped Isandro's breath. These were not a prospector's sketches. They were a record. Every closure date matched the company's own orders, which were supposed to be confidential. Every sealed portal, every pulled rail, every dynamited switchback was marked with the method and the date and—in small marginal script—the number of men assigned to the demolition crews.

And beneath that layer, another. The numbered circles. Isandro traced them with one finger: thirty-one of them, strung through the high country like beads on a wire, each annotated with a date spanning four decades. Campsites, he'd thought at first. Then, reading the margin notes beside circle nineteen—*"told the Vasseur story to the muleteer here; he wept"*—he understood they were waypoints of a different kind. Places where a man had stopped, year after year, keeping something alive by moving it.

Vasseur. The name snagged. He filed it.

Then he turned to the sheet showing the great bore at Kilometer 44, and the guilt he had spent three years burying came up out of the ground like groundwater finding a crack.

He knew this terrain. That was the thing he hadn't let himself know since he'd opened the first sheet: he *knew* it. He had walked the north fork in his third year with the company, young and hungry and precise, running levels along alignments that were already slated for closure, and he had signed the inspection reports that gave the closure orders their engineering justification. Landslides, the reports said. Unstable cuttings. Unsound tunnels. Economic rationalization. He had written the words himself in his neat, upright hand, and the words had been true in every measurable respect, and they had also been a wall built to keep questions out.

Now the same hand-held lantern lit the same terrain on forty-year-old linen, and the margin note beside the bore read:

*"They say the mountain fell on the night shift. The mountain did not fall. I was outside when it happened. I heard what fell. Forty years and I still hear it."*

Isandro sat back on his heels. The lantern hissed. The wind dragged its feet across the roof tin, and far off in the dark, thunder muttered over the massif—the season's first, weeks early, a warning shot fired across the whole sky.

He thought about the neat reports in his own handwriting. He thought about a bridge he did not let himself think about, and the crew he had signed off as fit to cross it, and the three years he had spent measuring his life in chainage markers so he would never again have to measure it in anything that mattered.

Mateo Reyes had carried his stone for forty years. Isandro had spent three trying to convince himself he carried nothing at all.

He rolled the maps with care, bound them, and stowed them deep in his pack, against his spine. Then he took out his notebook—the one where he recorded inspections, distances, the arithmetic of a life pared down to mileage—and stared at the blank next page for a long time.

In the end he wrote only: *Km 29. Carrier engaged. Destination: Meridian Courier.* And beneath it, after a pause, the address from the scrap of paper, copied twice, once in code of his own devising, because polite well-paid men came looking, and he intended to be harder to rob than an old man on foot.

Outside, the thunder rolled closer, counting down toward the monsoon like a surveyor pacing off a distance.

At first light, Isandro broke camp and turned—not down the valley toward the railhead and the easy motorcar back to the world he knew, but up the abandoned grade, toward Kilometer 44, because the maps' final annotation, in handwriting gone shaky with age, pointed that way, and because a promise made to a dying man deserved to be delivered whole.

He had walked perhaps two kilometers when he heard the sound that stopped him mid-stride: voices, ahead, around the shoulder of the cut. More than one. And the sharp metallic ring of a padlock being cut off a company gate that was supposed to open for no one.
