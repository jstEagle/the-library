## 3. A Crew of Strays

The map stayed under Isandro's pillow for three nights, and he slept badly all three, as though the paper were a coal that cooled only when he touched it.

He had considered burning it. That was the honest arithmetic of it: one dead man's obsession against one living man's peace. He had built his life on the principle that trouble could be surveyed around—that if you walked the ground carefully enough, read the grades and the watercourses and the fault lines, you could route yourself past anything. Three years of chainage markers and instrument checks and never once learning the name of the cook's dog. It had worked. It had kept him breathing, which he had come to understand was not quite the same as living, but it was adjacent to it, and adjacency was enough.

But Mateo Reyes had died holding his wrist with fingers like dry roots, and dying men's promises did not survey cleanly around. They cut straight lines through whatever stood in their way.

So on the fourth morning, before the sun cleared the ridge and turned the valley fog to hammered tin, Isandro sat at the camp's folding table with the map spread flat and weighted at the corners with four stones, and he began, for the first time in three years, to plan something he could not measure.

* * *

Odile Brant found him there. She always moved quietly for a woman built like a bridge piling—she claimed it came from twenty years of walking ballast, where a heavy foot meant a twisted ankle—and she stood over the table drinking her tea for a long moment before she said anything.

"That's closed-line country," she said.

It was not a question, so he did not answer it. She pulled up an empty crate, sat, and looked at the map upside down, which she could do; Odile read terrain the way other people read faces, from any angle, in any light.

"Kilometer forty-four," she said, tapping the air above a bend in the blue line. "The viaduct over the Río Blanco. I helped pour those piers when I was nineteen. My first job." Her finger drifted north along the line, into the hatch-marked blankness where the company's own maps simply stopped, as though the mountains beyond had been erased by decree. "And this here is nothing. Officially nothing. You don't route an expedition through officially nothing."

"I'm not routing an expedition," Isandro said. "I'm delivering a letter."

"Letters go in envelopes. That goes in a crew." She set down her tea. "You're going to need someone who knows how the old bridges sit after fifteen years of nobody loving them. Someone who can tell you which trestle will hold a mule and which will hold its breath while you cross and drop you into the gorge on the way back."

"You're a foreman, Odile. You have a crew."

"My crew is laying culverts until the rains come, and then my crew is laid off until spring, same as every year." She looked at him then, directly, with those pale eyes that made new hires confess mistakes they hadn't yet made. "And you're going to say no, because you don't ask anybody for anything, Isandro Zhou. You've spent three years being furniture. Well-made furniture. Furniture that shows up on time and never borrows a man's tools or tells him about his day."

The words landed harder than they should have, because they were true, and because she said them without cruelty—the way you'd note a crack in a rail head. Inspection, not judgment.

"The last time I led men across a structure," Isandro said quietly, "it fell down."

"I know." She said it the way she'd have said *the last train was late*. No flinch, no softening. "Half the valley knows. The other half doesn't care." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and gathered her cup. "I'll be at the supply shed at six tomorrow. If you've got the sense to say yes, bring a manifest. If you haven't, I'll know by the empty chair, and I'll stop wasting my mornings on you."

She left him with the map and the fog and the strange, unwelcome sensation of having been seen—which was, he realized, exactly what he had spent three years arranging his life to avoid.

* * *

Pell Wexler he found in the county seat, in the back room of a print shop that smelled of ink and damp paper, drawing charts for a living that no longer deserved the name *chart*—county drainage plans, plat maps, a funeral notice for the printer's brother-in-law, ornate and sorrowful.

He was thinner than Isandro remembered, grayer, with ink permanently shadowing the creases of his knuckles like a stain he'd stopped trying to wash out. When Isandro laid the prospector's map on his worktable, Wexler went still in a way that told Isandro everything before he said a word.

"Where did you get this?" The cartographer's voice had gone careful, the way a man walks on ice.

"A dying man gave it to me. Mateo Reyes."

Wexler sat down slowly, as though his legs had been let out of a contract. He put on his spectacles and bent over the map, and Isandro watched thirty years of training move through the man's hands—he didn't touch the paper, but his fingers hovered above it, tracing the linework in the air, reading the hachures, the hand-lettered elevations, the marginal annotations in faded brown ink.

"This isn't a prospector's sketch," Wexler murmured. "This is survey-grade. Chain and transit. Somebody ran actual instruments over this ground." His finger stopped at the sealed section, the blankness beyond kilometer fifty-one. "And somebody else tried very hard to make sure no official chart ever agreed with it."

"That's why I came to you."

"No." Wexler took off the spectacles. "That's why you came to me, but it isn't why you should have. Do you know what I am now, Zhou? I'm the man who draws funeral notices. Before that I was the man who drew a river where a river wasn't, because a man with a better office than mine asked me to, and eleven years later everyone who ever trusted a line I drew has good reason not to." He laughed, short and airless. "I falsified one chart. One. And it ended three careers, mine included, and cost the company a season's grading and a lawsuit, and none of it killed anyone, which is the part that keeps me up at night—if I could do that much damage *by accident*, obediently, what would I do on purpose?"

"I'm not asking you to draw anything," Isandro said. "I'm asking you to read. This map has margin notes—forty years of them, it looks like. Corrections, observations, dates. Whoever wrote them knew this country better than any office ever did. I need someone who can speak that language."

Wexler looked at the map a long time. Then he looked at the funeral notice, half-finished, the brother-in-law's name rendered in beautiful mourning script.

"You know what the worst part was?" he said, conversationally, as though answering a question Isandro hadn't asked. "Not the disgrace. The quiet. Afterward, nobody argued with me about anything. A man can survive losing his reputation. What he can't survive is being handed no more chances to be useful." He began rolling down his sleeves, buttoning them at the wrist with the deliberation of a priest vesting. "When do we leave?"

"As soon as I find one more person."

"Who?"

Isandro hesitated. "Her name is Suri Reyes."

Wexler's hands stopped on the last button.

* * *

Suri Reyes found *him*, which he should have expected. He'd sent a letter to an address in the lowland port town—a forwarding address, a hope—and ten days later she walked into the survey camp at dusk, mud to her knees, a canvas pack on her shoulders and fury riding her like a second spine.

She was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, sun-browned, with her father's exact jaw and none of his gentleness. She stood at the edge of the firelight while the crew pretended not to watch, and she said, "Which one of you is Isandro Zhou?"

"I am."

"You wrote to me that my father died." Her voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "Four sentences. Four sentences about a man I hadn't seen in nine years, from a stranger whose name I didn't know, telling me he collapsed at a tunnel mouth on a line that's been closed since before I could walk. So you're going to tell me what he was doing there, and what he gave you, and why your letter smelled like a man trying to get out of something."

The crew had gone very quiet. Odile, who had arrived two days early and taken over the supply shed like a general annexing territory, set down the crate she was loading and folded her arms.

Isandro met the daughter's eyes across the fire.

"He was prospecting the closed line," he said. "He had a map. Forty years of notes on it. He made me promise to take it somewhere safe before the monsoon, and he died before he told me where *somewhere safe* was."

"Safefrom whom?"

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do." She stepped into the firelight. Up close he could see the resemblance was more than bone—it was in the way her eyes moved over terrain, cataloguing, measuring, the inherited habit of a man who had spent a lifetime looking at country instead of at his daughter. "Nobody carries a map for forty years unless someone wants it burned. Who?"

"I met a man named Vasseur two days ago," Isandro said. "Well-dressed. Polite. Offered me five hundred dollars for it. When I said no, he smiled like I'd confirmed something for him."

Something shifted in Suri's face—not fear, he noted, but recognition, the grim satisfaction of a suspicion vindicated. "Then we leave tonight," she said. "Because Vasseur doesn't offer twice. He offers, and then he sends men."

"We leave at dawn," Odile corrected, from the shadows of the shed. "Tonight you'd walk off a cliff in the dark, girl, and I'm not carrying you. Dawn, with proper loads and proper boots. If you're coming."

"I'm coming." Suri said it like a door closing. "He was my father. Whatever he found, whatever he was running from, it's mine before it's anyone's."

"It's not yours," Isandro said, and heard his own voice come out flat and hard, surprising himself. "It's a promise I made to a dying man. You can come. But the map travels with me, and it goes where I decide it goes."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and abandoned.

Suri stared at him, and he watched her weigh it—the stranger, the promise, the four-sentence letter—and he saw the moment she decided that a flawed guardian was better than none, and hated herself a little for deciding it.

"Dawn," she said finally. "And Zhou—you'll tell me everything he said. Every word. On the trail, tonight, tomorrow, however long it takes. I've lost nine years to that man's silences. I won't lose his last ones too."

* * *

They assembled at first light in gray drizzle: Odile with two mules loaded to her exacting specifications, a coil of rope, and a pry bar she called *persuasion*; Wexler with a waterproof satchel of pencils, a field notebook, and a look of a man reporting to a prison he'd requested; Suri in trail clothes worn shiny at the elbows, saying goodbye to no one because there was no one.

Isandro stood at the head of it with the map in an oilcloth wrap against his chest, and looked at the four of them—foreman, forger, orphan, coward—and thought that if Mateo Reyes had planned this, he had a cruel sense of humor. Not one of them whole. Not one of them with anything to lose except each other, and none of them yet aware they'd acquired that.

"Rules," Odile announced, swinging up beside her mules. "We move at the pace of the slowest animal. Nobody wanders. Nobody plays hero. And nobody—" she looked pointedly at each of them in turn, "—nobody keeps a secret that gets somebody killed. We've all got our scars. Fine. Scars are load-bearing. Secrets aren't."

Wexler's face did something complicated and quick. Isandro felt the map's weight against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and said nothing.

Suri mounted without help and looked north, up the valley, toward the haze where the closed line vanished into officially nothing.

"Well then," she said. "Lead on, engineer. Let's see what my father wouldn't tell me."

They moved out into the rain, four strays and two mules, and behind them the camp shrank to smoke and memory, and ahead the mountains waited with their mouths shut.
