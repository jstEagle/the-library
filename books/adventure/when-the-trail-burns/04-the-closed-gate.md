## 4. The Closed Gate

The gate was exactly where the map said it would be, and that was the first thing that made Suri Reyes angry.

She had spent eleven days in the company of strangers, walking a line her father had drawn from memory and obsession, and every single time she braced herself to find him wrong—every time she allowed herself the small, private satisfaction of proving Mateo Reyes a fool even in death—the map was right. The trail forked at the lightning-split cedar: right. The spring ran bitter with iron: right. The landslide scar they'd skirted for half a morning, loose shale hissing down into the gorge like something breathing: right again, marked on the map in her father's cramped hand with a note she could almost hear him muttering—*unstable after rain, keep to the high side, no shortcuts*.

No shortcuts. As if he were still watching. As if he'd ever watched anything but paper.

Suri stood now at the treeline's ragged edge and looked up at the closed gate of the Cordillera section, and hated how well she understood it.

The railroad had sealed this stretch twenty-two years ago, when she was four years old and her father was already more absent than present. Officially: landslides. Unofficially: nobody said anything at all, which was its own kind of answer. The company had dropped a steel gate across the old right-of-way where the line entered the first cut, welded it shut, posted signs that had long since bleached to ghosts of themselves—*DANGER, CLOSED TO TRAFFIC, TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED*—and then, apparently, forgotten the whole business. The forest had been patient about reclaiming it. Alder grew through the ballast in silver-green spears. Moss upholstered the rails themselves, turning forty pounds of steel per yard into soft green spines disappearing into the cut like the vertebrae of something buried alive.

Beyond the gate, the mountain rose in folded walls of granite and hemlock, and somewhere up there, threaded through it all, was the tunnel. *The* tunnel. Her father's last annotation on the map—a note added so recently the ink had barely browned—said only: *the bore holds what they paid for. Bring witnesses.*

Bring witnesses. Not bring me home. Not tell my daughter.

Suri pressed her thumb against the annotation until the paper dented.

"You're doing the thing," Odile Brant said beside her.

Suri didn't look at her. "What thing."

"The thing where you stare at the map like it insulted your mother." Odile was a broad woman built like something meant to bear loads, foreman's hands scarred and split, gray coming into her cropped hair the way frost comes into a field—one cold night at a time. She carried a pry bar over one shoulder the way other people carried umbrellas. "I've known men who talked to their maps. Your father just wrote back."

"He didn't write back." Suri folded the map along its worn creases—creases she was beginning to know by touch, which was another thing to be angry about. "He wrote *forward*. Forty years of forward. There isn't a single note in here about—" She stopped. Swallowed it. "It doesn't matter. Let's get the gate open before the light goes."

Behind them, Pell Wexler was repacking his satchel with the fussy precision of a man whose entire life had once fit inside a leather case and now fit nowhere at all. He'd been quiet all morning, quieter than usual, and Suri had caught him twice looking north along the line with an expression she couldn't read—something between recognition and dread. The disgraced cartographer. The man who drew beautiful charts and had, according to the story Isandro told her on the second night, ended his career with one of them. He hadn't denied it. He hadn't confirmed it either. He just adjusted his spectacles and said, "Maps are arguments, Miss Reyes. Some of them win, and some of them lose, and the losing is rarely fair," and then he'd walked on ahead.

And then there was Isandro Zhou.

He stood apart from all of them, as he had stood apart for eleven days, studying the gate with an engineer's flat attention. Three years of survey work had taught Suri to read the types: the ones who counted miles because miles couldn't die, the ones who measured everything because measuring was safer than remembering. Isandro was the worst kind. He wasn't cold, exactly. Cold implied a temperature you could warm. He was *level*, like water finding its plane, and twice on the trail when she'd stumbled he'd simply extended a hand without comment, without warmth, without asking anything in return—and she'd taken it both times, and hated that too.

"Cutting torch is going to be useless," he said now. "That weld is structural. They wanted it permanent."

"So we go around," Odile said.

"There's no around. Look at the cut walls." He pointed with his chin. "They blasted this approach into solid rock. The only way through is the way the trains went through."

"Then we're not cutting," Suri said. "We're lifting."

Three faces turned to her.

"The gate's hung on pins," she said, and heard her own voice take on the lecturing edge she'd sworn she wouldn't use with these people. "Company standard for emergency access gates—they have to be removable if the line needs reopening. My father worked maintenance contracts on sections like this for six years. He used to sketch the hardware in his margins because he thought it was interesting." She hated that she knew this. She hated that some part of her had absorbed her father's obsessions through sheer proximity, like moss absorbing rain, without ever being asked. "Bottom hinge pin on each side. You drive them up and out, and the gate lifts free. Two strong backs and a lever."

Odile grinned slowly. "Two strong backs. I count three."

"I'm counting the two that aren't mine," Suri said, and was rewarded—or punished—with a short laugh from Isandro, the first she'd heard from him. It changed his face entirely, and she looked away quickly, annoyed at having caused it.

* * *

The pins fought them.

Of course they did. Twenty-two years of rust had fused pin to housing, and the first hour was spent in a war of leverage and language—Odile swearing with the fluency of a dockworker, Pell holding the lantern and offering historical commentary nobody requested ("In the northern districts they'd pour tallow and ash into the housings; the old track-walkers swore by it"), Isandro working in silence with a hammer and a drift punch, striking with a rhythm so metronomic it began to feel like a heartbeat in the gathering dusk.

Suri held the second lantern and tried not to watch him.

But it was hard not to watch him. There was something almost devotional in the way Isandro worked metal—no wasted motion, no frustration, just problem after problem received and answered. When the drift punch mushroomed, he dressed it on a rock without complaint. When the pin gave a quarter-inch and then seized again, he simply shifted his stance, found new purchase, struck again. Once, in the space between blows, he glanced up at her and said, "Your father's margin notes on the gate hardware. Were there torque figures?"

"What?"

"For the pins. Did he record how much force—"

"How should I know? I didn't read his homework." A lie. She'd read every page of the map twice. There *were* torque figures, penciled small and neat, and she had no intention of handing Isandro Zhou another reason to admire Mateo Reyes.

The pin came free at full dark, with a shriek of tearing oxide that echoed off the cut walls and rolled away up the mountain like something waking. The second took another forty minutes. Then Odile and Isandro set their shoulders under the gate's lower rail, Pell wedged the lever, and on Suri's count the steel came up and swung aside on its remaining hinges with a groan that sounded, to her ears, disturbingly like a door opening onto a room that had been locked for a reason.

Cold air breathed out of the cut. It smelled of stone and standing water and something older underneath—mineral, faintly sulfurous, like the breath of deep places.

"Well," Odile said, straightening, rolling her shoulders. "Either we're geniuses or we're burglars."

"Both," Pell said mildly. "Cartography has always been adjacent to burglary. We steal the shape of the world and sell it back to itself."

Nobody laughed. The air out of the cut was still moving against their faces, though there was no wind above the treeline, and Suri found she was holding her lantern higher than she needed to, as if light were a thing you could post as a sentry.

They camped that night inside the cut itself, sheltered under an overhang fifty yards past the gate, and Suri lay awake listening to the mountain tick and settle, and thinking about her father's handwriting. Forty years of annotations. Springs, grades, slide zones, bearing trees, the names of every track-walker who'd ever signed a station log on this line. He had known this country the way she'd resented knowing him: intimately, constantly, without ever being invited.

*He chose this over you,* she reminded herself. *Every field season. Every birthday. Every letter that was really a report.*

She fell asleep angry, which at least was familiar.

* * *

Morning came up gray and dripping, cloud sitting on the ridge like a lid on a pot, and they broke camp and started up the dead line toward the tunnel.

Walking an abandoned railroad is a particular kind of haunting. The grade is easy—the engineers who built it refused steepness the way priests refuse heresy—but everything else argues with you. Ties rotted to black lace underfoot. Ballast shifted and rolled ankles. And always the rails themselves, running ahead into fog, doubling the world, two bright-green lines pointing at something you couldn't yet see. Suri walked point because nobody had assigned it and somebody had to, and because walking point meant she didn't have to make conversation.

By midmorning the cut widened into the first bench above the river gorge, and the line curved along a shelf blasted from the mountainside, the drop falling away to their left into white noise and mist. Water was everywhere now, feeding down from the snowfields in a hundred threads, and the sound of it filled the whole corridor of the mountain like applause from an enormous invisible crowd.

It was Pell who stopped first.

He had gone ahead a few paces—uncharacteristic—and stood very still, head tilted, one hand raised. Suri came up beside him and followed his gaze down to the ballast at the outer edge of the shelf.

Boot prints.

Fresh ones. Pressed into the soft mud of the drainage ditch where seep water had softened the ground, sharp-edged, unfaded, the tread pattern crisp as a stamp. Hobnails, military pattern, large. A dozen strides of them, walking *up*-line, in the direction of the tunnel. And beside them, lighter, smaller prints—rubber-soled, quick, pacing back and forth across the ties like someone waiting. Or watching.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Above them the cloud sat on the ridge. The river applauded. Somewhere upslope, water dripped from a ledge with the regularity of a clock.

"That's days old at most," Isandro said quietly. He crouched, not touching, reading the mud the way he'd read the gate. "Rain two nights ago would have softened these further. The edges are still clean. One day. Maybe less."

"People don't walk this line," Odile said. It wasn't a question; it was a statement of geography. "There's nothing up there to walk to. That's why they sealed it. Nobody comes to a closed line unless they're paid to or hiding from something."

"Then which are they?" Pell asked.

Suri crouched beside the prints. Up close she could see the hobnail pattern clearly, and something else—a scuff at the edge of the ditch where a heavy load had been set down and dragged. Something had been carried up this line recently. Something heavy enough to need resting.

Her father's map weighed suddenly enormous in her pack. *Others have come looking,* the hermit at Kilometer 44 hadn't said yet—that warning was still nine days and one terrible fire away—but the map itself had already told her, in its wordless way, that Mateo Reyes had not been the only man obsessed with this mountain. Obsession, she was learning, was a country with more than one citizen.

"We should turn back," Pell said. His voice had gone thin. "This changes the calculation. Whatever's up there, someone else has anticipated us. Anticipation is the enemy of—"

"Of what?" Odile asked.

"Of being early," Pell finished, and pushed his spectacles up, and looked, for a moment, genuinely frightened.

Isandro stood and brushed off his knees. When he spoke, his voice was level as ever, but Suri noticed—because she was learning to notice things about him against her will—that his eyes weren't on the prints anymore. They were on the tunnel's direction, up-line, where the rails vanished into fog.

"They didn't come down," he said. "Whoever it was. The prints go up. None come back."

The words settled over the group like the cloud settling over the ridge.

"So they're still up there," Suri said.

"Or they went through," Isandro said. "Into the bore."

Nobody moved. The river applauded. The drip from the ledge kept its time.

Suri found her father's map in her hands without remembering taking it out. She unfolded it to the section they were standing in, and there it was, in his hand, dated thirty-one years ago: a note at this exact bend of the shelf, marking a seep, a safe camp, a sightline to the far wall. He had stood here. He had stood in this exact place, probably in weather exactly like this, and written down what he saw, because writing down what he saw was the only way Mateo Reyes had ever known how to love anything.

*You knew every inch of this mountain,* she thought. *Did you know who else would come?*

She refolded the map. The anger was still there—it was always there, her oldest companion, more faithful than any father—but beneath it now, for the first time, something else had begun to move. Unease, maybe. Or the beginning of understanding. The map hadn't led them anywhere wrong yet. That was precisely what frightened her.

"Point position's mine," she said, louder than she needed to. "Keep spacing. Nobody walks on the ties—stay on the ballast, watch the ditch side."

Odile unslung the pry bar and held it low against her thigh, and her grin from yesterday was gone entirely. Pell tucked his satchel tight to his ribs like armor. Isandro fell in behind Suri without a word, close enough that she could hear his breathing, steady as a metronome, and she hated how much she didn't hate it.

They went up the dead line together, four strangers on a road built for trains, following boot prints that had no business existing, toward a tunnel her father had died trying to describe.

Above them, the cloud lowered. And somewhere in the fog ahead, faint and rhythmic, so regular it might have been water dripping from a ledge—

Something was knocking. Metal on metal. Patient as a clock.

Keeping time for whoever was waiting inside the mountain.
