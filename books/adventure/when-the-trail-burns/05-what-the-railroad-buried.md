## 5. What the Railroad Buried

The depot announced itself before they saw it: a smell of creosote and cold iron drifting down the cut like a rumor. Pell Wexler smelled it first, which surprised no one. A cartographer's nose, Odile said, was just his eyes relocated to his face, and Pell had learned long ago to accept her insults as a species of affection.

They came around the shoulder of the embankment in the last grey hour of afternoon, and there it sat in its saucer of weeds—the Kilometer 31 maintenance depot, if the faded stenciling on the water tower was to be believed. A long shed with a roof of corrugated iron gone the color of dead leaves. A coal bunker caved in on one side like a bitten biscuit. Two handcars rusting to their axles, their wooden beds furred with moss so thick they looked upholstered. Beyond it all, the tunnel mouth yawned in the mountainside, its timber portal dark with forty years of rain, and the masonry seal across it broken open just wide enough to admit a man who had decided, somewhere on the trail, that he was the kind of man who walked through broken seals.

The company had not bothered to tear the depot down when they closed the line. That was the first thing Pell noticed, and the first thing that troubled him. Companies tore down what they wanted forgotten. They left standing what they wanted available—for whoever came back.

"Roof's sound over the office end," Odile said, already moving, already the foreman, her lantern swinging from one hand and her crowbar from the other. "We'll make camp inside. Suri, with me. Isandro—check that shed wall, I don't like the way it's leaning. Pell."

He braced himself.

"See what there is to see."

It was the work she always gave him, and he had never decided whether it was trust or sentence. *See what there is to see.* Four years ago those words would have been his whole religion. He had seen what there was to see, once, in a chart room in the capital, and he had drawn the line where he was told to draw it, and a river had shifted three degrees from his beautiful false contour, and no one had died—but a surveyor had lost his license, a district chief had lost his career, and Pell Wexler had lost the only thing he'd ever been good at, or so he had believed until a stubborn foreman and a dead prospector's map had handed him a compass again.

Now he had a lantern, a knee that ached when the weather turned, and an abandoned depot full of paper.

Paper was the one thing the mountains could not quite kill.

* * *

The office was a room the size of a coffin stood on end, and twice as comfortable. A desk with one leg shimmed on a brick. A stool. A filing cabinet with its drawers hanging open like a man gasping. Rain had found a seam in the roof years ago and had been patiently writing its signature down one wall in black water-stain, a long spidery flourish Pell half expected to be able to read.

He set his lantern on the desk and opened the cabinet.

Most of it was nothing. Requisition forms gone soft as cloth. Time sheets for men whose names meant nothing—Alvarenga, Cho, Mbeki, Ferreira, the same dozen surnames cycling through the weeks like weather. Fuel ledgers. A complaint about the mess car's rice. The sediment of ordinary work, and Pell found it oddly moving, this drift of small paperwork settled at the bottom of an abandoned place, the way silt settles in a channel after the river stops caring.

Then, at the back of the bottom drawer, wedged upright behind the cabinet's false panel—a false panel, in a maintenance depot, which was a thing no honest depot needed—he found the oilskin packet.

His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself with a kind of detached professional interest, the way he'd once noticed his own hand not shaking while he inked the falsified contour. Steadiness, he had learned, was not the same as courage. Sometimes it was just the stillness of a man standing on a crack in the ice, afraid that moving would finish the job.

He carried the packet to the lantern and sat on the stool, and the stool complained, and he ignored it.

Inside: correspondence. Maybe forty sheets, some carbon-copies flaking like moth wings, some originals in a hand so hard it had embossed the paper. He sorted them by date with the automatic tenderness of a man who had spent his life putting things in order, and began at the beginning.

The earliest was dated fourteen months before the closure—before the landslide, before the official story, before the elegant memo he himself had once read in a company archive and accepted without question, because accepting was easier and he had been a man who chose easy. It was from the office of the Chief Engineer, Western Division, to the resident engineer at this very depot. The handwriting was beautiful. That was the thing that got him first, how beautiful it was. Cruelty nearly always came in a fine hand.

*...with reference to your report of the 9th, you are instructed to suspend all further excavation of the secondary bore. The matter is now above this Division's authority. You will render no further reports in writing on this subject. Acknowledge by wire only.*

Pell read it three times.

*The secondary bore.* He knew the main tunnel—knew it from the charts, from the closure orders, from the long memorized litany of the sealed lines. But a secondary bore. A second heading, driven off the first, that appeared on none of the surveys he had ever seen. He was, he reminded himself, one of perhaps a dozen men alive who had memorized the closed lines' engineering files, and he had never once seen a reference to a secondary bore at Kilometer 31.

You did not drive a tunnel heading and then erase it from the drawings unless you were ashamed of it.

The next letter was three weeks later, and it was different. The beautiful hand had given way to something cramped and hurried, a resident engineer writing fast and afraid, and the carbon was smudged where someone had gripped the page hard enough to warp it.

*I must protest, again, the disposition of the remains. The families of the deceased are entitled to notification under the Act of '09, and I cannot in conscience continue to certify a workforce roster that lists men as transferred who are in fact—*

The sentence stopped. Not faded—*stopped*, mid-word, the pen lifted, and beneath it in different ink, in that beautiful hand again, a single line:

*Mr. Osei. Your conscience is noted. Your continued certification is required. The alternative to your cooperation has been explained to Mr. Osei's family.*

Pell set the letter down on the desk.

The lantern hissed. Outside, faintly, he could hear the others—Odile's voice pitched low, giving orders; the ring of the crowbar against something; Isandro's quieter tones, measuring something, because Isandro measured everything. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who trusted him, camped forty feet from a room where he sat holding the reason the line had been closed, the reason Mateo Reyes had spent forty years carrying a map no one was supposed to want, the reason men with polished boots came looking for paper in the high country.

The line hadn't been closed for landslides.

The line had been closed because men had died in the secondary bore—dozens of them, the letter's arithmetic implied hundreds—and the company had sealed the tunnel with the men still in it, erased them from the rolls, transferred them on paper to divisions that had never heard their names, and dynamited the portal shut over the whole unbearable truth. And a resident engineer named Osei had certified the lie because the alternative had been explained to his family, and then Osei had hidden this correspondence behind a false panel in a depot at Kilometer 31, which meant Osei had wanted it found, someday, by someone, and had then—Pell checked the last letters, the dates thinning out, the signatures changing—been transferred. Or disappeared. The final letter was a receipt, signed by a man who was not Osei, acknowledging *one (1) oilskin packet, contents unlisted*, and the depot's paper trail went dark six weeks before the official closure.

Pell sat with it.

This was what he was good at. This was, in the end, the whole of his disgraced gift: he could read a document the way other men read weather. And what he read here was a storm that had been blowing for forty years behind a wall of official masonry, and that he was now the only living person who knew it was coming.

He thought about carrying the letters out to the fire. He pictured it precisely: the lantern light, Suri's face—Suri, who had spent the whole trail learning her father's margins, learning to love a dead man through his handwriting—and the moment she understood that Mateo Reyes had not been a crank or an obsessive, that the map in her hands was a witness statement, that her father had known. Odile would go quiet in the way she went quiet before hard work. Isandro would go still in the way he went still around anything that reminded him of weight and collapse.

And then what?

Then the letters would stop being his. They would be everyone's, and everyone would have to decide, and decisions made by tired people around a fire at the edge of a sealed tunnel were the decisions that got people captured, or followed, or dead. Vasseur's men were somewhere behind them on the trail—Odile had found the boot prints two days ago, fresh ones, patient ones. If the company had sent Mr. Vasseur all this way for a *map*, what would they send for *letters*? A map could be dismissed as an old man's obsession. Letters with signatures were a different animal entirely. Letters were evidence. Evidence was the one thing that could not be negotiated with, which meant it was the one thing that would be taken at any cost.

*The alternative to your cooperation has been explained to Mr. Osei's family.*

Pell looked at the firelight leaking under the office door and thought: I know what truth costs. I have paid retail. And the price was not mine alone—it never is. That was the lesson of the falsified chart. Not that lying was dangerous. That truth, handled carelessly, in the wrong light, at the wrong hour, could kill just as dead, and the man holding it got to choose who stood in the splash radius.

He was still telling himself this when he heard Suri laugh outside at something Odile said, a short, surprised sound, the laugh of a woman who had not expected to laugh on this trail, and the sound went through him like a splinter.

He folded the letters back into their original order—he could not help himself; order was his prayer—and he did not put them in the oilskin. He put them inside his own shirt, against his chest, flat and cold as a second skin, and he put the empty oilskin packet back behind the false panel, and he weighted the panel with the coal shovel, and he stood.

The stool complained again. He ignored that too.

* * *

Dinner was rice and tinned sausage and Odile's coffee, which could have stripped paint and was therefore, in Odile's cosmology, perfect. They ate in the depot's main shed around a fire built in an old forge hearth, because Odile did not believe in cooking outdoors when a roof existed, and the firelight moved on the corrugated walls and made the whole space breathe like the inside of some enormous sleeping animal.

Pell sat with his tin plate and his secret against his ribs and told himself he was not lying. He was *staging*. He was a cartographer; he understood that a map revealed itself in passes. First pass: the terrain. Second pass: the contours. Third pass: the truth. He would tell them. He would tell them tomorrow, or the day after, when they were rested, when he had thought through the security of it, when—

"Paper?" Suri asked.

He looked up. She was nodding at his hands, which had ink on them—he'd taken notes, of course he'd taken notes, his fingers had been moving all evening the way they moved when his mind was elsewhere, little unconscious cartographies on any surface.

"Inventory," he said. "The office. Requisitions, time sheets. Nothing."

The word *nothing* came out of him smooth and easy and it was the smoothest, easiest lie he had ever told, and it tasted like the ink it was written in.

"Nothing," Odile repeated, and there was something in her voice—not suspicion, never suspicion, she trusted her crew the way she trusted her bridges, absolutely and without inspection—and that was so much worse. "Company walked away clean, then."

"Companies always do," Pell said.

Isandro said nothing. Isandro was at the edge of the firelight with the map, as he always was in the evenings, studying Mateo Reyes's annotations with that engineer's squint, tracing the dead man's route with one finger. But at Pell's words his finger stopped. Just stopped, on the paper, on the contour lines of the country around Kilometer 31, and he looked up—not at Pell, at the dark mouth of the tunnel visible through the shed's broken doors—and his face did the thing it did when he was thinking about weight and collapse, and then he looked back down at the map.

Pell told himself Isandro had heard nothing. Isandro was a man who lived inside his own weather; his silences were not directed at anyone.

He told himself that twice.

Suri was reading her father's margins by the fire, her lips moving, and once she said, softly, to no one: "He measured everything. Even the things he wouldn't write down." And Pell's chest, where the letters lay flat and cold, felt like a load-bearing wall with a crack running through it, and he thought: *a structure doesn't fail all at once. It fails at the point where the load is greatest and the material is weakest, and the failure is slow, and it is visible, if anyone thinks to look, long before it is fatal.*

He was a surveyor of cracks. He had made his living, once, knowing exactly where the ground was about to give.

He knew, lying awake that night on a bedroll in a dead man's depot with forty letters against his heart and the tunnel breathing its cold air through the broken doors, that he was the weak material. He knew that the load was coming—Suri's questions, Isandro's silences, Odile's terrible trusting eyes—and he knew that he would have to choose, soon, between the two things he had never managed to hold at once: the truth, and the people he was beginning to love.

He had chosen wrong once before, in a warm chart room with good light, and it had cost him everything except his life.

He closed his eyes and listened to the mountain, and beneath the wind he could have sworn—though he was a man of documents and not of omens—that the tunnel was breathing. In. Out. In. Out. Like something patient, waiting to be asked what it had been carrying all these years.

And far off down the cut, so faint it might have been the wind worrying the rails, came the sound he had been dreading without knowing its name: footsteps on old ballast. Measured. Unhurried. Coming on.
