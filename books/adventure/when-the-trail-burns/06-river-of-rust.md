## 6. River of Rust

We heard the river before we saw it.

That was the first wrong thing. Rivers in the high country are supposed to announce themselves politely—a glint through the trees, a white noise you notice gradually, the way you notice your own heartbeat. This one roared. It came up the valley like a rumor of war, and by the time we crested the last ridge above the gorge, the sound had worked its way into my teeth.

"There it is," Odile said, and spat over the edge, which was her way of paying respect to anything larger than herself.

The viaduct crossed the gorge maybe eighty feet below us: nine spans of riveted iron on cut-stone piers, running dead straight through the mist, or what should have been dead straight. The river had come up over its banks somewhere upstream—monsoon-fed, weeks early—and now it ran brown and muscular through the gorge, swallowing the lower third of the structure. Water broke against the second pier in a standing wave of foam and debris, whole trees cartwheeling past like they weighed nothing, and where the track bed dipped toward the middle spans, the flood had found the ballast and was eating it alive.

The ironwork itself was rusted to the color of dried blood. Hence, I suppose, the name some forgotten surveyor had given it. River of Rust. He'd probably meant the color of the water in dry season, orange with laterite. Today it was the color of strong tea left out too long, and it moved like something with an opinion about us.

"That's not a crossing," Pell said. "That's a dare."

"It's a crossing," Odile said. "The map says so."

"The map," Suri said quietly, "is forty years old." She stood a little apart from us, as she always did at rest stops, holding her father's map rolled in one hand like a baton she hadn't decided whether to run with or beat someone with. But her eyes were on the viaduct, and I watched her do what I did—read the structure, count the spans, look for the lie in it. She'd been doing more of that lately. Reading terrain like her father. It seemed to annoy her every time she caught herself at it.

I knelt at the lip of the gorge and got my bearings properly, because that was the one thing I was still good for. The deck was intact for the first four spans. Then the ballast was gone—washed clean off the deck beams, leaving bare iron ribs slick with spray—and after that, span six showed a twist in the lateral bracing that made my stomach drop in a way I recognized and hated. Not collapsed. Deformed. Loaded past its working stress sometime in the last decade and frozen there, holding its breath.

"Deck's walkable to span five," I said. "After that we're on bare stringers. They're wet, they're rusted, and there's no parapet worth the name. One slip and the river takes you under the seventh pier, and nothing comes back up."

"Cheerful," Odile said.

"I'm an engineer. Cheerfulness is extra."

But I kept looking at the water, because that was the thing I couldn't stop looking at, and I knew—I knew with the cold certainty of a man who has already lived his worst day once—that the water was still rising. You could read it in the stain line on the stone piers, a dark band two feet above the current surface, fresh, no moss on it yet. The river had been higher this morning. It would be higher tonight.

Whatever window we had, it was closing.

* * *

We camped that night on the flat above the eastern abutment, under a stand of shivering casuarina, and nobody talked much. Rain came and went in fitful gusts, warm rain, the kind that doesn't cool anything but just adds weight to the world. Pell spread his charts under a tarp and pretended to study them. Suri sat with her back against a trunk, the map open across her knees, tracing her father's annotations by lantern light. And Odile—

Odile was looking at the equipment.

We were carrying too much of it. That was the honest arithmetic of the expedition: the photographic plates and the document case, the survey instruments, the food for eight days that was really food for six if the weather turned, the rope, the tools. We'd shed weight twice already, and each time it had felt like shedding skin. What remained was the part we couldn't afford to lose—the evidence, or the promise of evidence, whatever Mateo Reyes had sent his daughter into these mountains to find. Without the instruments and the plates, whatever we found would stay found only by us, and I had learned three years ago how long a thing stays true when only witnesses carry it.

"We cross at first light," Odile announced, in the tone she used for things that were not discussions. "Before the sun gets the melt going. Water'll be lowest at dawn."

"Water's fed by rain, not melt," Pell said.

"Then it'll be lowest at dawn anyway, because even the sky sleeps sometimes." She started repacking the rope, coiling it with the quick violence of a woman who needed her hands busy. "Two loads. Equipment goes first, lashed to the rope line, ferried across the bare section hand over hand. Then people. Wexler, you're the lightest, you take the line across and anchor the far side. Zhou rigs the anchors. I bring the heavy cases over last."

"And me?" Suri asked.

"You're second across. You hold the map and you don't argue."

Suri opened her mouth—to argue, obviously—but something in Odile's face closed the argument before it started. I'd seen Odile Brant do that to men twice her size for eleven years. It never stopped being slightly frightening.

I lay awake most of the night listening to the river. Somewhere below us, in the dark, it was climbing the piers, foot by foot, patient as arithmetic. I told myself the numbers. Deck elevation, thirty-one meters above low water. Current stage, call it nineteen. Rate of rise, half a meter in the last six hours, decelerating if the rain held off. Margin of safety, generous. The numbers were fine. The numbers were always fine.

It wasn't the numbers that kept me up. It was the sound. Water moving fast under iron has a particular voice—a deep, resonant hum, the whole structure singing faintly in its sleep—and I had heard that voice once before, at a place called Kestrel Gorge, on a night when the singing stopped meaning anything except that the world was about to rearrange itself and take four men with it.

Three years, and I could still hear the exact pitch.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, and the river had risen another foot in the night. I measured it against the stain line and didn't tell anyone, which was cowardice dressed up as calm, and we went down to the abutment anyway, because the map said the depot on the far side was a day's walk and the monsoon was a week out at best, and none of us had become the kind of people who turn around.

I rigged the anchors myself, and I'll say this for what was left of me: my hands remembered their work even when the rest of me was elsewhere. Two bolts into sound masonry on the east headwall, a tensioned line across the first four spans to a stanchion on span five, a secondary lifeline clipped through the deck brackets. Good work. Textbook. Odile watched me set the second anchor and gave me the small nod she gave when something met her standards, which was rarer than praise and worth more.

Pell went across first, arms shaking, swearing steadily in two languages, and made it to span five and anchored the far end with a competence that surprised everyone including him. Then Suri, moving carefully along the wet stringers with the map case strapped tight to her back, face set in that expression she wore when she refused to be afraid in front of us. She reached the far side. Four spans of open air between us and her, and the river roaring underneath, and she turned and shouted something that the water ate whole, and Odile laughed.

"Your turn," Odile said to me. "Take the instrument case across, then come back for the rope relay. I'll bring the document case and the plates last—they're the ones that can't swim."

I picked up the instrument case.

And I stepped onto the first span, and the bridge began to sing.

It was nothing. I want to be clear about that, because I have gone over it ten thousand times since, and it was nothing—a low harmonic, wind through the truss members, the same note any steel structure gives you when the air moves through it right. My body knew that. My body had surveyed forty bridges since Kestrel Gorge and walked every one of them without turning a hair.

But the note was almost exactly the note.

I got three steps onto the bare stringers of span four and my legs simply declined the assignment. No decision involved. One moment I was walking; the next I was crouched down with both hands locked around a rusted lateral brace, the instrument case swinging from my shoulder over open air, and my heart going like a trip-hammer, and the river underneath me not a river anymore but that other river, that night, the black water full of splinters and lamp-oil and voices, and the specific horrible slowness of watching a thing you certified fail exactly the way you'd privately known it might.

"Zhou." Odile's voice, from behind me, level. "Talk to me."

"I'm—" My mouth was dry as ballast. "Give me a minute."

She gave me a minute. She was good like that. Behind her I could see Pell and Suri on the far deck, small in the mist, and I could feel them watching, and the shame of being watched was somehow worse than the fear. Three years of being invisible, of being the man who checked the gauges and signed the forms and never stayed for drinks, and all of it built precisely so that no one would ever see this—see me folded over a brace like a man praying to a bridge, undone by a sound.

"Line's good," Odile said. "Anchors are good. Your work always is. Come on, engineer. Ten more steps."

I couldn't. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly what this story is for. I gripped that rusted brace and I looked down at the brown water taking apart a tree, and I could not make my hands let go.

What happened next happened fast.

Odile swore—shortly, professionally—and then she came past me along the stringers, unclipped from the lifeline for the four steps where the brackets were too far apart, carrying the document case and the plate box lashed together across her back, walking the bare iron like it was a corridor in her own house. She reached me, stepped around me without touching me, without a word of contempt or pity, and kept going.

And that was when the stringer let go.

Not the whole span—just a section of deck beam, rusted through at the connection where the flood spray had done forty years of work in one season. It went with a crack like a rifle shot, a piece of iron the size of a door peeling away under her left foot, and Odile dropped—not fell, dropped, controlled, trying to get her weight onto the adjacent stringer—but the load on her back swung her, and her boot hit wet iron, and then she was sliding, and then she was in the water.

I heard it happen more than saw it. The river took her the way it took the trees—one instant visible, thrashing at the surface with one hand hooked into the broken beam's trailing rebar, the next dragged under the churning boil off pier five, the document case tearing loose and vanishing downstream, the plate box still strapped to her, dragging her deeper.

Everything in me went white and silent.

Later, Suri told me what I actually did in those seconds, because I couldn't remember. I froze. That was the truth of it; that was the thing I had built three years of chainage markers specifically never to find out, and here it was, found: Isandro Zhou, standing on solid iron twelve feet from a drowning woman, doing nothing. Hands empty. Mind a blank gauge. The river humming its one note.

It was Suri who moved. She came down the far deck at a run, dropped flat at the break, got the lifeline free of its bracket, and threw the coil—underhand, perfect, a loop that opened in the air and dropped over Odile's head and shoulder on her second surfacing. Then she leaned back against the line with her whole slight body and screamed at me, one word, my name, and the sound went through the white silence like a bolt through a fishplate.

My hands woke up.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the iron under my boots, and the count—three steps, four, five—and then I was at the break hauling on the line beside Suri, our shoulders jammed together, both of us leaning back over the gap while the river tried to tear Odile out of the loop, and Pell was suddenly there too, all skin and panic and surprising strength, and between the three of us we walked her up the current, hand over hand, until her free hand found the sound stringer and she got a knee up over the break and came out of the water like something born instead of something saved, coughing, furious, alive.

She lay on the deck breathing for a while. Then she sat up, wrung out her sleeve, and said, "Document case is gone."

Nobody said anything.

"Well," Odile said, "it floated. Things float. Wexler, when we get to the depot, you're writing to whoever holds the downstream telegraph office, and we're offering a reward for a waterproof case with brass corners. Don't look at me like that. I've pulled worse out of rivers than hope."

* * *

We crossed the rest of the way in silence, single file, and made camp that evening in the shell of a signal box on the far side, its glass long gone, its levers fused with rust. Odile stripped off her soaked shirt and wrapped herself in the blanket Suri handed her without being asked, and the two of them did that thing women do of communicating entire paragraphs through the exchange of dry cloth.

I sat outside under the eave, out of the lamplight, where I had put myself, and listened to the river go on being enormous in the dark.

Footsteps. Suri. She didn't sit down so much as arrive at sitting, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, far enough that it was clearly her choice. For a long time she said nothing, and I was grateful, because I had nothing, and I think she knew that.

"When I was fourteen," she said finally, "my father missed my mother's death anniversary. Third year running. I told him he loved maps more than people, and I packed a bag, and I stood at the door, and I dared him to stop me." The lantern inside the signal box threw her shadow long across the gravel. "He didn't stop me. He stood up from his table like his joints were full of sand, and he looked at me, and I waited for him to say something, anything—and he just looked lost. Like a man reading a chart in a language he'd forgotten. So I left. Six years. And I have spent every day of this walk telling myself he chose the map, and hating him for it, and it has been very convenient, that hatred. Very sturdy. Carries a lot of gear."

"Suri—"

"He froze too," she said. "At the door. That's what I've only just understood. It wasn't that he didn't care. It was that everything he cared about had gotten so big it locked his knees." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes in the dark were steady and unsparing and kind, which is a combination I didn't know existed until I met her. "You froze today. Odile knows. Pell knows. I know. And tomorrow morning you'll get up and rig the next crossing, because that's what you do, and none of us will say a word about it unless you do first. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

"Why?" I said. It came out rougher than I meant. "Why would you cover for me? You barely know me. I'm the man who stood on safe iron and watched."

"You're the man whose anchors held," she said. "Twice today. Once for the rope, and once—" she nodded back toward the signal box, toward Odile, "—for her. That loop you threw her, after mine. I threw wide. Yours caught. I saw your hands, Isandro. Whatever happened to you, it didn't get your hands." She stood, brushing grit from her trousers, and paused. "My father carried that map forty years and never once told anyone why. I used to think that made him a coward. Now I think he was just waiting until he could carry it without dropping it. Take whatever time you need. The trail's long."

She went inside. Through the broken window I watched her check Odile's blanket, add wood to the stove, say something to Pell that made him snort despite himself—the small domestic machinery of people becoming necessary to each other.

I stayed outside a while longer, because shame is a habit and habits keep their own hours. But eventually I got out the notebook, because the notebook was where I put things I couldn't say, and I wrote down the day's figures the way I always did: stage height, rate of rise, span condition, load ratings. And then, beneath the figures, in the margin where Mateo Reyes had written his little mysteries in pencil, I wrote a line of my own, the first non-numerical entry in three years:

*Hands held. Everything else is repairable.*

And it was then, folding the notebook away, that I noticed the light.

Downriver, far off through the rain-haze where the gorge bent toward the old depot, a lantern was moving along the track bed. Steady. Deliberate. Too regular for a wanderer, too confident for a ghost. It paused opposite our position—paused, I would have sworn, deliberately—and then it went out.

The river roared on, keeping its own counsel, and I sat in the dark and understood that we were no longer alone on this line.
