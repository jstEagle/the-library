## 7. The Hermit of Kilometer 44

The rain found us an hour before the hermit did.

It came first as a smell—that green, mineral breath the mountains exhale before a storm—and then as a fine mist beading on the lantern glass, and then as a proper rain, hissing through the pines and turning the old ballast to soup. We were still a kilometer from shelter when Odile called the halt, her voice pitched low over the drumming.

"Kilometer forty-four," she said, tapping the map where Mateo had penciled a small, careful cross beside a squiggle that might have been a hut. "There's a mark here. Track-walker's station, maybe."

"Track-walkers are gone," Pell said. He had his collar up and his spectacles fogged, and he looked like a drowned scholar, which was more or less what he was. "The company pulled them all when they closed the line. There's nobody up here but ghosts and us."

"Then we'll ask the ghosts for tea." Odile shouldered her pack and started walking.

I hung back a moment, letting the others pass me in the rain, and looked at my father's map again. The little cross at kilometer forty-four wasn't drawn the way he drew everything else—not the confident surveyor's hand, not the quick shorthand of a man who thought in contours and grades. It was small. Almost shy. As if he'd hesitated before putting it down at all, or as if it marked something he hadn't wanted to find but couldn't leave unrecorded.

I had spent twenty-six years learning to read my father's silences from a distance of three hundred miles, through letters that arrived late and said little. I could read this one instantly: *I knew someone here. I don't know if they're still alive.*

* * *

The station materialized out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen—a squat stone cottage crouched against the cut slope, its roof sagging under a beard of moss, its single window glowing with a light so steady and domestic that I actually stopped walking. After four days of tunnels and rust and the company's long abandonment, a lit window in the wilderness felt like a trick. Like bait.

Odile knocked anyway. She was not a woman who believed in tricks; she believed in doors.

We heard movement inside—slow, deliberate, the shuffle of someone who had long ago stopped hurrying for anyone. Then a bolt drawn back, and the door opened onto lamplight and the smell of woodsmoke and drying herbs, and there stood a man who must have been seventy if he was a day, small and wiry as a rail spike, with a white mustache stained amber by decades of tobacco and eyes that went from Odile to Pell to Isandro to me with the unhurried precision of a man reading a signal board.

"Well," he said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly. "Four of you. And one of you is carrying Mateo Reyes's map."

My hand went to my satchel before I could stop it.

The old man smiled, and the smile cracked his face into a hundred weathered lines. "Don't look so startled, girl. He drew that map sitting right where you're standing. Come in out of the wet before you drown on my doorstep. I'm Aurelio Sancha. I walked this line for thirty-one years, and I never left when they told me to, and I have soup on the stove, which is more than any ghost can say."

* * *

Inside, the cottage was a museum of a life lived entirely in defiance of an eviction notice. Tools hung on pegs in order of use. A wall of shelves held jars labeled in a careful hand—dried mushrooms, pickled something, pine resin, salt. A track-walker's lamp, brass-bright from constant polishing, sat on the mantel beside a photograph gone soft with age: a younger Aurelio standing on trestle number nine, arm around another man whose face had faded to a smudge.

And everywhere, everywhere, maps. Not just on the walls—in stacks, in drawers left open, folded into books, pinned to the ceiling beams. Hand-drawn sections of the closed line, annotated in three different inks, some of them so old the paper had gone the color of weak tea.

Pell made a sound I'd only ever heard from him once before, at the depot—a small, involuntary noise, like a man finding scripture in a burning building. He drifted toward the shelves with his hands half-raised, not quite touching anything, and Aurelio watched him go with an expression of vast, dry amusement.

"Cartographer," Aurelio said. Not a question.

"Disgraced cartographer," Pell admitted, without turning around.

"Mm. The disgraceful ones draw best. The honest ones are too busy being honest to notice what the mountains actually look like." Aurelio turned to me, and the amusement drained out of his face, leaving something older and sadder underneath. "You're his daughter. You have his chin. He talked about you the way other men talk about saints."

The words landed harder than I expected them to. I had come on this expedition, if I was honest with myself, to demand things of a dead man—to shake answers out of his map the way you'd shake coins out of a coat pocket. I had not prepared for strangers handing me pieces of him I didn't know existed.

"He talked about you too," I managed. "In his letters. 'A friend at forty-four.' He never gave a name."

"He wouldn't. Names get people killed up here." Aurelio said it lightly, ladling soup into bowls, but the lightness had a blade in it, and Isandro—who had been silent since the door opened, watching everything with those gray engineer's eyes—caught it. I saw him catch it. His bowl stayed untouched in front of him.

* * *

Aurelio fed us the way mountain people feed travelers: relentlessly, without negotiation. Soup with wild garlic, dark bread, tea brewed from something piney that tasted like the forest had been boiled down to its essence. Odile ate like a woman doing a job. Pell ate like a man who had forgotten food existed until tonight. Isandro ate mechanically, his attention elsewhere, and I understood why when I followed his gaze to the window—the rain had slackened, and beyond the glass the black shoulder of the mountain held a shape I recognized now, having slept beneath it two nights running.

The great bore. The tunnel they'd dynamited shut. From here, in the lamplight, it looked almost gentle—a dark seam in the rock, high above the treeline, like a closed eye.

"That's where he was going," Aurelio said quietly, following our stares. "Your father. Every year, same week, he'd come down off the ridge and sit at my table and eat my terrible cooking and tell me nothing at all about what he was doing up there. Forty years of visits. Forty years of nothing." He set down his spoon. "Until last spring. Last spring he asked me for paper and ink, and he sat where you're sitting, girl, and he drew for six hours straight. And when he finished, he rolled that map up and said, 'If anything happens to me, it has to go on. It has to reach the light.'"

"What did you tell him?" I asked.

"I told him nothing happens to old men in the mountains except old age." Aurelio's laugh was short and had no joy in it. "I was wrong. Tell me how he died."

"Flooded crossing," Isandro said. "Exposure after. He was hurt, and cold, and alone, and he held on long enough to make a stranger promise to finish the job." His voice was flat, but I had learned to hear what lived underneath Isandro Zhou's flatness—the way you learn to hear water moving under ice. "He made me swear it before I even saw the map. That was the bargain. The promise first. Always the promise first."

"Yes," Aurelio said softly. "That was Mateo. The promise first. He learned that lesson somewhere expensive." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the shelf above the fire, and took down a tin box that had clearly not been opened in years. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a second map—or rather, a fragment of one, a section of the upper line drawn in a different hand than Mateo's, older, official, stamped with the company seal.

He laid it flat on the table between the lamps, and Pell leaned over it so fast his spectacles nearly touched the paper.

"This is the original survey," Pell breathed. "Pre-closure. This is—" His finger moved along a penciled annotation, and stopped. "This is dated eleven days before the closure order. Someone amended the alignment here. Moved the portal. Why would they move the—"

"Because the men who dug the first portal are still behind the second one," Aurelio said.

The fire popped. Rain ticked against the window. Nobody moved.

"I walked supply trains through that bore while they built it," Aurelio went on, and his voice had dropped into the register of a man reciting something he had repeated to himself ten thousand times in the dark, a rosary of grief. "Three hundred men on that heading. Good men. Men whose boots I resoled, whose letters I carried down the mountain to their wives. Then one week in the autumn, the trains stopped going up. Company men came instead, in clean coats, and they sealed both portals, and they put the closure in the papers as landslide damage, and every man on that crew was struck from the rolls as transferred. Transferred." He spat the word like a bad tooth. "Thirty-one years I walked this line, and I will tell you what no transfer record ever showed me: not one of those three hundred men ever came down the mountain again."

I looked at Isandro. He had gone very still—the stillness of a man doing structural math in his head, load-bearing calculations of a different kind. When he spoke, his voice was careful.

"You've told people this. Over the years."

"I've told the wind. I've told the rain." Aurelio sat back down heavily. "And twice, I've told the wrong people."

* * *

He got up then and fetched a bottle and four tin cups, and poured something colorless that smelled like it could strip paint, and only Odile drank hers without flinching.

"The first ones came eight years ago," Aurelio said. "Two men, city clothes ruined properly for the trip, which was its own kind of lie—you don't dress like that unless you want to seem harmless. They said they were historians. They paid me well for stories about the closure. I gave them stories. Cheap ones. Landslides, weather, the usual. They thanked me, they left, and a month later a third man came asking whether I'd mentioned the tunnel crews to anyone. He wasn't dressed like a historian. He left money too." Aurelio turned his cup slowly on the table. "Men who pay well and leave nothing. That's the pattern, girl. Remember it. They pay for your silence the way you'd pay for timber—they take delivery, and what's left standing afterward is less than what was there before."

"And the second time?" Odile asked.

"Last summer. Four men, armed, better equipped than any survey crew I've ever seen, asking specifically after Mateo Reyes. Where he camped. What he carried. Whether he kept papers." Aurelio's eyes came to rest on my satchel, and this time I didn't flinch away from it. "They offered me enough money to buy this whole valley. I told them the old man was dead to me. Which was true, in the way that mattered—he'd stopped coming down, stopped trusting anyone, stopped sleeping under roofs. Whatever he found up there, it taught him that." He drained his cup. "They went up the ridge the next morning. They came down three days later, empty-handed and angry, and one of them had a bandaged arm, and none of them would say what happened up there. But they'll come again. Men like that always come again. And now they'll know he's dead, because death gets written down, and written things travel faster than secrets."

"So we're carrying the thing they've already killed for once," I said. My voice sounded steadier than I felt. "And they know the terrain, and they're armed, and we're four strays with a borrowed tent and a cartographer who owes everyone an apology."

"Five strays," Aurelio said.

We all looked at him.

"I'm coming as far as the switchback yard," he said, in the tone of a man announcing weather. "I know every culvert and every collapse on the middle section, and you'll need water readings before the monsoon turns the gorge into a blender. Besides—" and here the dry amusement came back, thin but real, "—I've waited forty years for someone to carry that truth down the mountain. Did you think I'd let four children do it without an old man walking point?"

Isandro studied him for a long moment. "Why now? You've kept quiet for forty years. What changed?"

Aurelio looked at the faded photograph on the mantel—at the smudge where his friend's face used to be—and then at me.

"She did," he said simply. "Mateo's daughter, walking his route. That's the answer arriving, forty years late. Things change when the waiting ends." He stood, and began pulling gear from hooks with brisk, practiced hands. "Now sleep. All of you. Because tomorrow morning, before first light, I'm going to show you something on the ridge trail that those hired men missed, and you're going to wish to God they'd found it first."

"What is it?" Pell asked.

Aurelio paused at the doorway, lamplight catching the amber stain of his mustache, and for a moment he looked every one of his seventy years and every one of his thirty-one miles of track.

"Fresh graves," he said. "Three of them. Dug within the last month."
