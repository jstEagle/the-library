## 8. Company Men

The rain had been threatening for two days without committing, hanging over the ridgelines like a debt unpaid. Isandro felt it in the way the air went thick and metallic, in the way the mules grew restless and kept turning their ears back toward the valley they'd climbed out of. Monsoon country. He'd worked through three of them on survey crews, and his body had learned to read the sky the way other men read timetables.

They came down out of the high scrub mid-morning, following an old service road that the map said would carry them to the river fork by nightfall. The road was a memory of a road—gravel gone to grass, culverts rusted into lace—but it ran true, and true was what they needed. Odile walked point with her surveyor's staff swinging like a metronome. Pell trailed behind the mules, muttering to himself and correcting the map's margins in pencil. Suri ranged along the cutbanks, reading her father's annotations against the actual stone, and every time the two agreed she made a small sound in her throat that Isandro had learned meant *he really was here, he knew this place like his own hand.*

It should have been a good morning. It was a good morning, right up until the moment Odile stopped walking.

She didn't say anything at first. She just planted the staff, stood very still, and tilted her head the way she did when a rail sang wrong under a wheel load. Isandro caught up beside her and listened.

Boots. More than one pair. Coming up the old road behind them at a pace too even to be accidental, too disciplined to be lost.

"Company men," Odile said quietly. "That's a cadence. That's men who've marched together."

Isandro turned. The road curved around a shoulder of granite a half mile back, and out from behind that shoulder came five figures in matching khaki, moving in loose formation, unhurried, carrying themselves with the particular ease of people who had never once worried about being where they were. No packs worth mentioning. Clean boots. One of them had a rifle slung across his back with the casualness of a man who carried it the way other men carried umbrellas.

"Everyone off the road," Isandro said. "Into the trees. Now."

"They've seen us," Suri said. "They were coming straight at us."

"Then we choose the ground." He heard his own voice come out flat and steady and was briefly grateful for the three years of numbness that made steadiness cheap. "Rock wall on our left, drop on our right. We don't want to be caught in the open with mules."

Odile was already moving, hauling the lead mule by its halter toward a shelf of boulders where the cutbank rose steep and broken. Pell needed no instruction; he'd been hiding from things his whole life and had gotten good at it. Suri hesitated one heartbeat, hand pressed flat against the rock face as if the mountain itself might confirm something, then followed.

The five men reached their abandoned position on the road and stopped. And then the man at the front did something Isandro had not expected: he smiled, raised one hand in a courteous wave, and called out in a voice pitched to carry without shouting.

"Good morning! I wonder if I might impose on you for a few minutes of conversation."

* * *

He introduced himself as Mr. Vasseur, and he pronounced it the way a man pronounces a name he has decided is important. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in field clothes so well-cut they looked less like clothing than a statement about what field clothes ought to be. His boots were polished. His hands were clean. His eyes moved over their little camp—the mules, the gear, the four of them arranged uneasily among the boulders—with the pleasant thoroughness of a man taking inventory.

"I apologize for the theatrical approach," Vasseur said, stopping a respectful twenty paces off. "But roads are public things, and one learns not to surprise armed men or startled mules. May I?" He gestured at the space between them as though asking permission to exist.

"You're imposing already," Odile said. She'd positioned herself slightly forward of Isandro, arms crossed, and Isandro let her. Her bulk and her glare were worth more than any weapon they carried, which was none.

"Then I'll be brief." Vasseur folded his hands. Behind him, his four men spread out with practiced idleness—one drifting toward the drop-off side, one leaning on the cutbank, none of them far from their principal but all of them, Isandro noted, quietly closing angles. "I represent a consortium with interests in this region. Historical interests. Archival interests. We understand you may have come into possession of certain documents relating to the closed lines—survey materials, route notes, that sort of thing."

"We understand a lot of things," Odile said.

"Of course you do." Vasseur's smile didn't flicker. "I'm told a prospector named Mateo Reyes spent the last years of his life compiling such materials. A sad story. A man alone in the hills, obsessed with ground everyone else had written off. Such men often leave behind papers of great sentimental value and no practical value whatsoever. The consortium would be happy to relieve your party of the burden. Generously. There would be no questions, no receipts, no further inconvenience to anyone."

The silence after that had weight. Isandro watched Suri. She had gone very still, the stillness of a drawn wire, and her hand had drifted to the oilskin roll inside her jacket where the map lived. Vasseur saw it too. Isandro saw him see it—a small brightening around the eyes, a predator's arithmetic done in half a second—and something cold settled into Isandro's stomach that had nothing to do with the weather.

"How do you know about Reyes," Isandro said. Not a question. A test.

Vasseur turned to him for the first time, and gave him the full courtesy of his attention, and Isandro understood immediately that this man had been waiting for exactly this exchange. "We make it our business to know about anyone asking after the sealed sections," Vasseur said pleasantly. "There aren't many. In fact, until this season there hadn't been any in years. Then suddenly: a dying prospector, a railroad engineer on sabbatical, a foreman between contracts, a cartographer with a complicated reputation, and the daughter nobody knew the old man had. You must forgive us our curiosity. It's such an unusual collection of people to find walking the same forgotten road."

Pell made a small strangled noise. Isandro didn't look at him. He kept his eyes on Vasseur and did the arithmetic himself, and the sum was ugly: this man knew their names, their histories, their wounds. Which meant someone had been paid to compile those histories. Which meant someone had been watching before they ever set foot on this road.

"The answer's no," Isandro said.

"Mr.—Zhou, isn't it? Isandro Zhou." Vasseur said the name gently, almost kindly, and Isandro felt it land like a hook set behind the eye. "I admire loyalty. Truly. But consider the practicalities. You're four days' walk from anywhere, monsoon season is eleven days out by my meteorologists' estimate—you'll want to check my figures against yours—and you're carrying paper through country the railroad closed for reasons that are, I assure you, better left closed. Whatever you imagine those documents are worth, I'm offering more than any market will. And I'm offering it now, while the transaction can be friendly."

"Friendlier than what?" Suri asked.

Vasseur looked at her, and for just a moment the polish thinned and something older and harder showed underneath, the way bedrock shows through thin soil. "Friendlier than the alternative," he said. "Which I sincerely hope we never need to discuss. The offer stands for the length of this conversation. After that—" he spread his clean hands, regretful, magnanimous, "—well. Mountains are dangerous. Weather is unpredictable. Accidents happen to parties who insist on traveling routes that sensible people avoid."

"Get off our road," Odile said.

"It's hardly your road."

"It's ours today." She stepped forward, and there was enough of her, and enough iron in her voice, that even the man leaning on the cutbank straightened. "You've made your offer. He's refused it. Anything else you've got to say, say it walking backward."

For a long moment Vasseur studied Odile Brant the way he'd studied the mules—as equipment, as variable, as a problem with a solution. Then he inclined his head, gracious to the last, and took one step back, and another, and his men flowed in around him without appearing to move at all.

"Gentlemen. Ladies." He touched two fingers to his brow in something adjacent to a salute. "I do hope the weather treats you kindly. Do give my regards to the mountain."

They watched the five figures diminish down the old road, around the granite shoulder, gone. The birds started up again in the scrub a full minute later, and only then did Isandro realize how completely the morning had gone silent around them.

* * *

They didn't stop for lunch. They didn't discuss it; Odile simply set the pace and the pace was punishing, and everyone understood.

It was Suri who finally broke, two miles on, when the road bent away from the drop-off and the trees closed overhead. "He knew about me." Her voice came out low and furious. "He knew my name. Nobody knows my name. I didn't know my father existed until eight months ago, and this man in polished boots knows my whole life story."

"He knows all of ours," Pell said miserably. "That's the part I keep circling. That isn't surveillance of a rumor. That's a dossier. Someone built files on each of us before we ever met each other." He wiped his glasses on his shirt with hands that weren't quite steady. "Do you know what that costs? That kind of research? That's not a company protecting a landslide zone. Landslide zones don't require background checks on a disgraced mapmaker."

"So what does it require?" Odile asked.

Nobody answered. The trail switchbacked down through wet fern, and the river appeared below them at last, brown-green and swollen with the first melt-fed pulse of the coming season, running fast and loud through a gorge the color of iron.

It was Isandro who finally said it, because someone had to, and because he had spent three years perfecting the art of saying unpleasant things in a level voice. "It requires something buried worth killing to keep buried. Vasseur isn't buying a map. He's buying the only copy of whatever the map proves. Which means the map isn't a treasure. It's a witness." He looked at Suri. "Your father wasn't prospecting for ore. He was collecting evidence for forty years, and he knew exactly what it would cost to carry it out, because he watched them close the line over it."

Suri's jaw tightened. "Then why give it to strangers?"

"Because strangers were the only ones left who could walk."

They made the river fork by late afternoon, and Isandro chose the campsite like a man choosing a defensive position, because that was what he was doing now—back to rock, sightlines down both watercourses, mules picketed within reach. While Odile rigged the tarp and Pell coaxed a smokeless fire from damp wood, Isandro climbed the rise above camp with his binoculars and swept the backtrail.

He found them in nine minutes.

Not on the road—they wouldn't be so crude again. On the opposite ridge, a quarter mile east and holding parallel, a single figure in khaki, stationary against a dead pine, glassing their camp. When Isandro found him, the man was looking directly at Isandro's position, and after a moment he lifted one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *We see you seeing us.* Then he turned and walked north along the ridgeline, unhurried, keeping pace.

Isandro lowered the binoculars and stood in the wind coming up the gorge, and felt the shape of the expedition change beneath him. For six days they had been travelers in a hostile wilderness, and the wilderness had been the enemy—weather, water, rot, distance. All of that was still true. But now the wilderness had acquired a second enemy wearing clean boots, one that knew their names and their failures and their exact position on the earth, and would know them tomorrow and the day after, patient as pressure, all the way to whatever it was at the end of Mateo Reyes's inked lines.

He thought about the bridge. Three years of not thinking about it, and here it came anyway: the groan of overstressed steel, the specific silence after, the arithmetic of forty-one names. He had signed the survey that failed. He had measured that crossing and called it sound. If Vasseur's people had dug deep enough to find Suri's parentage and Pell's disgrace, they had certainly found that. Isandro understood, standing on the rise with the light going copper and the rain finally beginning to fall in fat, deliberate drops, that the polished man had not offered money at all. He had offered a price list, and everything Isandro had ever done wrong was printed on it.

He climbed down into camp. Suri looked up from the map, which lay open on her knees, her father's marginal handwriting crawling up its edges like ivy. Rain ticked against the tarp. Downriver, somewhere in the darkening gorge, water thundered over rocks that would be underwater in a week.

"They're on the ridge," Isandro said. "One watcher, pacing us. There'll be more by morning."

Odile fed a stick to the fire and didn't look surprised. "How many days to the tunnel mouth?"

"Three. Four if the river keeps rising."

"And the monsoon?"

"Six days. Maybe five." He crouched by the fire, palms out, watching the flames bend sideways in the wind coming down from the heights. "So we travel in the dark if we have to, cross water when they don't expect it, and get underground before they can corner us in the open. Because out here we're four people and two mules, and they're a payroll." He glanced up, and found Suri watching him with her father's stubbornness and none of his distance. "But in the tunnel, whatever's down there belongs to us. To the truth. He can't buy what we've already seen."

"That's a pretty speech," Odile said. "Now tell me the part where we outrun professional trackers through monsoon flooding with a cartographer who gets vertigo above the treeline."

"I'm working on that part," Isandro said.

Above the camp, on the black ridge, a match flared briefly—a watcher lighting a cigarette, unbothered, unhurried, certain. The rain thickened. Somewhere north, under a hundred feet of dynamited rock, the thing Mateo Reyes had carried for forty years waited with the patience of the dead.

Three days, Isandro thought. Stay ahead of them for three days.

Behind him, unnoticed by anyone, Pell sat apart with his notebook open, writing quickly in the firelight, and when Suri asked what he was doing, he closed the book too fast and said, "Nothing. Distances. Just distances."
