## 9. Suri's Inheritance

The fire was small, because small fires were all the mountain allowed them now. They had climbed past the last scrub pine by midafternoon, into a country of grey stone and wind-bent grass, and everything up here seemed rationed—oxygen, warmth, light. Suri fed the flames one stick at a time, as if each were a coin she might need back later.

They had made camp in the lee of an old snow-shed, its timber roof sagging under decades of avalanche winters, its walls still bearing the soot-shadow of lanterns that hadn't burned in forty years. The railroad had built these sheds to protect the line from the mountain's temper. Now the mountain owned them again, and it lent shelter the way a landlord lends a room to squatters: grudgingly, and with interest.

Odile had taken first watch on the shelf of rock above camp. Pell was asleep almost before his bedroll settled, curled around his satchel of charts like a man sleeping beside a woman he'd wronged. And Isandro sat across the fire from her, working at something with his hands—a length of wire, a broken buckle from a pack strap, some small mechanical grief he could fix instead of speaking.

Suri had the map open on her knees.

She had been carrying it for six days without really looking at it. That was the truth she kept circling and avoiding: she had carried her father's obsession through the sealed gate, along the drowned viaduct, up the switchbacks, and she had treated it the way you treat a stranger's luggage—useful, heavy, not yours. She read the route lines and the elevation marks because the expedition needed them read. She did not read the margins.

Tonight, with the fire low and the others quiet, she finally did.

* * *

Her father had written in pencil, in a hand that started confident and grew shakier as the pages went on. The map itself was old—older than she'd guessed, the paper soft as cloth, the ink gone brown—and Mateo Reyes had annotated it across what must have been four decades. Dates in the corners. Weather notes. Little crosses where he'd cached supplies. And everywhere, crowding the edges, the margins: dense, slanting, obsessive script that Suri had assumed was more survey data, more numbers, more of the language her father had always preferred to hers.

She turned the map toward the firelight and began to read.

*Altitude 2,900. Wind from the north. The marmots are fat this year.*

*Ate the last of the dried peaches. Should have brought two bags. You always said I packed like a man expecting to die happy.*

Suri went still.

*You always said.* Not *I*. Not the impersonal *one*. A *you*, addressed to someone, threaded through notes about altitude and weather like a vein of gold through worthless quartz.

She turned back to the earliest pages, near the map's edge where the paper had been handled most, and there it was—the beginning of the habit, dated forty years ago, in a younger man's strong hand:

*Bought this map off a dying freight clerk for a bottle of aguardiente. He told me the line was closed for landslides. He crossed himself when he said it. First lesson of the mountains: when a man crosses himself over geology, believe him.*

And beneath it, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Suri born today. Seven pounds. Her mother says she has my stubborn chin. God help us both.*

Suri's breath left her slowly, like air out of a punctured tank.

She knew the date. Of course she knew the date. It was her birthday. She had spent thirty-one birthdays learning to hate that her father had never once been home for any of them, and here it was, written in pencil on the edge of a mountain survey: *Suri born today.* He had been on this mountain when she was born. He had learned of it up here, in the cold, and the first thing he'd done with the news was write it down—not in a letter, not in a ledger, but here, on the thing he trusted most in the world.

He had told her the map mattered more than she did. He had said it without saying it, a hundred times, with every departure and every missed dinner and every birthday celebrated by telegram. She had built her whole grievance on that foundation, stone by stone, year by year, the way he'd taught her—without her knowing—to build things.

And the whole time, the foundation had been hollow.

* * *

"Your fire's getting away from you."

Isandro's voice came quietly across the coals. Suri blinked and realized she'd been feeding sticks to the flames without watching them; they'd climbed high, throwing her shadow huge against the snow-shed wall.

"It's fine," she said.

He set down the buckle he'd been mending and looked at her. He had a way of looking at people that wasn't quite looking at them—more like taking a bearing, the way a surveyor does, fixing your position against known points. She'd found it unnerving for the first few days. Tonight it felt less like being measured and more like being seen.

"You've been reading," he said.

"How could you tell?"

"You stopped turning pages an hour ago."

Suri looked down at the map. "It's nothing. Route notes."

"I read the margins," Isandro said. "The first night. While you were asleep." He paused. "I didn't tell you. It felt like reading someone's letters."

"It is someone's letters."

"Yes." He stirred the fire with a stick, and sparks rose and died. "My father wrote letters. Real ones. Every week, eleven years, to me at boarding school. I never answered a single one. When he died, my mother gave me the box of his drafts—the ones he never sent, because he couldn't afford the postage some months. Eleven years of letters to a boy who never wrote back." His voice stayed level, engineer-flat, but his hands had stopped moving. "So I know what it is to find out too late that someone was talking to you the whole time. I'm not going to pretend I know what yours says. But I know the weight of it."

Suri hadn't asked for this. She wanted to be angry about that—that he would offer his own wound as if it were currency—but the anger wouldn't catch. Up here, in the rationed light, everything false burned away and only the dry fuel of true things remained.

"He was on this mountain when I was born," she said. "Did you know that? The day I came into the world, he was up here writing about marmots."

"And about you."

"And about me." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Thirty-one years. Thirty-one years I thought I was competing with a piece of paper. Do you know what that's like? Every school recital, every exam, every time I got sick—he'd send money and a note in his terrible handwriting, and the note was always about the next survey, the next ridge, the next thing the mountains had shown him. As if I were a subscription he kept paying on. As if I were—" Her voice caught. "As if I were the obligation and the map was the love."

Isandro said nothing. He was good at that, she was learning. He let silence do work that words would only have damaged.

She pulled the map onto her knees again and turned to a page near the middle, one she'd read three times now without understanding until tonight.

*Found the marker at Kilometer 44 half-buried. Set it upright. Thought of teaching Suri to read contour lines. She'd take to it faster than any apprentice I've had. She has the eye. She has the eye and I never told her so, because telling her would mean admitting what I took from her.*

"There," Suri said, her finger trembling on the page. "Read that. Read what he wrote. *She has the eye.* He thought I had the eye. He watched me draw maps in the dirt when I was eight years old and he thought—"

She stopped, because her throat had closed.

"He thought I was like him," she finished, barely above the fire's crackle. "And instead of bringing me with him, instead of teaching me, he left. Because leaving was easier than admitting what leaving cost. Because the mountains don't argue with you. Mountains don't ask where you were on their birthday."

* * *

The wind shifted, and somewhere above them Odile's silhouette shifted with it, patient as the stone she sat on. Below, the valley they'd climbed out of filled with blue dusk. Suri turned another page, then another, and the margins unspooled their secret history: not a survey at all, but a correspondence conducted across forty years with a daughter who had never received a word of it.

*She graduated second in her class. Second. She'll be furious. She'll be right to be furious.*

*Saw a girl in the market with her build, her walk. Followed her half a block before I understood. Felt like a fool. Felt like a rich man.*

*The company men came asking questions again. Told them I'd lost the map years ago. Lied to their faces and slept well for the first time in a decade. If anything happens to me, the map goes to Suri. Not because she'll want it. Because she deserves to choose.*

*If anything happens to me, the map goes to Suri.*

Suri read the line five times. Around her the fire settled, the wind moved through the shed timbers with a sound like slow breathing, and thirty-one years of carefully constructed resentment sat in her chest like a load-bearing wall that had just been shown to be made of paper.

He hadn't chosen the map over her. He had chosen the map *for* her—in the only language he had ever been fluent in, the language of terrain and distance and witness, the language of a man who believed that the truest inheritance was not money or land but knowledge of where the bodies were buried. Literally. Where the bodies were buried.

That was what the map was. She understood it now, fully, the way you understand a bridge only after you've walked it. It wasn't treasure. It wasn't even evidence, not exactly. It was a confession forty years long, written in the only ink that doesn't fade: place names, elevations, dates. Her father had spent his whole life carrying testimony he couldn't deliver, and he had addressed it to her, and she had spent her whole life hating him for the postage.

"I hated him," she said aloud. It was important to say it to the fire, to the mountain, to the dark. "I want that on the record. I hated him for years. Properly, thoroughly, with craft."

"I know," Isandro said.

"And now I—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Now I find out he loved me the whole time, in pencil, in margins, and he never sent it, and I can't even be angry at him anymore because he's dead, and being angry at dead men is like shouting down a well. You just hear your own voice come back."

"No," Isandro said. "You hear the well."

She lowered her hand and looked at him. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadows, a face built for landscapes rather than portraits, and there was something in it she recognized now—something she'd been seeing since the tunnel mouth, since the moment he'd knelt beside her dying father and made a promise he didn't want. It was the look of a man who had also spent years shouting down wells.

"What did you hear?" she asked. "In your well. Your father's letters."

Isandro was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"That I was allowed to come home," he said finally. "Every letter ended the same way. *The door is open whenever you want it.* Eleven years. And I decided answering meant admitting I needed the door, so I stayed out in the weather and called it independence." He picked up the buckle again, turned it over. "Then the bridge happened, and I learned there are worse things than needing a door. There's having no door at all. So now I—" He stopped. Started again, more honestly. "I fix buckles. I mend straps. Small repairs. Things I can finish."

Suri looked at his hands, quick and competent in the firelight, and understood that she was watching a man perform the same ritual she had performed her whole life: keeping busy so the grief couldn't get a word in edgewise.

"My father fixed things too," she said. "That's what I could never forgive. He'd come home and repair everything in the house—hinges, chairs, my mother's sewing machine—anything broken except the actual break. He'd tighten every screw in the building and leave the two of us standing in the middle of it, loose."

"And now?"

"Now I think maybe the margins were his way of tightening screws he couldn't reach." She smoothed the map flat across her knees. "Maybe he knew he was a coward about the big things. Maybe the map was him trying to be brave in installments."

* * *

She read until the fire burned down to a red eye and the cold drove her closer to it. Page by page, the margins gave up their hoard. Notes on her mother's cooking. Notes on her graduation, her first job, the argument they'd had when she was twenty-four—the one where she'd told him she never wanted to see him again unless it was at her mother's funeral, and he'd written, that same night, in a hand gone suddenly careful:

*She said she never wants to see me. Fair. I said worse to her mother once, and meant it less. But I keep the map current anyway. Routes change. Rivers move. If she ever comes looking—for me, for the truth, for anything—I want the trail to be walkable. I want her path to be easier than mine was.*

*I want her path to be easier than mine was.*

Suri folded the map closed with exaggerated care, the way you close a book over a flower you mean to press, and held it against her chest, and for the first time since the tunnel mouth she wept—not the furious tears of the funeral, which had been mostly rage wearing mourning clothes, but the slow, scalding tears of a woman discovering she had been loved by an idiot. By a shy, stubborn, cowardly, faithful idiot who had written her thirty-one years of letters and mailed none of them, and who had died on a mountain handing the whole unsent archive to a stranger because he trusted the stranger's promise more than he trusted his own welcome.

Isandro said nothing. He simply moved around the fire and sat beside her, not touching, just present, the way you stand near a structure while it settles—close enough to catch it, far enough not to interfere. After a while, without either of them deciding it, her shoulder came to rest against his arm, and his arm did not move away, and the fire ticked and settled, and the mountain breathed around them.

Above, Odile watched the dark and let them have their silence. Below, Pell slept his guilty sleep. And Suri Reyes, orphaned twice over—once by distance and once by death—sat with her inheritance open on her knees and understood, at last, what it was.

Not a map. A letter. Forty years late, postage due, addressed in pencil.

* * *

She woke before dawn with the map still in her arms and frost on her eyelashes, and found Isandro already up, boiling water, and found herself—strangely, unexpectedly—hungry. They ate in the grey light, passing a bag of oats between them, and when Odile came down from watch, stamping feeling back into her feet, Suri stood and spread the map on a flat stone.

"We should go over the route," she said. "All of it. Together. There are things in the margins we've been walking past." She tapped a cluster of annotations near the upper reaches of the line, where the map's oldest ink and newest pencil overlapped in a smudge of meaning. "He marked something here. Dated it, re-dated it, came back to it four times over forty years. Whatever he kept returning to—" She looked up, and her voice, when it came, was steady and new. "Whatever he kept coming back to is where he wants us to look."

Pell was awake now, charts in his lap, eyes sharp behind their smudged lenses. Odile crouched at the stone's edge, studying the margin notes upside down with a foreman's instinct for other people's handwriting.

Isandro met Suri's eyes across the map—her father's map, her letter, her inheritance—and nodded once, the nod of one surveyor acknowledging another's bearing.

But it was Odile, squinting at the oldest annotation, who went still first. Her finger stopped above the page.

"This date," she said slowly. "This isn't a survey mark. This is a crew roster number." She looked up, and the firelight was gone from the morning, replaced by something colder. "Suri—your father wasn't just mapping the line. He was counting men."
