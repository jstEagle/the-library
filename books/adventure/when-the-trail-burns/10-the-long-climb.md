## 10. The Long Climb

Pell Wexler had spent his professional life flattening the world. That was the cartographer's first sin and first gift: to take a mountain that would kill you in an afternoon and render it as contour lines, gentle as woodgrain, a thing you could hold in one hand. He had always found comfort in that. Paper did not lie the way terrain did—or rather, paper lied in ways you could check, could measure, could hold to account. A chart was a promise you made to strangers you would never meet, and for eleven years Pell had kept those promises beautifully, and then for one afternoon he had not, and everything since had been a long descent from that afternoon.

The mountain did not care about any of it.

They had left the hermit's hut at Kilometer 44 before dawn, moving on Odile's insistence, because the old track-walker had looked at the smoke on the southern ridgeline and said, flatly, *someone is walking your back trail*, and no one had argued. Now the trail did what trails did up here: it gave up pretending to be a trail. It turned to switchbacks, cut into the shoulder of the mountain by hands long dead, each turn a hairpin of broken slate and old timber retaining walls gone soft with rot. Below them the clouds had come in like a tide, filling the valleys to the brim, so that they climbed not up a mountain but out of a white sea, and the peaks around them rose like the masts of drowned ships.

Pell counted the switchbacks because counting was what he did instead of praying. Eleven. Fourteen. Nineteen. Each one gained them perhaps thirty meters of height and cost them a quarter hour of breath, and the breath was the currency that mattered. The air had gone thin and clean and cold, tasting of stone and the mineral smell of old snowmelt. Suri climbed ahead of him with the map wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, her father's obsession beating against her heart with every step. Odile brought up the rear, and Pell was aware of her the way you are aware of a load-bearing wall—always there, always holding, never once complaining about the weight.

Isandro led. He had changed in the last two days, Pell thought. Not softened—*focused*, the way a river focuses when its banks narrow. The man who had frozen at the flooded viaduct, who had stood on the broken span with his hands useless and his eyes gone somewhere three years away, had been replaced by something with a surveyor's patience and a mule-driver's stubbornness. He read the switchbacks like a document, stopping at each turn to examine the cut, the drainage, the old culverts choked with scree. At the fourteenth turn he knelt and brushed debris from a carved benchmark, a brass disk set into living rock, and said nothing, but his jaw tightened.

"You know this cut," Odile said. Not a question.

"I helped survey the closure," Isandro said. "Not the original line. The closure. We came up here with instruments and made it official." He stood, wiped his hands on his thighs. "There's a benchmark every hundred meters. The company wanted the abandonment documented to the centimeter. Like writing an epitaph in brass."

They climbed on.

* * *

The clouds broke at midday, and the world opened.

They came around a shoulder of rock and the sky was suddenly everywhere, a blue so deep and dry it seemed engineered, and below them the cloud sea rolled away to the horizon with the sun lying on it like spilled brass. Above, the switchbacks continued, stitched up a face of gray stone patched with lichen the color of old bruises. The wind up here had teeth. It came off the snowfields in long steady gusts that made Pell lean into the slope like a man wading a river.

They ate cold food in the lee of a cut, backs to the rock, and it was there, with his mouth full of hardtack and his lungs burning, that Pell understood he was going to tell them. He could not have said what triggered it. Perhaps it was the altitude, which thins the blood and thins the lies with it. Perhaps it was the benchmark, the epitaph in brass. Perhaps it was simply that they had been climbing for six hours and he had spent all six composing the confession and discarding it, composing and discarding, and the mountain had worn him down to the sentence itself.

"Odile," he said.

She looked up. She had a way of looking at people that made them feel surveyed—contoured, classified, filed. He had hated it when they met and had come, without noticing the transition, to depend on it.

"I have to tell you all something. Before we go any higher."

Suri stopped unwrapping her second piece of hardtack. Isandro set down his canteen. The wind filled the pause, worrying at their collars.

"I told you I was disgraced," Pell said. "I told you I falsified a chart. That part was true. But I let you believe the falsification was found—that I was caught, that the company caught me and threw me out. That's not what happened." He made himself look at Odile. "No one ever found out. I confessed. I walked into the chief engineer's office eleven days after the survey was published and told him I'd made it up."

The wind again. Somewhere below them, a bird rode the updraft in great slow circles, indifferent.

"Tell it properly," Odile said. "All of it. You don't get to summarize your own crime. None of us do."

Pell nodded. His hands, he noticed, were steady. That surprised him. He had expected to shake.

"The line was the Corvera extension. Mountain branch, high altitude, and the client wanted a route through the Salta Gap that the terrain didn't want to give. I was the lead topographer. Twenty-six years old, best eye in the office, and the survey kept coming back impossible—the gradient through the gap exceeded anything a locomotive of the day could climb with a paying load. Every route. I ran it four ways, four times. The gap could not be crossed at an acceptable grade. That was the truth of the land, and the land does not negotiate."

"So you changed the number," Isandro said quietly.

"I changed the number." The words came out of him with a strange relief, like a splinter leaving flesh. "Point-nine percent. That's all. Nine-tenths of one percent, on one stretch of four kilometers. I moved a contour. I redrew a slope. I told the board the gap could be climbed." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it, a dry bark the wind took away. "Do you know what a falsified gradient means? It means the engineers who came after me designed to my lie. It means they cut the grade they thought they had, and when the trains hit that four kilometers they stalled, or they ran away, or they needed double locomotives and double crews and the operating costs ate the branch alive. It means the line I certified was a line that could not work, and everyone downstream of my one afternoon of cowardice spent thirty years paying for it."

"Killed no one," Suri said. "You said that. Killed no one."

"Killed no one," Pell agreed. "There was a runaway, the second winter. A freight crew rode it down the gap with the brakes burning and brought it into the valley siding at walking speed, on fire, both of them with their eyebrows gone and their hands blistered to the bone. They lived. They were the lucky ones, because they never knew. The inquiry blamed the brakeman. A man named Aldous Ferris. They took his ticket. He died ten years later, I'm told, still insisting the grade was wrong, still insisting the mountain was steeper than the charts said." Pell looked at his own hands. "The charts were wrong because of me. He was telling the truth his whole life and the truth was the one thing I'd stolen."

"And you confessed," Odile said.

"I confessed. And here is the part I have never said out loud, the part that makes it worse than a crime: the chief engineer believed me. He checked my field books, he re-ran my calculations, and when he saw what I'd done he didn't rage. He sat there and he wept. Not for the line. For me. He said, *Pell, you were the best of us, and you thought the truth needed your help.*" Pell's throat closed. He worked past it. "He could have had me prosecuted. Instead he ended me quietly. Blacklisted me from every firm on the coast. My name on a chart became a liability. I have spent nineteen years drafting harbor plans and subdivision plats for men who pay cash and don't ask, and every single day I have known exactly what I am: a man who lied about a mountain, and then made other men pay for the lie."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of four people at altitude, where the air itself is thin and every word must be spent carefully.

Suri spoke first. "Why are you telling us now?"

"Because we're climbing toward something the company buried," Pell said, "and because I have watched each of you carry a weight, and I have carried mine in silence for nineteen years, and silence is not load-bearing. I know that sounds like a draftsman's metaphor. I mean it structurally. A bridge with one hidden flaw doesn't fail where the flaw is. It fails everywhere, all at once, without warning, and it takes whatever's on it down too. I am the hidden flaw. I have been the hidden flaw in every crew I've joined since Corvera, and I will not be that on this one. Not with Vasseur behind us and the monsoon ahead of us and whatever's at the top of this line waiting. If I'm going to be on your bridge, you deserve to know what I am."

He had expected anger. He had braced for Suri's contempt—she had spent years hating a man who chose maps over her, and here was a man who had falsified one—and for Isandro's cold engineer's judgment, and for Odile's flat professional dismissal. He had his answer ready for the dismissal: *I'll go back down alone, you'll be faster without me.*

Odile finished her hardtack. Brushed the crumbs from her gloves. Stood, and looked out at the cloud sea for a long moment, and when she turned around her face was doing something complicated that Pell could not chart.

"Nineteen years," she said. "You've carried it nineteen years."

"Every day."

"And you confessed when you didn't have to. When no one would ever have known." She said it like she was testing a weld, tapping it, listening. "I've run crews for twenty-two years, Wexler. You know what I've learned about men? Not what they do wrong. Everybody does something wrong; the mountain guarantees it. What I've learned is to watch what a man does *after*. Most men bury it. Most men spend the rest of their lives building little walls around the flaw and posting guards and hoping nobody surveys too close. And the ones who don't—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, something old, "—the ones who walk into the office and say *it was me*, those men I can build with. Because a crew is just a structure, and a structure is only as good as its members' willingness to announce their own cracks."

"You forgive me, then." Pell made it flat, disbelieving. "Just like that."

"Forgive?" Odile almost smiled. "I'm not the one owed forgiveness. Ferris is. The chief engineer is. The mountain, maybe." She picked up her pack, settled the straps with the economy of long habit. "What I'm offering isn't forgiveness. It's *trust*, and I'm offering it because you earned it this morning, on this rock, and not because of anything you did nineteen years ago. You want absolution, go find Ferris's grave and say your piece to the headstone. What I want is to know that the man reading our charts is a man who tells the truth about mountains. As of five minutes ago, I know it." She jerked her chin at the switchbacks above. "Now get up. We're losing the light window, and I don't intend to sleep on exposed rock with Vasseur's people on our back trail."

Pell stood. His legs were shaking now—reaction, the body presenting its bill after the soul had paid. Isandro clasped his shoulder once, briefly, a surveyor's grip, precise and firm, and said nothing, and the nothing said everything. Suri looked at him a long moment with her father's eyes, and said, "He kept margin notes. Forty years of them. Notes on things he got wrong, and went back to fix. Maybe you'd have liked him." And she turned and started climbing.

* * *

They made the ridge at last light.

The switchbacks ended at a col between two peaks, a saddle of wind-scoured stone where the old line had crossed from one watershed into another, and there, half-buried in the drifts, lay the ruins of a snowshed—the great timber gallery the company had built to keep the avalanches off the track, its roof collapsed, its walls leaning, a ribcage of blackened wood on the spine of the mountain. Beyond it, descending into the next valley, Pell could see the track itself, emerging from the snow in intermittent arcs of rusted rail, running down and down toward a darkness in the mountainside far below.

A tunnel. A great one, by the size of the portal's shadow, even at this distance. Sealed.

Isandro stood at the edge of the col with his binoculars up, and Pell watched his shoulders change, watched the tension come into them like a change in the weather.

"What is it?" Suri asked.

"Snowshed's intact enough to shelter in," Isandro said. "But look at the portal. Look at the talus fan below it." He passed the glasses to Pell, and Pell raised them, and the world jumped close: the tunnel mouth, a black arch in the gray rock, and below it a fan of broken stone that did not look like the mountain's own work. Too uniform. Too fresh-edged. And at the mouth itself, glinting in the last light—

"Concrete," Pell said. "They plugged it. Poured a plug right into the bore."

"Then why," Suri said slowly, taking the glasses, "is there a hole in it?"

Pell took the glasses back. She was right. At the base of the concrete plug, low, near the old rail line, there was a breach—a ragged gap the size of a cart, edges dark with fresh fracture, stone dust still visible even at this range, pale as a scar.

Someone had opened the grave. Recently.

And down at the fan's edge, tiny with distance, something else caught the light and held it: a line of canvas, a camp. Tents. And a figure moving among them, and then another.

Odile took the glasses last and was silent for a long time. When she lowered them her face had gone into the flat, hard calm that Pell had learned, in the weeks since the expedition began, to fear more than shouting.

"Light's gone in twenty minutes," she said. "We make the snowshed, we post watches, and nobody lights a fire. And Wexler—" she handed the glasses back to him, and her eyes held his, and there was no judgment in them, only weight, the weight she had told him he could carry now, "—I hope you meant what you said on the mountain. Because tomorrow we're going to need every honest man we've got."

Below them in the gathering dark, at the mouth of the sealed tunnel, a lantern came alight.
