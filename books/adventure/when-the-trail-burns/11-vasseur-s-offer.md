## 11. Vasseur's Offer

The rain came in the night, a thin rehearsal of the monsoon proper—just enough to wet the canvas and make the guylines sing—and by morning the whole camp smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and the iron tang of the river running high below them. Isandro was up before anyone else, as he always was. Three years of habit had made him a creature of first light: boil water, check the instruments, walk the line of chainage markers with his hands in his pockets and his mind mercifully empty.

Empty was the whole point. He had built the emptiness himself, plank by plank, like a trestle over a gorge. You could live on it if you kept it maintained.

He was crouched at the edge of camp, cleaning grit out of the theodolite's leveling screws with a stiff brush, when he heard footsteps behind him that were not Odile's boots or Pell's uneven shuffle or Suri's quick, deliberate stride. These steps were unhurried. Polished. The steps of a man who had never once had to hurry anywhere in his life.

"Mr. Zhou." The voice matched the steps. Smooth, faintly amused, pitched to carry no farther than it needed to. "You keep early hours."

Isandro did not turn around right away. He finished the screw he was cleaning, set the brush down on the folded cloth, closed the instrument case with a click that sounded, in the gray dawn, very loud. Then he stood and turned.

Mr. Vasseur stood at the edge of the cook fire's cold ring, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a garden. He was dry. That was the first thing Isandro noticed—the man had walked through a night of rain and his shoes were dry, his coat was dry, not a spot on him, which meant he had sheltered nearby and waited for exactly this hour, this moment, when the camp slept and only one man kept watch against nothing in particular.

"You're a long way from the railhead," Isandro said.

"I'm a long way from a great many things I'd rather be near." Vasseur smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind that had been fitted to his face by professionals. "But then, so are you. That's rather the point of places like this, isn't it? Everyone here is a long way from something."

Isandro said nothing. Behind Vasseur, the mountains stood up out of the mist in ranks, gray on gray, and somewhere above them, invisible from here, the sealed tunnel waited under its forty years of silence. Somewhere behind them, two of Vasseur's men had shadowed their camp for three days now—he'd seen the smoke where no smoke should be, heard the horse in the dark that moved off too quietly to be wild.

"You gave my offer a great deal of thought," Vasseur said. It wasn't a question. "I respect that. Deliberation is a virtue in an engineer. Measure twice, cut once—I believe that's the trade's old saying." He took a step closer, and his voice dropped into something warmer, more intimate, the register of a confessor rather than a salesman. "But I've come to understand that money was never going to be the lever with you. Money is for men who want things. You, Mr. Zhou—you're a man who wants *nothing*. Which makes you, frankly, a rarity, and a puzzle, and—" the smile again, thinner now, "—a problem."

"The answer hasn't changed," Isandro said. "We're delivering the map. That's all."

"Yes. The map." Vasseur turned, gazed up the valley as if reading the terrain the way Pell read charts. "A dying prospector's obsession. A piece of oilcloth covered in a dead man's handwriting. And four people willing to die for it in country the railroad itself won't enter. Do you know what that looks like from where I stand? It looks like a man carrying something worth more than he knows." He looked back. "Or worth more than he *wants* to know. There's a difference. The second kind is more common than you'd think."

The fire pit between them was cold ash. Isandro found himself staring at it, at the gray circles where flames had been, and hated himself for the stare because it felt like giving ground.

"Here is what I've learned about you," Vasseur went on, conversationally now, strolling a slow half-circle around the cold fire as though inspecting it. "Three years with the survey corps. Transferred in from the mountain division. Before that, the Kestrel River bridge." He pronounced the name gently, the way one sets down something fragile. "You know it. Of course you know it. Eleven men. A span that came down in a wind it should have shrugged off, on a morning it should have held. And a survey report signed"—he paused, delicately, savoring it—"by I. Zhou, assistant engineer, certifying the abutment sound."

The world went very quiet. Not silent—the river was still talking below, the birds still working the willows—but quiet in the way Isandro's head had gone quiet once before, standing on the wreckage with a clipboard in his hand while men he knew pulled bodies from the water. A white quiet. A pressure quiet, like deep water pressing on the eardrums.

"That report is public record," Isandro said. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of that. "It was investigated. Cleared."

"Investigated," Vasseur agreed pleasantly. "Cleared. Yes. Though investigations are such curious instruments, aren't they? They find what they're pointed at." He stopped circling. He faced Isandro fully now, and the warmth drained out of his face, leaving something older and colder underneath, the way a glacier leaves bare rock. "My employers hold certain documents that were never entered into any record. Field notes. Correspondence. A second survey, taken after yours, that flagged the same fault you certified as sound—a fault your superiors chose to bury, and you chose not to fight. Your signature is on the certification, Mr. Zhou. Your initials are in the margin of the memo that decided not to act on the warning. Eleven men went across that bridge on a morning they trusted you, and the mountain took them, and you signed the paper that let them cross."

"They didn't trust me," Isandro heard himself say. It came out flat, automatic, a sentence worn smooth by three years of saying it to himself in the dark. "They trusted the company. I was twenty-six years old. I flagged the fault myself in my field notes. They filed them and told me to sign the summary."

"And you signed."

"And I signed."

Vasseur spread his hands, almost kindly. "There it is. You see how simple it is? I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm merely observing that the truth of you exists in writing, in several hands, and that my employers would be very distressed if it reached the newspapers alongside—well. Alongside whatever it is your dead prospector found up that tunnel." He tilted his head. "Imagine the story. *Disgraced engineer exposes cover-up.* The journalists would love it. They always want a villain, and when there isn't one, they'll take the nearest man with a signature. Your name beside the names of eleven drowned men, printed in every paper from the coast to the capital. Every crew you ever worked with. Every foreman who ever shook your hand. Odile Brant reads the papers, Mr. Zhou. So does the girl."

The name landed harder than the rest of it. Isandro felt it go in under the ribs, precise as a chisel finding a seam. *Odile Brant reads the papers.*

"So," Vasseur said, and his voice softened into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse than everything before it. "The map. All of it—the original, the copies, the girl's father's notes. You give them to me at the pass tomorrow, and you walk away clean. No story. No headlines. The past stays where you buried it, and you go on measuring your miles in peace. Or you refuse, and I release what I hold, and you spend the rest of your life watching every person you work beside read about the day you helped kill eleven men." He smiled again, and this time the smile had real feeling in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "I'm told you've spent three years making sure no one would care enough to be hurt by that. But that was before, wasn't it? Before the foreman who nearly drowned pulling your equipment out of the river. Before the cartographer who trusts you. Before the daughter." He let the word sit. "You've acquired people, Mr. Zhou. People are leverage. It's the oldest engineering principle in the world: everything that bears load can be brought down."

For a long moment Isandro said nothing. The mist was lifting off the valley in slow rags. Somewhere up-slope, Suri was breaking camp—he could hear her voice low, talking to the mule, the way she talked to animals and not yet to people. Odile would be checking the rigging. Pell would be complaining about his boots.

He looked at Vasseur and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that the man had made one mistake. He had assumed Isandro was still the man who signed. Still the man who kept his mouth shut and his head down and let the paperwork carry the weight. Still a man who measured his life in miles because miles asked nothing of him.

"You've done your homework," Isandro said finally.

"It's what I'm paid for."

"Then do one more calculation." Isandro picked up the theodolite case and slung it over his shoulder. "If I give you the map, and you let us walk away, and whatever's in that tunnel stays buried—that's eleven men on my ledger, plus whatever number is waiting up there under the rock. I've carried eleven for three years. I know exactly what they weigh." He met Vasseur's eyes. "You're offering to add to the load and call it mercy. So my answer is the same as it was, and it'll be the same tomorrow at the pass: no."

Vasseur studied him for a moment, and something flickered behind the polish—not respect, exactly, but a recalculation, an engineer's own measure-twice. "Loyalty," he said at last, tasting the word. "How quaint. How expensive." He stepped back, brushed a speck of ash from his sleeve that may not have been there at all. "I'll confess I hoped it wouldn't come to this. But you should know that my instructions allow for... other methods. The map will reach my employers one way or another, Mr. Zhou. The only variable left is what happens to the people carrying it." He turned to go, then paused, half-smiling over his shoulder. "Oh—one more thing. Your young cartographer. Wexler. Ask him sometime about the Halvard chart. Ask him why he really left the service. You might find the timing... instructive."

He walked away down the trail, dry-shod even on the wet stones, and the mist took him the way it takes everything in these mountains—gradually, then completely.

Isandro stood alone at the cold fire for a long time. Above him the peaks burned pale gold with the first sun, beautiful and indifferent, and below him the river ran high and brown with the coming season. He thought about the bridge. He thought about the sound it had made—not the crash, everyone remembered the crash, but the sound just before, the groan of steel deciding, the sound he still heard sometimes in the space between sleeping and waking. He thought about a signature in ink that had dried thirty seconds after he made it and had never, in three years, finished drying.

Then he shouldered the case and walked back into camp, where Odile was tightening a pack strap and Suri was arguing with the mule and Pell was burning the porridge, and none of them looked up at him with anything but the ordinary trust of people who believed they knew the man walking among them.

He opened his mouth to say something—anything—and found that the emptiness he had built so carefully was full to the brim with the weight of what he was carrying, and that for the first time in three years, he had no idea how much longer it would hold.

Behind them, up the trail, a single rifle shot cracked off the rockface—close enough to raise birds from the willows, far enough to be a message.

Vasseur's men wanted them to know the clock had started.
