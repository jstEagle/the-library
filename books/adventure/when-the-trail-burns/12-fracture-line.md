## 12. Fracture Line

Odile Brant had spent twenty-two years reading rail crews the way other people read weather. She could tell a storm coming in the set of a man's shoulders, in the way conversation thinned around a fire, in who stopped meeting whose eyes. And for two days now, the air between Isandro Zhou and the rest of them had carried that particular pressure that comes before something breaks.

It started with small things. Isandro taking the first watch alone when watch rotations were supposed to be paired—*safety in pairs*, she'd drilled into every crew she'd ever run, and he knew it, he'd helped her write the rule. Isandro walking point on the switchbacks without being asked, putting distance between himself and Suri. Isandro's lantern burning past midnight at the edge of camp, angled away from the others, his shadow bent over something small and flat that he closed quickly whenever anyone stirred.

Odile told herself it was the mountain. Altitude did strange things to careful men. The climb above Kilometer 44 had taken something out of all of them—the thin cold air, the switchbacks stacked like dropped ribbon, Pell wheezing and laughing at nothing, Suri going quiet as the trees gave way to scree. They were all fraying. That was all.

But then there was Vasseur.

The man had come to them polished as a new rail spike, all soft words and harder arithmetic, offering money for a dead prospector's map. Isandro had refused him—Odile had stood close enough to hear it, had felt the refusal come off him like heat off ballast stone in summer. She'd believed it. She'd *wanted* to believe it, which should have been her first warning. Wanting was how rails buckled: not from the load you expected but from the expansion nobody accounted for.

And this morning, breaking camp below the last shoulder before the high col, she'd seen it.

She'd gone back down the trail fifty meters for the dropped water tin—her own fault, her own carelessness—and found Isandro standing apart with a slip of paper in his hand. Not the map. A note, folded once, creased sharp. He heard her boot on the gravel and the paper vanished into his coat with a speed that told her everything before she'd read a single word of anything.

He'd smiled at her. That was the worst part. He'd smiled like a man greeting a stationmaster, pleasant and empty, and said, "Dropped something?" and held out the water tin she hadn't even been looking for yet.

"No," Odile had said slowly. "I came back for that, though." And she'd taken it, and watched his eyes, and his eyes had done what guilty men's eyes do—they'd gone somewhere else entirely.

Now they climbed. The col waited above them, a notch of bruised sky between two shoulders of black rock, and beyond it, according to Mateo Reyes's map, the sealed tunnel mouth where whatever truth the railroad had dynamited shut still slept behind forty years of rubble. Four days, maybe less, until the monsoon made landfall. The clouds already massing in the west had a greenish underbelly that Odile didn't like at all; she'd seen that color ride in ahead of storms that ate whole valleys.

Plenty of time, she thought grimly, for one more thing to go wrong.

They made the col by late afternoon and camped in the lee of an old snow-fence, its posts leaning like tired soldiers, wind combing through the wire with a sound like someone humming through their teeth. Pell got a fire going in a ring of stones some forgotten track crew had left, and Suri spread the map out flat on a slab of granite, weighting the corners with rocks, tracing her father's annotations by firelight. Her finger moved along the inked line of the old grade, pausing at each marginal note in Mateo's cramped hand—elevations, bearing corrections, and those strange little symbols no one had fully decoded, circles with tails, like tadpoles swimming upstream through the mountains.

"Tomorrow we make the portal," Suri said. "If the talus hasn't shifted since his last survey." Her voice was steady, but Odile saw the way her thumb pressed white against the map's edge, holding it down as if the paper might fly home without her permission.

Isandro sat apart. Not far—ten feet, maybe twelve—but apart, the way a man sits when he's keeping a border around himself. He was cleaning the grit out of the clinometer case, working the hinge with a rag, and he wasn't looking at the map, and he wasn't looking at any of them, and Odile had run out of patience somewhere back on the switchbacks.

She crossed the ten feet and crouched beside him, voice pitched low, under the wind. "Show me what was in your pocket this morning."

His hands didn't stop moving. That was answer enough, and they both knew it. "I don't know what you mean."

"I've hauled mules over passes with more subtlety than you, Isandro. The paper. This morning. You folded it away fast enough to tear it."

"It's nothing. Chainage notes."

"Chainage notes," Odile repeated. She let the word sit there between them like a dead animal. Then she stood up, because this was not a conversation to have in a whisper at the edge of a fire. Some fractures you could feel running through rock, and if you didn't set the charge yourself, on your own terms, the mountain would do it for you at the worst possible hour.

"Isandro," she said, loud enough for all of them. "Show the crew what's in your coat."

* * *

The fire cracked. The wind hummed through the snow-fence. Nobody moved except Isandro, who went very still, the way machinery goes still just before a bearing seizes.

Pell looked up from his notebook, pencil hovering. Suri rose slowly to her feet, one hand still resting on the corner of the map, and Odile watched her face do the thing faces do when hope and dread arrive at the same door together.

"Odile." Isandro's voice was quiet, controlled, the voice of a man reading numbers aloud. "You're mistaken."

"Then show us and we'll all sleep well."

He looked at her for a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes and there was something behind them she'd never seen in three years of working beside him—not defiance, not anger. Resignation. The look of a man who has been waiting a long time for exactly this and is almost relieved.

Almost.

He reached into his coat and took out the folded paper. He didn't unfold it. He held it, and Odile saw his knuckles, and she thought: *there it is*, the same tremor she'd seen in him at the flooded viaduct, the day the water took the equipment crate and Odile went in after it and Isandro froze on the bank like a man watching his own drowning from thirty years away. She'd covered for him then too, in her way. Said nothing. Given him room. Room, she understood now, was the one thing she should never have given this man, because he used room the way a crack uses water.

"Suri," Isandro said, and his voice cracked on her name, just slightly, a hairline fracture in the word itself. "Before I say anything else—I want you to know that I never intended—"

"Open it," Suri said.

He opened it.

Even across the fire, even upside down, Odile could read enough. Fine paper, expensive paper, the kind that companies bought by the ream for men like Mr. Vasseur. A letterhead. A few lines in a clerk's precise hand. And at the bottom, a signature that was unmistakably Isandro's—she'd seen it on a hundred survey certifications, that distinctive downward slash through the Z.

Suri read it faster than any of them. Odile watched the words land on her one at a time, like stones dropped into still water, each one sending out rings.

"'In consideration of safe passage and the return of certain materials,'" Suri read aloud, her voice flat and terrible, "'the undersigned agrees to deliver the Reyes survey document to representatives of the holder upon completion of route verification.'" She looked up. "Route verification. You were going to walk us to the tunnel. You were going to let me find my father's life's work, and then you were going to *hand it to them*."

"It's not—" Isandro started.

"Don't." The word came out of Suri like a shot. "Don't you dare say it's not what it looks like. I've had forty years of men telling me things aren't what they look like. My father. The company. Every official who ever stamped a form over a closed gate." She stepped around the fire, and Odile half-rose, ready to move between them, but Suri wasn't going for violence. She was going for worse. She stopped three feet from Isandro and looked up at him with eyes that had stopped being angry and become something colder and more permanent. "Vasseur knows something about you. Doesn't he. That's the price. That's why a man who refused his money took his paper instead."

Isandro said nothing. The silence was a confession with better manners.

"What does he have?" Odile asked quietly.

Isandro's jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice had the hollow quality of a culvert with the stream gone out of it. "A bridge. Three years ago. The Kestrel River crossing. I signed the survey." He said it to the fire, not to any of them. "My figures. My certification. The span failed under a freight load it should have carried twice over. Eleven men. My crew. Men whose names I still—" His hand closed. Opened. "Vasseur has the inquiry record. The part that names me. He offered to bury it. All he wanted in exchange was the map, after we'd confirmed the route."

"And you said yes." Pell's voice, from beside the fire, very soft. The cartographer had gone pale, his pencil forgotten. "You shook his hand and you said yes, and then you led us up here."

"He gave me until the summit. I kept telling myself I'd find another way. That I'd copy the map, give them the copy—"

"You'd have given them a forgery," Suri said, "and when they discovered it, they'd have destroyed you anyway, and probably us with it. Or worse—you'd have given them the real one, because when the moment came, that's what men like you do. You sign." She spat the last word like gristle. "You put your name on things and other people die of it."

That landed. Odile saw it land, saw Isandro take it full in the chest like a coupling under compression, and she almost spoke—to soften it, to intervene, to do the foreman's job of keeping the crew from tearing itself apart on a cold mountainside with a storm four days out. Almost.

But she'd spent three years working beside a man and calling the silence between them companionship, and she was tired of the cost of that silence. So instead she said, "How long?"

Isandro looked at her.

"How long have you been carrying his paper? Since the viaduct? Since Kilometer 44? Before?" She held his gaze. "Because I've been watching you burn your lantern past midnight for two days, and I told myself it was the altitude. I *chose* to tell myself that. So help me understand when I became someone you decided not to trust."

"I trusted you," Isandro said. "That's not—"

"You trusted none of us. Trusting is what you didn't do. You calculated. You ran Vasseur's errand inside our expedition and called it protecting us." She turned to the others. "He put every one of us in the path of armed company men to save his own skin. That's what happened here. Let's not dress it."

The wind gusted through the snow-fence, and the fire leaned and recovered, throwing their shadows huge against the rock. Somewhere downslope, loose stone clattered—a natural sound, probably, though every head turned toward it, and Odile noted that too: they were all listening for Vasseur's men now, and they hadn't been, not really, not until tonight.

Suri bent and picked up the map. She rolled it with quick, practiced motions, sliding it into its oilcloth sleeve, and slung her pack onto her shoulder.

"Suri." Isandro was on his feet. "Wait."

"For what? An apology?" She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only altitude and hurt. "Take it up with Vasseur. Maybe he'll trade you something for it."

"I can fix this."

"No." She said it simply, and it was worse than shouting. "You can't. That's the thing about signatures, Isandro. My father taught me that, in his way. A mark on paper doesn't stay where you put it. It travels. It gets read by people you'll never meet, long after you mean anything by it." She adjusted the pack strap. "I'm going to the tunnel. Tonight, while there's light enough to pick the trail. Alone."

"Absolutely not," Odile said. "Not at elevation, not at dusk, not with a storm building—"

"Then come or don't." Suri was already moving, picking her way toward the notch of the col, her silhouette sharpening against the last gray light. "But I'm done letting other people decide what happens to my father's work. I've done that my whole life. Everyone decides—the company, the officials, the men with money. Everyone but me." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and for one moment her voice lost its armor. "He carried this map for forty years so that somebody would finally look. I'm not handing it to the last man who'd rather sell it."

Then she was gone, up into the dark, her boots crunching away with terrible steadiness.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while.

Pell fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Well," he said at last, in the voice of a man describing wreckage. "That could have gone better."

"Shut up, Pell." Odile rubbed both hands over her face. Above them the col was a blacker black against the sky, and the western clouds had eaten half the stars, and she was doing the arithmetic she always did—hours of light, hours of storm, distance, load, the weight of human bodies against the grade—and the arithmetic kept coming back wrong. Split the party and risk Suri alone on a trail her father had marked but no one had walked in decades. Hold together and drag a fracture up to a tunnel mouth where God knew what waited. Neither number balanced.

Isandro stood where Suri had left him, staring up the slope. In the firelight his face had gone the color of ash, and Odile thought—not unkindly, not kindly either—that she was looking at a man discovering the exact weight of what he'd signed. Again. It was always again, with him. The bridge, and now this. Two papers, eleven men and counting, and a woman walking into the dark rather than stand near him a moment longer.

"She'll kill herself on that trail," Isandro said. "The talus field above the col—after dark, with the wind up—"

"Then you'd better hope your legs work better than your promises." Odile kicked dirt over the fire's edge, killing it down to a red eye. "Get your kit. We're going after her. All of us." She jabbed a finger at Pell. "You too. If she finds that tunnel alone, she opens it alone, and whatever's in there, she shouldn't meet it by herself."

"And him?" Pell nodded at Isandro.

Odile looked at Isandro for a long moment. The wind pulled at the snow-fence wire, humming its one note.

"Him," she said, "walks in the middle. Where I can see him. And the first time he looks at that ridge line like he's measuring something for someone else, I will personally introduce him to the concept of gravity." She shouldered her pack. "Move. We're losing the light, and I don't need a map to tell me what's coming out of that sky."

Far to the west, thunder answered her—low and long, rolling down out of the green-bellied clouds like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Four days, she'd thought. Standing there in the dying firelight, watching Isandro buckle his pack with hands that almost didn't shake, Odile Brant revised the number downward, and prayed she was wrong.
