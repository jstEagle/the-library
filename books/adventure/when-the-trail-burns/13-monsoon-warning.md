## 13. Monsoon Warning

The birds told them first.

Isandro had learned to read weather the way other men read timetables—by signs, by pressure, by the small betrayals of the living world. For three days the cicadas had gone silent at noon instead of dusk. The swifts that usually stitched the valley air in high, careless loops were flying low and hard, wings clipped to the wind's new temper. And this morning, standing at the edge of the switchback camp with his coffee going cold in his hands, he watched a line of ants abandon their mound in the trail bank and march uphill, carrying their white eggs like a funeral procession moving to higher ground.

Everything that could climb was climbing.

"Four days," Pell said behind him. He had his barometer out, the brass case polished to a dull gold from forty years of nervous thumbs. "Maybe five if we're lucky. The pressure's falling faster than it did in '09—I was surveying the coast then, watched three inches of mercury drop in a day and a half. When it goes, it won't rain, Isandro. It will *arrive*."

Isandro didn't answer. He was looking at the mountains.

They had come down off the high country two days ago, out of the cloud line and into a green so dense it seemed to have weight, ferns shouldering over the old grade, water already running loud in every gully though not a drop had fallen on them. The sealed tunnel lay behind and above them now—two days' climb back up into that cold stone throat where Mateo Reyes had kept his terrible witness for forty years. In Isandro's pack, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin inside his spare shirt, rode the copies of everything: the photographs Pell had taken of the records, the ledger pages with their hundreds of erased names, the map itself with its margin notes in Mateo's cramped, patient hand.

The originals too. Suri carried those. She hadn't let them out of her sight since the tunnel, and Isandro understood. Some cargo you didn't trust to anyone else's spine.

Four days to the valley railhead. It was a six-day walk in good weather, five if they pushed, and the weather was about to turn murderous. Every river between here and the lowlands would become something else entirely—something with teeth. The fords they'd crossed coming up, ankle-deep and laughing, would be chest-deep and brown and fast as freight.

He did the arithmetic the way he always did, coldly, in chainage and gradient and cubic feet per second, and the arithmetic said what it always said: someone might not make it.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, older, the other voice. The one that said: *you know exactly how this ends. You've stood on a structure while it came apart beneath people who trusted you. You know the sound.*

Odile came up beside him and looked where he was looking. She'd been quiet since the pass—quieter than Odile had any right to be, after what she'd given there. Her wrists still bore the rope marks where Vasseur's men had bound her, and she turned them absently as she stood, like a woman worrying an old scar.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're building a bridge in your head and it's already falling down." She took the cup from his hands and drank his coffee without asking, which was Odile's way of saying she knew him. "Talk to me, engineer."

"There's a crossing at Kilometer 31," Isandro said. "The Tamarind Fork. Coming up it was knee-deep. Going down, after four days of monsoon landfall—" He shook his head. "It'll be a wall. And below it the gorge narrows, and there's nowhere to stand and nothing to rig to. If we're caught on the wrong side of that river, we don't wait it out. We die waiting."

"So we beat it down the mountain."

"We try. But the storm doesn't care about our schedule, and neither do Vasseur's men, and I can't—" He stopped. The words snagged on something in his chest, some splinter that had been working its way toward the surface for three years and now, with the pressure falling and the ants marching, would not stay buried.

Odile waited. She was good at that. Twenty-two years running crews had taught her that men talked when they talked and not before, but she'd also learned, somewhere along the way, to make the waiting feel like a door held open rather than a wall.

"I froze," Isandro said.

It came out flat. He made himself go on.

"At the viaduct. River of Rust. When you went under—the equipment went under and you went after it, and I stood on the bank like a man carved out of wood. Three seconds. Maybe four. Long enough for Suri to move and me not to. You want to know why? Because the last time I stood on failing ground and heard someone scream, my body decided it would rather stop than choose again. There were eleven men on the Kestrel Bridge when it went. I signed the survey. My instruments, my load figures, my signature on the drawing that said *this crossing is sound.*"

The words kept coming now, as if a gate had rusted open.

"It wasn't sabotage. It wasn't even negligence, not exactly—it was hurry. The company wanted the span opened before the dry season ended, and I wanted to be the man who delivered it, and I shaved margins I shouldn't have shaved. Scour depth. I underestimated scour on the center pier because the flood models were inconvenient and I trusted my own numbers more than the river's patience. Eleven men. Two of them boys, nineteen years old, brothers. They'd worked under me for a year. One of them used to sing while he set rivets, badly, all the time, and I can still hear it."

He heard his own voice shake and let it.

"Afterward there was an inquiry, and the company needed a story, and the story was *act of God, unprecedented flood.* They offered me a transfer and silence, and I took it, and I've been hiding in survey work ever since—miles, not years, that's how I counted, because miles don't ask you who you are. And then Vasseur found out. He knew before we ever left the railhead. That's what the offer in the dark was really about—not money. Silence. My past for your future, and I sat there at the fire afterward knowing what I'd traded and hating myself for the relief of it."

He finally turned to look at her.

"Suri knows. I told her before the tunnel—no. That's not true. I told her *after,* when the fracture came, when you caught me with Vasseur's terms and the whole thing came apart. But you—you carried me across that river country on your shoulders, Odile. You nearly drowned pulling gear that wasn't worth your life, and I stood there counting my shame instead of moving. You deserve to hear it from me, not from Vasseur, not secondhand, not ever."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the swifts wheeled low over the treeline, and somewhere upslope a branch cracked under no wind at all—the mountain settling, shrugging, getting ready.

"You know what I did before I ran crews?" she said at last. "I fired the shot that dropped a trestle span onto a riverbed on purpose. Demolition work. Clean, sanctioned, paid well. Except the third span didn't fall clean. Jammed on the pier, half-standing, and the company said leave it, nobody's under it, and I went out anyway because I couldn't sleep knowing it was hanging there crooked over the water. Fell while I was cutting it loose. Put me in the river too." She flexed her marked wrists. "Nobody wrote songs about it. Nobody needed to. Point is, engineer—I've stood on failing ground and heard it groan. What matters isn't the three seconds you froze. It's the thirty years you spend not freezing after."

"That's generous."

"That's experience." She handed the cup back. "You told us. That's the whole bridge, right there. Now let's talk about the river."

* * *

He told the others at midday, properly, all of them seated on the old grade with the packs down and the sky gone the color of pewter in the west.

Suri already knew most of it; she sat with her arms around her knees and listened anyway, giving him the dignity of a full telling. Pell took off his spectacles and cleaned them twice, which was his way of praying. When Isandro finished—with the bridge, with the inquiry, with Vasseur's blackmail and his own cowardice laid out end to end like rail on a grade—Pell put the spectacles back on and said:

"In 1889 I drew a chart that moved a reef eleven meters. Falsified it, neat as scripture, because a shipping concern paid me and I told myself the channel was deep enough anyway. A steamer grounded on it. No one died—but the pilot lost his ticket, the cartographer general lost his post, and I lost the only thing I was ever good at. I've spent twenty years redrawing honest maps to pay for one dishonest one." He smiled, thin and rueful. "We're a crew of forged documents, Isandro. It's a wonder we found each other."

"We're a crew," Suri said quietly, "that keeps choosing truth at the worst possible moments. My father carried a grave in his pocket for forty years because telling it cost too much. Then he gave it to a stranger on a mountainside." She looked at Isandro, and there was no judgment in her face—only the steady, exhausted clarity of a woman who had read her father's heart in his own handwriting. "You froze for three seconds. He froze for forty. Get up, engineer. We've got a river to beat."

Odile was already on her feet, rolling the map case closed. "Kilometer 31. Tamarind Fork. How do we cross it fast and alive?"

Isandro felt something shift in his chest—a load transferring, the way weight moves through a truss when one member finally takes the strain it was built for. He stood, and pulled the notebook from his coat, and began to draw.

"There's an old service trestle two hundred meters downstream," he said. "Dismantled on paper—company records say the timber was pulled in the closure. But companies lie. If even the piers are standing, I can deck it. Cable, planking from the depot ruins, two hours' work. We cross light, we cross fast, and we're on the right side of that river before the sky opens."

"And if the piers are gone?" Pell asked.

Isandro looked west, where the first true clouds were stacking themselves against the peaks like freight cars coupling, black and patient and immense.

"Then we swim for it," he said, "and I go first."

* * *

They broke camp within the hour and drove downslope through air that tasted of iron and green rot. By late afternoon the light had gone strange and yellow, the kind of light that makes shadows sharp as knife cuts, and the heat pressed down thick as wet wool. The forest had stopped its ordinary noise. No birdsong. No insects. Just their boots on the old ties and the sound of water growing louder in every ravine they passed, rivers rehearsing.

At Kilometer 29 they found the boot prints again—Vasseur's men, three of them at least, moving fast and parallel to their own route. Fresh. Hours old at most. Odile crouched over them and read the ground the way Pell read charts.

"They're racing us to the same crossing," she said. "They know the storm's coming too. They want the map before the river cuts the road behind us."

"Then we give them a road that closes," Isandro said. He was already walking, faster now, the engineer's stride, measuring with his eyes—span, height, flow. "There's a culvert at the fork, stone-arched, pre-closure. Once we're across, I can drop the debris jam upstream and flood the trail behind us. Buy us a day. Maybe two."

Suri fell into step beside him, the map case slung tight across her back. "You're sure about the culvert?"

"I'm sure about water," Isandro said. And found, to his surprise, that it was true—that the fear was still there, coiled in him like cable under tension, but it was bearing load now instead of breaking him. "Water's the one thing I never lied to."

Ahead, through a break in the trees, he saw it: the Tamarind Fork, already swollen brown and muscular with the storm's outriders, and beyond it, half-swallowed by vine, the black stumps of the old service trestle rising from the current like broken teeth.

Three piers. All three standing.

Isandro allowed himself one breath—one long breath of the charged, metallic, storm-smelling air—and then started down the bank, calling orders in a voice that carried clear over the rising water, a voice that belonged, at last, to the man saying them.

Behind them, far up the trail, thunder spoke for the first time.
