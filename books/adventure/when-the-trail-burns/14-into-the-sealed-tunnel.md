## 14. Into the Sealed Tunnel

The portal had been trying to become a mountain again for forty years.

Suri stood before it in the grey hour before dawn, when the fog came down the valley like a tide and turned everything—the scree slopes, the dead rails vanishing into talus, the four of them with their packs and lanterns—into shapes without edges. The dynamite had done its work in 1911 or thereabouts; her father's map said so, in his cramped hand: *Bore sealed 14 March, charge pattern irregular, rockfall continues to settle.* But mountains are patient, and stone remembers what it was, and in four decades the rubble at the tunnel mouth had begun to dream itself back into slope. Scrub pine grew from the crown of the collapse. A thread of water seeped out between two boulders and fell, bright as a wire, into a pool the color of old tea.

"Charming," Odile said. She said it the way she said most things, flat and loud enough to be heard over weather. "A hole in a hill full of loose rock, which we propose to enter on purpose."

"You don't have to," Suri said.

"I didn't say I wasn't coming." Odile spat, ceremonially, to one side. "I said it was charming. There's a difference. One is an opinion about aesthetics. The other is a decision about my feet."

Pell Wexler was already at the face of the collapse, down on his knees in the wet, holding her father's map open under an oilcloth against the drizzle. He'd been kneeling there for ten minutes, and when he looked up his glasses were fogged and his voice had gone strange and careful, the way it did when he was handling something he thought might break.

"Suri. Come look at this."

She went. The map lay between them, soft as cloth now from handling, its creases gone pale as scars. Her father had drawn the portal twice—once in ink, the surveyor's version, all clean lines and elevations—and once in pencil, pressed so hard the paper had dented, a rougher drawing beside it that showed not the tunnel but the *sealing* of it: the charge holes marked as small circles, numbered, with arrows showing how the rock would fall. And beneath the pencil drawing, in letters she needed Pell's magnifier to read:

*They did not seal a tunnel. They sealed a door. Go in low on the left where the charge lifted clean. The company wanted this shut because of what it held, not what it blocked.*

"He knew," Suri said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "He knew before we ever started. He knew exactly what we'd find."

"He knew what he'd found," Pell corrected gently. "Forty years ago. Whether it's still—" He stopped. Adjusted his glasses. "Whether it's still there. Yes. I think he was certain it would be. Certain things don't rot in cold dry rock. That's rather the problem."

Isandro had come up behind them without sound, the way he always moved, a big man who'd learned to make himself into a rumor. He crouched, studied the left side of the collapse where the boulders leaned together into something like a lintel, and Suri watched him do the thing he did—that silent arithmetic, eyes ticking across angles and loads, reading stone the way Pell read documents and Odile read people.

"There," he said finally, pointing. "See how the slab above is bridged? The charge on the left lifted clean—they undercut it, the whole block dropped as one piece instead of shattering. It's resting on its own edge. There'll be a gap underneath." He glanced at her, and in the fog his face was mostly shadow. "It'll be tight. Crawling for the first stretch. You can wait outside if—"

"No," Suri said.

He nodded once, no argument, and she understood that the offer hadn't been doubt of her. It had been the only kindness he knew how to give: an exit, always an exit, kept open for everyone including himself.

* * *

Going in was like being swallowed slowly by something that had all the time in the world.

Suri went first, because it was her father's map and her father's door and she would be damned before she let anyone else go through it ahead of her. She shed her pack and pushed it through the gap ahead of her, then went in on her belly through a space barely wider than her shoulders, cold stone pressing her back and chest at once, breathing the smell of wet granite and old iron and something else beneath it, older, sweeter, wrong—a smell like a cellar, like a church, like the inside of a closed room that had been closed a very long time.

Behind her she could hear the others breathing, the scrape of Odile's elbows, Isandro's low voice counting—*breathe out before you push, exhale makes you thinner*—and the light of her lantern making a small trembling sun of the three feet of world immediately before her face.

Then the crawl ended, and the mountain opened, and she stood up in a dark so total that her lantern seemed to shrink to a match flame.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Just the word, falling out of her.

The bore was enormous. Even with the light barely reaching the walls, she could feel its size the way you feel the size of a cathedral—not seen but *understood*, in the pressure of the air and the way her voice, when she spoke, went up and out and didn't come back. The original tunnel, uncollapsed here, ran straight as a rifle barrel into the heart of the mountain, and the railroad had built it to grand specifications: brick lining arched overhead, drainage channels cut along both sides, iron stanchions every twenty feet where timbers had braced the roof. Water dripped somewhere far off with a regular mineral tick, tick, tick, like a clock counting something other than seconds.

"It's intact." Isandro's voice, hushed, reverberating. "Forty years and the arch is sound. They built this to last a century." A pause. "And then they blew it shut within five."

"Because of what it held," Suri said, "not what it blocked."

They walked. There was nothing else to do. The rails still ran down the center, brown with rust but true, and walking the ties felt like walking the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. Pell had the map open again despite the dark, reading her father's final annotations by lantern: distances, features, a last line of instruction that made Pell stop walking so abruptly that Odile nearly went into his back.

"What?" Odile demanded.

"He says—" Pell swallowed. "He says, *Count the stanchions. At forty-one, look right.*"

"Look right at what?"

"He doesn't say." Pell's laugh came out thin. "He never says. Forty years of following this man's handwriting and he never once tells you what you're going to find. Only where to stand when you find it."

So they counted. Suri counted aloud, because someone had to and because the numbers kept the silence from getting inside her: *one, two, three*, the lanterns swinging, their shadows wheeling hugely across the curved brick, water ticking, boots ringing on iron and stone. At thirty her mouth had gone dry. At thirty-five Odile had stopped making jokes, which frightened Suri more than anything the mountain had done yet.

At forty-one, they looked right.

There was a gallery off the main bore—a heading driven sideways into the rock, maybe fifty meters deep, the kind of auxiliary drive dug for ventilation or muck removal during construction. Its mouth had been walled up with rough stone and mortar, hastily, badly, by men who wanted it closed fast and didn't care who knew it. Someone had knocked a hole in that wall long ago—recently? No. The broken edges were rounded, weathered, dark with age. Her father's hole. Her father had come through here, alone, decades ago, and wall or no wall, he had come back out and carried what he found for the rest of his life.

The gap was wide enough to walk through upright.

Suri raised her lantern and stepped through, and the light went in ahead of her and came back changed.

* * *

Later—in the years after, when people asked her, and they always asked, at dinners and hearings and once, unbearably, at a wedding—she would try to describe it and fail, because the mind protects itself with metaphor and the truth refused all of hers. Not a battlefield. Not a catacomb. What the lantern showed her, in that first white instant, was *order*.

Bunks. Iron frame bunk beds, construction-gauge, bolted to the rock floor in rows, dozens of them, the kind of temporary barracks bedding a contractor throws up for a tunnel crew. Blankets on some of them still, rotted to lace, collapsed into themselves like shed skins. Boots beneath the bunks, pairs, set neatly side by side the way tired men set their boots. Tin plates stacked. A stove, cold, its pipe still rising to a vent shaft choked with rubble. Tools hung on pegs—picks, hammers, a drill steel rack—with the terrible domestic tidiness of a place whose occupants expected to come back.

And among the bunks, in the spaces between, in the alcoves and against the far wall where the gallery deepened beyond her light: men.

Not skeletons scattered, not bones strewn by violence. Men lying down. Lying in their bunks, some of them, curled on their sides as if asleep, blankets drawn up over the places where their bodies had simplified into bone and cloth. Others on the floor, arranged—*arranged*, someone had arranged them, hands folded or arms at sides—rows of them receding into the dark past the reach of any lantern, and the smell that had followed them from the portal was here multiplied, sweet and mineral and old, the smell of a room sealed against the world for forty years.

Nobody screamed. Suri remembered being proud of that later, in a hollow distant way. Nobody screamed. Odile swore once, very quietly, a word she probably didn't know she knew, and then took off her hat, which was somehow the loudest thing anyone did.

"They didn't run," Isandro said. His voice had a crack in it she'd never heard before. He had gone to the nearest bunk and was standing over it, lantern high, and his face in the light was the face of a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd helped write. "Look at them. They're in their bunks. If there'd been a fire or a flood or a collapse they'd have died running, at the face, at the portal. These men—" He stopped. Started again. "These men were put to bed."

"Or put *in* beds," Pell whispered.

Suri turned to look at him. He had drifted, without seeming to move, to the far wall, where a shelf of sorts had been cut into the rock, and on it lay papers. She crossed to him through the rows of sleepers, careful where she put her feet, though it no longer mattered, though it had never mattered, and looked over his shoulder.

Company paper. Company letterhead, faded to the color of weak tea. Timebooks—she knew the format, had grown up around her father's copies—shift logs, pay ledgers, a stack of them bound with wire that had rusted to orange dust. And on top, a single sheet larger than the rest, dense with columns of names.

"The night book," Pell said. His hands were shaking so hard the pages rattled. "This is the shift record for the last rotation. See the date—see it—" He turned it toward her and his finger, trembling, indicated a column headed *MEN BELOW AT TIME OF INCIDENT*, and below the heading, names. Names and names and names. Three hundred and eleven of them. Each with a number beside it, and each number matching, when she looked, a tally mark on a separate sheet, a count, an inventory, as if—

As if the men were the incident. As if the men were the cargo.

"There was no disaster," Suri heard herself say. The words came out of her like water finding a crack, unstoppable, cold. "Or—there was, but it wasn't an accident. Look at the dates. The sealing order is dated six days after this log. Six days. You don't survey charges and mobilize a demolition crew in six days unless the decision was made before—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "They gassed them. Or starved them. Or sealed the headings and let the air go bad, and then they buried the evidence with the bodies, and they wrote it down as a landslide, and they struck three hundred and eleven names off the rolls, and they blew the mountain shut on top of them."

"And one man walked out," Odile said from the darkness. Her voice was very steady, which meant it was costing her everything. "One man was outside when it happened, or got out, and spent forty years carrying the proof because nobody with power would listen to a prospector with a map."

"Not nobody." Pell was crouched now at the end of the shelf, lifting the ledger with both hands, gentler than Suri had ever seen him handle anything, gentler than he handled his own instruments. "Someone listened. Someone had to have paid for this expedition of ours—no. No, that's not—" He laughed, a broken sound. "I keep looking for the angle. Forty years in cartography, you assume every document has an angle. But there's no angle here. There's just a man who couldn't carry it alone anymore, and a stranger he trusted because the stranger signed his name to a bridge that fell, and—" He stopped, and Suri saw him understand it, saw the understanding land on him physically, a blow. "Mateo chose Isandro because Isandro knows what it is to survive something that killed other men. That's why him. That's why *him*."

The words went through the gallery and came back off the brick, off the bunks, off the sleeping dead, and Suri watched Isandro take them the way a man takes a bullet—standing, refusing to fall, one hand closing slowly on the iron frame of a bunk until his knuckles went white.

"We should go," Isandro said at last, and his voice was wrecked and level at once. "We document everything. Photographs, notes, the ledger comes with us. We mark the location and we get out before the monsoon closes the passes. Then—" He looked down the gallery, into the dark where the lantern light gave out and the bunks went on, row after row, into the mountain's keeping. "Then we make sure the whole world counts them. Every name. Every single one."

Suri knelt beside the nearest bunk. Up close she could see what the first glance had hidden: a tin cup on the floor, a clay pipe, a boot heel worn through at the outside edge by a man who walked heavily on his right foot. And tucked into the bedframe, wedged there deliberately, a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around something small.

She drew it out. Inside the oilcloth was a wooden tag, the kind pinned to company kit, and on it, in pencil gone silver with age, a name: *E. VASQUEZ, DRILLER NO. 2*. And beneath the name, in different handwriting—younger, hurried, pressed hard—four words that her father must have written forty years ago, standing in this dark, alone, promising a dead man what he would spend his life trying to do:

*I will tell them.*

Suri closed her hand around the tag until the wood bit her palm, and above her, far off through the mountain, came a sound none of them had heard yet and all of them would remember: a low, rolling groan of stone settling deep in the bore, like the mountain clearing its throat—or like something, after forty years, finally beginning to wake.
