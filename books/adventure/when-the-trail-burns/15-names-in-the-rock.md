## 15. Names in the Rock

Pell Wexler had spent his professional life believing that paper was the most honest thing in the world.

Charts lied, of course—people made them lie—but paper itself was innocent. It held what it was given. Ink did not flatter. A contour line drawn in a trembling hand told you about the mountain and about the hand. He had built his career on that faith, and he had destroyed it with a single falsified sheet, and now, kneeling on the floor of a dynamited tomb two thousand meters above the valley fog, he felt the old faith return to him like blood into a numb limb: because here was paper, real paper, hundreds of sheets of it, and every one of them was telling the truth so loudly it hurt his teeth.

"Careful," Suri said behind him. Not a warning—an echo. She had said *careful* when they'd entered the bore an hour ago, and again at the first collapse barrier, and she said it now as Pell lifted the first ledger from its oilcloth wrapping, and he understood that she wasn't talking to him. She was talking to her father. To forty years of her father's care, which she was only now learning to see.

The chamber opened off the main tunnel like a lung. The company had not simply buried the disaster; they had buried it *neatly*, which was somehow worse. The great bore ran straight into the mountain for four hundred meters before the charges brought down the roof, and where the rockfall ended, the crews of 1911—or whoever they had been, the men whose names were not on any roll—had been left where they fell. Or been placed there. Pell could not decide, and the not-deciding sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Lantern light moved across the space in slow arcs. Isandro held the lamp high, and Odile stood at the edge of the fall zone with her arms folded, saying nothing, which from Odile was a kind of prayer. The grave goods were arranged along the walls in niches the survivors must have cut deliberately: tools laid parallel, boots paired, a tin cup set upright on a shelf of stone as if its owner might come back thirsty. And among them, wrapped against damp in waxed cloth and canvas and one remarkable envelope of oiled silk, the records.

Pell drew the first ledger into the lantern light and opened it, and the past exhaled dust into his face, and he breathed it in like incense.

*Roll of Employment — Eastern Extension, Contract 7.*

Not the company's name. Not the railroad's name. A contractor's name, a shell, the kind of arrangement Pell knew intimately because he had spent years studying how institutions laundered their own histories. He turned pages with fingers he kept deliberately steady. Names. Trades. Villages. Dates of hire. And then, beginning at a page marked by a pressed sprig of some alpine flower gone brown as rust, dates of death. Dozens. Then scores. The handwriting changed partway through—a second clerk, or the first clerk's hand failing—and the entries stopped being neat. The last twenty names were written in a different ink entirely, cramped against the margin, added by someone working in the dark or close to it.

"Isandro." His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "Come look at this."

Isandro came. The engineer crouched beside him, and Pell watched him read the way Isandro read everything—fast, structural, looking for load paths—and then watched the structure fail him. The man went very still.

"These aren't company rolls," Isandro said.

"No."

"I've seen the company rolls for this section. I helped file the closure reports." His jaw worked. "There were eleven fatalities listed for the whole eastern extension. Eleven. Landslide, three; tunnel fire, five; miscellaneous, three. I signed the summary page."

"You signed a lie," Odile said from the fall line. No accusation in it. Just the flatness of a woman confirming a measurement.

"I signed a lie," Isandro agreed, and the words seemed to cost him something physical, and Pell looked away to give him the privacy of it.

Suri knelt on the other side of the ledger. She had Mateo's map out—not using it, just holding it, the way you hold a hand. "Read me a name," she said quietly. "Any name."

Pell read: "*Anselmo Vaca, powder-man, from Corriente del Sur. Engaged March fourth, nineteen-oh-nine.*"

Suri closed her eyes. When she opened them, she pulled the map flat between them and turned it toward the lantern, to the margin notes—the ones she had been reading aloud around fires for three nights now, the ones meant for her. Her finger found a line of small, fierce script.

She read it aloud. "*A.V. taught me which seams hold a charge and which seams lie. He is owed a debt no bank can hold. If this map reaches kind hands, tell his people in Corriente del Sur that he died on a Tuesday, and that the mountain did not take him—it was taken from him.*"

Nobody spoke. Somewhere deeper in the sealed dark, water ticked through stone, patient as a clock.

"That's why," Suri said at last, and her voice cracked in the middle and she let it. "That's why he carried this for forty years. It's not a treasure map. It's not even a map. It's—" She pressed her palm flat over the margin. "It's a witness. He was the witness. The map was just how a witness survives long enough to speak."

Pell felt something rearrange itself in his chest, some load-bearing assumption shifting onto new foundations. He had thought they were delivering a document. Documents could be lost, stolen, burned. But testimony—that was different. Testimony lived in the carrying. You couldn't steal a promise out of a man's spine.

"We need copies," he said. "All of it. Everything that will survive copying."

* * *

They worked by lantern light while the mountain dripped around them.

Pell directed it the way he had once directed drafting rooms, back when his hands were trusted: Odile hauled crates into position and broke the seals with a pry bar, gentle as a surgeon; Isandro rigged three lamps into a single flood of light using wire, a mirror, and a length of surveyor's staff, muttering geometry under his breath like a man praying in his mother tongue; Suri unwrapped each bundle and handed it across with both hands, the way you hand someone a child.

Pell photographed.

The camera was his own—of course it was, Pell owned cameras the way other men owned regrets—and it was a good one, a folding plate camera with a bellows like a black accordion, bought secondhand in a port city during the worst year of his disgrace. He had carried it on every job since without quite admitting why. Now he knew. Some part of him had been waiting for a room like this one: a room where the truth had been stored against damp and time by a man who understood exactly what institutions did to inconvenient paper, and who had therefore wrapped it in silk.

He worked methodically. Ledger pages, one exposure each, the fiddler's crank of the shutter release sounding obscene in the silence. Grave goods in situ before anything was touched—boots, cups, the small cloth bundles that turned out, when Suri opened one with shaking hands, to hold letters. Unsent letters. Home letters, written by dying men or by men who did not yet know they were dying, addressed to villages up and down the valleys the line had been built to serve.

One letter was simply a list of debts, with instructions for repayment from wages never paid. One was a marriage proposal, dated nine days after the man's recorded death. One was from a boy who called himself sixteen and was probably fourteen, asking his mother to tell his father the work was good and the pay was coming, and Pell had to set the camera down for a full minute before he could photograph that one, and when he lifted it again his eyes were burning and nobody commented, because Odile was blowing her nose with tremendous dignity and Isandro had turned to face the rock wall.

"How many?" Odile asked finally.

Pell counted the ledgers, cross-checked against the loose sheets, did the arithmetic twice. "Four hundred and six names in the employment rolls. Three hundred and eighty-one marked deceased. Twenty-five unaccounted—which means either they walked out, or somebody else's list has them." He paused. "And the rolls themselves are incomplete. There are references to a first register, a 'List A,' that isn't here. Mateo may have split the collection. Or List A may be somewhere else in this mountain."

"Or somewhere else entirely," Isandro said grimly. "Which means we're not carrying a secret. We're carrying half of one."

"Then we carry it louder," Odile said.

They worked until the plates ran low. Pell shot the last frame on the final ledger page—the cramped marginal names in the wrong ink, the clerk writing past the edge of the ruled lines as if the dead were overflowing the form designed to contain them—and then he sat back on his heels amid the smell of plate chemicals and wet stone and forty years of careful oilcloth, and he understood, with the clarity that only comes at the bottom of things, what his own life had been rehearsing him for.

He had falsified one chart. One river crossing, one contour nudged, one bridge founded on numbers he had invented because the real numbers were inconvenient and the deadline was real and he had told himself the margin of safety would cover it. The bridge had stood. Nobody had died. But the inspection that followed had ended three careers—his chief's, his mentor's, his own—and he had spent eleven years telling himself the punishment outweighed the crime, that he was the victim of an unforgiving profession, that a man who erred once should be allowed to err in peace.

Kneeling here, he finally saw it plainly: he had never been punished for the falsification. He had been punished for being caught. Four hundred and six men had been erased by professionals who were never caught at all—who had been promoted, decorated, pensioned—and somewhere above this mountain those men's grandchildren still set places at tables for them, still said their names to children who assumed the missing great-uncle had simply drifted away, because that was what the paper said, and paper was honest, and the paper said these men had never existed.

"Mateo knew," Pell said aloud. He hadn't meant to say it. "He knew what happens to records. That's why he didn't go to the company. Why he didn't go to the regulators, the newspapers, anyone with a filing cabinet. Any institution would have absorbed this the way a river absorbs a stone. So he became the filing cabinet. Forty years. He walked this country with a witness in his shirt pocket and waited for someone he could trust to help him testify." He looked up at Suri. "He wasn't hiding it from the world. He was hiding the world from it. Until he found hands that wouldn't let go."

Suri was crying without noise, the way she did everything without noise, and she reached over and gripped Pell's forearm hard enough to bruise, and he let her, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the room hadn't already said better.

* * *

They rested before the descent—no one suggested moving immediately; the mountain had a gravity of its own and none of them pretended they could leave quickly. They ate cold food in the lantern light, sitting in a row along the wall of the chamber like mourners at a wake, which, Pell thought, was precisely what they were.

Odile broke the quiet first. "The families," she said. "Corriente del Sur. How many villages does that make?"

Pell had already tabulated it, because tabulating was how he loved things. "The rolls list home villages for three hundred and twelve of the dead. Sixty-one distinct settlements. Most within a week's walk of the old line."

"Then the memorial train has sixty-one stations to stop at," Odile said, "or sixty-one letters to send, and I'll write every one myself if it comes to that."

Isandro said nothing, but Pell watched him take out his notebook—the chainage book, the little volume where the man recorded his life in miles and markers—and watched him open it to a fresh page and begin, slowly, to copy out names. Not distances. Names. Anselmo Vaca. The boy who called himself sixteen. All of them, in an engineer's precise hand, as if he were setting grade stakes for the dead, establishing their elevation, fixing their position on the earth so they could never again be moved.

Suri rewrapped the originals with a care that made Pell's throat ache. Silk first, then waxed cloth, then canvas, each fold crisp, each tie snug. At the last bundle she paused, took out her father's map, and studied the margins one more time by lantern light.

"There's one more note," she said. "I thought it was about the route out. Listen." She read: "*When you have seen, do not run. Running is what they expect. Walk out slow, in daylight, like men with nothing to hide. And watch the portal—the last thing a witness needs is an audience.*"

Pell felt the hair rise on his neck. Beside him, Odile was already on her feet, hand going to the pry bar, and Isandro had pinched out the lowest lantern, plunging the chamber's mouth into shadow.

Because the note was right. Of course it was right. Mateo Reyes had spent forty years learning how institutions hunted witnesses, and he had left his daughter one final instruction, and it was not about grief at all.

It was about the door.

From far up the tunnel—back the way they had come, from the direction of the collapsed portal and the gray daylight beyond it—came a sound that did not belong to dripping water or settling stone.

Boots. Many of them, moving carefully, closing the distance.

Pell began packing the plates.
