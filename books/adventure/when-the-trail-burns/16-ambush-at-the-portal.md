## 16. Ambush at the Portal

Odile Brant had spent twenty-two years on the rails, and in all that time she had learned one truth above every other: you could tell what a crew was made of by how they came out of the dark.

She stood at the tunnel portal now, in the last grey light before dawn, and watched them emerge one by one into the thin mountain air. Pell first, hunched under the weight of the satchel, his spectacles fogged, his hands shaking so badly he had to steady them against the rock. Suri behind him, lantern in one hand, her father's map rolled and oilcloth-wrapped against her chest like a child. And last, Isandro, walking backward, watching the black mouth of the bore as if he expected the mountain itself to come after them.

They came out of the dark changed. She could see it. Whatever they had found down there—whatever the lantern light had shown them in that long silence before Suri's voice broke—it had gone into them like water into cracked timber. They moved differently. Slower. Heavier.

Odile understood. She had been carrying her own knowledge of it since the depot, since she'd caught Pell burning a page and made him show her the rest. The line hadn't been closed for landslides. The company had buried men in the mountain and buried the mountain's mouth after them, and for forty years the only witness had been a prospector with a map and a conscience.

Now the witness was dead, and the testimony was a satchel of photographs and a roll of oilcloth, and it was her job to get it down the mountain.

"Water first," she said, when they were all clear. "Half a canteen each, no more. We've got maybe three hours of dark left, and I want the switchbacks behind us before the sun finds this face of the ridge."

Suri was looking back at the portal. The dynamited masonry had been broken open at the crown—Mateo's annotations had known exactly where the old construction adit lay beneath the plug—and behind them the tunnel exhaled a slow breath of cold air that smelled of stone and old timber and something else, something sweeter and worse that Odile did not want to name. Limestone dust, maybe. Or forty years of silence.

"Close it," Suri said. Her voice was scraped raw. "We should close it behind us."

"With what?" Pell asked. "We don't have dynamite, and I'm not going back in there to stack rocks."

"I know. I know that." Suri pressed her palm flat against the broken masonry, the way a person touches a grave marker. "It just feels wrong to leave it open. Like they're exposed."

"They've been exposed for forty years," Isandro said quietly. "The company just made sure no one was looking. That's over now. That's what we're carrying."

Odile let them have the moment—ten seconds, no more—and then she clapped her hands once, sharp, the sound cracking off the rock.

"Save it for the valley. Move."

* * *

The pass above the portal was a throat of broken granite, the old construction road cut into the cliffside in a series of switchbacks that the company had let the mountain begin reclaiming. Odile took point because she always took point, because someone had to count the steps and read the ground and know, in her bones, how much distance a tired crew could cover before the light and the luck ran out together.

She counted. She always counted.

Two hundred steps to the first switchback. The rock under her boots still cold from the night, slick with the breath of the coming monsoon. The air had that taste to it, that green metallic taste she'd learned to dread in thirty years of mountain work—the taste of a sky holding more water than it could carry.

Three hundred and ten steps. Pell's breathing was bad behind her. He'd been coughing since the tunnel, some dust or spore or simple terror lodged in his chest.

Four hundred steps. She stopped.

Not because she heard anything. Because she stopped hearing anything.

The mountain at night was never silent. It ticked and settled and dripped; somewhere below, the river they'd crossed at the viaduct was running high and loud; there was always wind in the scree, always the small industrious sounds of things with more legs than sense going about their business. A mountain at night was an orchestra pit between movements, and Odile Brant had spent enough nights on it to know when the players had gone quiet because someone had walked onto the stage.

"Down," she breathed, and dropped to a crouch behind a shelf of granite the size of a coffin lid.

Behind her, to their credit, they obeyed without question. Pell folded like a dropped coat. Suri went flat against the cut face of the cliff. Isandro—Isandro moved like water, no wasted motion, and Odile noted it even now, the way three years of hiding had taught the man to vanish.

They waited. One minute. Two.

Then she heard it. Not voices. Boots. Many boots, moving with the particular discipline of men who had been told to move quietly and were being paid enough to do it well. Below them. Between them and the valley.

And above—she turned her head, slow, careful—a single lantern glow bobbing on the switchback above theirs, where no lantern had any business being at this hour, on a road the company had officially abandoned before most of these men were born.

"Vasseur," Suri whispered. It wasn't a question.

"He's been herding us," Odile said. The words came out flat and cold, and she was grateful for that, because underneath the flatness something was howling. "Since the hermit's cabin, maybe before. He let us do the hard part. Let us break into the mountain and carry the proof out. Now he just has to close his hand."

"Can we go around?" Pell whispered. His voice had a wheeze in it.

"Below's cut off. Above—" She listened again. The lantern above was stationary now. Waiting. "Above is the door of the trap."

Isandro crawled up beside her, close enough that she could see his face in the pre-dawn grey, and what she saw there was not fear. It was arithmetic. She had seen that look on engineers before, in flood season, when the water was rising faster than the sandbags could be stacked. The look of a man who has accepted that he cannot save everything and is deciding, with cold clarity, what he can save.

"There's a cut in the road," he said. "Forty meters down. Where the cliff face collapsed and they blasted a detour through the shoulder. The rubble makes a natural chokepoint—one man can hold it against ten."

"I know the cut," Odile said.

"Then you know what I'm about to say."

She did. She had known it since she heard the boots.

"The satchel goes down the mountain," Isandro said. "You hold the cut. We go over the shoulder—there's a goat track, I saw it on the way up, it drops into the ravine west of the switchbacks. Rough, but they won't expect it, and in the dark they can't cover it."

"And you three go down a goat track in the dark with a satchel of evidence, a dying map, and one cartographer who can't breathe." Odile looked at each of them in turn. Pell, grey-faced, clutching the satchel like a man clutching a life vest. Suri, jaw set, the map against her chest. Isandro, calm, terrible, calm. "And who leads them? Who knows this mountain?"

"You do," Suri said. "That's the point."

"No," Odile said. "That's the problem."

Because here was the thing Odile Brant had never said out loud, not in twenty-two years of rails and roadbeds and crews that came and went like weather: she had spent those twenty-two years being useful so that she would never have to be loved. A foreman was a tool. A tool got used and stored and replaced, and a tool never had to lie awake wondering if anyone would notice the empty bunk. She had built her whole life into a chokepoint, one person wide, and she had told herself it was strength.

Now the mountain was asking her to be what she had built herself to be. And she found, to her astonishment, that she didn't want to be it anymore.

"Listen to me," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "The cut is the only ground in this whole pass where one person can stop ten. If I don't hold it, they come down on you in the open, and everything Mateo carried for forty years goes into a company furnace, and those men in that tunnel stay buried twice. So it's not a discussion. It's arithmetic."

"Odile—" Isandro started.

"Isandro Zhou." She said his full name the way she'd say a chainage marker, to fix him in place. "Three years you spent making yourself invisible. These last three weeks, you stopped. You led us down a flooded viaduct and up a cliff in a storm and into a mountain that the company killed men to keep shut. You don't get to go back to invisible. Not after this. So you take them down the goat track, and you get that satchel to the valley, and you get those names into print if it's the last thing you do."

The sky was going from black to bruise at the eastern edge of the ridge. Time was a budget running dry.

"Pell." She turned to the cartographer. "The satchel. Give it here a moment."

He passed it over, fumbling. She opened it, checked the photographs—hundreds of names in faded ink, rows of them, men erased from the rolls—and took out one page. Just one. She folded it small and put it inside her jacket, over her heart.

"If I don't come out," she said, "this one name gets printed anyway. One is enough to start. One name with a family waiting is a crack, and cracks spread. You taught me that, Wexler."

Pell's eyes were wet behind the fogged spectacles. "Odile, there has to be—"

"There doesn't. There never does. That's the whole dirty secret of the work. There's never a version where everyone walks away clean." She closed the satchel and pushed it back against his chest. "Go."

* * *

The cut was exactly as Isandro had described: a wound in the mountain's shoulder where the old road had collapsed and the company had blasted a detour, leaving a channel of tumbled granite perhaps three meters wide between a wall of cliff and a drop into blackness. Odile reached it as the first light came up the valley, grey and sourceless, and she took stock the way she took stock of any piece of ground: what it gave her, what it cost anyone who wanted it.

It gave her loose rock on the high side, good for throwing or rolling. It gave her a single approach, up the switchback, that any attacker would have to take in single file, bent double, hands on rock. It cost her everything else. There was no second position. There was no retreat. If they came in numbers, or came patient, she would hold until she couldn't, and then she would be a foreman at the bottom of a ravine with one name folded over her heart.

She found a seat in the rocks—a slab angled so she could brace her back and see the whole approach—and she waited.

The sun came up. It did not warm her. The monsoon light was the color of pewter, flat and heavy, and the clouds had come down to sit on the ridgeline like a lid on a pot. Somewhere below, the river was louder than it had been yesterday. She could hear it even up here, a low continuous roar, the sound of the mountain shedding the season's first real water.

The boots came at mid-morning.

They came carefully, which told her they'd been told about her, or had guessed. Four men first, spread across the switchback below, moving between cover with a competence that made her revise her estimate of Vasseur upward. Not rent-a-guards. Professionals.

"Miss Brant." The voice came from below, unhurried, a voice used to being obeyed in boardrooms. "Mr. Vasseur wonders if we might resolve this without anyone being hurt."

"You can tell Mr. Vasseur," Odile called back, pitching her voice to carry, "that he's welcome to come up and resolve it himself. Or is he the kind of man who sends other people's sons up the hill?"

A pause. A soft laugh from somewhere in the rocks.

"He respects you," the voice said. "He does. He says a woman who holds a pass alone for strangers is either a fool or a professional, and he's hoping very much you're the latter."

"Both," Odile said. "Now get off my mountain."

What followed was not a battle. Battles were for armies. What followed was two hours of the ugliest, most patient work she had ever done: men testing her with thrown stones to draw her fire, men flanking along the cliff face while she rolled granite down on them, men talking, always talking, offering money, offering safety, offering, in one voice that sounded almost genuinely kind, the observation that no one was coming to help her and the mountain did not care how brave she was.

She made them pay for every meter. She rolled the high scree until her palms were bloody. She fired the signal flare she'd been saving for emergencies straight into the chest of the first man to reach the cut, and the scream he made would stay with her, and she found she could live with that, because the alternative was the names in the satchel going into a furnace.

But they were four, and then six, and then the light was going flat and pewter-dark as the storm front came down off the ridge, and her arms were lead, and the cut was only three meters wide, and she had known from the first hour that arithmetic was not on her side. Arithmetic was never on one person's side. It was only ever on the side of what you were willing to lose.

They took her in the end from above—rappelling down the cliff face on ropes, which she should have guessed, because Vasseur struck her as a man who read ground the way she did. A boot in the small of her back, a knee across her arms, the taste of granite dust and blood. She fought until there was nothing left to fight with, and then a little longer, because that was who she was.

They bound her wrists with the same kind of cord the company used to bundle survey stakes. She noticed that. She noticed everything. It was how she'd stayed alive this long.

The polished man himself came up the switchback as the first rain began to fall—fat, warm, enormous drops, the monsoon's opening argument. He crouched in front of her, immaculate even now, his shoes ruined and his face unreadable.

"The satchel, Miss Brant. Where is it?"

"Gone down the mountain," Odile said. She spat blood to the side, carefully, not on his shoes. She was a professional. "With three people who owe me their lives. You should think about what that means."

"It means you've cost me a great deal of money." He studied her the way he'd study a failing investment. "You understand I can't simply let you go."

"I'd be disappointed if you did."

He sighed, and gestured, and two men took her arms. "The map, at least. The original. Tell me where the old man's daughter is taking it, and this becomes a story you tell over drinks for the rest of a long life."

Odile looked at him. Rain was running down her face, mixing with the blood, and she thought about Mateo Reyes, forty years in the mountains with a folded paper and a promise. She thought about a girl who had hated her father for choosing maps, and a cartographer who had falsified one chart and spent a decade paying for it, and an engineer who had signed a survey that failed and then tried to disappear. Strays. All of them strays, and somehow, against every law of her long careful life, hers.

"You're asking the wrong question," she said.

"Am I?"

"You're asking where they're taking it." She smiled, and her split lip made it a terrible smile, and she gave it to him anyway, the whole thing, because some truths were weapons and she had just decided this was one of hers. "You should be asking what they're going to do with it."

Something flickered in Vasseur's polished face—the first crack she'd seen there, the first hairline fracture in all that expensive composure. He heard it. She watched him hear it. The difference between a map that could be bought and a testimony that could only be survived.

He straightened. The rain was coming down hard now, drumming on the granite, filling the cut with the smell of wet stone and the roar of the river below rising to meet it.

"Take her down," he said. "Carefully. She's worth more to me breathing."

As they marched her down the switchback, Odile Brant did the thing she had done every working day of her life. She counted. Steps, switchbacks, the men around her, the hours of light left, the hours of the storm's grace period, the distance to the valley railhead and the speed of a crew of strays moving over a goat track in the dark with a satchel full of the dead.

She counted, because counting was how a foreman held a mountain in her head.

And by her count—by her count, if Isandro Zhou was half the engineer she'd decided he was, they had a six-hour head start, a storm at their backs to drown pursuit, and a river rising to do their fighting for them.

She smiled again, into the rain, where no one could see it.

Hold the line, she thought. I bought you the morning. Spend it well.
