## 17. The Descent

The mountain told us it was going to kill us before we ever took the first step down.

I heard it in the way the wind had changed its mind—no longer the steady push of a storm approaching but something ragged, stuttering, like breath before a scream. I smelled it in the air, that green metallic tang of water still hours away but already claiming the sky. And I saw it in the clouds stacking themselves against the western ridgeline, gray going to black going to a color I had no name for, a bruise the size of a country.

Four days, the weather signs had said at dawn. Now I gave us two. Maybe less.

"Isandro." Suri stood at the lip of the pass with her father's map folded into her jacket, her hair whipping loose from its braid. Behind her, through the tunnel mouth we'd just fled, the dark held everything we'd found—the grave, the names, the four hundred men the railroad had erased from its ledgers and its memory both. Pell clutched the satchel of photographs and records against his chest like an infant. "Which way?"

There was no which way. There was only down.

"The old haul road," I said. "It follows the contour for two kilometers, then switchbacks through the scree field to the river gorge. From there—" I stopped myself. From there was a question I couldn't answer yet, because from there depended on water, and water was about to become the only authority on this mountain.

"And Vasseur's men?" Pell's voice cracked on the name.

"Behind us. An hour, maybe two. They'll move slower in the dark." I looked at the sky again. "But so will we."

Odile wasn't with us. Odile was back at the portal with a rifle and a rockfall she'd rigged herself, buying us time with her body if it came to that, and every second I spent not moving was a second that made her sacrifice worth less. I turned my back on the tunnel, on the dead, on the terrible light of what we knew now, and I pointed my compass downhill.

"We go fast," I said. "We go smart. And nobody stops until the storm makes them."

* * *

The haul road was a memory of itself—two ruts gone soft with forty years of moss, cut into the mountainside by men who'd believed the line would live forever. We moved along it at the pace exhaustion allows, which is to say faster than sense and slower than fear. Pell stumbled twice. The second time I caught his arm and felt the tremor running through him like current through wire.

"How far?" he kept asking. Not how far down. How far ahead. He meant the pursuers, the flood, the end of everything. He meant: tell me there is an end.

"Far enough," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it, and took it anyway, because sometimes a lie is load-bearing too.

The first trouble came where the road crossed a gully—a dry ravine most of the year, a chute for snowmelt in spring, and tonight, with the storm's leading edge already dumping rain somewhere above us, a place where the mountain would soon be shedding its skin. I heard it before I saw it: a low grinding hush under the wind, like the mountain clearing its throat.

I put out my arm and stopped them both.

The gully ran brown. Not flowing—*arriving*. A tongue of water thick as syrup, carrying gravel and pine limbs and half a rotted tie, pushing down the chute with the slow obscene confidence of something that has all night and all the weight of the sky behind it.

"That's not a crossing," Pell said flatly.

"No," I agreed. "That's a conveyor belt to the bottom of the gorge."

Suri crouched at the edge, watching the water with her father's eyes—I was learning to see Mateo Reyes in her more every hour, in the way she read terrain like handwriting. "Above," she said suddenly. "Look. The old flume brackets."

She was right. Rusted iron stanchions marched up the gully wall, the bones of a timber flume that had once carried water to some long-dead camp. The stanchions were spaced close enough to cross on—if they held, if the footing existed between them, if rust was only rust and not rot wearing rust's clothes.

Every reason to say no. Every hour pressing me to say yes.

I tested the first stanchion with my boot, then my full weight, then hung from it with both hands while the others watched. It groaned but held. The second was worse—one flange sheared clean off—and I made the call without letting myself think about bridges, about cables singing their last note, about the sound a structure makes when it decides to stop being a structure.

"Single file. Three points of contact. Pell, you give me the satchel first—it crosses lighter than you do." I slung the records across my back, cinched them tight, and went first, because that was the whole point of me now. Because three years ago I had stood on a bridge and frozen while it died under other men, and I had promised the dark inside myself that I would never freeze again, and promises made to darkness are the ones you have to keep.

The crossing took eleven minutes. I counted them in heartbeats, in handholds, in the water rising a finger-width at a time below our feet. When Suri dropped onto solid ground beside me, mud to her knees, she didn't celebrate. She just looked at me once—quick, assessing, the way Odile looked at a span under load—and nodded.

We went on.

* * *

The scree field nearly ended us.

By then the rain had found us—not the monsoon proper but its outriders, fat cold drops that came faster and faster until the dark was full of their hissing. The switchbacks were gone; forty years of freeze and thaw had chewed the trail into a suggestion. We descended by headlamp beams bouncing off wet stone, hands on rock, boots finding purchase by faith.

And then Pell fell.

Not far—four meters, five—but onto a slope that wanted to make it farther. He slid, scrabbled, caught a root that bent but didn't break, and hung there making a sound I recognized from three years ago, from the bottom of a gorge, from my own throat: the small animal noise of a man who believes the world has finished with him.

I didn't think. That was the difference. Three years ago I had thought, and thinking had been the trap—every calculation a door slamming shut, every option weighed until the weighing itself became paralysis. Now I simply moved. Down the slope on my belly, distributing my weight the way you distribute load on unconsolidated fill, talking the whole time because a voice gives a falling man something to hold besides rock.

"Pell. Look at me. Not down. Me. Give me your wrist. Your *wrist*, not your hand—hands slip, wrists don't."

He gave me his wrist. I braced against a boulder the size of a coffin, set my heels into the slope, and pulled, and the physics of it were simple and ancient and mine: one body anchoring another, force against friction, the oldest engineering there is.

When he was up, shaking, pressed against me in the rain, he said, "You've done that before."

"Once," I said. "Wrong."

He understood. They all did, eventually—that the bridge collapse hadn't been a story I told but a wound I carried, that the man who froze and the man pulling him up the mountain were the same man separated by three years and a dying prospector's map. He didn't say anything else. Neither did I. The mountain said enough for both of us.

* * *

We hit the gorge at what my watch called midnight and my body called the end of the world.

The river had doubled. Then doubled again. What had been a brown ribbon when we came up was now a brown *country*—water shouldering through the narrows with debris riding its back, whole trees cartwheeling, the roar of it so total it erased thought. Our exit route, the low ford where the haul road met the valley track, was gone. Not dangerous. Gone. Under four meters of flood.

Suri stood on the bank with rain streaming off her chin, and I watched her do the arithmetic and arrive at zero, and I watched her refuse to accept it.

"There has to be a way," she shouted over the water.

"There's always a way. That's not optimism, that's physics." I swept my light along the gorge walls, reading them the way Pell read documents, the way Suri read her father's margins. A railway engineer doesn't see a river. He sees gradient, volume, velocity, scour. He sees what the water is doing and therefore what it will do next. "There. Do you see it? Upstream, where the cliff overhangs—the water's cutting the outside of the bend. Which means the inside of the bend is deposition. Gravel bar. Slow water."

"It's still fifty meters of river," Pell said.

"It's fifteen meters of river and thirty-five meters of gravel bar that floods last and drains first." I was already moving upstream, testing the bank, and the strange thing—the thing I would remember later, in the quiet, in the aftermath—was how calm I was. Not numb, the way I had been numb for three years. Calm the way a bridge is calm. All the forces accounted for. Nothing wasted.

The gravel bar was exactly where the hydraulics said it would be, a pale shoulder of stone rising shin-deep above the flood, and beyond it the channel narrowed around a fallen spruce whose rootball had jammed against the far bank, forming a rough weir. Water poured through the gap in the trunk with a sound like tearing cloth, but the trunk itself—black, massive, wedged by its own branches—was the most honest structure on the river. It had been holding for years. It would hold for ten more minutes.

"Across the log," I said. "Straddle it. Move on my count, between surges. The water rises in pulses—you can hear them coming. Listen for the deepening of the roar, wait for the crest to pass, then move."

They listened. God help me, they listened—Suri first, because she'd have refused to go second, straddling that black timber with the flood trying to pluck her off like a burr, Pell after her with his eyes shut and his arms locked, and me last, counting pulses, feeling each surge come up the log through my palms like a pulse through a wrist.

Halfway across, the tree shifted.

A log the size of a church pillar, grinding a hand's width deeper into its jam, and for one lurching moment the whole crossing became a living thing deciding whether to let go. Suri, ahead of me, froze. I saw her freeze—saw the exact shape of it, the animal lock of a body that has understood it might die—and I reached past her and put my hand flat on the trunk in front of her face.

"Feel that?" I shouted. "It's settling, not failing. It's finding its seat. Ten seconds and it'll be steadier than it's been all night." I didn't know that was true. But I knew that a frozen climber doesn't need truth, she needs a floor, and I could be the floor. "Count with me. Out loud. One—"

She counted. Her voice shook and then it didn't. On seven the tree settled with a groan and went still, and on eight she moved, and on nine she was on the far bank hauling Pell out of the spray, and on ten I came off the log onto gravel with the flood licking my boots and my heart going like a trip-hammer and something in my chest that I hadn't felt in three years.

It took me a moment to name it. It wasn't relief. It was *usefulness*. The clean, burning joy of a thing done well under pressure, done for people who needed it done. I had built my exile out of the absence of that feeling, and here it was, arriving like the monsoon, impossible to hold back.

"Isandro." Suri gripped my sleeve, her face lit wild in the headlamp beam. "The map. My father marked something on the valley track—here, look—" She had the map out despite the rain, sheltering it under her jacket, her finger stabbing a notation in faded pencil near the river confluence. A symbol I'd taken for a survey mark when I first studied it, weeks ago, in another life.

Now, with the flood roaring proof of everything Mateo Reyes had known about this country, I understood it differently.

"It's not a benchmark," I said slowly. "It's a culvert. A big one. Big enough that he drew it separate from the line." My mind was running ahead of my mouth now, laying out the terrain from memory—the valley track climbing away from the river on a bench of dry ground, and beneath it, if Mateo's pencil was honest, a masonry culvert where a side creek passed under the railbed. A tunnel. Small, wet, ugly. And above the flood line.

"He left us a door," Suri said, and her voice broke on it, grief and gratitude in the same breath. "Even now. Even dead, he's still getting us home."

* * *

We found the culvert twenty minutes later, and it was everything his pencil had promised: a barrel vault of cut granite, tall enough to walk, running forty feet under the embankment, its upstream mouth choked with forty years of debris but its throat clear. Through it we could hear the creek in spate, but the masonry was old company work, built by men who'd expected to be building forever, and it shrugged off the flood like a coat.

We didn't stop. Stopping was death dressed as mercy. But for ninety seconds, huddled in the dripping dark of that stone throat while the storm hammered the mountain above us, we breathed. Pell laughed—a thin, unhinged, wonderful sound. Suri pressed her palm flat against the granite, against her father's door, and said nothing at all.

Then my headlamp caught movement downstream, on the far bank of the flooded river. Lights. Three of them, maybe four, bobbing along the opposite bank where Vasseur's men had followed our trail to the water's edge and stopped, because the water had won that argument for them.

"They can't cross," Pell whispered.

"Not tonight," I said. "Tonight the river's on our side."

But even as I said it, I was reading the flood again—reading its crest, its timing, the rhythm of a drainage basin I knew from survey maps—and I didn't like the numbers. This was the leading edge. The true body of the monsoon was still overhead, still gathering, and when it landed, this river wouldn't rise. It would *jump*. Whatever bought us the night would sell us out by morning, and Vasseur's men knew these valleys better than we did, and somewhere behind us on the mountain Odile was alone with a rifle and a promise.

Suri followed my gaze downstream, past the lights, toward the black seam of the valley where the railhead lay—where the last train out before landfall would leave at dawn, if it left at all.

"Then we don't sleep," she said. "We walk."

I tightened the straps on Pell's satchel of names, checked my watch against the pulse of the storm, and led them out of the culvert and into the drowning dark—downstream, toward the railhead, toward Odile, toward whatever the morning intended to make of us.

Behind us, faint under the rain, I thought I heard a whistle. Long. Short. Long.

The signal for a train making an unscheduled run.

Three hours early.
