## 18. Trade for Odile

Suri had learned her father's maps the way other children learned lullabies—not by choice, and not with love, but by repetition, until the lines of them ran beneath her skin like veins. She had hated that inheritance for eleven years. Now, crouched in the dripping dark beneath the aqueduct ruins at Kilometer 31, she spread a sheet of tracing paper over a lantern's yellow circle and understood, finally, what all those years at the kitchen table had been for.

"We need it to look *used*," she said. "Not drawn. *Used.*"

Pell Wexler knelt across from her, his ruined hands—ruined by a decade of penance and cheap gin, though the gin had stopped and the penance hadn't—hovering over the paper. Rain came through the broken arches above them in fine gray threads. Somewhere down the valley, the river was already talking to itself in a voice that meant business.

"Used," Pell repeated. He turned his head and spat, a surveyor's habit, checking the wind that wasn't there. "You want me to fake forty years of a dead man's hands."

"I want you to fake a dead man's *obsession*. There's a difference." Suri tapped the original, weighted at the corners with stones. Mateo Reyes's map, drawn on the back of a company survey sheet so old the letterhead had gone the color of weak tea. Every fold was a scar. Every margin held her father's cramped, patient handwriting—elevations, water levels, the names of dead men written small as prayers. "Vasseur has never seen the original. That's our only advantage, and it's a thin one. But he *has* seen maps. He'll know authenticity when he holds it. So we don't give him authenticity. We give him *belief*."

Pell's mouth twitched. It was the closest thing to a smile she'd seen from him since the tunnel. "You sound like a forger's daughter."

"I sound like a prospector's daughter. Same trade, different ore." She smoothed the tracing paper flat. "My father spent forty years making people believe things with paper. Claims, assays, promises. I watched him do it. I hated him for it. And then I spent three weeks learning that everything he wrote down was true." Her voice caught on the last word and she let it, because these people—Odile's people, Isandro's people, hers now whether she'd signed on or not—had earned her truths the hard way. "So. Teach me how a man fakes belief."

Pell was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against stone. Then he said, "First lesson: nobody believes a clean map. Clean maps are lies. Every real map is a map that's been *argued with*." He pulled his knife and, before she could stop him, dragged the tracing paper's edge across a stone, roughing it, aging it with abrasion instead of artifice. "Second lesson: the forger's instinct is to make the copy *better*. Clearer lines. Straighter roads. You have to do the opposite. You have to make it worse in the right places."

"Worse how?"

"Worse the way exhaustion is worse." He began to work as he talked, and Suri watched the disgraced cartographer become, for the first time since she'd known him, exactly what he had been trained to be. His hands stopped shaking. His voice found a lecturer's rhythm, the ghost of a man who had once stood in front of a hall of students. "Your father drew this over decades, in wind, in bad light, with cold fingers. So the copy has to carry that. A tremor in the long lines. A correction that was made twice and abandoned. Ink that changed because a pen was lost and replaced with a different pen." He glanced up. "You have his handwriting. I've seen the margins. Can you write tired?"

Suri thought of the kitchen table. Of her father coming home from four months in the hills, smelling of kerosene and stone, spreading his work across the wood while she sat in the doorway refusing to look. Of how she had looked anyway. Of how she had memorized, without wanting to, the exact way his hand slowed near the ends of lines, as if each word cost him something.

"I can write tired," she said. "I learned from the master."

* * *

They worked through the night, and it was the strangest night of Suri's life, because it was the first night in eleven years she had spent inside her father's head on purpose.

Pell built the forgery's bones: the terrain, the closed lines, the tunnel portal at Kilometer 44 rendered in the flat company style that Mateo had used as his base sheet. Suri did the soul. She copied her father's margin notes from the original—elevations, dates, the small crosses marking water sources—and then, following Pell's instruction, she *damaged* them. She smudged a coordinate and corrected it. She wrote a note, scribbled it out, wrote it again smaller in the corner. She let her hand shake on the long traverses and steadied only where her father's hand had steadied, at the names.

The names. Even in the decoy, she wrote them. Vargas. Okonkwo. Lindqvist. Ilyich. She wrote them small and careful, because that was the one thing she would not falsify, not even in a lie built to be caught.

Near dawn, Isandro came to check on them. He moved quietly for a big man; she'd noticed that the first week, a man practicing being unnoticed. He crouched at the edge of the lantern light with a tin cup of tea he didn't offer and she didn't ask for, and he studied the two maps side by side for a long time.

"Which one's real?" he asked finally.

Suri felt something loosen in her chest. "That's the point," she said.

Isandro nodded slowly. He looked tired in a way that went deeper than the climb, and she knew why—he'd spent the night after his confession not sleeping but *standing*, the way men stand watch over something they've finally set down. He'd told them everything. The bridge. The survey he'd signed. The crew he'd lost. And the sky had not fallen, and the team had not scattered, and this morning he had walked the riverbank for two hours reading the water and come back with a route down the mountain that made Pell swear in three languages.

"The exchange," Isandro said. "Walk me through it again."

"Vasseur wants the map because he believes it leads to the tunnel and the records are already gone—that's what Odile bought us with, those hours. What he *doesn't* know is that we don't need the map anymore. It's done its work. It's memory now, and memory's in our heads and in the photographs Pell hid in the survey tube." Suri rolled the decoy with hands that only slightly trembled, and she chose to believe the tremble was craft. "So we sell him the one thing he can't verify, at the price of the one thing he can't refuse. The map for Odile. Neutral ground, the old kilometer marker at the river fork. Two of us, two of them. He counts the map, we count Odile, everyone walks away believing they've won."

"And when he finds out it's a copy?"

"Then he'll be standing in the wrong valley in the wrong season," Isandro said, "with a storm coming down on him that his own company spent twenty years pretending didn't exist." He looked at the decoy again, at the tired handwriting, the honest names. "Your father would have—"

"Don't," Suri said, not unkindly. "But yes. He would have."

* * *

The river fork at midday was all slate and noise. The monsoon had sent its heralds ahead—wind that smelled of iron, clouds stacked over the peaks like quarried stone—and the river ran high and brown and certain of itself. The old kilometer marker stood at the gravel bar's center, a concrete post leaning like a drunk, the numeral 31 still legible beneath a skin of lichen.

Suri walked out with Pell at her shoulder, both of them empty-handed in every way that mattered, the decoy tube slung across her back where a hostage-taker's eyes would go first. Across the bar, four of Vasseur's men came down from the tree line in their company-gray slickers, and between them, wrists bound in front of her, walking with her chin up and her jaw set like a woman attending a meeting she intended to win—Odile Brant.

Even at thirty meters Suri could see the foreman had been worked over. A cut above her eye. A way of favoring her left side. But her eyes found Suri's across the gravel and did the thing Odile's eyes always did: measured, judged, and *approved*. Suri hadn't known how much she'd needed that until she got it.

Vasseur himself walked last. He had changed clothes since the ambush—of course he had; the man probably packed a spare civilization—and his slicker was dry in a way that seemed almost insulting. He stopped ten paces off and inclined his head, courteous as a bank manager.

"Ms. Reyes. Mr. Wexler." His voice carried over the water without effort. "You've had an eventful week."

"Give us Odile."

"All in good time." He extended a hand, and one of his men brought forward a leather case, opened it: money, banded, real, the sight of it obscene against the brown water. "The map, if you please."

Suri didn't move. "Her first. Unbound. She walks to us, the tube comes to you. Simultaneous. I know how these go, Mr. Vasseur—I've read my father's books on negotiation, and he read everyone's books on everything."

Something flickered behind Vasseur's polished face—amusement, maybe, or the predator's patience. "You're nineteen years old."

"I'm my father's daughter. It's worse."

For a moment the river filled the silence. Then Vasseur laughed, a short, genuine-sounding thing, and gestured. They cut Odile's wrists. She rubbed them, rolled her shoulders, and started across the gravel toward Suri—slowly, favoring the left side, her eyes doing arithmetic on every one of Vasseur's men the whole way. Suri unslung the tube and walked forward to meet her, and for four steps the two women passed each other in no-man's-land like ships exchanging colors.

Odile's voice, low, only for her: "The left one's a decoy too. His men. The real gun is on the rocks, upstream. Don't look."

"I wasn't going to look."

"I know. That's my girl." And Odile was past her, and Suri was setting the tube in Vasseur's gloved hand, and the transaction was done in the time it takes to hand someone a walking stick.

Vasseur broke the seal, slid the map out, and held it to the gray light. Suri watched him the way her father had taught her to watch a buyer—hands, eyes, breath. His eyes went to the margins. To the tired handwriting. To the smudged coordinate, corrected. To the names, small as prayers.

She saw the moment he believed it. It moved through his face like water through a channel: a softening, a settling, the specific greed-drunk calm of a man who thinks the hard part is over.

"Forty years," Vasseur murmured. "He carried it forty years." He rolled it, sleeved it, and smiled at her with what he probably imagined was warmth. "You're wondering if I'll simply take you both now. I've considered it. But a deal kept is worth more to a man in my position than two bodies in a river. Go home, Ms. Reyes. Take your engineer and your forger and whatever you think you found up there, and go home."

"We found names," Suri said. "Hundreds of them. You should know that's what you're carrying down the mountain. Not a map. A witness."

Something crossed Vasseur's face then—quick, quickly smoothed—and he turned and walked back toward his men, and Suri turned and walked with Odile and Pell toward the tree line, and no one shot anyone, and it was the most frightening walk of her life, one step at a time, until the gravel became pine duff and the trees closed around them and Odile said, "Run."

* * *

They ran. Not toward the valley—Vasseur would expect the valley—but along the contour, the high traverse Isandro had mapped, hard going through deadfall and slide rock, Odile grim and game with her ribs strapped, until the trees opened onto the overlook above the river fork and Suri, gasping, turned and looked back down.

Vasseur's party was still at the marker. Clustered. The leather case open. And even at this distance, even through rain that had begun to fall in earnest now, fat drops that struck the rocks like spent bullets, Suri could read the scene the way she'd learned to read her father's margins: the man holding the map up to the light again, turning it, the others crowding, and then Vasseur's arm rising, pointing—*not* toward the tunnel line, not toward Kilometer 44, but up the wrong valley. The eastern fork. The one that dead-ended in a box canyon that would become a trap the moment the flash floods came.

"He's reading it," Pell breathed beside her. He had his binoculars up, hands almost steady. "He's reading it as genuine. The corrections—the false portal notation we planted on the eastern branch—God, it's *working*. He thinks the tunnel access is up the east fork. He's going to spend the monsoon's first landfall in a box canyon with no exit, chasing paper."

Suri watched the gray figures begin to move, up-valley, away from them, away from the tunnel, away from everything. Vasseur walked last, as before, and just before the tree line swallowed him he stopped and looked back down the valley—back toward the west, back toward the true route—and for one long moment he stood motionless in the rain, and Suri felt the whole fragile architecture of the morning hang on that stillness.

Then thunder rolled down from the peaks, the first real voice of the storm, and the man turned and followed his men into the wrong country, and Odile said, "Don't watch him. Watch the river."

Suri watched the river. It had come up a hand's width since morning. The heralds were done; the storm itself was coming down the mountains now, and somewhere below, at the railhead in the valley, a telegraph line and a rolling stock and a narrow window of hours stood between them and the lowlands.

"Isandro's already gone ahead?" she asked.

"An hour ago. With the originals." Odile's grin, through the cut and the bruising, was a feral, glorious thing. "He's rigging the flood crossing at the old trestle. We move now, we sleep at Kilometer 9, we're at the railhead when the first train comes through tomorrow. And Suri—" Odile caught her arm, and her grip was iron and her eyes were bright. "That thing you did down there. The tired handwriting. Your father's tired handwriting."

Suri said nothing. The rain ran down her face and she let it.

"He'd have been proud," Odile said. "Now run."

They ran. And behind them, in the box canyon where no exit waited, the rain began to fall on Mr. Vasseur's umbrella, and the river he had never once bothered to read began, quietly, to keep its own accounts.
