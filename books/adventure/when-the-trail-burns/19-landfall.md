## 19. Landfall

The valley told us before the sky did.

I felt it in the rails first—laid my palm flat against the cold steel of the old spur line and read the tremor there like a man reading a pulse. A fine, constant shiver, too small for the eye to catch. The mountain was shedding water into its own veins. Somewhere above us, in the black throat of the tunnel we'd just left, the river that had been buried for forty years was remembering its old bed.

"Rain's coming down the gorge," I said. "Not falling. Coming."

Odile was gone. That fact sat in my chest like a stone dropped down a well—we hadn't heard it land yet, but we knew how deep the water was. Pell walked beside me with the satchel of records strapped across his body like a bandolier, one hand pressed flat against the canvas as if he could hold the dead men's names against his heart by pressure alone. Suri ranged ahead on the crumbling ballast, moving with a sureness her father's map had taught her, and every few hundred meters she'd stop and look back—not at me, at all of us—and wait until we'd closed the gap.

She wasn't going to lose anyone else. She'd said it once, two nights ago, to the fire, and none of us had answered because there was nothing to say that wasn't already understood.

The railhead came out of the mist an hour later: the valley station at Puerto Cobre, a place the company had built fat and abandoned lean. A rusting water tower. A goods shed with its doors hanging open like a slack jaw. Three kilometers of sidings where ore cars went to die, their beds full of standing rainwater from previous seasons, green as bottle glass. And beyond them, the river.

That was the thing about Puerto Cobre no survey report had ever bothered to note honestly. The town sat in the lap of the Río Blanco, and the Río Blanco was not a river so much as a debt the mountains collected every monsoon. The company had known it—the old correspondence Pell decoded had mentioned flood damage twice, dismissed twice, cheaper to rebuild the low bridge than move the yard. They had ignored the water for forty years the way they'd ignored everything else that didn't carry ore.

Now the water had come to collect.

* * *

We smelled the storm before we saw it. Wet stone and crushed vegetation, that green metallic breath the air takes when the whole sky is about to come apart. The light went the color of tarnished brass. Down-valley, over the ridgeline, a wall of cloud stood up from horizon to zenith, gray-black, boiling slowly forward like something poured rather than blown.

"The train," Suri said. "There has to be a maintenance run. There's always a run before landfall—they pull the track crews out."

"There was," Pell said grimly. "Look at the platform."

Empty. The lamp room dark. A single handcart overturned in the weeds, its handles furred with moss. Whatever schedule the company still kept on this ghost of a line, it had kept it without us, and the last southbound had gone through days ago, when we were still climbing switchbacks above the cloud line with Vasseur's men behind us and Odile holding a pass alone.

I wouldn't think about Odile. If I thought about Odile I would stop walking, and stopping was death now, plain as arithmetic.

"Then we go to the junction," I said. "Eight kilometers down-valley. The mainline crosses the Blanco there on the big trestle—if any rolling stock is moving tonight, it'll be staged at the junction camp."

"Eight kilometers." Pell looked at the sky. "And the storm?"

"The storm is our problem either way. But Vasseur is our problem *now*." I turned and looked back up the trail we'd descended, the black notch of the pass where Odile had stood her ground and bought us the hours we were spending like drunkards. "He doesn't know yet whether she talked. He doesn't know which way we've come. He knows only that she held him long enough for us to vanish, and that will make him furious, and fury makes men fast and stupid. We are eight kilometers ahead of a man who is faster than he is smart. So we walk."

Suri shouldered her pack without a word. Pell tightened the satchel strap. We went down the valley with the storm leaning on our backs like a hand.

* * *

It broke while we were crossing the ore yards.

No gentle beginning, no warning drizzle. The monsoon arrived the way a wall arrives—simply there, one moment absent, the next present, and the world behind it erased. Rain hit the tin roofs of the goods shed like gravel thrown by an angry mob. It hit the flooded ore cars and leapt back up a foot in the air. Within thirty seconds I couldn't see Suri ten meters ahead; within a minute the whole valley had been reduced to sound—thunder rolling down the gorge in long continuous avalanches, rain roaring on iron, and underneath it all, rising, a new voice.

The river.

I'd stood on bridges in flood before. I knew the sound a big river makes when it takes the voice of the whole watershed—the deep organ-note under the higher noises, felt in the sternum more than heard. The Río Blanco was singing that note now, and it was rising.

We sheltered in the goods shed just long enough to make the decision that mattered. Pell spread Mateo's map on a dry corner of floor, weighted with four spikes, and I traced the down-valley route with a wet finger.

"The low road follows the river. Faster. Dead if the Blanco jumps its banks—it will, tonight, it's already close." I moved my finger to a dotted line climbing the eastern bench. "The high road is the old mule track to the junction. Longer, slower, but above the flood line."

"Vasseur will take the low road," Suri said. "Fast and stupid, you said."

"And he'll find the low road underwater and have to climb to meet us anyway, angrier and wetter and an hour behind. Or—" I stopped. Looked at the map again. At the junction, and the trestle, and the river bending around it like a hand closing.

Pell saw it the same moment. His face went gray. "The trestle. If the Blanco takes the trestle—"

"Then the junction is an island and the last train south dies on the wrong side of the gap, and so do we." I rolled the map. "High road. Now. And we don't stop again until we can hear the junction camp generators."

We climbed. The mule track was a scar of mud and broken stone, and the rain made it a waterfall running downhill, and we went up it on hands and knees in places, three figures bent double against the deluge, and I will not pretend I was brave. I was terrified. But terror, I have learned, is just love wearing work clothes, and I loved these two stubborn damaged people stumbling ahead of me through the dark, and so I put my head down and climbed.

* * *

We reached the overlook above the junction at full dark, and the sight below stopped even Suri, who did not stop for weather.

The junction camp was a scatter of lamps in the rain—a locomotive under steam, white plume whipping sideways, boxcars being loaded, men running with lanterns. An evacuation run, exactly as I'd hoped: they were pulling out ahead of the flood. Between us and them lay the river.

The Río Blanco had come up six meters since afternoon. I could see it even in the dark, because it was no longer water—it was the mountain itself moving, a brown grinding slurry thick with trees and boulders and whole sections of fence, shouldering against the trestle piers with a boom we felt through the rock under our boots. The great trestle—eighty meters of timber and iron, older than me—was standing, but it was standing the way a tired man stands, swaying slightly, each wave of debris hitting it and the whole span shivering.

"They're crossing," Suri shouted over the storm. "They're taking the train across now, before it goes."

Below, the locomotive eased onto the trestle with its string of cars, slow as a funeral, headlamp carving a tunnel through the rain. Halfway across, it paused—drivers slipping, spinning on wet rail—and the whole structure groaned, a sound like a ship dying.

"We go down," I said. "We cross on foot behind it, or we wave them back and cross in the caboose. Either way we cross *tonight*, because by morning that trestle is kindling."

We went down. And that was when the lanterns appeared behind us on the mule track.

Three of them. Then five. Steady, unhurried, closing. Vasseur had found the high road after all—or been forced onto it by the drowned low road—and he had not spent his hours being angry. He had spent them walking.

"He'll take the originals at the trestle," Pell breathed. "It's a choke point. One span, one way across. He doesn't even have to catch us—he just waits on the far side."

"Then we don't give him a far side," I said, and the plan assembled itself in my head the way bridges assemble themselves, piece by piece, load by load, out of everything I knew about water and timber and the patience of rivers. "Suri. When we get down—you get aboard however you can. Pell goes with you, with the records. I stay on this bank."

"No," Suri said. Flat. Immediate.

"Suri—"

"You froze at the viaduct." Her eyes were bright with rain and fury in the lantern-light. "You froze and I covered for you and I never asked why, because you told us yourself, and I believed you. So whatever you're doing right now, Isandro Zhou, you tell me true or you don't do it."

So I told her true. "I'm not staying to be a hero. I'm staying because someone has to cut the span behind us, and I'm the only one who knows which members to cut so it drops clean instead of hanging. Vasseur gets a river, not a bridge. And then I cross on the handcar line—the single rail up top, the inspection trolley wire. It's rated for two hundred kilos. I weigh seventy-eight."

"It's forty years rusted," Pell said.

"So am I careful." I took Suri's face in my wet hands, briefly, the way you'd steady a level. "You carried your father's promise across a country that tried to kill you. Let me carry you across one river. That's the whole job. That's all any of this ever was—one person carrying another."

For a moment she was her father's daughter entirely, wanting to argue with the map itself. Then she pressed her forehead hard against mine, once, and let go, and we went down into the roar.

* * *

The trestle crossing was the worst hour of my life, and I include the night the bridge fell.

The train made it—barely, the last boxcars fishtailing over the span as the piers groaned—and Suri and Pell got aboard at the camp side in the chaos of the evacuation, shouting explanations nobody had time to hear. I watched the caboose lanterns swallow them and turned back to my work with a pry bar and a knowledge of this trestle's bones that I'd gathered in ninety seconds of study from the overlook, because that is what engineers do: we fall in love with structures quickly, out of necessity.

Vasseur reached the near abutment as I reached the third panel point.

He had six men. He had lanterns and rifles and that polished calm, though the calm had cracks in it now—I could see them even across the rain, the way light shows the hairline fractures in glazed porcelain. He shouted something at me across the water's roar. I couldn't hear the words. I imagined they were offers. Offers had always been his instrument, the way dynamite was mine.

I cut the members.

I won't describe it in detail because it wasn't dramatic. It was craft. Two tension rods at panel three, one chord splice at panel five, chosen so the failure would propagate toward the near shore, dropping the center span into the current while leaving the far end standing—stranding Vasseur on the wrong side of sixty meters of brown death, with his boatless, bridgeless, furious self.

The trestle gave with a sound I felt in my teeth. The center span folded down into the flood like a knee buckling, and the river took it the way the river takes everything—completely, instantly, without malice. Where eighty meters of bridge had been there was now only the Blanco, wide and white-crested and absolute.

Vasseur stood at the broken edge. Even at this distance, even through the rain, I saw him understand. And I saw him refuse to understand—to reject the arithmetic, to reach for some final offer, some purchase, some leverage, because a man who believes everything can be bought cannot believe a river cannot.

His men were already backing away up the bank, finding the high road around, already calculating their own survival. Vasseur stood alone at the edge of the ruined abutment with the flood rising around his boots.

I was halfway along the top inspection rail when I heard the timbers go.

The far end—the section I'd deliberately left standing, the section he was standing on—had taken the shock of the collapse badly. I'd known it might. I'd calculated the odds. But calculations are made of paper and the river is made of mountain, and a debris raft the size of a house came down the current and struck the remaining span amidships.

I heard Vasseur shout—not an offer this time. The far span tilted, slid, and went into the Río Blanco with Mr. Vasseur of the Company Security Detail still standing on it, upright to the last, a man destroyed at last not by anything he could buy or threaten or negotiate with, but by the accumulated interest of forty years of a company's contempt for water.

I hung on the inspection rail in the rain and I did not feel triumph. I felt the vertigo of watching any man die, even that man, even deserving it. The river didn't hate him. That was the terrible thing. The river didn't know his name. It simply collected what the living had refused to carry.

Then the rail shuddered under me, rust flaking away beneath my grip, and grief became a luxury, and I moved.

* * *

I crossed the last forty meters of rusted inspection rail with the flood chewing the piers below me and my arms shaking and the names of dead men—Mateo Reyes, and the crew of the Aldebaran Span, and now, God forgive me, Vasseur—running through my head like chainage markers. Not miles. Names. I was done measuring in miles.

The far abutment rose to meet me, and hands rose to meet me—strong hands, calloused hands, hauling me off the rail onto solid ballast, and I looked up into Odile Brant's face, streaming with rain, bruised along the jaw, alive, impossibly, furiously alive.

"How—" I started.

"Walked out while they were arguing about ransom," she rasped. "Men who pay well leave nothing, remember? Including guards who don't watch a woman who plays docile." She hauled me to my feet and shook me once, hard, like a partner. "You cut a bridge with me on the wrong side of it once, Zhou. Tonight you cut one with me safely on the right side. Improvement."

Behind her, the evacuation train waited, steam pluming, and in the lit doorway of the caboose stood Suri and Pell, and Suri was crying and laughing at once, and the satchel of names was safe against Pell's chest.

The trestle boomed again behind us. I didn't turn to look. Whatever the river wanted now, it could have.

Ahead, down the line, the storm was still making landfall over the whole coast of the world—but the train was pointed south, and the firebox was hot, and for the first time in three years I was not counting distance.

I was counting the people on board.

Four. All four. And tomorrow, in the city, the names in the rock would start their long journey into daylight—and I would be the one carrying them, in full view, under my own name.
