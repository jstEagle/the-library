## 20. Testimony

The city smelled of wet stone and diesel and, faintly, of the sea—Pell Wexler had forgotten how a city could hold so many smells at once, layered like sediment in a core sample. Three weeks of mountain air had scrubbed him down to something elemental: sweat, woodsmoke, ink. Now the harbor district pressed its complexity back onto him, and he found he didn't mind. He had spent eleven years believing he belonged nowhere, that disgrace was a country with borders you couldn't cross back over. Standing on the quay with a waterproof satchel heavy against his hip, he understood that he had been wrong about the geography.

The records had survived. That was the miracle he kept returning to, turning it over like a coin he expected to find counterfeit. Two hundred and six pages of company correspondence, forty-one worker rolls, three ledgers of supply requisitions dated to the construction years, and Mateo Reyes's map itself—the original, annotated in four decades of different inks—all of it sealed in oilcloth and carried down a mountain through the worst weather Pell had ever surveyed from the inside of. Odile was still in the city hospital with a fractured wrist and a bruised kidney and an expression that dared anyone to call her lucky. Suri hadn't left her side except to sleep. And Isandro—

Isandro had not slept much at all.

"He's rehearsing again," Suri said, coming up beside Pell on the quay. She nodded toward the end of the pier, where Isandro stood facing the water, his lips moving around words none of them could hear. He'd been doing it since dawn. Since before dawn, probably. "He's got the statement memorized. Dates, chainage numbers, everything."

"Of course he does." Pell adjusted the strap of the satchel. "He's an engineer. He'll have cross-referenced himself."

Suri almost smiled. It was a thin thing, her smile these days—grief and relief sharing the same small territory—but it reached her eyes more often than it used to. "You're nervous too. Don't think I can't tell. You've re-inked your pens twice this morning."

"A cartographer likes his instruments ready." It wasn't the truth, or not all of it. The truth was that Pell had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being believed about nothing that mattered, and today he would be asked to be believed about everything. Today the falsified chart that had ended his career would sit in the same room as the cover-up that had erased two hundred and six men, and he would have to say out loud which lies were forgivable and which were not, and he was no longer certain there was a difference.

Below them, the harbor ferries churned their wakes into gray-green water. Somewhere inland, past the warehouses and the tram lines, the offices of the *Coastal Ledger* waited, along with a regulatory commission that had granted them ninety minutes and a room with bad acoustics. Ninety minutes. Pell had waited eleven years for less time than that.

* * *

The *Ledger*'s building was narrow and tall, squeezed between a chandlery and a shuttered assay office, its windows fogged with the breath of too many deadlines. The editor—a woman named Corvus Hale, with ink-stained cuffs and the unblinking attention of a heron—had read the telegram Isandro sent from the valley railhead and cleared her morning without asking what it concerned. When they laid the oilcloth bundle on her table and unwrapped it, she went very still.

"That's the Reyes map," she said quietly. "There's been a reward posted for that for thirty years. The company calls it stolen property."

"The company calls a lot of things property," Suri said. "My father called it witness."

Hale looked at her for a long moment, then at each of them in turn, cataloguing. Pell recognized the technique; he'd used it himself when checking a survey against ground truth. She was comparing the people to the story, looking for the discrepancy that would unravel it.

"You'll want to start with him," she said finally, nodding at Isandro. "A railroad engineer giving testimony against the railroad carries weight. But I'll take all of you. One at a time, on the record, and I don't edit quotes for comfort. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Isandro said.

"And you—" Hale turned to Pell, and her voice gentled by a degree, which somehow made it worse. "I know your name, Mr. Wexler. The Meridian Pass chart. If you're part of this, I'll print that connection too. I won't bury it to protect you."

"I'd be insulted if you did," Pell said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out.

They set up in the paper's back room because Hale wanted privacy until the first edition broke, and because, as she put it, "the commission will want its own session and I intend to hand them something they can't refuse to act on." A stenographer arrived—a young man with careful hands—and Pell watched Isandro take the chair across the table from him and begin.

He started, as engineers do, with the structure. The bridge at Kilometer 112, the one that fell. He gave the date, the load ratings, the flood season, the crew of nine. His voice never wavered, but Pell, who had spent three weeks learning to read the man the way one reads contour lines—noting where the surface steepened, where the true shape showed through—saw the cost in his hands. They lay flat on the table, fingers spread, pressing down as if to hold the table itself against some current only Isandro could feel.

"I signed the survey," Isandro said. "My name is on the certification. The abutment soundings were taken during low water, and I knew—I knew—that the seasonal scour ran deeper than my instruments accounted for. There was a memo. I wrote it. I recommended a second season of measurement before final approval, and then I withdrew the recommendation under pressure from the regional office, and I told myself the margin was acceptable. Nine men crossed that bridge every morning believing the numbers I gave them. The bridge failed on a Tuesday. I was not on it. I have spent three years making sure I was never on anything."

The stenographer's pen scratched. Rain ticked against the window. Nobody moved.

"So when Mr. Vasseur came to me on the mountain and offered to trade silence about that failure for the map," Isandro continued, "he believed he was holding something over me. He was right. It was the heaviest thing I owned. But it wasn't shame anymore. It had become—" He paused, and Pell saw him reach for the word the way a man reaches for a handhold on wet rock. "It had become an excuse. Shame is a reason to hide. I had made it a career. What happened in that tunnel, what Mateo Reyes carried for forty years, is bigger than what I did at Kilometer 112, and I am not going to let my failure be the lever someone uses to keep it buried. Print all of it. My name, my signature, my decision. All of it."

Hale, who had been taking notes of her own in a hand fast enough to shame the stenographer, looked up. "You understand what this costs you. Your license. Possibly prosecution, if the commission decides the withdrawn recommendation constitutes negligence."

"Yes."

"You understand the company will say you're disgruntled. Unstable. That you conspired with a smuggler and a forger—"

"With a prospector who spent forty years keeping faith with dead men," Suri said sharply, "and a cartographer who's spent eleven years paying for a mistake nobody died from, and a foreman who's in a hospital bed right now because she held a pass against armed men so the rest of us could carry this out. You can print those descriptions if you want. We'll live with them."

"They're better than the ones the company would write," Hale said dryly, and turned back to Isandro. "Continue."

* * *

They worked through the morning and into the afternoon, and Pell gave his account third, after Suri's—hers was the shortest and the hardest, forty years of a father's absences compressed into twenty minutes of flat, controlled speech that broke exactly once, at the mention of the margin notes. Then it was Pell's turn, and he sat down in the chair feeling the way he imagined Isandro felt standing at the edge of the flooded viaduct: knowing the crossing had to be made, knowing the water was deep, knowing that freezing was the one unforgivable response.

He told it straight. The Meridian Pass chart, eight years ago, the deadline pressure, the corner he cut on the hydrological overlay because the client wanted the route approved before the fiscal quarter closed. No one died. A survey team walked into a gorge that his chart said was dry and found it running waist-deep with snowmelt; they lost their instruments and nearly lost two men to the cold, and when the inquiry traced the error back through the drafting office, it landed on Pell Wexler, and the profession closed its doors behind him with a sound he had heard every day since.

"I falsified data," he said to the stenographer, to Hale, to the room. "Not much. Enough. And I have spent eleven years telling myself that because no one died, my lie was a small one, and that the men who buried two hundred and six workers under a mountain are simply my lie continued at scale. That's not humility. That's cowardice wearing humility's coat. The difference between my chart and their cover-up is only a matter of degree, and degree is exactly what I'm not qualified to judge anymore. So I decoded their correspondence instead, and here is what it says."

He opened the folder he'd prepared—his own fair copies, keyed line by line to the originals, because he trusted his transcription more than he trusted anyone else's reading of a clerk's cramped hand. Construction year three. The lower gallery of the great bore, the one the maps marked as unstable shale. A collapse on the night shift. Not nine men. Not twelve. Two hundred and six, entombed so completely that recovery was declared impossible within seventy-two hours—which was, the correspondence made clear, a decision reached before any serious attempt at recovery had begun. The line was rerouted. The bore was dynamited shut with the men inside it. The rolls were purged; families received letters describing industrial accidents in distant sections, coffins filled with ballast stone in at least nineteen documented cases. The company's own internal audit flagged the payroll discrepancies for six consecutive quarters, and each flag was resolved with a single notation: *adjusted.*

"Adjusted," Hale repeated, when Pell finished. Her voice had gone quiet in the way of someone handling something fragile. "Two hundred and six men, and the word they chose was *adjusted*."

"There's worse," Pell said. "Page sixty-one of the requisition ledger. They ordered lime. Fourteen tons. Not for construction—for the gallery. For the bodies. Someone did the arithmetic on decomposition and signed for it under a materials code that reads as drainage aggregate. I checked the code against three other projects. It's consistent. It was deliberate."

The rain had thickened outside. Through the fogged glass, the harbor lights were smears of gold. Suri sat with her father's map spread before her, both hands flat on either side of it, guarding it even now, though there was no one left in the room who wanted it. Isandro stood at the window with his back to them, and Pell understood that he was not looking at the harbor but at nothing, at the inside of his own accounting, adding up what the day had cost and finding, perhaps for the first time in three years, that the total was worth paying.

* * *

The commission session was held two days later, in a government building whose grandeur felt obscene given the subject matter—marble floors, a coffered ceiling, the smell of floor wax over old power. Odile insisted on attending. She arrived with her wrist in a splint and her color still wrong, and when the chairman suggested she might prefer to give her statement seated, she said, "I sat down for six hours in a pass at kilometer fifty-one waiting for those men to come back for me. I can stand for twenty minutes."

She gave her account of the ambush in the same tone she used for reporting track defects: precise, unsentimental, complete. Vasseur's men, their weapons, their orders—they had instructions to recover the documents and leave no witnesses, and Vasseur himself had said, in words Odile reproduced verbatim from memory, *"Paper burns easier than people, madame, but people burn louder. Let's avoid the noise."* The commissioners wrote that down. Pell watched one of them, an older woman with a barrister's bearing, stop writing entirely for several seconds after that quote, her pen hovering, as if the sentence needed to be absorbed whole before it could be reduced to record.

Isandro spoke last. He had refused the anonymity the commission offered—there had been a procedural mechanism, a sealed identity protocol designed for whistleblowers inside the company, and the chairman had clearly expected him to use it. Isandro declined in front of the full panel.

"No," he said. "If my name is attached to the survey that failed at Kilometer 112, then it should be attached to this too. The same man signed both. You don't get the version of me that's convenient. I testified to the *Ledger* under my own name, including my own failures, and I'll do the same here. Anonymity is how this company operated for forty years. Adjusted. Unattributed. Sealed. I'm done contributing to the style."

The room was silent long enough that Pell heard the rain again, drumming somewhere beyond the tall windows, patient as arithmetic.

Then the chairman, a gray man named Aldous Renk who had spoken perhaps ten sentences all session, removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and said, "Mr. Zhou, the commission will need to verify the documents independently. That process takes time. Weeks, possibly months. In the interim, the company will deny everything, and they will deny it loudly, and they will come after every one of you with everything short of what they sent up the mountain. Knowing that—knowing what this will cost you personally, professionally, all of you—do you wish to withdraw your testimony?"

Pell had expected hesitation. He had expected, honestly, that some part of Isandro would flinch—that the man who had spent three years measuring safe distances would calculate the exposure and find it unacceptable. Instead Isandro looked down the table at Suri, at Odile with her splinted wrist held like a standard, at Pell himself, and something passed among the four of them that had no need of words, because it had been built the hard way, switchback by switchback, out of frozen rivers and burned bridges and a dying stranger's last request.

"No," Isandro said. "Read the names."

And Suri opened the roll book to its first page and began.

She read for forty minutes. Two hundred and six names, in her clear, carrying voice, filling the marble room the way water fills a cut channel—finding every low place, every hollow the grandeur had tried to seal off. *Tomás Iriarte. Wen Shu-fang. Ambrose Kettle. Dawit Girma.* Some of the commissioners bowed their heads. The stenographer's pen kept moving, and Pell thought: this is the chart that matters. This is the one I would sign. Forty years a dead man carried his testimony up and down the closed lines, sleeping in culverts, trading labor for bread, refusing to let the ink fade—and now it was entering the official record of the territory, name by name, and no adjustment anywhere could take it back.

When Suri finished, her voice was rough but level, and she closed the book the way one closes a door gently on a sleeping child.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The city steamed in the sudden light, and on the commission steps a crowd had gathered—reporters mostly, drawn by the *Ledger*'s morning edition, which lay folded under arms and spilled from satchels all down the street. Hale had run it above the fold: **THE SEALED LINE: 206 DEAD, BURIED BY THE COMPANY THAT BUILT THE RAILROAD** — and beneath it, smaller but unmistakable, the byline of testimony: *as stated by engineer Isandro Zhou, who also discloses his own role in the Kilometer 112 bridge failure.* Pell saw a copy of it in a newsboy's hand and watched strangers on the steps stop and read, and saw their faces change.

"It's real," Suri said quietly, beside him. "It's actually out of our hands now."

"It was never in our hands," Pell said. "We just carried it. Like Mateo did." He looked down the steps to where Isandro stood talking to a cluster of reporters, exhausted and upright, answering questions with the same precision he gave to load calculations—including, twice, unprompted, questions about his own culpability. "He's not hiding anymore."

"No," Suri said. "He isn't."

Then a commission aide came out through the bronze doors, a young woman slightly out of breath, and found them on the steps. "Mr. Wexler? Ms. Reyes? The chairman asks—you should come back inside. There's a telegram from the northern division." She hesitated, glancing at the reporters, lowering her voice. "The company has announced it will reopen the mountain line. Effective immediately. As a freight route." Her eyes went from face to face. "They're saying it's always been safe. They're saying there was never anything to find."

Odile laughed—a short, hard, disbelieving sound—and pushed past the aide through the doors, splinted wrist first, and the rest of them followed, because whatever the company intended to bury this time, it would not be done unwatched.
