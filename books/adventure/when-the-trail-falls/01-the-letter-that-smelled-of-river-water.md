## 1. The Letter That Smelled of River Water

The mud in the lowlands has a way of getting into everything—your boots, your teeth, your opinions about God. I'd been sifting it for eleven days when the telegram came, and I remember thinking, before I even opened it, that the paper smelled wrong. Not like the river here, which smells of silt and diesel and the slow rot of things the flood season gave back. It smelled like a different river. An upriver smell. Green and cold and old.

I should have known then. You always know after.

My name is Elodie Delacroix, which is a sentence I have spent thirty-one years trying to make mean something other than *son of Armand Delacroix*. If you've read a newspaper in the last two decades, you know my father's name better than mine. The man who found the Sunken Temple of Vess. The man who walked out of the Cordillera with four crates of pre-colonial gold and a mustache that photographed like it had been carved by a Renaissance hand. The man whose face was on the institute's brochure, smiling down at donors from behind glass, while his actual son dug through a flooded churchyard two hundred miles south, cataloguing other people's grandmothers' spoons.

Salvage work, they call it. Polite word. What it means is: when the government floods a valley for a dam, somebody gets paid to go in ahead of the water and pull out whatever history can be crated. You work fast, you work wet, and you work knowing that in six weeks none of it will exist except in your ledgers. There's a particular kind of humility the job installs in you, the way damp installs itself in plaster. You learn that most of what people leave behind is spoons.

I was good at it. That's the bitter joke. I was good at the one thing my father considered beneath him.

* * *

The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day the universe does its paperwork.

I was in the shade of the tarp we'd strung between the church's surviving wall and the bed of a truck that hadn't moved since the war, arguing with Ferro about whether a corroded brass fitting constituted an artifact or a paperweight. Ferro is my crew chief—a broad, patient man with hands like root vegetables and the unshakeable conviction that anything older than his grandmother deserves respect, and anything older than his great-grandmother deserves a museum. He was winning the argument, as he usually did, by simply refusing to accept that I might be right, when the boy came up the levee road on a bicycle too big for him, holding an envelope out like it might bite.

"Delacroix," he said. Not *Elodie*. Not *sir*. Just the surname, delivered flat, the way people say it when they mean the father and have decided the son will do.

That happens more than you'd think. I've stopped correcting it. Correcting it takes longer than the conversation.

I took the envelope. Cheap paper, water-stained at one corner, and that smell again—that green cold smell, like a river running through country no map bothers to finish. The telegraph office was forty miles east. Whatever this message had been, it had already traveled further than the wire that carried it.

Ferro leaned over to read it upside down, which is his version of privacy.

I'll copy it out here because I still have it, folded into the back of my field ledger, and because writing it down is easier than saying it:

> **EXPEDITION DELACROIX MISSED SCHEDULED CHECK-IN 14 JUNE AND 21 JUNE STOP INSTITUTE REQUESTS FAMILY CONTACT ADVISE STOP REPLY URGENT STOP**

Two missed check-ins. My father's expedition checked in every Friday at noon, frequency nine, without fail, without exception, without—this is the part that matters—without ever once missing one in fourteen years of poking into places that wanted him dead. Armand Delacroix treated that radio schedule the way other men treat religion. More seriously, probably. Religion forgives lapses; my father did not.

"Bad news?" Ferro asked.

"I don't know yet."

"That's usually how bad news starts."

I looked at the telegram a long time. Long enough that the boy on the bicycle shifted uncomfortably and pretended to inspect his chain. The thing about a name you've sworn off answering is that it doesn't stop calling. It just waits until you're standing in mud up to your shins, holding a piece of paper that smells like somewhere else, and then it calls very quietly indeed.

"Is there a reply?" the boy asked.

"No," I said.

Then: "Wait."

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about me and my father, and I'll try to keep it brief, because I could fill a book with it and neither of us would come out well.

Twenty years ago, my mother died in a hospital in the capital while Armand was three hundred miles upriver confirming a hypothesis about drainage patterns. He sent a telegram. It said, in its entirety: *REGRET STOP WILL RETURN WHEN THE SITE PERMITS STOP.* I was eleven. I read that telegram so many times I wore the crease soft, trying to find my mother in it somewhere, or myself, or any evidence that the man who wrote it understood that *the site permits* was not a phrase that applied to funerals.

He came back nine days later. He cried at the grave—I'll give him that, he genuinely cried—and then he stood up, dried his face, and told me, a child in a black coat too big for him, that grief was "a current, Elodie, and currents are best crossed quickly." Then he went back upriver in the spring. And the spring after that. And every spring until I was nineteen, when I told him, at a dinner held in his honor at the institute, in front of forty people who laughed at everything he said, that I would rather dig spoons out of drowned churches for the rest of my life than stand in his shadow one more season.

He didn't get angry. That was the worst part. He looked at me with something close to curiosity, the way he looked at a stratigraphy that didn't match the textbook, and he said, "You'll want a broader sample size before you draw conclusions," and went back to his soup.

I left that night. I changed cities. I took jobs he'd never touch. I built a small, honest, unremarkable career in the exact shape of his contempt, and I told myself it was independence. Some nights I even believed it.

So when the telegram said *INSTITUTE REQUESTS FAMILY CONTACT*, what it meant was: *your father may be dead, and you are the nearest thing to next of kin that isn't a museum.*

* * *

I didn't reply that Tuesday. I want that on the record. I slept on it, if you can call what happened in my tent sleeping—the mosquitoes saw to it that I mostly lay there composing arguments to a man who wasn't present, which, now that I think of it, describes most of our relationship.

Wednesday morning I walked the site instead of working it. The dam engineers had marked the water line with red stakes, and I followed them along what used to be the churchyard path, past the graves we'd documented and the ones we hadn't gotten to, and I thought about how the whole valley would be under forty feet of still water by August, and how everyone involved called that progress, and how my father would have called it vandalism and then taken the institute's money anyway, because Armand's principles, like his expeditions, were always funded by someone else.

At noon I sat on the levee and ate bread and sardines and watched the river move. It moves slow down here. It has all the time in the world, and it knows it, and there's a kind of arrogance in that which I recognized professionally.

Two check-ins. Fourteen years of perfect attendance, broken twice in a row.

There were innocent explanations. Radios fail. Batteries sour in the humidity. A generator dies, a part breaks, a week becomes two. Rivers eat equipment the way goats eat laundry. Any reasonable person would file those explanations first.

But I had grown up around that expedition the way other children grow up around a woodstove—close enough to feel its heat, far enough to get burned. I knew the redundancies. I knew there were two radios, spare batteries sealed in wax, a schedule with slack built into it, a protocol for silence that involved moving downstream to a fallback camp and transmitting from higher ground. My father designed that protocol himself, and he tested it annually, and he once made a surveyor cry over a misfiled frequency log.

For both check-ins to fail, either everything mechanical had failed at once—which happens—or the expedition had stopped being in a position to transmit.

Or it didn't want to be found.

I chewed the last of the bread and threw the crust to the water, where it vanished instantly, the river taking payment without comment. That's the lowlands for you. Everything goes under eventually, and the water doesn't gloat. It just keeps the change.

"Ferro," I said, walking back into camp. "How long to break down the grid squares?"

He looked up from the brass fitting, which he had apparently won custody of. "Why?"

"Hypothetically."

"Nothing's hypothetical with you when you make that face. That's your father's face."

"It is not."

"It is exactly. Same face, less mustache." He set the fitting down with the tenderness he reserved for objects and withheld from people. "Three days to break down clean. Two if we're sloppy, and you don't allow sloppy."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"No," Ferro agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing with weather.

* * *

Thursday I wired the institute. Not a reply to the telegram—an inquiry, carefully worded, asking whether search operations were being mounted and whether family assistance was required. I told myself it was due diligence. Due diligence has a nice sound. It sounds like a thing a professional does, a neutral act, a formality. It does not sound like a son checking whether he still has a father, because that would be sentimental, and sentiment, in my family, is a current best crossed quickly.

The reply came Friday afternoon, and it was a masterpiece of institutional nothing:

> **INSTITUTE REGrets**—the operator actually typed *REGrets*, lowercase g, which tells you everything—**THAT SEARCH OPERATIONS CANNOT BE AUTHORIZED WITHOUT FORMAL ASSESSMENT STOP FAMILY MAY WISH TO CONSULT EXPEDITION FINANCIER DIRECTLY STOP CONTACT MME O. FERRAND CAPITAL OFFICE STOP**

Financier. I turned the word over. My father had never mentioned a financier by name in any letter I'd received, though to be fair, his letters ran to artifacts and grievances and rarely to money, which he considered vulgar to discuss and essential to acquire. Still. Someone had funded fourteen years of boats and batteries and wax-sealed batteries, and that someone had a name, and the name was being handed to me like a key to a room I hadn't known was in the house.

I sat with the telegram on my knees while the light went long and orange across the dig. The crew knocked off. Ferro banked the cook fire. Somewhere down the valley the dam horns sounded their evening complaint, and the river kept moving, indifferent, patient, keeping score in its own ledger.

Here is the arithmetic I did, sitting there, and I did it honestly:

If I did nothing, the odds were decent that in a month or two the story would resolve itself. Either the expedition would surface sheepish and radio-broken, or the institute would eventually mount a token search, or the river would do what rivers do and return the answer in pieces. Either way, I would remain exactly where I was: a man who dug spoons, uninvolved, unbloodied, free. The name would stay unanswered. The vow would hold.

And if I did nothing, and my father was lying broken somewhere above the last rapids with a shattered radio and a crew going hungry—if some botanist or surveyor was walking out of the green dark right now, alive, expecting that someone, anyone, had come—

Well.

I have spent twenty years telling myself I am not my father's son. But there is one trait we share that I have never managed to excavate out of myself, and it's this: neither of us can walk past a question that someone else has failed to answer. He called it science. I call it stubbornness. The difference is mostly funding.

"Three days," I told Ferro that night. "Break it down clean."

He didn't ask where. He just nodded and started counting crates, because Ferro is the kind of man who understands that some decisions get made in the body long before the mouth agrees to them.

* * *

Saturday morning I sent two wires.

The first was to the capital, to this Ferrand woman, requesting a meeting. Three words over the necessary formalities: *regarding my father.*

The second was harder. It went to the telegraph office addressed to a man I hadn't spoken to in six years, Tomas Reyes, boatman, who worked the upper river runs and who had, the last time we spoke, told me with great precision what he thought of the entire Delacroix family, using vocabulary I would not reproduce here. But Reyes knows that river the way priests know liturgy, and if I was going up it, I was not going up it alone, and pride is a luxury I have never been able to afford at scale.

The wire said: *NEED BOAT AND MAN UPRIVER STOP PAYING STOP ELODIE.*

No surname. Let him wonder which Delacroix.

I walked back to camp through the red-staked graveyard, past the graves we'd saved and the ones the water would take, and the wind came down the valley carrying that same smell from the telegram—no, that's not true, that's memory editing itself—but I believed it in the moment, and belief is half of everything.

Two missed check-ins. A financier with a capital office. A boatman who hates my name. And a river running north into country where the maps go thin and the ink gives up before the water does.

I swore I'd stop answering to that name. Nobody warned me that someday it would stop asking, and that the silence would be worse.

On Monday I caught the mail barge east. Behind me the dam horns sounded, and the river rose, patient, keeping score.
