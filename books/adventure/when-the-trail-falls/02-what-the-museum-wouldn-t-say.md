## 2. What the Museum Wouldn't Say

The telegram had said *come to the capital*, and the capital said nothing back.

That was my first lesson in how institutions grieve: they don't. They file. I arrived on the morning boat with river mud still drying in the seams of my boots and a duffel bag that smelled of diesel and old rope, and I presented myself at the Institute of Antiquarian Studies at nine sharp, because my father taught me—among the few things he taught me deliberately—that bureaucracy respects punctuality the way a crocodile respects a fence. It doesn't fear it. It merely notes where you're standing before it decides what to do about you.

The building was marble and mahogany and the particular hush of money that has never been interrupted. A receptionist with excellent posture informed me that Dr. Delacroix's expedition was "a matter of ongoing institutional attention," which I took to mean they were paying attention to it the way you pay attention to a leak in the roof—hoping it stops on its own.

"I'm his son," I said.

"Of course." She smiled as though I'd confirmed a reservation. "Mr. Vasseur will see you at eleven."

It was nine-oh-four. I spent fifty-six minutes in a wingback chair designed by someone who hated spines, watching clerks carry folders with the solemnity of altar boys. On the wall hung a photograph of an expedition from thirty years ago: men in pith helmets squinting into the sun beside crates of something priceless, everyone grinning like they'd already forgotten the names of the porters standing behind them. My father wasn't in it. Even then, apparently, he'd been further upriver than the photograph could reach.

At eleven exactly, Mr. Vasseur received me in an office with a view of the harbor and no view of anything else. He was a compact man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair and the handshake of someone who has practiced warmth until it passes inspection.

"Elodie Delacroix." He said my name carefully, as if checking it for counterfeits. "Your father spoke of you. Once or twice."

"He had a gift for brevity."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a wince, but a cousin of one. "Please, sit. I want to be transparent with you."

In my experience, when a man announces his transparency, he has already selected which windows to open. I sat anyway.

"The situation," Vasseur began, lacing his fingers over a blotter bare except for a single folder, "is this. The Delacroix expedition missed its scheduled radio check-in on the fourteenth. And again on the twenty-first. Standard protocol allows a grace period—we assumed weather, equipment failure, the usual tribulations of fieldwork. But two missed windows is irregular. Three would be alarming."

"It's been twenty-six days since the first miss," I said. "You waited three weeks to send a telegram?"

"We sent inquiries through commercial channels first. River traffic, trading posts." He spread his hands, a gesture of institutional helplessness so well-rehearsed it might have had choreography credits. "The interior is not a place one simply *goes*, Mr. Delacroix. An official recovery operation requires authorization, insurance review, consultation with the ministry—"

"You mean it requires paperwork, and your father's signature isn't available to sign any of it."

Vasseur's smile thinned by a degree. "I mean the Institute takes its obligations seriously. Which is why we were relieved when you responded to our inquiry. Family initiative is often more... nimble than committee process."

There it was. The hook under the bait. They wanted me to go upriver so that if I drowned, I'd be a private tragedy, and if I found something, it would belong to them. Nimble, cheap, deniable.

"And if I find him?" I asked. "Or don't?"

"Then we will all know something we don't know now." He slid the folder across the blotter. Inside was a manifest—the expedition's outfitted inventory, itemized down to the number of tinned peaches—and a map of the river corridor with two basecamps marked in pencil. Someone had drawn the pencil marks recently; the graphite still shone. "Camp One at the confluence, Camp Two above the gorge. Beyond that, the survey ends. Your father declined to file coordinates for his final phase. Cited, ah—" Vasseur consulted nothing, reciting from memory with distaste, "—'site security concerns.'"

My father, who had never secured a site in his life except against other archaeologists, whom he considered a lower order of vermin. Site security. I turned the phrase over and found it didn't fit anything I knew about him, which bothered me more than the paperwork delay had.

"Who else knows the route?" I asked.

"The expedition roster is in the folder. Eleven members. Botanist, surveyor, two field technicians, medic, and so forth." Vasseur stood, ending the audience with the smoothness of a man closing a ledger. "The Institute wishes you every success, Mr. Delacroix. Do keep us informed."

I was almost to the door when he added, mildly, "One more thing. You'll likely be approached by Odile Ferrand. She holds the expedition's financing. Be polite. Be brief. And whatever she offers you, remember that generosity from financiers is always an investment, and investments are expected to return."

"That sounds like advice from experience."

"It sounds like advice," Vasseur said, "from arithmetic."

* * *

She found me that evening, which told me she'd known I was in the city before I did.

I was eating alone at a riverside café with the manifest open beside my plate, doing sums I didn't like—eleven people, twenty-six days of silence, and a supply list that assumed resupply from Camp Two, which assumed Camp Two still existed—when a woman stopped at my table without asking, the way weather arrives.

"Elodie Delacroix." Not a question either. She was perhaps fifty, silver at her temples cut short and practical, wearing a gray suit that probably cost more than my boat fare and looked like it hadn't cost anything at all, which is the most expensive kind of clothing there is. She carried no handbag. People who needed nothing carried nothing. "May I?"

"There's a chair."

"There are several. That's why I chose this café." She sat, signaled the waiter with two fingers, and ordered tea in a voice that made the waiter stand straighter. Then she looked at me, and I understood why Vasseur had warned me. Her eyes were the politest hostile thing I'd ever seen—warm brown, attentive, entirely without mercy. "You have his jaw," she said. "Armand's. He uses it like a crowbar. I imagine you do too, though you haven't noticed yet."

"You knew my father."

"I financed him. For six years. Which in my line of work is a form of knowing someone—more honest than friendship, frankly. Friendship lets you lie about what you'll tolerate." The tea came; she didn't touch it. "You're going upriver."

"I'm considering it."

"Don't prevaricate, it wastes both our evenings. You have his jaw and his stubbornness and you've been staring at that supply manifest like it owes you money. You're going." She leaned back, crossing her legs. "Good. Someone should. But let me tell you what the museum wouldn't say, since you've come all this way to hear it."

I closed the manifest. "I'm listening."

"Three things." She held up fingers, unhurried. "First: your father filed a discovery claim with the ministry eleven days before his last scheduled check-in. A preliminary notice—location withheld, contents unspecified. That notice makes the find legally *his*, provisionally, pending survey. It also makes it valuable. Very valuable, if the rumors reaching certain ears are even a tenth true."

"Rumors of what?"

"Second," she continued, ignoring me with real grace, "the Institute's board is not a monolith. Some members want Armand recovered out of genuine concern. Others want him recovered because a dead discoverer's claims get messy, and a living one can be negotiated with. And a third faction—" she smiled faintly, "—would prefer the whole matter resolved quietly, whichever direction quiet runs."

"And you? Which faction are you?"

"I'm the financier. I'm my own faction. My concern is the return on six years of capital, and dead men pay no dividends." She said it flatly, without apology, and I found I respected it more than all of Vasseur's upholstered sincerity. "Third."

The third finger rose. Outside, the harbor lights were coming on, one by one, like slow thoughts.

"Whatever happened up there," Ferrand said, "was not bandits and not weather. I've funded fourteen expeditions on that river. Bandits take outboard motors, medicine, ammunition. Weather takes schedules. Neither of them takes an entire team off the air for twenty-six days while leaving the satellite transponder powered down *deliberately*. Someone up there does not wish to be found. Either your father is hiding—or someone is hiding your father. In both cases, the person who goes looking should understand that looking itself may be the provocation."

The waiter drifted near; she waved him off without glancing at him.

"So here is what I'll give you, because I'd rather my investment survive than my options." From her jacket pocket she produced a small card, handwritten in fountain pen: a frequency. Nine-something megahertz, scheduled times noted in her precise slanted script. "The expedition's emergency channel. Officially decommissioned when they went silent. Unofficially, your father never decommissioned anything in his life—he hoarded frequencies the way other men hoard regrets. Try it at night. Skip propagation favors it after dark."

"Why give me this? If the find is as valuable as you say, why arm the son instead of sending your own people?"

For the first time, something moved behind the polish—a shadow, quickly governed. "Because my own people are how we got into this," she said quietly. Then, recovering, cooler: "And because you'll do it for free, or nearly, which makes you the cheapest option on the table. Don't flatter yourself into thinking it's sentiment."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"See that you don't." She stood, buttoning her jacket with two economical motions. "A warning, then, since you'll ignore advice. There are men who follow finds like this one the way sharks follow wounded fish—not the government kind, the private kind. If you see another boat on that river, a good one, crewed by quiet men who don't fish—turn around. Come home. Let the lawyers fight over whatever's up there after the water settles."

"And if I don't turn around?"

"Then be faster than they are." She put a few notes under her untouched teacup, payment for a service neither of us had used. At the edge of the lamplight she paused, her back half-turned, and said, without the polish now, just the words: "Your father sealed that discovery notice himself. Hand-delivered it to the ministry, alone, at night. Whatever he found, Elodie—he decided, before anyone else, that some people shouldn't know. Ask yourself whether you're going upriver to rescue him, or to find out which side of that decision you were on."

Then she was gone into the street noise, and I sat with the card in my hand and my tea going cold, feeling like a man who'd been handed a lantern and told, cheerfully, that the dark ahead was full of things that had already eaten better-prepared people.

* * *

I didn't sleep much. I sat in my rented room with the window open to the harbor smell—tar, brine, someone frying onions three floors down—and read the manifest again, then the roster, learning their names the way you learn the names of people you might be responsible for burying.

Ines Okafor, botanist. Lukas Brand, surveyor. Tomas Reyes, boatman—my father had written *T.R., reliable, difficult* in the margin, which described roughly half the people who ever worked with him, including, according to my mother, my father. A medic named Perrin. Two technicians whose surnames I had to sound out. Eleven names, twenty-six days of silence, and a frequency written in a stranger's fountain-pen hand.

Around midnight I caught myself doing the thing I hated: thinking like him. Weighing the find against the men. Wondering—not idly, not honestly admitting it—what kind of discovery makes a careful man seal his own records and go dark. What kind of thing sits under the falls of an unnamed river, worth silencing a team over. I felt the pull of it, that gravity every digger knows, the itch at the base of the skull that says *underneath, underneath*—and I hated that I felt it, because it was his itch, inherited, and I'd sworn at nineteen, walking out of his house with one bag, that I would never catch anything else from him.

By dawn I'd made the only decision available, which is to say the one I'd already made on the morning boat and had merely been dressing up as deliberation ever since.

I bought supplies through the day—rope, rice, medical kit, a second fuel drum—and by late afternoon I was at the waterfront chandlery district, asking after boats and boatmen, when a dockhand mentioned a name with a shrug that meant *good luck and God help you*.

"Tomas Reyes. Works the upper runs, when he works at all. Drinks at the Anchorline when he drinks at all. Tell him Delacroix sent you." The dockhand grinned. "Tell him exactly that. Nothing more. And stand back a little."

I found the Anchorline by its smell—spilled beer and bilgewater—and Reyes by the fact that he was the only man in the room drinking like the beer had personally wronged him. Big through the shoulders, gray coming into his beard, hands like mooring line. I gave him the message exactly as instructed.

He set down his glass very slowly.

"Delacroix," he said. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"His son. Elodie."

"I know who you are." He looked at me the way you look at weather coming in off the water—assessing tonnage, deciding whether to tie down or run. "You have his jaw."

"People keep saying that."

"They keep saying it because it's true and it's bad news." He turned back to his glass. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. I did my time with Armand Delacroix. Paid it in full, plus interest. Find another fool."

"He's missing. Eleven people. Twenty-six days."

"River's full of missing." But his hand had tightened on the glass, and I watched him hear the number and do something with it—file it somewhere deep, next to a debt he kept telling people was paid. "Twenty-six days," he repeated, quieter, mostly to himself.

"Two basecamps above the gorge. I leave tomorrow if I can find a boat and a man who knows the water past the maps."

"You won't find either."

"I found you."

He laughed then—one dry bark, no humor in it, the laugh of a man conceding a point to an enemy. He drained the glass, stood, and looked down at me with eyes that had spent too many seasons reading water to be fooled by anything on land.

"One condition," he said. "We settle terms tonight, in writing, and once we're on the river, you don't ask me about the last time I ran it with your father. What happened up there stays up there unless the river brings it down itself. Agreed?"

It was the strangest hiring condition I'd ever heard, and the most honest. I agreed.

He stuck out his hand—mooring line, callus, grip like a winch—and shook once.

"Be at the north dock at first light," he said, turning away. "Bring less than you think you need and twice the medicine. And Delacroix—" he paused at the door, not looking back, "—when you meet whoever's up there, and you will, remember your old man's rule. First one to the truth owns it. Everything after that is just salvage."

I walked back to my room under a sky going bruise-colored at the edges, the frequency card in my pocket and his words turning over in my head. *First one to the truth owns it.*

Somewhere upriver, my father had gotten there first. And everything since—the silence, the sealed notice, the polite vultures in marble offices—said that owning it was exactly what had gone wrong.

That night, before packing, I tuned the borrowed receiver to her handwritten numbers and let the static wash over me like river water. Nothing. Just the long hiss of a world not talking.

But tomorrow night, I'd be closer.
