## 3. Packing for a Place That Doesn't Want You

The capital in the wet season smells like a wet dog that has been reading philosophy—damp, heavy, and convinced of its own importance. I spent my first morning back from Ferrand's office sitting on the floor of a rented room above a chandlery, sorting through everything I owned that might keep me alive upriver, and discovering that the answer was: less than I'd hoped.

Salvage work teaches you to pack light and lie lighter. You learn that every kilogram you carry is a kilogram you'll eventually curse, usually at two in the morning, usually while sinking. So I made three piles on the bare boards: things that would save my life, things that would make my life easier, and things I only wanted because they reminded me of someone.

The third pile was embarrassingly large. It contained my father's old field compass—the brass one with the dent he never explained—and a photograph I told myself I'd brought for identification purposes, though there was no one upriver to show it to who hadn't already memorized Armand Delacroix's face, either as mentor or as menace. I put the compass in my breast pocket and the photograph in the bottom of my kit, under the spare socks, where sentiment belongs.

The first pile was short. A machete I'd re-hafted myself. Water purification tablets, half a tin. A fishing line and hooks. A mosquito net with more holes than doctrine. Quinine, which in this country was worth more than the money I had left after buying passage north. A revolver I'd won in a card game from a dredge engineer who swore it had never been fired, a claim I chose to believe for reasons of psychological comfort.

The second pile I kept trimming until it was nearly as short as the first. That's the real lesson of the lowlands: comfort is just weight you haven't met yet.

What I couldn't buy locally, I bought badly. The chandlery below sold me a tarpaulin that the owner swore was waterproof in the way that men swear to things they've never tested. I bought paraffin, rope, a spirit stove whose previous life I chose not to investigate, and a second-hand aneroid barometer whose needle trembled like a small nervous animal. If the needle was right, weather was coming. If the needle was wrong, I'd paid four days' wages for a paperweight. Either outcome felt representative of the expedition so far.

I also bought a ledger. A proper one, bound in oilcloth, because if I was going to walk into whatever had swallowed my father's expedition, I intended to write down what I found. Not for the institute. Not for Ferrand, whose card sat in my pocket like a splinter. For whoever came looking next, if it came to that. There's a particular kind of arrogance in assuming you'll be the last person to see a place, but salvage work breeds it, and I've never fully recovered.

By afternoon I had a manifest. I wrote it out twice—one copy in the ledger, one pinned inside my jacket—because my father taught me that, before he taught me anything else. *A man who doesn't know what he's carrying deserves what he finds.* It was one of the few lessons of his I'd kept, mostly because it required no affection to follow.

Which left the boat.

* * *

Finding a boatman in the capital during flood season is easy. Finding one willing to go past the last mission station, into country that maps describe with apologetic blankness, is a different trade entirely.

I worked the waterfront for two days. I heard prices that assumed I was a fool, then prices that assumed I was a dead fool, then offers to sell me a boat outright from men who visibly hoped I'd drown somewhere it wouldn't be their problem. The river has a reputation, and the reputation has teeth. Three expeditions had gone up that water in five years. One had come back. The arithmetic wasn't subtle.

On the second evening, drenched in a rain that fell with the enthusiasm of something settling a personal score, I ducked into a cantina at the end of the fuel dock, and that's where I heard his name.

"—Reyes took the Verrone survey up past the gorge," a dredge hand was saying, "and came back with half a crew and all his fingers. Man knows the water between here and the falls better than the water does."

"And he won't take the contract?" asked his companion.

"He won't take *that* contract." The dredge hand lowered his voice in the way men do when the story is about to get good. "Because of who's asking."

I didn't need to ask who. The whole waterfront knew by now that Delacroix's son had come north asking questions with an institute letterhead behind them. News travels faster than boats on that river; it always has. Somewhere upstream, if he was alive, my father was probably being told about my arrival by someone who'd heard it from someone who'd heard it from the chandlery boy.

Tomas Reyes was playing cards at the corner table, alone, which takes a specific talent. He was a compact man, fifty or so, with forearms that suggested a long professional relationship with resistance, and a face that suggested a long professional relationship with disappointment. He looked up when I stood over him, assessed me in perhaps a second and a half, and went back to his cards.

"Delacroix," I said.

"I know who you are." He turned a card. "You have his chin. It's not a compliment."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it's true." He gathered the deck with a motion too economical to watch properly. "Sit down or go away. Standing over a man makes him play worse, and I'm playing badly enough."

I sat. The table was sticky. Everything at the end of a fuel dock is sticky; it's the dock's one consistent virtue.

"I need a boatman," I said. "Up past the mission station. To the second basecamp, maybe farther."

"No."

"You haven't asked what it pays."

"It pays too much." He shuffled. "When a man offers too much money for a bad trip, it means he thinks he's going to die and wants company. I don't sell company."

"The institute will match any rate."

That got his eyes up. They were brown and flat and patient, like the river itself in the dry season, when it looks harmless. "The institute," he repeated. "Ferrand's people."

"Ferrand's money. I'm not sure those are the same thing anymore."

"They never are." He set the deck down and laced his fingers over it, which I would come to understand was the posture he took when he'd decided to tell you something unpleasant and wanted to enjoy your face while doing it. "I worked for your father. Two surveys. The second one, we came back downriver with a man missing and Armand Delacroix telling everyone the man had deserted at the mission station. Walked off. Just walked into the forest with his kit."

"Did he?"

"No." Reyes said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "He fell at the gorge portage. Bad footing, loaded like a mule because your father pushed the schedule through the rains. He fell, and the water took him, and we spent two days looking, and on the third day your father said we were losing the season and turned the boats around." He watched me. "I signed the report saying the man deserted. Because Armand asked. Because he said the truth would end the funding, and the funding meant the next survey, and the next survey meant—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "It meant something. It always meant something, with him. The something always changed."

The rain hammered the tin roof hard enough to make conversation feel private, which was probably why Reyes was talking at all.

"So you owe him," I said.

"I owe him a debt," Reyes corrected. "Those aren't the same thing either. A debt is what you carry when a man saves your life. Armand pulled me out of the gorge water in '09, after my own boatman froze at the sweep, and I've been carrying that ever since like a stone in my boot. But I also owe a debt to the man who went into the water at the portage, and I can't pay that one, because Armand helped me lose the address." He picked up the deck again, squared it against the table, once, twice. "So no. Find another boatman."

"There isn't another boatman."

"There's always another boatman. He'll be worse than me and more expensive, and he'll turn around at the mission station, and you'll die up there with a full purse. But he exists."

I should have walked away. Every instinct I'd developed in ten years of salvage work—the instinct that reads a wrecked hull and tells you exactly how many people lied about the loading—that instinct said *this man will be nothing but trouble*, and it said it loudly.

But underneath it was another voice, older, and it sounded unpleasantly like my father's: *A man who doesn't know what he's carrying deserves what he finds.* And I was beginning to suspect that what I was carrying, besides forty kilos of gear and a revolver of dubious history, was the other half of a story Reyes had been living inside for years. He knew the river. He knew the camps. He knew my father in ways I had never bothered to learn, back when learning them still would have cost me something I wasn't ready to pay.

"Name your price," I said. "Then name the thing you actually want. Everyone has both."

For the first time, something moved behind the flat brown eyes—not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but interest. The look of a man finding an unexpected current.

"My price is double the going rate, half up front, and you sign a paper saying that whatever we find up there, the decisions about turning back are mine. Not yours. Mine." He dealt himself a hand of solitaire without looking at the cards. "And the thing I actually want is to stand in front of Armand Delacroix and ask him one question, and have him answer it, and watch his face while he does."

"What question?"

"That's mine too." He turned a card. Red. He frowned at it as if it had disappointed him personally. "You're not going to stop asking, are you."

"No."

"You have his chin and his stubbornness. Between the two of us, I liked the chin better." He swept the cards together and stood, and up close I could see the gray coming into his hair and the old scar along his forearm that I'd later learn the origin of and wish I hadn't. "We load at first light. Slip three, end of the dock. My boat is the ugly one."

"How will I know it's yours?"

"Everything on this river that survives is ugly." He shrugged into an oilskin coat that had clearly survived a great deal. "Bring your manifest. If you've packed like a tourist, I'll leave half of it on the dock and you can wave goodbye to it."

* * *

He left more than half of it on the dock.

At dawn, in a mist so thick the river seemed to exist only as sound, Tomas Reyes went through my gear with the tenderness of a customs officer and the mercy of a fire. The spirit stove went—*"there's wood, you're not a tourist"*—along with the tarpaulin (*"it will leak directly onto your face at the exact moment you need sleep"*), two-thirds of my canned food (*"you'll fish or you'll learn"*), and, to my genuine protest, the second pair of boots.

"One pair of boots," he said, holding them up. "One. When they rot, you wrap your feet in bark like everyone else. This is not a hotel."

"This is extortion."

"This is arithmetic." He dropped the boots onto the rejected pile, which had grown to look like the estate sale of a very optimistic man. "Every kilogram you carry is a kilogram you'll curse at two in the morning while sinking. You know who taught me that?"

I did. Of course I did. I said nothing, and he looked at me for a moment with an expression I couldn't read in the gray light—something between contempt and recognition—and then handed me back the manifest and pointed at the boat.

She was ugly, as promised. Thirty feet of patched hardwood with an engine that had been rebuilt so many times it was arguably a different engine, a canopy of faded canvas, and a shallow draft that spoke of a man who'd run skinny water on purpose. Her name board read *LA PACIENTE*, which struck me, even then, as either a joke or a prayer.

"She doesn't look like patience," I said.

"She isn't. She's stubbornness with a hull." Reyes stowed the last crate and checked the lashings twice, the way some men check a door lock. "Manifest says you brought a ledger. Good. Write down what we take aboard, weight and all. If we're swimming, I want to know whose fault it was in writing."

"You think we're going to swim?"

"I think," he said, cranking the engine, which caught on the third try with a sound like a complaint being filed, "that a man is paying me double to go up a river that ate his father's expedition, carrying questions nobody wants answered, and that somewhere ahead of us is either a rescue or a reckoning, and I've done both kinds of trip, and they swim about equally often."

We pulled out of the slip into the mist. The capital dissolved behind us within minutes—first the docks, then the fuel smell, then the bells of the cathedral, going soft, going gone—until there was nothing but brown water, green walls, and the two of us and everything Reyes had allowed me to keep.

I opened the ledger on my knee and wrote the date, the cargo, the crew: *E. Delacroix, T. Reyes.* It looked absurdly short for what it was trying to say.

Behind us, far downstream, a second engine started. I noticed it the way you notice a word repeated in a conversation—you don't register it consciously, you just feel the sentence go wrong. I glanced back. Mist. Nothing. I wrote it off as a dredge.

Reyes didn't glance back at all. But his hands changed on the tiller, a half-degree of correction, the kind a man makes when he's listening to water that hasn't spoken yet.

"Get comfortable," he said. "It's four days to the mission station, and after that, the maps stop pretending."
