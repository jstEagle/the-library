## 4. Upriver, Where Maps Go Thin

The river began as a rumor and became a fact somewhere around the third set of rapids.

I want to say I was prepared for it. I'd grown up on my father's stories, after all—tales of water that chewed boats and country that swallowed men whole, delivered over breakfast with the relish of a man describing his favorite child. But there is a considerable distance between a story told over eggs and the moment when the water ahead of you turns from brown to white and Tomas Reyes says, without turning around, "Sit down and hold something you love."

I sat down. I held the gunwale. The river did the rest.

* * *

We had left the last town with fuel, two weeks of food, a boat that Tomas described as "ugly but honest," and a map that ended forty miles upstream in a coffee stain. That was not a joke. Someone before us had spilled coffee on the survey chart Tomas kept rolled under his bench, and the stain sat precisely where the mapped world gave up and the unmapped one began.

"Your father drew that line himself," Tomas said when I remarked on it. "The end of the map. He liked to say it was where the real work started."

"You worked with him."

"I worked for him." He adjusted the tiller by a degree that meant nothing to me and everything to him. "There's a difference. You'll learn it or you won't."

This was the shape of our conversation for the first day: he spoke in short strokes, I asked questions, and each answer arrived slightly damaged, like cargo that had been dropped once. I learned that he'd run this river for eleven years, that he'd piloted three of Armand Delacroix's expeditions, and that he would not tell me why he'd stopped.

"I didn't stop," he said, when I pushed. "He stopped calling."

"And you didn't call him?"

Tomas looked at me then—a long, level look, the kind a man gives a dog that keeps sniffing at a trap. "You want to know about your father, ask your father. If we find him. If we find anything." He turned back to the water. "The river will tell you what it wants you to know. Everything else is gossip."

* * *

By the second day I understood what he meant about the river keeping score.

It wasn't malevolent. That's what surprised me. I had expected hostility—the jungle as enemy, the water as adversary—but what I got instead was indifference of such purity it felt almost holy. The heat didn't hate us; it simply pressed down, hour after hour, until sweat stopped being a thing my body did and became a thing the air did to me. The insects didn't scheme; they arrived, in clouds and columns, with the punctual patience of tax collectors. When we tied up at dusk, the forest made its sounds around us—not threatening, not welcoming, just continuing, as it had continued before any Delacroix ever drew a line on a map and called it the edge of the known.

I kept a log, because my father kept logs and because habit is a kind of inheritance even when you despise the estate. *Day two. Ran the Narrows without incident. Tomas says the water is low for the season, which he calls good luck and I suspect means bad news arriving early.*

That night, moored against a fallen ceiba tree, I tried again.

"The last time you saw him," I said. "Just tell me that much. Was he well?"

Tomas was cleaning the outboard's carburetor by lantern light, his hands moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who has taken apart the same machine a hundred times. He didn't look up.

"He was thin," he said finally. "Thinner than you. Eating expedition food and calling it a diet." A pause. The lantern hissed. "He talked about you."

My chest did something complicated. "What did he say?"

"That you were stubborn." Tomas screwed a fitting into place, tested it with his thumb. "That you dug salvage in the lowlands for people who paid you late and thanked you never. That you were wasting—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and I watched a man physically swallow an opinion.

"Wasting what?"

"Nothing. Go sleep. Dawn comes whether you're ready or not, and the river doesn't care which."

But I lay in the boat that night staring up through the canopy at stars cut into coins by the leaves, and I turned the sentence over like a stone found in a field. *Wasting.* Wasting what? My talent? My time? My life? And who had told Armand Delacroix enough about my life for him to have opinions about it? Because I certainly hadn't written to him. Not in four years. Not since the argument at the institute, the one where he'd said I lacked ambition and I'd said he lacked honesty, and we had both been right, which is the worst way for an argument to end, because nobody gets to be wrong and walk away clean.

* * *

Day four brought the first real rapids since the Narrows, and with them the first proof that the river was keeping accounts.

Tomas saw it before I did—or rather, heard it, because he spent half of every day listening to the water the way other men listen to radio. He killed the engine while we were still around the bend, and the sudden silence was louder than the noise had been.

"What is it?"

"Listen." His head was cocked. Under the ordinary roar there was another sound, deeper, arrhythmic, like a drum being struck underwater. "Hole," he said. "Big one, mid-channel. We go left, tight to the rocks, and we don't touch the tiller unless I say."

"We could portage."

"With what? There's no bank. There's rock and there's water and one of them is going to win." He was already moving, shifting weight forward, handing me a bailer. "You bail if we ship water. You don't pray, you don't scream, you bail. Praying takes hands."

We went left. Tight to the rocks. The world became noise and cold spray and a single enormous green eye of water opening beside us—I will see that hole in my sleep for years, a pupil of nothing ringed in foam—and then we were through, into slack water below, both of us breathing like men who had run a mile underwater.

Tomas laughed. It startled me; it was the first unguarded sound I'd heard from him, rusty as an unused hinge.

"You bailed," he said. "Most of them pray."

"Who's 'most of them'?"

"Everyone I've ever carried up this river who wasn't me." He restarted the engine, and the laugh was gone, folded away. "Your father prayed. Once. At the Gorge. I never let him forget it."

"You said the gorge was—"

"I said a lot of things." He steered us toward the channel. "Ask him yourself."

* * *

On the fifth night I caught him doing something strange.

I'd woken to take my turn at anchor watch and found him standing in the bow with the carved token from the village in his hand—the elder's gift, the little figure of dark wood that had appeared in our boat unasked, that neither of us had discussed since. Tomas was turning it over, and in the lantern light his face wore an expression I hadn't seen on it: not resentment, not gruffness, but something older and more careful. Recognition, maybe. Or dread wearing recognition's clothes.

"It's a warning," I said quietly.

He didn't startle; he'd known I was awake. "It's a message. Warnings are for people who can still choose." He handed it back to me. "Keep it dry. Keep it close. And when someone from upriver comes to you—and they will—you show it to them before you speak. Before you explain anything. Understand?"

"Why?"

"Because it's the only word of theirs you'll be able to read." He went aft and lay down with his back to me, conversation concluded by decree.

I held the token a long time. In the dark it was just a shape, a knot of carved intention, and I thought about the village that had turned its backs, the elder who had spoken to me only through wood, and the growing suspicion that everyone on this river except me already knew how this story ended.

* * *

Day six. The map ran out.

Not gradually—all at once, the way a candle goes out. One bend the banks were familiar in their strangeness, marked in Tomas's memory if not on paper; the next bend, and he slowed the boat and looked at the water the way a man looks at a stranger's face in his own house.

"From here," he said, "we're guessing."

"You've been past here. With my father."

"I've been past here with your father." He let that sit. "Different river every year, Elodie. Flood moves the bars, drops new trees, closes old channels. What I knew is dead. What's alive now, nobody knows." He fed the engine throttle carefully. "So we guess slow, and we listen, and we thank whatever gods you keep that the water's still low."

The country changed too. The forest thickened until the banks were walls, and the sky shrank to a ribbon overhead, and the air took on a weight that felt less like weather than like presence. Birds called in patterns I couldn't parse. Twice we passed places where the trees had been broken back from the water in wide swaths—old flood damage, Tomas said, though he said it in the tone of a man saying something true while meaning something else.

And once—we were passing a long gravel bar, midday, heat shimmering off the stones—I saw footprints.

Human prints, barefoot, dozens of them, coming down to the water and going away again. Fresh enough that the edges hadn't crumbled.

Tomas didn't stop. Didn't slow. But his eyes tracked along the bar, and his hands on the tiller went very still, and when we were a mile past he said, without preamble: "People live up here who don't want to be counted. Your father counted things. That was always the trouble with him."

"Is that why you stopped working for him?"

The engine droned. The banks slid by, green and silent and utterly indifferent to my question. I thought he wouldn't answer.

"He found something," Tomas said at last, so quietly I nearly lost it under the motor. "Two seasons ago. He wouldn't say what. He came back downriver different—happy, and scared of being happy, like a man carrying a full cup across ice. And then Ferrand's people started asking questions, and he stopped answering his radio, and I—" His jaw tightened. "I owed him. From the gorge. And debts are heavier than fear, usually. So when your telegram came, I said yes. Don't make me regret the arithmetic."

"Ferrand's people. You know her security man? Brac?"

"I know the type." He glanced at me sideways. "Quiet ones are the dangerous ones. Loud men are selling something. Quiet men are buying."

We ran on through the afternoon, and the heat pressed down, and the forest watched us pass with the patience of something that had buried better men than either of us and intended to bury worse.

* * *

That evening, anchored in a side channel with the current combing past us toward the sea, I raised the radio.

Frequency nine. Odile Ferrand's frequency, given to me in her office with that cool smile and that colder warning. For six nights I had called into static. Tonight I called again, out of discipline more than hope, and was reaching to switch it off when the static changed.

It didn't resolve into words. It resolved into rhythm. A voice—thin, warped by distance and interference, unmistakably a woman's—counting. Numbers, descending, spaced with terrible regularity. I caught perhaps five of them before the signal folded in on itself and vanished, leaving behind the ordinary hiss of a world that wasn't talking to me.

Tomas stood in the bow, watching me.

"Well?" he said.

"Numbers. Counting down."

He was quiet a moment. Then he turned back to face the black water ahead, and what he said next I wrote down that night exactly as he said it, because some sentences arrive pre-loaded with dread:

"Then somebody upriver knows exactly how much time they've got left."
