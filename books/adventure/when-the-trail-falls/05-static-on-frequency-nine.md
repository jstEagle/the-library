## 5. Static on Frequency Nine

The radio came with the boat, the way rust comes with a hull. Tomas Reyes had it lashed under an oilcloth in the stern, wedged between a crate of diesel and a coil of rope that smelled like every bad decision he'd ever made on this river. It was a military surplus set, older than me by a decade, its casing scarred with cigarette burns and one long gouge that looked suspiciously like a machete had lost an argument with it.

"You'll want to check it before dark," Tomas said, not looking at me. He was coiling the anchor line with the particular violence of a man who'd rather be doing anything else, including drowning. "Storms come up the valley around nine. They eat signals."

"I know how radios work."

"Everyone knows how radios work." He cinched the knot and finally glanced at me, and there it was again—that look I was starting to collect like river stones. The look of a man measuring how much of my father he was going to have to put up with. "Knowing and doing are different animals out here. Your father knew how radios worked too."

I let that sit. It was becoming our whole conversation: my father's ghost, sitting in the boat between us like a third passenger who didn't pay his share.

We'd made camp on a gravel bar where the river bent wide and slow, the kind of place Tomas chose without consulting charts because, as he put it, "the water tells you." The water, apparently, told him things it refused to tell me, which was fair—I'd spent the last six years telling myself stories about water too, mostly involving salvage barges and insurance adjusters, and none of them had involved my father. That had been the point.

Now here I was, three days upriver from the last town with a name, chasing a telegram that said *missed two scheduled check-ins* as if that were information rather than a wound.

I set the radio on an ammunition box, ran the antenna wire up into a riverside alder, and waited for dark. Not because the procedure required darkness. Because I did.

* * *

There's a particular hour on a tropical river when the light gives up all pretense and simply leaves. The sky doesn't fade so much as resign, and the jungle on both banks goes from green to a black so dense it feels structural, load-bearing, like the night itself might collapse if you leaned on it wrong. Insects take over the acoustics with the enthusiasm of an orchestra that has never once been asked to play quieter. Somewhere across the water something large moved through shallows and thought better of it.

Tomas ate beans out of the pot with a spoon and watched me tune the set with the expression of a man watching someone else pet a dog he knows bites.

"Frequency nine," I said. "That's what Ferrand gave me."

"She give you anything else?"

"A warning."

"What kind?"

"The kind that comes free with a frequency." I turned the dial through static. "She said if I heard anything, I should consider carefully whether I wanted to know what it meant."

"That's not a warning," Tomas said. "That's an invoice. You pay later."

The static rolled. I worked the band the way my father taught me when I was eleven years old and still hungry for his approval—slow sweeps, patient ears, letting the signal come to you instead of hunting it like it owed you money. He'd been good at that. Patient, I mean. With machines. It was only people he treated like equipment to be operated until they broke.

Nothing. Band after band, nothing but the river's own noise translated into electricity—the crackle of heat leaving the air, the occasional whine of distant weather. I was beginning to build the small, bitter comfort of failure. If the set found nothing, then nothing was happening upriver. No news was the same as no disaster, if you were generous with the arithmetic. I could turn the boat around tomorrow and go back to my barge and my insurance adjusters and my life built specifically to require nothing from Armand Delacroix except a surname I didn't use.

Then the static changed.

It's hard to describe what a change in static sounds like unless you've waited for one. Most static is chaos, honest chaos, the universe shrugging at you in white noise. But when a carrier wave slides underneath it, the texture shifts—a smoothness, a directionality, like current under chop. My hand stopped on the dial. Tomas's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"There," I said, though he hadn't asked.

The voice came through in fragments, the way voices do when they've crossed half a country of storm cells to reach you. A woman. Young, or young-sounding; compression flattens everyone. She wasn't speaking words. She was counting.

"—seven... eight... nine..."

A pause, filled with the sound of her breathing, or wind, or both.

"...fourteen... fifteen..."

Another pause. Longer. Then, faintly, underneath the numbers, something else—a rhythm, a tapping, deliberate, spaced. Three beats. Pause. Three beats. Pause.

And then, clear as if she were sitting on the gravel bar with us, one word:

"*Delacroix.*"

My father's name, spoken by a stranger's voice out of a box of static, in the middle of a wilderness that keeps score.

The carrier dropped. Static rushed back in like water filling a hole, and I sat there with my hand frozen on the dial, tuning back and forth across the frequency like a man checking whether a body is still breathing.

"Say it again," I whispered, which is the stupidest prayer ever invented, and the radio, being a machine and not a god, declined.

* * *

Tomas took the beans off the fire. That was his way of processing things—domestic action in the face of the inexplicable. He stirred the pot, added water we could barely afford to spare, and said nothing for a long time.

"Counting," he said finally. "What's she counting?"

"I don't know."

"People count things when they're keeping time. Keeping time means waiting. Or hiding." He pointed the spoon at the radio. "Or dying slowly enough to be bored about it."

"Thank you. Very comforting."

"I'm not paid to comfort you. I'm barely paid at all." He sat back against a crate. "Three taps. You hear that? Three and pause. That's old survey code. Spacing marks. Your father used to—" He stopped himself, jaw working, like the sentence had teeth.

"My father used to what?"

Tomas looked at the fire. When he answered, his voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he'd fallen through before. "He used to tap three times on the table when he was thinking. Drove his crews mad. Whole camps would start doing it in their sleep." He shrugged elaborately. "Coincidence. Rivers are full of them."

"Is that what you believe?"

"What I believe is that we're three days from anywhere and you just got called by name through four hundred kilometers of static, and either your father's team is alive and signaling, or somebody wants you to think they are." He met my eyes. "Both of those should scare you equally, Elodie. One means rescue. The other means bait."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to say something cutting about paranoia and debts and whatever history he carried with Armand that he kept wrapped up tighter than the diesel. Instead I tuned the radio back to frequency nine and listened until midnight, and the voice didn't come back, and the river kept talking to itself in the dark like it always does, indifferent, fluent, keeping its own count of everything that goes down it.

* * *

I didn't sleep so much as negotiate with sleep and lose repeatedly. Around three in the morning I gave up, crawled out of the tent, and sat by the dead fire turning the evening over in my head like a stone with something written on it.

Here's what I couldn't stop chewing: the woman had said *Delacroix*. Not "expedition base." Not a callsign. A name—my name, our name—and names on open frequencies are choices. You don't broadcast a family name into the dark unless you want a specific person to hear it. Which meant either someone upriver knew I was coming, or someone downriver wanted me to believe they did.

Ferrand's warning came back to me. *Consider carefully whether you want to know what it meant.* An invoice, Tomas had said. Pay later.

I thought about the telegram, two lines long, official, smelling faintly of the institute's typewriter ribbon. I thought about how quickly Odile Ferrand had produced a frequency when I asked—as if she'd been holding it ready, as if the question were anticipated, as if the whole machinery of my search had been oiled in advance. Helpful people frighten me. Helplessness is honest; helpfulness has a budget.

And beneath all of it, the thing I kept refusing to look at directly: hope. Ugly, embarrassing hope. Because if the voice was real—if some member of my father's team was alive and transmitting—then the counting meant time, and time meant days, and days meant a chance. Twenty years of silence between Armand Delacroix and myself, and apparently all it took to make me feel it again was one word through static. That's the cruelty of family: distance doesn't actually protect you. It just stores the debt at interest.

The moon came up behind cloud, a smear of light like breath on glass. Across the bar, Tomas slept in the boat, one hand on the rifle he pretended he hadn't brought. I envied him his certainty that the world was hostile. Certainty lets you sleep.

I went back to the radio one more time. Not tuning—just listening, volume low, the way you leave a door open for a cat. Static, static, the river's shrug. And then, at 3:40 by my watch, so brief I'd have missed it if I'd been asleep like a sensible person, the carrier slid under again. No voice this time. Just the taps. Three. Pause. Three. Pause. Steady as a heartbeat, going on for maybe a minute, and then gone, and the silence afterward felt less like absence than like a held breath.

Someone was alive upriver. Someone was keeping time.

Or someone very patient was teaching me to keep coming back to this frequency, night after night, like a fish learning the shape of a hook.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, mist standing on the water in columns. Tomas was already awake—he struck me as a man who considered sleep a rumor—and had coffee going, which was his single concession to hospitality and probably cost him physically.

"We push today?" he asked.

"We push today."

"Faster than planned."

"Plans changed at 3:40 this morning."

He handed me a cup and studied me over the steam. Whatever he saw in my face seemed to settle something for him, though whether it settled toward respect or resignation I couldn't tell. "You heard it again."

"Taps. Same pattern."

"And you're sure it's not weather. Not interference. Not your own head wanting it so badly it built the signal out of static." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "I've seen men hear their mothers in engine noise, Elodie. The river will give you any voice you ask it for."

"It gave me a name."

"Rivers know names." He drank his coffee. "All right. Faster, then. But we do it my way—daylight running, no rapids after noon, boat tied before the light goes. And the radio stays on frequency nine day and night. If whoever's tapping wants us, they can keep tapping while we come."

I almost told him then. About the other possibility I'd been carrying since midnight—the one shaped like Odile Ferrand's helpfulness, like a frequency handed over too easily, like bait on a hook I was already swallowing. But saying it aloud would make it real, and real things demand decisions, and I wasn't ready to decide whether I was rescuing my father's team or delivering myself to whoever had arranged this rescue.

So I drank my coffee and said, "Your way, then," and started breaking camp, and tried not to notice that my hands weren't entirely steady.

By midmorning the mist had burned off and the river had resumed its great indifferent business, carrying us north into country the maps gave up on with increasing honesty—blank spaces annotated only with hatching and apology. Behind us lay the last check-in station, the last telegraph line, the last anyone would think to look. Ahead, somewhere past the second gorge, was Camp One and whatever truth it held.

And in the stern, under oilcloth, the radio hissed to itself on frequency nine, faithful as a dog at a door, waiting for a voice that had counted fourteen numbers and spoken one name and then, like everything else my father ever promised me, gone quiet.
