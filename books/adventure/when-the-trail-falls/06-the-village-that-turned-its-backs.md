## 6. The Village That Turned Its Backs

We smelled the village before we saw it—woodsmoke and river mud and something roasting over coals that made my stomach file a formal complaint. Three days of canned meat and Tomas's cooking, which consisted of opening canned meat with an air of grievance, will do that to a man.

The settlement sat on the north bank where the river bent wide and slow, maybe forty structures on stilts above the flood line, nets drying on racks like the skeletons of enormous fish. Canoes were pulled up in rows. Children spotted us first—they always do—and shouted something upstream, and by the time we nosed toward the landing there were perhaps thirty people watching us from the bank with expressions I can only describe as *closed*.

Not hostile. Closed. The difference matters. Hostility is a door slammed in your face; this was a door that had never been a door at all, just wall painted to look like one.

"Kamari," Tomas said, cutting the engine. "They trade with the logging boats sometimes. Neutral ground."

"Neutral," I said. "That's promising."

"It's not promising. It's neutral. There's a difference."

I was learning to hear the difference between Tomas's pessimism and his information. Pessimism came with a shrug. Information came with him not looking at you while he said it. This was information.

I raised both hands as we drifted in—the universal grammar of *I'm not here to take anything*—and called out a greeting in trade Spanish, then in the pidgin the lowland crews used. A few faces flickered at the Spanish. Nobody answered it.

An older man came down the landing steps alone. He was lean in the way of men who have carried heavy things for fifty years, wearing a shirt that had once been white and had since been promoted through gray to a kind of philosophical brown. He stopped at the edge of the planks and looked at our boat for a long time—not at us, at the boat. At the outboard. At the fuel drums. At the gear lashed under canvas.

Then he looked at me, and I understood that everything he needed to know about us had already been decided before we arrived, and that my role in this encounter was largely decorative.

"You come from downriver," he said. His Spanish was better than mine.

"Yes. We're going up."

"Everyone comes from downriver." He said it without bitterness, the way you'd say *the sun rises*. "Everyone is going up."

"We're looking for an expedition. Archaeologists. My father among them. They've missed their check-ins."

Something moved through the crowd behind him—not a murmur exactly, more like a wind through wheat, bodies shifting weight, eyes finding other eyes. The old man didn't turn to look at it. He'd felt it too, and whatever it told him, he kept it off his face with professional skill.

"Foreigners," he said. Not a question.

"Some. Yes."

He considered the river for a while. Downstream, the water slid past brown and patient, carrying its usual cargo of leaves, branches, and things better not identified.

"There was rain in the hills," he said finally. "The river will rise early this year. You should go home."

"I can't. Not yet."

"Everyone says that also." And he turned and walked back up the steps, and the crowd opened for him and closed behind him like water, and that, apparently, was the interview.

* * *

Tomas found us a spot at the downstream end of the landing where a fisherman's family let us tie up for a price that was less rent than tribute. We ate aboard. Walking into the village proper was clearly not on offer; when I tried, drifting toward the net racks with what I hoped was harmless curiosity, conversations within twenty meters simply ceased, the way birds stop singing before a storm. I turned around. The conversations resumed. It was the most efficient social mechanism I have ever witnessed, and I include my father in that assessment, who could end a faculty meeting with a single raised eyebrow.

"They know something," I said, back on the boat, watching smoke stand straight up from cookfires in the windless evening.

"Of course they know something. Forty people passed through here heading upriver in the last two months. Boats don't vanish. Fuel has to come from somewhere. Food. Guides." Tomas cleaned his nails with a sliver of bamboo, not looking at me. "The question isn't whether they know. The question is why they're not telling."

"And?"

"And I'm a boatman, not a priest. People confess to priests."

But he was wrong about that, or half wrong, because that evening the confession came—to me, sort of, sideways, in the form of a boy.

He couldn't have been more than ten, all elbows and audacity, and he appeared at the gunwale after dark as if materialized from the woodpile, drawn by the miracle of a stranger's flashlight. I showed him how the switch worked. He showed me how fast a flashlight battery dies when operated by a ten-year-old. Fair trade. His name was something I mangled twice and he gave up correcting. He spoke trade Spanish in bursts, the way children speak anything—entirely, then not at all.

His grandmother appeared at the top of the landing steps with the speed and silence of a woman who had been doing exactly this her whole life: extracting children from the vicinity of strangers. She took his wrist. He went, but at the last second he twisted around and fired one sentence back at me across the water, quick and low, the way you'd throw something you weren't supposed to be holding:

"The doctor gave them medicine and they gave nothing back."

Then he was gone up the steps, scolded in a language I didn't know, and the night closed over the whole transaction as if it hadn't happened.

I sat with that for a long time. *The doctor.* Ines Okafor was the expedition's botanist, but she'd trained in field medicine—her published papers mentioned clinic work during a cholera season, though I only knew that because I'd read everything my father's team had ever published, which is either devotion or illness, and I've stopped pretending to know which.

Medicine given. Nothing given back. In the arithmetic of the river, that was a debt—and debts here, I was beginning to understand, ran in directions the ledger-keepers downriver never imagined.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping, mist standing on the water in columns. I woke to the sound of Tomas arguing quietly with someone on the dock, which meant either trouble or commerce, and with Tomas those were often the same thing.

It was commerce. A woman was selling us fruit—plantains, three papayas, a bag of something citrus-adjacent—and refusing, with great firmness, to answer any question asked in the same breath as money changed hands. I tried anyway.

"The foreigners who went upriver. How many boats?"

She counted my coins twice, found them correct, and looked at me with mild, bottomless patience. "You want fish? Fish is good today."

"How long ago did they pass?"

"The fish is very good today."

I bought the fish. It seemed rude not to. It was, for the record, very good.

By midmorning I'd worked the landing the way I'd once worked a salvage site: systematically, patiently, reading what people showed me instead of what they told me. And they were showing me plenty, if you knew how to look.

A dugout beached above the flood line with a repair patch on its hull—a patch made from canvas, military green, the kind expedition boats carry. A child playing with a whistle carved from pale hardwood that no one in the village was carving, because the wood was ash, and ash doesn't grow here. A cooking pot of the exact dented aluminum pattern that outfitters sell in the capital by the gross, hanging in a fish rack, repurposed, its expedition stencil half-scrubbed away.

And under one stilt house, stacked neatly: four empty fuel drums, stamped with the logo of the Ferrand Institute's logistics contractor.

I stood there a moment doing math. Four drums, at standard consumption, meant roughly six hundred kilometers of range bought and burned. The falls country was four hundred upriver. Whoever had passed through had topped off here and gone on with full tanks—which meant they'd planned to go far, or planned not to need to come back this way soon.

Or planned not to come back at all.

"You shouldn't touch those."

The voice came from behind me, dry as August. The old man from the landing. Up close, in daylight, I could see he was older than I'd guessed—eighty, maybe more—and that his eyes had a yellowish cast that spoke of a lifetime of river light.

"I wasn't going to take one," I said.

"No. You were going to count them. You already did." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth I'd gotten in eighteen hours, and it wasn't warm. "Your father's son."

The words landed harder than he could have known. Or maybe he knew exactly. Maybe that was the point.

"You know who I am," I said.

"I know who Armand Delacroix is. Everyone on this river knows. He came through here eleven years ago with cameras and gifts and promises about a book." He spat, precisely, over the rail. "The book never came. The photographs went into magazines. Our faces went into magazines. The money stayed in the city."

"That wasn't—" I started, and stopped, because I genuinely didn't know whether it was true or not. Eleven years ago I hadn't spoken to my father. I still couldn't have told you what he promised anyone, because he'd stopped making promises to me around the same era, and I'd stopped listening before that.

"He paid the guides?" I asked instead.

"He paid the guides." A pause with weight in it. "He did not pay for the sickness."

"What sickness?"

The old man looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face—ignorance, probably, plain and unperformed—it seemed to decide something for him. Or against something. Hard to say which.

"Ask the doctor," he said. "If you find her alive."

* * *

We spent the afternoon pretending to prepare for departure and actually waiting, because I'd learned something about villages like this in the salvage camps: nobody tells you anything while you're leaving. Information is currency, and you spend it on people who are staying.

Tomas repacked the fuel lines. I checked the bilge, greased what needed grease, and watched the village watch me back. Toward late afternoon the mist burned off and the light went gold and horizontal, and the children came down to the landing again, braver now, orbiting the boat at a radius determined by some invisible treaty. The boy from last night was among them. He wouldn't meet my eye. Someone had talked to him. I could guess the content of the talk.

At dusk, the elder came down the steps again.

This time he brought another man with him—younger, broad, with a hunter's rolled sleeves and a machete worn to a silver crescent—and between them they carried nothing at all, which I noted, because everyone else who approached our boat carried something to sell or trade, and these two carried only themselves.

"Tomorrow you go upriver," the elder said.

"At first light," I agreed.

"The river rises early this year. The gorge will run high by the time you reach it. If you reach it." He delivered this the way a weather report is delivered: impersonal, factual, faintly bored by your plans. Then he looked past me, into the boat, at the space beneath the stern canvas where I kept the spare line and the tool roll.

And I swear to you, I watched him do it. Watched him see that I saw him do it. He set something down inside the gunwale with two fingers, unhurried, the way you'd leave a coin on a bar, and stepped back, and his face throughout was the face of a man handing you nothing whatsoever.

Then he and the hunter walked away, and the crowd on the bank dispersed to their fires, and I was left holding the evening and a small carved object.

It was hardwood, palm-sized, dense enough to feel like stone. Carved into it, wrapping around it in a continuous line, was a figure I spent a long time trying to name and finally had to describe honestly: a spiral that was also a mouth, or a mouth that was also a doorway, cut deep and rubbed with something dark so the lines read black against the pale wood. Around the rim, smaller marks—tally-like, deliberate, in groups I couldn't parse.

It was beautiful. It was also, unmistakably, a message, and the message was not *welcome*.

"Tomas," I said. "Come look at this."

He came. He held it under the lantern, turned it twice, and I watched his face do something complicated.

"You know it," I said.

"I've seen the design." He handed it back like it had gotten heavier in his hands. "On the rocks. Above the falls. When I ran Armand's boats, years ago—the older guides wouldn't paddle past that stretch without touching the water first. Saying words. I asked one of them what the carving meant." He paused, and I waited, because waiting for Tomas was a skill I was acquiring whether I wanted it or not.

"And?"

"And he said: *that is where the river remembers what was taken.*" Tomas shrugged, picked up his bamboo nail-cleaner, resumed work on a thumbnail. "Guides say a lot of things."

"Do you believe him?"

"I believe the river rises early this year," he said. "That's what I believe. Sleep. First light."

* * *

I didn't sleep well. The token sat on the crate beside my hammock all night like a small dense accusation, and every time I woke—one of the six or eight times a body does, out here—I saw it there in the dark and thought: *he left it unasked.*

That was the part that gnawed. Everything else about the village had been refusal—refusal to talk, refuse to guide, refuse to even lie to me, which would at least have been engagement. But the token was the opposite of refusal. It was an offering I hadn't requested, from a man who owed me nothing, in a place that had already been hurt by people exactly like me and mine.

Warnings you ask for are advice. Warnings you don't ask for are debts. He'd put me in his debt on purpose, and I lay in the hammock turning over why, and the best I could come up with was this: somewhere upriver was something that had hurt these people once—or hurt people they'd traded with, sheltered, married into—and the elder had looked at me, Armand Delacroix's son, and decided I might be the one person coming up this river who could still choose differently.

Or he'd decided I was doomed and the token was a funeral gift. With river people, I was learning, the two gestures can look identical.

First light came gray again, mist on the water, the village already awake around us—smoke, nets, the slap of someone washing clothes against a plank with metronomic patience. We loaded in silence. As Tomas untied the line, I looked up at the bank, at the crowd that had gathered to watch us go with those same closed faces, and I raised a hand.

Nobody raised one back. But the old man was there, at the top of the steps, and he touched two fingers to his chest—not a wave, something older than a wave—and held them there until we turned the bend and lost them.

We ran upstream into the narrowing channel, the banks closing in, the current stiffening against us like a hand pressed flat against the hull. An hour on, the boy appeared on a rock shelf above the water—he must have run along the bank, God knows how, ahead of us—and as we passed below him he cupped his hands and shouted one word down through the mist:

"*Falls!*"

Then the rock took him, and the river took the sound, and we went on into country where the maps went thin and the silence ahead of us had, I realized, already been speaking to me for two days—in refusals, in patched canvas, in ash-wood whistles, in a carved mouth dropped silently into my boat.

It had been saying: *turn back.*

And I had been answering, with every kilometer of fuel I burned: *no.*
