## 7. First Blood on the Water

The second boat appeared the way trouble always does in my experience: politely, at a distance, pretending to be something else.

We'd been on the water three days. Three days of heat that pressed down like a hand you couldn't shove off, of Tomas Reyes saying perhaps forty words total, most of them navigational and all of them correct. The river had widened into a brown ribbon stitched with sandbars, and the forest on either bank had settled into its long performance of indifference—herons stalking the shallows with the air of creditors, monkeys throwing commentary from the canopy at anything foolish enough to pass below.

I was dozing against the cargo when I noticed it: a smudge of wake two hundred meters back, holding our line exactly. Not gaining. Not falling behind. Matching.

"Tomas."

"I see it." He didn't turn his head. He'd been watching it for some time, apparently, and had decided I didn't need to know yet. This was, I would learn, his entire philosophy of information management.

"Fishermen?"

"Fishermen don't hold a line for an hour." He spat over the gunwale, a gesture that seemed less like habit than punctuation. "They've been there since the bend. Waiting for us to stop being interesting before they stop being careful."

I looked again. The smudge resolved into a shape—a long, low boat with an engine housing tarpaulined against glare, and figures aboard who were very still in the way people are still when they're working rather than resting.

"Could be surveyors," I said, because someone had to say it.

"Could be." Tomas adjusted the tiller a degree, no more. "Surveyors don't usually follow salvage men upriver past the last map that admits to existing. But sure. Surveyors."

* * *

That night we pulled into a side channel and killed the lamp early. No fire. We ate cold cassava and dried fish while mosquitoes conducted their audit of our exposed skin, and Tomas sat facing backward toward the main channel like a man listening to a conversation he'd heard before.

"You knew this might happen," I said.

He chewed. Swallowed. "I knew your father's expeditions attracted attention. Attention is what he collected. Gold, artifacts, glory—those were just how he kept score between collections." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "I told myself this time would be different because he wasn't even on the boat. Then I remembered the last time I said that about one of Armand Delacroix's projects."

"What happened last time?"

Tomas looked at me for a long moment. In the dark his face was mostly suggestion—a slope of cheekbone, the shine of an eye. "Ask him," he said, "if you find him alive enough to answer questions."

I let that sit. It deserved to sit. Somewhere across the channel, a caiman slid off a mudbank with a sound like a door closing in a house where nobody should be.

"Whoever they are," I said finally, "they haven't done anything yet. Maybe they're Ferrand's people making sure I don't get lost. She did give me the frequency. Maybe this is her version of concern."

"Concern has a face," Tomas said. "This has a tarp over its engine."

* * *

The next day they closed to a hundred meters, and by afternoon, fifty. Close enough now to count them: four men, maybe five, sun-darkened and deliberately unremarkable, the kind of faces designed to be described badly afterward. One of them had binoculars and used them without any pretense of looking at birds.

I raised a hand in greeting around midday. Nothing came back. That nothing traveled up my arm and lodged somewhere behind my sternum, where it stayed.

"They want us to know they're there," I said.

"Yes."

"Why? If you're going to attack someone, why announce yourself?"

Tomas considered this with the seriousness he gave everything except me. "Two reasons. Either they're not planning to attack—they're herding. Making sure we stay on the river, keep moving, keep predictable. Or—" he paused, and the pause had weight, "—they want us tired. A man who sleeps badly makes mistakes, and mistakes on this river are permanent."

"Wonderful. So we're being managed or we're being softened up."

"Now you understand expedition work." For the first time in three days, something that was almost humor crossed his face. It vanished quickly, embarrassed to have been seen.

We ran until the light went copper, then tied up in a narrows where the current ran fast enough to discourage casual approach. I took first watch, which meant I sat in the bow with a machete across my knees feeling like a child playing at soldiers, listening to the forest perform its nightly inventory of everything that wanted to eat me.

Around ten, I heard the engine.

It was far off—just a thread of sound woven into the river noise—but I knew it, the way you know a voice through a wall. It came closer, held steady somewhere downstream of us for perhaps twenty minutes, and withdrew. They were checking. Marking where we slept. Filing us away.

I woke Tomas. He listened, nodded once, and lay back down.

"Aren't you worried?"

"I'm already worried," he said. "Being surprised by it wouldn't improve the situation."

* * *

Dusk the following day came down through the canopy in stages, gold to copper to a bruised violet, and we were running late—Tomas had insisted on pushing for the bend below the escarpment before dark, which put us on open water as the light failed. Behind us, the second boat had stopped pretending. It was closing.

"Faster than us," Tomas said quietly. "Bigger engine. They've been saving it."

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes if we do nothing. Fifteen if they're eager." He glanced at the sky, then at the water ahead, and I watched him make a calculation I didn't have the data for. "There are rapids past the bend. Grade four, maybe five this time of year. Charts end before them. My chart ends before them."

"You've run them?"

"No."

"But you know them?"

"I know what my father told me, and he ran them twice, and he only drowned once." He said it flatly, and it took me a full three seconds to realize it was a joke, and another three to decide whether to laugh. I didn't. Neither did he. "Elodie. When I say blind, I mean blind. Dark water, standing waves, rocks that move every flood season. If I run it, we hold on and pray to whatever each of us keeps in reserve. If we don't run it—"

Behind us, a sound cracked across the water.

Not thunder. Thunder doesn't come with a muzzle flash, and thunder doesn't skip once off the surface a meter to port and go whining away into the trees like an angry insect with ambitions.

"Down!" Tomas was already flat, hauling the tiller hard, and I went down into the bilge water and fish smell with my ears ringing. A second shot. A third. Splinters jumped off the gunwale near my hand, close enough that I felt the wood bite the air beside my knuckles.

"They're shooting!" I shouted, which I grant was not analysis.

"They're shooting wide!" Tomas roared back. "Warning shots! They want us to stop!"

"And if we don't?"

Another shot answered me, this one lower, closer, punching a hole through the canvas awning with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The next one would not be a warning. Everyone on that boat understood the grammar of gunfire; we'd all just been hoping to fail the exam.

Tomas made his decision the way he made all decisions—without consulting anyone, including God.

"Hold onto something that loves you back!" he bellowed, and drove us at the bend.

* * *

What happened next, I can only report in fragments, because fear does not take notes. Fear grabs the pen out of your hand and shoves you under the table.

The river bent and the bottom fell out of the world. The escarpment rose black on both sides, funneling the whole river into a throat of white noise, and Tomas stood up in the boat—stood up, this madman, feet braced on the thwart, steering by a language of shadows and memory-of-memory—and we went in.

The first wave hit us like a building deciding to relocate. I remember cold. I remember the taste of the river, silt and rot and something mineral underneath, ancient, like drinking the floor of the world. I remember Tomas shouting words I couldn't parse—"LEFT! LEFT, YOU STUPID—" though whether at me, the boat, or the river itself remained unclear. I remember a rock the size of a chapel sliding past my shoulder close enough to touch, wet stone gleaming in the last violet light, and understanding with perfect clarity that if we touched it we would die, and touching nothing anyway, and not knowing whether that was skill or luck or the river simply declining us for the moment.

Somewhere behind us—I learned this later, piecing it together—the other boat hesitated at the lip of the rapids. Hesitation is a choice too. Their pilot looked at that water in the dark and declined the invitation. I can't blame him. I was on the invitation and I'd have declined it myself if anyone had asked.

We dropped. Twice more. The third drop turned us sideways and for one long, articulate second we surfed along the face of a standing wave, the whole forest wheeling overhead, stars smeared across the sky like thumbprints, and I thought—with a calm that still embarrasses me—*so this is where the trail goes silent, and it isn't even Father's fault.*

Then the wave let go, and the river caught us, and we were through.

* * *

We drifted in the aftermath for a long time. Below the rapids the water went strangely gentle, pooling in a wide reach where mist stood on the surface like the ghosts of everyone who hadn't made it. The engine was gone. The shots were gone. There was only the tick of water draining off the gear, my own breathing doing something arrhythmic, and Tomas sitting down at last, slowly, the way old men sit, though he wasn't old. Fear had aged him forty years in ninety seconds and he'd wear them off by morning.

"We lost them," I said.

"For tonight." He began checking the boat by feel—cargo lines, fuel drum, the wrapped bundle of instruments I'd brought. Everything accounted for. A hole in the awning. A gouge in the gunwale where something had kissed us in passing. "They'll run the portage track along the north bank and be below the falls by dawn. They know these rivers better than I hoped they would."

"Then we gained nothing."

"We gained the night." He found the water bag and drank, then passed it to me. My hands shook taking it. I noted the shaking with a kind of detached disgust, the way you note a stain on a good shirt. "And we gained this." He tapped the gouge in the wood. "Proof. Whatever they tell themselves they are—security, contractors, concerned friends of Madame Ferrand—men who fire on an unarmed boat on a public river are past the part where anyone asks permission. You understand what that means?"

I did. I'd been refusing to understand it for two days, carrying it around unopened like a letter with bad news. "This isn't a rescue anymore."

"No."

"It's a pursuit. We're not chasing Armand. We're being chased, and Armand is just the direction we happen to be fleeing in."

Tomas said nothing, which from Tomas was a full paragraph of agreement.

We tied up in a fold of the bank, deep in the shadow of the escarpment, and I took the watch again because sleep was a country I couldn't get visas for. The forest breathed around me. Bats stitched the dark above the water. And I sat there with the machete across my knees, doing arithmetic I hated: four or five men, rifles, a faster boat, and a financier in the capital whose helpfulness had been, all along, the politest form of threat I'd ever received.

She'd given me the frequency. She'd given me a warning. I'd thought she was testing whether I'd come.

She'd known I'd come. The boat had probably left the dock before I did.

Near dawn, half-dreaming against the cargo, I heard it again—that thread of radio static from the set I'd rigged, faint as breath. I crawled to it and cupped my ear to the speaker, and through the hiss came a voice. A woman's voice. Thin, broken by distance and interference, but unmistakably counting.

"...nine... eight... seven..."

Then static, thick and absolute, like a door of noise swinging shut.

I sat back in the grey light with my heart going like the rapids' echo, and woke Tomas with a hand on his shoulder.

"She's alive," I said. "Someone's alive, and they're counting down to something."

Tomas opened his eyes, and for once he had no answer ready at all.
