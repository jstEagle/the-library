## 8. Camp One, Empty and Wrong

The river told us we were close before anything else did.

Tomas cut the engine a full kilometer downstream and let us drift, and I thought at first it was caution—the same animal patience he'd shown since the shooting at the rapids—but then I smelled it too. Woodsmoke, old and cold. Charcoal that had finished its business days ago and was now just sitting in the air like an unpaid bill.

"Camp One," he said, as if announcing a funeral.

"You can tell from the smoke?"

"I can tell from the birds." He nodded upstream, where the canopy should have been loud with midmorning argument and wasn't. "Nothing nests near a place where men have been dying. Or leaving. Sometimes those are the same thing."

We tied off below a clay bank and walked the rest, which Tomas insisted on and I didn't argue with. There is a particular humility in approaching a place on foot that you could reach by boat—something about letting the ground feel your weight before you ask it for answers. My father taught me that, or rather I absorbed it by watching him do it, back when watching him was still the main occupation of my life. He used to say the river gives you everything twice: once in the water, once in the mud it leaves behind. I was eleven. I thought he meant it as poetry. Later I understood he meant it as method, and later than that I understood he mostly used poetry to avoid method, or method to avoid me, depending on the decade.

The camp announced itself through the trees the way ruin always does—not all at once but in pieces, each one worse than the last. First a torn strip of canvas hanging from a branch like a flag of surrender nobody had agreed to. Then a crate, split open, its contents picked clean. Then the clearing itself.

I've stood in ransacked places before. Salvage work attracts them; there's always some cousin of greed who arrives after the flood and goes through the drowned houses. So I know the grammar of it. What struck me about Camp One was not the destruction but its *order*. The tents hadn't been looted—they'd been opened. Slashed from the inside, every one of them, the blades cutting outward through the fabric in long deliberate strokes, as though whoever slept there had needed out quickly and hadn't cared what the tent thought about it. Sleeping rolls dragged into the open. Instruments laid in a row and then broken—not scattered, not thrown, but *broken*, one by one, a theodolite with its lens crushed by a boot heel, a rangefinder snapped at the hinge, a barometer whose glass face had been ground into the dirt until it read nothing forever.

"Somebody wanted the equipment dead," I said.

"Somebody wanted the *expedition* dead," Tomas said. He crouched over the theodolite without touching it. "Equipment you smash so no one can follow your measurements. Tents you cut from inside so no one thinks you were taken." He looked up at me. "Your father's people left this camp running, and they wanted it to look like they were never organized enough to leave anything behind."

I turned a slow circle. Fire pit cold, ash raked flat—raked, note that, someone had taken the time. A cooking table with its legs folded. And everywhere the small archaeology of haste: a tin cup half-buried, a boot print in dried mud going two directions and then only one, a scatter of rice gone to sprout in the rain, green threads stitching the ground where someone had dropped a bag and kept moving.

"How many?"

Tomas walked the perimeter the way he did everything, without wasted motion and without explaining himself, counting under his breath. "Eight, maybe nine sets of prints. Older ones underneath, going upriver. Newer ones coming back down." He stopped at the treeline and pointed with his chin. "And these."

These went off at an angle, away from the river, into the green. Two people, by the depth and spacing. One walking. One being helped—or carried partway and set down.

"Ines Okafor," I said. "The botanist. Ferrand's manifest listed her second."

"You remember the manifest better than you remembered your father's birthday."

"That's not fair."

"It isn't," he agreed, entirely without apology, and moved off toward the supply tent.

That's the thing about Tomas Reyes. He deals in debts the way other men deal in weather—constantly, personally, and with a conviction that everything is somebody's fault. His grievance against my father is fourteen years old and he has never once told me what it consists of, only that it exists, the way you might acknowledge a mountain. You plan your route around it. You don't expect it to explain itself.

* * *

The supply tent had been searched by professionals. I could tell because nothing was overturned; things had been lifted, examined, and replaced with a neatness that was somehow more frightening than wreckage. Someone had gone through Armand's stores item by item—tinned food (mostly taken), medical kit (emptied of morphine, left otherwise intact), survey records—

The survey records were what stopped me.

A steel document case sat on the folding table, unlocked, its lid open. Inside: the expedition logbook, official issue, stamped with the institute's crest, the one Ferrand's office would eventually demand returned. I knew the format. I'd grown up around it. Daily entries, coordinates, personnel notes, all in my father's small severe hand, the handwriting of a man who wrote as though the paper owed him money.

I read it standing up, which took ten minutes, and by minute four I had stopped feeling my hands.

The log was wrong.

Not damaged-wrong. Not incomplete-wrong. *Edited*-wrong. The entries marched forward day by day, tidy and bland—grid surveys, soil samples, a mention of Ines Okafor cataloguing riparian flora, a complaint about the humidity that sounded exactly like my father and was probably even true. But the dates had gaps. Small ones. Three days here, four there, bridged by entries that referenced work "continuing per schedule" in language so smooth it might have been ironed. And my father, whatever else he was—and the list of what else he was could fill a dock—was never smooth. His real field notes read like arguments. He fought with the terrain, with his instruments, with his own previous conclusions. These entries didn't fight anything. They just lay there.

"Tomas."

He came over. I handed him the book open to the gap and watched him read with the expression of a man confirming a suspicion he'd been carrying like a stone in his shoe.

"He falsified the log," I said.

"He falsified it *well*," Tomas said. "Which is worse. Amateurs lie badly. Professionals lie in a way that holds up until someone who knows the hand looks closely." He tapped the page. "This bridge entry? 'Survey continuing per schedule'? Your father has never in his life continued anything per schedule. He once held up an entire season because a potsherd disagreed with him."

Despite everything—despite the slashed tents and the dead birds and the two sets of prints walking into the forest—I almost smiled. Because it was true, and because for one moment the man in the handwriting was the man I remembered, difficult and exacting and alive.

Then I found the loose page.

It had been tucked inside the back cover of the logbook, folded in quarters, the kind of fold you make when you intend to come back for something and aren't sure you will. It was ledger paper, expedition stock, and it was covered in my father's hand—but the *other* hand, the fighting one, the margins crowded with corrections and a coffee ring and one long diagonal streak where the pen had been dragged in frustration or hurry.

I'll set it down here as nearly as I can, because I have read it perhaps four hundred times since and it has not improved with familiarity:

*Day 41. The falls. Confirmed. Below the third cascade, behind the curtain—structural void, dressed stone, pre-colonial, older than anything in the literature. Older than anything in ANY literature. Sealed from the inside originally; we've opened a working breach only. Contents appear intact. This changes the ownership question for the entire valley. EVERYTHING hinges on who reports first and how. Must control the record. F. cannot see this entry. Recopying official log accordingly — A.D.*

*F.* I didn't need a decoder ring. Odile Ferrand had signed the financing contract; her initials were on every page of it. I'd met her for eleven minutes in the capital and she had made more impression on me than most people manage in eleven years—a woman built entirely of competence and cold storage, who had given me a radio frequency and a warning in the same breath, and I still couldn't tell you which one had been the kindness.

But it was the date that did the damage. Day 41. The official log's first mention of "anomalous stonework near the upper falls, possibly natural formation"—the sentence on which the entire sanctioned narrative of the expedition rested—was dated Day 58. Seventeen days later. Seventeen days during which my father had known exactly what he'd found, had written it down in furious exultation, and had then sat down and fabricated a parallel history of his own expedition to keep it dark.

He hadn't discovered something and hidden it in panic. He had discovered something and hidden it in *method*. There was a plan in that handwriting. There was a strategy. There was a man weighing who to deceive and settling on everyone—Ferrand, the institute, the national registry, the colleagues who'd have given a decade to stand where he stood, and, incidentally, the son who hadn't spoken to him in years and therefore, presumably, didn't count among the people owed the truth.

I sat down on a supply crate that may or may not have been the same kind of crate I'd sat on as a boy in his first field camp, and I felt something I want to describe accurately, because I'm writing this in ink and ink deserves accuracy. It wasn't surprise. Some part of me had been waiting for this since the telegram, since the frequency, since the village that turned its backs. It was more like recognition. The specific, nauseating recognition of a family trait surfacing in a parent's handwriting. Because I have lied in logs too. Small ones. Salvage manifests, salvage valuations, the little fictions that keep a lowland dig funded one more season. I told myself mine were different—survival lies, lies with receipts. Sitting in his camp, reading his forgery, I understood that his probably came with receipts too.

"Tomas," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "How much did you know?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. That's another thing about him; he wastes nothing, including denials. "I knew he found something big. I knew he went strange about it. Fourteen years ago he found a site upriver and within a month three men in institute suits were offering to buy his notes, and he burned the notes instead, and after that he stopped trusting institutions." Tomas looked at the ledger page without reaching for it. "I didn't know about this. But I'm not surprised, and I notice you're not either."

"I'm furious."

"Yes. Furious and not surprised. Those go together in families."

I folded the page along its original creases and put it inside my shirt, against the skin, which is where I keep documents I don't yet trust myself to be reasonable about. The paper was warm immediately, as if it had opinions about body heat.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing the work anyway, because that's the discipline my father beat into the profession whether he beat it into me or not: when a site is wrong, you document why it's wrong. Photographs of the tents, numbered. Measurements of the blade cuts—I counted forty-one slashes across six shelters, all outward, all consistent with a machete drawn fast by someone kneeling. Rubbings of the boot prints heading inland. An inventory of what was taken versus what was destroyed, which told its own quiet story: they'd taken medicine, food, ammunition, and the portable radio's battery pack, and they'd destroyed every instrument capable of fixing a position. Whoever left Camp One had stripped themselves for travel and blinded anyone following.

"They knew someone would come," I said.

"Everyone knows someone comes," Tomas said. "In this country, the only question is whether they arrive as help or as bill collectors."

Near dusk I made the find that rearranged the whole day. It was nothing—a scrap, really. Wedged under the collapsed leg of the instrument table, a corner of graph paper, water-stained, bearing a fragment of my father's hand: *...cannot hold both positions. If I send word ahead she will simply move faster. Tell E—*

Tell E. Tell Elodie. The sentence broke off there, eaten by water or torn deliberately, and I sat with it while the light went bronze and then gray, turning the two words over like stones. Tell Elodie what? That he was coming for me? That he needed me? That he was sorry? Twenty years of silence and the man leaves me half a sentence in a ruined camp, addressed to no one, delivered by accident.

"You hope it means he wants you there," Tomas said from the fire he was building. He said it the way he said most true things—without looking at me, pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no farther.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it means he needs you, which is the same thing wearing different clothes. Men like your father don't distinguish."

The fire caught. We ate rice and tinned fish from our own stores, because neither of us trusted the camp's, and the darkness came down the way it does here, completely and without negotiation. Somewhere across the river something large entered the water. The birds stayed silent. The ash stayed cold.

I lay in the open under a net, unable to sleep, doing arithmetic I didn't like. A father who forged his own log seventeen days deep. A financier whose initials appeared in his private notes as a threat. A team that had cut its way out of its own tents and walked into the forest carrying a wounded woman. And somewhere upriver, past the falls, a door under the water that changed who owned a valley—and freshly, recently, according to no evidence I had yet but every instinct I possessed, *opened*.

I was still turning that instinct over, trying to decide whether it was evidence or just fear wearing a compass, when Tomas's hand closed on my shoulder in the dark.

"Listen," he whispered.

At first there was nothing. Then, from the treeline where the two sets of prints had vanished hours ago—a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, I suspect, however long the rest of it runs. A voice. A woman's voice, cracked with fever and distance, saying the same thing over and over, patient as water:

"The door. The door under the falls. Don't let them open the door."

She was walking toward our fire. She was not stopping.
