## 9. The Survivor Who Won't Stop Walking

She came out of the treeline the way fever comes out of a body—gradually, then all at once, and never in a shape you'd have chosen.

Tomas saw her first. He didn't shout. He did what he always did when something appeared on the river: he went quiet and still, the way water goes quiet over something deep enough to drown you. Then he said, "Elodie," which was his way of shouting.

I turned and there she was on the mudbank, forty meters downstream, walking.

Not staggering. Not crawling. *Walking*—with the terrible upright purpose of someone whose body has stopped taking her instructions but is too polite to mention it. She wore what had once been an expedition shirt, khaki gone green with mold at the seams, and one boot. The other foot was wrapped in strips of something that might have started life as a bandage or a shirt sleeve; either way it had lost the argument with the jungle some days ago. She walked along the bank as if she were late for a meeting that had been canceled weeks ago.

"Ines Okafor," I said, before I could stop myself. Her face was on the expedition manifest Ferrand's people had given me, third from the top, under a photograph of a woman grinning like the camera owed her money. Botanist. Thirty-four. Next of kin: mother, Lagos.

"You know her?" Tomas asked.

"I know her file."

"Files don't walk out of the bush." He swung the boat toward the bank without being asked, which from Tomas was practically a hug.

We grounded the bow and I waded out, and here is the thing I will remember about that moment for the rest of my life: she didn't stop. I stood directly in her path, close enough to see that her lips were cracked into gray continents and her eyes had the glassy shine of river stones, and she simply turned, adjusted her course by two degrees, and kept walking around me. As if I were furniture. As if I were weather.

"Ines," I said. "Ines Okafor. My name is Elodie Delacroix. Armand Delacroix is my father. I'm looking for his expedition."

Something moved behind her eyes. A fish under ice. "The door," she said.

"What door?"

"The door under the falls." Her voice was sandpaper dragged across wet wood. "It has to stay shut. Someone has to count. Nobody's counting anymore."

"Tomas!" I called. "Blanket, water, the kit!"

"She needs a hospital," he said, already moving.

"She needs to not be walking," I said, and caught her arm.

What happened next I can only describe as being fought by a ghost. She had no strength left—I could feel the architecture of her shoulder through the shirt, all cable and no meat—and yet she twisted free of me with a practiced, furious economy, stumbled, caught herself, and kept going. Toward nothing. Upstream, if anything, back the way we'd come, deeper into country that had already eaten her team.

"Stop," I said. "Please. We're here to help you."

"No," she said, conversationally, as if declining a second cup of coffee. And then, after three more steps: "You have to go home. All of you. Everyone has to go home."

* * *

Fever has its own grammar. I learned some of it that afternoon, kneeling in the boat while Tomas held her down—not cruelly, the way you hold a hatch closed in a storm—and I forced water and quinine into a woman who believed, with total conviction, that drinking was a betrayal of some duty only she understood.

The numbers were the worst part. She counted them under her breath, always descending. Forty-one. Forty. Thirty-nine. When she reached thirty she would start again at forty-one, and if you interrupted her mid-count she looked at you with such naked anguish that I found myself apologizing. To a delirious woman. For interrupting arithmetic.

"What's she counting?" Tomas asked.

"If I knew that, I'd know where my father is."

He grunted. Tomas's grunts are a complete language; this one meant *you're lying to yourself, boy*, a phrase he communicated in grunt form roughly six times a day.

By dusk the quinine had done its blunt work. The fever broke—not gone, but beaten below the waterline, the way a storm drops behind a ridge and keeps raining there. She slept in the boat, wedged between cargo crates, and I sat up watching the firelight move on her face and felt the two things I'd been carrying since the telegram finally collide: the pity, and the frustration. Pity because she had walked out of hell on one boot. Frustration because every hour she couldn't speak was an hour Brac's boat gained on us, and because some small, shameful part of me—the part that had spent twenty years learning not to need my father—was furious at her for surviving and then having the bad manners to be unconscious about it.

Around midnight her eyes opened. Clear ones, this time. Brown, level, appallingly intelligent.

"You're the son," she said.

"Yes."

"He talks about you." A pause while she worked moisture into her mouth. "Past tense. 'My son used to'—that's how he says it. Used to dig. Used to argue. Used to call." She watched me flinch and didn't pretend not to notice. "He does it when he thinks no one's listening. Which means he does it constantly, because Armand Delacroix thinks everyone is deaf except himself."

"That sounds like him."

"It is him." She tried to sit up, failed, settled for propping herself against the crate. "Give me your canteen."

I gave it. She drank half of it in one long pull, and I watched her throat work and thought about how many days of discipline it must have taken to ration herself downriver, alone, one boot, counting numbers to stay sane.

"Now," she said, handing it back. "Ask me what you've been dying to ask since the bank."

"The camp. Camp One. What happened to it?"

"Men came by boat. Not soldiers—soldiers would've been better. Soldiers follow rules you can read." Her voice was steady, but her hands, I noticed, had found each other in her lap and were holding on. "They wanted what your father found. He wouldn't tell them. So they made examples. They cut the tents open from inside—" she saw my face and nodded grimly, "—yes, from inside, so anyone finding the camp later would think something got *in*. That it was animals. That it was the river. They're careful men. The quiet one especially. Brac."

"You know his name."

"He introduced himself. Politely. While two of his men held Lukas Brand's head over the fire." She closed her eyes. "Brand talked. Of course he talked—he's a surveyor, not a martyr. But he didn't know anything worth telling, so they left him alive as a message. And they took Amara and Duval onto their boat, and Ines—" she opened her eyes again, and now the steadiness cost her visibly, "—Ines ran. Ines ran into the forest like a coward and walked for nine days counting backward from forty-one because her grandmother taught her that a mind with a job doesn't have room to scream."

"That's not cowardice," I said. "That's the bravest thing I've heard all month."

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard. "Don't spend your kindness on me. Spend it on getting off this river. Because I am going to say this once, Elodie Delacroix, while I'm lucid enough to mean it." She leaned forward, gripping the gunwale, and her fever-bright eyes fixed on mine with a force that made the fire seem dim. "Turn the boat around. Go home. Take me if you want, take Brand if you find him, take whatever's left of this disaster—but do not go up to those falls. Whatever your father sealed there, it stays sealed. Not because of what's inside. Because of who wants it."

"Ferrand."

"You've met her." It wasn't a question. "Then you understand faster than your father did. He thought he could hide a cathedral under a leaf. She sent eight men and a man named Brac to lift the leaf." She sagged back against the crate, and I watched the fever climb back into her face like water rising in a lock. "Go home," she whispered. "Everyone just... go home."

Then the numbers took her again. Forty-one. Forty. Thirty-nine.

I sat there until dawn, listening to her count, and did not turn the boat around.

* * *

From the inside, fever is not heat. People imagine heat, and they are wrong. Fever is a country. You wake up in it already a citizen, with laws you've always known and cannot question, and the laws are simple:

Someone must count. If the counting stops, the door opens.

I knew, even in the country of the fever, that this was nonsense. I knew the door was a stone mouth behind falling water, that I had seen it with my own eyes on the morning everything ended, that stones do not open and close like shopkeepers. But knowing is a passport, not a pardon. The law held anyway: count, or the door opens, and if the door opens—

If the door opens, the valley changes hands.

That was the part I could never explain to the healthy. Not the gold—they'd understand the gold, gold is a language everyone speaks badly. The documents. Papers older than any border on any map, saying in ink that has not faded that this valley was never sold, never ceded, never signed away, that every treaty in the archives downstream is built on a forgery or a silence. Men have burned cities for less. A woman like Odile Ferrand would burn a river.

So I counted. Forty-one. Forty. Thirty-nine. My grandmother's trick, taught to me at seven, on a night in Lagos when the lights failed and the storms came off the water and I was certain the world was ending: *a mind with a job has no room to scream.* She gave me other things too, my grandmother. She gave me the habit of naming plants to calm myself, which is why I became a botanist, which is why I was on that boat, which is why I am alive to write this—which is a chain of causes I try not to examine too closely, because somewhere in it sits the corpse of a woman who deserved better than a granddaughter who ran.

Because that's the truth of it. I ran. Nine days ago I stood in the shallows below the falls and watched Brac's boat take Amara and Duval, and I did the arithmetic every survivor does and hates themselves for: two of them, one of me, the forest right there. I ran. And the running saved me, and the saving damns me, and both facts live in my chest like birds in a cage, pecking.

When I surfaced from the fever country, there was a young man watching me. Delacroix's son, though you'd never guess it from the face—too much of his mother in it, thank God, all sharpness where Armand is granite. He had the look of a man who hadn't slept and had spent the night losing an argument with himself. I recognized the look. I'd been wearing it for nine days.

"You didn't turn around," I said.

"No," he said.

"I told you what's upstream."

"You told me a woman named Ferrand wants what my father found. You told me her man burns camps and drowns surveyors near fires. You told me to go home." He handed me the canteen again, and broth this time, hot, salty, real. "Here's what you didn't tell me. Where my father is. Whether he's alive. What's actually behind the door. And why a botanist counts backward from forty-one instead of forward."

"Forward is waiting for something," I said. "Backward is bringing something home. Grandmother's rule."

He almost smiled. It was strange, seeing the family resemblance arrive all at once like that—in the almost. "Ines. I'm not going to pretend I'm a good man on a mission. Half the reason I'm on this river is a telegram and twenty years of unfinished sentences. But I'm going up to those falls whether you guide me or not. So you can lie in this boat counting until your legs work again, or you can come with me and make sure I don't do something stupid with whatever we find." He paused. "And I'll get you home. That part I promise. Every living member of that expedition goes home. That's the whole reason I came."

I looked at him for a long time. Fever makes you honest, they say, but I've found the opposite—it's exhaustion that does it, the specific honesty of a body that has no budget left for performance.

"You sound like him," I said. "The promise part. He said the same thing, the day we found the vault. 'Nobody gets left on this river.' And then he sealed the door and lied in his own logbook and got most of us killed trying to keep a secret none of us agreed to keep."

The almost-smile died on his face. Good, I thought. Let it die. Let it die now, upriver of everything, rather than later, downstream of worse.

"So here is my price for guiding you," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and clear, the voice I use for specimen labels, for facts. "You swear to me—not to your father, not to your pride, to me—that whatever is behind that door, you'll decide with open eyes. Not bury it because he told you to. Not sell it because she'll pay you to. Look at it, and choose."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"Then you'll be the third Delacroix-shaped mistake this valley has survived," I said, "and I'll count backward from forty-one about you, too."

He laughed—one short, surprised bark, gone as fast as it came—and I realized it was probably the first laugh this boat had carried since the capital.

Behind us, far downstream, or maybe imagined, or maybe neither, a motor coughed once against the current and went quiet.

Tomas heard it too. I saw it in his shoulders.

"We should move," he said.
