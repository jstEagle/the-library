## 10. What Ines Saw at the Falls

Fever is a liar with excellent diction.

That's what I kept telling myself in the days after Tomas and Elodie pulled me out of the green, when my skin burned and my thoughts arrived pre-arranged into sentences I hadn't chosen. Fever told me I was still walking. Fever told me the river was a corridor and every door along it was locked. Fever told me, once, with great authority, that my mother was waiting at the end of it with rice and scolding, and I wept like a child until the quinine took hold and I remembered she had been dead eleven years and had never once waited for anybody.

So you will understand why nobody pressed me for answers right away. They fed me broth. They let me sleep. Elodie Delacroix sat across the fire from me for two full days saying nothing at all, which I came to understand was his native language, and which I found — I'll admit it here, where he can't hear me — less annoying than I expected. Most men who say nothing are composing speeches. He seemed to be simply enduring the silence, the way you endure weather.

On the third evening, when the fever had broken and left me feeling wrung out and faintly embarrassed, like a woman caught singing in public, he finally asked.

"Ines. What happened up there?"

And because fever is a liar but honesty is its own kind of illness, one I've carried since childhood, I told him.

* * *

"You have to understand what your father was like before," I said. "Not the man in the newspapers. Not even the man you remember, if you ever knew him at all. The man in the field."

Elodie didn't flinch at that, though I saw the words land somewhere below his ribs. Good. I hadn't survived a month of walking through hell to be gentle.

"We were nineteen days above the last village on any map worth the paper. Nineteen days of portage and leeches and Lukas Brand complaining about humidity as if the humidity were a personal enemy he intended to sue." I wrapped my hands around the tin cup someone had filled — Elodie's, I think; he was careful about small kindnesses and careless about large ones. "Your father loved it. That's the thing nobody tells you about brilliant men. They love the work more than they love anything the work produces. Armand would sit up past midnight just looking at his survey maps, not annotating them, just *looking*, the way other men look at photographs of their children."

"Did he talk about me?" Elodie asked. Then, immediately: "Forget it. It doesn't matter."

"It matters," I said, "but not tonight. Tonight you get the falls."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downriver a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and Tomas, who had been pretending to mend a net, went very still. He'd earned the right to listen too. Everyone who'd bled on this river had.

"We heard it before we saw it. A sound like the sky clearing its throat. Brand said it was thunder; your father said no, thunder doesn't repeat itself on a schedule. We cut inland through the rainforest for two days — two days, Mr. Delacroix, of cutting vine with machetes while the air tried to drown us standing upright — and then the trees simply gave up, the way trees do, all at once, and there it was."

I closed my eyes. I could see it still, fever or no fever: the whole river gathering itself and throwing itself off a shelf of black stone into a gorge so deep the spray rose back up out of it like breath. And behind the water —

"There's a seam in the cliff," I said. "Behind the curtain of the fall. Your father saw it from the far bank through his glass — a line too straight to be geology. Straight lines are either God or people, and God doesn't build in this valley."

"And he went in."

"He went in the way he did everything. First alone, then with me, because I was the only one whose hands he trusted to be steady and whose mouth he trusted to stay shut. The second part should have warned me." I looked at him over the rim of the cup. "You want to know what's under the falls. Everyone wants to know. But knowing is the cheap part. Let me tell you instead what happened *after* the knowing, because that's the part that got people killed."

* * *

The chamber behind the water was dry. That was the first miracle — the falls roared past the entrance and the air inside hung still and cool as a cellar, as if the stone had been holding its breath for five hundred years.

"The floor was paved," I told Elodie. "Cut stone, fitted so close you couldn't slide a knife between the blocks. And the walls —" I stopped, because the walls deserved better than a tired woman's summary. "There were figures carved into them. Processions. Hundreds of figures, each one different, and every figure carrying something — a seed, a child, a bundle of reeds, a flame. It wasn't decoration. It was a record. Somebody had written their entire world onto those walls in people instead of words."

"Pre-colonial," Elodie said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something heavy.

"Older than anything anyone has catalogued in this basin. Older than the river kingdoms. Your father dated the paving by the silt line and sat down on the floor of a five-hundred-year-old room and laughed. Actually laughed. I'd worked with him fourteen months and I'd never heard him laugh."

"And the artifacts?"

I turned the cup in my hands. "Crates. Sealed with resin, stacked against the far wall like the people who built the place meant to come back. Textiles that hadn't rotted. Metalwork — gold, but worked in styles no museum has a drawer for. And documents, Mr. Delacroix. Bark-paper documents, preserved in sealed tubes. Your father could read enough of the script to know what they were before he could read what they said."

"What did they say?"

"That's the question," I said, "isn't it? That's always the question. And here is where I need you to stop thinking like an archaeologist's son and start thinking like a lawyer, because that's what saved our lives and damned everything else."

* * *

Armand spent six days in that chamber. Six days photographing, sketching, cataloguing — and three of those six days reading. On the seventh day he came out of the falls looking like a man who had aged a decade and lost twenty pounds of certainty, and he called a meeting of the senior staff around the cook fire, and he told us the truth.

Or most of it.

"The documents are deeds," I said now, watching Elodie's face. "Land records. Treaties of cession — or rather, treaties of *non*-cession. The valley was never sold. Never signed away. Every colonial claim, every concession, every title that Ferrand's institute has been quietly acquiring options on for the last eight years rests on paperwork that these stones prove is fiction. The valley belongs — legally, provably, in ink older than any European archive — to the peoples who never agreed to leave it."

Elodie set his cup down very slowly. "That's not a discovery. That's a bomb."

"Yes," I said. "And your father was holding it when he realized the fuse was already lit."

Because here was the arithmetic Armand had done in the dark behind the waterfall, alone, with his beautiful maps spread out around him: Odile Ferrand's contract. The one every member of the expedition had signed without reading past the salary line. Claim rights. If the expedition documented a find of significant cultural or monetary value, the institute — meaning Ferrand, meaning whoever Ferrand actually answered to — held first claim on the materials, the site, and the publication rights. Everything would pass through her hands. The documents proving the valley had never been ceded would arrive in the capital inside crates owned by the very people profiting from the cession.

"She wouldn't destroy them," Elodie said. "She's not stupid. Documents like that —"

"— are leverage," I finished. "Exactly. She wouldn't burn them. She'd bury them. File them. Trade them, piece by piece, to whichever government or company needed the valley's history to stay negotiable. Or she'd release just enough to crash the price of land she'd already bought cheap. Your father understood within a week that the find was real, and within a week after that that the find, delivered honestly, would change nothing except make Ferrand richer and the valley's people poorer. Truth in the wrong hands isn't truth. It's inventory."

The fire had burned low. Across the camp, Brand was awake in his hammock — Brand was always awake; the jungle had gotten into him like a splinter — and I lowered my voice anyway.

"So he sealed it," Elodie said. Not a question. "He put the crates back, closed the door, and falsified the log."

"He sealed it and he lied," I said. "To Ferrand, in her coded telegrams — 'season poor, finds minor, recommend withdrawal.' To the team, mostly by silence. To the museum, by omission. And yes, to himself, if lying to yourself counts, and in my experience it's the only kind that always counts." I leaned forward. "But hear the rest, because everyone will want to paint him one color and he is not one color. He didn't seal it to keep it for himself. There was no private cache plan, no secret deal. He sealed it to keep it from *her*. From the contract. He used to say —" and here my throat did something embarrassing, so I paused and drank cold tea before continuing — "he used to say that some truths have to be carried carefully, like a full cup across a crowded room, and that spilling them isn't honesty, it's just spillage."

"And you believed him."

"I wanted to believe him," I said. "Which is worse, and I know it. Loyalty feels exactly like integrity from the inside, Mr. Delacroix. Same warmth. Same righteousness. It's only from the outside that anyone can tell the difference, and none of us were outside. We were his team. We were *his*."

* * *

What broke it open — what turned a secret into a catastrophe — was small, the way all catastrophes are small at the beginning. A radio schedule missed. A supply boat early. Brac's men moving equipment that had no business being moved. And then one evening I came back from collecting specimens and found Armand burning papers in the cook fire, and the look on his face was the look of a man who has discovered the lock he installed has been picking itself from the other side.

"Ferrand knew," I told Elodie. "Maybe not the location. Maybe not yet. But she knew there was something worth hiding, because your father was a bad liar and getting worse. After that it was only a question of whether we could get out ahead of her security team, and we couldn't. You've seen how that ended. Tents cut from the inside. Instruments smashed. Me walking south with a fever and a head full of falling water."

Elodie was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had that flatness again, but underneath it something was moving, the way current moves under ice.

"My father always said the record belongs to everyone or it belongs to no one. He wrote that. Published it. Built a career on it." He looked into the fire. "And then he found the one thing that proved it, and he put it in a box behind a waterfall because the world wasn't ready."

"No," I said. "Because *he* wasn't ready. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole story." I set down the cup. "Loyalty to a man and loyalty to the truth feel identical right up until the moment they point in opposite directions. Your father chose the man — himself, the version of himself he needed to be. I stood in that firelight and said nothing, which means I chose him too. Don't ask me whether I'd choose differently now. Ask yourself what you'll do when it's your turn, because it's coming. It comes for everyone who carries a full cup."

He didn't answer. He was staring at the fire with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it on his father's face behind the falls: the expression of a man doing arithmetic with things that shouldn't be countable.

* * *

It was Tomas who ended the evening, and not gently. He'd given up the pretense of mending the net and sat forward into the firelight, and when he spoke his voice had the gravel of a man who had been rehearsing silence for days.

"The boat," he said. "Downstream. Running dark."

We all heard it then — or rather we heard the absence, the gap in the river's noise where an engine note should have been, and the engine note arriving to fill it. Far down the black water, a light blinked twice and went out.

Elodie was on his feet before I finished standing. "How far?"

"Too far to identify. Close enough that they aren't lost." Tomas was already moving toward the boats, and over his shoulder he added, in the tone of a man stating weather: "Nobody runs this stretch at night unless they know it. And nobody knows it except us and the dead."

I looked at Elodie Delacroix, standing between the fire and the dark, and I thought: there it is. The turn. Loyalty or truth, son — pick fast.

The light downstream blinked again. Twice. Patient as arithmetic.
