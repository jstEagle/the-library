## 11. Ferrand's Second Boat

The morning after the rapids, we woke to the sound of an engine that wasn't ours.

I want to say I leapt up with a soldier's instincts, heart already calculating windage and escape routes. What actually happened was that I lay in the bottom of Tomas's boat with my cheek against a coil of rope and thought, with real irritation, *can't a man be shot at one day and sleep in the next?* Grief and fear are exhausting in ways no dig ever prepared me for. Archaeology teaches you patience; it does not teach you what to do when patience might get you killed.

Tomas was already awake, of course. He had been awake, I suspected, since before dawn, sitting motionless in the stern like a heron deciding whether the water was worth the trouble.

"Engine," he said, by way of good morning.

"I heard."

"You didn't hear. You heard a boat. There is a difference." He tilted his head, listening the way other men read. "Long shaft. Two-stroke, running rich on the low end. Not a trading boat. Trading boats are maintained badly and loved loudly. This one is maintained well and running quiet." He spat over the side, a gesture I'd come to understand as his version of punctuation. "Somebody who doesn't want to be heard until it matters."

Ines stirred under the tarp amidships, feverish but sleeping, which was more than she'd managed two nights ago. The quinine was doing its slow work. Brand sat at the bow with his rifle across his knees, and when our eyes met he gave me a look that said, plainly, *I told you so*, though as far as I could recall he hadn't actually told me so about anything specific. With Brand, the telling-so was ambient. A weather system of vindication.

"Options," I said to Tomas.

"Run, hide, or meet them."

"That's not options, that's a menu."

"A menu is options." He shrugged his bad shoulder and winced, and I remembered the portage, the way he'd kept working long after the shoulder stopped being a shoulder and became a negotiation between bone and stubbornness. "Running, they are faster. Hiding, this river has maybe four places to hide and they know all of them, because whoever runs that engine has run this river before. So."

"So we meet them."

"We *see* them first," Tomas corrected. "Meeting implies both parties agreed."

We tucked into a side channel where the flooded forest came down to the water — a green corridor of buttress roots and hanging vines, the kind of place where the river forgets itself. Tomas killed our engine and poled us into the shadow of a fallen ceiba, and we waited. Insects conducted their usual audit of my exposed skin. Somewhere overhead a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and I found myself thinking absurdly of my father's study, the brass fittings, the way he'd oil the hinges himself rather than let a servant hear the house complain. Twenty years of silence between us, and here I was, hiding from men with guns, thinking about door hinges. The mind protects itself how it can.

The second boat came around the bend at half past nine.

It was everything Tomas had promised and worse. A long aluminum hull, military surplus or something dressed to look like it, riding low with weight. Canvas over the midsection. Four men visible, plus the pilot, and a fifth figure standing at the bow who did not move the entire time the boat passed us — not to shift his weight, not to shield his eyes, not even, as far as I could tell, to breathe. He was a big man built out of rectangles, wearing a canvas hat pulled low, and there was a stillness about him that I recognized from somewhere before I placed it: museum storage. The stillness of things that are valuable and know it. Crated antiquities have exactly that quality. So did he.

"Tomas," I breathed. "Who—"

"No," he said quietly. "Don't name him yet. Naming comes later, when you know what a thing is."

The boat slid past our channel mouth without slowing, without searching, without any of the behavior of men looking for someone. Which frightened me more than a search would have. Men who search don't know where you are. Men who don't search have decided they already do.

They passed. The engine note faded upstream, toward the falls, toward my father.

Then Brand ruined everything, because Brand was going to ruin something eventually; it was simply a question of which day. He'd spotted, lashed to the stern of the passing boat, a dinghy — a small rubber tender, half-deflated, dragging on a line. And Brand, whose paranoia had a certain genius to it, whispered: "That tender is worth more than my life right now. It means they're planning to send men ashore somewhere. It means they'll split up." A pause. "It also means that if we follow the tender line back to wherever they've staged from, there may be supplies. Radios. Papers."

"Papers," I repeated.

"Organizations like that write everything down," Brand said. "It's how they protect themselves from each other. I worked for organizations like that for eleven years." He said it the way you'd confess to a venereal disease.

And that is how, an hour later, three grown men came to be committing what I can only describe as petty theft with extra steps, poling silently along a game trail that doubled as a creek bed toward a sandbar camp where Ferrand's security had cached equipment. We found it exactly as Brand predicted: a tarp shelter, fuel drums, crates, a cook fire cold since morning, and — unattended, because the tender meant they'd split their force and taken most of it upstream — a radio set in a green metal case, a document pouch, and a man.

The man was young. That's the detail that stays with me. Nineteen, maybe twenty, asleep on his watch with a shotgun across his lap, sunburned, chapped-lipped, with a paperback novel gone soft with humidity splayed open on his chest. He looked like every deckhand I'd ever hired and fired. He looked like me at nineteen, if my nineteen had involved less university and more river.

Tomas disarmed him before the boy finished inhaling. It was almost gentle. One moment the shotgun was there; the next it was leaning against a tree ten feet away, and Tomas had a hand flat on the boy's chest, pressing him back down into the sand, and was saying, in accented but fluent river-Spanish, "Stay. Sleep is safer than being brave."

The boy stayed. His eyes were enormous. I knelt where he could see me and held up the document pouch.

"I'm taking these," I said. "Not the food, not the fuel. If your people ask, tell them a tall man with bad taste in hats robbed you while you dreamed about whatever's in that book."

He glanced down at the paperback. "It's about pirates," he said, absurdly.

"Appropriate," I said, and felt sick doing it, because robbing children was now something I did.

* * *

We took the radio and the papers back to the boat and read them in the shade while Ines slept and the boy — left with a canteen and an apology — presumably composed his own report.

The documents were mundane, which is what made them terrible. Waybills. A supply manifest listing, among ordinary provisions, items that did not belong on any legitimate expedition: two hundred rounds of buckshot, a crate marked MEDICAL that the manifest honestly admitted contained restraints, and — this was the entry I read three times — "compensation reserve, discretionary, per F.F. instruction." Money set aside for something. For whom, and for what, the manifest declined to say.

But the radio was worse, because radios talk.

Brand got it powered up — of course he did; men like Brand always have one competence that surprises you — and found the set tuned to a working frequency, squelch hissing, and then, at intervals, traffic. Routine check-ins. Position reports. And then, late in the afternoon, a voice I will hear for the rest of my life.

It was a quiet voice. That's the thing nobody tells you about evil in the field; it rarely raises its volume. The voice said: "Brac, confirm artifact recovery timeline." A pause, static. Then Brac's voice — and yes, it was the still man from the bow; I'd have known that flatness anywhere — saying only, "Timeline holds. Falls by week's end."

Then the quiet voice again, and this is where I stopped feeling like an adventurer and started feeling like a man standing in a burning house:

"And the personnel?"

Another pause. Long enough that I leaned toward the radio as if proximity could change the answer.

"Recovery first," Brac said. "Personnel disposition per original instruction."

"Confirm 'original instruction' explicitly, Brac. The institute wants it in the log."

A silence with weight in it. Even through static, even through cheap speakers, I heard a man choosing his words the way you choose footing on scree.

"Recover the artifacts," Brac said. "Leave no witnesses."

The transmission ended. The squelch hissed. Nobody on our boat moved for a long time.

It was Ines who broke the silence, and she did it by laughing — a small, cracked, terrible laugh from under the tarp, the laugh of a fever breaking sideways into clarity. She'd woken somewhere in the middle of it. She pushed the tarp back and looked at me with eyes that the quinine had made too bright.

"Now you understand," she said. "Now you understand why I told you to go home. It was never about the artifacts, Elodie. It was never about gold or documents or your father's reputation. It's arithmetic. We are the rounding error." She pressed her palm flat against the boat's gunwale, as if checking that the world was still solid. "Ferrand doesn't need to kill anyone. She just needs everyone who saw the vault to stop existing, and the valley keeps its own dead so quietly."

"The boy," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "The one asleep on watch. He's somebody's rounding error too."

"Yes," Ines said, without cruelty, which is the cruelest way to say yes.

Tomas took the manifest from my hands and read it top to bottom, lips moving slightly, and then folded it with great care, the way he did everything, and put it inside his shirt against his chest. "Compensation reserve," he said. "Money for families, maybe. Or money for the men who do it. Either way, she priced it." He looked at me. "Your father knew. That's why he sealed the vault. Not for science. He read a contract like this one and understood what he'd dug up — not the past. A price list."

I thought of the ledger page at Camp One, my father's handwriting recording the discovery weeks before the official log admitted it. I had read it as betrayal then — a man hiding his triumph, hoarding glory. Now the same handwriting read differently. A man buying time. A man standing between a door and the people paid to open it.

It didn't forgive him. Twenty years of silence doesn't get forgiven by a paragraph in a supply manifest. But it complicated him, and complication, I was learning, is what love looks like when you finally take off the gloves.

"We can't outrun them," Brand said. He'd gone very still, in the way of a man who has finally stopped being paranoid and started being accurate. "They're faster, better armed, and they know the river. Our only assets are that they think we're scattered survivors, and that Brac follows instructions literally. Men like that are predictable right up until the moment they aren't."

"Then we use both," I said.

Everyone looked at me, and I realized with a small internal jolt that they were waiting — not for my father's son, but for me. Tomas's face gave away nothing, but he shifted his weight off the injured shoulder, settling in to listen, and that was its own kind of endorsement.

"They expect scattered survivors," I said. "So we stay scattered-looking. Small movements, separate camps, no fires after dark. And Brac follows instructions — recover first, personnel second. Which means every hour he spends chasing crates is an hour he isn't spending on us." I picked up the radio, turned it over in my hands. Its green case was warm from the sun. "Which means we need to know where the crates are. Where my father hid them. Ines—" I turned to her, and gentled my voice as much as I could, which wasn't much; urgency had eaten my gentleness weeks ago. "You said it. The door under the falls. You've seen where he sealed it."

Ines looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh something — not whether to tell me, but what telling me would make me become. Fever had burned away most of her filters; what remained was more honest than kindness.

"If I take you there," she said, "you'll stand in front of it, and you'll feel what I felt. And then you'll have to decide, right there, with your hands on the stone, whether you're the kind of Delacroix who opens doors or the kind who guards them." She pulled the tarp back over her knees. "Because I'll tell you now, Elodie: your father stood there a long time before he chose. And look what it cost him to choose wrong."

Upstream, faint as a memory, the two-stroke engine note carried across the water, heading for the falls.

Heading for my father.

Tomas was already unshipping the pole.
