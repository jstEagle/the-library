## 12. The Portage Nobody Survives Twice

The gorge announced itself the way bad news does—not all at once, but as a sound you feel in your teeth before you understand it. We'd been hearing it for an hour: a low, continuous roar from around the bend, like the river had opinions about us and was rehearsing them.

Tomas stood in the bow with one hand shading his eyes and said nothing for a long time. That was how I knew it was bad. Tomas's silences came in grades, and I'd learned to read them the way a sailor reads cloud. There was silence-annoyed, silence-thinking, silence-you're-an-idiot. This was none of those. This was silence-respectful, which was the worst one.

"Well?" I said.

"It's the Maw," he said finally. "Six kilometers of white water through rock walls you couldn't climb with wings. In the dry season, men run pieces of it. In flood season—" He made a small gesture, palm flat, tipping over. "Nobody runs it now."

"And the portage?"

"Overland. Around." He looked at the near bank, which rose out of the water in a wall of green so dense it looked less like vegetation than like a decision the forest had made to exclude us. "There is a trail. The hunters use it. It goes up, along the ridge, down past the falls above the gorge, and back to the water below."

"How long?"

"Two days, if everything is kind." He turned and looked at me, and his face had that particular set it got when he was about to enjoy himself at my expense. "Nothing here is kind, patrón. But two days, if we are lucky and strong."

Ines sat in the stern with her fever finally broken but her strength still living somewhere else, on loan, not yet returned. She'd slept most of the morning against the cargo, and she woke now at the change in Tomas's voice the way soldiers wake—at the tone, not the sound.

"Portage?" she said.

"Portage," I confirmed.

She looked up at the green wall and then at me, and something passed across her face—she'd done this before, or something like it, coming *out*. "You'll want to carry the medicine yourself," she said. "And the photographs. Everything else can be replaced."

"Everything else," Tomas muttered, "is what keeps us alive."

"Everything else," Ines said, with the mild certainty of someone who has watched men die protecting things that could be replaced, "is what gets people killed arguing about it."

I didn't have a clever answer for that, so I started unloading the boat.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about portaging, because the people who know it are usually too tired to write books: it is not walking. Walking is what your body does when it has nothing else to do. Portaging is war conducted against geography, using your own spine as the battlefield.

We made three loads per trip. The boat itself—Tomas's boat, forty years old and heavier than sin—came last, hauled up rollers of cut saplings by all three of us and whatever dignity we had left. The first hundred meters of trail gained elevation like a grudge. The switchbacks were cut into clay that the early rains had already gotten to, so that every foothold was a negotiation and every handhold was either root, mud, or a plant that stung you for the presumption.

I took the front position on the first carry because somebody had to, and because I'd spent my whole life watching other people go first—my father, mostly, striding ahead into trenches and tombs while I held the surveying tape. There is a particular flavor of cowardice that looks like patience from the inside. I was done seasoning mine.

The pack weighed maybe thirty-five kilos. By the end of the first switchback it weighed fifty. By the third it weighed whatever it wanted, and I was no longer carrying it so much as being slowly absorbed into it, the way a swamp absorbs a cart.

"Breath," Tomas said behind me, not unkindly. "In through the nose. Small steps. The mountain is not going anywhere, and neither are you if you fall."

"I'm fine."

"You are lying beautifully. Your father also lied beautifully when he was tired. It is a family instrument."

I wanted to snap at him. I catalogued the desire, filed it under *later*, and kept climbing. This, I would learn over the next two days, was ninety percent of leadership: the filing system. Everyone below you hands you their fear, their anger, their exhaustion, and your actual job—the entire job—is to hold it without dropping any of it on them.

By midday we'd moved the first third of the cargo to a cache point Tomas called, without irony, *the halfway place*, though it was nowhere near halfway. Ines carried the medical kit and the film tins strapped to her chest like a mother with twins, and refused help twice, and accepted it once, silently, when her knee buckled on a root stair—a transaction conducted entirely in glances, as if admitting it aloud would make it true.

We ate cassava flour and dried fish and drank water Tomas made us boil even though the spring looked like it had been poured by God personally. "Clear water kills more boatmen than rapids," he said. "Rapids at least have the decency to look angry."

That night we slept at the halfway place under a lean-to of palm fronds, and I lay awake listening to the gorge roaring below us in the dark, six kilometers of it, the river saying its long opinion, and I thought about my father somewhere upstream doing this same arithmetic—loads, kilometers, hours—with a team that had scattered like spilled seed. I thought about the ledger page in my breast pocket, his handwriting, the discovery recorded weeks before he'd admitted it to anyone official. My father, keeping secrets even from his own logbook. Keeping secrets, maybe, especially there.

Sleep took me mid-thought, the way it does when the body votes and overrides the mind.

* * *

It went wrong on the second day, in the way things go wrong—not dramatically, not cinematically, but stupidly, sideways, in the gap between one careful decision and the next careless second.

The descent to the lower river was steeper than the ascent, a chute of slick stone where the trail dropped through a cleft toward the water below the falls. We were roping the heavy crates down one at a time. Tomas insisted on handling the rope work himself—he'd been doing it forty years, he said, and didn't need a lecture from a boy who thought a dig site was the wilderness—and I let him, because he was right, and because I was busy hauling the boat skids with Ines above.

I heard it before I understood it: a short cry, cut off, the sound a person makes when they're trying not to make a sound. Then the crack of something giving way.

Tomas had been lowering the last crate. The anchor root—sound enough, he'd tested it—had held. What hadn't held was the ledge beneath his feet, a shelf of limestone that the seep water had been quietly dissolving since before anyone's grandmother was born. It went out from under him like a trapdoor. He caught himself on the rope, which saved his life and cost him his shoulder: the full weight of man plus crate wrenched down the line, and I watched from ten meters above as his left arm took all of it, the joint visibly wronging itself, and heard the sound he made, which was worse than the cry because it wasn't a sound at all, just air leaving a man in a shape pain had folded him into.

We got him off the rope and onto flat stone. His shoulder sat high and forward, deformed, and his face had gone the color of the clay.

"Out," he said through his teeth. "Put it back in."

"You don't know that's—"

"I have put shoulders back in four times, patrón. Twice mine. Do it before the muscle remembers to fight you."

Ines was already there, sleeves pushed up, her hands steady in a way that told me her fever had left behind something harder than it found. "On my count," she said to me. "You pull. Steady traction, down and out, then up and over. Don't be gentle. Gentle is cruelty here."

I had set broken bones before. I had splinted, wrapped, dosed. I had never done *this*—never taken a screaming man's arm and moved it against every instinct in both our bodies. But there was no one else. That was the whole lesson of the portage, arriving early: there is no one else. Whatever you're waiting for—an expert, a permission, a braver version of yourself—it isn't coming. You are the expedition now.

"On three," Ines said. "One. Two."

Tomas said, "Now," on two, because he was Tomas, and we pulled.

The joint went home with a deep, wet click I felt in my own chest. Tomas made a sound like a door closing and then lay breathing in long, deliberate draughts, sweat standing on his forehead in beads, and after a minute he said, in a voice almost normal, "Your father fainted. When I did this for him, in '61. Fainted like a church girl."

"Noted," I said, and discovered my own hands were shaking only now that it was over, which was apparently the correct order of operations.

We splinted and slung him. He argued about the sling; he lost. Then came the arithmetic, and it was ugly.

Below us: perhaps two kilometers of trail to the water, including the worst pitch. Above and behind: everything we hadn't yet brought down. And Tomas—one-armed, in shock's gray aftermath—could not haul, could not paddle properly, could barely walk the slick sections unassisted.

"We rest," Ines said. "A day. He needs—"

"A day costs us nothing except a day," I said, and heard my father's cadence come out of my mouth and hated it, and kept going anyway, because the sentence was still true regardless of whose voice wore it. "But we can't afford two. Ferrand's boat was behind us on the river. On foot, around the gorge? Maybe a day behind. Maybe less, if Brac hires carriers from the villages."

"The villages won't carry for him," Ines said.

"The villages won't carry for *us*, either. We've seen how they look at us." I looked at Tomas, who was watching me with an expression I couldn't read—pain, or appraisal, or both. "So. Here's what I think. Tell me if I'm wrong."

They waited. Nobody had ever waited for me before. It was terrible. It was, I noticed distantly, also a kind of power, and I understood in that moment why my father had loved it and what it had cost him to be loved for it.

"We split the difference. Half a day's rest, tonight, here. At first light, Ines takes Tomas down the lower trail slow—I'll rig a hand-line for the bad pitch. I go back up alone, bring down the remaining loads double-time, and meet you at the boats by tomorrow noon. If I'm not there by dusk—"

"You'll be there," Tomas said.

"If I'm not there by dusk, don't wait past dark. Take the boat downstream. There's a mission outpost—"

"I know the river, patrón." Something moved in his face, and for a moment I thought he was going to say something else, something older, about debts or about my father or about the last time a Delacroix had given him orders. He didn't. He said, "Noon. I will have the boat loaded and the coffee burnt, as is tradition."

* * *

Going back up alone was the hardest thing I'd done, and I include in that category every conversation I ever failed to have with my father over twenty years.

The trail in late afternoon was a different animal than the trail at dawn. The light came down through the canopy in shafts and died on the clay. The heat had weight. Each time I shouldered a load and turned uphill, some reasonable voice in my skull pointed out that we could abandon the reserve cargo—that food and fuel were replaceable, that speed was survival, that Ines was right and everything else was what got people killed.

But the reserve cargo included the radio batteries. And the radio batteries were the reason any of this might matter—because somewhere ahead of us was a frequency, and a woman's voice counting numbers, and a father who had gone silent, and a truth sitting in a vault under a waterfall that powerful men had already killed for. You don't get to choose which burdens are worth carrying. Not if you're the one leading. You carry all of it, or you admit you're only out here for yourself.

I made four trips. On the third, at the top of the cleft, I stopped and let myself have sixty seconds of pure self-pity, standing in the green dark with thirty-five kilos grinding my collarbones, sweat and rain indistinguishable, and I thought: *he would have hired porters. He would have paid, charmed, or bullied twelve men into doing this.* And then I thought: *and half of them would have talked afterward, and two of them would have sold him to Ferrand, and he'd have deserved it.* Solitude was the price of secrecy, and secrecy was the price of—of what? Of loyalty? Of pride? I turned the question over and couldn't tell anymore which side of it my father had been on, or which side I was on, and I picked up the load and went down.

Dusk was bleeding into the trees when I came off the last pitch and saw firelight on the water.

They'd done it. Boat loaded, Tomas propped against a crate with his sling freshly tied, Ines boiling water over a fire built small and smokeless the way Tomas had taught her in a single afternoon of pointing and scowling. The burnt coffee sat steaming on a flat stone, tradition honored.

"Noon," Tomas said, as I dropped the last crate and very nearly dropped myself beside it. "You said noon. It is nearly dark."

"Dusk. I said dusk."

"You said you would be here by dusk, and here you are, at dusk, exactly. This is suspicious competence, patrón. I am not sure I trust it."

I laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, the first real laugh since the telegram, and Ines smiled into her cup, and for about four minutes we were just three tired people by a fire instead of fugitives in a race we hadn't chosen.

Then Tomas sobered, and nodded downstream, and said quietly:

"There is one more thing. Below the falls, where the river gathers itself again—the water is high, higher than it should be for the season. Two weeks early, maybe three." He looked at me with the firelight moving in his eyes. "The flood is coming, patrón. The rains have broken upcountry. Which means the river we ran coming in is not the river waiting for us going out."

I looked downstream, into the dark where the gorge's roar had faded to a mutter and the water slid past black and swollen and patient.

Everything we'd survived to get here—the shots on the water, the empty camp, the fever, the shoulder—suddenly felt like tuition paid on a course that hadn't started yet. Somewhere behind us, Brac was making his own arithmetic. Somewhere ahead, under a curtain of falling water, a door stood open that my father had sworn shut.

"All right," I said, to Tomas, to Ines, to the river, to whoever in me had finally stopped waiting for someone else to speak first. "Then we don't sleep past first light. Load the boat tonight. We run at dawn."

Tomas grinned, one-armed and exhausted and entirely himself. "There he is," he said. "I was wondering when the son would show up."
