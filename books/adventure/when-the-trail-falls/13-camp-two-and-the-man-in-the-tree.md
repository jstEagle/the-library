## 13. Camp Two and the Man in the Tree

I want it on the record, whoever ends up reading this, that I was not crazy. I was *early*. There's a difference, and the difference is about nine days and one nearly-fatal misunderstanding, which I'll get to.

Nine days. That's how long I'd been alone at Camp Two when they came up the trail. Nine days since Brac's men walked through here like a weather system with rifles, since I watched them put a boot through my theodolite — my theodolite, eleven years of careful calibration, ground into red mud because one of them thought the tripod looked like a weapon stand — since I climbed this tree with a water bag, a tin of biscuits, and the growing certainty that everyone I'd ever worked with was either dead or bought.

From up here you can see the whole camp laid out like a diagram of my own stupidity. The mess tent with its slashed walls. The survey bench where I'd left my field books in a locked box, which turned out to be a lock for other people's convenience. The latrine trench. The little flag I'd planted on the cache mound before I understood what the cache mound meant to certain parties. And me, forty feet up a kapok tree, living like a very anxious bird, watching the river do its patient brown arithmetic below.

My name is Lukas Brand. I am the senior surveyor on the Delacroix expedition, or I was, and I have spent nine days learning the exact weight of silence. It is heavier than instruments. Nobody tells you that. Instruments you can carry; silence carries you, whether you consent or not.

* * *

Let me back up, because if I start with the men in the boat you'll think I'm paranoid from the first line, and I need you to walk with me a while before you make that judgment.

The expedition had two camps because the river has two moods. Camp One sat below the gorge rapids, comfortable, established, the kind of place where Armand Delacroix could hold court over dinner and pretend he wasn't the most difficult man on three continents. Camp Two was the forward position, up near the falls country, where Ines Okafor did her botany and I did my measuring and the rest of the team rotated through on supply runs. It was supposed to be temporary. Everything about this expedition was supposed to be temporary. That's the joke I keep telling myself up here: we all thought we were passing through.

The trouble started, as far as I could see it, with the radio schedule. We were supposed to check in twice weekly, frequency nine, dusk and dawn windows. For about ten days the check-ins got strange. Not silent — strange. Someone answering who wasn't quite the someone who should have been answering. Wrong cadence. Wrong phrasing. Once, a voice that used Armand's call sign but pronounced the coordinates in an accent Armand doesn't have, and I know his accent, God help me, I've listened to it argue with cartographers for years.

I mentioned it. That was my mistake, if mistakes are what these things are. I mentioned it to Reyes — no, wait, Reyes had gone downriver by then. I mentioned it to Voss, the logistics man, and Voss said don't worry about it, atmospheric conditions, and then Voss started asking me questions about what I'd recorded in my field books. Friendly questions. Questions with a shape underneath them.

Then the second boat came. Not our boat. A long grey thing with an awning and too many men sitting too still under it, and it didn't stop at Camp One, it went straight past, upstream, toward the falls, and Voss watched it go with an expression I can only describe as a man doing sums he doesn't like.

I'm a surveyor. My entire profession is noticing where things actually are versus where people say they are. So when the grey boat passed, and when Voss stopped meeting my eyes, and when Ines went into the green on her collecting walk and didn't come back on schedule — I made a decision that has been described to me, since, as "extreme," and I would describe it as "thorough."

I took my field books, the duplicate set, the ones with the real coordinates including the ones Armand told us to log wrong. Because yes, there were falsified entries, and no, I didn't understand why at the time, and yes, I copied both versions, because a surveyor who keeps only the official story is not a surveyor, he's a typist. Then I climbed the tree, and I stayed there, and I watched.

Two days later the men came through the camp. Six of them, moving like professionals, led by a big quiet one they called Brac. They searched for four hours. They found the locked box with my decoy books — I'd left duplicates of the duplicates, false coordinates dressed up as real ones, because if you're going to be thorough, be thorough — and Brac read them for twenty minutes and then put his fist through the box lid and said something short in a language I didn't catch, and they burned the mess tent's stores and left.

They left me alive. I've thought about that a lot, up here, with nothing but time. Either they didn't know I existed, which meant Voss hadn't sold me yet, or they knew and decided a hiding surveyor wasn't worth the bullet. Both possibilities are instructive. Neither is comforting.

* * *

Day six, I saw the vultures over by the old portage trail, and I will not write down what I did about that except to say I did not go look, and I have decided that this was wisdom rather than cowardice, and some mornings I even believe it.

Day eight, it rained for eleven hours and I learned that a water bag suspended incorrectly will drip directly onto your collarbone at a rate of one drop per breath, forever, and that a man can hate a drop of water with a purity of feeling he usually reserves for people.

Day nine, they came up the trail.

Three figures. One tall, moving like he owned the ground and was disappointed in it — and I knew that walk, I'd have known it anywhere, because I'd spent two years being shouted at by its owner. Elodie Delacroix. The son. What the son was doing five hundred miles from his salvage dig in the lowlands, I couldn't imagine, unless the telegram had finally gone out and the institute had sent family, which would be either touching or tactical, and after nine days in a tree I was no longer equipped to tell the difference.

With him: a broad-shouldered boatman I recognized with a jolt — Tomas Reyes, who owed Armand something old and heavy, though nobody ever told me what. And a woman half-carried between them, feverish, dark-skinned, and —

Ines. Ines Okafor, alive. Something in my chest did a complicated maneuver at that, relief and grief shaking hands. Alive, but wrong, muttering, her head lolling, and the two men got her into the least-burned tent and started working over her with the brisk competence of people who have recently learned field medicine the hard way.

And here is where I must confess to the nearly-fatal part, because this account is supposed to be honest and honesty is the one luxury I've decided to keep.

I had a machete. I had nine days of silence in me, and nine days of silence does something to a man's reasoning. It takes every fact and grinds off the edges until everything fits the same terrible shape. I watched Elodie Delacroix go through the camp — through *my* camp, through the wreckage of an expedition I'd given two years to — and I watched him pause at the cache mound, at my little flag, and crouch there looking at the ground the way a man looks at a safe, and every corroded gear in my head engaged at once.

He knows about the cache. He's here for the cache. They sent the son because the son has a claim, blood is a claim, and Ferrand's contract — I'd read the contract, I read everything, that was my job — Ferrand's contract ran through family proxies if the principal was incapacitated. Incapacitated. The word sat in my skull like a stone.

So when he stood up from the cache mound and started walking toward my tree — not knowing it was my tree, just walking, scanning the treeline the way a careful man does — I did the arithmetic of nine days of silence, and the sum was: compromised, all of them, and the books in my shirt cannot leave this forest in anyone else's hands.

I dropped out of the tree behind him with the machete raised, and I want to say, in my defense, that I shouted a warning first. I did. I yelled "Drop it, drop whatever you're carrying" in three languages, which in retrospect sounds less like a warning and more like a threat issued by a committee.

What happened next happened fast. Reyes spun — the man moves well for his size — and there was a pistol in his hand that I hadn't seen him draw, and Elodie turned with his arms out, empty, saying something calm, and the machete in my grip was shaking, and I realized distantly that my hands hadn't shaken once in nine days alone and were shaking now, now that there were people, and I understood with awful clarity that the tree had done something to me that the forest hadn't.

"Brand." Elodie said my name. Just that. He was looking at me the way you look at a dog that's been chained too long — carefully, without pity, because pity makes it worse. "Lukas. It's Elodie. Armand's son. Put it down."

"The radio's wrong," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like gravel in a tin. "The check-ins. Someone's been answering on his call sign and it isn't him. Voss asked about my books. The grey boat went upriver past Camp One and never stopped. Everyone left is either dead or paid for, and you—" and the machete point drifted toward him, traitorous, "—you're standing at the cache mound."

"I'm standing at a hole in the ground," he said. "It's empty. Whatever was flagged here, somebody took it or buried it deeper. I've been here four minutes. You've been watching for nine days — you know more than I do. So lower the blade and tell me, because right now you're the only man on this river holding a complete map."

That was good. I'll grant him that; the Delacroixs are manipulators the way rivers are wet, but it was *good*, because it was also true, and flattery built on truth is load-bearing.

Reyes hadn't lowered the pistol. "You point that thing one inch closer to him," Reyes said to me, conversational, almost friendly, "and I stop being polite." And I believed him completely, and somehow that helped. There's a clarity that comes from a credible threat that nine days of ambiguity had starved out of me.

I lowered the machete. My knees informed me, retroactively, that they had been holding up a siege, and I sat down in the mud rather hard, and the last thing I remember clearly of that afternoon is Ines's voice from inside the tent, feverish, repeating numbers downward — counting down, counting down — and Elodie going very still when he heard it, the way a man goes still when a sound confirms something he'd been hoping was imagination.

* * *

They fed me. That deserves its own paragraph, because after nine days of biscuits and rainwater, the tin of rice and dried fish Reyes put together tasted like a decision about the future. Food is an argument that tomorrow exists. I ate like a convert.

Elodie sat across the fire from me and did the thing his father does — the listening posture, the total attention, the sense that your answer is being weighed against others you haven't given yet — but where Armand weighs you against himself, the son weighed me against the situation, which I found more bearable and more frightening in roughly equal measure.

I gave him everything. The radio irregularities, logged day by day. Voss's questions. The grey boat, timed, headed, described — Brac's face I could render from memory down to the scar through his eyebrow, because faces are data and data is my trade. The falsified coordinate entries in Armand's own orders, which made Elodie's jaw tighten in a way that told me the son had suspected and the confirmation still cost him. And the books. I produced the real field books from the oilcloth wrap inside my shirt, swollen with damp, every page legible, and I watched him hold them and understood that I had just handed over the most dangerous object on this river to a man I'd tried to carve up an hour earlier.

Trust, it turns out, is not a feeling. It's a transaction you conduct while terrified, and then you find out afterward whether you were a fool.

"Why the tree?" he asked, eventually. "Why not run downriver? Reyes could have carried you out."

"Run where?" I said, and heard how it sounded, and made myself finish it. "Downriver is Ferrand's territory. The institute answers to her money. Every checkpoint, every mission station, every radio operator on the lower river is a place where a man carrying these books stops being a witness and starts being a problem that gets solved. Up here, at least, the only things trying to kill me were honest about it." I nodded at the forest, the dark coming down through it, patient as ever. "Out there it's just the jungle. In here—" I tapped my own chest, and hated the theatricality of it and did it anyway, "—in here it's the jungle too now. But at least I know which direction the danger comes from."

He considered that longer than a healthy man would have. "And now?"

"Now there are three of us and a fever case, and the danger comes from everywhere, and I'd like to state for the record that I was not crazy, I was early."

"You were both," Reyes said, from across the fire, not unkindly, and I found I could accept that from him. Boatmen get to be blunt. It's in the union rules.

* * *

Night watch split three ways. I took the middle shift, naturally — I'd had nine days of practice sleeping in fragments, and besides, I didn't trust my own exhaustion not to tip over into the kind of sleep where you wake up disoriented and dangerous.

It was during my watch that I did the thing I'd been circling all evening. I opened the field books to the last real entry, the one from the morning before everything went wrong, and I read again the note Armand had dictated to me at the falls — the instruction to log the approach coordinates falsely, offset by six kilometers east, and beneath it, in his handwriting in my book because he'd grabbed it from my hands and written it himself:

*If anything happens to me, the true bearing goes to no one who asks for it. Only to someone who already knows why.*

I had spent nine days turning that sentence over like a stone, looking for what lived under it. Sitting there in the ruined camp with the fire low and Ines murmuring countdown numbers in her fever, I finally admitted what I'd known since the grey boat went past: Armand Delacroix had found something at those falls that he valued more than the truth, or feared more than death, or both — and he had distributed pieces of it to people he trusted individually precisely so that no one of us could betray the whole.

Which meant the son, sitting ten feet away, was either the key or the last lock.

Dawn came up grey and dripping. Ines's fever broke a little near morning — enough that she surfaced, looked at the three of us around her, and said, clear as a bell, "You shouldn't have come. None of you should have come. He sealed it for a reason." Then she was gone again, under, counting.

Elodie looked at me across her. "The door under the falls," he said. "She keeps saying it. You surveyed that country. Is there a door under the falls?"

And I, Lukas Brand, senior surveyor, keeper of the true bearings, nine days early and not crazy, gave him the only honest answer I had.

"There's a door under the falls," I said, "and according to my instruments, someone has been opening it."
