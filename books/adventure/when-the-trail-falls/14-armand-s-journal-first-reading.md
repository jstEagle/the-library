## 14. Armand's Journal, First Reading

Brand didn't sleep that night, and neither did I.

He sat across the fire with his knees drawn up like a man guarding his own ribs, the journal pages wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and every time one of us shifted he flinched as though the forest itself had reached for him. Ines slept in fits, fever still burning off her in slow tides. Tomas kept first watch and pretended not to watch Brand at all, which was its own kind of watching.

"Those pages," I said, somewhere past midnight. "You've read them."

"I've read everything he wrote." Brand's voice came out flat as river stone. "I was the surveyor. He needed someone to map what he found. Do you understand what that means? He *needed* me. And then he decided needing me was a liability."

"So you took the pages when you ran."

"I took the pages because they were the only thing in that camp worth more than my life." He said it without shame, and I found I couldn't argue with the arithmetic. "And now you're sitting there looking at me the way your father looked at me when I asked too many questions, and I'd like you to stop."

I hadn't known I was looking at him at all. That's how it starts, I suppose — the wanting. It doesn't announce itself. It just sits down beside you at the fire and waits for you to notice it's warm.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping, the kind of light that makes everything look already abandoned. Brand unwrapped the oilcloth on a flat stone by the water's edge, and for a long moment nobody touched the pages.

They were Armand's handwriting. Of course they were. I'd know it anywhere — I'd grown up watching it march across dinner-table napkins, correcting my schoolwork without being asked, annotating my mother's grocery lists with marginalia about sediment layers. A slanted, impatient hand, each letter leaning forward like a man walking fast into weather. Twenty years since I'd seen it daily and it still hit me in the sternum like a door swinging open into a house I'd been told was sold.

"You should know," Brand said, "that reading these changed how I think about him. Not favorably. But not simply unfavorably either. Complicatedly." He almost smiled. "He would have hated that word."

"He hates most words with more than two syllables of honesty in them."

Ines laughed — a small, cracked sound, but a laugh — and then pressed her hand to her mouth as if she'd broken something.

I picked up the first page.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary expedition business: supply tallies, crew complaints, the interminable politics of river permits. Armand wrote the way he talked, in bursts, crossing out half of what he started. There was a joke about Tomas's cooking that I chose not to read aloud, on the grounds that Tomas was standing right there sharpening a machete with unnecessary attention.

Then, dated six weeks back, the entries changed.

*The falls are not marked on any chart I trust. The hunter's people call the place behind them something I will not write here, because writing it makes it real, and if it becomes real before I understand what we have found, it will be taken from us before noon.*

My father, who had spent fifty years publishing everything, who believed obscurity was a sin against the dead — writing about *not writing.*

The next entry was three days later and the handwriting had gone strange, cramped, as if written in a moving boat or a shaking hand.

*I have stood inside it. I don't have words yet and I am a man who has never once lacked for words. The chamber is dry. Dry, Elodie — no, I keep forgetting you will never read this — dry after five hundred years at minimum, sealed behind the falls with a precision that implies hydraulic engineering we do not credit to anyone in this valley, ever. The preservation is impossible. Textiles intact. Wood carved and unwarped. And documents. Documents, documents, documents. Bark-paper, bound, hundreds of them. If even a fraction are what the iconography suggests—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. The next began:

*I have made a decision and I am recording it here so that someday, if anyone finds this, they will know it was a decision and not an accident. Ferrand's contract gives her institute claim rights over anything of "significant cultural-material value." I signed it. I signed it because she funded the dig when no one else would, and because I told myself significant meant gold masks and thrones, and this is not that. This is bigger than that. Which means by her definition it is exactly that.*

*So I am going to seal it. All of it. I will record a false find — minor ceramics, a burial cache, nothing to excite a lawyer — and I will seal the true vault so well that even my own crew will doubt what they saw. Ines will hate me. Brand will hate me. Let them. Better they hate me than that the valley's history becomes collateral in a negotiation between people who have never touched its soil.*

I set the page down. My hands were not entirely steady, and I noticed that everyone had seen it, and I noticed that I didn't care.

"She knew," Ines said quietly. "Even then. She suspected he'd found something and hidden it. That's why the second boat. That's why Brac."

"That's why we're all here," Brand said. "That's why Okafor walked until her feet split. Because a man who taught me stratigraphy decided he loved the truth so much he buried it alive."

"Tomas?" I said.

Tomas looked up from the machete. For once he didn't reach for a grievance. "Your father saved my brother's life in '09. Pulled him out of the gorge water when every sensible man said let him go. I've owed him since." He turned the blade over. "I also watched him lie to a room full of people who trusted him, and smile while doing it, and I'll tell you what I told him then: a debt is not a leash. Pay it or don't. But don't confuse owing a man with belonging to him."

It was the longest speech I'd heard from him that wasn't about cargo weight. I nodded, and he went back to the blade, and the conversation was over in the way Tomas ended things — completely.

* * *

We read through the middle sections together, taking turns, because Brand refused to let any single person hold the pages — "compartmentalization," he said, "it's the only reason I'm alive" — and because, honestly, none of us wanted to be alone with them.

The technical entries were where the awe lived.

Armand had documented everything, because of course he had; you could seal a man's conscience but not his methodology. Radiocarbon samples sent under false labels to a lab in the capital. Ceramic sequences that predated every known tradition in the basin by centuries. Stonework whose tooling matched nothing in the regional corpus. And threaded through it all, growing louder entry by entry, the thing that made Brand stop reading aloud twice:

*The documents appear to constitute records of tenure. Not myth-history — tenure. Boundaries. Stewardship obligations. Succession. If I am reading the glyphic conventions correctly — and I am, I am certain I am — these are legal documents. Pre-colonial land law. Continuous occupation attested in writing from a period at least two centuries before the first Spanish grant in this region.*

*Which means every colonial title downstream is built on sand. Every concession, every plantation deed, every mining lease the republic has ever honored — all of it rests on the premise that this land was empty of claimants, or that its claimants had no standing. These documents are standing. Centuries of it, in ink.*

Brand lowered the page. "Do you understand what this means?"

"It means lawyers," Tomas said darkly.

"It means the entire valley," Brand said. "Every hectare from the gorge to the delta. Whoever holds those originals holds the ability to make or unmake every land title in the province. Museums would fight wars over the ceramics alone. Governments—" He stopped himself, looked at the trees, looked at the water. "Governments would burn villages to keep this quiet. Or burn them to be the ones who announce it. Either way."

"Ferrand understands that," Ines said. "That's why leave no witnesses isn't a figure of speech to her. It's a line item."

I listened to them and felt something move in me that I did not like.

It wasn't horror. Horror would have been respectable. It was closer to vertigo — that lurch you get at the edge of a high place when some traitorous part of you wonders what the fall would feel like. I was holding my father's handwriting, and in it was a power that could redraw maps, and some cold little clerk in the back office of my mind was already drafting the memo: *you could be the one who brings this home. You could be the Delacroix who finally matters. One crate. One document. Photographed, authenticated, presented by you —*

I want to be clear: I despised that voice. I want to be equally clear that it was mine.

Because here is the inheritance nobody puts in the will. My father didn't just give me his jaw and his impatience and his habit of annotating other people's sentences. He gave me the appetite. The belief that a find can redeem a life. I had spent twenty years telling myself I'd escaped it by digging in salvage mud for other people's lost cargo instead of glory. But salvage pays, and I liked that it paid, and I had never once asked myself whether I'd chosen humility or just a smaller stage.

Now the stage was the size of a valley, and the clerk in the back office was licking his pencil.

"Elodie." Ines was watching me. Fever-bright, but sharp underneath. "What are you thinking?"

"That my father is a coward," I said, "and that I'm his son, and that the difference between us is currently measuring itself in hours."

Nobody had an answer to that. Brand wrapped the pages back in their oilcloth with the tenderness of a man handling explosives, which was accurate.

* * *

Midday brought the hunter.

He appeared the way his people apparently always appeared — simply present, between one blink and the next, standing at the treeline with his blowgun slung and his face arranged into polite blankness. He looked at our fire, our boat, our faces, and lingered last on me, and I understood I was being assessed against some standard I couldn't see.

"The token," I said, and took it from around my neck.

He studied the carving for a long time. Then he said something in his own language, soft, almost conversational, and waited.

"He says," Ines translated slowly — she'd been picking up phrases during her weeks upstream, God knew how, fevered and walking — "he says the mark is the door-keeper's sign. That only the door-keeper's line may open what is behind the falls, and only for reasons the elders name aloud." She paused. "He says your father carried this token too. Given freely. And that your father swore."

"Swore what?"

Another exchange, longer this time, the hunter's voice low and unhurried. Ines listened with her eyes closed, catching the shape of it.

"He swore the things would stay buried until the valley's own people chose otherwise. That no paper, no payment, no government would move them. Only consent." She opened her eyes. "Elodie — he says your father broke that oath. Or that men came who forced it. He wants to know which."

The question landed on me like cargo. Behind the hunter, the river ran on toward the falls, indifferent, carrying its answer somewhere in the green distance.

And I thought about the pages in Brand's oilcloth, and the clerk in the back office of my skull, and my father's slanted handwriting leaning forward into weather, and I realized the choice Brand had described — complicatedly — was now sitting in my lap wearing a carved face.

"We didn't force it," I said. "But I don't yet know if he broke it. Take me to the falls, and I'll swear the same oath he swore — out loud, before witnesses. And if the door has been opened from the other side—" I glanced at Brand, at Ines, at the oilcloth. "Then what happens next gets decided by more than one man's pride. Including mine."

The hunter considered me for a long moment. Then he said one word, and pointed upriver.

Ines exhaled. "He says: tomorrow. Before dawn. Bring the papers your father wrote." A beat. "All of them."

Brand's arms tightened around the oilcloth like a man being asked to surrender a kidney. And far off, beneath the birdsong, so faint I might have invented it, I heard the river change its note — the deepening hum of water approaching a fall.

Tomorrow, then. The door under the falls. And whatever my father had sworn, and whatever I was about to.
