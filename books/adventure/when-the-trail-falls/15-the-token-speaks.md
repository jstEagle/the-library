## 15. The Token Speaks

The token had been riding in my breast pocket for six days by then, and I'd stopped pretending it was just a souvenir.

It was a disc of dark hardwood, palm-sized, carved on one face with a spiral that broke apart halfway through and reassembled as a serpent—or a river, or a lightning strike, depending on how tired you were when you looked at it. The elder at the turned-back village had slipped it into our boat without a word, without meeting my eyes, in the particular way people give you something they hope you'll understand and dread that you won't.

I'd shown it to Ines twice. Both times she'd gone quiet in a way that wasn't restful.

"It's not jewelry," she said the second time, from her place in the bow, where she'd insisted on sitting despite a fever that had broken only two days prior and left her thin as a wading bird. "I've seen that spiral before. On the door."

"The door under the falls."

"On the door under the falls." She said it like a man repeating a diagnosis. "Which means either that elder has been somewhere no one from his village admits to going, or the token is older than the village and they're passing it around like a hot coal nobody wants to hold."

"And they gave it to me because—"

"Because you're the one walking toward the fire, obviously." She adjusted her hat against the sun. "People who don't want to be burned hand the coal to someone else. It's the oldest diplomacy there is."

Tomas, at the tiller, said nothing, which from Tomas was a paragraph.

We were camped that evening on a mud bank below a bluff of red clay, the river running wide and brown and patient, the way it had run for three days since the shooting. No second boat. No shots. Just the water and the insects and the enormous indifferent green pressing in from both banks, and me turning a piece of carved wood over in my fingers like a man worrying a loose tooth.

Brand had taken to sleeping with his machete under his arm and waking at every sound, which meant none of us slept much. He'd improved from *paranoid wreck* to *paranoid colleague*, which in expedition terms is practically a promotion. That evening he sat across the fire from me, eating rice with the focused hostility of a man who distrusts even his own dinner.

"You keep looking at that thing," he said.

"I'm trying to read it."

"It's wood, Delacroix. It doesn't read back."

"Everything reads back," I said. "You just have to ask the right questions. My father taught me that." I heard myself say it and winced. Six days upriver and I was already quoting him like scripture, which was exactly the kind of inheritance I'd sworn off at nineteen.

Brand grunted. "Your father also taught you to walk into a valley where men shoot at boats. Some lessons you should've skipped."

Before I could answer—and I'm glad I didn't have to, because my answers to Brand were getting shorter and less dignified—the firelight found something at the edge of our camp that hadn't been there when the light last swept past.

A man was standing between two trees.

Not approaching. Not hiding. Standing the way furniture stands, as though he'd been part of the forest all along and had simply decided, this evening, to be visible. He was lean and grey-streaked, barefoot, with a blowgun slung across his back and a woven bag at his hip, and he watched us with the unhurried attention of someone counting.

Tomas came off the tiller so fast the boat rocked. He didn't reach for a weapon. He put both hands out, palms open, and said something in a language I didn't know—three words, low, almost formal.

The man answered in the same language. Then, in accented but fluent river-trade Spanish: "You are the son of Armand Delacroix."

It wasn't a question, but it deserved an answer, so I gave it. "I am."

"He looks like you. When he was younger. Angrier." A pause that might have been humor. "Now he only looks tired."

My heart did something complicated. "You've seen him."

"I have seen what he left behind." The man stepped forward into the firelight, and up close I could see the fine lines tattooed or scarred along his jaw—geometric, deliberate, the same broken-spiral pattern repeated small. "My name is Kaue. I am a hunter of the river people. The elder who would not speak to you sent me."

"He sent a piece of wood instead of words."

"He sent the wood because the wood says more than words, and cannot be misquoted." Kaue crouched at the edge of the fire, uninvited, entirely comfortable. "May I?"

He held out his hand for the token. I gave it to him, and felt oddly naked doing it—as though I'd been carrying someone else's heart in my pocket and here, finally, was its owner.

Kaue turned the disc over once, twice, reading it the way Tomas read water. Then he held it up beside his own forearm, and I saw it: the spiral on the token matched the marks along his jaw, mark for mark, break for break.

"This is a path-sign," he said. "Older than my grandmother's grandmother. It marks the way to a place my people call—" he used his own word, then translated it carefully, "—*the mouth that drinks the sky*. Your maps call it nothing. Your father called it the door under the falls."

Ines made a small sound. Brand's hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, toward the machete.

"You know about my father," I said.

"I know that a season ago a boat of pale strangers went up the gorge singing. And I know that a week later some of them came down again, not singing, and that among them was a tall old man with a bandaged arm who stood on the bank below the falls and wept like a boy." Kaue set the token down on the flat stone between us, precisely centered, deliberately returned. "And I know that since then, other boats have come. Boats with guns. Boats that burn."

"Ferrand's," I said.

"If that is the name of the arithmetic, yes." He looked at me then, directly, and his eyes were the flattest, most patient thing I'd seen since the river itself. "So now I will ask you the question my elder could not ask you at the landing, because asking it aloud in front of the wrong ears gets villages emptied. What do you want, son of Armand? Do you want to find your father? Or do you want what he found?"

*

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that the question took me apart a little.

Because the true answer—the answer underneath the answer I prepared—was *both*, and *both* is never a clean thing. There was the son in me, twenty years of unanswered letters coiled in his chest, who wanted his father alive and within shouting distance if only so he could finish the argument. And there was the archaeologist in me, the one I'd become precisely to spite that same father, who heard the phrase *door under the falls* and felt the old hunger open its mouth. A pre-colonial vault, intact, undocumented. Every dig I'd ever worked was a eulogy. This would be a birth. One crate of what waited behind that door would fund ten careers. It would buy back every year the river had eaten. It would make my name bigger than his.

I knew that voice. I'd spent my whole life pretending it was my father's voice, when it had been mine all along.

"The truth," I said finally, "is that I came for my father. But I'll be honest with you, because I think you'll know if I'm not—I want to see what he saw. Any man who tells you otherwise is lying about something larger."

Kaue considered this for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere upriver, thunder cleared its throat.

"Honesty is expensive," he said at last. "Good. Now hear the price of the rest."

He laid it out plainly, the way men lay out trade goods. He would guide us inland to the falls—a route no chart knew, through country where Brac's boats couldn't follow. He would do it because his elder judged that Armand Delacroix had behaved, in the end, with a kind of honor: the old man had come to the river people himself, before anyone else knew what he'd found, and asked them to help him seal it. Had given them the means to close the door. Had kept their name out of his logs, so that no map, no contract, no gunboat would ever point at them.

"He protected us," Kaue said. "A stranger protected us. So we owe him a debt, and debts to the dead or the lost must be paid. That is why I am here."

"But?" I said, because there was a *but*; you could hear it waiting behind his teeth like a fish under a lily pad.

"But." He picked the token up again and held it between us. "This sign opens nothing. It *vouches*. It says: this one comes as a guest, not a thief. And a guest obeys the house." His grip tightened on the disc until his knuckles paled. "Swear me the oath, Delacroix. Swear that what sleeps behind the door stays asleep. Whatever you find inside—papers, gold, the bones of kings—you carry out nothing that belongs to that place. You look. You learn what you need to save your father. And then you help us close it again, and you tell no one living where it lies. Not your museum. Not your press. Not your friends. No one."

The fire popped. Ines had gone very still. Brand was staring at me with an expression I couldn't parse—something between warning and envy.

"And my father's discovery," I said slowly, "just... disappears. Again."

"It does not disappear. It goes home." Kaue's voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "You pale men think a thing is lost unless it is written in your books and locked in your buildings. But that place was ours before your books existed. It will be ours after. The documents your father found—they prove the valley was never sold. Do you understand what that means? They are not artifacts. They are *deeds*. And deeds belong to the people whose land they describe." He set the token down in front of me, gently, like a card played face-up. "Take them out, and within a year they sit in a glass case in your capital while lawyers argue whether a photograph counts as evidence. Leave them, and they remain a wall that no contract can climb."

"That's not how courts work," Brand said. "Originals matter. Provenance matters—"

"Then copy them," Kaue said, without looking away from me. "Copy every page. Photograph everything. Let the whole world read the words. But the paper itself, the things themselves—those stay in the ground where the ground can keep them. Paper in a vault can be stolen. Truth in a thousand minds cannot."

I looked at the token. Broken spiral, mended spiral. A path that breaks and continues.

Here is what it cost me, and I want to be exact about it, because the whole shape of my life afterward bends around this moment by the fire: swearing that oath meant giving up the find. Not sharing it—*relinquishing* it. My father's life's work, the discovery of the century, the thing that would have dragged the Delacroix name out of the mud and planted it in marble—it would go back into the dark, and I would spend the rest of my career digging in other people's middens while some unnamed door stayed shut behind falling water. And worse, quieter, meaner: some part of me understood that Ferrand was right about one thing. Secrets buried stay claimable. If we sealed it and told no one, then the valley's ownership rested on our silence, and silence is a flimsy wall. Men with guns had already proven they could cross any wall we built.

But Kaue was right too, and I knew it with the certainty of a man recognizing his own face in an unflattering mirror. The moment those originals left the ground, they became *property*. Property gets owned, bought, litigated, burned. The truth needed witnesses, not a warehouse.

There was a third option, of course. The one I wouldn't let myself look at yet, the one squatting at the edge of my thoughts like Brac at the edge of a treeline: broadcast everything, everywhere, immediately. But that oath hadn't offered itself yet, and I was a long way from understanding that it was the only oath worth keeping. That night, by the fire, I still believed oaths could be narrow.

"Swear it," Kaue said again, "and tomorrow we walk. Refuse, and I vanish, and you find the falls the hard way—if the falls let you find them at all. The river people have drowned better searchers than you, son of Armand. We are very good at geography."

I thought of my father weeping on the bank below the falls. I thought of Ines muttering *go home, go home* through her fever. I thought of a ledger page in Armand's handwriting, dated weeks before his official log admitted anything—a man who'd learned to hide things so well he hid them from himself.

And I thought, with a shame I can still locate in my body, of the crate. Of the future it would buy. Of the version of me that said yes to Kaue and hated himself quietly forever, and the version that said no and became rich and hollow, and the third version, the one standing there in the firelight with mud to his knees, who wanted—God help me—to be the kind of man his father had briefly been on that riverbank, before fear got him.

"I swear," I said. "What sleeps behind the door stays asleep. Nothing leaves that belongs to that place. I tell no one where it lies." I picked up the token and held it out to him. "On my name."

Kaue didn't take it back. He folded my fingers closed over the disc, one by one, with a hunter's economy.

"Keep it," he said. "If you break the oath, I will know where to find you. That is what the token is for." And then, horribly, he smiled—not unkindly, the way you'd smile at a child signing his first contract. "Besides. My elder says the path may yet need you to carry it further than I think. Old women are always saying things like that. Come. Sleep. We leave before the birds."

*

He was gone into the trees before I could ask a single practical question—route, distance, what to do about the boat, whether *before the birds* was proverbial or literal. Tomas watched the treeline where he'd been for a long moment, then began breaking camp without being asked, moving with the brisk energy of a man whose opinion of the trip had just improved.

"You trust him?" Brand muttered, packing with unnecessary force.

"No."

"Then why—"

"Because I trust the alternative less." I tucked the token back into my breast pocket, where it lay against my sternum like a second heartbeat. "Brac's men follow rivers. Kaue says the way inland has no river. For the first time since the shooting, we'd be somewhere they can't bring guns."

"Or somewhere they can bring guns and we can't see them coming," Brand said. "Forests are full of places to die, Delacroix. I've been counting them since camp two."

"Then count one more and get some sleep."

Ines caught my arm as I passed her bedroll. Her fever had thinned her voice down to wire. "Elodie. The oath you swore tonight. Think hard about whether you can keep it. Because I've met men who swear easily and keep badly, and the river eats them last, after everyone who trusted them."

"I keep my word."

"I know," she said. "That's what frightens me. Men who keep their word will keep it straight into a burning house." She lay back and closed her eyes. "Wake me when the birds start."

I didn't sleep. I sat by the dying fire and turned the token over and over, and somewhere past midnight I noticed what I should have noticed days ago: the spiral didn't just match the marks on Kaue's jaw. Held at the right angle against the firelight, the break in the spiral—the gap where the line stops and restarts—matched, stroke for stroke, the chisel scars I'd seen photographed in my father's journal. The marks on the door itself.

The token wasn't a key. It was a *record*. Someone had copied the door onto wood, carried it downriver, and handed it to strangers as a message: *we know what you found, and we are watching.*

I was still holding it up to the embers when the first bird started, and Kaue materialized at the edge of the light with his blowgun already strung across his back, and said the sentence that ended my night and started everything that came after:

"Wake your people quickly, son of Armand. There is smoke downstream. Someone is burning the fishing boats at the next village—and they are doing it slowly, so that everyone watches."
