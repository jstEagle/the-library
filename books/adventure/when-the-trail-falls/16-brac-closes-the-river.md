## 16. Brac Closes the River

I have burned a lot of things in my life. Warehouses, mostly. A vineyard once, which I regretted, because the wine had been good and the owner had wept in a way that stayed with me longer than the insurance payout did. Boats burn beautifully — that's the trouble with them. Resin and dry hardwood and a river of air moving over the water: you strike one match and the fire does the rest like it's been waiting for the invitation.

But I want to be clear about something, because the people downstream of me that night would not have believed it if I'd told them, and because nobody ever believes it about men like me: I don't enjoy this. Enjoyment is for amateurs. What I do is arithmetic. You put numbers on either side of an equation and you make them balance, and the fact that the numbers are sometimes people is not my invention. I inherited that notation from men with better tailors.

The arithmetic that night went like this.

On one side: six boats belonging to the village of Tseremo, forty-one souls, a hunter named Kaelen who had gone upriver to guide my quarry, two dugout canoes too small to matter, and a mission outpost eight hours' walk away with a radio older than I was. On the other side: four men on my payroll, one launch with a working engine and a fuel problem I hadn't solved yet, and an employer who communicated exclusively through cables that read like weather reports for a climate only she could feel.

If the Delacroix party came downriver, they would stop at Tseremo. That was certain. Nobody runs the gorge at night, and nobody with a wounded boatman and a feverish woman passes a friendly village without stopping. So the village was not a village anymore. It was a door. And my orders — recover the artifacts, leave no witnesses — required me to close doors.

* * *

We came down from the ridge at dusk, which is when you do this kind of work, and I made my men wait another twenty minutes past dark because the village would be eating. There is a window, right after the meal, when the fires are banked low and everyone is heavy and slow and talking, and no one is looking at the water. I learned that window from a man in another country whose name I've paid to forget, and he learned it from someone worse. The knowledge comes down like everything else — hand to hand, stained.

"Four boats," said Voss, my second, crouching beside me in the cane grass. He said it the way other men say *four beers*. "Plus the two canoes."

"The canoes stay."

Voss looked at me. He has a face like a closed ledger, which is why I keep him, but even ledgers raise an eyebrow sometimes. "Orders say leave no witnesses."

"Orders also say recover the artifacts," I said, "and there are no artifacts in those canoes. Burn the fishing boats. Leave the canoes. A man with a canoe can fish. A man with nothing burns your compound while you sleep, and I'd rather not explain arson to Ferrand's lawyers along with everything else."

It wasn't mercy. I want that understood too. It was maintenance. A village that can still feed itself is a village that doesn't follow you upriver with machetes. I have seen what happens when you take everything from people who live close to the ground, and the interest rates on that kind of debt are ruinous.

We went in quiet. The dogs didn't bark, because I had brought meat for the dogs, because I am thorough, and because a dog that eats your meat belongs to you in a way that is cheaper than shooting it. Two men to the boats. One to the path inland, in case anyone ran that way. Voss with me to the elder's house, because there is always an elder, and elders are where villages keep their sense.

The old man came out holding a lamp like it was a weapon he'd forgotten how to use. He looked at me, then at the men already pouring kerosene into the hulls of his neighbors' livelihoods, and he did something I did not expect. He set the lamp down. Carefully. On the step. As if to say: whatever you're going to take, take it, but not the light.

"You are the ones the river spoke of," he said. His trade language was better than mine. "The ones who buy silence."

"I'm not buying anything tonight," I said.

"No," he agreed. "Tonight you are burning."

There was no fear in it. That unsettled me more than screaming would have. Screaming I know how to stand inside of; it's just weather. But this old man looked at me the way an accountant looks at a figure that won't reconcile, and I felt, for one unprofessional moment, like the error in someone else's column.

"The man you hunt," he said. "Kaelen went to him. My sister's son."

"I know."

"He swore the buried things would stay buried. Do you swear they will?"

And here is where I should tell you what I said, except what I actually said was nothing, because there is no honest answer to that question that serves my employer, and I have built a long career on declining to lie when lying would cost me later. I looked at him instead, and he read the look, and something in his face closed like a door over water.

"Your sister's son made a bad wager," I said finally. "He bet that a stranger's conscience weighs more than a stranger's money. Come get him before the flood season, and maybe both of you live to collect."

"He will not come back," the elder said. Not a question. A forecast.

"Then the boats were never your real problem."

I turned to go, and he said — quietly, to my back, in the tone of a man setting a figure into a ledger he keeps for God — "You count us like cargo, soldier. But the river counts too. It has been counting longer than you."

I have heard threats with guns behind them, threats with governments behind them, threats with nothing behind them at all. This was none of these. It was worse. It was bookkeeping.

* * *

The boats caught on the third try — the first two matches drowned in evening damp, which I mention only because professionals are allowed their failures as long as they budget for them — and the fire stood up off the water like something being born. Orange light ran all the way across the river and up the far bank and into the faces of the villagers who had come down to watch, and I made my men stand back and let them watch, because there is a cruelty in hiding a thing like this, and cruelty of the pointless kind is how amateurs announce themselves.

A woman screamed. Then children, the high mechanical wail that isn't grief yet, just alarm looking for somewhere to land. And one boy — twelve, maybe, Kaelen's build, Kaelen's jaw — broke from the crowd and ran straight at Voss with a fish knife, which is the bravest and stupidest thing I saw that whole season, and I have seen men charge machine guns over less than a burned boat.

Voss raised his rifle.

"No," I said.

Voss paused. That pause is the entire difference between my operation and the ones Ferrand would hire if I weren't available, and I live inside that difference the way a man lives inside a coat.

The boy stopped three meters out, knife shaking, lit orange, breathing like a bellows. Behind him the woman — his mother, presumably; the arithmetic of families is usually legible if you look — was coming fast, and I stepped between her line and her son and held up one hand.

"Put it down," I told the boy. "You can't win this and I don't want to add you to anything."

"You burned our boats."

"Yes."

"We'll starve."

"No," I said, "you won't. The canoes are untouched. The nets are untouched. Your gardens are untouched. I burned your ability to leave, not your ability to live. Tell your uncle, when the river brings him home, that the difference was deliberate."

The mother reached us then and pulled the boy back by the arm, and she looked at me with a hatred so clean and complete that it was almost restful. Hatred with no ambiguity in it. Most people hate me with qualifications — hate what I do, tolerate the man, or the reverse. She hated the whole package, no deductions, and I found I preferred it. It simplified the books.

We walked back up the path while the fire settled into its long work, and Voss fell in beside me, and for a kilometer neither of us said anything, which is how Voss and I conduct most of our conversations.

Finally: "The kid," he said. "Why?"

"Because in eleven years he'd have been a man with a reason, and reasons are expensive."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give."

He grunted. We walked. Behind us the village was already passing water bucket to bucket, saving what could be saved, and I noted — because noting is my profession — that they saved the church roof first and the elder's house second and their own homes third, and I filed that away under things about these people that my employer's spreadsheets would never contain.

* * *

Here is the part I don't put in reports, and barely put in my own head, and am putting down now only because a man alone on a river at night with a kerosene smell on his hands starts telling himself the truth whether he wants it or not.

I was not always this. Before the wars I was a schoolteacher. Two years, a valley not unlike this one, boys who called me *sir* and meant it. I taught arithmetic, which tells you the universe has a sense of humor. I left because of a woman, or because of a war, depending on which account you audit — the two arrived together, the way disasters do — and by the time I looked up from the wreckage I had discovered that I was very good at exactly one thing, and that the world pays that one thing extremely well, and that a man who is good at one well-paid thing does not get to also be innocent.

Ferrand thinks I'm a tool. I know she thinks this because she talks to me the way people talk to tools — precise, economical, with occasional irritation that the tool has opinions. Her cables come addressed to a number, not a name. I signed on under a number. I understood the terms.

But here is what Ferrand doesn't model, because her spreadsheets have no row for it: I remember the schoolteacher. He sits in the back room of me like an unpaid creditor, and every job adds interest, and some nights — nights like this one, smelling of burning resin and somebody's grandmother's cooking — the creditor calls in a little of the debt, and I pay it in small installments. A boy not shot. Canoes not burned. Dogs fed instead of killed. These are not mercies. They are minimum payments, and I know, the way any honest debtor knows, that minimum payments only delay the reckoning.

The cable had come that morning. I keep them all, folded in a tin, because a man should be able to produce evidence of his own instructions when the reckoning comes, and because reading them again is a form of penance I understand. This one said:

*PARTY LIKELY AT FALLS STOP RECOVER MATERIALS ANY MEANS STOP WITNESS PROBLEM RESOLVES ITSELF IF PARTY DOES NOT RETURN STOP FERRAND*

Read it twice and you see the architecture. *Witness problem resolves itself if party does not return.* She wasn't ordering deaths. She was ordering a situation in which deaths would be convenient and deniable, and leaving me to do the arithmetic, because that is what she pays me for — to be the man who converts her weather reports into bodies, so that her hands stay clean and mine stay busy.

Six people upriver, by my latest count. Delacroix the younger, whom I had never met but whose file I knew the way I know tide tables — salvage digger, competent, estranged, the kind of man who goes up rivers for fathers who don't deserve it. The botanist Okafor, feverish, last seen walking. Brand the surveyor, paranoid, armed, dangerous mostly to himself. Reyes the boatman, wounded or not. The hunter Kaelen, who had sworn an oath to people who could not protect him. And somewhere ahead of all of them, Armand Delacroix himself, guarding crates of a history that could unmake every claim Ferrand's institute had ever laundered.

Eight names, if you counted the old man's forecast correctly and Kaelen never came home. Eight lines in an equation. On the other side: one woman in a capital city who had never smelled a river in her life, and a fortune measured in documents proving that a valley full of living people had never actually been sold.

I sat on the deck of the launch that night with the tin of cables open on my knee and the fire still glowing orange against the clouds downstream, and I did the thing I do, the only thing I do, the arithmetic.

Option one: proceed to the falls, take the materials, resolve the witness problem per instruction. Cost: between three and eight lives, plus a village's memory, plus whatever is left of the schoolteacher after the creditor finishes calling. Probability of success: high, if the river cooperates. Probability of the river cooperating during early flood season: poor.

Option two: report the party lost to the rapids, come home empty, let Ferrand find a colder instrument. Cost: my contract, possibly my life — Ferrand's instruments retire badly; I have seen the retirement plan — but no new blood in the water. Probability of survival: unknown, which is a category of number I dislike.

Option three: the one I did not write down anywhere, the one that sat in the back room with the schoolteacher and refused to be priced.

I closed the tin.

"Course?" Voss asked from the wheel.

"Upstream," I said. "Slow. I want to reach the falls after they do."

"After? We're supposed to—"

"I know what we're supposed to do." I watched the last ember-light fade off the water, and thought about a boy with a fish knife, and an old man's ledger kept for God, and a river that had been counting longer than any of us. "But if we arrive first, we choose the ground blind. If we arrive second, we let them open the vault, carry the weight, mark the trail — and then we take everything at once. Materials, witnesses, the whole column. One entry. Clean."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true — arriving second *is* tactically superior, any professional will tell you so. But the truest part of it was the part I didn't say aloud, which was that every hour of delay was another hour in which the river might do my employer's work without my hands, and some part of me — the part that still owed money to a dead schoolteacher — wanted the river to have the chance.

Voss turned us upstream. The engine took up its complaint. Behind us, Tseremo shrank to a smudge of smoke and lamplight, and the boy on the bank watched us go with his uncle's jaw set against us, and I understood that I had just made an enemy with a long memory and short odds, and entered him in the book under *liabilities, accruing.*

Somewhere above us, in the green dark, a door under a waterfall stood freshly chiseled open, and a father and a son who had twenty years of silence between them were about to meet in a room full of gold and paper.

I gave us until dawn.

Then the arithmetic would have to balance — one way or the other, and for the first time in a long career of balancing it, I noticed that my own hands were shaking slightly on the rail, and could not tell whether it was the cold, or the kerosene, or the beginning of the reckoning the old man had promised me, arriving early, as debts do, with interest.
