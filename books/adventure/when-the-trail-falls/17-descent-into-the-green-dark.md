## 17. Descent Into the Green Dark

I want it on the record—I am a botanist, not a pilgrim. I catalog things. I press things flat between pages and give them Latin names so they can't surprise me. The rainforest does not care about my system of order. The rainforest has been running its own archive for forty million years, and mine is a pamphlet left out in the rain.

We left the boats at dawn on the third day after Brac burned the village fleet, or maybe the fourth—the days had begun to blur like wet ink. Tomas stood at the waterline with his arm bound in a sling made from a tent fly, arguing he could carry his share.

"You can't lift a kettle," Elodie said.

"I can lift half a kettle."

"You'll stay with the boats."

"The boats are ash," Brand said helpfully, from somewhere behind us. He had developed a talent for stating the worst possible fact at the worst possible moment, as if pessimism were a survival skill he intended to teach at university someday.

"The *canoes* are ash," Tomas corrected. "The boats are hidden. And I'm not staying anywhere."

What settled it wasn't argument. It was Teo, our hunter, who looked at Tomas's shoulder the way a man looks at weather he's decided to walk through anyway, and said, "He comes. Slowly." Then he turned into the trees and we followed, because by that point following Teo was simply what bodies did when their minds had run out of instructions.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about cutting inland through rainforest toward a waterfall you've only heard described by a feverish woman: the forest is not empty. It is the opposite of empty. It is a crowd pressed against you on all sides, breathing.

I knew this academically. I had published on it. Epiphytic load, canopy stratification, the staggering biomass per hectare—all numbers, all safe. But knowing a thing in a journal and knowing it against your skin are different literacies. Within an hour my shirt had become a second skin I would never be separated from again. Within two hours I had stopped flinching at the things that moved across my boots, because flinching cost calories and calories had become currency.

We lost supplies the way a bleeding man loses blood—not all at once, but steadily, in ways you only measure later. A canvas pack split along a seam Teo had warned us about, and a week of rice went into the mud before anyone noticed. The river took our soap on the first portage, which sounds trivial until you understand that soap was also our morale. Brand's theodolite case flooded and the instrument died with a small internal sound, like a sigh, and Brand sat down beside it in the wet leaf litter and grieved openly, and none of us laughed, because we each had our own dead packed away somewhere in our own canvas.

Mine was the specimen press. Forty pounds of collected plants, three months of work, pressed flowers from the valley that no one had cataloged. On the second day Elodie did the arithmetic aloud—weight versus distance versus Tomas's shoulder—and looked at me, and didn't say anything, which was worse than saying something.

"Burn them," I said. "Or leave them. Don't make me watch either one."

He left them under a root buttress, wrapped in oilcloth, with a note giving coordinates in case anyone ever came back this way. It was a stupid kindness. Nobody was ever coming back this way. I loved him for it anyway, in the specific way you love someone who wastes something on you when wasting is the only luxury left.

* * *

You learn people fast when the forest strips everything else away. You learn them faster than you want to.

Brand, for instance. In camp he'd been the paranoid one, the man who slept sitting up with a machete across his knees and accused everyone of being compromised. But on the trail, paranoia turned out to have a useful cousin called attentiveness. Brand noticed things. He noticed that Teo always paused three steps before any stream crossing, listening. He noticed that the leeches came in waves that tracked with the humidity, and started timing our rest stops around them. He noticed, on the fourth day, that we were being paced.

"Something parallel to us," he said quietly, crouched over a broken fern. "Not following. Flanking."

Teo examined the break without touching it. "Tapir," he said.

"It broke at shoulder height."

"Big tapir."

Brand looked at me. I looked at the fern. Neither of us believed in the big tapir, and neither of us said so, because there is a particular mercy in agreeing to be afraid together quietly rather than apart loudly.

Elodie, meanwhile—I kept watching him for the father in him and finding, instead, strangers. Armand Delacroix moved through wild country like a man reading a document he'd written himself, annotating, correcting. His son moved through it like a man learning the language from scratch, badly, humbly, asking Teo questions that would have made the old man wince. Which rope fiber holds a wet knot. Whether the orchids meant water below. How you know a storm is coming when the sky won't tell you. Teo answered most of them. Some he answered with silence, which Elodie had learned to read as *watch me and do what I do*, and he did, clumsily, and got better.

On the fifth night I found him rewrapping Tomas's shoulder by firelight, and Tomas was asleep, and Elodie worked one-handed and gentle, and I thought: the old man would have delegated this. The old man would have found someone whose job it was. The son does it himself, badly, every night, and pretends his hands don't shake.

"What?" Elodie said, without looking up. He'd learned to feel me watching. We all had. That was part of the intimacy too—the terrible kind, where privacy dies of exposure and what grows in its place isn't quite friendship yet, but isn't nothing.

"Nothing," I said. "Your knot's wrong."

"I know. It's holding anyway."

"That's the family motto, I expect."

He almost smiled. With Elodie that was practically a laugh.

* * *

My own sacrifice announced itself on the sixth day, and it was not noble.

It was my fever. It came back—the same low burn I'd carried out of the falls, the one that had walked me half-mad downriver until these people found me. I'd told myself it was gone. Bodies lie. Mine lied beautifully for five days and then, somewhere in the green hour between afternoon and evening, handed in its resignation.

I remember fragments. Teo's hands turning my wrist over, reading my pulse like a track in mud. Brand arguing about quinine rations with an intensity usually reserved for theology. Elodie's face above me, out of focus, saying my name in the tone people use when they've said it several times already.

And I remember saying—the fever talking, or maybe not—"Leave me. Go home. All of you, go home, it isn't worth it, whatever's under the falls, it isn't worth—"

"Hush," Elodie said.

"It isn't worth your lives. He sealed it for a reason. Even he knew—"

"Ines." Firmer now. "Hush. Drink."

They didn't leave me. Of course they didn't. I lay in the dark under a lean-to of palm fronds while the rain did its drumming and I understood, with the terrible clarity fevers grant and then revoke, that endurance is just devotion wearing work clothes. Nobody out here was enduring for glory. Brand endured because leaving meant admitting the world was as unsafe as he feared. Teo endured because his valley was at the end of this trail. Elodie endured because of a man he claimed to hate, carrying twenty years of silence uphill like ballast.

And I endured—well. I endured because I had seen the door under the falls, and some part of me, the part that presses flowers flat and gives them names, needed to know what was behind it. Curiosity is devotion too. Devotion to the truth, which is the coldest mistress of the lot, and the only one who never lies to you in the end.

* * *

The seventh day, the forest changed.

I was strong enough to walk, barely—Teo had fed me some bitter bark tea that tasted like punishment and worked like a miracle, and I intend to publish on it if I live, under a name that will make my colleagues weep. We climbed. The ground rose in long sweating switchbacks, the air thickened, and the character of the light shifted from green to a deeper green, almost blue, the light of old growth, of trees that had been arguing with the sky since before empires.

Teo stopped us at midday on a ridge and pointed, and there it was—not the falls themselves but their breath, a column of mist standing up off the valley floor like smoke from a chimney of the world. From this far it looked peaceful. It looked like a painting of peace. I knew better. I had been inside that mist, feverish, and I remembered the sound: not roaring exactly, but a pressure, a constant white noise that erased thought, that erased *you*, that made the vault behind it feel less like a discovery than like a hallucination the mountain permitted a few people at a time.

"How far?" Elodie asked.

"A day," Teo said. "If the trail is as I remember."

"And if it isn't?"

Teo looked at him for a long moment. "Then longer."

That night we camped early, in a hollow Teo chose, and ate the last of the decent food, and nobody spoke much. Tomas dozed against a log with his sling propped on his knee. Brand cleaned a machete that didn't need cleaning. And I sat with my back against a tree and pressed one last flower—a small red thing I'd picked that morning, growing straight out of a crack in bare rock, thriving on nothing—and slid it into the back cover of my field notebook, and thought: this is what we're made of, really. Not the plans. The pressing-flat. The refusal to let a thing vanish unrecorded.

Which is why, I suppose, I was the one still awake when Brand touched Elodie's boot in the dark.

* * *

"Smoke," Brand whispered.

I smelled it a heartbeat later—thin, wrong, domestic. Woodsmoke and something else beneath it, something chemical. Not a hunter's fire. Hunters burn dry wood and keep it small. This was wet wood and lamp oil, a fire built by people who wanted heat more than concealment, which meant people who weren't afraid, which meant people with numbers.

Teo was already a shadow moving upslope. We waited—an hour, it felt like a season—until he returned and crouched among us, and even in the dark I could see his jaw set the way stone sets.

"Four men," he said. "Below us, on the trail. Between here and the falls. They have a radio."

Elodie closed his eyes. When he opened them, he asked the question I was already dreading, the question that had been circling us like the flanker in the ferns since the first day:

"Did they see you?"

"No," Teo said. Then, after a pause that cost him something: "But they have a prisoner."

The word went through our little circle like cold water. Tomas lifted his head. Brand stopped cleaning the machete.

"Who?" Elodie asked.

Teo shook his head once. "Small. Tied at the wrists. They were laughing at him." Another pause, longer. "He was cursing them in French."

I felt it land in Elodie before he moved—saw it in the way his whole body went still, the stillness of a man hearing a voice he knows from a lifetime ago come out of a nightmare. Because there was only one Frenchman missing from our count. Only one brilliant, cowardly, impossible man unaccounted for since the camps went silent.

Armand Delacroix was alive. And he was half a day ahead of us, in the hands of Brac's men, on the trail to the door under the falls.

Elodie reached for his pack and began, silently, to buckle it tighter.

"*Wait*—" Brand started, and Elodie said, in a whisper with iron under it:

"They're taking him to the vault. Which means they haven't opened it yet. Which means we still have one night to be the ones who decide what happens there." He looked around at us—at Tomas with his ruined shoulder, at Brand with his machete, at me with my fever still smoldering in my blood like a banked coal. "All of you could still turn back. I want that said plainly."

Nobody moved.

"Well then," said Brand, into the dark, and for once in his life he sounded not paranoid but certain. "Let's go be witnesses."

We put out the fire and stepped into the green dark, downhill, toward the smoke.
