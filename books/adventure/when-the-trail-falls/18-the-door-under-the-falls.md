## 18. The Door Under the Falls

The hunter's name was Kaelen, and he walked the way water moves—without hurry, without hesitation, without any visible interest in whether we followed him or fell off the face of the earth. He had appeared at dawn on the third day of our inland march, stepping out of the green like a man returning from a stroll to check on some property he owned, which in a sense he did.

"You came," was all he said, looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse. Not surprise. Something closer to disappointment that had been expecting company.

"I swore," I said.

"Yes." He turned and started walking. "We will see what your oath weighs when you are standing inside."

I have thought about that sentence a great deal since. At the time it struck me as mysticism—the kind of thing elders say to make younger men feel small, a trick as old as temples. Now I understand it was simply arithmetic. He knew something about me that I didn't yet know myself: that oaths are easy to carry until they're heavy, and you never learn the weight until someone hands you the other end.

* * *

The rainforest did not get worse as we went inland. It got *more*. More rain, more mud, more roots arranged by some malicious gardener specifically at ankle height, more insects who regarded us less as intruders than as a delivery service. Ines Okafor, three weeks recovered from her fever but not from whatever she'd seen at the falls, kept pace beside me with the grim economy of a woman who had already died once out here and found it overrated.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," she said, for perhaps the ninth time that morning. It wasn't complaint, exactly. It was inventory. She liked to keep track of our reasons the way Tomas kept track of fuel, so she'd know precisely when we ran dry.

"Because my father sealed a door under a waterfall," I said, "and somebody has opened it from the wrong side."

"And because Ferrand's men are behind us."

"And because Ferrand's men are behind us."

"And because if we don't get there first, everyone who knows anything about it dies, including us, including possibly the valley, and definitely your father's reputation, such as it is."

"That one's optional," I said.

She gave me a look. Ines had a whole taxonomy of looks; this one was filed under *Elodie is lying to himself again, entry four hundred and six.* "You've been walking toward that door like a man walking toward his own funeral," she said. "Or his wedding. I haven't decided which."

Behind us, Lukas Brand carried the radio in its oilcloth sling and checked over his shoulder every forty seconds, a habit he'd acquired alone in the ruins of Camp Two and showed no interest in rehabilitating. He'd stopped counting Brac's possible positions aloud, which Ines said was progress. Kaelen moved ahead of us all, silent as a held breath, occasionally pausing to touch a tree trunk or move a leaf aside with two fingers, as though the forest were a house whose furniture he was politely straightening.

Tomas brought up the rear with his bad shoulder strapped tight against his chest, refusing help, refusing sympathy, refusing everything except the trail itself. His shoulder had been wrecked on the portage around the gorge—dislocated, badly reset by Ines with a stick, a strap, and language I won't reproduce—and every step inland cost him something he pretended was nothing. When I offered once to take his pack, he looked at me with genuine offense.

"The day I can't carry my own bag," he said, "put me in it."

* * *

We heard the falls before we saw them, which sounds obvious until you've heard them. It wasn't sound so much as weather—a low, continuous pressure in the air, felt in the sternum before the ears, growing hour by hour until conversation became gesture and gesture became shouting. The ground changed too, going soft, then spongy, then treacherous with black wet stone furred in moss that gave underfoot like meat.

Kaelen stopped us at midafternoon in a fold of terrain where the river we'd been paralleling suddenly dropped away into a gorge, and there it was.

I want to describe it honestly, which means admitting that my first feeling was not dread. It was awe, plain and stupid and total. The falls came down a wall of basalt maybe sixty meters high, not in one curtain but in a braid of white ropes, splitting and rejoining around islands of stone, throwing up a permanent fog that hung in the gorge like the mountain exhaling. Rainbows lived in that fog the way birds live in trees—several at once, casual, unimpressed. The noise erased thought. You could stand there and feel your own mind being wiped clean, and part of you wanted to let it.

"There," Kaelen shouted, pointing not at the falls but at their left flank, where the white water bent around a shoulder of rock.

I saw nothing. Then I saw it: a dark seam in the cliff behind the falling water, visible only when the wind pushed the curtain aside—a vertical slot, man-made, its edges too straight for geology. The door. My father's door. Sealed, according to Ines, with a collapse engineered by Armand Delacroix himself, using charges placed by men who were now scattered, mad, or dead.

We made camp above the gorge, out of the spray, and Kaelen laid out the approach with sticks and stones in the firelight. It was not complicated, which made it worse. We would descend the gorge wall on a root-ladder his people maintained. We would cross a ledge behind the lower falls, soaked through, single file, in near-darkness. And then we would stand in front of the door, and we would see.

"See what?" Brand asked. He'd been quiet all evening, which for Brand was louder than talking.

"What was done to it," Kaelen said.

* * *

Nobody slept well, which meant everybody lay awake listening to the falls, which sounded like the earth whispering the same enormous word over and over. Around what I judged to be three in the morning I gave up and found Kaelen sitting at the fire's edge, awake, watching the dark with the patience of a man who had been watching things for longer than I'd been alive.

"Ask," he said, without turning.

"Why did you come back for us? At the village, nobody would even say my father's name. Then you leave a token in my boat and vanish, and now you're walking us to the very place everyone acted like didn't exist."

He considered this for a while. Firelight moved on his face and didn't change it.

"When the foreigners came the first time," he said, "they asked questions the way children ask—touching everything, breaking some of it. Your father was different. He asked questions the way a thief cases a house. Quietly. Carefully. He learned our words. He sat with our elders. And when he found what he found—" Kaelen turned a stick in the coals, "—he came to us and told us the truth. That others would come for it. That he could hide it, or sell it, and he chose to hide it. He swore it would stay buried. We believed him because lying to us would have been easier."

"So you trusted him."

"We *bet* on him," Kaelen said. "It is not the same thing, and it is why I am here. A bet can go wrong. When it does, you don't walk away from the table. You collect." He looked at me then, fully, and the fire made his eyes unreadable. "You are his collection, Elodie Delacroix. Him, and the door, and whatever comes out of it. I am here to see what your family owes."

I didn't sleep after that either.

* * *

We descended at first light, though light down in the gorge was a rumor rather than a fact. The root-ladder was real and horrifying—living roots as thick as my wrist, laced down the basalt by generations of hands, slick with spray, creaking under weight like ship's rigging. Tomas went first despite his shoulder, on the logic that if the ladder failed he'd be the cheapest loss, and no argument to the contrary survived contact with his stare. Then Brand, then Ines, then me, then Kaelen, who climbed down facing outward, casually, like a man descending stairs he'd built himself.

The ledge behind the falls was everything Kaelen had promised and less. Half a meter wide in places, black stone polished by ten thousand years of spray, with the full weight of the river roaring past close enough to touch. We roped together and went sideways, pressed against the cliff, the waterfall a moving wall of cold concussion on our right. Water hit us not as rain but as presence—it got into my collar, my boots, my teeth. Shouting was useless. We communicated by rope-tugs and by Ines's hand on my back, pressing me forward at each hesitation.

And through gaps in the curtain, strobing past like frames of film, the door grew larger.

Here is what I had imagined, walking nine days inland: a crack in the rock, a hole, something geological wearing a human shape. What I had not imagined was architecture. As the falls thinned near the seam, the workmanship resolved out of the spray—dressed stone blocks fitted without mortar, each the size of a coffin, their faces carved in low relief with figures I recognized instantly because I'd spent a feverish night with them weeks ago, comparing Ines's ravings against the token in my pocket. The coiled serpent. The bird with a fish in its mouth. The row of seated figures whose hands were open, palms up, in a gesture that managed to be welcoming and warning at once.

And across the center of it all, a seal: a disc of fitted stones, two meters across, marked with a spiral that ended in a closed eye.

My father's work. I knew it the way you know handwriting. The collapse he'd ordered had brought down tons of basalt across this entrance—but he'd been careful, even in destruction. The seal remained legible. He had buried the door without erasing it, which told me everything about the man: he couldn't bring himself to destroy beauty, only access to it.

Ines reached the seam ahead of me and stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her. Her hand came up—not for balance. To stop me.

"No," she said. Just that. Just *no*, said to the stone, quietly, the way you speak to something you once begged for your life.

I squeezed past her into the gap.

* * *

The space between the seal and the cliff face was a throat of dry air, utterly still after the roar outside, so silent my ears rang with the absence. Kaelen lit a lamp. Its light found the walls, the floor, the ceiling of a vestibule cut by human hands before any map of this country existed—

—and found the rubble.

Armand's collapse should have filled this chamber. That was its entire purpose: tons of stone, deliberately dropped, a burial by engineer. The floor should have been a slope of broken basalt climbing back into darkness.

Instead the rubble lay *pushed aside*, stacked with methodical neatness along both walls, and the passage continued inward, open, cleared, floored with fresh-cut stone chips still pale and sharp-edged.

Brand saw it before I did. I heard his breathing change—that fast shallow pull of a man whose paranoia has just been promoted to prophecy.

"How long?" he whispered.

Kaelen crouched at the nearest stack of displaced stone and touched the cut faces. He rubbed his fingers. He smelled them. Then he did something I had never seen him do in twelve days of walking: he went still in a way that had fear in it.

"Days," he said. "Perhaps a week. The chips are dry. No mold on the cuts. Someone opened my father's-grandfather's seal from within, and recently."

"From *within*?" Ines's voice cracked on the second syllable. "There's no way in from within. Armand collapsed the only entrance. He told us—he stood right here and told us—"

"He told you what he believed," Kaelen said. He rose, and his voice had gone flat, controlled, the voice of a man rationing his own alarm. "But this door is older than his maps. Older than his people's stories of it. Whoever built it built *inward* as well as outward. There may be ways through this mountain that no living soul remembers." He paused. "Or the ones who remember are not souls."

Nobody laughed. Nobody even exhaled.

I knelt at the cleared threshold and picked up one of the stone chips. Pale, sharp, dry. Days old. Maybe a week. While I'd been raising a dead frequency in the dark and hearing a woman count numbers into static—while I'd been pulling Ines out of the bush and running rapids blind and bleeding on a portage—someone had been in here, calmly, patiently, unburying what my father had sworn to the valley would stay buried.

And they hadn't come in from outside. They hadn't needed to. They'd been *home* the whole time.

"Elodie." Ines's hand closed on my arm. Her grip was shaking. "The ledger page. At Camp One. Your father recorded the discovery weeks before his official log admitted it. We thought it was Ferrand—covering herself, backdating claims."

"I remember."

"What if it wasn't backdating?" Her eyes were huge in the lamplight. "What if the discovery recorded itself? What if something in there decided when it was found?"

I wanted to tell her she was exhausted, grief-stricken, still half-fevered. I wanted to say it was Brac's men, or treasure hunters, or any explanation with a human face, because a human face is a problem you can shoot or outrun or out-argue.

Then Kaelen raised the lamp higher, and the light reached down the cleared passage to where it bent out of sight, and we all saw it at once.

Mud. A line of footprints in the fine stone dust, leading *outward*—bare feet, narrow, longer than any foot I'd ever seen, the toes too many by one.

They led past us. Out of the door. Into the world.

Brand made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. Ines stepped backward into the spray. And far above us, muffled by tons of falling water, thin and impossible and unmistakable, we heard the expedition's emergency frequency crackle to life in Brand's oilcloth sling—a woman's voice, calm as arithmetic, counting down numbers it had begun counting before any of us were born.

Kaelen looked at me, and for the first time since the village, he looked afraid.

"They are not opening the door," he said. "They are answering it. Come. Quickly. Before whatever walks out finds your father first."
