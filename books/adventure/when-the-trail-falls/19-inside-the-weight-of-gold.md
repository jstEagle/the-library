## 19. Inside, the Weight of Gold

The door under the falls had been opened from the other side.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left by then. We'd cut inland for two days through rainforest that treated us like trespassers—leeches, rot, a rain so constant it stopped being weather and became a condition—and when we finally stood behind the curtain of falling water with our lamps shaking and our lungs full of spray, I expected to find my father's seals intact. His wax. His chalk marks. The careful archaeologist's grammar of *closed until properly excavated*.

Instead: fresh stone dust on the threshold. Chisel scars, bright as wounds. And the great slab standing a hand's width ajar, exhaling air that smelled of cold earth and something older than cold earth.

"Nobody's been here," Brand said, which was his way of saying somebody had been here.

Ines didn't say anything at all. She put her palm flat against the slab, the way you'd check a fevered child's forehead, and her face did something complicated. Grief and fury sharing a chair.

The hunter—Kwame, though he hadn't given us the name so much as let us overhear it—stood back from the entrance entirely. He would not cross under the water. He made that clear without words, which was his preferred language. He pointed at me, then at the gap in the stone, then held up three fingers. Three hours. Then he sat down on a root with his machete across his knees and became part of the forest.

Three hours to see what my father had killed himself hiding.

* * *

You should understand what I expected. I'd grown up on Armand Delacroix's dinner-table lectures; I knew what a pre-colonial vault was supposed to contain by the standards of every dig he'd ever bragged about. Pottery. Bones. Beads. The dignified debris of people who died too long ago to sue anyone.

What I got instead was gold that looked like yesterday.

The passage beyond the slab ran maybe forty meters into the hill, and it wasn't a tomb. That was the first wrongness. It was a *room*. A chamber cut with such geometric confidence that my lamp beam kept sliding along walls that met in perfect corners, floors level after five hundred years or five thousand—I couldn't tell, and neither could Ines, and she had the training. Along those walls, in niches carved at eye height, objects waited like books on shelves.

Ceremonial regalia, worked in gold so fine it moved when our breath touched it. Textiles whose dyes hadn't surrendered anything—not to time, not to damp, not to the centuries of river humidity that eat iron and bone alike within a generation down here. Wooden masks with pigment still wet-looking, still *present*, as if the carver had stepped out for water and meant to come back.

"It's not possible," Ines said, and she said it the way scientists say it, which is to say hungrily. She was already cataloguing under her breath. "The preservation profile is wrong. Everything about this is wrong. This isn't luck, Elodie. This is *engineering*. Sealed atmosphere, drainage, some kind of mineral lining—" She stopped herself, because we both heard it: she was describing exactly the kind of discovery that rewrites careers. Rewrites textbooks. Rewrites who gets to say what happened here.

And there, at the far end of the chamber, set apart on a stone plinth like an afterthought that was actually the whole point: a long box of dark wood, banded in copper, holding documents.

Paper. Actual paper, soft with age but whole. Bundles of it, tied with cord, stacked with the tidy despair of someone who understood they were leaving a message for strangers.

I untied the first bundle with hands that didn't feel like mine.

* * *

Here is what I can tell you about reading a document that proves your entire world is built on a polite fiction: your hands shake, and then they stop shaking, because the shaking feels disrespectful.

The script was old—older colonial Spanish, mixed with a native notation I couldn't read but Ines half could, enough to translate the bones of it aloud while I held the lamp. Deeds. Correspondence. A treaty record, witnessed and countersigned, establishing that the valley and its river rights belonged to its peoples *in perpetuity*, with boundaries drawn in language no crown lawyer had managed to argue away. And beneath that, later layers: colonial petitions acknowledging the claim. Court records. A chain of custody of *non-ownership*, unbroken, running right up to the edge of living memory.

"This valley was never ceded," Ines said quietly. "Not sold. Not lost. There's paperwork." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Five hundred years of lawyers, and the answer was sitting behind a waterfall."

Do you understand what that means? Not academically—in your chest? Every plantation deed, every mining concession, every government land grant in this valley descends from a lie. Or rather, from an omission. The documents don't just prove the past. They *unmake* the present. Whoever holds them holds the legal spine of an entire region.

Whoever holds them could break it, too. Burn them, and the fiction stands forever.

My father hadn't burned them. He'd sealed them behind stone and falling water and sworn three people to silence, and then he'd falsified his own logs to hide that he'd ever found them, and somewhere in that arithmetic he'd decided he loved truth more than Ferrand but less than control.

I stood there holding the perpetuity of a nation in hands that smelled of river mud, and I thought—with a clarity that shames me even now—*one crate.*

One crate of that gold would buy every future I'd ever wanted. My own institute, no more salvage scraps in the lowlands, no more answering telegrams from men who only remembered my name when they needed a Delacroix. One mask off that wall, quietly, and I'd never have to be anyone's son again. I could be simply, finally, myself—funded, free, unburdened.

The chamber hummed around me. Or my blood did. Hard to say.

"Elodie." Ines's voice came from very far away. "Your camera."

Right. Yes. That was why we were here. Not to take. To *record*.

* * *

I photographed everything. I want that on the record, since so much of what follows turned on the record.

Every niche, every object, front and oblique, scale bar in frame where I could manage it. The construction details Ines flagged—the drainage channels, the mineral crust on the walls, the ventilation shaft my father must have found and followed. The document bundles, page by page, my hands steadier now because work steadies me, always has. The light was terrible, my flash batteries dying by degrees, and I worked faster and sloppier than any archivist should, and none of it mattered because the content was the thing, and the content was fireproof.

Somewhere in the second hour Brand appeared beside me, which startled me badly—he moved like a man who'd learned invisibility in the trees and hadn't unlearned it yet.

"You're photographing the proof," he said.

"I'm documenting the site."

"You're photographing the thing that gets people killed." He wasn't accusing me. That was the worst part. He sounded like a man reading a weather report. "Ferrand doesn't want the gold. Gold can be bought anywhere. She wants these—" he tapped the document box, gently, almost fondly— "to stay a rumor. You're making them real. Real things have to be dealt with."

"Then she'll have to deal with them."

"She'll deal with *you*."

I kept shooting. But I'll confess what the shutter was really doing for me: each frame was a small act of deciding. Every exposure was me choosing the documents over the crate, the record over the inheritance, the stranger who'd signed that treaty five hundred years ago over the man I might still become if I got hungry enough. I took two hundred photographs and made the choice two hundred times, and by the end it had started to hold its shape, the way a habit does.

Near the end I found the last bundle, separate from the others, wrapped in oiled cloth. Inside: a single sheet in English, in handwriting I've known longer than any face I love. My father's. Dated six weeks ago.

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I have failed, and I find I cannot plan honestly for the difference. The contents of this room belong to the people the papers name. Do not let them leave this valley. Do not let them reach a capital. Do not trust the woman who paid for the boats.*

He'd known. He'd known what I'd feel standing here, because he'd felt it first, and he'd trusted a piece of paper to do what twenty years of silence never had: tell me who to be.

I hated him for it. I photographed the letter too.

* * *

We were ten minutes from done when Ines grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise.

"Listen."

Under the falls' white noise: voices. Downstream side. More than two. And the particular disciplined quiet of men moving because they've been ordered to move, not because they want to.

Kwame was already gone from his post at the entrance—or he'd never been there, or he'd been there the whole time watching us decide what we were—and the last thing I saw before we killed our lamps and pressed into the black of the passage was the slab, that beautiful, terrible door, beginning to move.

Someone was opening it from outside.

And from deep in the dark behind us, further into the hill than we'd explored, further than any map allowed—a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

Footsteps. Coming toward us.
