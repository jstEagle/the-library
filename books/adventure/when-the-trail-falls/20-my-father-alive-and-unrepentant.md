## 20. My Father, Alive and Unrepentant

The hunter—Kaelo, he'd finally given us a name on the third day of the inland march, as if names were rations to be spent carefully—stopped so suddenly that Ines walked into my back.

"Smoke," he said.

I smelled it too. Not cooking smoke. Not the green wet smolder of a lightning-struck tree. This was resin and cut hardwood, the smell of someone keeping a fire small and fed, the way you do when you don't want to be found but can't quite bring yourself to freeze.

"He's close," Ines whispered. She'd been saying some version of that for two days—not *he's alive*, which would have been hope, but *he's close*, which was worse, because it meant every step might be the one where I stopped being a son with grievances and became a son with an audience.

Kaelo held up two fingers, then pointed down-slope toward a fold of limestone where the jungle grew in a tangle of strangler figs. There was a cleft there, half-swallowed by roots, and from the cleft came the smoke-smell and something else: the particular silence of a place that has a man in it who is listening back.

"You should go alone," Kaelo said. "This is not my business." He said it the way he said everything, flat as a ledger line, and then he melted backward into the green with his machete and his terrible patience, leaving me holding a decision I'd been dodging since the telegram.

Brand wanted to come. Brand always wanted to come; paranoia is a social disease. "If it's him," Brand said, "he might be compromised. If it's not him, you're walking into a hole alone."

"If it's not him," I said, "then whoever's in that hole has been burning hardwood and keeping watch for weeks, and they deserve better than the two of us crashing around like tourists."

Ines put her hand flat against my chest—not an embrace, just a check, the way you steady a crate before lifting it. "Whatever you're going to say to him," she said, "say the true thing first. You only get one first sentence."

That's the trouble with botanists. They notice what grows.

I went down alone.

* * *

Twenty years is a long time to rehearse a conversation. I know because I did the rehearsal work. In salvage bars in the lowlands, in the grey hour before dawn when the river fog comes off the water like breath held too long, I had delivered this speech hundreds of times. Sometimes I shouted it. Once or twice, drunk enough to be honest, I forgave him entirely and woke up furious at myself for the weakness.

The speech never once began with what actually came out of my mouth, standing in the root-shadow of that cleft, looking at a man sitting against a crate with a rifle across his knees and his left leg bound in filthy canvas.

"Dad," I said. "You look terrible."

He looked up. And here is the thing I had not planned for, the variable no rehearsal accounted for: he didn't look surprised. He looked like a man who has been expecting a delivery and finally hears the truck. His beard had gone fully white, which was new—he'd been iron-grey at the estrangement—and he'd lost weight he couldn't afford, and there was a crusted wound high on his thigh that made my stomach drop in a way I refused to name. But his eyes were exactly the same. That was the obscenity of it. Twenty years, a jungle, a bullet or a blade or whatever had opened his leg, and Armand Delacroix still looked at me like a problem he intended to solve.

"Elodie." He said my name carefully, tasting it, checking whether it still worked. "You got my message."

"I got Ferrand's message. Two missed check-ins. Very official. Very concerned." I stayed where I was, ten feet off, because moving closer felt like conceding something. "Then I got shot at on the river, found your camp gutted, and pulled three of your people out of the bush one at a time. So if any part of this was your message, Dad, you've developed a hell of a writing style."

Something moved through his face—guilt, maybe, or its more durable cousin, regret—but he banked it fast. He'd always been good at that. My mother used to say your father could feel a feeling the way other men caught fish: briefly, expertly, and always with an eye toward releasing it.

"The camp wasn't mine to protect," he said. "Not after what I found. The fewer people who knew the location, the fewer people Brac could—" He stopped himself. Recalculated. "How many did I lose?"

"Ines Okafor. Lukas Brand. A river guide named Reyes who isn't even yours, technically, and a hunter called Kaelo who adopted us out of spite." I let that sit. "You want the number of the dead? Because I don't have it yet. Nobody does. That's the gift you gave everyone downstream of your discovery, Dad—a missing persons problem with a body count pending."

"That's unfair."

"Oh, thank God. I was worried we'd start this reunion with you being reasonable."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hating a man for twenty years: hate requires maintenance. It wants feeding. Every year I had to go back into the archive of my grievances and re-shelve them, dust them, confirm their provenance—the conference he missed, the graduation, the way he'd introduced me at fourteen to a visiting professor as "my associate" because *son* implied sentiment and sentiment implied liability. I had done the work. I had earned my anger the way he'd earned his reputation: methodically, over decades, with total commitment.

And then he looked at me with those same eyes and I discovered that all that careful curation amounted to nothing in the face of one unbearable fact, which is that he was hurt, and alone, and had been keeping a fire alive in a hole in the ground for weeks on the assumption—I understood this now, with a clarity that felt like vertigo—that somebody would eventually come looking.

He'd assumed it would be me.

That was either love or arrogance, and with Armand the two had always shared a bloodstream.

"Sit down," he said. "Your mother's son never could stand still while working an angle."

"Don't." The word came out harder than I intended, and I watched it land, watched him absorb it without flinching, and hated that too—hated that he'd take the hit rather than give me the satisfaction of a fight. "Don't invoke her. You don't get to invoke her."

"No," he agreed quietly. "I suppose I don't." He shifted, and the movement cost him; I saw the pain cross his face like weather. "But you should sit anyway. If Brac's scouts are following you—and they will be, Elodie, they've been running this river like a private company runs a ledger—we may have very little time, and there are things you need to understand before you decide what kind of fool you're going to be."

"There it is," I said. "There's the old man. Twenty years and the first real sentence out of you is a criticism of my judgment."

"It's not a criticism. It's a forecast." He patted the crate beside him. "Sit. Please."

*Please.* From Armand Delacroix. I sat, mostly because my legs had opinions of their own, and found myself inches from the crate, which was iron-banded and stenciled with a museum inventory code that had been scratched out and rewritten in my father's hand.

Up close, the wound on his leg smelled bad. Up close, everything about him smelled of woodsmoke and fever and the specific sourness of a man who has been his own nurse for too long. Up close, twenty years collapsed into about four feet of air, and I found I could neither cross it nor retreat from it.

"Start talking," I said.

* * *

I had imagined this meeting so many times that reality kept snagging on the details. In my imaginings I was always eloquent. In my imaginings he always flinched. In my imaginings there was rain, usually, because I am a novelist's idea of a dramatic man even inside my own head.

Instead there was firelight on limestone, and my son sitting an arm's length away wearing my stubbornness like a borrowed coat, and the only rain was the condensation dripping somewhere behind me in the dark of the cache.

He had Marisol's mouth when he was angry. That was the detail that undid me. Not the eyes—everyone said he had my eyes, and eyes are cheap, eyes are everywhere. But the mouth, the set of it, the way the lower lip went flat and forward before the words came—that was hers, and she had been dead eleven years, and I had sent flowers to a funeral I did not attend because attending would have required standing next to my son, and I had told myself at the time that grief was a private country and I had no passport.

Cowardice always sounds reasonable in the moment. That's how you recognize it later.

"I need you to listen before you speak," I told him, "because you inherited my talent for interruption and none of my reasons."

"Generous of you."

"I sealed the vault." I said it plainly, because the plain version was damning enough and dressing it up would insult us both. "After we opened the outer chamber—after Ines catalogued what was in there—I ordered it closed, disguised the approach, and split the documentation. Half went into the official log with dates falsified backward. Half came here, with me, into a hole I dug myself."

"Why?" And there it was, the question, arriving exactly on schedule, wearing exactly the tone I deserved.

"Because of what was in the crates, Elodie. Because of the documents." I reached behind me, slowly, an old man's careful movement, and drew out a leather folder gone soft with damp. "Pre-colonial charters. Treaties between the valley peoples and a crown that never honored them—which means the cession papers in the national archive, the ones that give men like Ferrand's investors title to half this watershed, are built on a fiction. These say the land was never sold. Never surrendered. Held in trust, in perpetuity, by families whose descendants are alive right now, fishing that river, farming that floodplain, legally invisible."

I let him take the folder. I watched him read. He read the way I taught him to read, before I stopped teaching him anything—fast, suspicious, twice.

"This rewrites ownership," he said. "Of the entire valley."

"Yes."

"And Ferrand knows?"

"Ferrand suspects. Ferrand's contract gives her claim rights to anything the expedition recovers. Which means these documents, in her hands, become leverage—or become ash. Either way, the valley loses." I leaned my head back against the stone. The leg was singing its usual song. "So I buried them. I lied on the log. I sent my team downriver with a story, and I stayed here with the truth and a rifle, waiting for either rescue or the end of the monsoon, whichever came first."

"And the camp? The slashed tents? Okafor walking out of the bush with a fever, calling it 'the door under the falls' like a woman reciting a spell?"

"Brac came the night after we sealed it. We scattered on my order. Deliberately. A team that scatters leaves no single thread to pull." I paused. "It seemed elegant at the time."

"And now?"

"Now it seems like what it was. An old man's vanity wearing a strategist's clothes." I looked at him straight on, because he deserved that much and I owed him that much. "I hid the find to protect it from Ferrand. I will not pretend otherwise. But ask yourself, Elodie—who taught me that the truth is a thing you control? Who published selectively for thirty years? Who decided, over and over, what the world was ready to know?" I smiled, and felt how thin it was. "You're angry at your own reflection. I recognize the face. It's mine."

* * *

He said it like a confession and a weapon in the same breath, which was the most Armand thing imaginable—even his surrender came pre-armed.

I turned pages. The documents were real. I'm not an archaeologist, but I grew up in the shadow of this work; I know provenance paper when I hold it, know the difference between a forgery's eagerness and an original's boredom. These documents were bored. Centuries bored. And they meant what he said they meant, and if they were right, then every lease, every concession, every development scheme stamped with government approval along this whole watershed rested on paper that these papers contradicted.

One crate. One crate of this would buy every future I'd ever pretended not to want. The dig of my own, funded forever, my name out from under his shadow, unshadowed at last by simple purchase. I felt the wanting arrive, warm and reasonable, introducing itself as prudence: *keep them safe, keep them hidden, decide later.*

I recognized the voice. It had his accent.

"So you falsified the record," I said, "to keep the truth out of the hands of a woman who'd falsify it worse. And the cost was your team scattered, your guide shot at, Okafor half-dead in the bush, and me—" I stopped. Started again. "And me spending twenty years not speaking to you, and then three weeks nearly dying to reach a man who apparently planned to sit in this hole until the monsoon ended, guarding boxes, alone, with a hole in his leg."

"Somebody had to."

"No, Dad. Somebody had to *tell someone*. That's the job. That was always the job, and you treated it like a negotiation where you got to hold things back." I stood up. My knees cracked; I was thirty-four and I sounded like him. "Ines begged us to turn back. Brand won't sleep near the rest of us. Tomas took shrapnel in the shoulder portaging the gorge—for your secret, a secret he didn't even believe in. And you're going to sit there and tell me the lie was elegant."

"I'm going to tell you," he said, and his voice dropped into something I hadn't heard since I was a child eavesdropping past bedtime—something raw, unedited, the voice under the lectures, "that I have spent twenty years being right in ways that cost me everything except the work, and the work was the only thing that never left. And then I found this vault, and I understood, standing in front of it, that here at last was a thing worth being wrong for. Worth lying for. Worth—" he gestured at the leg, at the fire, at the whole ridiculous hole in the earth, "—this. I chose wrong, Elodie. I know it. But I chose it for a reason you will understand the moment Brac's boat rounds the bend below us, because that man does not carry lawyers. He carries shovels."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. The fire popped.

"You should have told the museum," I said. "From the first day. You should have told everyone."

"Yes," my father said simply. "I should have. I am, it turns out, exactly the man you always said I was." He looked at me then with an expression I had no shelf for—no grievance fit it, no memory filed it. Something naked and old and terrified. "The question is what you are. You've read the documents. You've felt the weight of them. So tell me, son—are you going to finish what I started, or are you going to be the first Delacroix in three generations to do the right thing at the wrong moment and call it character?"

Before I could answer, Kaelo's whistle came down the slope—two notes, low, urgent. The signal we'd agreed on. Boats on the water below.

My father reached for his rifle, and I saw him wince, and I understood with perfect, sickening clarity that whatever happened next, it was going to happen at dawn, and it was going to happen here.
