## 22. The Ambush at the Cache

Dawn in the rainforest doesn't arrive so much as seep. The dark thins by degrees, gray to pearl to a green-gold wash that turns every leaf into a small lamp, and for about four minutes the whole world looks like something out of one of my father's lantern slides—the ones he'd project on the parlor wall when I was nine, narrating in that voice he used for audiences instead of sons.

I was thinking about those slides when the first bullet came.

Not because I sensed danger. Because I was tired enough that my mind had wandered to safer country, and because the light was beautiful, and because I have learned—this trip has been nothing if not educational—that beauty is usually the last thing standing between me and catastrophe. The river had been beautiful before the shots crossed it. Ines's fever had broken at dusk, and she'd been beautiful, sleeping with her cheek against a supply crate like a woman napping on a train. Even Brand, hunched over his instruments with his paranoia temporarily in remission, had looked almost human in that light.

The bullet took the top off a crate of rice six feet from my head. Rice geysered up white as snow, absurdly festive, and then the sound arrived—a flat crack rolling back off the gorge walls, doubled and tripled until it seemed the cliffs themselves were shooting at us.

"Down!" Tomas was already moving, which is the thing about Tomas: he complains about everything except the moments that matter, and in those moments he moves like water finding its level. He hit me mid-spine with his good shoulder and put us both behind the stone lip of the cache entrance, and a second round sparked off rock where my head had been conducting its sentimental tour of childhood memories.

"Brac," I said. It wasn't a question. Nobody else in this valley hated us with such professional patience.

"Dawn attack." Tomas spat rice dust. "He waited for the light. That means he wants to see what he's killing."

That should have comforted me. A man who checks his targets is a man with limits. Instead it made my hands shake worse, because I understood the corollary: Brac wasn't shooting blind into the camp hoping for luck. He was shooting *selectively*. And the thing he wanted most—the crates, the documents, the proof that could unmake Ferrand's claim—was twenty feet from where we crouched, guarded by a botanist with a rifle she'd never fired in anger, a surveyor who trusted no one including himself, a hunter whose family lived downstream of Brac's mercy, and me.

*Me.* Six weeks ago my most dangerous possession was a trowel with a cracked handle.

* * *

Let me reconstruct those first ninety seconds properly, because memory afterward went to porridge and I've had to rebuild it from evidence, which is at least a trade I know.

The cache sat in a fold of limestone below the falls' thunder—a natural alcove my father had walled with dry-stone and camouflaged so well that even standing ten feet from it you'd call it geology. Inside: eleven crates. Documents in oilcloth. Gold and jade and things older than any name the valley had ever been given. Armand had been sleeping against them like a dragon with a head wound, bandaged, furious, alive.

Our camp proper—two tents, the fire pit, the boats' remaining stores—was strung along the terrace above, maybe forty meters of open ground between us and the treeline across the stream. That treeline was where the shooting came from. Three positions, maybe four, spaced wide. They'd been in place before first light. Which meant they'd moved through the night, through country that had taken us two days and a hunter named Kaelen to cross, and nobody had heard them.

"They were here when we arrived," Kaelen said later, and I believed him, and I have not fully stopped being cold about it since.

Ines came off her crate at the first shots like a soldier, which surprised everyone including Ines. She'd spent the previous day burning at forty degrees and begging us to go home; now she rolled behind the stone wall, checked her rifle's action with fingers that knew it, and said, "Left side, past the fig tree—they'll try to flank toward the water."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's what I'd do, and I've been lying here all night listening to them get into position." Her eyes were clear and terrible. Fever sometimes burns a person down to their load-bearing beams. "Elodie. The crates. If they take the crates, everyone who saw them dies anyway. You understand? Dead men tell no tales, but neither do men who got what they came for."

I did understand. I'd been understanding it in increments since the ledger page in Camp One, since the radio orders, since the burned boats. But there is a difference between understanding a thing and having it stand next to you breathing while bullets knock rice out of the sky.

Brand was the problem. Brand was always the problem. He'd gone to ground under the overturned canoe and refused to come out, calling in a voice pitched high and cracking that it was a trap, all of it, that Brac had let us find the cache precisely so he could kill us gathered around it. And the awful thing—the thing I still turn over at three in the morning—is that Brand's theory wasn't crazy. It was only wrong. There's a difference, and the difference was about to cost us blood.

* * *

Armand saved us first. My father, who hadn't run more than fifty meters since his wound, who moved like a man negotiating with his own body, came out of the cache mouth low and fast and dragged the nearest crate—not gold, not jade, but the oilcloth packet of documents—back behind the stone. Then he stood up in the open, deliberately, an old man silhouetted against the falls, and shouted in Spanish and then in the river tongue, words I didn't know but whose grammar was unmistakable:

*You want these? Come and take them from my hands.*

It was either magnificent or insane, and with Armand the categories have never been mutually exclusive. It also worked, briefly. The firing stopped. In that silence I heard the stream, the falls, my own pulse—and then a voice from the treeline, quiet and carrying, a voice I'd heard once through a captured radio and recognized the way you recognize a knife:

"Professor Delacroix. Put the packet down and walk away. My contract is with the objects, not with you."

Brac. Close. Fifty meters, maybe less, close enough that I could hear he wasn't breathing hard. A man doing arithmetic with lives, Ines had called him once, and I'd thought it metaphor. Now I understood it as literal. He was counting. He was deciding how many of us the objects were worth, and the answer kept revising downward as the morning went on.

"You'll burn them," Armand called back. "That's your arithmetic. Burn the originals, keep the copies, own the story."

"I follow orders, Professor. I don't write them."

"Neither did the men who burned the library at Alexandria, I imagine. Someone wrote *them* orders too."

A pause. Long enough that I started to hope—stupidly, filially—that some door in Brac would open. Then Brac said, almost gently, "Last chance," and the world came apart.

They didn't shoot at Armand. They shot at the cache wall above him, at the dry-stone and the shelf of limestone, and I finally grasped the plan a half-second too late to do anything but scream: they weren't trying to kill us. They were trying to *bury* us. Bring the alcove down, seal the vault with my father inside it, dig out the crates at leisure. No witnesses, no firefight, no bodies to explain. Just a rockslide in unmapped country, and a story about flood season.

The first charge—I never saw it, only heard it, a dull cough from the treeline—took the top course of the wall off. Stone came down like judgment. Dust went up in a column, and through it I saw Armand stagger, saw him clutch the document packet to his chest, saw him go down not from a bullet but from the concussion, folding like a coat dropped off a hook.

And the cache caught fire.

Later, Kaelen explained it: the charges had cracked a seep of natural gas in the limestone, or perhaps Brac had simply included incendiaries among his tools, because why bury witnesses when fire does both jobs. It didn't matter. What mattered was flame licking up through the dust, orange and obscene against all that green, and eleven crates of the oldest truth in the valley beginning to cook.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I record it without pride because there wasn't any in it. There was only the animal arithmetic of a son.

I ran for the fire.

Tomas grabbed for me and missed. I remember his hand closing on air and his voice saying my name in a tone I'd never heard from him—not annoyance, not grievance, but naked fear, the fear of a man watching someone walk into water he knows drowns people. I ran through smoke that tasted of hot stone and old wood, and I got two crates out—one under each arm, staggering, eyes streaming—and I turned for a third, and that is when the shooting resumed, because Brac's patience, whatever else it was, was finite.

The third crate was heavy. Documents, I think, or the jade. I had it chest-high when the bullet came.

I want to be honest about the physics of it, because the movies lie. I didn't see Tomas move. I felt him. One moment I was upright with a crate in my arms; the next the crate was gone and the sky was where the ground should be, and Tomas was on top of me, and there was a sound like a fist hitting wet canvas, and it took me three full seconds to understand that the sound had been a bullet entering my friend's back.

He rolled off me and lay in the ash line, and his shirt was already dark, spreading, terribly fast, and his face had gone the color of paper left out in rain.

"Tomas—"

"Get the crate," he said. His voice was steady. That was the worst part; it was completely, maddeningly steady. "It's heavier than it looks. Use your legs."

"Tomas, you're shot—"

"Yes." He frowned at his own chest with mild academic interest, the way he looked at cargo manifests. "Well. I always said your father would be the death of me. Turns out it was you. Closer than I expected."

Ines was suddenly there—she must have run the whole forty meters under fire, and I will never understand how none of those bullets found her—kneeling, cutting the shirt away, her hands doing triage with a speed that frightened me more than the blood. "Through and through," she said, mostly to herself. "Below the shoulder blade. He's lucky. He's *lucky*, Elodie, help me hold him down."

"He's shot!"

"And if you stand there narrating it, he'll be dead! Pressure. Here. Both hands. *Now.*"

I put my hands where she said and pressed, and Tomas hissed, and then—God help me—he laughed. An actual laugh, thin and bloody at the edges, but real.

"There it is," he wheezed. "The Delacroix grip. Your father has the same hands. Crushes everything he holds onto and calls it dedication."

* * *

The rest happened in pieces, and the pieces don't fit together neatly, so I'll give them to you as they come.

Piece one: Kaelen. While we knelt in the ash, the hunter did the thing hunters do and the rest of us only pretend to understand. He vanished. No signal, no word—just gone into the smoke on the flank, and four minutes later the treeline erupted in shouting that wasn't ours, and two of Brac's positions started shooting at each other in the confusion, and a man screamed somewhere in the green in a way I will hear for the rest of my life.

Piece two: Brand. The man under the canoe, the coward, the paranoid—the man whose theory had been wrong but not crazy—crawled out of cover during the chaos, reached the cache mouth, and did the single most useful act of the entire expedition: he threw a coil of rope over the burning lintel stone and hauled the remaining crates out by main strength, singeing his eyebrows off, coughing, cursing in three languages, refusing all help. When it was done he sat down hard in the mud and shook, and said, to no one, "I told you it was a trap," and began to laugh, and Ines put a hand on his shoulder and let him.

Piece three: the fire won anyway. We saved seven crates of eleven. Four burned—two of them, Armand told me afterward in a voice like gravel underwater, had held the ceramics, the ones that couldn't be replaced, the ones that proved the depth of time. He watched them go with an expression I couldn't read then and can read now, because I've seen it in mirrors since: a man watching part of himself become smoke and understanding that he'd helped choose which part.

Piece four: Brac withdrew. Cleanly, professionally, the way water withdraws from a beach. By full light the treeline was empty, and the only evidence he'd ever been there was shell casings, a smear of blood Kaelen wouldn't discuss, and the silence.

* * *

We treated Tomas on the terrace above the smoking ruin, because moving him was impossible and staying was nearly as bad, and because Ines said—cleaning the wound with boiled water and the last of our iodine—that the bullet had missed everything that kills quickly and hit enough that matters slowly. A lung bruised, maybe nicked. Muscle torn. Fever certain. Travel, in days, possible. Travel, today, fatal.

"So we stay," I said.

"We stay," she agreed. "And Brac knows it. He'll count our supplies from the ridge tonight. He doesn't need to attack again, Elodie. He just needs to wait. We're a fixed position now. Fixed positions are just graves with better organization."

Tomas, propped against a log with a blanket around his shoulders and a bullet hole through him, listened to all this with his eyes closed. Then he said, "Give me the rifle."

"No," said Ines.

"Give me the rifle and leave me the radio and go. I can't travel anyway. I'm dead weight with excellent aim." He opened his eyes and looked at me, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before—something settled, resolved, a debt being counted out to its final coin. "Besides. I owe this river a conversation, and I'd rather have it alone."

"What conversation?" I asked, and immediately wished I hadn't, because his face did something complicated, and Armand, sitting apart with the document packet still clutched to his chest like a child with a stolen sweet, looked away.

"The one about your father," Tomas said. "And a year I've never talked about. And why a man who hates Armand Delacroix as thoroughly as I do keeps pulling him out of rivers." He shifted, winced, and grinned at me with blood on his teeth. "But that's a story for when you're not pressing on my lung. Get the camp packed. Get the crates hidden. And Elodie—"

"Yes."

"When they come back at dawn tomorrow—and they will—you make sure you're gone before the light. Promise me that much. Let me be the last stupid thing this expedition spends."

I didn't promise. I said I'd think about it, which is what cowards say, and he knew it, and let me have the lie, which is its own kind of kindness.

* * *

Night came early under the smoke haze. We buried what remained of the burned crates' contents in a trench Kaelen cut with his machete—ash and shattered ceramic and half-melted gold, because gold doesn't burn, it only changes shape, which struck me as the most honest thing metal has ever done. Armand spoke a few words over the trench. Not a eulogy. An inventory. He listed what had been lost the way other men list the dead, and I realized, watching him, that for my father the distinction had never existed.

Then I sat down next to Tomas, who was breathing shallowly and watching the stars come up through the smoke gap, and I asked him the question I'd been carrying since the first rapids.

"The debt," I said. "Everyone keeps saying you owed my father. Nobody says for what."

Tomas was quiet long enough that I thought he'd fallen asleep. Then, without opening his eyes, in a voice sanded down by blood loss and honesty, he began to talk.

What he told me belongs to the next day, and to him, and I won't spend it here. But I will say this much: by the time he finished, the fire in the cache had gone to embers, the ridge was silent, and I understood that the man lying beside me had been paying for my father's life in installments for twenty years—and that tomorrow, unless I found a way to stop him, he intended to pay the balance all at once.
