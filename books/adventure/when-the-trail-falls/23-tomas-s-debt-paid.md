## 23. Tomas's Debt, Paid

Pain is an honest accountant. It keeps its books in the open, itemizes every entry, and never lets you forget what you owe. My shoulder had been keeping accounts since dawn, and by midmorning it was demanding interest.

Ines had dug the bullet out with a boat knife boiled in river water, which is exactly as pleasant as it sounds. She'd packed the wound with the last of the antiseptic powder, bound it tight, and given me a look that said she'd done her part and the rest was between me and God. Then she'd gone back to Elodie to argue about maps, because that was the only conversation anyone was having anymore: where next, how fast, who carries what.

I lay under a lean-to of palm fronds and watched the river go by, doing what I do best, which is nothing loudly.

The bullet had gone through the meat below my collarbone and out the back, clean enough, Ines said, if you liked your wounds like you liked your fish—gutted fast and bled out on the planks. I'd taken it standing in front of Elodie Delacroix at the cache, which surprised me more than anyone. I'd spent twenty years telling myself I hated that family. Turns out hate is just a coat you wear over something else, and when the shooting starts, the coat comes off whether you want it to or not.

Armand came to sit with me around noon.

He moved like a man made of hinges someone else had oiled badly—wounded himself, burned hands bandaged, pride dented worse than either. He lowered himself onto a crate across from my cot with the care of a man sitting down to a negotiation, and maybe that's what it was. Maybe it always had been.

"Tomas."

"Señor Armand." I gave him the old title like handing back a borrowed tool. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other side of the argument." His mouth did the thing it used to do, half a smile, quickly rationed, as though joy were a supply he'd learned to stretch. "Ines says you'll live."

"Ines says I'll live slow. There's a difference."

"Yes." He looked at his hands. The bandages were already gray at the knuckles. "There is."

The river talked behind us. It always talks; that's the thing about rivers nobody tells you until you've lived on one—you can't get them to shut up, and after a while you stop wanting to. Silence on land is empty. Silence on water is full.

"I owe you an accounting," he said.

I laughed, and the laugh pulled the wound, so the laugh turned into something uglier. "An accounting. You sound like Ferrand."

He flinched. Good, I thought. Let it cost you.

"No," he said quietly. "Ferrand counts things so she can own them. I'm trying to count things so I can pay for them. It's a different arithmetic, even if the numbers are ugly."

* * *

Here is the story I never told him, and I'll tell it now the way I finally told it then, flat, without decoration, because decoration is how men lie to themselves and I had done enough of that for two lifetimes.

Twenty-two years ago I was a young boatman with a young wife and a baby coming, working the middle river for whatever cargo paid. Armand hired me for a survey run—three weeks upriver, good money, and a reputation that would bring me other contracts. I was proud to take it. Proud to be chosen by the great Delacroix, whose name opened doors in the capital that men like me couldn't even find in the dark.

On the fourth day we came through the narrows above the gorge. High water, early rains, the kind of channel that shifts overnight. Armand wanted to push through. The survey schedule demanded it, and Armand served his schedules the way priests serve their saints. I told him it was wrong. I told him the water was lying. He overruled me—it was his expedition, his contract, his name on the permit—and I steered us into it anyway, because I was paid to steer and too green to refuse harder than I did.

We lost the second boat. Not to the river—to the rocks, and then to the river, which took it apart with the patience of all large indifferent things. Two men went in. One came out. The one who didn't come out was Mateo Sandoval, my cousin, nineteen years old, who'd begged me for the berth because he wanted to see the unmapped country and I'd wanted, God forgive me, to show off a little, to have someone aboard who'd tell our grandmother about the great things Tomas Reyes was doing with his life.

Armand stood on the bank afterward, soaked, shaking, and he said—I have carried this sentence for twenty-two years, so I know its weight to the gram—he said: *This is on me. I will carry it.*

And then he didn't.

Oh, he sent money. He wrote letters. He put Mateo's name in the acknowledgments of a monograph nobody read, spelled correctly, which I suppose was meant to be a kindness. But he did not come to the funeral, because there was a conference. He did not come to our house, because there was another expedition. He did not say the words again, not once, not ever, and I understood—slowly, the way you understand anything true—that what he'd meant was not *I will carry this grief beside you.* What he'd meant was *I will carry this guilt privately, in a room you are not invited into, and I will call that carrying.*

So I hated him. Professionally. Thoroughly. The way a man hates a current: without expecting the current to notice.

But here is the part I left out even in my own telling, all these years, the part that sat in my chest like a stone in a boot: I could have refused him. At the narrows, with the water lying, I could have tied off, waited a day, lost the schedule and kept the man. I was the pilot. The river answers to the pilot or it kills everyone. I let his name outweigh my knowledge, because his name fed my pride, and Mateo died in the gap between those two vanities.

That was the debt. Not his alone. Ours. And a shared debt is the worst kind, because each man spends his whole life waiting for the other to pay first.

* * *

"So," I said to him now, under the palm fronds, with my shoulder screaming its honest ledger. "You wanted an accounting. Here it is. All of it. Even the parts that make me look bad, which—" I gestured at the bandage, the camp, the burned cache, the general catastrophe of the last three weeks, "—you may have noticed is most of them."

He listened without moving. That was always his gift and his crime: he could listen like stone, take everything in, and give back nothing you could hold.

When I finished, he was quiet a long time. The river filled it, the way rivers do.

"I didn't know about Mateo being your cousin," he said finally. "Not until years later. When I found out, I—" He stopped. Started again. "There is no version of the next sentence that isn't cowardice, Tomas, so I'll say the cowardly one: I stayed away because staying away was easier than standing in front of you."

"And now?"

"Now Brac shot you standing in front of my son, and I find I've run out of places to hide." He took a breath. "I am sorry. Not the word—the thing itself. The thing itself is late by twenty-two years and it knows it."

I looked at him. Really looked, maybe for the first time since I was twenty-four years old and star-struck. What I saw was an old man in bandages who had spent his whole brilliant life building a museum around his own mistakes and calling it scholarship. A man who sealed a vault to protect a valley and falsified a log to protect himself, and could not, right up to the end, tell the difference between those two acts. A man who loved the truth the way some men love wild animals—from a distance, behind glass, feeding it on a schedule.

And underneath all of it, still faintly warm, the man who had once sat up all night with a seasick young boatman teaching him to read stars, laughing at his own terrible jokes, treating him—for one season, before the narrows—like a colleague instead of a hire.

"I don't forgive you," I said. "I want that clear. Forgiveness is a door and I'm not ready to walk through it. But I'm putting down the ledger."

His head came up.

"The debt," I said. "Mine and yours both. I'm releasing it. Not because it's paid—it'll never be paid, Mateo's dead, dead doesn't invoice—but because carrying it has been costing me more than owing it ever did. Twenty-two years I steered my whole life around one bend in one river. That's over. I'm tired of the detour."

Something moved in his face—not relief, nothing so cheap. More like a man setting down a load so heavy his arms keep remembering the weight for hours after.

"What do I owe you, then?" he asked. Which was, I had to admit, the correct question at last. Not *what do you want*, not *how can I fix this*. What do I owe.

"Nothing," I said. "That's what released means. Look it up."

* * *

Elodie came at dusk, when the light went copper and the birds started their evening arguments. He crouched by my cot with that face of his—all his father's bones arranged by a different, kinder architect—and studied me the way he studies stratigraphy, reading layers.

"You're going to say something noble," he said. "I can tell. You've got the face on."

"The face on."

"The one where you're pretending you haven't decided yet, but your jaw decided Tuesday."

I almost laughed, and paid for it. "Your jaw decide anything lately?"

"Constantly. Mostly wrong things." He settled onto his heels. "Talk."

So I talked. I laid it out the way I'd worked it out through the hot afternoon while Ines changed my dressing and pretended not to listen, because Ines hears everything and pretends it's a professional skill.

Brac had two boats. We'd burned one at the cache—or rather, the fire had, and none of us were going to split hairs about authorship—but the other was intact, and Brac was alive, and Brac did not strike me as a man who retired wounded from a fight. He'd regroup. He knew the river. And he knew, as surely as he knew his own trigger finger, that we had to come down past the gorge narrows, past the drowned forest, past every choke point between here and the delta, and that we'd be slow—one wounded boatman, one wounded old man, a botanist running on stubbornness, a paranoid surveyor, and a hunter guiding for strangers.

Slow boats get caught. That's not pessimism. That's hydrology.

"But," I said, "a slow boat with one man aboard, moving loud, running lights at night, radio chattering on the frequencies Brac monitors—that boat is interesting. And an interesting boat gets followed. And a followed boat gets boarded. And a boarded boat costs the boarders time. Time they're not spending on the real one."

Elodie went very still. "No."

"It wasn't a question."

"Tomas. You can barely lift your arm."

"I can lift it enough to steer with the good one and die with the other, which covers the job description." I kept my voice level, because if I let it climb he'd hear what was under it. "Listen to me, boy. Your father owes me a death. Not mine—Mateo's. I've spent two decades deciding whose death would settle it, and every answer I found was ugly. This one isn't ugly. This one is arithmetic even I can love: my slow boat buys your fast one a day. Maybe two. On this river, in flood season, a day is a country."

"That's not arithmetic, that's suicide with a rudder."

"That's what I said about the narrows, and I was wrong there too. Seems to be a pattern with me and water."

He was quiet. The copper light failed by degrees. Somewhere behind us Brand was rigging the radios, and I heard him testing, testing, the little chirp of carrier waves like insects.

"Your father offered to stay," Elodie said at last, low. "I told him no. He's in no condition, and—" He stopped, jaw working. "And I'm not losing both of you in one week."

"He offered?" That surprised me. Old Armand, volunteering for sacrifice—twenty-two years ago he'd have calculated the odds and hired someone else to stand in the open. People change, apparently. Slowly, expensively, the way rivers cut canyons.

"He offered," Elodie said. "Badly. With conditions."

"Of course with conditions." I reached over with my good arm and gripped his forearm, and felt him not pull away, which from a Delacroix is practically an embrace. "Elodie. Hear me. This isn't you sending me. This isn't duty, and it isn't debt—God knows we've had enough of debts on this river to float a cathedral. This is me choosing. First time in twenty-two years I've chosen something instead of being pushed by it. Let me have it. A man should get to pick the current that takes him, at least once."

"That's a horrible sentence."

"It's a true one. True sentences usually are."

He looked out at the river a long moment. When he spoke again his voice had gone rough. "What do I tell Ines? She'll want to strap you to the deck and haul you along out of spite."

"Tell her the truth. She'll respect it. Terrifying woman, that one—she respects things."

"And Brand?"

"Brand stays with you. He's earned it. Tell him his counter-signals saved us and watch him become insufferable for a week. It'll be good for him." I let go of his arm. "And you—"

"I know."

"Do you? Because I'm going to say it anyway, and you're going to stand there and take it like your father never could." I made him look at me. "Run the truth down that river, boy. Loud. Every frequency, every village, every ear that owns a radio or a grudge. Brac can't shoot a secret that everybody's already heard. You figured that out before any of us. Don't you dare get shy about it now because an old boatman bought you a day."

* * *

They launched me at first light, which is the hour liars and heroes prefer, being the only ones awake to witness each other.

Ines packed the wound again, checked the bindings twice, and then did something I didn't expect: she pressed her flask into my hand. Quinine and rum, she said, for the fever, and if I wasted it she would know, because she seemed to believe she'd know things at any distance, and honestly I believed her too.

Brand shook my hand like it was a business transaction he intended to win. The hunter—Kwame, though he answered to nothing so reliably as to silence—touched two fingers to his chest and then to mine, which among his people, he informed me, was what you did for a man walking ahead of the others. Walking ahead. I liked that translation better than decoy. Decoy is what you call bait when you're being polite to it.

Armand came last. He stood at the water's edge with his bandaged hands at his sides, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we'd said the big things yesterday and small talk between us would have been like lace on a coffin.

"Mateo," he said finally. "Tell me about him sometime. When this is over. Whatever you remember. All of it."

"He sang off-key," I said. "Terribly. Whole boat suffered."

"Then I'll suffer it too." He nodded once, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

Elodie held the painter while I got the tiller sorted one-handed, and for a second he just looked at me across the gunwale, and I saw it plain as a mark on a chart: he was afraid, and he was letting me go anyway, and that—more than the photographs, more than the broadcast he was planning, more than all his fine decisions—was the moment I stopped thinking of him as his father's son and started thinking of him as his own man entirely.

"Reyes," he said. "Come downriver after. Come find us. That's not a request."

"Everything's a request on a river," I said. "The water decides what it grants."

I pushed off. The current took the bow the way it takes everything—without malice, without mercy, without once looking back—and I turned us downstream toward the narrows, running my lamp in broad daylight where Brac's watchers couldn't miss it, keying the radio every quarter hour with cheerful, stupid, magnificent noise.

Behind me, fainter and fainter, I could hear the other engine starting. Going the other way. Fast.

Ahead of me, around the first bend, the sky over the gorge had gone the color of a bruise, and the wind that came off it smelled like rain that hadn't fallen anywhere yet but was clearly in no mood to wait.

Early floods. Of course. The river keeps its own schedule, and it had never once cared whom it owed.
