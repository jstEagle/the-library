## 24. Running the Flood Season

The rain came in the night the way bad news comes—first a rumor at the edge of hearing, then all at once, everywhere, and no way to pretend you didn't notice.

I woke with water dripping on my face and thought for one merciful second that it was the tent leaking. Then I heard the river.

You have to understand what a river sounds like when it's thinking about killing you. It doesn't roar, not at first. It *deepens*. The pitch drops, like something enormous clearing its throat downstream. All night I'd been sleeping maybe thirty meters from water that had spent three weeks politely confined between clay banks, and now it was unconfined, and it wanted me to know.

I got up, stood in the downpour in my underwear, and listened like a man listening to his own heartbeat after a doctor's expression has already told him everything.

"Elodie." Ines's voice from her tent, hoarse with fever residue and no patience left over. "Tell me you're hearing this."

"I'm hearing it."

"Then tell me we're leaving before it finishes deciding what it wants to be."

* * *

We broke camp in the dark and the rain together, which is to say badly. Brand dropped the fuel tin in the mud and swore with a fluency that would have impressed my father, who considered profanity a form of engineering. The hunter—I'd stopped asking him for a name he didn't want to give; the village called him Kaya and that seemed to be enough—stood at the treeline watching the water with an expression I was learning to read. It wasn't fear. It was arithmetic.

"The season broke early," he said, when I joined him. "By how much?"

"Two weeks. Maybe more." He turned his palm up and let rain pool in it, as if weighing it. "The old people say when the first storm comes out of the west instead of the east, the river takes back its road all at once. Your maps will be wrong by tomorrow. The channels move. The sandbars drown. What was shallow yesterday is a hole today, and what was a hole is gone entirely."

"And running it now?"

He looked at me the way you look at someone who has asked whether the fire is hot. "No one runs it this time of year. That is why your enemy will not expect us to try. It is also why most of us will not arrive."

There it was, laid out flat: the whole bargain of the next two days, priced honestly. I appreciated that about Kaya. He never lied to me about costs. Everyone else in my life quoted low and invoiced later.

Tomas should have been with us. Should have been standing at the stern of the big boat doing that thing he did with his jaw when he was reading water, the thing that looked like contempt and was actually concentration. But Tomas was forty kilometers behind us at the mission outpost junction, bandaged shoulder propped against a crate, volunteering—with a grin I could still see—to run his engine loud and his radio louder and let Brac's men chase the decoy while we slipped past in the flood. He'd shaken my hand doing it. That was the part I kept replaying. Twenty years of grievance between Reyes and my family, settled with a handshake and a bad joke, and then he'd pointed his boat into the current going the wrong direction so that mine could go the right one.

"You're thinking about him," Ines said beside me. She'd come up quietly, which for a woman who'd walked out of the green dark half-dead was still remarkable.

"I'm thinking about the water."

"Liar."

"Yes," I said. "But productively."

* * *

The first hour was almost kind, and I want to state for the record that I noticed, because rivers that start kind are making a point they'll collect on later.

The channel below our camp had been, on Brand's chart, a lazy S through drowned grassland. Now it was a single brown muscle, wide as a street, carrying whole trees sideways at a speed that made my eyes hurt to track them. We ran the center because Kaya said the center was where the old channel lived, and the old channel was the only thing under the flood that still remembered being a river instead of a sea.

Brand handled the second boat alone, which terrified me until I watched him do it. Paranoia, it turns out, is just vigilance with nowhere to go, and give a man like Lukas Brand a tiller and a reason to distrust every wave personally, and he becomes very good indeed. He read the water like he read people—for threats—and the water, unlike people, never pretended.

Ines sat forward in my boat with the medical kit lashed to her chest like a child, calling depths off the sounder and calling them well. Somewhere between the camp and the falls she had stopped being a survivor we were escorting and become crew. Nobody remarked on it. Rivers don't remark. They just promote you or kill you.

The trouble started at the bend where the chart showed an island.

Islands, in flood season, are a rumor. This one had decided to stop existing, and where it had been there was now a standing wave the size of a chapel, folding over itself with a sound like wet applause. Kaya said one word—"left"—and I put the tiller over and the world went white and loud and briefly organized itself around the single principle of getting us underwater.

We didn't. I don't know entirely why. Partly Kaya's weight shifting when mine couldn't decide, partly the boat being better than it had any right to be, partly the specific dumb animal luck that follows you once you've stopped believing in it. We came out the far side shipping half a ton of green water, Ines bailing with the med kit's waterproof case, me laughing—a high, unhinged sound I didn't recognize as mine until it was already out.

"That," Brand shouted across the gap between boats, "was not on the chart."

"The chart," I shouted back, "is at the bottom of the island."

* * *

We pulled out at midday on a shelf of red clay where the forest had lost an argument with the bank, and ate cold rice and watched the river do its work.

That's the thing I want to get down correctly, because everyone who writes about wilderness makes it either a villain or a cathedral, and it is neither. It's indifferent. Completely, perfectly indifferent. Brac's men were somewhere behind us with orders to leave no witnesses, and the river did not care. My father was somewhere ahead with twenty years of silence and a vault full of other people's history, and the river did not care about that either. It rose for us exactly as it would rise for them. It would drown the man shooting and the man fleeing with the same brown hand, and it would not even be cruelty, because cruelty requires you to matter.

There was something in that, sitting there in the rain with my ribs aching from the wave, that felt almost like mercy. Not comfort—mercy. The mercy of knowing that at least one power in this valley kept no ledger of who deserved what.

"Ferrand's boats can't run this," Brand said suddenly. He'd been quiet since the island, working something. "Think about it. They're heavier. Deeper draft. They need their engines, and half these channels are going to log-jam by nightfall. If we're committed to the flood, we've got a day, maybe two, where the river is on *our* side."

Kaya shook his head slowly. "Or it is on no one's side, and we all learn that together."

"He's right though," Ines said. "Brac is careful. Careful men wait for safe water. Safe water is two days away. Whatever he does to us, he does after the flood—or not at all."

I looked upstream, the way you do, as if the threat were a thing you could see coming. Rain. Trees. The gray-green wall of a country that had swallowed better-prepared men than any of us.

"Then we keep moving," I said. "While the water's too angry for anyone to stop us—including it."

* * *

The afternoon gave us the gorge approach, and the gorge approach gave me the worst hour of my life up to that point, which given the competition was saying something.

Where the valley narrows, the flood has nowhere to spread, so it stands up. The charts called this section Class Three in dry season. In flood, with the tributaries feeding it and the rain still coming down like a personal grudge, it was something the classification system hadn't gotten around to naming. Kaya went ashore and climbed to look, and came back down with his face doing the arithmetic again.

"There is a line," he said. "Down the middle-left, where the old channel runs deepest. If we hold it, we live. If we lose it, the water decides, and the water has been deciding things here longer than your God has been keeping records."

"Odds?" Brand asked.

Kaya looked at him. "You are surveyors. You know what a margin of error is."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only honest one."

We ran it anyway. Of course we ran it. There was a woman behind us who needed a hospital, a father ahead of us who needed arresting, a boatman behind us spending himself as a decoy, and a truth in a stone room under a waterfall that four people had already bled for. You do not portage a story like that. You run it.

I won't pretend I remember it clearly. Memory in whitewater is a series of photographs with the negatives burned. Kaya's arm rigid, pointing. Ines's voice counting strokes—not panicking, *counting*, the way she'd counted numbers on that broken radio frequency a lifetime ago, as if counting were the only prayer she trusted. The bow lifting. A hole opening off the starboard side like the river showing us its throat. Brand's boat a gray smear somewhere behind and above us, airborne, then not. Water hitting my face hard enough to feel like a decision being made about me.

And then, somehow, calm water, and all three of us breathing like landed fish, and the gorge walls sliding past, ancient and unimpressed.

"Ines," I said. "Report."

"We're alive," she gasped.

"Anything else?"

"Not currently."

From behind: Brand's boat, listing, bailing, intact, with Brand upright at the tiller wearing the expression of a man who has just audited death and found the books in order. He raised one finger—the universal gesture of *one moment*—brought his boat alongside, and said, with tremendous dignity:

"I want it noted that I never doubted."

"Noted," I said. "Also noted: you screamed."

"I emitted a warning."

"You screamed like a kettle."

"Elodie," Ines said mildly, "let the man have this. We all screamed. Mine was just internal."

And we laughed—all three of us, and Kaya too, one short bark of a laugh that I suspected he'd deny later—there in the rain, in the gorge, in the middle of the worst water on the continent. Because that's what terror does when it recedes: it leaves laughter behind the way a flood leaves silt. Fertile. Absurd. Covering everything.

* * *

We made the lower pools by last light and camped on high ground Kaya chose without consulting anything but his grandfather's voice in his head. The rain had thinned to a steady whisper. Downstream, faint through the trees, we could hear the falls—not the door, not yet, but the same water, arriving ahead of us like a rumor of home.

I sat up first watch, which nobody assigned me and everybody understood. The others slept the way only the genuinely exhausted sleep, instantly and completely, Brand still holding the tiller rope in one hand like a child with a toy he refuses to surrender even unconscious.

I got out the pages of my father's journal that Brand had given me, though I barely needed to anymore. I knew the relevant passage by heart now, the way you know a wound by heart.

*The contents cannot be allowed to reach Ferrand's hands. Nor, I begin to suspect, can they be allowed to reach anyone's. Some knowledge is a load-bearing wall. Remove it and the house comes down on whoever lives inside.*

He'd written that in a neat, certain hand, the hand that had signed my childhood report cards with the same flourish, the hand I'd spent twenty years refusing to imitate and apparently imitated anyway every time I held a pencil.

He was wrong, of course. Or rather—he was half right, which is worse than wrong, because half-right ideas build fortresses. Knowledge isn't a wall. Secrets are walls. Knowledge is the river: it rises whether you approve or not, and the only question is whether you've built your houses on the floodplain and called it prudence.

Tomorrow, or the day after, we'd reach the falls. And whatever we found there—my father alive or dead, the vault sealed or opened, Brac waiting or not waiting—one thing had already been decided, somewhere between the island that wasn't there and the gorge that shouldn't have been runnable. I was done keeping other people's secrets in exchange for their love. I'd paid for that arrangement in blood already: Tomas's shoulder, the village's burned boats, Ines's fever, whatever nameless ones lay in the mud behind us. The interest alone was ruinous.

The truth was going downstream with me. All of it. To the museum, to the press, to the elders whose backs had turned to us in that riverside village because turning backs was the only defense they'd been left. Ferrand could send lawyers. Brac could send bullets. The river had sent its flood, indifferent and fair, and if it could rise over everything—over guilt and innocence, over contracts and claims—then so could the truth. It just needed someone to open the sluice.

I folded the journal pages away and looked south, toward the sound of falling water.

That was when Ines's hand closed on my ankle in the dark—awake after all, or woken by the same instinct—and she said, very quietly:

"Elodie. Listen."

I listened. Under the rain, under the falls, thin as a thread pulled through cloth: a radio. Far off, downstream, at the edge of range.

Someone was broadcasting on Frequency Nine.

And the voice, when the static parted for half a breath, was one I'd known my whole life—even through twenty years of silence, even broken up into pieces by a hundred miles of rising water.

My father was transmitting.
