## 25. The Radio Answers

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the world smelled like a drowned book—wet leaves, wet earth, wet everything, as if the forest itself were being read by something patient and enormous.

I sat on an overturned supply crate with the radio on my knees and my father's journal open beside me, and I will be honest with you, because this chapter deserves honesty more than any other: for about forty minutes, I did nothing at all. I just held the handset and listened to the static breathe.

Static has a way of sounding like an audience waiting for you to embarrass yourself.

Tomas was awake across the fire, or pretending not to be, which is his version of prayer. Ines slept sitting up against a root, her fever finally broken but her hands still trembling in that way that says the body remembers what the mind is trying to file away. Brand was up a tree with the captured radio set from Brac's boat, monitoring their frequencies like a man watching a wasp nest he'd personally poked. And Armand—

Armand was asleep ten feet from me, bandaged, gray-faced, alive, and utterly unaware of what I was about to do to him.

I want you to understand the arithmetic I was doing, because it wasn't noble arithmetic. It was ugly, small, human arithmetic, done by lamplight with cold fingers.

If I stayed silent, Ferrand didn't know exactly where we were. She knew roughly—the second boat, the burned village, the portage—but roughly isn't precisely, and precisely is what her security needed. Silence bought us days, maybe. Days to move my father, who couldn't walk unaided. Days to run a river already climbing its banks. Days in which Brac's men could keep doing what they'd been doing, which was hunting us through country where we were slower, sicker, and fewer.

Silence bought us time. It also bought Ferrand the only thing she actually wanted: a version of events in which the vault existed for her alone, in which the documents under the falls could be quietly recovered—or quietly destroyed—and in which every person who'd seen them became a loose end with a name.

And here was the other column of the ledger: the village downstream, the hunter's family, the elders who'd given us passage on a promise. Every day the secret stayed secret was another day they were the only people standing between Ferrand's claim and the truth, without knowing there was a fight on at all. You can't defend yourself against a knife you don't know exists.

My father had made his choice twenty years ago, in a different way, with different silences. He'd chosen to protect things by burying them. Look how well that had gone. Look at the camp with the tents slashed from the inside. Look at Ines, walking until her feet gave out. Look at Tomas's shoulder, at the burn scar the cache fire left on the night sky, at the empty places in our boat where two of the crew used to sit.

Protection, in this family, had a body count.

So I keyed the handset.

* * *

The museum frequency came up first, because I'm a coward in orderly ways and I wanted to rehearse on someone institutional before I got to anyone who might actually be listening with their heart.

"This is Elodie Delacroix, transmitting on behalf of the Ashgrove expedition—" no. Wrong. That was the old reflex, wrapping myself in his name like a coat. I started again.

"This is Elodie Delacroix. Independent transmission. Is anyone reading?"

The static thought about it. Then a voice, clipped, official, half-asleep: "This is the National Museum relay station. Identify your position and nature of emergency."

"My position," I said, "is approximately eleven kilometers upstream of the confluence marked on no chart you own, behind the third cataract." I read out the coordinates from Brand's survey notes, slow enough to be copied, twice. "The nature of my transmission is not an emergency. It's a disclosure."

"Say again your last."

"You heard me." My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is one of the few genetic gifts my father ever gave me. "I am transmitting the location and contents of a pre-colonial vault site discovered by the Delacroix-Ashgrove expedition. The site contains funerary goods, stonework, and—this is the important part—a sealed archive of documents indicating that the upper valley was never ceded by treaty to any colonial power or their successors. Those documents are original. They are intact. And as of this transmission, their existence and location are public record."

Silence on the line. Long enough that I checked the power gauge.

Then, carefully: "Mr. Delacroix. Do you understand what you're claiming?"

"Better than you do, probably. I've been carrying it through a flood season."

"That information is subject to the institute's contractual—"

"The institute's contract," I said, "was signed with a man who falsified his own field logs to hide what he found, from a financier whose security detail has shot at us twice, burned a village's boats, and left instructions on a captured radio that I can quote verbatim if your recorder is running. Which I hope it is. Because I'm about to make sure everyone's recorder is running."

I read them the coordinates again. Then the contents, itemized from the journal and my own photographs: the burial chamber, the metalwork, the document cache, the seal marks matching the token the elder had pressed into my hand back when I still thought this was a rescue mission. I described the chisel work on the outer door—fresh, from the far side, someone else's hands having gotten there first—which I confess I left slightly vague, because some fears I wasn't ready to hand to strangers over open air.

Then I said the sentence I'd been building toward all night, the one I'd turned over so many times it had worn smooth:

"I am asking the museum to assert custodial interest immediately, publicly, and loudly. Not quietly. Not through channels. Loudly. Because the moment this becomes public, it stops being something that can be solved with guns, and starts being something that has to be solved with lawyers. And I have met the guns. I'd rather take my chances with the lawyers."

The relay operator—give him credit—said, "Transmission logged and forwarded, Mr. Delacroix. For what it's worth: God help you."

"He's been conspicuously absent so far," I said, "but I'll pass along the request."

* * *

The press frequencies were next, and those were easier, because journalists don't ask whether you understand what you're claiming. They ask you to say it louder.

I gave them everything. Coordinates, contents, names—Ines Okafor, botanist; Lukas Brand, surveyor; Tomas Reyes, boatman and the best man on this river, currently sleeping with a bullet-damaged shoulder and a debt he insists is paid. I named the financier. I named Odile Ferrand, spelled it out letter by letter, and quoted the recovered orders: *recover the artifacts, leave no witnesses.* I told them about the burned boats. I told them about the ledger page in my father's handwriting showing a discovery recorded weeks before the official log admitted it, because if I was going to burn down the secrecy, I was going to start with my own house.

That part cost more than I expected. Saying my father's name and the word *falsified* in the same breath, into a machine that would carry both to ten thousand breakfast tables—it felt like breaking something that hadn't finished healing, except it had never healed, it had only been wrapped, and underneath the wrapping it had been broken for twenty years.

Brand came down out of his tree while I was transmitting. He stood at the edge of the firelight holding the captured set, listening, and when I finished he said, quietly, "Their channel just went active. Three separate stations calling Ferrand's boat. Somebody at the institute woke up."

"Good," I said.

"You've just made us the most famous people on this river," he said. "Famous and findable. You know that, right? Every receiver within four hundred kilometers just heard exactly where we are."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're sure that's better than being hidden?"

I looked at Ines, asleep against the root, her feet ruined from walking. At Tomas, whose shoulder would heal crooked. At the space in the boat. At the token in my pocket, carved with the same mark that stood over a sealed door under falling water, given to me by people who had already been hurt once by foreigners with contracts.

"No," I said. "But hidden was only ever safe for us. Public might be safe for everybody. I'm done confusing the two."

Brand nodded slowly, the nod of a man who'd spent weeks trusting no one learning to trust one specific paranoid decision. "Then I'll keep watch," he said. "Loudly famous people need somebody watching the trees."

* * *

The last call was the hardest, and I almost didn't make it, because it wasn't a broadcast. It was a conversation.

The hunter had shown us how to reach the elders' shortwave through the mission relay—a chain of borrowed antennas strung downriver like a rumor. When the connection crackled alive and an old voice answered in the valley tongue, I handed the handset to the hunter, because some apologies should be made in the language they're owed in.

He spoke for a long time. I caught pieces: the falls, the door, the documents, the words *never ceded*, the word *stolen* applied to me and my father with a precision that made me flinch even though I agreed with it. Then he paused, and turned to me, and asked me something directly, and I understood enough of it to know it was the question:

*Will they believe you?*

"They'll have to," I said. "I've told everyone at once. There's no version now where only one side of this story survives."

He considered that, then spoke into the handset again, and whatever he said made the old voice at the other end go quiet for a long moment, and then laugh—not a happy laugh, but the kind of laugh that comes when grief finally gets company.

When he lowered the handset, he said, in my language, carefully: "My grandfather says the river has been trying to tell this story for a hundred years. He says it kept sending floods, and sickness, and men like your father, and none of you listened." He looked at the radio with something close to respect. "Now you have made the river very loud."

"That was the idea."

"It was a good idea." A pause. "It was also a dangerous one. The woman with the money will not send lawyers first."

"I know," I said. "But she can't shoot a story out of the air. Not all of it. Not anymore. I made sure of the *not anymore*."

He studied me the way he studied animal sign, looking for what was really there underneath what was pretending. Whatever he found seemed to satisfy him, mostly.

"My grandfather asks one thing," he said. "When the documents are read—in the capital, in the light, by people with clean hands—he asks that an elder stands in the room. Not to speak. Just to be seen. So that no one can say afterward that the valley was a thing discussed, instead of a person present."

"Tell him yes," I said. "Tell him I'll carry him there myself if the boats won't take him."

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold through the mist, and I sat back down on my crate and discovered that my hands were shaking now that the work was done, which is the body's habit of invoicing you after the fact.

Armand was awake. I don't know how long he'd been awake. Long enough. He was propped against his pack with the journal's absence visible in the way he held himself—Brand still had the pages, and my father kept glancing at Brand's pack the way a man glances at a dog he suspects is chewing his correspondence.

"You transmitted," he said. Not a question. He'd heard. Of course he'd heard; the whole forest had heard; Ferrand's security had heard; history had heard.

"I transmitted."

"Do you have any idea," he said, and his voice had that terrible controlled quality it got when the control was load-bearing, "what you've done? Do you understand that the moment those coordinates entered a logbook, they stopped being a secret and started being a target? Every government ministry, every collector, every syndicate man between here and the coast now knows exactly where to dig. You haven't protected anything. You've drawn a map for every thief on the continent and called it justice."

"I called it daylight," I said. "Thieves work in the dark, Father. You taught me that. You taught me everything I know about working in the dark—I learned it from your logs."

His face went white around the mouth. "You don't understand the politics. The documents prove nothing if they're seized before they're authenticated. If Ferrand's people reach the vault first—"

"Then the whole world will be watching them do it," I said. "That's the point. That's the entire point. Before today, if her men reached that door, it would be a quiet story with a quiet ending and a crate on a private barge. Now it's a story with witnesses. You can't disappear a thing that ten thousand people have already heard described."

"And your name?" he said. "You've tied your name to it. To mine. Do you know what happens to a Delacroix who publishes coordinates before authentication? Who broadcasts accusations against the expedition's own financier? They will eat you, Elodie. They will eat you the way they ate me, and you're doing it voluntarily, and I cannot—" His voice cracked, and for one raw second underneath the anger there was something so nakedly afraid that I nearly put the handset down and went to him.

Nearly.

"I know what they'll do," I said. "I watched what they did to you. That's why I'm not going to stand in the dark holding a secret and hoping the dark holds it too. That was your plan, Father. Twenty years of it. How is it working out? Count the campfires. Count the graves."

"That is not fair."

"No," I said. "It isn't. Fairness is for people who weren't shot at on account of your filing system."

He stared at me, and I stared back, and between us lay twenty years of silence suddenly full of noise—radio noise, press noise, the noise of a story escaping its cage in every direction at once, which is the only direction stories go once you open the door.

Across the fire, Tomas had stopped pretending to sleep. Ines had opened her eyes. Even the hunter had turned from the river to watch us, the way you watch weather.

Armand opened his mouth—to say what, I never learned, because that was the moment Brand's voice cracked down from the tree line, sharp and flat with adrenaline:

"Boat! Upstream—two engines, running dark!"

And my father and I both turned toward the water, toward the sound of oars shipping and engines throttling low, and I remember thinking, with a strange and terrible calm, that I had just told the whole world where we were—

—and the whole world, evidently, had been listening.
