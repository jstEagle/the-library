## 27. Brac's Last Order

I have killed men for less than a river, and I have been paid better for it.

That's not a confession. Confessions are for men who believe the ledger can still be balanced. I'm an accountant of a different kind — the kind whose entries are made in the only currency that never inflates, never devalues, never gets argued about at a dock with lawyers present. A life is a life. It buys exactly what it buys. The trick, the whole profession, is making sure you never have to spend your own.

I was thinking about this in the flat, unhelpful way I think about everything, standing in the stern of the *Magdalena's Mercy* — Ferrand's idea of a joke, naming a gunboat after a saint — while the river went brown and fat with the early rains and the mission outpost of San Rafael de los Ríos came up out of the treeline like a bad memory.

Six of my men aboard. Two boats behind us with four more. Radios that had gone strange in my hands for two days, ever since Delacroix's son did the one thing I had not priced into the operation.

He broadcast it. All of it. Coordinates, contents, the documents, the whole buried inheritance of the valley — sent open-frequency to the museum, the press, the elders of villages I'd burned boats at, anyone with a receiver and a grievance. I listened to it happen in real time and felt something I hadn't felt since I was a boy watching a priest count coins in front of my mother's coffin: the specific vertigo of watching a man spend something priceless and not even flinch.

Ferrand's voice on the satellite link that night had been very calm. Ferrand is always calm. It's her most expensive feature.

"Brac," she said, "the mission has changed. There is no longer anything to recover. There is only the question of who can testify."

I stood on the deck with the handset and let the rain hit the back of my neck and did not answer immediately, which for me is a kind of screaming.

*Who can testify.* A beautiful phrase. Legal, almost tender. It did not say *kill the archaeologist and his son and the botanist and the surveyor and the boatman and the hunter and whoever else was standing near them when the shooting started.* It said *who can testify*, as if we were discussing subpoenas.

"Define testify," I said.

"You know what I mean."

"I want it in the contract language."

A pause on the line. Rain on the canvas over my head. Downriver, somewhere behind us, the flood was coming on like a debt collector.

"The original contract had a clause," Ferrand said. "Contingency Seven. You signed it."

I had signed it. Eleven years ago, on a boat not unlike this one, with a pen I still own. Contingency Seven was one line long and I had read it a hundred times over the years the way a man reads a scar. *In the event of irrecoverable exposure, the security contractor will take all necessary measures to preserve the principal's position.* All necessary measures. The beauty of that sentence is that it means nothing and everything, and the horror of it is the same thing.

"Preserve her position," I said now, to the river, to nobody. My second, a broad, dull, loyal man named Etienne who had once taken a machete wound for me and never once asked what we were actually doing out here, looked up from the ammunition crates.

"Boss?"

"Nothing. Count the flares again."

* * *

Here is what I have learned about this work, and I learned it the way you learn anything worth knowing: slowly, expensively, and too late to matter.

Men like me are not made by cruelty. Cruelty is common as rain. We are made by arithmetic. Somebody — a father, a recruiter, a war, a woman with a contract — teaches you that everything can be counted, and once you believe that, you are lost, because counting is the one sin that feels like virtue. You tell yourself you're the honest one. The sentimental men flinch and hesitate and get people killed *and* fail the job. You don't flinch. You do the sum. You carry it.

I had done the sums for Odile Ferrand for eleven years. Boats burned at the downstream village: four, and I had paid the headman's cousin in outboard motors to keep the arithmetic quiet. Men discouraged, men disappeared: the number is not large, and I have never once told myself the lie that it was zero. I did not enjoy it. Enjoyment is for amateurs. I did it the way a surgeon cuts — cleanly, minimally, with a professional's respect for the tissue.

And in eleven years she had never once asked me to do what Contingency Seven actually meant, because in eleven years no one had ever made it necessary. Secrecy had always been enough. A burned boat, a bought elder, a frightened botanist sent home with a story about bandits. The valley kept its mouth shut because the valley understood, better than any of us, what happened to people who didn't.

Then a salvage diver's son with his father's stubborn jaw pointed a radio at the sky and told the truth to everybody, and the arithmetic changed.

That's the thing about the work. The numbers never change gradually. They flip. One day the cheapest move is silence; the next day the cheapest move is seven graves, and the man who hesitates between those two days is the man who ends up in one of them.

* * *

The mission at San Rafael had been on my charts for years as a neutral point. A dot with a cross beside it. Father Anselmo ran it — a Spaniard gone soft and brown as old leather, who had been in the river country longer than I had been alive and longer than Ferrand had been rich. He traded medicine for fish, kept a shortwave radio for emergencies, buried the dead of both sides of every feud the river ever produced, and asked no questions, which was the treaty that had kept his roof unburned through three wars that had burned everything else.

We put in at his dock at four in the afternoon, in the rain, with six boats' worth of men and hardware, and I watched the old priest come down to the water and stop when he saw the guns, and I watched him decide — visibly, the way an old man decides things, with his whole body — that he was too old to be afraid.

"Brac," he said. "You've brought the whole parish."

"We need the roof for a night."

"You need it for what?"

"To wait for travelers."

He looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off his hat brim. Behind him the mission bell hung silent in its frame, and the river behind us had risen so high it was licking at the third step of his dock.

"Travelers," he said. "On this river. In this season."

"Yes."

"And what will you do with them when they arrive, Brac?"

I have been asked many questions in my life by men with guns, and I have lied to all of them fluently. Father Anselmo had no gun. He had a hat and a wet cassock and sixty years of the river in his face, and I heard myself say, "Nothing they'd thank me for," which was the closest to a confession I had come in eleven years, and which surprised me so much I almost didn't hear his answer.

"Then you'll do it somewhere that isn't my roof," he said, and turned, and walked back up to his church, and did not lock his door, because there was nothing in it worth locking and because he was, I think, the only honest man on the river.

I let him have his roof. Not out of mercy. Out of a superstition I don't have a better name for: that the flood was coming, and God was in it, and I didn't want to owe Him anything extra.

* * *

That night I sat in the mission's dry refectory with the satellite phone and the contract and the rain coming down like the sky was settling its own accounts, and I did the thing I do, the counting.

The witnesses. Let me name them, because a professional names them, because a list is a wall between you and what the list means.

Delacroix the elder. Seventy-one years old, a bullet through the meat of his shoulder that I put there myself at the cache — I am not proud of that shot, but I am not ashamed of it either; he was armed, and he was between me and a crate, and the crate was the job. He could testify. His logs were already in the hands of the museum, though — Ferrand's lawyers said the falsified originals were the only thing that could still hang her, and the falsified originals were in his head and possibly in a waterproof tube he was known to sleep with. So: the old man was a document with legs.

Delacroix the younger. The one who had done this. The one who had stood at the delta with the cameras and refused Ferrand's money — and I had been there for that, standing in her shadow at the dock with the rain coming down, watching a man turn down enough money to buy the whole river, and I remember thinking: *this is either a saint or a fool, and both kinds die the same.* He could testify. Worse, he already had. Killing him now was not silence. It was only spite. I noted that in the ledger and underlined it.

The botanist, Okafor. The surveyor, Brand — who had, I was told, out-signaled my own radio man at the outpost siege, which I confess I admired the way you admire a card sharp. The boatman Reyes, shot through the chest and somehow alive, somehow still on the water. The hunter from the turned-back village, whose name I never learned and who had stood up at the delta and spoken the valley's claim into a lawyer's recorder while his family was, at that moment, under my men's guns — and I want it recorded, in whatever ledger receives this, that when he stepped forward I had given the order to hold fire, and my men had held, and that is the only entry in eleven years I have ever read twice.

Seven names. Eight with the priest, if he talked, and he would, because that was the treaty — he kept no secrets, that was the whole of him.

And the arithmetic said: seven graves, and the flood coming, and the river police already sniffing at the delta because a lawyer had made noise, and Ferrand's position — *preserve the principal's position* — preserved only if every name on the list stopped being a name and became a number.

I sat there with the pen over the paper for a long time.

Here is what stopped me, and I have turned it over since and found no better answer. It was not the priest. It was not God, whom I have always suspected of being Ferrand's senior partner. It was not even the hunter's family, though that had sat badly in me since the burning — I had told myself it was necessary, and necessary is the word I use when the other words won't fit in my mouth.

What stopped me was the son's face at the delta. Not when he refused the money — refusal I understand, refusal is just another price. It was afterward, when Ferrand pivoted, when her voice went soft and reasonable and she mentioned the villages, and the young man didn't flinch and didn't rage. He just looked at her the way you look at a broken instrument. Like she had failed at being a person, and he was noting it down without anger, the way I note things down.

He was counting *her.* That was the moment I understood the flood had already come, and it was not the river.

* * *

Etienne found me at midnight. He's the only one of my men who has been with me the whole eleven years, and he has one virtue: he can tell the difference between me thinking and me not thinking, which most men can't.

"They're coming upriver," he said. "Two boats, running dark, running fast. Reyes is piloting. The radio man says it's them."

"Of course it's them. The mission is the only roof between the delta and the falls. They have the wounded man. They need a wall and a roof and a night's rest." I closed the notebook. "And we're the wall."

"What's the order, boss?"

And there it was. The whole eleven years in four words. *What's the order.* Because Etienne had never once in his life asked whether the order was right. That was my job, he believed. That was what I was paid for — not the shooting, the deciding. I had built a machine where the conscience was all in one place, in me, and the rest of it was hands.

Ferrand's voice was in my ear from two nights ago, calm as still water: *Contingency Seven, Brac. You signed it. All necessary measures. Or I will find a contractor who reads his contract.*

And she would. And the man she found would not sit up past midnight doing the arithmetic twice. He would do it once, fast, the way I used to.

I looked out the window at the rain and the black river, and somewhere up that river, running dark, were the people I had been sent to erase, and behind them the flood, and behind that a delta full of lawyers and cameras, and behind that a woman who had never in her life spent anything of her own — not money she couldn't replace, not blood, not a night's sleep, not one hour of one conscience — and who had decided that I was the thing she could spend.

"Boss," Etienne said. "What's the order?"

I picked up the pen. I looked at the seven names. I thought about a man who had told the truth to the whole sky and slept afterward — I knew he slept afterward, the radio man picked up his camp chatter, ordinary, tired, human — and I thought about the fact that I had not slept properly in eleven years, and I understood, finally, with the terrible clarity that comes too late and costs everything, which of us the secret had been buried under all along.

"Etienne," I said, "put out the lamps."

"Boss?"

"All of them. And get me the flare gun, and wake the radio man. And Etienne—" I stood, and my knees made the sound of a man my age, and outside the rain kept falling on a roof that belonged to the last honest man on the river. "When they come to the dock, nobody fires. Nobody fires unless I'm dead. Is that understood?"

He looked at me the way you look at a compass that's started pointing the wrong way. "What are we doing, boss?"

I didn't have an answer I liked. I had only an order I'd never given before, in eleven years of orders, and the flood coming up the dock steps, and two boats running dark toward a mission full of people I had been paid to make into numbers.

"Get the lamps out," I said again. "They're going to need to see the dock."
