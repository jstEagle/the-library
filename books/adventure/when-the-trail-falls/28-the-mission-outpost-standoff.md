## 28. The Mission Outpost Standoff

I want it on the record—my record, the one I keep in my head because paper can be burned—that I was right about everything.

Not in a gloating way. Well. A little in a gloating way. A man who has spent eleven days hiding in a tree eating weevils has earned some gloating.

But here is the thing nobody tells you about being right: when you're right about people being compromised, and then those same people save your life anyway, you have to sit with the arithmetic of that. And arithmetic was always my best subject. It's why Armand Delacroix hired me to survey his maps instead of letting me survey anything with people in it. Numbers don't lie. People lie constantly, beautifully, and sometimes they lie by running toward gunfire on behalf of a man they've known for two weeks.

Let me back up. The mission outpost sat where the river bent east around a bluff—a squat chapel, a dormitory with a tin roof, a bell tower that hadn't rung since before the war, and Father Anselm, who had been there long enough to remember when the river had a different name. He met us at the gate with a lantern and the expression of a man who had seen every kind of trouble float past his door and had decided, decades ago, to stop being surprised by any of it.

"You'll want the dormitory," he said. "The chapel roof leaks."

"Father," Elodie said, "we may be bringing trouble with us."

"I assumed. You have the look." He held up the lantern so the light caught all five of us—Elodie soaked to the ribs, Ines leaning on a walking stick with her fever finally broken but her eyes still somewhere upstream, the hunter whose name I will not write down because he asked me not to, Tomas with his arm strapped across his chest and gray as old rope—and me, Lukas Brand, carrying a satchel of my dead employer's journal pages like they were scripture, which after a fashion they were.

"What look?" I asked.

"The look of people being followed."

* * *

We barricaded that night. Not dramatically—I want to be clear that there was no furniture dragged across doorways, no heroic hammering. Father Anselm simply showed us where the shutters were, and Elodie assigned watches, and the hunter went out into the dark without being asked and came back an hour later smelling of wet leaves and said one word: "Coming."

"How many?" Elodie asked.

"Six. Maybe seven. They are walking. They left their boat below the bend."

Brac. Of course Brac. The man had burned the downstream village's boats, threatened a family of children to make a point, and now he was coming upriver on foot through the rain with six men and a mission full of witnesses between him and whatever his orders said witnesses didn't get to be.

Here is what you need to know about me, and I say this without pride: I am not brave. I hid in a tree for eleven days while my colleagues scattered or died, and I do not regret it, because a dead surveyor surveys nothing. When the shooting started at dawn at the cache, I got behind a root and stayed there. When Ines begged us to go home, part of me agreed with her so strongly it felt religious.

But I am also not stupid, and there is a difference between cowardice and conservation, and I had been conserving myself for exactly this kind of night. Because I had noticed something, back at Camp Two, while I was up my tree watching the world through a crack in my own sanity: I had noticed the radio.

Specifically, I had noticed that Brac's men used a relay. Their transmissions from the second boat were weak—the set was small, portable—but every evening at a quarter past seven, someone keyed a stronger transmitter downstream, and Brac's traffic bounced off it. I'd heard the pattern for three nights before I understood it. A repeater station. Ferrand's network ran on one, probably several, strung down the river like beads.

And a repeater, gentlemen and ladies, is a door that swings both ways.

* * *

At midnight I found Elodie on the dormitory porch, sitting with his father's journal open on his knees and not reading it. Rain came off the roof in a silver curtain. He looked, I thought, exactly like Armand would have looked thirty years ago, if Armand had ever once in his life let his shoulders come down from around his ears.

"Brand," he said. "You should be sleeping."

"I don't sleep before sieges. It's a character flaw. It's most of my character, actually." I sat down next to him and put the radio on the boards between us. "I need to show you something, and then I need you to trust me, and I'm aware that's a lot to ask from a man who held a machete on Ines four days ago."

"It was a knife," Elodie said. "And she forgave you faster than I did."

"She's a better person than both of us. Listen." I turned the dial to the frequency I'd mapped, low volume, and let the static breathe. At a quarter past seven every evening, Brac reported position to the repeater. But repeaters are dumb machines. They take whatever comes in on one band and shout it out on another, and they don't check credentials, and they certainly don't know that the man who built this particular message wasn't the man sending it.

"Tomorrow at seven-fifteen," I said, "Brac calls in. And tomorrow at seven-sixteen, I call in too—from inside the mission, on the mission's ancient set, which Father Anselm tells me still works because God protects fools and missionaries. And I send a distress signal. Expedition frequency. Our names, our position, shots fired, request immediate river police."

Elodie stared at me. Rain ticked on the tin roof. Somewhere in the dark, the hunter moved between the trees without sound.

"They'll trace it," Elodie said finally. "Brac will hear it."

"He'll hear it and he'll know exactly where it came from, yes. That's the flaw in the plan. The flaw in the plan is enormous. But—" I tapped the radio. "He also knows that if he attacks the mission tonight, he confirms the distress call. Every hour he waits, the police get closer. His orders are to leave no witnesses, but his orders were written when no one was listening. Now everyone's listening. I'm making everyone listen."

"That's—" Elodie rubbed his face. "That's insane."

"It's triangulation," I said. "It's the only thing I'm good at. Give me three fixed points and I can tell you where anything is. I'm giving the river police three fixed points: my signal, Brac's repeater, and the coordinates you already broadcast. Let them draw the lines. Lines drawn on paper are the one weapon Brac can't burn."

For a long moment he didn't answer. Then he said, quietly, "You could have just told me the idea. Instead you handed me the radio and waited."

"I wanted to see your face when you realized I'd stopped being the man in the tree."

His mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile, but it was adjacent to one, and I decided to count it.

"Father Anselm's set," he said. "Will it reach?"

"It reached the diocese in '09 during the flood," said a voice behind us, and we both nearly went over the porch rail. Father Anselm stood in the doorway with two cups of something hot and terrible-smelling. "It reached Rome once, though the Vatican says otherwise. What frequency?"

I told him. He nodded as if I'd asked him to pass the salt.

"One condition," he said. "When they come—the soldiers, the police, whoever answers—you tell them the truth about what happened here. All of it. This river has carried away enough lies that the fish have learned to swim around them."

"We've been broadcasting the truth for days, Father," Elodie said.

"Yes," said Anselm. "And tomorrow you'll find out whether anyone was praying."

* * *

They came at first light, or rather they announced themselves at first light, which is different. A shot cracked off the bell tower—not aimed, punctuation. Then Brac's voice, carrying across the compound with the flat courtesy of a man reading a menu.

"Delacroix. You have until noon. Walk out with the documents and the photographs and nothing happens to anyone. Stay, and I come in, and things happen to everyone, including the priest, whom I would prefer to spare."

Father Anselm, standing calmly in the churchyard in his cassock with his hands folded, called back: "Young man, I have buried four bishops. Do you imagine I fear your schedule?"

I was in the bell tower. Don't ask me how I got up there; it involved a ladder, a prayer, and Ines Okafor holding the ladder and telling me that if I fell she would not laugh, which from her was a great tenderness. From the tower I could see the tree line, and I could see them—six shapes moving along the drainage ditch, patient, professional, closing like a hand. And beyond them, downstream, the gray ribbon of the river, empty, empty, empty.

Seven-ten. Seven-twelve. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Fear, I find, burns off after the first few hours and leaves behind a strange clean calm, like the air after lightning.

Seven-fifteen. Downstream, faint through the tower's broken louvers, I heard it: the carrier wave, Brac keying his report. Position. Status. The word *complication*, delivered in his voice like a man reporting weather.

Seven-sixteen. I pressed the key.

I won't transcribe the whole message; it's in the police record now, which is more immortality than I ever wanted. But I gave our names—all of them, including Tomas Reyes, including the hunter, including Father Anselm by name because a named priest is a shield no soldier wants on his conscience. I gave the position. I said *shots fired, armed party approaching mission outpost, repeat, armed party*. And then, because I am a surveyor and precision is my love language, I gave the bearing of Brac's approach vector to within two degrees, which I had measured from the tower with a borrowed sextant while everyone else was barricading shutters.

Then I sent it twice more, and sat back, and listened to the silence afterward, which was the loudest silence of my life.

It took Brac nine minutes to react. Nine minutes—he must have finished his report, packed his set, and only then heard mine bleeding through on the expedition band. When it came, his voice had changed. The menu-reading courtesy was gone. What replaced it was worse: quietness.

"Brand." A pause. "You're the one from the tree."

"I'm the one from the tree," I confirmed, keying slowly, keeping my transmission short because every second was a fingerprint.

"You understand what you've done."

"I've done arithmetic," I said. "It's what I do. Right now the sum says: attack this mission and you hang. Withdraw and you live to be paid another day. I know which number I'd pick."

Another pause, longer. Through the louvers I watched the shapes in the drainage ditch go still, then begin, one by one, to slide backward into the green.

"Tell Delacroix," Brac said at last, and there was something in his voice I hadn't expected—not rage, not even frustration. Tiredness. The tiredness of a man who has done the sums too many times and keeps getting the same ugly total. "Tell him the delta. Tell him I keep accounts, and the delta settles them. He'll understand."

Then the channel went dead, and the tree line emptied, and the rain came back on like a tap.

* * *

They were gone by eight. We didn't believe it for another two hours—the hunter went out and read the ground the way I read contour lines, and came back and said they'd withdrawn to the boats, leaving rearguard sign, disciplined, unhurried. A withdrawal, not a retreat. There's a difference, and the difference had teeth.

But by ten o'clock, when the sun actually broke through—which felt like a personal favor—we heard the engines. Two of them, heavy, official, the unmistakable bored thunder of government patrol boats that would rather be anywhere else. They rounded the bend flying colors, and a lieutenant with a clipboard and a magnificent mustache came ashore and demanded, in the tone of a man beginning a very long day, to know who had transmitted a distress call on a closed expedition frequency.

"That would be me," I said, climbing down from the bell tower on legs that had gone philosophical about the whole enterprise.

Ines caught my elbow at the bottom of the ladder. She looked at me for a moment with those fever-scoured eyes, and then she said, "Lukas Brand. The man in the tree." And she smiled, and it was the first time I had seen her smile since the falls, and I confess it undid me somewhat. I had to pretend to check the ladder.

Elodie shook my hand. He didn't say anything clever. He just held on a half-second longer than a handshake requires, and that was the whole speech, and it was enough.

The lieutenant wanted statements. Of course he did. We gave them—Elodie first, calm and complete, laying out the vault, the documents, the broadcasts, the burning of the village boats, all of it, while the mustache grew progressively more alarmed and the junior officer filled page after page. Father Anselm brought coffee that tasted like punishment and stood behind Elodie the entire time with his arms folded, radiating the specific authority of a man who has heard ten thousand confessions and knows exactly how much of any story is true.

I gave my statement last. The lieutenant asked me why I'd hidden in a tree for eleven days instead of going for help.

"Because I'm a coward," I said, "and cowards notice things. Brave men walk into ambushes. Cowards count the ambushers."

He wrote that down verbatim, which suggests either that he found it useful or that he intends to quote it in a report designed to get someone laughed at. Either way, history will have my words, and my words were true, and lately that's the only currency I deal in.

* * *

Evening came, and with it a strange peace. The patrol boats anchored midstream with their lights on, fat and yellow and absurdly comforting. Ines slept a real sleep for the first time in a week. Tomas sat on the churchyard wall with his bound arm and told Father Anselm a long story in rapid Spanish that made the old priest laugh twice, which I gathered was rare currency. The hunter sat apart, as he always did, sharpening something, guarding something, being what he was.

I found Elodie at the water's edge, looking downstream, where the river ran black and smooth toward the delta.

"He said the delta," Elodie said, without turning. "You heard it."

"I heard it."

"My father taught me chess when I was nine. He never once let me win. He said a gift was an insult between players." Elodie turned a stone over with his boot, considering it. "Brac doesn't play games. He plays accounts. If he's promised the delta, it's because the delta is where the ledger balances—in his favor. Ferrand will be there. Lawyers, money, the whole theater of respectability. And behind the theater, whatever Brac couldn't finish upriver."

"So we don't go to the delta."

"We go to the delta." He picked up the stone and threw it far out into the dark water. "Everything we have—journals, photographs, the documents themselves—is worth nothing in a drawer. It's only worth something entered into the record, in front of witnesses, where it can't be burned or buried. And the record, Lukas, is at the river mouth. Where the law lives. Where she'll be forced to stand in the open and make her offer to our faces."

"And if her offer is good? If it's generous? Money, reputation, your father's name laundered clean?"

Elodie was quiet for a while. The patrol boat lights wobbled on the current.

"Then it'll be the most reasonable voice I've ever refused," he said. "Come on. The lieutenant wants your bearings again. Apparently your numbers are the only ones on this river anyone trusts."

I followed him up the bank. Behind us the mission bell hung silent in its tower, and ahead the river ran on toward the sea, carrying everything—our names, our dead, our one unburnable truth—toward the place where accounts are settled.

I am Lukas Brand, and I was right about everything.

I only hope, for once, that being right is enough.
