## 29. Downriver Through the Drowned Forest

I have spent eleven years cataloguing plants that survive by drowning—mangroves, water hyacinth, the shameless floating ferns that will colonize anything wet and unattended—and I thought this gave me some professional understanding of inundation. I was wrong. There is a difference between a plant adapted to flood and a forest actively being murdered by one, and I learned it the morning we paddled into the drowned country below the gorge.

The river had climbed twenty feet in three days. Where the charts showed a channel cutting through dry-land timber, there was now nothing but treetops—whole canopy crowns standing in brown water like the heads of drowned congregation, leaves still green, still trying to photosynthesize, still doing their jobs as if the world hadn't ended beneath them. The current barely moved. The river had spread itself so thin across the land that it had forgotten which way it was going, and for the first hour we went by dead reckoning and the hunter's memory, sliding between trunks in a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse in my ears.

Nobody spoke. Nobody had to. We had all learned the same lesson somewhere between the cache and the rapids: noise is a currency, and we were broke.

Elodie sat in the bow of Tomas's old boat with the photographs wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and I watched him watch the water, and I thought: *there is a man carrying a library of evidence through a drowned world, and he has not slept properly in nine days.* The bruise on his jaw from Brac's ambush had gone the yellow-green of overripe fruit. His hands were steady on the paddle, though. That surprised me. Grief should shake a man. Tomas had taken a bullet meant for him, and Tomas was back at the mission outpost with a bandaged shoulder and a rifle across his lap, insisting he'd stay behind as decoy because his debt was paid and paid debts don't travel. Elodie had argued. Tomas had won. And now Elodie paddled like a man who had decided that the only acceptable apology was arrival.

"You're staring," he said quietly, without turning around.

"I'm a botanist. Staring is my profession."

"At me?"

"At the trees." Which was true, mostly. "That one's a fig. Ficus. They fruit even when flooded—the birds follow them. If we see parrots, we're near high ground."

He absorbed this the way he'd begun absorbing everything I said lately: completely, immediately, without the defensive flicker his father would have given it. Somewhere in the last two weeks I had stopped being the feverish woman they'd scraped off a trail and become the expedition's memory, its conscience, and apparently its ornithologist. I didn't mind. A person who has been carried learns what carrying costs.

* * *

Let me tell you about the cattle.

We came around a buttress root the size of a cathedral pillar and there they were—a herd of longhorns, or what had been a herd, standing belly-deep in the floodwater on a rise that used to be pasture and was now an island maybe forty meters across. Most of them were alive. They stood in a huddle, mud to their shoulders, chewing at nothing, regarding us with the flat ancient patience of animals who have concluded that humans are weather—something that happens to you, not something you can negotiate with.

Two of them were not alive. They floated nearby, swollen and pale, turning slowly in the current like bad thoughts.

"Señor Reyes said the ranches were upstream," Brand whispered. He'd come along despite everything—despite his week alone in a tree eating grubs and paranoia, despite nearly shooting the hunter on sight when we collected him. Isolation had hollowed Lukas Brand out and then refilled him with something more careful. He flinched at loud noises but he counted everything twice, and twice-counting saves lives.

"The ranches are everywhere upstream," the hunter said. His name was Kaelo, and he gave it to us grudgingly, the way you hand someone a knife handle-first. "The water does not check papers."

We paddled past the island. One old cow lowed at us—a sound I felt in my sternum, low and reproachful—and Elodie raised a hand toward her as if she might be comforted by the gesture of a man in a leaking boat. She wasn't. But I understood the impulse. When the world drowns, you want to apologize to something.

Kaelo guided us left where the chart said right. He did it without ceremony, without explanation, tapping the gunwale twice and pointing with his chin, and Tomas's replacement paddle—Brand's, actually; Brand had surrendered it with the expression of a man donating a kidney—swung us obediently into a channel that appeared out of nowhere between two submerged fences. Fence posts underwater. Barbed wire just beneath the surface, visible only as a dark suggestion, waiting to open a boat's hull like a letter.

"How do you know these things?" Brand asked him. It was the first question he'd asked Kaelo that wasn't an accusation.

"My grandmother's village is under that hill." Kaelo nodded at a green mound ahead, crowned with a church roof poking above the waterline, tiles intact, bell still hanging. "She is buried above the flood line. My family moved downriver before I was born. But the old people walked this country every year until the water came, and they talked while walking, and children repeat what they hear whether anyone wants them to or not."

"So your charts are stories."

"Our stories are charts." Kaelo almost smiled. Almost. "Yours are paper. Paper floats away."

I wrote that down later, in my field book, under a sketch of the drowned church. I have written a great many things in field books over the years—soil pH, leaf venation, the Latin names of things nobody will ever love—and I can say without sentimentality that this was the first time the notebook felt like an act of witness rather than an act of possession. That distinction matters. It took a drowned valley and a dead man's vault to teach it to me, and I resent the tuition, but I paid it.

* * *

We made camp that night on the church mound itself—the highest ground for kilometers, a hill turned island, the ruined church our roofless hotel. Kaelo built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and cooked river fish wrapped in wild ginger leaves, and for twenty minutes we ate in something adjacent to peace.

It was Elodie who broke it, quietly, passing me the last fish.

"Ines. When you were walking. After the falls, after everyone scattered—" He hesitated. Elodie Delacroix hesitates the way his father never does; it's his best feature. "You were muttering numbers. Counting down. We heard you on the radio frequency too, before we found you. Someone else was counting. Do you remember why?"

I did remember. I had been keeping it in the same sealed room where I kept the image of Dr. Amara's face, and the sound the door made when Brac's men kicked it in, and the specific weight of the surveyor's transit as I used it as a club. You don't open that room for comfort. But he had earned the key, I supposed. He'd carried me three days when I couldn't walk. He'd photographed every document in the vault instead of taking one. He'd chosen truth over his father with his whole body shaking while he did it.

"I count," I said, "because counting is a thing the mind can do when it refuses to do any other thing. In the camp, after the raid, I counted the seconds between their generator cycles. Four hundred and twelve. Every night. Because a generator that runs exactly four hundred and twelve seconds means discipline, and discipline means schedule, and schedule means I could predict when the guard walked past our tent." I looked at the fire. "Counting is how you stay a person. The numbers go down and you are still here to see them land."

"And on the radio?"

"On the radio I was calling for anyone. Counting down is how you make silence into a message. *Nine, eight, seven*—it says: I am still decreasing, I am not yet zero, come find me before I am." I shrugged, and the shrug cost more than I let it show. "Someone answered once. A voice. Then static. I assumed I'd invented it."

Elodie was quiet a moment. "You didn't invent it. That was me. Night four, upriver. Frequency nine."

Well. What do you do with that? Two people, days apart, lost in the same green labyrinth, holding a wire between them across forty miles of floodwater and hostile men, neither knowing the other heard. I laughed—actually laughed, a short ugly bark that startled a heron off the mound—and Elodie looked alarmed until I waved him off.

"We are the two worst rescuers in history," I said. "You found me by accident and I led you to your father by fever. Between us we couldn't organize a picnic."

"We got everyone out."

"Not everyone." The words came out before I could stop them, and I watched him take the hit—Amara, and the two porters whose names I say to myself every night now, Mbeki and Sefu, because the official report will list them as *local hires, deceased* and I refuse to let that be the only ledger they exist in. "No. I'm sorry. That wasn't fair. You got out everyone who could be gotten."

"No," Elodie said slowly. "Don't take it back. It's true, and I need it to be true, because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because everyone keeps telling me I did the right thing. The museum, the elders, Brand, even Kaelo nods at me like I'm a sermon. And I need one person to remind me that the right thing and the good outcome are not the same animal, or I'll start believing I'm clean."

I looked at this man—this exhausted, stubborn, self-flagellating man who had traded his inheritance for a filing cabinet in the capital—and I felt the thing I had been feeling since the falls finally put down roots and admit its own name.

Gratitude. Not the polite kind. The kind that takes.

"Then hear this," I said. "You are not clean. None of us are. I hid inside a fallen tree for six hours while Amara screamed, because moving meant dying, and I have decided to live with that arithmetic every day for the rest of my life. You broadcast the coordinates and the flood took the low villages' planting grounds a week early and no one will ever trace that consequence back to your voice, but it happened. Clean is for instruments. We are people. People are just damage that keeps functioning." I handed him back the fish skin, because wasting food in a drowned world is a sin I still believe in. "And functioning together is the entire trick, Elodie. That's all evolution ever figured out. Everything else is decoration."

He stared at me. Then he did something I hadn't seen him do since the cache burned: he smiled, briefly, crookedly, like a door opening onto a room with furniture still in it.

"Dr. Okafor," he said, "you are the most comforting pessimist I have ever met."

"I'm a botanist. We specialize in things that grow in poor conditions."

* * *

The second day of the drowned forest, the birds confirmed my theory about the figs.

They came at midmorning—a riot of parrots and toucans and one insane flock of ibis, streaming overhead toward the southwest, screaming their opinions about fruit. Kaelo read them instantly. "High ground, two hours, maybe three. The ridge above the delta marshes. After that, the water goes tidal and the current turns against us."

"Tidal?" Brand's voice cracked on the word. "Nobody said anything about tides."

"Nobody asked," Kaelo said serenely.

But it was during those two hours that I saw the thing I will carry longer than the fear, longer than the gunfire, possibly longer than the grief. We rounded a drowned mango tree—an enormous one, crown still alive, fruit ripening impossibly on branches that stood in six feet of water—and there, wedged in the fork of the trunk ten feet above the flood line, was a dugout canoe. Tied off with a rope gone gray with age. Moss grown over the gunwales.

"Somebody escaped up here," Brand said. "Years ago. During the last big flood."

"Somebody escaped," Kaelo agreed, "and tied their boat well, because boats are life, and climbed higher, because climbing is habit, and lived." He touched the rope as we passed, briefly, the way you'd touch a gravestone or a lucky charm. "My people mark trees like this. So the next ones know: others survived here. The water wins ground. It does not win everything."

I looked back at that little moss-covered boat for as long as I could see it, and I thought about Tomas, sitting at the mission outpost with his paid-off debt and his loaded rifle, being our decoy, being somebody's escape rope. I thought about Armand Delacroix, disgraced somewhere behind us, who had sealed a vault full of truth because he loved his work more than the world and then, in the end, in whatever broken arithmetic passed for his conscience, had tried to protect the valley from the woman who owned his contract. I thought about Elodie in the bow, shoulders working, carrying his father's ruin and his own redemption in equal weight and refusing to set either down.

The water wins ground. It does not win everything.

I am a scientist. I am supposed to believe in mechanisms, not omens. But I wrote the sentence in my field book anyway, under the sketch of the drowned church, and if that makes me a sentimental woman, then the record should show that sentiment kept me walking when the fever wanted me dead, and I owe it my life.

* * *

We reached the ridge at dusk.

It rose out of the flood like the spine of some sleeping animal—black basalt, tree-crowned, real honest dry land—and we dragged the boats up its flank and collapsed among the roots. From the crest, looking south, we could see the delta beginning: a vast silver-brown confusion of channels braiding apart, mangrove islands, the river giving up its single identity and becoming a thousand arguments with the sea. Somewhere down there was the river mouth. Somewhere down there, Odile Ferrand was waiting with lawyers and money and a final offer, because men like Brac fail upward into women like Ferrand, and women like Ferrand do not send security forces when a checkbook will do.

Somewhere down there, too, was the hearing. The museum's lawyers had confirmed it by shortwave that morning: an inquiry convened on the dock at Porto Verde, judges arriving by patrol boat, the whole absurd machinery of law assembling itself at the edge of the wilderness because one stubborn salvage diver had shouted the truth into a radio until the world had no choice but to listen.

Elodie stood beside me at the crest, watching the delta eat the light.

"Ferrand will offer me everything tomorrow," he said. "She'll be reasonable. That's the frightening part. She'll be the most reasonable person I've ever met."

"And?"

"And I keep rehearsing the refusal, and it keeps sounding smaller than her offer." He glanced at me. "Talk me out of myself, Doctor. That's an order. Well—not an order. A request. From a functioning damage to another."

I considered the delta, the drowned church behind us, the moss-grown escape boat, the names in my notebook. Then I gave him the only answer I actually believe in.

"When she offers," I said, "look at her hands. People show you what they value. Hers have never been dirty a day in her life. Yours are filthy, Elodie. Keep it that way."

Below us, far out in the braided channels, a light appeared. Then a second. Moving against the current, steady, deliberate, heading for the river mouth.

Boats, coming to meet us—or to meet whoever we were about to become.

Elodie saw them too. He didn't say anything. He just started checking the ties on the boats, getting ready for the last stretch of water, the last argument, the last offer he'd have to refuse with his hands dirty and his heart pounding and the whole valley watching to see what kind of Delacroix finally came down the river.

I picked up my paddle. Gratitude, I've decided, is just love that remembers what it cost. Mine had taken root somewhere in the drowned forest, between the cattle and the church and the counting, and it was coming downriver with us.

All the way to the delta. All the way to her.
