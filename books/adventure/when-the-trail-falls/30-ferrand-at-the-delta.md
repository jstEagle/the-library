## 30. Ferrand at the Delta

The river ended the way most things end—not with a crash but with a widening. For two hundred kilometers it had been a single-minded animal, all muscle and current, and then, without ceremony, it began to spread itself thin across a delta of silt bars and drowned palms, braiding into channels that couldn't agree on which one was the main event. The water went brown-green and slow. The banks went soft. Somewhere in that spreading, I understood, we had left the last territory where a man could hide.

Tomas would have said something wry about it. Tomas was three hundred kilometers upstream in a mission infirmary with a bullet through his shoulder and a debt he'd finally stopped carrying, so instead it was Ines who stood at the bow of our borrowed boat and said, quietly, "You can smell the sea from here."

She was right. Under the mud and the rot and the diesel stink of the outboard there was salt now, faint as a rumor. The delta was already negotiating with the ocean. And moored in the widest channel, where every boat coming downriver would have to pass or stop, sat a white-hulled launch with twin engines and an air conditioner's worth of condensation dripping off its stern like the boat itself was sweating on our behalf.

"Company," Brand said, from his seat among the crates. He'd spent eleven days in a tree learning to hate surprises; he still flinched at wakes. "That's not police."

"No," I said. "Police don't serve lunch."

Because that was the other thing. On the launch's aft deck, under a striped awning, someone had set up a table. White cloth. A silver coffee service catching the light. Two men in linen suits standing behind it with the patient posture of people whose entire job is waiting, and between them, checking her watch against the sun as though the river were running late for *her*, stood Odile Ferrand.

I cut the engine and let us drift the last hundred meters. It seemed appropriate. You don't arrive at a thing like this under your own power.

* * *

She greeted me by name before our hulls touched, and she pronounced it correctly—*El-oh-dee*, the French way, the way my father said it when he was disappointed in me—and I felt the old hook set itself behind my ribs, gentle as a fisherman testing his line.

"Mr. Delacroix." She extended a hand across the gap. Her grip was cool and dry, a hand that had never held anything heavier than a pen. "You look terrible. That's not criticism—it's data. Eleven days of rainforest, a gunshot wound in your party, and you've lost maybe eight kilos. I mention it because it matters to what I'm about to say."

"You've been reading reports on us," I said.

"I write them." She gestured to the table. "Please. Sit. Your people too—there's food, real food, and I'm told the doctor among you hasn't slept since the portage. Dr. Okafor, I believe? Your paper on riparian seed dispersal was excellent, by the way. Cited it myself once, years ago, before any of this."

Ines looked at Ferrand the way you look at a snake that has just complimented your garden. But she was also, I saw, looking at the food. We all were. Hunger makes honest creatures of us, and Ferrand knew it, and the coffee service wasn't hospitality—it was leverage, poured hot into cups.

We ate. That's the humiliating truth of chapter thirty of any expedition: at some point the villain feeds you and you let her, because your body has stopped taking orders from your principles. There was bread that didn't come from a tin. There was fruit. There was coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in, and while we ate, Ferrand talked about nothing—the weather, the flood season arriving early, the regrettable state of river policing in the upper territories—and she talked about it with such unremarkable competence that I caught myself almost relaxing, and hated myself for it, and relaxed a little more anyway.

Then the lawyers unfolded their documents, and the weather talk was over.

* * *

Here is what I will say for Odile Ferrand, and I say it knowing how it sounds: she was the most reasonable person I met on that entire river. Brac was arithmetic wearing a man's face. My father was pride wearing a scholar's. The institute men in the capital had been cowardice in committee form. But Ferrand—Ferrand had done the math on every possible version of this ending, and she had arrived at the delta prepared to offer me the only deal that a rational man could take, and she presented it without threat, without theater, with the weary courtesy of a bank manager explaining compound interest.

"Three items," she said, tapping the first folder. "One. Your father's reputation. The falsified logs, the sealed discovery, the missing check-ins—all of it becomes an administrative error. A filing mistake by the institute, already corrected. Armand Delacroix retires with honors. There are men on the awards committee who owe me; it will be painless, even dignified. He'll hate it, of course. He'll hate being rescued more than he'd hate ruin. But he'll have his name back, and you'll have given him that."

I said nothing. She took my silence for progress, which, to be fair, it was.

"Two." Second folder. "You. Salvage work in the lowlands pays what—a pittance, grant to grant, digging in other people's middens? I will endow a chair. Not a grant. A chair. Institute of Delta Archaeology, your name over the door, funding guaranteed for twenty years, publication freedom absolute. You will never again have to choose between a trowel and a train ticket. Every site you ever wanted to open, opened. Every student you ever wanted to train, trained. Do you understand what I'm offering, Mr. Delacroix? I'm offering you the career your father had, without the compromises he made to keep it."

"That's a lie," Ines said quietly. "It's exactly the compromises. Wrapped better."

Ferrand didn't even look at her. That was its own kind of message—that Ines was scenery, that the offer was addressed to the Delacroix bloodline and no one else. "Three," she continued, "the valley itself. This is the part everyone gets wrong, so listen carefully. I am not asking you to destroy the documents. I'm asking you to give them to me *instead of to a courtroom*. Think about what happens otherwise. The originals enter evidence. Lawyers for three governments get involved. The valley becomes a legal battlefield for thirty years while the peoples who actually live there watch their rivers partitioned by men in suits who've never seen them. I can deliver stewardship—real stewardship, funded, enforced—in eighteen months, quietly, because I own the people who would otherwise obstruct it. Or you can have truth, loud and slow, and the valley drowns in procedure. Which does the living villages more good?"

And God help me, the question landed. Because it was a good question. It was the best question anyone had asked me since the letter that smelled of river water, and it was built, deliberately, expertly, out of true things. The courts *were* slow. The governments *were* vultures. Everything she said about procedure was accurate, and everything she offered was real, and the only false note in the entire symphony was the small matter of what she intended to do with the originals once she held them—which was, according to a radio we'd pulled out of Brac's boat, to make sure they ceased to exist, along with anyone who could testify to their contents.

"In exchange," I said, "you want the originals. All of them. The photographs too."

"The photographs are irrelevant—you'll find copies get made regardless, and I've budgeted for that. What matters is provenance. Whoever holds the originals holds the claim. I hold claims the way your father holds grudges: permanently, and with intent." She folded her hands. "That's the whole transaction, Mr. Delacroix. Reputation, future, and a faster justice, in exchange for paper. Paper that, I remind you, you found eleven days ago and have carried through hell at the cost of one man shot and another nearly mad. Ask yourself what the paper is worth at that price. Ask yourself whether the dead paid for truth or for your certainty."

There it was. The hook behind my ribs, reeled in a click. Because she knew about Tomas. Of course she knew—Brac reported to her, and somewhere in her files was a line item reading *one (1) boatman, shoulder, non-fatal*, and she had read that line item aloud to me over coffee as if grief were a discount I might apply to my own conscience.

Brand shifted beside me, and I heard him murmur, half to himself, "Don't answer yet. People who need an answer fast are buying something." Eleven days in a tree had made him a philosopher of suspicion, and for once I was grateful for it.

"I need until morning," I said.

Something flickered across Ferrand's face—not disappointment. Recalculation. "Until morning," she agreed. "My launch will anchor here. Yours can tie alongside; there's no reason anyone should sleep on the water tonight with the flood coming down. Consider it—" she paused, and chose the word with visible care, "—a courtesy. One of the last ones available."

* * *

We tied up at dusk. The delta did its evening business around us—egrets going to roost like thrown linen, a fishing family poling past without looking at either boat, because people who live on rivers learn early which vessels to pretend not to see. Ines checked Brand's hands for tremor and found them steady, which surprised neither of them more than it should have. I sat on the stern with the document case between my knees and watched Ferrand's lit windows across ten meters of black water, and I thought about my father.

Twenty years of silence, and in the last month I'd learned more about him than in all the years before: that he falsified logs out of fear dressed as principle, that he hid a discovery out of love deformed into possession, that he would rather burn his life's work than see it used. None of that excused him. All of it explained the exact shape of the trap Ferrand was building for me, because it was the same trap he'd lived in—*protect the work, protect the name, protect the secret*, and the protecting eats everything you were protecting it *for*.

Armand would take the deal. That was the terrible clarity of it. Given the choice between ruin and rescue, my father would choose rescue and spend the rest of his life despising himself competently. And Ferrand knew that too. The offer wasn't really for me. It was a demonstration—*look what I can do for the Delacroix name; imagine what I can do to it*—and I was meant to carry it upstream like a sample case.

Ines came aft and sat down without asking, the way people do after they've shared a gunfight with you.

"She's good," she said. "Better than Brac. Brac you can hate. She makes hating feel like bad manners."

"I know."

"The chair is real, Elodie. That's what frightens me. She'd fund it. She funds things—it's what she *is*. And in five years you'd be the most respected archaeologist in the country, and in ten you'd be her, and you wouldn't even notice the year it happened."

Across the water, a door opened on the white launch. Light spilled out, and a figure stepped onto the deck—one of the linen suits, alone, cigarette tip glowing. He stood at the rail a long moment, then did something strange: he looked directly at our boat, at me, and tapped two fingers against his breast pocket. Twice. Then he went back inside.

Brand materialized at my shoulder like a man who'd never fully left his tree. "Message," he said. "Or bait."

"With Ferrand those are the same product," I said. But I wrote it down anyway, because eleven days on this river had taught me that everything is information, especially the things that seem like mistakes.

* * *

Morning came gray and heavy, the sky already working up its first real rain. Ferrand received me on her deck at seven sharp, freshly pressed, coffee refilled, patience reinstalled overnight like new firmware.

"Well?" she said.

I put the document case on the table between us. Her eyes went to it and stayed there, and for the first time since the capital I saw something move behind the competence—not greed exactly. Appetite. The look of a woman watching the last variable resolve.

"Before you answer," she said, "understand that this is the only version of events where everyone you love comes out whole. After this table, there are only costs. I'm offering you the last free thing you will ever be given."

"You're right," I said. "It's generous. It's the most reasonable offer anyone's made me in my life. And I've spent eleven days trying to find the flaw in it, and the flaw is that it requires me to believe you're the exception." I opened the case—not to surrender it, but so she could see the folders inside, the photographs, the journal pages, the documents older than every border she owned. "Every person on this river who touched this secret got bought, buried, or burned. The village that turned its backs. The hunter's family. Tomas. Ines. Even Brac—he's not evil, he's *employed*, and employment with you is a terminal condition. You're not offering me a way out of the story, Madame Ferrand. You're offering to write the ending yourself."

Her expression didn't change. That was the frightening part. Somewhere behind her eyes a ledger simply updated, and the number next to my name moved from *asset* to *expense*.

"So it's the museum," she said. "The broadcast. My sources tell me you transmitted coordinates four days ago—to the national museum, the press bureau, the territorial office. Unencrypted. Repeated twice." She said it without heat, and that was worse than heat. "Then this meeting was theater on both sides. You came to see corruption's reasonable voice, and I came to price yours." She closed the empty folder in front of her, gently, the way one closes a coffin lid on someone still breathing. "For the record, Mr. Delacroix: I would have honored every term. That's what none of you will ever understand. I keep my contracts. It's the world that doesn't keep its people."

"And the valley?" Ines asked from the rail, where she'd appeared without either of us noticing. "Does the contract cover the valley?"

"The valley," Ferrand said, rising, gathering her papers, "is about to discover what thirty years of litigation feels like. I told you the truth about that too. You chose the slow knife." She turned to me one last time, and gave me—of all things—a smile, brief and genuine and utterly chilling, the smile of a chess player acknowledging a move she hadn't anticipated but had already priced in. "Your father, at least, will be easy to ruin. He built his reputation on foundations I own. Give him my regards. Tell him the filing error has been... unresolved."

Behind her, the linen suits began striking the awning. The launch's engines came awake with a sound like a throat clearing before a verdict.

But as Ferrand moved toward her cabin, the man with the cigarettes—the one from the rail—stepped into her path, spoke three words too low for us to hear, and handed her a telegram. She read it. And I watched Odile Ferrand, for the first time in two meetings and one very long river, go completely still.

Whatever was on that paper, it hadn't come from her side of the board.

She looked up at me across the deck, and all the reasonableness had drained out of her face, leaving something I recognized at last, because I'd seen it in my father's eyes at the cache, in Brand's in the tree, and once, horribly, in a mirror:

Fear.

"Change of plans," she said quietly, to no one, and the delta rain finally broke.
