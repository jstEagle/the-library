## 32. Brac's Reckoning

There is a particular kind of silence a man learns to hear when he has spent twenty years being the instrument of other people's decisions. It isn't the silence of empty rooms. It's the silence of a radio operator who stops answering your check-ins on schedule. I'd been hearing it for two days before I understood what it meant.

My name is Brac. I don't use my first name anymore because the last person who used it affectionately was a woman in Barranquilla who also stole my wallet, and I decided after that that names were a liability. Ferrand called me "my quiet man," which was her way of saying she never wanted to hear me speak unless it was to confirm something she'd already decided. I confirmed things for eleven years. Confirming is easy work if you don't look at the arithmetic underneath.

The arithmetic was the problem now.

* * *

Let me back up, because a man turning on his employer deserves at least the dignity of an explanation, even if he only gives it to himself, alone, in a mission outpost that smells of mildew and old hymnals.

I came up this river with six men and a contract. The contract said: recover the artifacts, secure the site, manage the situation. "Manage" is a beautiful word. It can mean anything from "escort them out politely" to "leave no witnesses," depending on which page you read and whether you read page nine at all. I had read page nine. I had initialed page nine. That's the thing people don't understand about men like me—we're not monsters who wander into atrocity by accident. We sign.

For most of the trip upriver, the work was what I expected. A chase, mostly. The Delacroix son and his ragged collection of survivors—a botanist with fever scars, a surveyor who flinched at his own shadow, a boatman with a shoulder I'd put a bullet through, and some local hunter whose name I never bothered learning, which turned out to be the single most expensive piece of ignorance of my career. We burned their boats at the village. I ordered it myself. I told myself it was logistics: deny them transport, control the river, end the pursuit without ending anyone. My men were grateful for the order. Men like mine are always grateful when you find a way to be cruel that doesn't require killing, because cruelty without corpses doesn't follow you home.

But the radio traffic from downriver had changed its texture. Ferrand's dispatches used to come twice daily, precise as invoices. Then they slowed. Then the language shifted. Where she once wrote *secure and hold*, she began writing *resolve*. Where she once listed names of personnel to extract, she stopped listing names entirely. And three nights ago, on the coded channel we shared with her delta office—thinking, in our arrogance, that no one else could read it—I caught a fragment of a conversation between her office and a legal firm in the capital. Not mercenaries. Lawyers. They were discussing, in the flat diction of people pricing real estate, "the disposition of field assets."

Field assets. That was us. That was me and my six men, filed somewhere between the boats and the crates.

I sat with that word for a long time in the dark of the outpost chapel, listening to the river eat the banks outside. *Disposition.* You dispose of garbage. You dispose of evidence. You do not dispose of a man who has spent eleven years confirming things for you.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being good at this work: competence is a leash. Every year I got better at managing situations, and every year Ferrand trusted me with less explanation and more consequence. She never lied to me—that would have required her to think of me as someone who needed persuading. She simply told me less each time, the way you stop explaining yourself to a dog. By this season I was receiving orders like *the son is not to reach the delta* with no context at all, and I executed them, because executing orders was the one skill I had that kept the world arranged in a shape I could stand.

I want to be honest about why I did what I did next, because there are two versions and only one is true.

The false version is the noble one: that I turned state's evidence out of conscience, that the burning village or the bullet in Tomas Reyes woke something in me. Conscience makes a better story. But conscience is slow, and what happened to me happened fast, in the space of one decoded transmission.

The true version is this: I realized I was worth more to her dead than alive.

It was in the numbers. Ferrand thought in numbers the way priests think in Latin—it was her native tongue, and she assumed no one around her was fluent. But I'd done her books for a decade. I knew what the expedition had cost, what the artifacts were conservatively worth, what the insurance and the legal exposure ran. And when the vault's existence went public—when the Delacroix boy broadcast those coordinates to every museum and newsroom on the coast—the entire calculus inverted overnight. As long as the find was secret, witnesses were a threat. Once it was public, witnesses were proof. Proof of what? Of everything she'd done to keep it secret. Every burned boat, every shot across the water, every man I'd frightened or hurt—all of it converted, the moment truth went public, from asset to liability.

She didn't need the valley silenced anymore. She needed the men who'd tried to silence it.

And so the transmission I decoded in the chapel, the one that ended my career of confirming things, was addressed not to me but past me—to a secondary contractor, a cleanup crew out of the delta, with my own position coordinates attached and the notation: *primary team compromised; disposition upon contact.*

Disposition upon contact. I read it four times. Then I laughed, actually laughed, alone in a house of God, because eleven years of loyalty and the woman had priced me below the cost of a lawyer's retainer. I wasn't even expensive to kill. That was the insult of it. Not the dying—dying had been on the table since page nine—but the cheapness.

* * *

My men. Six of them. I should say something about my men, because whatever else I am, I am not the kind of man who disposes of his own.

They were not idealists. None of them had joined up to protect archaeological heritage or whatever the institute's brochures claimed. They'd joined for pay that arrived on time, and they stayed because I never wasted them. In eleven years I'd brought home every man I'd ever led—not unscratched, but breathing. It was the one ledger I kept clean, and I kept it clean partly out of decency and partly because a leader whose men die starts losing men, and I was very good at my job.

So when I gathered them in the outpost yard at dawn and told them what I'd decoded, I watched six faces do the same calculation I had done, at six different speeds. Voss, my second, got there first—he always did; it was why he was my second and why he'd never take the job, because getting there first meant seeing the worst of it. He looked at the ground and said, quietly, "How long do we have?"

"Cleanup crews travel light and fast. Three days. Maybe four if the weather argues."

"And the Delacroix party?"

"Two days ahead of us, running flooded water, half of them wounded." I paused. "Heading for the same dock we are."

Voss understood immediately, because Voss always understood immediately. There was only one place on this river where anything could be settled—the delta, the hearing that was already assembling itself, lawyers and river police and museum men converging on the mouth of the river like flies on a wound. Ferrand would be there. Her cleanup crew would be there. And the people we'd spent a month trying to bury would be there, alive, talking.

"You want to defect," Voss said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I want to live," I said. "Defection is just living with paperwork."

One of the younger ones, Reyes-adjacent in temperament, all jaw and grievance, spat and said we'd hang. Maybe. But hanging required someone to catch us first, and right now the only person hunting us was a woman who'd already sold us, and the only alternative was waiting in the weeds for a cleanup crew whose contract, I could promise him from personal experience, contained a page nine.

I gave them the choice. That matters to me, that it was a choice. Four came. One didn't—Marek, who had a wife in Cayenne and a faith in Ferrand's arithmetic that I envied for exactly as long as it took him to pack his kit and walk into the forest toward the delta road, alone, trusting the woman who'd filed him under *disposition*. I didn't stop him. I've thought about that every hour since. I tell myself it was respect for his choice. Some nights I almost believe it.

* * *

What does a man do with the night before he betrays everything?

I inventoried. It's what I know. I laid out on the chapel floor everything I had that could hurt Ferrand: the original contract with page nine initialed in my own hand. Eleven years of coded dispatch logs, copied against regulations because I learned early that the man who keeps copies is the man who survives audits. The transmission ordering my disposal, transcribed and timestamped. The manifest of what we'd burned at the village, which I had written honestly because even my cruelties I documented accurately—a habit I now understood had been preparing me for this night my whole life.

And then there was the thing I hadn't planned to give anyone: the recording. Three weeks ago, before the falls, before any of this, Ferrand had spoken to me directly over the coded channel—voice, not cipher text, which she reserved for moments she considered important enough to trust to her own mouth. She had said, in that cool invoice voice: *"Brac. If the son reaches the documents, there will be no extraction. Understand me. Nothing leaves that valley that can testify."* I had recorded it, out of habit, out of the same instinct that made me copy the logs. I had listened to it perhaps forty times since, and each time I heard something new in it. This time I finally heard what had been there all along: she wasn't warning me. She was rehearsing my eulogy.

I sat on the chapel steps until dawn with the recorder in my pocket like a stone, watching the river run brown and swollen toward the sea, carrying everything upstream toward everything downstream, indifferent as arithmetic.

* * *

We moved at first light, my five and I, down the old portage trail toward the delta, and I'll grant the situation one irony: we traveled lighter and faster than any crew I'd ever commanded, because we carried nothing we intended to keep. No crates. No claims. Just paper and testimony and the particular lightness of men who have stopped pretending their employer's interests and their own are the same thing.

Voss walked beside me through the drowned grass and asked the question I'd been avoiding. "Why now?" he said. "You read page nine years ago. You burned the boats. What changed?"

I could have given him the noble answer. Instead I gave him the true one, because if we were going to hang together, we'd hang honest.

"She taught me the math," I said. "Eleven years of her math. And the math says a witness who knows too much is worth more dead than paid. I just finally checked which column I was in."

He was quiet for a while. Then: "And the Delacroix boy? The one you were sent to stop?"

"What about him?"

"He's doing what you're doing. Telling the truth to save himself." Voss shrugged. "Seems like you're on his side now."

"I'm not on anyone's side," I said, and it was mostly true. "I'm on the side of the man holding the evidence. Today that's me. Tomorrow, God help me, it might have to be everyone."

* * *

We reached the delta outskirts on the second evening, ahead of schedule, ahead of the cleanup crew, and found the river mouth transformed. The hearing had grown teeth while we walked: floodlights on the dock, river police launches riding at anchor, a museum delegation under canvas, journalists—actual journalists, with cameras—corralled behind a rope line. And at the center of it, immaculate despite a month of river war, Odile Ferrand, holding court on the veranda of the customs house with lawyers fanned around her like cards.

I watched her from the treeline for a full hour, through my glass, and I felt nothing I expected. No rage. No fear, even. Something colder and more useful: recognition. She was doing arithmetic. I could see it in the way her eyes moved—dock, police launch, crowd, dock again—counting assets, counting liabilities, pricing the room. Somewhere in that accounting I appeared as a line item, and somewhere in that accounting a cleanup crew was en route to adjust my figure downward.

The last of the light went off the water. Behind me, Voss cleared his throat.

"So how do we play it?" he asked. "Walk in tonight? Wait for the hearing?"

"Neither," I said. "If we walk in tonight, her lawyers bury us in procedure before we finish a sentence. If we wait, the cleanup crew finds us in the reeds." I pocketed the glass. "We go to the police launch first. Tonight. We hand them the recordings, the contracts, the transmission ordering our own deaths—and we ask for protection in exchange for testimony. State's evidence. Full disclosure. Everything."

"And if the police are bought?"

"Then we're dead a day early," I said, "and I'll have saved myself the embarrassment of testifying."

He almost smiled. Five tired killers walking toward a police launch to confess, and one of us nearly smiled.

As we came down onto the mud flats, the floodlights swept the water, and I saw the Delacroix party's boat tied at the far end of the dock—scarred, overloaded, alive. Somewhere on that dock was the son I'd been contracted to erase, and tomorrow morning, in front of lawyers and police and the rising river, we would be on the same side of the table for the first time. I found I minded that less than I'd expected. Instruments don't get to choose their symphonies. But a witness—ah. A witness chooses exactly one thing: which side of the truth he stands on.

The sentry on the police launch saw us coming and racked his rifle. I raised both hands, slow, and called out across the black water in my flattest, most confirmatory voice:

"Don't shoot. My name is Brac, and I'm here to make a confession that's going to ruin a very respectable woman—provided you keep me alive until sunrise."
