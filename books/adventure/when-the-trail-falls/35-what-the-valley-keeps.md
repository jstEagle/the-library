## 35. What the Valley Keeps

The hearing on the dock lasted eleven days, which by the standards of legal proceedings in this country was practically a gunshot. I'd expected months. I'd expected lawyers to arrive like weather systems, front after front of them, each one wetter and colder than the last. Instead we got a magistrate named Corvalán who had been sent upriver because nobody senior wanted the assignment, and who turned out to be exactly the man the moment required: a small, dry, unimpressed person with reading glasses he cleaned on his sleeve and a habit of asking questions as though he already knew the answers and was merely checking whether you did too.

He held court in the mission outpost's long room, which smelled of varnish and river damp, with the water rising visibly past the window ledges day by day like an hourglass running in the wrong direction. The flood season had come early and come angry. Twice the proceedings paused while men moved the evidence crates onto tables. Once they paused because a caiman came up the boat ramp with the unhurried confidence of a latecomer who knows his seat is reserved.

I testified for three days. Photographs, journal pages, the ledger with my father's two handwritings in it—the careful one and the hurried one, the scholar and the man covering his tracks. Corvalán read everything twice. He asked me once, near the end, whether I understood that by surrendering the coordinates I had surrendered any claim I or my family might have made to the find.

"I never had a claim," I said. "I had a map."

He looked at me over the glasses for a long moment, and then he wrote something down, and I have spent some time since wondering what it was. Probably *subject is either honest or very good at this.* With magistrates it's always one of those two.

* * *

Ferrand's people fought the way drowning people fight—flailing, expensive, and aimed mostly at their own rescuers. Her lawyers produced contracts, and Corvalán read the contracts, and then he produced the documents from inside the vault, and there was a silence in that room I expect none of us will forget. Because here is what my father found under the falls, stripped of gold and glamour: paper. Treaties. A record, in ink older than every border on the modern maps, showing that the valley had never been ceded, sold, signed away, or surrendered. Not by anyone. Every colonial claim to that land rested on a signature that had never existed, and Ferrand's entire financial architecture—her concessions, her options, her "claim rights"—rested on the colonial claims.

Her lead counsel stood up and said something about provenance and jurisdiction, and Corvalán said, mildly, "Whose jurisdiction, precisely?" and the counsel sat back down, and that was more or less the end of it.

Not entirely the end. There were ugly days. A lawyer for the institute argued that the vault's contents should pass to the state as "ownerless property," and the hunter—Kaelen, though he never told me his name; Ines learned it from the village elders and guarded it like ammunition—stood up in the middle of the argument without being called on, which should have been fatal, and said nothing at all. He simply set the carved token on the table in front of Corvalán and sat back down. And Corvalán picked it up, turned it in the light, looked at the same markings I'd photographed on the vault door, and said, "Ownerless," in a tone that made the word sound like an insult someone had just paid him to repeat.

The ruling came on the eleventh morning, delivered standing, quickly, before the river could rise another inch. Stewardship of the vault and its contents awarded jointly to the valley peoples, represented by the council of elders of the river settlements, with the national museum holding a custodial role—custodial, Corvalán emphasized, not proprietary—and all commercial claims annulled. The valley would be sealed under their law. Their law, he said, which apparently involved seasons, ceremonies, and a prohibition on entry that Kaelen later explained to me was less a lock than a promise: the door stays shut because everyone agrees it stays shut, and if anyone breaks the agreement, everyone else remembers.

"Not forgotten," Corvalán added, almost as an afterthought, gathering his papers. "Sealed is not forgotten. Let the record show the distinction."

Let the record show. I've thought about that phrase since. It may be the only sentence spoken in that room that will outlast all of us.

* * *

Ferrand did not attend the ruling. She had left on the third day, when it became clear which way the current ran, taking her lawyers and her money and leaving Brac behind to face whatever came next—which, given what Brac knew and what he'd begun telling the police investigators, was going to be considerably worse than losing a contract. I watched her boat go from the dock. She didn't look back. People like her never do; looking back implies there was something behind them worth having been.

What struck me, watching the wake flatten against the pilings, was how little I felt. I'd spent weeks imagining this woman as the engine of everything—the pursuit, the burned boats, Tomas's shoulder, the bullet meant for me that found him instead. And she was all of those things, and she was also, in the end, just a person in a boat, going away, smaller and smaller until the river took her the way it takes everything.

Hate needs a face that stays still long enough to hate. Hers kept moving.

* * *

The inheritance question came up the way these things do—not dramatically, but sideways, in paperwork, in a conversation I didn't want to be having with a museum registrar who had arrived on the supply boat with a clipboard and an apologetic smile.

"The elder Delacroix's personal effects," she said. "Journals, field notes, the survey instruments. As next of kin—"

"They're not mine."

She blinked. "Legally—"

"Legally, half of what he wrote was falsified and the other half belongs in the archive where the valley council can see it. Take all of it. Catalogue it. Publish it." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, and I made myself soften it, because the registrar hadn't done anything except stand between me and a decision I'd already made. "There's a page missing from the second journal. If it turns up, it goes to the council first, then the archive. That's the order."

She wrote it down. Everyone writes everything down now. It's the whole world's new religion, and I converted somewhere around chapter twenty-five of my own story, so I can't complain.

But walking back along the dock afterward, I felt the shape of what I'd given up settle onto my shoulders properly for the first time. Not the money—I'd never counted on the money, or told myself I hadn't, which is nearly the same thing until it isn't. The name. That was the real cargo I'd jettisoned. For twenty years I had been Armand Delacroix's son, defined by distance from him, and even the distance was a kind of inheritance, a plot of land I squatted on. Now the name was going into the archive too, catalogued, cross-referenced, footnoted with asterisks. *Discovered the vault. Falsified the logs. Sealed the door for reasons part principle, part cowardice, part love, proportions disputed.*

Every dig I ever lead will begin with that footnote. I know how academia works. Footnotes are forever.

And here is the thing I keep circling and failing to land on: I don't regret it. I keep waiting for the regret, the way you wait for rain in the dry season, checking the sky. It doesn't come. What comes instead is something quieter and harder to carry—this sense that I finally own exactly what I actually earned, no more, no less, and that the weight of it is honest weight. My father spent his life accumulating a reputation the size of a cathedral, and it collapsed on him in a week. I'm left with a shed. But the shed is mine, and the tools in it work.

* * *

On the last evening, before the boats went downriver, Kaelen came to find me.

He'd been present for the whole hearing in the manner of his people—present the way a mountain is present, unannounced and unimpressed—and I'd learned to read him a little, or thought I had. He communicated chiefly through timing. When he appeared somewhere, something was about to matter.

He found me at the end of the dock, where I was watching the water climb the pilings and doing a poor job of pretending it wasn't a metaphor. He sat down beside me without asking, which by then I understood as courtesy rather than intrusion. We watched the river together for a while. It was doing what it does—carrying the whole sky upside down, moving enormous weights without visible effort, keeping its own counsel.

"You gave it away," he said at last. His trade-language was precise but slow, each word set down like a stone in a crossing.

"I didn't give away anything of mine."

"Your father's name." He turned the token over in his fingers. I hadn't seen him take it out. "His life's work. You put it in the water and let the current decide."

"That's roughly the shape of it, yes."

He nodded slowly, and then he did the thing I will remember longer than the ruling, longer than Corvalán's dry voice, longer probably than my own name. He held the token out to me.

"It goes back to the door," he said. "That is our law. It was carried out; it must be carried home. But the one who carried it out—" he tapped his chest, then pointed at me, "—was you. So the returning is yours to make. Not mine. I only walk beside you."

I stared at the token. Carved hardwood, palm-sized, worn smooth at the edges by generations of hands, marked with the spiral-and-notch pattern that matched the vault door the way a key matches a lock it was never meant to open—only to witness.

"I can't read your law," I said. "I'll get it wrong."

"You already got it right." Almost a smile. The first I'd seen. "You opened your hand. That is the whole of it. Everything else is ceremony."

We went at dawn, Kaelen and I, with Ines and Brand asking to come and Kaelen allowing it with a shrug that contained multitudes. The falls were louder than I remembered, the river swollen with the early rains, the curtain of water thrown wide and white. Behind it, the stone mouth of the vault, sealed again—new mortar in the old seams, laid by the elders' masons in the days since the ruling, and above the door, freshly cut, the spiral-and-notch, deep enough to outlast the language of whoever reads it.

Kaelen stopped at the edge of the spray and gestured me forward alone.

I stood in the thunder and the cold drifting mist and held the token a while, feeling its weight, thinking about my father, who was even then downriver in a police launch heading for the capital and a hearing of his own, thinking about the letter I was going to have to write him and had not yet found the first sentence of. Then I pressed the token into a recess above the door that might have been carved for it ten years ago or three hundred, and it fit the way true things fit, and I stepped back.

Ines cried a little. Brand pretended he hadn't noticed, which was its own kindness. Kaelen touched the door once, flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and said words in his own language that I didn't understand and didn't need to. Sealed is not forgotten. The valley keeps what the valley keeps.

Walking back through the green dark toward the boats, Ines fell into step beside me.

"So," she said. "What does the famous Elodie Delacroix do now? The papers will want a quote. Something dignified. 'It belongs to history,' perhaps."

"It belongs to the valley," I said. "History can visit."

"And you?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I usually managed that month. Ahead of us the river waited, high and brown and indifferent, ready to carry us home through country that had tried to kill us and failed and would try again someday without malice. Somewhere downriver, a father who couldn't say sorry and a son who couldn't say it either, and twenty years of silence that one letter might—or might not—begin to drain.

"I'm going to write a letter," I said. "And then I'm going to start a dig that tells the truth."

Ines smiled. "In that order?"

"Haven't decided."

Behind us, under the falls, the water kept saying the only word it has ever known, over the sealed door, to no one, forever.
