## 36. Father and Son, Unfinished

The hearing ended the way hearings do—not with thunder but with paperwork. A magistrate with ink-stained cuffs read the final disposition aloud in a voice that could have been reciting a grocery list: stewardship of the vault and its contents transferred to the joint council of valley communities, pending formal recognition by the national museum; all expedition materials seized as evidence; Dr. Armand Delacroix's professional credentials suspended pending review; no criminal charges filed at this time.

*At this time.* The phrase hung in the air like river mist—present, damp, and promising to burn off into something worse later.

Armand sat through it without moving. He'd worn his good jacket for the occasion, the one with the brass buttons he used to polish before symposiums, except now it hung on him like a flag of a country that had stopped existing. When the magistrate said *suspended*, Armand nodded once, the way a man nods when a doctor confirms what he already suspected about the lump.

Ferrand was gone by then. Escorted off the dock two days earlier with her lawyers orbiting her like anxious birds, her face arranged into an expression I can only describe as *audited*. Brac had turned state's evidence the morning before, and whatever he'd told the authorities had done more damage to Ferrand in four hours than my photographs had done in three weeks. Power, it turns out, devours its own instruments—and then files a complaint about the quality of the meal.

I stood at the edge of the dock after everyone else had drifted away, watching the river carry its usual cargo of silt and secrets toward the sea, and I realized I had absolutely no idea what to say to my father.

Twenty years of silence, and then six weeks of shared catastrophe, and now this: a dock, a sunset, and two men who were related by blood and nothing else they could name out loud.

He solved it first, because he always did. He picked up his single bag—the famous Armand Delacroix, whose expeditions once required forty porters and a chartered steamer, reduced to one canvas duffel—and walked past me toward the town.

"I'm taking the morning boat," he said. "You don't have to come."

"Good," I said. "Because I wasn't going to."

We both knew it was a lie. We'd gotten very good at lies over the years. It was practically our family language.

* * *

The boarding house in the delta town had exactly two rooms left, which is the kind of coincidence that happens in small towns where the innkeeper has already decided you're father and son whether you like it or not. Señora Vasquez gave us keys, pointed at the dining room, and informed us that dinner was at seven and attendance was not optional. She'd lost a son to the river herself, she said, in a tone that made clear the subject was closed and so was any argument about skipping meals.

So we ate dinner across from each other under a lamp with a moth bumping against the glass, eating stew that tasted mostly of pepper and patience.

"You should know," Armand said, somewhere around the second bowl, "that I'm not going to apologize."

I set down my spoon. "That's a bold opening move."

"It's not an apology you want." He looked tired in a way I'd never seen—not expedition-tired, not dig-tired, but something structural, like a building whose foundation had finally been inspected. "If I apologize, you'll be generous, and you'll forgive me, and then in ten years we'll be right back here, because I will have learned nothing except that apologizing works. And I refuse to learn nothing. It's the only thing I've ever refused."

"That's not true," I said. "You also refused to file honest logs for eleven months."

The moth hit the glass again. Somewhere in the kitchen, Señora Vasquez dropped a pot, either accidentally or as commentary.

"No," Armand said quietly. "It isn't. You're right. I falsified the logs. I let Ferrand believe the find was smaller than it was while I decided what to do, and by the time I'd decided, deciding had become hiding, and hiding had become lying, and Ines nearly died of fever in the gap between who I claimed to be and who I was." He turned his bowl a quarter-turn, aligning it with the table edge, a habit I remembered from childhood—he straightened things when he couldn't straighten himself. "I'm not asking you to understand it. Understanding would imply there's a system to comprehend. There wasn't. There was just a man who loved his work more than his word, and discovered too late that they were supposed to be the same thing."

I waited for the rest of it—for the *but*, the justification, the brilliant man's explanation of why his brilliance excused him. It didn't come. He just sat there with his aligned bowl and his dead career and asked for nothing.

And that, more than anything he could have said, made me angry.

"You could ask," I said. "For anything. Money—I don't have much, but I have some. A reference. Help getting your papers in order. You're sitting across from the only person alive who watched you seal that vault and unseal your own reputation, and you're asking for *nothing*?"

"I'm asking for nothing," he said, "because everything I might ask for, I'd be asking you to give me instead of earning. And I've spent twenty years teaching you that inheritance is theft if you don't earn it. I'd hate for the lesson to go to waste."

"You didn't teach me that," I said. "You taught me that you'd rather be right than reachable. Everything else I taught myself."

He flinched. Actually flinched—a small motion around the eyes, quickly controlled, but I saw it, and seeing it gave me no satisfaction whatsoever. That was the worst discovery of the whole delta: that winning an argument against my father felt exactly like losing one.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the boarding house steps with a beer gone warm and watched the moon do its slow accounting over the water, and around midnight the door opened behind me and Armand came out carrying two chairs, which he set down next to mine with the exaggerated care of a man handling explosives.

"The innkeeper says the morning boat leaves at six," he said.

"I heard."

"She also says you paid for my room."

"I paid for the room."

"Why?"

"Because you're broke and proud, and only one of those is fixable this week."

He almost smiled. I saw it happen at the corner of his mouth and then get suppressed, like a fire spotted early. He lowered himself into the other chair with a sound I pretended not to hear—knees, back, the whole orchestra of a man in his sixties who'd slept in a cave guarding gold crates for a month.

"When you were nine," he said, "you asked me why I kept going back to the field. Every year. You wanted to know what was out there that was worth leaving."

"And you said—" I searched for it. It was there, buried under twenty years of resentment like a shard under topsoil. "You said the truth doesn't stay put. You said somebody has to chase it or it gets buried by people with shovels and agendas."

"Mm." He looked at the river. "Do you know what I never told you? I wasn't talking about archaeology. Not entirely. I was talking about your mother."

The beer stopped halfway to my mouth.

"She left because I wouldn't stop digging—literally, figuratively, every way a man can refuse to come home. And I told myself a story about it, the way we all do. That she couldn't handle the life. That the truth of us was something she'd failed to appreciate." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "But the truth doesn't stay put, Elodie. It took me thirty years to catch up to the truth about myself, and when I finally dug down to it, what I found was a coward holding a very impressive trowel."

I didn't say anything. The moth had followed us out and was now bumping against the porch lamp, committed to its wrong light.

"You exposed me," he went on. "On the radio, in front of the museum, the press, the elders—you burned my name to save those people, and it was the correct thing to do, and I have hated you for it every day since, including today, including right now, and I am telling you this because you deserve to know that doing right can still cost you someone's love. That's not a threat. It's a warning label. Read it before you open the package."

"Noted," I said, when I could speak. "Anything else on the label? Storage instructions? Keep away from children?"

"One thing." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the lamplight caught him the old way—the symposium way, the *this man has found something* way—and my chest did a stupid, involuntary thing. "The next time you lead a dig—and you will; you led a portage nobody survives twice, you ran flood-season water, you brought every living soul home; you're leading one whether you accept the title or not—publish everything. Real time. No sealed vaults, no private claims, no clever Armand Delacroix maneuvers. Let the world watch you work. It will cost you half your discoveries to the poachers and the plagiarists, and it will buy you the only thing worth having in this profession, which is being able to look at yourself in the morning without conducting an excavation."

"That sounds like advice."

"It is. Take it or leave it. I've been wrong about most things, but I've been wrong *expertly*."

* * *

The morning boat came at six, as promised, a flat-bottomed ferry smelling of diesel and dried fish. Armand stood at the rail with his duffel, and I stood on the dock with my hands in my pockets, and we performed the ancient ritual of people who love each other badly: neither hugging nor shaking hands, occupying the exact neutral zone between gestures.

"The hearing review is in three weeks," I said. "In the capital. They'll decide whether to strip your credentials permanently or just suspend them."

"I know."

"If you fight it—if you argue your case, publish your real field notes, show them the parts of the work that were honest—they might reduce it. Brand offered to testify. Ines too. Even Brac, God help us, apparently respects the quality of your surveying."

"I won't fight it."

"Why not?" It came out harder than I meant. "You fought Ferrand for months. You hid a national treasure in a cave. You can spend three weeks defending your own name?"

"Because my name is the thing that got people hurt." He said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "Every hour I spend polishing it is an hour the valley's council spends fighting lawyers for their own land. Let them have the attention. Let them have the funding. I'll write up my notes and send them wherever they ask and stand in the back of whatever room will have me, and I will be *quiet*, Elodie. It's the one skill I never mastered. Consider it my final field project."

The ferry horn sounded. Passengers filed aboard. Somewhere behind us, Señora Vasquez appeared on the boarding house porch with a wrapped parcel—food for the journey, obviously, because attendance was not optional even at departure—and pressed it into Armand's hands with instructions I couldn't hear but could guess involved eating.

He climbed the gangway. Stopped at the top. Turned.

And here is what I want recorded accurately, because I've thought about it every day since: he didn't ask to come with me. He didn't ask for forgiveness, or money, or a letter of introduction, or even a promise to write. He asked for nothing. He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly, more like a salute rendered by a man who no longer trusted his own rank—and held it there for three seconds, and then he went through the cabin door and was gone.

I stood on the dock until the ferry was a smudge, then a rumor, then nothing.

* * *

Tomas found me an hour later, shoulder still strapped, moving with the careful economy of a man whose body had sent him a strongly worded letter. He sat down beside me on the dock without asking, which was how Tomas did everything.

"He's gone, then."

"Gone."

"You let him go." It wasn't an accusation. With Tomas it was never quite an accusation—it was arithmetic, delivered flat.

"He wouldn't stay. And I wasn't going to beg a man to sit in my shadow while he figures out how to live without his reputation. He needs to do that alone, or it won't take."

"Hm." Tomas watched the water. "When I owed your father, I stayed anyway. Worked for him two seasons I hated, because debt is debt. You know what I learned?" He didn't wait for me to guess, which was also how Tomas did everything. "Debt and love look identical from the outside. Only the inside knows which one is keeping you there."

"And which one kept you on that river?"

"Both." He said it without embarrassment, which took practice. "That's the trick nobody tells you. It's allowed to be both."

I laughed—actually laughed, for the first time since the cache burned, a short ugly sound that surprised us both. Tomas permitted himself half a smile, which from Tomas was a standing ovation.

"So," he said. "The museum called while you were staring at the water like a widow. They want a meeting. Something about a joint stewardship survey, upstream sites, a proper team." He paused. "They asked for you by name. Said you were recommended."

"Recommended by whom?"

"By everybody, apparently. The elders. The hunter. Even the council lawyer, who I'm told does not recommend anyone, ever, on principle." He shrugged his good shoulder. "They want transparency. Real-time publication. Full documentation, local partnership, the whole reform program. Sounds miserable. Sounds like nothing ever gets looted or hoarded or lied about." Another pause, longer. "Sounds like a dig where nobody has to hide anything from anybody."

I looked at the river. The same river that had tried to kill us, that had carried my father away, that had drowned our dead and fed our living and kept score the entire way without once showing its ledger. The flood season was ending. In a few weeks the water would drop and the banks would emerge, muddy and rearranged, everything the river had moved now lying somewhere new, waiting to be read.

"Tomas," I said, "how do you feel about going back upriver?"

"I feel like a man with one working shoulder and a deep spiritual objection to rapids." He stood, wincing, and extended his good hand anyway. "Which means yes. Obviously. Somebody has to keep you from drowning."

* * *

That evening I wrote the first letter of my adult life that I didn't send. It was addressed to my father, care of the capital review board, and it said:

*The museum wants a survey team for the upper valley. Joint stewardship, full disclosure, everything published as it's found. I'm leading it. I need a surveyor who reads terrain like scripture and has already made every mistake there is, so he can teach the rest of us which ones not to repeat.*

*I'm not forgiving you yet. I want to be clear about that, because you'd smell a lie through the envelope. Twenty years doesn't dissolve in six weeks, and what you did to Ines, to Brand, to the truth itself—that bill is still open, and I intend to keep it open until it's paid honestly.*

*But the position pays nothing, the conditions are terrible, and the company will include a botanist who argues, a surveyor who trusts no one, and a boatman who resents you slightly less than he used to. If you come, you come as crew. Not as Delacroix. As the man who carries the transit and shuts up at camp meetings.*

*The boat leaves on the first of the month. Attendance, I'm told, is not optional—but it is. Entirely. Your choice, Father. First one I've ever given you.*

*—E.*

I read it twice. Then I folded it, sealed it, and set it on the table by the door, where it sat all night like a door of its own—unlocked, unopened, waiting to see whether anyone would walk through.

Outside, the river kept moving. It always does. That's the first thing it teaches you and the last thing it lets you forget: nothing stays put. Not water, not truth, not fathers.

Not, if I was honest with myself, sons either.

In the morning, I mailed the letter—and started packing for a place that, this time, might actually want us.
