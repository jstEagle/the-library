## 37. The Long Paddle Home

Nobody tells you that the trip home is longer than the trip out.

It isn't, of course—not in kilometers. The river does the arithmetic the same in both directions. But going up, every stroke was a question: *What happened to them? Are they alive? What's under the falls?* And questions are fuel. They burn hot and they burn fast and they make you forget your shoulders exist.

Coming home, there are no questions left. There are only answers, and answers weigh more than you'd think. We carried them in the boats like ballast—Elodie with his photographs wrapped in oilcloth, Brand with his survey instruments he kept touching like a man checking his own pulse, the hunter Kaelo sitting in the bow of Tomas's old boat as if he'd been born into it, which maybe he had. And me, Ines Okafor, botanist, keeper of pressed specimens that no longer existed, pressing instead the memory of everyone who wasn't in these boats anymore.

Four boats went up this river in March. Two came back in June. I did that math every morning on the water, and every morning it came out wrong no matter how carefully I counted.

* * *

The first day downriver, nobody spoke above a murmur. The flood had changed the channel so thoroughly that even Kaelo navigated by guesswork and prayer, poling through water where trees had stood. We passed a grove of kapok standing waist-deep, their buttress roots fanned out like the ribs of drowned cathedrals. A cattle egret rode one of them, absurdly white, absurdly calm, and I remember thinking: *that bird has the right idea.* Sit still. Let the water carry what it carries. Don't try to hold the world to its old shape.

I am a scientist. Holding the world to its old shape is supposed to be my whole profession. But three months in this valley had taught me that the world here keeps its own ledger, and it writes in water.

Elodie sat in the stern of our boat and didn't look at the kapoks at all. He looked at his hands. He'd been looking at his hands for two days, since the hearing on the dock, since his father was led away under escort with his falsified logs in an evidence box, since Elodie signed the papers that transferred everything—the coordinates, the photographs, the documents proving the valley had never been ceded—to the museum and the stewardship council. He had done the right thing. Everyone said so. The magistrate said so. Kaelo's elders said so, in their careful formal way. Even Brac, testifying from behind a table with his wrists cuffed to it, had said so, in the flat voice of a man reading someone else's eulogy.

But doing the right thing against your father has a specific flavor, and I recognized it, because I'd done it once myself against my mother, years ago, over something smaller and unforgivable in its own way. It tastes like copper. It tastes like being right and wishing with your whole body that you were wrong.

"You're staring," Elodie said, without looking up.

"I'm a botanist. Staring is my profession."

"At my hands."

"They're very interesting hands. They've been holding a pen for eleven days straight."

That got half a breath out of him—not quite a laugh, but the fossil record of one. I counted it as progress. In the weeks since the cache burned and Tomas took the bullet meant for him, Elodie had been running on some fuel I couldn't identify. Duty, probably. The same reluctant duty that had dragged him upriver in the first place. But fuel runs out, and I could hear it sputtering in him now, in the silences between his sentences, in the way he checked the oilcloth bundle every twenty minutes as if the photographs might develop legs.

Brand, in the second boat, was arguing with Kaelo about the channel.

I want to be clear about Lukas Brand: the man spent nineteen days alone in a tree at Camp Two, eating grubs and rationing his own sanity, and when we found him he tried to shoot Tomas because he was certain we were all compromised. That was then. Now he sat in broad daylight correcting a man who had lived on this river his entire life, using a chart that the flood had rendered into abstract art.

"The channel bends east here," Brand insisted. "According to the survey—"

"According to the survey," Kaelo said, "there is land where we are currently floating."

Brand opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the water lapping over what his instruments insisted was dry ground, and I watched something in him surrender—not to Kaelo, exactly, but to the larger truth that had been chasing him since the tree: that maps are promises the earth is not obliged to keep.

"Fine," he said. "East-ish."

"There is no east-ish," Kaelo said gravely. "There is only the river's opinion."

And that—God help us—that was the laugh. All six of us, scattered across two boats, laughing for the first time since the ambush, since the fire, since the numbers started. It wasn't much of a laugh. It cracked and wobbled and Brand snorted in a way that would have mortified him in any capital city. But it rose off the water and joined itself together, boat to boat, and for about ten seconds we were not survivors. We were just people on a river, laughing at a joke about maps.

Then it passed, the way weather passes, and we paddled on, and I noticed that Elodie had looked up from his hands after all, and that he was watching all of us with an expression I can only describe as inventory. Counting us. Doing his own arithmetic. Four boats up, two boats back, minus one who stayed behind as decoy and one who—

No. I'll get to that. Not yet.

* * *

We made camp that night on a sandbar that hadn't existed in March, a long pale tongue of new land with driftwood stacked along its spine like the bones of some patient animal. Kaelo built the fire. Brand filtered water and complained about sediment with the contentment of a man who had earned the right to complain about anything at all. Elodie spread the oilcloth on a tarp and, for the hundredth time, checked the photographs.

I sat down next to him. Not because he wanted company—I don't think he wanted company—but because wanting things is a luxury and I've learned to work without it.

"How many?" I asked.

He knew what I meant. "Two hundred and fourteen exposures. Forty-one usable. Eleven critical." He tapped a stack. "These are the ones that matter. The boundary stones. The treaty document. The—" His voice caught on the word, just slightly. "The genealogy tablets. Proof that the valley's peoples held title before anyone arrived to take it from them."

"And now it belongs to them again."

"Now it belongs to them again." He said it like a man reciting a creed he believed in and hadn't yet learned to live inside. Then, quieter: "My father found those tablets first. He understood what they were. And he put them in a crate."

"He put them in a crate to keep them from Ferrand."

"He put them in a crate." Elodie's jaw worked. "Ines, I've had eleven days to make peace with him and I can't get past the crate. Everything else—the hiding, the lies, the sealed door—I can almost see it. But he let her believe she owned it. He let her send armed men upriver. Tomas took a bullet because my father loved a secret more than he loved—"

He stopped. I waited. The fire popped. Down the sandbar, Brand had fallen asleep mid-complaint, which was the most honest thing I'd ever seen him do.

"More than he loved the truth?" I offered.

"More than he loved *people*." Elodie stared into the flames. "You were there. You saw what the secrecy cost. Mbaye. Osei. The whole first crew that walked into the green dark and didn't walk out. If he'd published from the falls—if he'd done what I did on the radio—Ferrand would have had nothing to chase. Nobody dies for a secret that belongs to everybody."

"That's not entirely true," I said. "People die for public truths too. Ask any suffragist. Ask anyone who ever printed the wrong newspaper." I pulled my knees up. "But you're right that they die slower, and they die in daylight, and other people can see why. That's worth something. Maybe that's the whole thing that's worth something."

He looked at me sideways. "When did you get wise?"

"In a tree, mostly. You'd be amazed what nineteen days of grubs will teach you about priorities." I nudged the photograph stack with one finger. "For what it's worth—I don't think your father loved the secret more than people. I think he thought the secret *was* the people. The valley, the tablets, the proof—he thought protecting it was protecting them. He was wrong about the method. He may not have been wrong about the love."

Elodie was quiet for a long time. The river talked to itself out in the dark, the way rivers do, rehearsing arguments it never finishes.

"He asked me for nothing," Elodie finally said. "At the hearing. When they took him away. He looked at me and he asked for nothing. Do you know what that costs a man like Armand Delacroix? Asking for nothing?"

"It's the most expensive thing he's ever given you," I said.

* * *

The third day, we entered the drowned forest.

Kaelo had warned us about it at the delta camp: when the flood comes early, the river leaves its bed and wanders through the forest like a drunk walking home, and the only way through is to follow the high ground that isn't on any chart, the ridges and termite mounds and old levees that Kaelo's people have memorized the way scholars memorize scripture. So we paddled in silence among the tops of trees, our hulls whispering through green shade, past fence posts with a hand's width showing, past a cattle skull wedged in a fork of branches forty feet above the waterline—a record of some previous flood, some previous grief, hung up by the current like a warning or a memorial or both.

It was beautiful. I want to say that honestly, because everything else about it was terrible. The silence was total except for our paddles and the occasional soft collapse of a saturated branch somewhere in the interior. Light came down through the canopy in coins. An orchid I didn't recognize bloomed on a submerged trunk, white and obscene and perfect, and I caught myself reaching for a collecting bag I no longer had, and laughed at myself, alone, silently, the way you laugh at a ghost limb.

Brand broke the silence first, because of course he did.

"We should talk about the names."

Nobody answered. He persisted, because persistence was the only muscle he had left unatrophied.

"For the markers. When we get downriver. Someone should decide the order, and the wording, and—" His voice did something complicated. "Mbaye should be first. He was first."

"Mbaye was first," Elodie agreed quietly.

"And Osei."

"And Osei."

"And the two boatmen from the first crew whose names I never learned." Brand's jaw tightened. "Which I intend to correct. I've been writing to the institute. There are personnel files. I'll have their full names before we reach the mission outpost, and their families' addresses, and I'll write to every one of them personally, and if the institute stonewalls me I'll—" He ran out of threat and stood there in the middle of the sentence, breathing.

"I'll help," Elodie said.

"So will I," I said.

Kaelo, poling the lead boat, spoke without turning around. "The families of the boatmen are of the river folk. My mother's cousin married into one of the families. I will take you to them myself. Names spoken on the water reach the dead faster than names written on paper." A pause. "Though paper is also good. Paper travels farther."

So we made a plan, there in the drowned forest, for the dead: markers carved by Tomas if his shoulder healed enough to hold a chisel, names gathered by Brand from the institute's dusty files, addresses written by me in my best field-notebook hand, and Kaelo carrying word upriver to the villages so that no family learned about their dead from a stranger's telegram. It was, I realized, the first expedition business we'd conducted since the hearing that had nothing to do with gold or claims or evidence boxes. Just names. Just the plain administrative labor of grief.

It steadied us. I think grief always steadies people when you give it a task. It's the formless kind that drowns you.

* * *

On the fifth day we reached the stretch of river below the gorge rapids, where the banks widened and the current gentled, and we found Tomas.

He was waiting for us at a fishing camp on the north bank, sitting propped against a log with his arm in a sling he'd clearly improvised himself, thinner than when we'd left him, browner, and grinning like a man who had won a bet with God. His decoy run had worked—had worked beautifully, had cost Brac's men two days and a swamped boat—and then he'd spent three weeks convalescing with a fishing family who fed him smoked catfish and opinions in equal measure.

"You're late," he said, as we grounded the boats.

"You're supposed to be resting," Elodie said.

"I rested for three weeks. I'm bored of it. Also," and here his grin shifted into something older and stranger, "I owe this river a return trip. It carried me up when I was angry and it carried me down when I was bleeding, and I don't leave a debt with a river. Bad luck." He looked at each of us in turn, and his gaze snagged on Elodie and stayed. "Besides. Somebody has to carve the markers. And somebody has to see you home. Your father hired me twenty-two years ago to bring his crews back alive. I failed him twice. I don't intend to fail his son."

"Tomas—"

"Don't." He raised his good hand. "Don't thank me and don't argue. Both are worse than mosquitoes." Then, softer, in the tone of a man setting down something heavy: "The debt's paid, boy. Whatever I owed him. Paid in full, with interest, in blood, on a sandbar at dawn. From today I paddle for you, and for them—" he nodded at the empty spaces in our boats, the spaces we all pretended not to see, "—and for the river. That's all. That's everything. Now give me a paddle before my arm heals wrong from idleness."

So we were seven. One boatman with a healing shoulder who refused to be cargo, one hunter who navigated by memory, one paranoid surveyor turned meticulous correspondent, one novelist-turned-witness clutching two hundred and fourteen photographs, and me.

I keep saying "me" like I'm separate from it. Let me try once, honestly, to say what I was.

I was the one who begged them to go home. Back at the first camp, feverish, half-dead, I grabbed Elodie's sleeve and told him to turn around, that the falls weren't worth it, that the door under the water should stay shut. And he didn't listen—which saved my life, ironically, because the medicine and rest I got on that journey is the only reason I'm writing this. But I've thought about that moment a great deal, paddling home. About how I was right and wrong at once. The door should have been shut—but not hidden. Sealed, but seen. Kept by the people it belonged to, not buried under a rich man's contract and a proud man's pride. Everything that went wrong upriver went wrong because of that one confusion: *shut* mistaken for *hidden*. I'd made the same mistake, begging Elodie to flee. Fleeing was just hiding with better manners.

Coming home, we were doing neither. We were carrying it all out into the light, piece by piece, name by name, photograph by photograph. It hurt. Truth is supposed to hurt; anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. But it hurt the way setting a bone hurts—terrible, and then better, and then strong.

* * *

The last night on the water, we camped within sound of the delta. You could smell it—brackish air, distant woodsmoke, the faint mineral tang of a river meeting the sea and losing the argument. Tomorrow: the mission outpost, the final reports, the burials on the bank. Tonight: fire, rice, smoked fish Tomas had procured through methods he declined to specify.

Brand was asleep. Kaelo was keeping first watch, a still dark shape at the edge of the firelight. Tomas was telling a story about the fishing family's grandmother, who had apparently diagnosed his bullet wound by tasting his soup, and it was a good story, and we were laughing—that second laugh, smaller than the first, easier, the laugh of people remembering how.

Afterward, Elodie sat beside me at the fire's edge and took out his notebook. Not the photograph oilcloth—his actual notebook, the one he'd kept since the lowlands salvage dig, back when his biggest problem was a stubborn barge captain. He wrote for a while. Then he stopped, and looked at the fire, and said:

"Ines. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've asked me stranger."

"When we get downriver. When the reports are filed and the markers are carved and the museum has everything." He turned the pencil over in his fingers. "What do I do? I spent my whole life not being my father. Digs he wouldn't touch, methods he sneered at, a name I barely used. And now I've spent his name, spent his find, spent his reputation to do the right thing, and it turns out—" he huffed, almost a laugh, "—it turns out I'm good at this. At leading. At the ugly arithmetic of who paddles and who rests. And I don't know if that's mine or if it's his, inherited, like the color of my eyes. I don't know who I am when the trail stops telling me who to be."

I considered the question properly, because he deserved that. Out on the black water, something big moved—a tapir, maybe, or a caiman, indifferent either way.

"You're asking the wrong survivor," I said. "I spent nineteen days in a tree learning who I am when nobody's watching, and I'll tell you what I learned: you're whoever shows up. That's the whole answer. Not whoever plans, not whoever intends. Whoever shows up, tomorrow, at the burial ground, with a shovel. Whoever shows up at the next dig, and the one after. Identity isn't inheritance, Elodie. It's attendance."

He was quiet. Then he wrote something in the notebook—one line, short—and closed it.

"What was that?" I asked.

"My attendance record," he said. "Starting today."

The fire settled. Somewhere across the water, in the direction of the delta, a dog barked twice and went quiet, and I felt the whole journey tilt gently toward its ending—the way a canoe tilts when you finally stop fighting the current and lean into the turn.

Tomorrow we would bury our dead. Tomorrow the names would go into hardwood and the letters would go out to strangers who deserved better news than they'd get. Tomorrow Ferrand's lawyers would still be circling the wreckage of her claim like gulls, and Brac's testimony would still be working its way through courts like slow poison, and Armand Delacroix would still be sitting in some detention cell with his brilliant ruined hands folded, waiting for a son who hadn't decided whether to visit.

But tonight there was fire, and rice, and seven of us where four boats had gone out, and the river running easy at last beneath the stars, carrying us home.

I slept without dreaming. For the first time since the falls, I slept without dreaming at all.

* * *

We reached the mission outpost at mid-morning, and the bell was ringing.

Not the call-to-meal bell. The other one—the urgent one, the one I'd heard only once before, the day the river police brought word of trouble downstream. Father Anselme stood in the compound gate, waving us in with both arms, and even at fifty meters I could read his face, and my stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat.

"Thank God," he called, as we grounded. "Thank God you've come. There's a telegram—it came yesterday—we couldn't send anyone upriver, the water's still—" He pressed the paper into Elodie's hands, and I watched my friend read it, and I watched the blood leave his face in a single slow tide.

"What is it?" Brand demanded. "What's happened? Is it the museum? Ferrand?"

Elodie didn't answer. He read it a second time, his lips moving, and then he lowered the paper very carefully, the way you lower something that might go off.

"It's my father," he said. "They've released him. All charges dropped—Brac's testimony, the documents, the whole case collapsed against Ferrand instead." He swallowed. "And he's gone. No one knows where. The telegram says—" his voice cracked, and steadied, and cracked again, "—the telegram says he left a forwarding address. Upstream. At the falls."
