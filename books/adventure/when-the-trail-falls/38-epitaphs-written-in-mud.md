## 38. Epitaphs Written in Mud

The river gave back what it wanted to give and kept the rest. That was the arithmetic of the whole journey, if I'm honest about it: two men we never found, one man we buried twice—once in water, once in earth—and a list of names that Tomas now carried in his head like a debt ledger no one had asked him to keep.

We made camp on the south bank where the floodwater had begun its slow retreat, leaving the ground soft as bread dough and striped with silt lines like the rings of a tree. The hunter—Kwame, though he'd only given us the name three days ago, as if names were rations to be spent carefully—chose the spot. He said the bank faced east, toward the valley, and that the dead should be able to see what they died for.

I didn't argue. I'd stopped arguing with the river country somewhere around the drowned forest, when Ines steered us between the tops of submerged trees for six hours in silence and I understood that this place had opinions about us and we were not going to change them.

* * *

Digging graves in mud after a flood is not like digging graves anywhere else. The shovel goes in easy and comes up heavy, and every spadeful smells like something ancient being turned over—rot and river and the mineral dark of the deep ground. My father would have loved it. That thought arrived uninvited, the way his thoughts always did, and I set it down beside the hole and kept digging.

Four graves. We'd agreed on four.

Dawit, the expedition's cook, who had gone into the water at the cache and never surfaced. The two porters from Brac's boat—the ones who'd laid down their rifles at the mission outpost and then simply walked into the treeline rather than follow orders anymore; Kwame found one of them two days later, and the other was a matter of faith rather than evidence, but Tomas said you don't split that kind of hair at a burial. And the fourth grave was for the man whose name none of us ever learned: the young security guard Brac had shot at the falls for the crime of hesitating. We didn't know if he had family. We didn't know anything about him except that he was maybe twenty-two and that he'd looked at Brac for a long second before moving, and that second had cost him everything.

"He wasn't theirs," Brand had said, when we marked out the plot. "The guard. He was hired local. He might have been from any of these villages."

"Then he's home," Kwame said simply, and that settled it.

Ines organized the shrouds—canvas from the ruined tents, sewn by lamplight with a sailmaker's stitch she claimed her grandmother taught her, though her grandmother had been a schoolteacher in Lagos and Ines admitted as much eventually, laughing, the first laugh any of us had managed since the delta. Grief does strange bookkeeping. It keeps a column for laughter whether you authorize it or not.

My father did not help dig. He sat apart on an overturned crate, his arm bound where the bullet had grazed him at the ambush, watching us work with an expression I couldn't file under any of the old categories—not arrogance, not regret, something newer and more uncomfortable, something like a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

At noon he stood, walked to the edge of the excavation, and picked up a shovel.

Nobody spoke. He dug the last foot of Dawit's grave himself, badly, in the wrong rhythm, and when he finished he leaned on the handle and breathed like a man who'd climbed something. Then he went back to his crate and said nothing, and I decided that counted. I decided that was the whole of what he had to give today, and that refusing it would have cost more than accepting it.

* * *

We buried them in the order they'd died, which is the only fair ordering left to the living.

The service was nobody's tradition and everybody's. Kwame spoke words in his own language, low and rolling, and translated only the last line: "The river took them; the ground will hold them; the valley knows their faces now." Ines read something—she wouldn't tell me what, some poem she'd carried folded in her kit since university, softened to pulp by sweat and rain, held together by tape and stubbornness. Brand, of all people, sang. A thin, cracked, off-key hymn in German that he got through the first verse of before his voice failed him, and we all stood there in the dripping heat while he composed himself and got through the second anyway, because Brand had learned on this trip that finishing things mattered more than doing them well.

Tomas stood at the back with his arm in a sling and said nothing at all, which from Tomas was a eulogy.

When the last grave was filled, the mud already beginning to dry into a pale crust around the edges, he stepped forward with the piece of hardwood he'd been working on for two days.

I'd seen him at it without understanding. Every evening since the outpost, while the rest of us collapsed into our bedrolls, Tomas had sat by the fire with a short length of ironwood salvaged from a flooded snag, turning it, cutting it, burning lines into it with a heated nail. His shoulder was wrecked; the doctors at the mission had told him plainly that he'd never run rapids again, that the joint had been torn too badly, that a man his age either healed around the damage or didn't. He'd listened with the expression of a man being told the weather. Then he'd gone back to the wood.

It was a marker. Four feet of dense black-grained hardwood, planed smooth despite the sling, and burned into its face in careful capitals:

**DAWIT MENGESHA — COOK — HE FED US**
**THE TWO WHO WALKED FREE — NAMES WITH THE RIVER**
**A YOUNG MAN WHO HESITATED — AND WAS RIGHT TO**

Below them, smaller, almost an afterthought and clearly not one:

**THE RIVER FORGIVES NOTHING. WE REMEMBER ANYWAY.**

He drove it into the earth at the head of the graves with three blows of a mallet, each one landing like a period at the end of a sentence.

"You made a mistake," I said quietly, coming to stand beside him. "Third line."

His eyes came around, wary. Tomas and I had achieved, over the course of the journey, a kind of peace built on mutual grievance—he resented my father, I resented my father, and somehow that common enemy had curdled into friendship the way shared misery always does. But he still braced when I approached, old habit.

"The young man," I said. "He didn't hesitate because he was right. He hesitated because he was human. There's a difference."

Tomas considered this for a long moment, looking at the marker, at the mounded graves, at the brown water sliding past with its terrible indifference.

"No," he said finally. "There isn't. Being human *is* being right, in a place like this. Everything else is arithmetic." He spat, river habit. "Brac taught me that word. Arithmetic. He used it about people." He touched the marker's edge with two fingers, the way you'd touch a doorframe you weren't sure you were welcome through. "So I put the opposite of arithmetic in the wood."

* * *

That evening the camp did the thing camps do after burials, which is refuse to sleep.

Ferrand's lawyers had descended on the delta like a weather system and moved on; the hearing on the dock was over, the documents photographed and entered, the stewardship ruling handed down in language so bureaucratic it took Kwame's cousin—a schoolteacher who'd come downriver to meet us—half an hour to render it into terms the villagers could act on. The valley belonged, formally, provisionally, with appeals pending, to the peoples of the valley. The vault would be sealed under their law. Ferrand's claim was dead, or dying, or at minimum bleeding out in a courtroom she'd have to fight in daylight, which was the one arena she'd built her whole operation to avoid.

And my father was ruined. Formally, publicly, with his falsified logs read aloud on a dock while the river rose around his boots.

He sat by the fire now, apart again but less far apart than before, drinking nothing, eating the plate Ines pressed on him because Ines had decided he was going to eat and resistance was futile. The firelight did him no favors. Twenty years of estrangement and I'd catalogued every version of his face—the lecture-hall face, the discovery face, the face from the telegram that summoned me upriver—and tonight I saw a version I hadn't known existed: the face of a man who has been caught and has stopped pretending the catching was unfair.

"Elodie." He said my name the way he used to say it across dinner tables in three countries: as the opening move of an argument.

"Not tonight," I said.

"It's never going to be night enough for you, is it? Some evening twenty years from now, you'll still be—" He stopped himself. Closed his mouth. Looked at the fire. When he spoke again it was quieter, and worse for being quieter. "You did the right thing. At the delta. With the coordinates, the broadcast, all of it. I've had a month to hate it and I can't make the hatred hold. You did the right thing and it cost me everything and I am trying to say thank you and it keeps coming out wrong."

I looked at him. The fire popped. Somewhere across the river a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"That's the closest you've come," I said. "Don't spend it all in one place."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth that on another man's face would have been a smile. On his it registered as seismic activity.

Brand broke the moment, which was probably mercy. He came over waving a page of notes—he was always waving pages of notes now, transformed by the siege at the mission into a man who believed signals could save the world—and announced that the museum's survey team had confirmed receipt of our transmission and would reach the falls within the fortnight, and that the joint stewardship council wanted a representative on site for the sealing ceremony, and that everyone was looking at me.

"Why is everyone looking at me?"

"Because you're the one who gave it away," Brand said, with the tactless precision that had nearly gotten someone killed at Camp Two and now mostly just got things done. "They trust the man who chose truth over his own father. Apparently that's rare enough to be load-bearing."

"I didn't choose truth over my father," I said. "I chose truth over a lie my father was telling. It's different."

"Is it?" Brand asked, genuinely curious, and I had no answer, and he nodded as if I had, and went off to bother Ines.

* * *

Near midnight I walked down to the water alone. This had become a habit I neither endorsed nor resisted: the river at night, running high and slow with the flood's retreat, carrying its cargo of leaves and silt and things I preferred not to identify. I stood at the bank above the four graves and let the dark do its work.

Kwame found me there, which shouldn't have surprised me. He had a way of arriving exactly when solitude was about to curdle into something less healthy.

"In my village," he said, settling onto a root beside me without invitation, "we say the dead are not gone. They are upstream. Everything goes upstream eventually—the water evaporates, becomes cloud, becomes rain on the high forest, comes back down. So grief is just impatience. You are waiting for them to complete the circuit."

"That's either very comforting or very circular."

"Yes," he agreed comfortably. "Both. Most true things are."

We sat with that. The river ran. Behind us, faintly, I could hear Tomas's knife working on a second piece of wood—I'd heard it every night since the burial, and hadn't asked, and wasn't going to. Some projects announce themselves when they're ready.

"The token," Kwame said.

I took it out. I'd carried it since the village that turned its backs, through the rapids and the portage and the green dark and the door under the falls, and I'd meant to return it at the delta and hadn't found the moment, and here it was, warm from my pocket, the carved spiral catching what little light the sky offered.

He turned it over in his fingers without taking it. "My grandmother cut this. Before your father was born. It was given to travelers we trusted and travelers we feared, and the traveler's own hands told us which they were." He closed my fingers back around it. "Keep it until the vault is sealed. Then bring it to the falls yourself. Not sent. Brought. There's a difference."

"There seems to be a lot of that going around."

"It's the season for it," he said gravely, and I laughed, and the laugh echoed off the water and came back sounding like permission.

* * *

Morning came gray and clean, the first morning in weeks that didn't smell of smoke or blood or fear, just river and wet green and woodsmoke from a cookfire that Ines was building because Dawit had taught her his rice and she refused to let the recipe die with him. She stirred and muttered and tasted and adjusted, and when she handed me a bowl she watched my face with the ferocity of a woman defending a thesis.

"Well?"

"It's better than his."

"Liar." She hit me with the spoon. "But eat it anyway."

Tomas was at the graves when I finished eating, standing before the ironwood marker with his good hand resting on top of it. His sling was gone—the mission doctor had grudgingly approved a brace instead, trading support for movement—and he flexed the arm slowly, testing, wincing, testing again.

"They said I'll paddle again," he said, without turning. "Not guide. Not run water. Paddle. Flat water, easy current. A man my age, they said, should be content with that."

"What do you say?"

He was quiet a moment. "I say the river doesn't owe me a career. It owes me nothing. It took what it took." He turned, and there was something in his face I'd never seen there—not the resentment, not the grudging respect, something underneath both, older. "But I've been thinking about what comes after the sealing. After the survey teams and the councils and the lawyers go home. Somebody has to teach the valley's kids the river. The safe channels, the flood signs, where the snags lie. Somebody who owes this water everything and paid most of it back." He looked past me, upriver, toward the falls we'd barely survived and the country beyond that no chart had ever bothered with. "Your dig. The honest one. The one Brand says you're already planning, because he reads your notebook upside down and you write big."

I felt my ears go hot. "It's not—it's just notes. Ideas."

"Notes with supply lists," Tomas said. "And a line item for a boatman." He held out his hand, the bad one, offering the grip like a man laying a card face-up. "Flat water only. And I carve the markers. All of them, from now on. That's my price."

I took the hand. His grip was half what it had been and he squeezed with everything the half could give.

"One condition," I said. "No more epitaphs written in advance. Next time we plan the trip so nobody needs one."

Tomas smiled with exactly half his mouth, the half that had survived everything. "Then you're planning the wrong kind of trip," he said. "But I'm in."

Behind us, up the bank, my father stood at the edge of camp holding a letter—the first mail to reach us, brought down by the schoolteacher's cousin, forwarded through three offices and smelling of every one of them—and he was looking at it the way a man looks at a door he isn't sure opens anymore. He caught me watching. He didn't hide the letter and he didn't wave it. He just stood there, holding it, waiting to find out what kind of man I'd decided to be next.

I wiped my hands on my trousers and started up the bank.
