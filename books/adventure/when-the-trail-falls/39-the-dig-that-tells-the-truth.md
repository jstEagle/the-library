## 39. The Dig That Tells the Truth

The dry season came back the way it always does—slowly, then all at once, as if the river had been holding its breath and finally decided to exhale. By the time we broke ground at the falls, the water had dropped enough to show the old landing stones again, and the green wall of the forest had pulled back a few meters from the bank like a crowd giving a performer room.

I stood on those stones with a clipboard I didn't trust and a crew that I did, and I thought: my father would hate this.

Not the dig itself. Armand Delacroix loved dirt the way other men love music. He'd have knelt in the first test pit and been home inside ten minutes, hands black to the wrist, happier than he'd been in twenty years. What he'd hate was everything around the dirt—the scaffolding of witnesses we'd built over it.

Because this was the deal, and I'd helped write it, and every clause of it was designed to make secrecy impossible.

* * *

It had taken four months to get here. Four months of hearings, most of them held in rooms with bad air conditioning and worse coffee, while lawyers for Ferrand's consortium tried to argue that a valley could belong to a paper corporation because a paper said so a hundred and fifty years ago. The documents from the vault ended that argument faster than anyone expected. You can't out-lawyer a deed signed by nobody, witnessed by everybody, older than the country doing the claiming. The tribunal—three judges, two anthropologists, and one elderly hydrologist who kept falling asleep until someone mentioned water rights, at which point he woke like a man electrocuted—awarded stewardship to the confederation of valley peoples, with the national museum holding custodial rights to the artifacts themselves.

Which meant, in practice, that when we dug, we dug under two flags. The hunter—Kaelen, though it took me weeks to learn that was even half his name—sat on our steering committee alongside museum appointees and, in a gesture nobody planned but everyone defended, me.

"Why you?" a journalist asked him once, in front of everyone.

Kaelen considered the question with the patience of a man who has watched rivers make decisions for centuries. "Because he gave back what he found," he said. "We want to watch what a man does the second time."

No pressure, then.

* * *

The rules of the excavation were painted on a board at the trailhead in three languages, and I'd written them myself, which meant I'd had to argue myself down twice:

**Everything found is published within seventy-two hours.**
**Every visitor is welcome. Every record is public.**
**Nothing leaves the valley without the council's seal.**
**No exceptions, including for the director.**

Ines Okafor had laughed herself sick at that last line. "You wrote yourself into the corner," she said, delighted. "Deliberately."

"Somebody had to be the example," I said. "Better me than Brand."

Lukas Brand had come back too, which surprised everyone including Lukas. The man who'd spent three weeks in a tree shooting at shadows now ran our site security—and our site communications—with a zeal that bordered on liturgical. He'd built a radio mast from salvaged pipe, rigged a solar array that looked like a sculpture gone wrong, and established a broadcast schedule so rigid that the river police used *our* transmissions to set their watches. Every evening at six, he read the day's findings aloud on an open frequency, plain language, no code, while Ines transcribed for the written log that went out on the weekly mail boat.

"Anyone listening?" I asked him the first week.

"Everyone," Brand said, with the satisfaction of a man who'd learned paranoia the hard way and now prescribed it like a vaccine. "Fishermen. Mission stations. Probably Ferrand's lawyers. Good. Let them hear a shovel go into the ground and nothing terrible happen."

That was the thesis of the whole enterprise, really. Nothing terrible happens in daylight. It had cost us Tomas's blood and Kaelen's burned boats and a year of all our lives to learn it, and now we were going to say it out loud, every evening, at six o'clock sharp, until the lesson stuck.

* * *

The site itself was a terrace above the falls, maybe forty meters by sixty, where the forest floor hid a scatter of worked stone and the ghost-lines of postholes. The vault door behind the waterfall stayed sealed—that was the council's law, not ours, and I'd have defended it with my body if it came to that—but the terrace above told its own story, patiently, in centimeters.

And here is the thing I wasn't prepared for, the thing I'd have mocked a year ago if someone had told me: publishing in real time made the work *better*.

I'd grown up believing that science was a fortress. My father taught me that. You dig in silence, you guard your notes, you publish when *you're* ready, in *your* words, and heaven help the colleague who scoops you. Armand ran his expeditions like sieges conducted inward. And look where it got him—a falsified ledger, a sealed door, a financier with a gun, and a son who didn't speak to him for two decades.

Our open pit, by contrast, hummed. Fishermen came up from the village to watch and stayed to point out things—"that stone is not from here," one boy said, dead right about a piece of greenstone that had traveled three hundred kilometers somehow. Elders came and sat with Ines, and half of what she published about the terrace's use came from their accounts cross-checked against her pollen cores. A schoolteacher from the mission brought eleven students who asked questions better than the journalists did. When we hit the burial layer—careful, careful, brushes and bamboo splints and Ines holding her breath—the whole valley knew within a day, and instead of trespassers we got a delegation asking how *they* should honor it.

"We were going to ask you that," I admitted.

"You were going to guess," the eldest said, not unkindly. "Guessing is how your people dug up half the world."

Seventy-two hours later, the burial was published in full: photographs, drawings, the council's own account beside mine, disagreements included. Two scholars in the capital wrote in—one to correct a date, one to accuse us of sensationalism—and we printed both letters in the log, unedited, because the board was not decorative.

My father, I knew, was reading the logs. The mail boat carried copies downriver, and Brand's evening broadcasts carried summaries farther than that. I never asked how I knew. Some things you know the way you know weather coming.

* * *

Tomas Reyes arrived in the second month, walking up the landing stones with his arm healed crooked and a duffel bag over the good shoulder, and announced to no one in particular that the decoy life was boring and he wanted his real job back.

His real job, it turned out, was arguing with me about boats.

"You cannot land there." He pointed his chin at the gravel bar I'd marked on the site plan as our supply landing. "Flood season, that bar moves. It moved in my grandfather's time, it moved last year, it will move while you are standing on it, because the river hates a fixed idea."

"The survey says—"

"The survey was done by a man who visited in October." Tomas spat with great precision off the edge of the terrace. "The river has twelve personalities. Your survey met one."

He was right, of course. We moved the landing, and in November the original bar vanished entirely, and I bought him a beer at the mission and told him he was insufferable, and he said, "Yes. It is why your father kept hiring me," and neither of us said anything else for a while, because between us sat the whole shape of that old debt—what Armand owed him, what Tomas had paid at the cache, the bullet meant for me—and we had finally gotten to the place where the debt was just a story they told, not a weight either man carried.

"How is he?" Tomas asked eventually. Quietly. Not looking at me.

"He writes," I said. "Long letters. He's teaching at the university still, technically, though half his colleagues won't share a corridor with him. He's writing a monograph on the vault—about the sealing of it, the ethics of it. He wants to call it *What Should Stay*. The publisher wants to call it something with 'Treasure' in the title. They're fighting."

Tomas snorted. "Your father, fighting a publisher. There is a war with no survivors."

"And you? Staying through flood season?"

"I am staying forever, I think." He said it lightly, but his eyes went downriver, toward the stretch of water where he'd taken the bullet, where Brac's men had burned the village boats, where the river had run red-brown with silt and worse. "A man should live near the thing that nearly killed him. Otherwise it follows him."

* * *

Brac himself was in the capital, testifying. That story had unfolded the way slow machinery unfolds—cog catching cog, each revelation grinding the last one flat. He'd turned state's evidence the week after the delta, given the prosecutors Ferrand's entire operational history in exchange for immunity and, I suspected, for the simple relief of putting the arithmetic down. His testimony had gutted the consortium. Ferrand's trial was scheduled for the spring; her lawyers had stopped returning calls; the last I'd heard, she was selling assets to fund a defense that everyone, including possibly her, knew was theater.

Brand read the wire reports aloud at dinner sometimes, in the tone of a man checking a fever chart. "She's appealing the seizure of the second boat." Pause. "Her counsel claims the artifacts' discovery was 'prematurely disclosed.'" Longer pause. "Elodie, she's arguing *you* broke confidentiality."

"She can argue the moon is cheese," I said. "The logs are public. That was the point."

"Yes, well." Brand folded the paper with unnecessary crispness. "I spent three weeks in a tree because of confidentiality. Forgive me if I enjoy watching it die slowly."

* * *

The find of the season came in the sixth week, and it came—as the important ones do—not from the pit but from the margins.

A girl from the mission school, maybe fourteen, named Beatriz, had taken to sketching our finds for practice, sitting at the pit edge with a pencil stub and terrifying concentration. One afternoon she tugged my sleeve and showed me her drawing of a sherd motif we'd catalogued as "geometric, indeterminate."

"That's not a pattern," she said. "That's the falls. Look—there's the curtain, there's the door, there's the steps. It's a map."

I looked. She was right. The "indeterminate geometry" was a plan view of the vault entrance, rendered in clay, centuries old—which meant the people who'd made it hadn't hidden the door. They'd *recorded* it. Drawn it into their pots, their everyday objects, passed it hand to hand. The secret had never been a secret to them. It had been a fact, owned by everyone, protected by being ordinary.

I sat down hard on the pit edge and laughed until Ines came running with water, thinking I'd caught heat.

"What?" she demanded.

"They never buried it," I said. "They published it. Four hundred years before us. On *pottery*." I wiped my eyes. "We've been so proud of our seventy-two-hour rule, Ines. They had a four-hundred-year rule. It was called *telling your children*."

Ines took the sherd, turned it, and went very still in the way she did when a hypothesis locked into place. "This changes the interpretation," she said. "This changes *everything*. If the motif is widespread—"

"It'll be widespread. Check the earlier collection reports from the lowland surveys. Nobody was looking for it."

She was already writing. By six o'clock, Brand read Beatriz's finding aloud on the open frequency, credited to her by name, and the mission teacher reported later that the girl walked differently for a week.

That, I thought. That is the vocation. Not the fortress. The pot on the everyday shelf, carrying the map for anyone with eyes.

* * *

Armand's letters came with the mail boat, thick envelopes, his handwriting slanting across the pages like a man hurrying downhill. He wrote about the monograph, about the tribunal's aftermath, about small things—the university's ivy dying of blight, a colleague's retirement dinner, the taste of proper coffee after a year of river-boiled leaf. He wrote around the center of things, the way he always had, orbiting the actual subject without landing.

Until the letter that came in the seventh week.

*I have been reading the public logs,* he wrote. *All of them. Brand's broadcasts reach further than you think; a former student plays them for me over the telephone, badly, full of static, and I sit in my office at night listening to a shovel strike stone eight hundred kilometers away.*

*The burial you published in week five—I examined the drawings for three days. I had found that layer in my first season, twenty-two years ago. I covered it back up. I recorded it in a private notebook I have since surrendered to the tribunal, so this is not confession so much as confirmation: I found it, and I hid it, because I was afraid of what opening it would cost me. You opened it in front of the whole valley and it cost you nothing but work.*

*I have spent my life believing that knowledge is dangerous and must be rationed by the responsible. You have spent one season demonstrating that knowledge hoarded is what makes it dangerous. I do not yet know how to believe what you believe. But I am an old man with good evidence, and I am trying.*

*Tell the girl with the pencil that she has done more for the valley in an afternoon than I did in thirty years. Tell Reyes his crooked arm is the truest monument I ever earned. And tell yourself—no. I will tell you myself, when I find the sentence that isn't an excuse wearing a coat.*

*Your father,*

*who is learning to spell your name without flinching*

I read it twice, standing at the pit edge while the light went long and gold across the terrace. Then I folded it into my shirt pocket, where it rode against my ribs for the rest of the day like a second heartbeat, and went back to work.

* * *

At the end of the season, we did something no expedition in this river's history had done, as far as any record showed. We put the finds back.

Not all of them—the museum's custodial collection traveled downriver under council seal, documented to the gram, destined for a gallery whose design the elders had helped shape. But the burial went home to its resting place, reinterred with the council conducting rites I watched from a respectful distance, hat in my hands, understanding perhaps one word in ten and all of it anyway. The terrace was backfilled in measured layers, each one logged, photographed, and published, so that the next diggers—decades hence, or centuries—would find not a wound but a readable page.

On the last evening, Brand broadcast the closing summary at six o'clock. Total finds, total publications, total visitors, zero incidents requiring force. Then he paused, cleared his throat, and added a line I hadn't written into the script:

"This is Lukas Brand, formerly of the trees, signing off from a site where nobody had anything to hide, and it turns out that's enough."

Kaelen, who had appeared at these things only when the spirit moved him, stood at the landing stones afterward, looking at the smoothed earth where the pit had been.

"You will come back," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"When the council asks."

"The council is asking now." He reached into his shirt and drew out the carved token—the one his elder had left in my boat a lifetime ago, the one that matched the marks at the falls, the one he'd taken back at the hearing when stewardship was awarded. He turned it over in his palm. "There is a second terrace. Downstream, under the fig trees. Our own stories say there are doors there too. We could dig them ourselves. Or—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile— "we could dig them the loud way. With the pencil girl and the crooked-armed boatman and the man who reads the numbers out loud every night."

"And me?"

"You," Kaelen said, "are the reason we know the loud way works." He held out the token. "So. Second terrace. Dry season after this one. Bring your father, if he can stand to work where everyone can see him."

I took the token. It was warm from his hand, heavier than wood had any right to feel, and I thought about the letter in my pocket, the sentence he was still hunting for, the man learning to spell my name without flinching.

"He'd come," I said. "He'd hate every minute of the transparency and he'd come, and he'd be the best field archaeologist on the site within a week, and we'd all have to listen to him explain stratigraphy at dinner."

"So that is a yes."

"That's a yes."

Downriver, the first storm clouds of the wet season were stacking up over the far hills, blue-black and patient, and the river below the falls ran high and bright and utterly indifferent to all of us, keeping its own records, publishing nothing, forgiving everything.
