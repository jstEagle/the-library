## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The ledger did not care that it was Sunday.

Vesper Zhou knew this the way she knew the weight of her own hands — intimately, without affection. She sat at the kitchen table of the rented house on Corvane Street with a lamp turned low to save oil, and she added the column again, as if arithmetic might repent overnight and show her mercy. It did not. Four hundred and twelve dollars, six months past due, accruing interest at a rate the bank described in polite language and practiced like surgery. The Sable River Survey Company had folded three years ago, but its debts had survived the collapse the way rats survive a flood: swimming, patient, finding every dry pocket of a person's life.

She wet the pencil stub between her lips and wrote the total at the bottom anyway, small and neat, the way Aldous Reeve had taught her. *A number you're ashamed of should still be written straight,* he used to say. *Crooked figures are how liars sleep at night.*

Aldous Reeve had been missing for four years. His debts, apparently, were more loyal.

Outside, Harrow's Crossing was settling into its evening cough. The town had been born for the railroad and was now dying of it, slowly, the way a man dies of a wound he refuses to look at. The main line had been rerouted south a decade back, and what remained was a spur, a depot with two windows boarded, a grain elevator, and four hundred souls who stayed because leaving cost money they didn't have either. At dusk the rails along the riverbank caught the last light and held it, two bright threads running toward mountains nobody here had business with anymore.

Vesper closed the ledger. Opened it. Closed it again, harder, as if the sound could settle something.

From the other room came the smell of Maren's stew — turnip, mostly, and barley, and a bone she'd wheedled from the butcher by promising him a survey of his fence line, which was the kind of currency their crew dealt in these days. Odd jobs. That was the honest word for it. Fence lines, drainage ditches, a grade assessment for a timber outfit that paid half up front and never the rest. Once they'd been a proper survey crew, eleven strong, with instruments that gleamed and a contract book that filled itself. Now they were five, and Vesper kept the instrument case locked so she wouldn't have to look at the transit's cracked lens, cracked the same winter the money ran out.

She took out the compass instead.

It lived in her breast pocket during the day and under her pillow at night, which Odile Brack had once called morbid and Vesper had once called practical, and neither of them had raised the subject since. It was a surveyor's compass, brass-cased, its needle jeweled and true, and stamped into the lid in Aldous Reeve's own hand — he'd done it with an awl, badly, laughing — were the words *STRAIGHT IS A PROMISE.* He'd left it to her the way he left everything: without a note, without a goodbye, just gone one morning from the camp above Miller's Fork with his bedroll and his field books, leaving his kit behind like a man who intended to come back for it and then didn't.

Four years. No body. No letter. Nothing but the compass and the map.

The map was worse than the compass. It hung behind the loose panel in her closet, and some nights she took it out and spread it on the floor and told herself she was only checking it for damp. It was Aldous's master sheet of the Sable headwaters, drawn in his tight engineer's hand, and it showed things that appeared on no official map anywhere: spurs that had never been surveyed, grades through country marked IMPASSABLE in every government record, and at the top, where the river came down out of the ice, a single line of his handwriting that read *the source lies under the mountain's debt* — which meant nothing, or everything, depending on how many nights you'd spent staring at it.

She had spent enough nights.

"Supper," Maren called from the doorway, and then, seeing the ledger open again, said nothing else, because Maren Oduya had cooked for this crew through three lean winters and had learned that some hungers weren't fed at the table.

* * *

They ate around the stove while rain worked at the roof. Fenlo Marsh, nineteen and built like a question mark, ate the way he did everything — too fast, too loud, narrating between bites about a charge he'd set that afternoon to stump a fence post for the widow Halloran, how he'd judged the powder to the ounce and the stump had gone up "clean as a hymn." Tam Corrigan, sixty-one, riveter, hands like braided rope, listened with his eyes half shut and corrected nothing, though later Vesper saw him slide his last piece of bread onto Fenlo's plate when the boy wasn't looking, an old riveter's trick, feeding the young ones first because the young ones were the future and the old ones were only the past eating supper.

Odile Brack sat across from Vesper and watched her the way she watched weather coming over a ridge.

"You've checked that ledger four times since Tuesday," Odile said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, next to the salt.

"The numbers don't move."

"Numbers never do. That's their whole trick." Odile turned her cup, didn't drink. She was fifty, gray at the temples, foreman of this crew since before it was a crew, and she had a face made for ridge lines — long, weathered, hard to read at distance. "Bank send another letter?"

"They don't send letters anymore. They send notices."

"Notices. Fancy word for a hand reaching into your pocket."

"It's my pocket," Vesper said, and heard how it came out, too quick, too sharp, and Maren's ladle paused against the pot for exactly one second before resuming, and Fenlo stopped chewing, and Tam opened his eyes all the way.

Because that was the thing. The debt was in *her* name — Vesper Zhou, sole signatory, sole guarantor, sole owner of every dollar of it — and she had told them, four years ago, that it was the company's debt, the crew's shared burden, and they had tightened their belts together for it ever since, Fenlo giving up boots, Maren selling her mother's silver, Tam working a season underground at an age no man should go underground. They had carried it as a family carries a load. And every month Vesper signed the notice alone in her room and burned the envelope and said nothing, and the saying-nothing got heavier each time, the way snow gets heavier, until it stops being weather and becomes a burial.

"I'll take the Halloran job tomorrow," she said instead. "And there's talk of a culvert inspection out past Kettle Bend. We'll make November."

"We always make November," Tam said mildly. "It's December I worry about."

Fenlo laughed. Maren served seconds nobody asked for, which was her way of ending conversations. And Odile looked at Vesper a moment longer, that long ridge-line face unreadable, before she let it go — for now, the look said, the way a foreman lets a hairline crack in a rail go, knowing it will either hold or it won't, and both answers are already written somewhere.

* * *

The letter came on Thursday.

Vesper found it wedged in the depot door among the circulars and a funeral notice, and she knew before she touched it, knew from the weight of the paper and the wax and the way her stomach dropped like a dropped instrument. Black wax. A seal pressed with a stylized V crossed by a rail line — the mark of Vanetransport Consolidated, second generation, the house that Cassius Vane had built out of his father's fortune the way a man builds a house on a grave.

She stood in the cold depot doorway a long time before she broke it.

Inside, one page, heavy stock, ink the color of good whiskey:

*Ms. Zhou — You will forgive the directness of an old acquaintance. I find myself in need of the finest survey crew west of the divide, and everyone I ask gives me the same answer, which is your name spoken reluctantly, as men speak of weather they respect. One job. The closed Sable line, from Harrow's Crossing to the river's source. Terms generous. Discretion assured. I am aware of your circumstances and prepared to be thorough about them. Six weeks is all I require. — C.V.*

*P.S. Your mentor's map is famous in certain rooms. I would very much like to see it again.*

She read it twice. Then she walked to the stove in the depot office, where the stationmaster's fire had burned down to orange coals, and she fed the letter to them page by page, holding the corner until the flame climbed her fingers' worth of air and let go. The wax bubbled and went black. The V curled like a living thing and was gone.

She told herself it was finished. She walked home through the rain with her collar up and her breath smoking, past the rusted spur, past the grain elevator, past the rails catching what little light the sky had left, and she told herself it was finished, and it was a lie, and she knew it was a lie by the time she reached her own door, because the letter had done what letters like that do — it hadn't asked her anything. It had only reminded her of everything.

*Thorough about your circumstances.* He knew about the debt. Of course he knew; men like Cassius Vane employed people whose whole trade was knowing what a woman owed and to whom and how close the hand was to her pocket. Which meant the offer wasn't an offer. It was a hook dressed as a rope.

But underneath the anger, quieter and worse, was the other thing. *The source.* Six weeks. The closed line. The route up the Sable that Aldous Reeve had sworn them off forever — Tam remembered the oath, Vesper had seen it in the old man's face whenever the river's name came up, a flinching like an old scar in cold weather. The source where Aldous had gone and from which he had never come back. The source that lay at the end of a line on a hidden map, under the mountain's debt, whatever that meant, and she had spent four years and four hundred and twelve dollars plus interest trying to buy her way toward it and gotten nowhere but poorer.

One job. Six weeks. Enough money to bury the debt whole and have the crew fed through two winters besides.

Enough to follow the river all the way up to the place where her life had come apart, with someone else paying the fare.

Vesper sat on her bed with the compass in her palm and let the needle settle. North, always north, steady as a promise kept. *Straight is a promise.* She thought about crooked figures and how liars sleep, and about Odile's eyes across the supper table, and about the loose panel in the closet, and about how a person can burn a letter and still hear it, word for word, in the dark.

Down the hall, the house creaked. Rain on the roof. Somewhere a dog, and beyond the dog the river, low and constant, talking to itself the way rivers do, going about its long business of wearing down stone.

She put the compass under her pillow and lay down and did not sleep.

In the morning there would be the Halloran job, and the crew, and the ledgers, and the lie she kept telling with her silence. But that night, in the dark, Vesper Zhou admitted the truth to the only witness she trusted: the letter was ash, and the ash was still burning, and sooner or later — a week, a month, a knock at the door — the past was going to stop writing letters and start arriving in person.

It arrived on Saturday, in a gleaming black landau with brass fittings, rolling up Corvane Street through the mud like it owned the mud, and Cassius Vane stepped down from it in front of her rented house, smiling, hat in hand, while her whole crew stood in the yard watching her watch him come.
