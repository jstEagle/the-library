## 2. An Offer With Teeth

Cassius Vane had learned long ago that desperation was a garment best worn underneath. Over it, one buttoned a good coat, shined the boots, and smiled as though the world owed him nothing — because the moment a man looked hungry, the world began circling.

So he sat in the private car he could no longer afford, in the gray light of a fading rail town called Harrow's Bend, and waited for Vesper Zhou to arrive with her ledger-face and her stubborn chin, wearing his best coat like armor over a man coming apart at the seams.

The car smelled of leather and old money. That was the point of it. The upholstery was genuine, the decanter was crystal, the brass fittings had been polished that morning by his own hand because he'd let the last two servants go in spring. Outside the window, Harrow's Bend did what fading towns do: it rusted quietly, patiently, like something settling into its own grave.

She came up the steps without knocking. Of course she didn't knock.

"Vane." Vesper Zhou stood in the doorway with rain on her shoulders and suspicion already settled on her face like it lived there. She was thinner than he remembered from the survey school days, when they'd competed for top marks and she'd won most of them. Her hair was tied back with what looked suspiciously like baling wire. "Your letter said noon. It's noon."

"Punctual as ever." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit. Please."

"I'll stand."

He'd expected that too. He poured two fingers of whiskey anyway — hers as well as his — and set the glass where she'd have to pick it up or pointedly ignore it. She ignored it. He drank his own to have something to do with his hands.

"You burned my first letter," he said.

"It made a satisfying sound."

"And yet here you are."

"Here I am," she agreed, "to tell you no in person, since you seem to have trouble reading ash."

Cassius laughed before he could stop himself. It startled them both. There had been a time — a brief, bright, unbearable time — when Vesper Zhou's contempt had been the only honest thing in his life. Everyone else flattered him because of his name. She'd once told him, in front of the entire graduating class, that his survey maps were "pretty lies drawn by a man who's never been cold." He had hated her for a decade and trusted her more than anyone alive, and he had never once managed to separate the two feelings.

"The Sable line," he said. "The closed route. Upriver to the source."

"There is no closed route. Officially those rails were never laid. Your family paid good money to make sure of it."

"My family paid good money to make sure of a great many things." He set down the glass. Here it was, then. The part where he opened the coat and let her see how thin the man inside had gotten. "Six weeks from today, the land office auctions the Sable headwaters. Mineral rights, water rights, right-of-way — all of it, sold to the highest bidder, and my claim lapses with it. My grandfather's claim. The last thing holding the Vane name above water."

"Then bid at your own auction."

"I don't *have* anything to bid with, Vesper. That's rather the point." He heard his own voice go quiet and hated it, so he pushed through. "There's a clause. An old one, from the original grant. If a licensed surveyor certifies the route from the river mouth to the source — physically walks it, stakes it, files the map — the claim reverts to active status. Full protection. No auction."

"Then hire a licensed surveyor."

"I'm hiring the best one I've ever met, and she's standing in my car telling me no."

Vesper looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her sleeve onto his carpet, and he found himself watching the small dark spots bloom there, one after another, like a countdown.

"Aldous Reeve," she said finally, "swore that valley off. Did you know that? Twenty years ago he walked out of the source country, collapsed his own survey, and told every rail man who'd listen never to follow him up there. Tam Brack was there. He remembers."

"I know what Reeve said."

"Do you know *why*?"

And there it was — the question he'd spent six weeks and most of his remaining dignity trying not to hear. Because he did know why. Or he knew enough. Enough to be afraid of it, which for a Vane amounted to the same thing.

"I know my father spent three years up that river," Cassius said carefully, "and came back with financing no bank would explain, and a tunnel that officially never existed, and a fortune that built everything you're sitting in. And I know that whatever's sealed up at the source has been sealed by Vanes, and that men who ask about it tend to lose their licenses, their pensions, or their teeth." He lifted his glass. "I also know that if I don't reach that valley in six weeks, I lose the name anyway. So I'd rather find out what my father buried than starve outside the grave of it. Does that satisfy you?"

"No," said Vesper. "But it's the most honest thing you've ever said to me, which is almost worse."

She turned to go.

"Vesper." His voice cracked on her name, and he let it. Desperation was a garment worn underneath, but sometimes the coat slipped, and there was no use re-buttoning it now. "I know about the debt."

She stopped. Didn't turn around. But she stopped, and that was worse than turning around, because it meant he'd aimed true.

"Fourteen thousand," he said softly. "Held paper, sold twice, currently sitting in the portfolio of a man named Grubb who breaks fingers for late payments. I know because I bought a look at the ledger. I know because I've spent the last year learning exactly how deep every hole in this territory goes, including yours." He rose, crossed the swaying floor, and stood close enough to see the pulse jump in her throat. "Come up the Sable with me. Certify the route. And when we're done, I buy your paper from Grubb and burn it in front of you. Every cent. You walk away owing nothing to anyone — free, Vesper, actually free — for six weeks of work you're better suited to than any living soul."

For a moment — just a moment — he saw it land. Saw the arithmetic move behind her eyes, the terrible seductive math of a woman who had been carrying weight so long she'd forgotten what her shoulders used to feel like. He watched her want it.

That was the cruelest part, he thought later. He hadn't lied to her. The offer was real. He'd have paid it gladly. For six weeks he could have given her the one thing no one else could, and she would have owed him not money but something far heavier, and some small rotten corner of him had counted on that.

"No," Vesper Zhou said.

Just that. Quiet. Final. She picked up her satchel.

"Your crew is starving," he said to her back. "Fenlo hasn't eaten properly since winter. Maren's boots are held together with tar. Odile sends half her pay to a sister in the sanatorium — yes, I know that too. I'm not offering to buy *you*, Vesper. I'm offering to feed the people you love. There's a difference, and I think you know it, and I think it's eating you alive right now."

She paused in the doorway. Rain behind her, crystal and decay around her, and between them twenty years of rivalry and one debt and a dead man's oath lying across the tracks like a fallen tree.

"Don't follow us, Vane," she said. "Whatever you send up that river, we won't carry it."

Then she was gone, down the steps into the rain, and Cassius Vane stood alone in his beautiful dying car and finished the whiskey she hadn't touched.

* * *

He should have let it end there. A smarter man would have.

But that evening, walking the muddy length of Harrow's Bend's single street toward the land office to file papers he couldn't afford to file, he passed the survey camp at the edge of town — four bedrolls, one cook fire, a mule named something ridiculous — and heard the argument carrying through the cold air.

They'd gathered around the fire. He kept to the shadow of a freight car, an old habit from a childhood spent eavesdropping on servants who spoke freely when they thought no Vanes were listening. Fenlo's voice, young and sharp: *"Fourteen thousand. Fourteen! We eat beans for another decade or we eat for a year, Odile, it's not complicated."*

Maren, low and flat: *"It's Vane money. Nothing bought with Vane money stays bought. Ask anyone whose grandfather worked the Sable."*

And then Odile Brack, foreman's voice, gravel and iron, and Cassius leaned closer against the cold iron because he could barely believe what he was hearing:

*"Vesper said no. I heard her say it. But she didn't speak for the crew, and the crew gets a vote, and I'm calling it."*

A silence. Then Fenlo: *"You'd take it? Behind her back?"*

*"I'd take it in front of her face and dare her to stop me,"* Odile said. *"That woman's carried us three years on pride and short rations while her own debt eats her alive — yes, I know about the debt, boy, I do the books. She'll never say yes to save herself. So we say yes to save each other, and when she's furious, she can be furious fed."* A pause, and when Odile spoke again her voice had gone quieter, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. *"We vote tomorrow. And nobody tells her I talked to Vane's man tonight."*

Cassius stepped back into the dark, heart hammering, and felt something twist in his chest that he refused to name. He had come to Harrow's Bend to buy a surveyor. Instead he'd just heard a family decide to sell a piece of itself to keep from starving — and had learned, in the same breath, that his rival's foreman had already been negotiating with his agent behind her captain's back.

Good, said the polished part of him, the Vane part. The claim lives. The name survives.

And underneath, in the garment worn next to the skin, a colder thought he couldn't shake:

*Aldous Reeve swore them off that valley. Vesper knows why he swore it. And in six weeks, whatever her father buried — whatever my father buried — is going to come out of the ground whether any of us are ready for it or not.*

Up the street, the land office lamp burned late. Down by the river, the Sable ran black and patient toward its source, carrying everything it remembered.

Cassius Vane turned up his collar and went to wire his guide, Silas Crane, with new orders: *They're coming. Be ready. And Crane — watch the one with the compass.*
