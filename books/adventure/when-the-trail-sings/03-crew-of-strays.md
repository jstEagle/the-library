## 3. Crew of Strays

Odile Brack had learned to read a crew the way other people read weather. You watched the smoke lean. You watched who sat where. Tonight the fire at their camp outside Harrow's Bend was burning low and honest, and her people had arranged themselves around it like a jury that hadn't yet decided whether the accused was guilty or merely doomed.

Vesper Zhou sat farthest from the flames, on a fallen tie of creosote-blackened timber, turning her mentor's compass over in her long fingers. She wasn't looking at it. She was looking at nothing, which for Vesper meant she was looking at everything.

Fenlo sat closest to the fire, because Fenlo always did — nineteen years old and built like a question mark, his dynamite kit locked in the wagon behind him with the reverence other boys reserved for sweethearts. Maren had claimed the flat rock by the cook pot, arms crossed over her apron, her maps rolled and stacked beside her like ammunition. And old Tam — Tam with his silver-stubbled jaw and hands like cured leather — sat apart from all of them on an upturned crate, whittling a stick into nothing.

The letter with Cassius Vane's seal lay in the dirt between them all, weighted down with a stone. Odile had retrieved it from the fire herself. Vesper had burned the first one; this second one Odile had pulled out of the stove before it caught, and now it sat there, half-scorched at one corner, its wax seal cracked like a knuckle.

"Well," said Fenlo. "Somebody say something."

"Something," said Maren.

"That's not—"

"You want talk? Fine." Maren leaned forward, and the firelight turned her face to copper. "Cassius Vane doesn't write letters. Cassius Vane sends them. There's a difference. Men who need something write. Men who own things send. That seal is worth more than every wage we've earned in three years combined, and he spent it on us. Ask yourself what a man like that buys with paper."

"He buys surveyors," Fenlo said. "That's the whole point. We're the best crew between here and the coast and everybody knows it, even if nobody pays like they know it."

"We're not the best crew," Tam said mildly, without looking up from his whittling. "We're the cheapest good one. Different animal."

"The Sable line, though." Fenlo got up, couldn't sit still, paced to the edge of the firelight and back. He did that when he wanted something badly — wore a groove in the ground like a caged thing. "Nobody's touched the Sable in twenty years. Closed lines, closed country, and Vane wants *us* to open it. Do you know what a job like that pays? Not the number he put in the letter — I mean what it really pays. Reputation. After this we don't take odd jobs draining culverts in towns that smell like fish. After this we *name our price*."

"After this," Maren said, "we're dead in a gorge somewhere, or we're rich and owned. Those are the two endings I can see."

"You always see the bad one first."

"Somebody has to. It keeps the rest of you alive."

Odile let them go at each other for a while. That was part of reading a crew too — knowing when the argument needed to burn itself down to coals before anyone could build on it. She chewed the end of a cold pipe she wasn't going to light. Her knee ached the way it did before rain, and before trouble, and it had been aching for two days straight.

Her eyes went to Vesper, and found Vesper already watching her. Just for a moment. Then the engineer looked back down at the compass, and Odile felt the ache in her knee sharpen into certainty: Vesper knew something about that letter the rest of them didn't. Had known since it arrived. And whatever it was, she'd folded it up small and swallowed it whole, the way she did with everything that hurt.

*Later,* Odile told herself. *Get the vote done first.*

"Tam," she said. "You've been quiet, and you're never quiet. Say your piece."

The whittling stopped. Tam set the knife across his knee and looked into the fire for a long moment, and when he spoke, his voice had the gravel-and-patience sound of a man laying rail ties one at a time.

"Aldous Reeve stood where I'm standing once," he said. "Not here exactly. But a fire like this, a night like this, and us younger than you are now, Fenlo, so hush and listen. It was after the Sable job. The one that isn't in any of the books." He tapped his temple. "It's in here instead. Reeve came off that mountain looking like a man who'd seen the bottom of a well. Wouldn't eat. Wouldn't sleep right. And then one morning he gathered the whole original crew and he swore us. Swore us proper, hand on the compass there that Vesper's holding. Said no one was ever to follow him up the Sable again. Not for money. Not for him. Not for anything under heaven. And then he walked out of our lives so slow it took him ten years to finish leaving."

"You think a dead man's oath binds us?" Fenlo asked.

"I think Aldous Reeve is the only reason any of us ever ate regular," Tam said. "He took strays. That's what he did. Drunk riveters, runaway girls, boys too strange for the mines. He built this family out of people nobody else would hire, and the one price he ever asked was that we leave one river alone." He picked the knife back up. "I keep my oaths. That's all I've got to say about it."

Silence, except for the fire and the far-off complaint of the river itself, somewhere down in the dark.

It was Maren who broke it, quieter now. "Here's what I know about Vane. My cousin worked the Vane docks, before. Men hurt, men paid off, men disappeared into lawsuits until there was nothing left of them but signatures. The Vanes don't lose money. Other people lose money *to* them. And now suddenly the heir comes crawling to a stray crew with an offer fat enough to choke on, for a route that's been sealed since before Fenlo could walk?" She shook her head. "A man offers you that much, it's because he's more afraid than you are. I want to know what scares a Vane."

"An auction," Odile said.

They all turned. She took the pipe out of her mouth.

"Second letter had more in it than the first. I read what Vesper wouldn't let us read." She nodded at the scorched envelope. "Six weeks. There's a land auction in six weeks, and if Vane can't prove working claim to the source valley before the gavel falls, the Sable headwaters go to the state, and the Vane name goes into the ledger as the family that lost the last thing it had. That's why the hurry. That's why us. He needs bodies on that mountain fast, and he needs them good, and he needs them discreet."

"And the pay?" Fenlo said.

"Honest number. Better than honest."

"There it is." Fenlo threw up his hands. "There it is, sitting right there. Six weeks' work for five years' wages, and we're sitting here quoting scripture at each other. Tam — I love you, old man, but Aldous Reeve left. He left *us*, he left Vesper, he left everything, and he's been gone so long he's probably bones on that mountain. An oath to a ghost is just a habit you're proud of."

"Fenlo—" Maren started.

"No. Let me finish. I'm the youngest here, so I'm the only one who has to live longest with being poor." His voice cracked and he hated it, and pushed through anyway. "Maren, you've got a sister whose letters get thinner every year because medicine costs what it costs. Tam, your hands shake now, and riveting's done for you whether you admit it or not. Odile's knee is shot — don't look surprised, we all hear you on cold mornings. And Vesper—" He stopped. Looked at her. "Vesper won't say what's eating her, but something is, and money fixes most things that eat people."

The fire popped. Nobody moved.

"So vote," Fenlo said, hoarse. "Vote it down if you want. But vote it like it's real, because it is."

Odile stood. Her knee screamed and she ignored it, the way she'd been ignoring it for eleven years. She looked around the circle — at Maren's set jaw, at Tam's bowed head, at Fenlo standing there raw and desperate and right in all the ways that mattered, at Vesper with the compass gone still in her hands.

"One condition," Odile said. "Before anybody votes. We do this job for ourselves. Not for Vane. Whatever he thinks he's buying, he's renting — a crew, six weeks, nothing more. We find what we find, we survey what we survey, and we come home with our own names still ours. Anybody asks us to do a thing that smells wrong, we walk, wages be damned. That's the deal on the table. Take it or leave it."

"And if walking means climbing back out of a gorge?" Maren asked.

"Then we climb."

Maren held her eyes for a long moment. Then, slowly, she uncrossed her arms. "For each other," she said. "Not for him. All right. Aye."

"Aye," said Tam, heavy as a dropped rail. "But I'll say this once and then I'll hold my tongue: if we meet what made Aldous swear that oath, we turn around. Even mid-gorge. Even mid-auction. We turn around."

"Aye," said Fenlo, and the word came out of him like something unstoppered, relief and hunger tangled together.

Four voices. Odile turned last to Vesper, who had risen without seeming to move, the compass closed in her fist.

"Boss," Odile said. "It's your call. Always has been."

Vesper looked at each of them in turn, and Odile watched her do the thing she always did — weigh, measure, calculate load-bearing capacity on human hearts the way she calculated it on trestles. And Odile saw, clear as a signal lamp, the moment Vesper decided *not* to say whatever it was she knew. Saw her swallow it down behind those dark eyes and lock the door.

"The Sable it is," Vesper said quietly. "We leave at first light. Maren, I want your old county surveys — the ones that aren't supposed to exist. Tam, check every fuse and charge twice. Fenlo—" the ghost of a smile, "—try to sleep."

She walked toward her tent. At its edge she stopped, half-turned, firelight catching the edge of her face.

"Thank you," she said. "All of you. For — trusting me."

Then she was gone behind the canvas, and the compass's brass glinted once, last of all, like an eye closing.

Odile stood alone at the fire a while after the others bedded down. She fed it the last of the kindling and thought about the way Vesper had flinched — barely, a hairline crack, invisible unless you'd spent years learning to read her — when Fenlo said *money fixes most things that eat people.*

Something was eating Vesper Zhou. Something with teeth, and Odile would have bet her pension — such as it was — that it had a name, and a figure, and that Cassius Vane knew both.

She banked the coals against the morning and went to her bedroll, and above the black shoulder of the ridge the stars were coming out hard and cold, the same stars that must have hung over Aldous Reeve the night he swore his oath, wherever he was, whatever he'd buried.

First light, then. The Sable.

And God help whichever of them was lying to the rest.
