## 4. Where the Maps Go Quiet

Maren had cooked for men who lied about everything except their appetites, so she knew the difference between a map that was wrong and a map that was hiding something. Vesper Zhou's map did neither. It simply stopped pretending.

They crossed into closed-line territory on a Tuesday, though Maren only knew it because Tam still counted days like a churchman and Fenlo counted them like a debt collector. The boundary wasn't marked. It didn't need to be. One moment they were walking a spur that had carried timber and mail and hope as recently as eleven years ago, and the next the rails under their boots went from neglected to abandoned — rust blooming along the web of the steel in orange ferns so thick you could have mistaken it for moss if you'd never watched a rail die before.

Maren had. She'd grown up in a town where the last train left with her father's trade in its pocket and never sent word back. She knew what dead track looked like, and this track hadn't died. It had been killed.

"Look at the ties," Odile said, crouching, running a thumb along creosote gone gray and split. "Somebody pulled maintenance money out of this line years before the closure order."

"Somebody wanted it forgotten," Vesper said. She walked ahead with her compass open in her palm, and Maren watched her the way she always did — not because she distrusted Vesper, but because watching Vesper navigate was like watching someone read a letter from a person they missed. Her thumb moved over the map's inked lines, some of which ran straight through country that appeared on no official survey at all. Ghost lines. Lines that had been surveyed, graded, even partly ballasted, and then struck from every record the company kept.

"Vesper," Maren said, falling into step beside her. "This stretch here. The Sable Junction cutoff. I've got the 1898 atlas memorized and it isn't in it."

"It's in mine."

"That's my point."

Vesper folded the map without answering, which was an answer of its own, and Maren let it go the way she let most things go — into the ledger she kept behind her eyes, where every debt and every silence got entered in her own cramped hand and balanced later, or not.

The gorge took them by afternoon. The Sable River ran below in white braids, loud enough to make conversation a chore, and the old line clung to the canyon wall on trestle footings that Tam inspected with the grim tenderness of a man checking his own pulse. They camped that night in the mouth of Tunnel Nine, because the tunnel breathed cold air like a sleeping animal and the cold meant airflow, and airflow meant the far portal wasn't sealed. Maren made beans and salt pork and hard biscuits she'd soaked in river water to soften, and nobody complained, because complaining about Maren's cooking was one of the few privileges the crew had never extended to anyone.

It was Fenlo who found the first sign, near midnight, when he went out to check the fuse stores.

"Maren." His voice came through the dark low and flat, and flat was worse than shouting. Flat meant he was scared and managing it. "Maren, come look at this."

She went. By the light of a shuttered lantern she saw what he saw: boot prints in the tunnel dust. Fresh — the edges still crisp, the rain of grit from the ceiling undisturbed inside them. Someone had stood here within the day. Had stood exactly where Fenlo was standing now, looking down the line ahead of them.

And beside the prints, pressed into the dust with deliberate care, a single item: a company survey stake, snapped in half.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. Above them the stars moved through the tunnel's throat, and the river said what rivers say, which is nothing useful.

"Crane," Odile said finally.

"You don't know that," Maren said.

"I know we're three days ahead of Vane's party and somebody's already walked this line looking at our campsites." Odile turned the broken stake over in her hands. "I know Silas Crane asked me at the depot how many days' provisions we were carrying. And I know a man who asks that question isn't asking for his health."

* * *

In the morning they found the cache.

Maren had buried it herself — two crates of flour, the bacon, the medical roll, the spare rope — tucked in a rock scree half a mile past the tunnel, marked by a cairn she'd built to look like natural fall. She'd built a hundred such caches in twenty years of moving crews through country that didn't want them, and she had never once come back to find one touched.

The cairn stood. That was the insult of it. Whoever had done this had lifted the capstones off, taken what they wanted, and rebuilt the pile so carefully that if Maren hadn't known the weight of her own work she might have walked past. But the rocks sat wrong. A cairn has a balance to it, like a well-made loaf, and this one had been reassembled by hands that understood stones but not intention.

She dug anyway, knowing, and knelt there a long time with her hands in the empty hole while the crew gathered around her in a slow, silent arc.

Gone: both flour crates. Gone: the bacon. Gone: the medical roll, which was the part that made Tam swear, quietly and comprehensively, in three languages he claimed not to speak.

Left behind, arranged on top of the scree where the sun would find it: a tin of matches, ruined by water. A note, weighted under a stone, written in a neat clerk's hand:

*Turn back. The river keeps what it's given.*

"Well," Fenlo said. "That's friendly."

"He didn't take the rope," Maren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Or the tools. Or the spare boots. He took the food and the medicine and left us the things that would let us keep going a little longer and suffer doing it."

"That's not robbery," Odile said. "That's strategy."

Vesper had picked up the note. She read it twice, and Maren watched her face do something complicated — not fear, something older than fear, the look of a woman recognizing handwriting the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

"You know this hand," Maren said.

"I know Crane worked the Sable office before it closed." Vesper handed the note back. Her voice was level. Too level; Maren had heard her level voice lie about train schedules. "He'd have seen Reeve's correspondence. This is close to Aldous's style. Close enough that either he learned it from the files, or—" She stopped.

"Or what?" Fenlo asked.

"Or he wants us to think the ghost wrote it." Vesper looked up the canyon, where the river came down out of country no atlas admitted to. "Either way, we're four days from anywhere, our food is gone, and the bridge behind us is the only way back. So the question isn't who did this. The question is what we do before whoever it is does the next thing."

* * *

What they did was arithmetic.

Maren laid it out that night on the flat top of a supply crate, using dried beans as counters, because numbers were honest even when people weren't. Seven mouths. Eleven days of what remained, stretched to fourteen if they cut rations to hunger's edge and ate the mule's grain too, which meant the mule would fail, which meant carrying everything themselves through gorge country where the trail was a rumor.

"Fourteen days to the source," she said. "If the source is where Vesper says it is. If the trail holds. If nothing else goes missing."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Tam asked.

"Then we eat our boots and envy each other's."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst of it — nobody laughed, and Maren realized she'd spent the whole day waiting for Fenlo to crack a joke and give them all permission to be afraid out loud, and he hadn't, and his silence was louder than the river.

It was Odile who broke it. She stood, rolled her shoulders the way she did before heavy work, and said, "Then we move at first light and we stop being a survey crew. From here on we're travelers, and travelers watch each other's backs, their water, and their stores. Nothing gets cached again. Everything we own stays on our bodies or between us at night. And nobody—" she looked around the fire, at each face in turn, and Maren felt the look land on her like a hand on the shoulder— "nobody walks alone. Not to relieve themselves, not to scout, not to sulk. We didn't come up this river to feed it."

"We came up it for Vane's money," Fenlo said.

"We came up it for each other," Odile said. "The money's just how it's counted."

Maren caught Vesper's eye across the fire then, and held it, and asked the question she'd been carrying since the cache: *do you know more than you're saying?* Vesper held the look without flinching, which was either honesty or very good practice, and gave back nothing at all.

* * *

They climbed out of the canyon country over the next two days, off the dead rails and onto a bench trail above the river, and the world changed around them the way weather changes — gradually, then all at once. The pines thickened. The light went green and underwater. And the ghost lines began.

Maren found the first one herself, because finding things was her gift and her curse. A grade cut into the hillside, ballasted with stone that had never rotted, running dead straight into forest so old it had swallowed the right-of-way whole. No record. No atlas entry. Culverts still clear, as if maintained — but by whom, and for what?

"Vesper," she called, and when Vesper came and stood beside her on the grade, Maren put the question plainly. "These lines exist. Graded, built, drained. Somebody spent real money on them and then erased them from every paper in the territory. You've known about them since before Vane's letter. Your map proves it."

"Yes."

"Yes what? Yes you knew?"

"Yes, I knew." Vesper looked down the vanished right-of-way, north, toward the source. "Aldous drew this map. All of it. He told me once that the Sable valley had more miles of track than any ledger admitted, and that the difference between the two numbers was worth killing for. I thought he was being dramatic. Aldous was dramatic."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was being precise." She rolled the map closed. "Maren. Whatever's at the head of this river, Vane's family paid to forget it. And somebody up here is willing to starve us to keep it forgotten. Those are the same secret. I can feel it."

Maren believed her. That was the trouble — she believed her completely, and belief didn't fill a belly or replace a medicine roll, and somewhere behind them, patient as rust, Silas Crane or whatever name he wore was following their smoke.

That night, the last night on the bench trail, Maren took the second watch and saw the light.

It was far upstream, high on the opposite wall of the valley, a pinpoint of flame that burned steady for a minute — too steady for a campfire flicker, too deliberate for chance — and then went out. A signal. An answer to a question she couldn't hear, asked by someone close enough to touch.

She woke Vesper without a word, and Vesper came and stood beside her in the cold and looked at the dark place where the light had been, and said, quietly, in a voice Maren had never heard her use:

"He knows we've passed the cutoff. Which means tomorrow he stops following us."

"Is that good?" Maren asked.

Vesper didn't answer. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, carried on the night air like a promise or a threat, came a sound none of them had heard in three days and none of them had wanted to hear again:

Dynamite. A single charge, deep in the canyon behind them — where the only bridge home stood.
