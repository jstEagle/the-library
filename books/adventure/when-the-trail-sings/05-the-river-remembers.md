## 5. The River Remembers

Fenlo had blown his first charge at eleven years old, crouched behind a slag heap in the Kettleworks district with a fuse he'd cut too short, and the memory of that whistle-and-roar had lived in his chest ever since like a second heartbeat. He was twenty-three now, and he liked to think the powder respected him. Powder always knew who respected it.

The Sable gorge did not respect anybody.

They'd been following the river upstream for four days, and the country kept folding in on itself — walls of black granite rising on either side until the sky was a ragged blue seam overhead and the water below ran green and mean, churning through slots no wider than a wagon. The old rail bed clung to the canyon wall in places, cantilevered out on rotting trestles, and in other places it simply gave up, drowned under scree or swallowed by slides that had come down sometime in the twenty years since the line was closed. That was where Fenlo earned his keep.

"Third slide this week," Odile said, standing beside him at the lip of it. The rockfall had come off the south wall and piled itself forty feet high across the rail bench, house-sized blocks stacked like a drunkard's firewood, with the river backed up behind it into a brown, impatient lake. "You sure about this?"

"I'm never sure," Fenlo said. "That's why I measure twice."

He measured three times, actually. Worked his way down the tumbled face with a hand drill and a sledge, Maren spotting him from above with the rope, and set his charges in the seams the way Aldous Reeve's old field manual taught — low, tight, angled to throw the rock toward the river and not toward the crew. It was good work. It was the only kind of work Fenlo had ever been good at, and for a few hours the gorge fell away and there was just him and the stone and the sweet smell of nitroglycerin leaking faintly from his kit.

Which was when he saw the crack.

It ran along the underside of the overhanging shelf above the slide — a seam of pale quartz maybe sixty feet up, thin as a hair, except it wasn't thin anymore. It was widening. He could hear it, actually hear it, a sound like ice settling on a pond, and every hair on his arms stood up under his shirt.

"MAREN!" he screamed, and threw himself sideways off the rock pile.

He didn't make it far enough.

The shelf let go with a sound like the world's largest door slamming, and Fenlo felt the whole face of the slide shift beneath him, tilting, sliding, carrying him down toward the backed-up water like a raft coming loose from its moorings. He got one arm free and grabbed for anything — a block edge, a root, God, anything — and found nothing but slick granite moving under his boots. Below him the river lake churned. He'd seen what the Sable did to a man it caught. Tam had told the story twice around the fire, about a floater they'd pulled out of these same waters thirty years back, bent at angles a body wasn't built for.

Then a hand closed on his wrist.

Not a gentle hand. A hard one, calloused, locked around his forearm like a shackle, and Fenlo looked up into Vesper Zhou's face above him, braced against a boulder with her heels dug into a seam, her whole body straining, the rope from Maren's belay snapping taut behind her. She hadn't hesitated. He would remember that later, in the dark hours when suspicion came creeping — she hadn't shouted a warning or waited for the rock to settle or done any of the smart things. She'd just come down the slide after him while it was still moving, because that was where he was.

"Hold," she gritted, teeth clenched. "Fenlo. *Hold.*"

He held. The slide groaned, shifted another foot, stopped. For ten heartbeats the two of them hung there, his weight and hers and all that loose stone deciding whether it meant to kill them, and then Odile and Tam were hauling on the rope and the world dragged them both up onto solid ground, and Fenlo lay on his back in the gravel breathing like a landed fish while Vesper knelt beside him and checked his pulse with two fingers, quick and businesslike, as if he were a gauge she was reading.

"You set your charges under an unstable face," she said.

"The crack wasn't there when I drilled." His voice came out small. "I checked."

"I know you checked." Something moved across her face — not softness exactly, more like recognition. "Powder doesn't lie, Fenlo. Mountains do. Come on. Up. We've still got a slide to move."

And she went back to work. That was the thing about Vesper that nobody outside the crew ever understood: she didn't make a ceremony out of anything, not danger, not rescue, not grief. She just put her shoulder against the world and pushed.

* * *

They cleared the slide by midafternoon, using charges Fenlo reset with hands that shook for the first hour and then, stubbornly, stopped shaking. The backed-up water broke through with a roar that echoed up the gorge for a full minute, and Tam took off his hat and held it to his chest until the noise died, which was his way of saying a prayer without anyone having to hear it.

That night they camped on a gravel bar where the canyon widened just enough to build a fire, and Maren produced a stew of salt pork, dried apples, and the last of the decent coffee, which she doled out like a miser. Fenlo sat close to the flames. His wrist, where Vesper had gripped him, wore a bruise going purple in the shape of her fingers, and he kept turning it over in the firelight like a talisman.

"She didn't think," he said to Tam, quietly. "Down on the slide. She just came."

"Vesper don't think much when it comes to her people," Tam said, poking the coals. "Thinks too much when it comes to everything else, if you ask me."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

Tam just looked at him, the way old men look at young ones when they're deciding how much truth a fellow can carry, and then Odile called for first watch assignments and the moment folded itself away.

Fenlo drew middle watch, midnight to two. He was relieved on time, but he couldn't sleep after, so he walked the edge of the camp instead, past the sleeping forms under tarps, past the picketed mules, out toward the rocks at the gravel bar's upstream end where the firelight didn't reach.

That was where he saw Vesper.

She was crouched by the water's edge, and she had a letter in her hands. Not just any letter — Fenlo knew that heavy cream paper, that wax seal, even by starlight. Vane's seal. The same seal that had started all this, back in the dying town before Odile signed them onto the job. Vesper held it over a match, and the flame caught, and the paper curled and blackened, and she watched it burn with an expression Fenlo couldn't read — not relief, not anger. Something hungrier than either.

She burned it all the way down. Then she reached into her coat, and pulled out *another one.*

Two letters. Two seals. And the second one she didn't burn right away — she opened it first, tilted it toward the moonlight, and read it, lips moving slightly, and whatever was written there made her shoulders go stiff as bridge iron. Then she fed it to the flame too, piece by piece, watching each scrap catch and die, and stood there a long moment afterward looking at the river like it might answer a question she hadn't asked out loud.

Fenlo's stomach turned over.

*She's writing to him,* he thought. *Or he's writing to her. Either way — she's talking to Cassius Vane behind our backs, and burning the evidence.*

He thought about the debt. Everybody knew the crew carried debts; that was half of why they took odd jobs in dying towns. But Vesper had fought the Sable contract hardest of anyone, and then she'd gone quiet, and now here she was at midnight reading Vane's handwriting by moonlight. Tam's look by the fire came back to him. *Thinks too much when it comes to everything else.*

The bruise on his wrist throbbed. He looked at it and hated it a little — hated that she'd saved him, because it was harder to suspect a woman whose grip was printed on your skin. But Tam had also told them, back before they voted, that Aldous Reeve had sworn them all off this river forever, and Reeve had been Vesper's mentor, and mentors leave things behind, and debts get paid in strange coin.

He stepped back from the rocks before she could turn, quiet as powder smoke, and walked back to his bedroll, and lay staring up at the seam of stars between the canyon walls.

Tomorrow they'd follow the river deeper into country no map admitted to. Tomorrow Vesper would lead them, the way she always led, straight and certain, and Fenlo would follow, because she'd caught his wrist when the mountain let go and you didn't forget a thing like that.

But tonight, lying awake with the Sable muttering in the dark below him, Fenlo understood something new and cold: the river wasn't the only thing running through this gorge with hidden depths. Somewhere ahead was the source, and somewhere ahead was whatever Vesper Zhou wasn't telling them — and the worst part, the part that sat in his chest like undetonated powder, was that he still trusted her anyway.

Trust, his father used to say, was just a fuse you hadn't lit yet.

Fenlo closed his eyes and listened to the water, and wondered how long the fuse had left.
