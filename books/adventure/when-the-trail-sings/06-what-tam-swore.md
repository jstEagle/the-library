## 6. What Tam Swore

Tam had carried the oath for twenty years the way a man carries a bad knee — quietly, favoring it on cold mornings, never mentioning it unless it gave out.

It gave out now, in the blue hour before dawn, with Odile Brack sitting across the dead fire from him, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. She'd been at it for an hour. Tam knew what that meant. Odile only worked steel when her hands wanted to do something worse.

"You're going to ask me," he said, "so I'll save you the trouble."

She didn't look up. "Ask you what?"

"About Aldous. About why he swore us off the Sable." He fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. "You've been chewing on it since the bridge over Miller's Fork. I can hear your teeth from here."

Odile set the knife down. In the firelight her face was all planes and patience, a face that had been deciding things for other people's good for thirty years. "Fenlo thinks Vesper's selling us out. Maren thinks Crane's working for someone bigger than Vane. And you—" she pointed the whetstone at him like a small gray moon, "—you've been walking like a man following a coffin. So yes, Tam. I'm asking."

Tam looked upstream, where the Sable ran loud and white through the dark, going where they were all going whether they liked it or not.

"Aldous came back," he said. "That's where the story starts. Everybody remembers that he vanished up this river. Nobody remembers that he came back first — before the last time. Before he disappeared for good."

The stick burned through and folded into the coals.

"It was spring, twenty years back. Survey season. Reeve took six of us up the Sable — me, and five men whose names you'd know if their families hadn't spent two decades pretending they were never born. We were mapping the source extension. The closed line. The one that officially never existed." He smiled without any humor in it. "Except it did exist. We laid eleven miles of it ourselves. Good rail, too. I drove every fourth spike with my own arm."

"And then?"

"And then we got to the valley." Tam's voice went flat and careful, the way it did when he talked about collapsed tunnels and other things that had fallen in on themselves. "There's a place where the river comes down out of a wall of granite maybe eight hundred feet high, and the whole valley below it is green in a way that green isn't supposed to be this far north. Aldous stood there looking at it, and I swear on my mother's grave, Odile, the man *wept*. Not sad-weeping. Like a man finding something he'd buried alive and hearing it still breathing."

"He found the tunnel."

"He found the mouth of it. Sealed. Not rockfall-sealed — *sealed*. Dressed stone, packed charge, done by somebody who knew exactly what they were doing. And Aldous knew, because the handwriting on the survey stake beside it was his own."

Odile had gone very still. Even the knife stayed untouched.

"We camped three days while he read those stakes," Tam went on. "He didn't sleep. On the third night he got up, walked out of his tent, and started collapsing our own work behind us. Blew the culverts. Pulled the rails and bent them. Burned the maps — the originals, his own hand, everything. And when we got back to town he stood in the freight office in front of the whole crew and swore us off the Sable. All of us. Individually. By name." Tam's jaw worked. "He made me swear last because I was the one who'd have kept it longest. He said—"

Tam stopped. Twenty years of not saying it, and now it wouldn't come out smooth.

"What did he say?" Odile asked, quiet.

"He said: 'Tam, I'm not asking you to be afraid. I'm asking you to love them enough to keep them off this river.' Meaning the crew. Meaning *us*." He rubbed his scarred knuckles. "And then he turned around and went back up the Sable alone, and that was the last anyone saw of him until Vesper started spending herself broke looking for his bones."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downstream, Fenlo laughed in his sleep, a young man's laugh, easy and unearned, and Tam flinched at it.

"So the tunnel," Odile said slowly, "is sealed by Aldous Reeve himself."

"Sealed by whoever taught him why it had to be sealed. There's a difference, and I've had twenty years to wonder about it." Tam looked at her straight on. "I kept the oath because I loved you all. Not because I was afraid of what's in that valley. Because I was afraid of what Aldous became when he walked out of it — a man who'd burn his own life's work rather than let another soul see it. That's not fear, Odile. That's grief with its teeth in something."

Odile picked the knife back up, but she didn't sharpen it. She just held it, blade flat across her palm, weighing something heavier than steel.

"Vesper doesn't know any of this."

"No. And she can't. Not yet." Tam shook his head. "If I tell her Aldous sealed that tunnel himself, she'll stop sleeping, stop eating, start climbing faster. She'll think her mentor left her a grave to dig. But if Vane gets what he wants up there—" he nodded toward the black gorge, "—then whatever Aldous died protecting stays buried under a Vane signature instead of a Reeve one, and none of it will ever come up again."

"And if we turn back?"

"There is no turning back." Tam said it simply, and watched her understand. "I checked the trestle at Miller's Fork when we crossed. Old growth, sound timber. If it's cut when we come back through—"

* * *

They heard it at midmorning, before they saw it: a sound like the world clearing its throat, rolling down the canyon from behind them.

Every head turned. Fenlo dropped the pack he was repacking. Maren came out of the willows with her shirt sleeves wet to the elbow. Vesper stood motionless on the bank, compass in her open hand, though a compass couldn't tell her anything about a sound coming from the south.

"The cache line," Maren said. "That came from the cache line."

They ran. Not all the way — the trail bent twice through the narrows and cost them twenty minutes they didn't have — but they ran until the trees opened onto the cut where Miller's Fork met the Sable, and there it was.

The trestle was gone.

Not damaged. Not sagging. *Gone* — both spans folded into the water like a broken wing, charred stumps standing at either bank, the creek running brown with splintered timber. Someone had set the charges low, at the footings, and set them well. Tam could read the work the way Maren read maps: patient hands, professional hands, a man who'd taken his time and enjoyed taking it.

Silas Crane's time.

"Fenlo." Vesper's voice came out level, which was how Tam knew how far from level she actually was. "How much powder would that take?"

The boy stared at the ruin, and Tam watched him do the arithmetic and hate the answer. "Footings like those? Two, maybe three charges, tamped proper. That's not a rockfall job. That's a *demolition* job." His voice cracked on the last word. "That's my trade. Somebody's doing my trade better than me, behind us, in the dark."

"Crane doesn't want to slow us down," Maren said. She'd gone pale under her freckles, and her eyes were moving — always moving — counting. "Slowing us down is what cutting the cache was. This is different. This is a door closing."

Nobody said it. Tam said it anyway, because old men get to say the true thing.

"He's shut the way home. Which means one of two things. Either he wants us committed to the source so deep we'll do anything he says to get out—" Tam let it hang, "—or he wants us at that tunnel when whatever happens up there happens. Either way, we walk forward or we starve right here."

Vesper crouched at the bank, studying the wreckage, and Tam saw her hand close around the old brass compass in her pocket — the one Aldous had given her, the one that pointed nowhere useful anymore. When she stood, her face had settled into something Tam recognized from twenty years ago, from a freight office and a broken man swearing oaths.

It was Aldous's face. Determination wearing grief as a coat.

"Then we go forward," she said. "All the way. Whatever's at the source, we look at it with our own eyes and decide for ourselves. No more secrets between us." Her gaze crossed Tam's and held there, one long second, and he felt the oath shift in his chest like a bone setting wrong. "No more secrets. Agreed?"

Murmurs. Fenlo's too fast, Maren's too quiet, Odile's not at all — just a slow nod, and a look at Tam that said *your move, old man.*

Tam agreed. Out loud, with the rest of them, with a liar's steady voice.

And behind them, brown water worried at the footings of a bridge that no longer existed, and ahead the Sable climbed toward its source through country where the maps went quiet, and somewhere in the high silence above the gorge, Tam could have sworn he heard — faint, impossible, twenty years dead — the sound of a single survey stake being driven into stone.

* * *

They made nine miles that afternoon, which was nine miles of penance. The trail left the river and switchbacked up through scree fields where every step rolled, where the mountain shrugged constantly against them, and by the time they made the shelf of flat ground Maren called a camp, nobody had breath left for argument.

Tam took first watch because his body wouldn't have let him sleep anyway. He sat with his back against a boulder, rifle across his knees, watching the moon come up over the eastern ridge and light the glacier at the head of the valley — the source, white and remote as a held breath.

Aldous was up there. Or Aldous's bones were. Or Aldous's secret was, which amounted to the same thing and weighed considerably more.

He heard footsteps on the scree and didn't reach for the rifle, because he knew the weight of them. Odile settled beside him, close enough to share warmth, far enough to leave a man his dignity.

"You lied to her today," she said.

"I kept an oath today."

"Same thing, some days." She pulled her coat tighter. "Twenty years, Tam. You kept it out of love. I believe that. But love that turns into a lock is just a cage with kind bars, and you built one around that girl without her ever seeing the door."

Tam said nothing. Below them the Sable sang over its stones, that endless silver syllable it had been singing since before there were ears to hear it, and Tam thought about what Aldous had told him once, drunk, the only time: *the river doesn't hide things, Tam. It just waits for people brave enough to listen.*

"I'll tell her," he said finally. "Tomorrow. When we're close enough that the truth can't make her turn back — because there's nowhere to turn back *to*." He nodded south, toward the smoke still faintly rising off the ruined trestle. "But I'll tell her before she sees that valley. She deserves to hear it from me, on my knees if it comes to that, before she stands where Aldous stood."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Then she leaned her shoulder against his, briefly, the way she'd done for thirty years when words weren't the tool for the job.

"Better on your feet," she said. "She'll need to see your face."

They sat together while the moon climbed, two old soldiers of the rail keeping watch over a crew of strays, and the glacier at the valley's head caught the light and threw it back down the canyon like something signaling.

Tam didn't sleep. Around midnight, far upstream, past where any campfire should be, he saw a light flicker once against the ice — orange, brief, deliberate. A lantern. Raised, held, lowered.

Like a man who had been waiting twenty years for someone to come, and was letting them know he'd seen them.

Tam's blood went cold, then hot, then cold again. He didn't wake the others. Some news keeps until morning, and some mornings arrive faster than a man is ready for.

But he sat straighter, and he gripped the rifle, and he watched that distant point of dark where the light had been, and he began — for the first time in twenty years — to rehearse the words of a broken oath.

*"Aldous,"* he practiced silently, to the glacier, to the river, to the man who might or might not be alive up there in the singing dark. *"Aldous, I kept them away as long as I could. But they're coming now. And I think maybe — God forgive me — I think maybe they should."*
