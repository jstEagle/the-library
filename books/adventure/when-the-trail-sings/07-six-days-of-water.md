## 7. Six Days of Water

The flour was the first thing to go. Maren opened the third crate at dawn and lifted out a brick of something gray and moving, and she held it up in both hands like a stillborn thing, and nobody said anything for a long time.

Crane had gotten to the cache two days ahead of them. He'd been careful about it — repacked the crates, restacked the stones, even re-tied the tarp with the same knots Vesper used, which was its own kind of message: *I know your hands.* But he'd punched holes in the flour sacks and let the damp do his work, poured lamp oil into the sugar, scattered the dried apples across the canyon floor where the crows and the pack rats had taken their tithe. What remained was salt pork gone white at the edges, a sack of beans, half the coffee, and hardtack enough for six days if they ate like monks.

Six days of water, Odile said, because that's what it came down to. The Sable ran loud and clean beside them the whole way up, mocking them with abundance. A man could drink his fill and still starve with wet boots.

"We turn back," Fenlo said. He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his hands were busy with a fuse he didn't need to be cutting. "Bridge is out behind us. That's not an accident, that's a door closing. Somebody's telling us to keep going."

"Somebody's telling us to die going forward instead of backward," Maren said.

"Same direction either way."

Vesper stood apart, doing arithmetic on the back of her survey pad. Rations by weight, miles by grade, hours of daylight shrinking as autumn leaned into the gorge. The numbers were bad. She'd known they were bad since the cache, and she'd said nothing, because saying nothing had become a habit somewhere along this river and she couldn't remember when it started.

"Vesper." Odile's voice, low. Not asking. Telling her to speak.

"The numbers hold," Vesper said. "If we make twelve miles a day through the narrows, we reach the head of the canyon with food left. If we don't, we don't."

"And if the narrows don't give us twelve miles?"

"Then we eat our boots and keep walking anyway." She closed the pad. "We're four days from the source valley. Crane wants us stopped here. I'd rather disappoint him."

Nobody laughed. But nobody argued either, and on this crew that was a kind of vote.

* * *

The narrows took them the way the river took everything — patiently, without malice, taking anyway. The trail became a shelf of rock no wider than a coffin, the water roaring thirty feet below, close enough that spray iced their sleeves by midmorning. They roped together in pairs. Tam went first, old and deliberate, testing each foothold with his boot before trusting it, muttering to the mountain the way he muttered to rivets, some old litany of respect. Maren carried the maps against her chest. Fenlo carried what was left of the dynamite, which he treated more gently than he'd ever treated anything alive.

They made nine miles the first day. Seven the second. On the third day the canyon pinched shut entirely and they had to climb — four hundred feet of scree and deadfall above the rapids, hauling the packs up hand over hand while the river shouted at them from below, and when they crested the ridge at dusk Vesper looked back and saw, far down the line of their own trail, a thread of smoke that hadn't been there yesterday.

Crane wasn't following them. He was herding them.

She didn't say that either. There was a whole ledger inside her now, entries she kept off the books: the smoke, the cut bridge, the way Fenlo watched her when he thought she wouldn't notice, the letter-shaped absence in her coat pocket where the last one from Vane had been. She told herself she was protecting them. She was beginning to understand that protection and lying had worn grooves so similar in her that she could no longer tell which one her feet were following.

That night they camped in a hollow of rock above the water, and Odile dealt out rations with a scale's precision — three hardtack, a palmful of beans, coffee thin enough to read a map through. Hunger made everyone quiet. Even Fenlo.

It was Tam who broke the silence, the way old men do, sideways, aimed at no one.

"Aldous Reeve used to say a hungry crew tells the truth faster than a fed one." He turned his tin cup slowly. "Said you never really know a person till you've seen what they do when the beans run short."

"That so," Maren said.

"He also said—" Tam stopped. Started again. "He said the worst debts aren't the ones you owe money on."

The fire popped. Vesper felt every face turn toward her without anyone turning, the way a compass needle feels north before it moves.

* * *

She meant to sleep. Instead she found herself standing at the edge of the firelight with her coat in her hands, and then she was sitting down among them, all of them, hollow-eyed and patient, and the words came out of her the way water comes out of a cracked dam — not all at once, but unstoppable once started.

"You should hear it from me before you hear it wrong," she said. "The debt. You've all been wondering why Vane wanted us, why he offered what he offered, and Odile took the job thinking — I know what you've been thinking. That I sold us. That I cut terms with him in some room in Corvane while the rest of you slept."

Odile's jaw tightened. "Haven't you?"

"No." Vesper pulled the folded papers from her coat — not a letter from Vane but her own handwriting, pages of it, years of it. "The debt is mine. All of it. Fourteen hundred dollars owed to men in Corvane who don't forgive and don't forget. And I incurred it looking for Aldous."

The name landed in the fire between them.

"After he vanished — after the Sable was closed and everyone stopped asking questions — I didn't stop. I hired guides. I bought passage on freight runs that got me closer to this river than any map allowed. I paid informants who told me stories and none of them true. Every dollar I borrowed went into that search, and every year the interest grew, and the search went nowhere, and I kept borrowing anyway because the alternative was—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she kept going. "The alternative was admitting he was gone. And I couldn't do that. So I buried us all in debt chasing a ghost, and then Vane showed up holding the one key to the ghost's last door, and I burned his letters because I knew — I *knew* — that if I sat across a table from him long enough, I'd take whatever deal he offered. Not for me. For him. For Aldous. And I'd have traded every one of you to get it."

Silence. The river filled it, roaring its indifference thirty feet below.

Fenlo spoke first, and gently, which was somehow worse than anger. "You burned the letters."

"I burned the letters."

"And the debt collectors. When they come—"

"They've come twice already. That's what the odd jobs were. That's why we never stayed anywhere longer than a season." She looked around the fire, at each of them. "Every town we left too soon, every contract we walked away from — that was me running. You thought we were free spirits. We were fugitives from my own signature."

Maren set down her cup with great care, as though the ground might be offended otherwise. "Fourteen hundred dollars."

"Sixteen, with the interest accrued last month."

Maren closed her eyes.

It was Odile Vesper feared, and Odile who said nothing longest. The foreman stared into the fire with an expression Vesper had seen on her exactly once before — the day a trestle failed under a load Odile had certified, and a car of rails went into the gorge, and no one died only because God was distracted that afternoon. It was the face of a woman recalculating everything she'd built her judgment on.

"You let Brack take the fall," Odile said finally. "In Corvane. You let the crew believe Odile signed us onto Vane's job for the money, and you sat there and said nothing, because saying something meant explaining the debt, and explaining the debt meant explaining Aldous, and explaining Aldous meant admitting you'd spent six years and sixteen hundred dollars failing to find a man who maybe didn't want finding."

"Yes."

Odile nodded slowly. Then she stood, and Vesper braced for it — the fury, the walking out, the end of everything — but Odile only walked to the edge of the hollow and stood looking down at the black water, her back straight as a rail, and when she came back her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You stupid, stubborn woman," she said, and her voice broke on *woman*, and she sat down heavily and put her face in her hands. "Six years. Six years you carried that alone and let us think — I thought I knew you. I thought I knew exactly what kind of person would sign that contract, and I defended you, do you understand? I stood in front of that fire and I vouched for you and the whole time—"

"I know."

"—and the whole time you were the reason we needed vouching for."

"I know."

Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, rough, angry at her own grief. "Aldous Reeve pulled me off a bridge gang in the winter of '89 when I had nowhere to go and less to eat. Him and you both. Everything I have is built on that man, and you spent everything trying to bring him home, and I can't even—" She laughed, a terrible sound. "I can't even manage to stay angry at you. Do you know how infuriating that is?"

"I have some idea."

"Sixteen hundred dollars." Odile shook her head. "We'll owe it together, then. All of us. Because that's what a crew is — it's not people who don't fail each other. It's people who split the weight when they do."

Tam grunted agreement from his corner, deep as bedrock. Maren reached over and took the ration bag, and dealt Vesper three hardtack and a palmful of beans, exactly her share, no more and no less, which was its own forgiveness — the arithmetic unchanged, the family intact.

Fenlo grinned, tired and crooked. "For the record, I still think you're a liar. Just ours, now."

* * *

They walked upstream in the gray of the next morning, thinner and quieter and somehow lighter, the way a bridge is lighter after you've removed the load it was never meant to carry alone. The canyon began to open around them. By noon the walls fell back and they could see the river's whole argument laid out below — white water braiding over stone, hurrying toward the source the way rivers don't, and Vesper stopped on the trail and frowned at it.

Rivers don't hurry uphill. But this one did. The current ran strong against their direction of travel, and yet the water itself seemed eager, almost joyful, as though it knew something waited at its beginning worth reaching.

Tam came up beside her and followed her gaze. "Twenty years ago," he said quietly, "your mentor stood right about there. Looked at that same water. And whatever he saw made him swear us all off this valley forever." He paused. "I never asked him what it was. Maybe I should have."

Ahead, where the canyon walls finally parted, the land rose into mountains they had no map for — peaks that appeared on none of Maren's charts, wearing snow out of season. And cut into the shoulder of the nearest peak, faint but unmistakable in the afternoon light, was a line too straight to be natural.

A tunnel mouth. Sealed. Waiting.

And standing small and dark before it, a figure — watching them come.
