## 8. The Source Valley

The river changed its voice on the sixth morning.

Maren heard it before she saw it — the Sable's constant growl softening into something wider, deeper, almost patient, the way a dog stops barking when it comes home. She stood at the head of the column with her map case hugged to her chest and listened, and around her the crew went quiet too, one by one, as if they'd all been waiting twenty years for that sound without knowing it.

The gorge opened like a held breath let go.

They came out of the narrows single file, boots slick on wet stone, and the valley unfolded ahead of them: a long green throat of a place, walled in by cliffs the color of wet slate, the river running broad and silver through its floor. Mist hung in the bends of it. Wild rye grew waist-high where no surveyor had ever been paid to look. Maren had drawn maps her whole life — coastlines, cuttings, the neat geometry of grades and curves — and she had never once drawn anything like this, because nothing like this was supposed to exist.

"It's not on any chart," Fenlo said behind her, hushed, as if loudness might break it.

"Because every chart was bought," Tam said.

Vesper stopped at the valley mouth and took out the compass. Maren watched her hold it flat on her palm, needle trembling, and watched Vesper's face do something complicated — grief and relief fighting over the same ground. The needle swung once, twice, then settled pointing straight up the valley, toward the source.

"Aldous," Vesper said, so quietly only Maren caught it.

They walked in. The going was easy after the canyon — almost insultingly easy, grass underfoot instead of scree, the river ambling beside them like an escort. But nobody relaxed. Odile kept the crew bunched tight, her eyes working the treeline, and Tam carried his riveting hammer slung across his back like a rifle. Six days of half-rations had hollowed them all out; Maren could feel her own ribs when she breathed, and Fenlo's jokes had run out two days ago and not come back.

The first sign of the camp was a tin plate, green with age, half-buried in the rye.

Then a boot sole, rotted loose from its leather. Then a line of clothesline strung between two alders, still standing, still bearing three wooden clothespins gone gray as driftwood. Maren crouched by a fire ring stacked with stones so precisely fitted they might have been laid by a mason, and felt the hair rise on her arms. The stones were warm.

"Someone's kept this," she said. "Recently."

Odile's hand closed on her shoulder. "Eyes up, then."

The camp sat on a bench of land above the river's north bank: a lean-to roofed with split shakes gone silver, a rack of split fish drying in the shade, a garden plot of turnips and bitter greens weeded with obsessive care. Tools hung on pegs in strict order — a hatchet, a skinning knife, a coil of snares — each one clean, each one oiled. Twenty years of living, arranged like a man expected company any day.

And beyond the camp, maybe four hundred yards upstream, set into the cliff face itself, was the tunnel mouth.

It was sealed. Not collapsed — *sealed*, deliberately, with mortared stone laid course over course, a wall of it fifteen feet high and wide as a barn door, and at its center, set deep in the mortar, an iron plate the size of a dinner plate. Maren squinted at it through the mist. There was lettering on the plate. She started forward to read it—

"Don't."

The voice came from the rocks above the lean-to, cracked and rusted as an old hinge, and every weapon on the crew came up at once.

He stepped down from the stones like something the mountain had grown: a man in clothes made of deerhide and patched canvas, beard to his waist, hair white as the mist, a long-handled pick gripped in both fists. He was gaunt as a fence rail but he moved sure, planting his feet wide, and his eyes — pale, fierce, unbearably familiar — went around the circle of them and stopped on Vesper Zhou.

For a long moment nobody breathed.

"You have her compass," the old man said.

Vesper's hand closed over the compass at her belt. Her voice, when it came, was wrecked and steady at once, the way it had been in the canyon when she'd confessed the debt. "Aldous Reeve gave it to me."

"I know what I gave." He didn't lower the pick. "I asked who carries it now. You're not carrying it the way she did. She held it like a question. You hold it like a bill come due." His gaze sharpened. "Say your name."

"Vesper. Vesper Zhou. You taught me to read a grade when I was fourteen. You said—" her voice caught, "—you said a line is just a promise the ground agrees to keep."

Something moved through the old man's face, weather cracking off a wall. The pick head lowered an inch. "Little Ves," he said. "You got old."

"You got *lost*."

"I got *honest*." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as gravel. "There's a difference, girl, and it cost me everything I had. Come away from the wall. All of you. And don't touch the iron plate. Touch nothing with the Vane mark on it."

* * *

He fed them first. That was the thing Maren couldn't get over, sitting later around his fire eating smoked trout and boiled greens while their host moved among them like a ghost playing housekeeper: he fed them first, because Aldous Reeve had always said you didn't ask a hungry man hard questions. Maren sketched while she ate — quick lines, the valley, the camp, the sealed wall — because drawing was how she thought, and because some part of her already knew this day would need witnesses.

Tam sat closest to him. The old riveter hadn't spoken since the valley mouth; he just stared, and stared, and finally Aldous looked over at him and said, "Hello, Tam. You got gray."

"You told us never to follow you here." Tam's voice shook. "Twenty years. You made me swear on my mother's grave."

"And you kept it." Aldous nodded slowly. "Good. It wasn't ready to be found. Now—" he looked at Vesper, at Odile, at the rawboned hunger in all their faces, "—it may be past ready. Tell me why you've come. And don't lie to me. I've had twenty years of nobody to lie to. I'll smell it on you like smoke."

So Vesper told him. The letter from Cassius Vane. The offer. Odile accepting on the crew's behalf. The sabotage, the cut bridge, six days of water, Crane's shadow dogging them up the whole length of the Sable. She told it plain, no varnish, and when she reached the part about the debt — hers alone, incurred hunting for *him*, for years, borrowing against everything she'd ever earn — Aldous closed his eyes.

"You spent yourself looking for me."

"We thought you were dead."

"I was worse." He opened his eyes. "I was a coward. Sit close, all of you. This is the story I sealed a wall to keep, and if Vane's boy has sent you, then the keeping is done whether I like it or not."

He told it in the flat voice of a man reciting freight manifests, which somehow made it worse.

"The Sable extension was financed in '61. On paper, the money came from eastern investors through the Vane family trust. I was the chief engineer; I signed the surveys; I believed in the route like a man believes in God. But the numbers never added. The payroll was thin, the timber thin, the steel thin — and yet the financing kept coming. So near the end, with the source tunnel half-driven, I did what engineers aren't supposed to do. I went into the books."

Maren's pencil stopped.

"There was no eastern money," Aldous said. "There never was. Edmund Vane stole it. He took the workers' pension fund — the fund every man on that line paid into from his own wage, month after month, to buy their old age — and he laundered it through his family trust and called it investment capital. The line was built on the backs of the men who built it, twice over. And when the audit came within a season of finding it, Edmund Vane did the arithmetic of a thief. A dead tunnel hides a ledger better than any vault. He had the source tunnel charged and collapsed with the company records inside — the real books, the pension rolls, eleven hundred names of men whose futures he'd spent on granite and rails. Eleven hundred names, girl. I copied them. That was my mistake and my salvation both. I copied the rolls before the charges went in, because I loved those men, and Edmund Vane knew I knew."

"He tried to kill you," Odile said.

"He tried to buy me first. Offered me partnership, silence, a pension of my own." Aldous smiled without any warmth in it. "When I refused, he offered the other thing. So I walked out of this valley, swore everyone away from the Sable, and came back alone to stand guard over a wall of stone. Because the evidence isn't buried, children. It's *entombed*. Intact. Dry. Behind that mortar is a chamber with the true ledgers, the pension rolls, correspondence in Edmund Vane's own hand — enough paper to hang his name and restore eleven hundred fortunes. Enough to break the Vanes forever. And enough, if it fell into the wrong hands, to be burned by supper."

Silence, except the river.

Fenlo spoke first, hoarse. "Cassius Vane doesn't want the tunnel open. He wants it *gone*. Before the land auction voids his claim—"

"—because the claim isn't worth anything if the fraud surfaces," Vesper finished. Her face had gone very still. "The auction was never about land. It's a fuse. Six weeks, and the last legal thread tying the Vanes to this valley burns away — unless the truth comes out first."

"And here's young Vane's dilemma," Aldous said softly. "He can't seal what's already sealed without raising questions. So he needs someone with the skill to bring the wall down *quietly* — and the standing to make the collapse look like nature. An engineer. A crew." His pale eyes rested on Vesper. "He didn't hire you to find the inheritance, Little Ves. He hired you to bury it deeper."

Maren saw it land on Vesper like a rockfall — every kindness Vane had shown, every concession, reassembling itself into its true shape. Beside her, Odile had gone gray. Tam's knuckles were white around his hammer haft.

"That's why the sabotage," Maren whispered. "Crane wasn't trying to stop us. He was herding us. Making sure we arrived — desperate, depleted, dependent. Making sure we'd do whatever Vane asked at the end."

"Then he knows we're here," Fenlo said. "Or he will the moment—"

The sound came down the valley like a dropped door: a whistle, sharp and doubled, echoing off the slate cliffs. Then voices. Then, rounding the bend below the camp, moving fast and fanned out like beaters driving game, came armed men — eight of them, rifles slung, and at their head two figures Maren knew by silhouette alone.

Silas Crane, unshaven, unhurried, rifle cradled like a sleeping child.

And behind him, coat immaculate despite six weeks of wilderness, hat in hand as though arriving at church — Cassius Vane.

Aldous Reeve rose from the fire without hurry, took up his long-handled pick, and planted himself between the crew and the tunnel wall, an old man making of his own body the last course of mortar.

"Well," he said. "Company."

Vane stopped ten paces off and put his hat back on, and when he spoke his voice carried the warmth of a bank lobby. "Mr. Reeve. Miss Zhou. Forgive the dramatics — the valley's narrow, and narrow places make men nervous." His eyes flicked to the sealed wall, hungry as a wolf's. "Now. Let's discuss what happens to that stone."
