## 9. The Tunnel and the Truth

The tunnel mouth was a black seam in the valley wall, half-swallowed by twenty years of scree and alder, breathing cold air against Vesper's face like something sleeping. Aldous Reeve stood before it with a rusted pick held the way a priest holds a cross, and behind her she could hear Cassius Vane's boots crunching up the talus slope, unhurried, certain, the way rich men walk when they believe the world has been arranged for their convenience.

"Miss Zhou." Vane's voice carried in the thin mountain air, smooth as river-polished stone. "You've made good time. I did wonder."

She turned. He had come up the valley with Silas Crane at his shoulder and two hired men flanking them, rifles slung with studied casualness. Crane's face was unreadable as slate. Vane himself looked worse than he had in the rail town — the polish worn through at the edges, eyes rimmed red, a man who had not slept well since the auction notice went out.

"You cut our cache," Vesper said. "You cut the bridge behind us. You nearly killed Fenlo in the gorge."

"I bought you time," Vane said. "There's a difference, though I understand it's a subtle one out here." His gaze slid past her to the tunnel, and something moved across his face — hunger, fear, both braided tight. "Is he alive? Reeve?"

"He's alive."

"Then everything can still be mended." Vane stepped closer, hands open, palms out — the gesture of a man showing he held nothing, which was itself a kind of lie. "Listen to me carefully, because I'll only say this once, and I mean every word of it. Your debt. All of it — the notes, the interest, the men who've been circling your crew like wolves since spring. Gone. Erased this afternoon by wire if we reach a telegraph office together. Aldous Reeve found, restored to his senses, his name cleared of whatever madness they pinned on him when he vanished. And the past—" his voice softened, went almost gentle, "—the past forgiven, Vesper. Whatever happened between us, whatever my father did or didn't do, we let it stay under the mountain. We walk out of here clean. All of us."

Behind her, she heard Tam shift his weight on the rocks, heard Maren's breath catch. They knew. Of course they knew now — she'd told them six days ago in the canyon, hollow-eyed and rationing water, that the debt was hers alone, incurred chasing a ghost up a dead line. They knew what Vane was offering, and they knew what it would cost.

Vesper looked at the tunnel mouth. Cold air breathed out of it, carrying the smell of old timber and old lies.

"What's inside, Cassius?" she asked quietly. "Tell me what's inside, and then tell me again why it needs destroying."

His jaw tightened. For a moment the mask slipped entirely, and she saw the son beneath the silk — a boy who had grown up counting another man's money and learning, too young, where it came from.

"Ledgers," he said. "My father's ledgers. The Sable line was financed twice, Vesper. Once on paper, once in truth. The second financing — the workers' pension fund, four hundred men's savings, their widows' money — it never built a foot of rail. It built my family's name. When Reeve found the books, my father collapsed the tunnel with the fund sealed inside and called it a survey accident. Three men died in the blast. Reeve walked out of the valley changed because he *knew*, and no one would listen, and the only proof was buried under a hundred tons of rock." He laughed, short and bitter. "There. You have it. Now help me keep it buried, and everyone walks away whole."

"Whole," Vesper repeated. She thought of Odile's fury in the canyon, gone soft with grief. She thought of Tam keeping an oath for twenty years out of love. She thought of four hundred names in a ledger, men who'd swung hammers until their hands split and been paid in nothing.

"No," she said.

"Vesper—"

"Fenlo," she called, without turning. "The charges. Left drift, high pocket, just like we planned."

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way violence always does — faster than thought, all noise and motion.

Tam moved first, planting himself between Vane and the tunnel like a gate coming down, riveting hammer loose in his grip, and the two hired men hesitated at the sight of him, an old man with white stubble and absolutely no intention of moving. Maren was already at the cache bags, hauling out the ledger satchels they'd packed for exactly this, straps over her shoulders. Crane started forward and stopped, because Fenlo came down the talus with a charge bag in one hand and a lit fuse spool in the other and a look on his young face that said he'd spent six days nearly dying in a gorge and was done being polite about it.

"You'll ruin yourself," Vane said to Vesper, and there was real bewilderment in it, genuine, the bafflement of a man who had offered gold and been refused. "The debt will eat you. It will eat all of them. You're choosing a hole in a mountain over your own people."

"I'm choosing them *over* the mountain," Vesper said. "That's the whole point. You'd know that if you'd ever worked for anything."

Fenlo set the charges high in the rockfall plug with quick, sure hands — three sticks, tamped, wired back to a plunger fifty feet down the slope. Vesper checked his work with a touch, the way Aldous had taught her, feeling for the seated charge like feeling for a pulse.

"Aldous!" she called toward the tunnel mouth. "Stand clear! We're opening it!"

The old man looked at her for a long moment — wild-eyed, gray-bearded, twenty years of guarding a secret carved into his face — and then, slowly, he stepped aside. Stepped *away* from the door he'd guarded for two decades, and sat down on a boulder, and put his face in his hands.

Vesper threw the plunger.

The mountain spoke. The crack of it rolled down the valley and came back off the far walls, doubled and trebled, and the rockfall plug shuddered, sagged, and slumped inward into whatever dark chamber waited beyond. Dust rolled out of the new gap like breath from a opened lung.

"Maren — go!" Vesper shouted. "Straight through, don't stop for anything!"

Maren went, satchels banging, small and fast as a wren disappearing into the dust-dark. Tam backed toward the mouth, hammer raised, holding Vane's men at the edge of the rubble field. And Vesper turned to follow—

—and the mountain, having spoken once, decided to speak again.

The secondary collapse came without warning: the charges had loosened more than the plug, and the arch above the original bore, old timber rotted past saving, let go all at once. The sound was enormous and intimate at once — a sound she felt in her teeth, in her chest — and the world turned to falling stone and blinding dust.

She was running. She felt the mouth of the tunnel ahead of her, daylight a gray smudge through the murk — and then the floor bucked, a slab the size of a wagon came down between her and the light, and the darkness closed like a hand.

* * *

When the dust settled enough to breathe, Vesper was on her knees in absolute blackness, coughing, one ear ringing so hard the other seemed to hear nothing at all. Somewhere close, another man was coughing too.

A match scraped. Light bloomed, and she saw Cassius Vane ten feet away, hatless, bleeding from a gash above his brow, holding a trembling match with the expression of a man who had just discovered the price of following his own greed underground.

"The entrance," he rasped. "Is it—"

"Gone. Behind us and above us both." Vesper got her feet under her, tested her limbs — bruised, whole. Her compass was in her coat. Her lamp was somewhere under forty tons of mountain. "Congratulations, Cassius. You're finally inside your father's secret."

"Don't." The match died. In the dark his voice cracked wide open. "Don't mock me. My God, Vesper, we're buried alive."

"We're not." She struck a match of her own and held it up, turning slowly, reading the chamber the way she read maps — because it *was* a map, every strut and sill and drainage cut a sentence written by men who'd worked this bore before it was murdered. Old timber, Sable-line pattern, spaced four feet. A drainage channel along the left wall, cut deep, dry now but unmistakable. And ahead, where the matchlight barely reached, the glint of wet iron rail.

"There's a ventilation adit," she said. "Every mile of the Sable bore had one — Aldous designed them. Sloped up at twelve degrees to the hillside, big enough to crawl. If the pattern holds, it's eighty yards ahead, left side."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we die in my mentor's tunnel, and you can explain yourself to your father personally."

She started walking, and after a moment, boots scraped behind her — Vane following, because a man who had nothing left would follow even the person who'd ruined him, toward even the smallest light.

They found the ledgers first. Maren hadn't reached them; the collapse had caught her route, and the satchels lay burst open in the dust, oilcloth torn, and pages fanned everywhere like a flock of dead birds. Vesper knelt among them. Columns of figures. Names. *Pension Fund — Sable Workers' Trust.* Four hundred men looking up at her out of the ink.

"Take them," Vane said hoarsely. She looked up. He stood over her, blood drying on his face, and something had gone out of him — or come into him; she couldn't tell which. "All of them. Every page. Take them out and burn my name with them. I'll testify. I'll sign whatever you want. Just get us out of here."

"That's the offer I should have gotten six weeks ago," Vesper said. She gathered the pages with careful hands, squared them, bound them with the strap. "But yes. All right."

The ventilation adit was where the pattern said it would be — a low black slot behind a fall of rubble, breathing faintly, blessedly, outward. Vesper went first on her belly, pulling the ledger bundle behind her on its strap, talking the whole way to keep herself sane and Vane calm behind her: grades and chainages and the names of dead surveyors, the whole litany of the line, while the mountain pressed down close enough to taste.

Then gray light, real light, ahead — widening — and hands grabbing her arms, hauling her out onto the hillside grass. Odile's face above hers, filthy and furious and crying without shame. Fenlo. Tam. And beyond them, Maren sitting dazed with the rest of the rescued pages, and Aldous Reeve standing upright for the first time in twenty years, watching the tunnel mouth with tears cutting channels through the dust on his cheeks.

Last out, coughing, half-crawling, came Cassius Vane — and stopped dead at the sight of the crew arrayed around him, every one of them armed with nothing but their presence.

He looked at Vesper. "The auction," he said quietly. "Six days. The evidence has to reach a court before the land changes hands, or none of it means anything."

Vesper stood, legs shaking, ledger strapped across her back like a child.

"Then we'd better catch the morning freight," she said.
