## 10. When the Trail Sings

Odile Brack had spent thirty years reading rails the way other folk read faces, and the spur line out of the Sable valley sang a different song than any she'd ever heard.

It was a thin, bright note — new spikes in old ties, the crew's own hands having cleared and re-tamped the first three miles themselves while they waited for the world to catch up to what they'd carried out of the mountain. The wheels kept time with it. *Tak-a-tak, tak-a-tak,* and under that, faint as a pulse, the river running free beside them through the gorge, no longer dammed behind Vane's father's fraudulent works, the sluice gates opened by their own crowbars two weeks past.

She sat on the flatcar with her boots hanging over the coupler and watched the country come back to life. Alders where the flood had scoured. Deer tracks in the fresh mud. A hawk riding the thermals above the cut where Fenlo had nearly died, where Vesper had gone over the edge after him without so much as a rope thrown ahead of her.

"You're smiling," Maren said, settling beside her with a tin cup of coffee she'd brewed from the last of the good beans, hoarded for exactly this occasion. "I haven't seen you smile since the Harlow job."

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late." Maren blew steam off her cup. "The whole territory's talking. You know the *Gazette* ran the ledger pages on the front sheet? Full facsimile. Old man Vane's signature right there next to 'pension fund — transferred,' like he was signing for a delivery of nails."

Odile grunted. She'd seen it. Everyone from here to Copperline had seen it. The story had run ahead of them downriver faster than the water itself — the lost tunnel, the buried fund, the eleven hundred rail workers whose wages-for-old-age had been stolen clean out from under them twenty years ago by a man whose name was carved into half the depots in the territory.

Eleven hundred names. And now, eleven hundred settlements being paid out, one by one, by a commission that had arrived at the source valley within nine days of Maren carrying those ledger books toward daylight.

"Vane," Odile said. "You hear anything?"

"He left." Maren turned her cup slowly. "Took the morning coach out of Sable Junction three days back. No escort, no luggage worth mentioning. Silas Crane wasn't with him."

"No."

"No," Maren agreed quietly. "Crane's in the county jail, awaiting trial for the cache cutting, the bridge, and attempted murder besides. He asked for Vane at his arraignment. Vane didn't come."

Odile watched the water flash silver between the trees. There was a kind of ruin worse than ruin, she thought — the kind where they let you walk away whole, and everyone knows your name means nothing anymore. The commission could have pressed charges on Cassius Vane for conspiracy, for obstruction, for a dozen things. They hadn't. Vesper had asked them not to, and nobody quite knew why, least of all Vesper herself, though Odile had a guess: mercy given freely costs more than vengeance, and Vesper Zhou had always been willing to pay more than anyone else at the table.

"He'll live a long life," Odile said, "and hate every day of it."

"Good," said Maren. Then, softer: "Is it?"

* * *

They found Tam and Aldous at the valley mouth, because the train stopped there — the commission had ordered it, a ceremonial first run to the source, and the crew had refused to ride up without stopping.

Aldous Reeve sat in a chair Tam had built him from driftwood and packing-crate pine, wrapped in two blankets despite the mild morning, looking smaller than any man Odile remembered. Twenty years of living alone above the treeline had whittled him down to sinew and stubbornness and a pair of eyes that were still, unmistakably, surveyor's eyes — measuring everything, holding everything.

He'd refused to come downriver. That had been settled in the first five minutes, the way things with Aldous were always settled.

"The river needs watching," he'd said. "Twenty years I watched it alone. Now there's work again — real work. Gauge readings. Bank surveys. Someone has to know this water." And he'd looked at Tam, who had not left his side since the day they'd broken open the tunnel, and something had passed between the two old men that needed no contract and no oath.

Now Tam stood behind the chair with his hand on Aldous's shoulder, and when the crew climbed down onto the ballast, Aldous's gaze went straight past all of them to Vesper.

"You're thinner," he said.

"You're older."

"I'm ancient. You're thin. We've both been negligent." His voice was dry as old paper, but there was warmth under it, banked like coals. "Come here, girl. Let me look at you."

Vesper crossed the ballast and crouched by the chair, and Odile saw her take the old man's hand the way you hold something you once believed lost forever. Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, loud and glad and unimpeded.

"They gave me copies," Aldous said finally. "Of the payout rolls. Eleven hundred families. Do you know how many of them I could name? Their fathers worked for me. Their mothers cooked in my camps." His grip tightened on Vesper's fingers. "I collapsed that tunnel because I couldn't bear to be the one who dug it open. Cowardice, dressed up as protection. You did in six weeks what I couldn't do in twenty years."

"You didn't collapse it alone," Vesper said. "The charges were set before you ever got there. You just chose not to reopen what reopening would cost. That's not cowardice, old man. That's a wound."

Aldous laughed — a rusty, startling sound — and then coughed, and Tam's hand moved to his shoulder like it had done it ten thousand times already.

"Well," Aldous managed. "Regardless. I have an inheritance to disburse properly, and a river to mind, and apparently a foreman of my own again." He glanced up at Tam. "If he'll have the position. It pays nothing. The benefits are terrible. Mostly it's hauling water and arguing about gauge widths."

Tam's face, seamed like old leather, cracked into something that might have been a grin on a younger man. "I've had worse offers."

Fenlo whooped. Even Maren smiled into her coffee.

* * *

The last leg home, the light went long and gold across the water meadows, and Odile rode the platform of the caboose because some things deserved to be felt with wind in your teeth.

Vesper came to stand beside her as the spur line curved down toward Sable Junction, its lamps just beginning to glow in the dusk. In Vesper's hand was the compass — Aldous Reeve's compass, brass worn to shine, the case dented where a rockfall had nearly taken Fenlo and nearly taken her.

"It doesn't point north anymore," Vesper said. "Hasn't since the valley. I keep checking. It just... points along the line."

Odile looked at it. The needle lay steady, quivering only slightly, aimed dead down the track ahead of them, into the darkening country where the reopened rails ran.

"Magnetic anomaly," Odile said. "Probably ore body under the valley. Nothing to worry about."

"That's what I told myself."

"And?"

Vesper turned the compass over in her palm. "And I think maybe a compass is just a tool for finding your way back to somewhere you left. And I'm not going back anywhere." She closed the lid with a small click. "Aldous says he'll leave it to me. I told him I'd only take it if it agreed to point forward."

"Compasses don't argue much," Odile said. "Take after their owners."

Vesper huffed — almost a laugh — and leaned on the rail beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd stood through a hundred hard years of hard jobs. Below them the wheels sang. Above them the first stars came out over the Sable watershed, and somewhere behind them the river ran loud and free through a valley that had finally given up its dead.

"Debt's paid," Odile said. It wasn't a question. The commission had confirmed it that morning — the amount Vesper had borrowed, year after year, chasing a missing man up a forbidden river, all of it forgiven, folded into the restoration as recovery costs. Her name was clean. First time in a decade.

"Paid," Vesper agreed. Then, quieter: "You took that job from Vane without asking me. Accepted on the crew's behalf. I never decided whether I was angry about that."

"Are you?"

Vesper was quiet long enough for three telegraph poles to slide past. "No," she said. "Because you bet on us. Not on him, not on the money. On us. And we came out the other side still a crew. That's the only wager that's ever mattered on this line."

Odile nodded, throat too tight for words, and let the silence be enough.

Ahead, Sable Junction spread out its lamplight — the depot repainted, the board listing new work: a branch survey up the Alder Fork, a trestle rebuild at Miller's Crossing, contracts enough for a year and then some, addressed to *Zhou Survey Company, Crew of Record.* Word had gotten around about what this crew could do when the maps went quiet.

Maren stuck her head out the caboose door. "Supper in twenty. Fenlo's helping, which means either we eat early or we eat ash."

"Both," Fenlo called from inside.

Vesper pushed off the rail, compass in her pocket, and paused at the door. "Odile. When we get in — the Alder Fork job. You want first pick of the route?"

Odile looked out at the rails unspooling into the blue evening, singing under the wheels, going forward.

"Line's open," she said. "Let's see where it goes."
