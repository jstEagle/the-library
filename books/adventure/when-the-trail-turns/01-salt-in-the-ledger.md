## 1. Salt in the Ledger

The atoll had no name on any chart Sable owned, which was exactly how she liked her work sites: anonymous, unloved, and unlikely to be visited by anyone with a clipboard. The locals called it *el diente*—the tooth—because from a distance it looked like a molar of rock and palm jutting out of a sea so blue it seemed embarrassed by its own clarity.

Sable Villalobos had been diving the tooth for eleven days, and in that time she had learned three things: the wreck forty feet down was older than her contract claimed, someone had gone to considerable trouble to erase who it belonged to, and the parrotfish that lived in the coral head near the bow had opinions about her dive light and expressed them daily by headbutting it.

"Good morning to you too," she told the fish, whose name was Admiral, because everything in Sable's life got named eventually. Her mother had been the same way. Boats, storms, particularly stubborn barnacles—if you spent enough time with a thing, it earned a name. It was one of the few inheritances that had survived intact.

She hung in the water column above the wreck and let the morning light sort itself into shafts around her. Below, the hull of the ship rose out of the sand like the ribcage of something that had once been magnificent and was now mostly furniture for coral. Her job, per the contract, was simple: survey the structure, recover anything of salvage value, log coordinates, get paid, leave. She'd done a hundred jobs like it. This one had started feeling wrong around day four.

The trouble was the hull markings.

Ships wore their names like people wore scars—proudly, or at least honestly. Even wrecks usually kept some lettering, some plate, some ghost of paint along the bow where a nameplate had been riveted. This hull had been ground smooth. Not weathered, not corroded—*ground*, with a tool, in long even strokes that no storm had ever made. Sable had run her gloved fingers over those strokes on three separate dives, tracing the deliberate arcs, and each time she came up with the same thought settling heavier in her chest: somebody had stood on this deck, or hung off this bow, and sanded away this ship's identity before she ever got here.

You didn't do that to a fishing boat. You didn't do that to a tramp freighter hauling rice. You did that when there was something aboard—or something about the vessel itself—that needed forgetting.

"Survey the structure," she muttered through her regulator. "Recover valuables. Log coordinates." The Admiral butted her light again. "Yes, thank you, I'm going."

She dropped toward the bow, fins kicking lazily, and began the day's work: photographing the hull sections, checking the corrosion pins she'd set last week, and generally pretending to herself that she was an ordinary salvager doing an ordinary job for an ordinary client, instead of a woman slowly collecting evidence that nothing about this job was ordinary.

The brass fitting found her almost by accident.

It sat in the scree of the collapsed superstructure, half-buried in sand, glinting when her light swept across it—a valve housing, maybe six inches long, the kind of chunky machined hardware that outlives everything else on a ship out of sheer stubbornness. Sable picked it up to log it as potential scrap value, turned it over in her hand, and stopped breathing for a second.

Stamped into the base, small and crisp beneath a skin of marine growth she rubbed away with her thumb, was a serial number. And not just any serial number. She knew the format. Every working diver in these waters knew the format, the way farmers know rain clouds. It was a naval registry stamp—the kind of marking that appeared on government vessels and nowhere else, the kind of marking that made customs officials stand up straighter and insurance companies develop sudden amnesia.

Sable floated there, holding six inches of brass that said *the navy was here* on a wreck whose paperwork said *private cargo, unremarkable, please don't look closely.*

Her handler's name was Ferro, and Ferro had hired her through a broker through another broker, which was already two layers more anonymity than most jobs carried. The contract had come with generous terms and vague language—"structural survey, selective recovery, discretion appreciated"—and Sable, who had rent due on her mooring slip and a boat engine held together largely by prayer and hose clamps, had signed without asking the questions her mother would have asked.

Her mother would have asked all of them. Then again, her mother had also once tried to pay a harbormaster's fine with a jar of sea glass and a story, so perhaps her judgment wasn't the gold standard Sable remembered it being.

Still. She looked at the fitting turning slowly in her hand, catching the light, and felt the old itch start up between her shoulder blades. It was a familiar itch. It was the itch of being paid to not know things—and Sable had learned, expensively, over years of contracts just like this one, that the money never covered the cost of the not-knowing. The not-knowing compounded. It gathered interest in the dark parts of your mind and came due at three in the morning when you couldn't sleep and your brain served up questions like *who grinds the name off a hull?* and *what does a navy ship carry that needs grinding?*

She should put it back. That was the professional move. Log it as unidentified scrap, leave it in the sand, collect her fee, sail away clean.

Instead, Sable Villalobos slipped the brass fitting into the thigh pocket of her wetsuit, patted it flat, and continued her survey with the serene expression of a woman who had definitely not just done that.

The Admiral watched her do it. Fish were terrible gossips.

* * *

The Mariposa rode at anchor in the atoll's lee, rolling gently in the afternoon swell, looking every one of her thirty-odd years. She was a converted island freighter with a rusted rail, a wheelhouse the size of a closet, and an engine named Beatrice who started when she felt like it and not otherwise. Sable had inherited her along with the restlessness, and if you'd asked her—which nobody did—she'd have said the two inheritances were the same inheritance, wearing different clothes.

She climbed aboard, stripped her gear, and rinsed off with the deck shower while the sun cooked the salt onto her shoulders. The fitting sat in a bucket of fresh water on the bench, doing its slow secret work of existing.

Ferro was due tomorrow. The contract said fourteen days; he'd radioed that morning to say he'd be there in one, payment complete, no need to finish the final survey grid. *Weather window closing,* he'd said, which was true enough—the monsoon season was stacking up out east like a debt collector—but Sable had read the message four times and heard something underneath it that sounded less like scheduling and more like *stop digging.*

Which was ridiculous, because she hadn't dug anywhere. She'd surveyed. Professionally. With photographs.

She dried off, wrapped herself in the faded sarong that lived on the stern cleat, and made coffee on the single burner in the galley while the Admiral's reef glittered offshore like a rumor. The Mariposa creaked her evening creaks. Somewhere below, Beatrice ticked as she cooled, sounding vaguely disapproving, as always.

"You'd have taken it too," Sable told the bucket.

Beatrice did not dignify that with a response.

* * *

Ferro arrived at midday in a gray-hulled launch that moved like it had somewhere better to be. He was a compact man with a face designed to be forgotten—middle height, middle years, middle coloring, the human equivalent of beige—and he came up the Mariposa's ladder with the careful politeness of a man visiting a place he intended never to think about again.

"Señorita Villalobos." He shook her hand. His palm was dry. Nobody who worked the water had palms that dry. "You are well?"

"I'm tan and solvent-adjacent," Sable said. "Coffee?"

"Thank you, no." He produced an envelope from inside his jacket—an actual paper envelope, full of actual cash, because men like Ferro believed paper trails were for people with nothing to hide—and set it on the chart table. "Full payment. Plus the early-completion bonus we discussed."

"We didn't discuss a bonus."

"We discussed it implicitly." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced, the kind that arrived and departed on schedule. "Your survey materials, please."

Sable handed over the data chip with her photos and logs, and watched him tuck it away with the same care he'd shown the envelope. On the bench behind him, the bucket of fresh water sat with the brass fitting in it, hidden under a rag, radiating guilt like a stove.

"The site," Ferro said. He paused, choosing words the way a man chooses stepping stones across a creek. "You found it... interesting?"

"It's a wreck." Sable leaned against the bulkhead and kept her voice bored, which took effort, because the itch between her shoulder blades had graduated to a full spinal chorus. "Corroded, collapsed, stripped. Whoever salvaged it before me got the good stuff."

"Mm." He studied her with eyes that were trying very hard to be unremarkable and failing, because they were the only part of him with any color in them—a pale, watery blue, like ice over deep water. "There is sometimes confusion, on jobs like this. Salvage divers are curious people. Curiosity is useful in your profession, I understand. But this site—" he gestured vaguely seaward, "—is closed now. Surveyed, logged, finished. There is nothing further required of anyone. I'm sure you understand."

"Perfectly," Sable said. "I'll forget it immediately. What was I saying?"

The smile again, thinner this time. "Exactly." He moved to the ladder, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and turned halfway back. "One thing more. A professional courtesy." His voice dropped, though there was no one within a mile to hear it but her, the bucket, and God. "Divers talk. In port towns, over drinks. It's natural. But this site—this particular site—is not a topic that rewards conversation. Not for you. Not for anyone you might mention it to. Some wrecks are only dangerous because they're deep." He tapped the rail twice, lightly, like knocking on a door he hoped wouldn't open. "This one is dangerous because of what it was carrying. Do you take my meaning?"

"I take that you're paying me to forget," Sable said. "Which, for the record, is my favorite genre of employment."

Something flickered across his forgettable face—not quite amusement, not quite warning. "Then we understand each other." He went down the ladder, cast off, and the gray launch pulled away toward the open sea, leaving a wake like a drawn line under their conversation.

Sable stood at the rail until it was gone, then a while longer, watching the line of the wake dissolve into the general shimmer of the sea, which was what lines did out here eventually. Everything dissolved eventually. That was the whole philosophy of the ocean, and she'd built a career on agreeing with it.

Then she went inside, fished the brass fitting out of the bucket, and set it on the chart table next to the envelope of money.

Side by side, they made a tidy little still life: the payment on the left, the proof on the right. Take the money, forget the number. That was the deal. That was always the deal, and she'd honored it before, more times than she could count, because honoring it kept her employed and employed meant fueled and fueled meant moving, and moving was the whole architecture of her life. Keep the hull wet. Never let the anchor grow roots.

But the serial number sat there, stamped small and certain, and the itch spread from her spine into her hands.

Because here was the thing about being paid to not know things: the knowing didn't actually go away. It just went underground, into the silt at the bottom of you, and things buried in silt had a way of surfacing. Her mother used to say that about secrets and about anchors, in the same breath, usually while doing something inadvisable.

Sable picked up the fitting. Turned it over. Ran her thumb across the stamped digits, feeling them the way she'd felt the grind marks on the hull—deliberate, human, recent. Somebody had wanted this ship unborn. Somebody had paid her to agree it had never existed. And the amount in the envelope, she noticed now that she counted it, was roughly double what the contract specified, which wasn't generosity. Generosity didn't need silencing. Only knowledge did.

"Well," she said to the galley, to Beatrice, to the fitting. "That's a lot of money to not know a thing."

She poured the last of the coffee, carried both mug and fitting up to the wheelhouse, and sat in the captain's chair with her boots on the chart shelf, watching the light die over the reef. Out east, past the atoll's edge, the sky had begun assembling the monsoon—great stacked towers of cloud, purple-bottomed, patient. Weather coming. Ferro hadn't lied about that part.

Sable turned the fitting in her fingers one last time, then opened the drawer beneath the chart table—the drawer where she kept the things she wasn't supposed to keep: a compass with someone else's initials, a photograph of a harbor she couldn't identify, a key to a locker in a port she'd never returned to. The museum of unfinished business. She laid the fitting inside, next to its quiet colleagues, and shut the drawer.

The envelope stayed on the table. She'd count it properly later, and not spend a peso of it until she'd decided what it was buying: her silence, or her ticket.

Below, the reef settled into its evening colors, and the Admiral went wherever admirals go at night. Sable sat in the failing light with salt drying on her skin and a question taking root where the itch had been, and it was not the question Ferro wanted her asking. It was older than that, and hungrier, and it sounded exactly like her mother's voice:

*What else did they bury out here?*

She didn't have an answer. But back in Puerto Hondo, in the locker under her bunk, wrapped in oilcloth inside a sea chest that smelled forever of camphor and cigarettes, there was a half-finished map in her mother's handwriting—and suddenly, for the first time since the funeral, Sable very much wanted to look at it.
