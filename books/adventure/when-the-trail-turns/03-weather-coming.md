## 3. Weather Coming

Puerto Hondo smelled the way all port towns smell when the weather is about to turn: diesel and salt and a low, nervous hum of activity that nobody acknowledges out loud. The fishermen were doubling their lines. The chandler had moved his paint cans inside. Even the dogs had stopped lounging in the middle of the road and taken up strategic positions under awnings, as if they'd read the barometer and drawn conclusions.

Sable tied the *Mariposa* off at the public dock with the particular knot her mother had taught her—a bowline with an extra turn that she'd been told was "for boats you intend to come back to"—and stood for a moment on the deck, looking up the hill at the town.

Puerto Hondo was three streets stacked on a hillside like dishes left to dry. There was a church whose bell hadn't rung since before Sable was born, a cantina whose bell rang constantly, and between them, everything else: the chandler, the fuel depot, the ice house, a woman named Doña Fer who sold fruit from a cart and gossip from somewhere deeper and more reliable than any well.

"Mariposa," Doña Fer said when Sable reached the top of the dock ramp, squinting at the boat's name painted on the transom. "Your mother's boat."

"You knew my mother?"

"Everyone knew your mother." Doña Fer handed her a mango without being asked, the way you'd hand a coin to a beggar or a crown to a queen—firmly, and with the expectation of no argument. "She bought limes here. Always too many limes. Nobody eats that many limes."

"She did," Sable said, and something in her chest went tight and warm at once. She took a bite of the mango to cover it. It was perfect, which annoyed her slightly.

"How long are you staying?"

"Just resupplying. Fuel, water, provisions. Then north."

Doña Fer's eyebrows performed a small, eloquent migration upward. "North." She said it the way other people said *cancer* or *tax audit*.

"That's a problem?"

"It's monsoon season."

"I've been diving through worse."

"Everyone says that right before they stop saying anything at all." Doña Fer refilled Sable's hand with another mango, uninvited, inexhaustible. "Where north? The Serena passage?"

The name landed somewhere behind Sable's ribs. Isla Serena. The circled island. *Ask about the quiet ones.*

"Why do you ask?" Sable said, aiming for casual and hearing, instead, the sound of a woman trying very hard not to look at a locked door.

"Because I ask everyone. It's my business. Fruit and questions." Doña Fer smiled with about forty percent of her face. "And because a government boat came through two weeks ago. Big one. Grey. Unloaded supplies onto a barge headed for Serena. Survey equipment, generators, crates marked with tape. Nobody loads crates with tape unless they don't want you reading them."

"What kind of survey?"

"The kind where the men doing it wouldn't tell Doña Fer what kind." She shrugged, magnificent in her ignorance. "Eight of them. Young. Polite. One of them bought oranges from me and asked if the island had fresh water. I told him it has a spring on the east side, seasonal. He wrote it down like it was scripture."

Sable chewed her mango slowly. Eight men. A grey ship. Crates with tape over their markings. Her mother's pencil circling that same island years ago, pressing hard enough to nearly tear the paper.

*Ask about the quiet ones.*

"Doña Fer," she said carefully, "what do people say about Isla Serena? Around here. Old stories, whatever."

The old woman's smile faded into something more careful. She glanced down the street, then back, and lowered her voice even though the nearest living soul was a cat asleep in a fish crate.

"People say the island is quiet. Not empty—quiet. Birds land there and go silent. Fishermen say their compasses wander near it, but only near it, like the island pulls at them gently and lets go. My grandmother said there was a village there once, long ago, and it left in one night. Took nothing. Left the fires burning." She picked up her mango knife and began peeling another fruit with slow, economical strokes. "People also say nonsense. But nonsense usually has a fish bone in it somewhere. You just have to find the bone."

"And the government survey team? What do people say about them?"

"That they went to count fish." Doña Fer snorted. "Nobody sends eight men and a grey ship to count fish. You send one boy with a notebook to count fish. You send eight men and taped crates when you're counting something else."

* * *

The chandler's shop was hot and dim and smelled of rope tar and old canvas, and its proprietor, a barrel-shaped man called Esteban, greeted Sable like a returning daughter he vaguely disapproved of.

"Sable Villalobos. Still alive. The sea is getting careless."

"Esteban. Still charging double. The economy is getting careless."

He laughed, a sound like a winch under load, and got down her usual list without being told: diesel filters, zincs, a coil of new line, canned goods, spare impellers. While he tallied, Sable wandered the aisles, letting her fingers trail across shelves of things that might save her life someday, which was most of the inventory.

It was while she was comparing dive lights that the door opened and the temperature of the room changed—not literally, though it felt that way. Two men entered. One was a local harbormaster's assistant she half-recognized; the other wore whites so crisp they seemed to have arrived ironed by their own sense of importance.

Navy.

The officer was young—younger than his rank suggested someone had decided he should be—and he had the particular stillness of a man who has been taught that stillness reads as authority. He surveyed the shop, found Sable, and dismissed her in the same glance, which was a mistake people kept making around her and kept regretting.

"Esteban," the officer said. "Fuel for a patrol skiff. Sixty liters. And I need a list of every civilian vessel that's taken on provisions here in the last week."

Esteban's face did something complicated. "A list, señor?"

"Destinations included."

"Well." Esteban scratched his jaw, buying time the way he bought everything, slowly and at a markup. "I sell rope and rice, señor. Where boats go after they leave my shop is between them and God."

The officer's jaw tightened. He turned, and this time when his eyes passed over Sable they stopped, because she was looking directly at him with the frank, unhurried attention of someone cataloguing details: the sweat darkening his collar despite the air conditioning posture, the way his left hand kept touching a folded paper in his breast pocket, the fact that his shoes were wrong for a port town—too clean, too new, as if he'd been issued this posting recently and hadn't yet learned what the mud did to everything.

"You're a diver," he said. It wasn't a question. Her gear bag sat at her feet, and the calluses on her hands had apparently spoken for her.

"Salvage," Sable said. "Recreational diving is for people with trust funds."

"Then you'll want to stay clear of the northern islands. There's a restricted zone effective immediately, ahead of the monsoon. Isla Serena and surrounding waters. No civilian traffic." He produced the folded paper—the order, freshly printed, still creased sharp—and held it out. "For your records."

Sable took it. It was a standard exclusion notice, dense with legalese, except for one detail that snagged her like a hook: the restriction wasn't dated from today. It was dated eleven days ago. Backdated, or pre-dated, or whatever you called a government deciding after the fact when a rule should have started existing.

"Restricted for what?" she asked.

"Weather safety."

"The monsoon doesn't hit for two weeks."

"Precautionary."

"For what?"

"For the weather," the officer said, with the flat patience of a man reciting a script he didn't write, "that we are precautioning against."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. He looked back with the weary defiance of someone who had answered this question already, probably several times, probably to Doña Fer, who had likely extracted more from him in five minutes than Sable would manage in fifty.

"Understood," she said finally, folding the paper and putting it in her pocket. "I'll be sure to stay away from Isla Serena entirely. Completely. In every way."

Something flickered across his face—relief, or its cousin—and he nodded once, paid for the fuel in cash, and left. The bell above the door gave a small ironic jingle.

Esteban exhaled. "That one's been in twice. Asking about boats heading north."

"Any heading north?"

"One. A charter skipper, took paying passengers to Serena waters last month before the order. Came back early, wouldn't say why. Sold his charter business a week later and moved inland." Esteban wrote up her total with a pencil worn to a stub. "You're going anyway."

It wasn't a question either. Port towns could read a hull the way priests read palms.

"I'm considering my options," Sable said.

"Consider faster. Weather window's closing." He slid the receipt across. "And Sable. Your mother came through here before her last trip. Bought limes."

"I know. Too many limes."

"No." Esteban paused, pencil hovering. "She asked me the same thing you just asked Doña Fer. About Serena. About the quiet ones." He shook his head slowly. "I told her what I knew, which was nothing useful, and she thanked me like I'd given her a treasure. Three days later she was gone." He met Sable's eyes. "Whatever she was looking for up there—I'd think hard about whether you want to find it."

* * *

Evening came down on Puerto Hondo like a lid. Sable carried her provisions aboard in failing light, stowed them with the muscle-memory economy of twenty years of small-boat living, and ended up sitting in the cockpit with the exclusion notice spread on her knee and her mother's map open beside it.

The chart was beautiful in the way unfinished things sometimes are—more honest than completion ever manages. Her mother's hand was confident in the coastlines, playful in the depth soundings (she'd written one as "deep enough to lose a secret"), and increasingly sparse as the chart ran north, until it simply stopped, mid-passage, the pencil line trailing off like a sentence abandoned.

Except for Isla Serena. That island was drawn completely. Circled twice. Annotated in that same slanted hand:

*Ask about the quiet ones.*

Sable had spent two nights in her bunk turning that phrase over. Ask whom? The quiet ones—who were they? People? Or something else—something the island itself kept quiet?

Now she had answers, of a sort. A grey ship. Taped crates. Eight young men sent to count something that wasn't fish. A navy officer handing out warnings with dates that didn't add up. And a word that kept surfacing in her mind with the patience of a diver's bubble rising toward light:

*Restricted.*

Her mother had come through this town asking these questions and sailed north and never come back. And now the government had drawn a circle around the same island her mother had, except theirs was made of law and hers had been made of pencil.

Sable looked at the exclusion notice. Then at the map. Then out at the harbor, where the water lay flat and oily and expectant, holding the first stars the way a held breath holds words.

Every instinct she'd inherited—the restlessness, the itch, the need to know what was being kept from her—was leaning north like wind in rigging.

She should take a contract south. Easy wreck, easy money, easy season. She should let the navy keep its secrets; they were heavy, and she'd seen what carrying them did to people.

But her mother had pressed that pencil into the paper hard enough to nearly tear it. And Sable had spent her whole life following maps other people drew.

Tomorrow, she'd post a notice at the cantina: *Passage north, experienced skipper, reasonable rates.* If someone wanted a ride to the edge of the restricted water, she'd take them. If someone wanted answers—

Well. Answers were cargo too. They just weighed nothing until you tried to put them down.

She rolled the map closed, careful along her mother's pencil lines, and was reaching to switch off the cabin light when she heard footsteps on the dock. Measured ones. Unhurried. The kind that belonged to someone who had been watching the *Mariposa* for a while and had decided, finally, to introduce himself.

Sable slid the map into its case, picked up the wrench she kept for exactly this purpose, and stepped out into the dark.

"Can I help you?" she called.

A figure detached itself from the shadow of the fuel shed. A man. Average height, average build, forgettable face—which meant he'd worked at being forgettable, because nobody was born that good at it.

"As a matter of fact," he said, in a voice as calm and deep as still water, "I'm hoping you can help us both."

Behind him, far to the north, lightning stitched the horizon in silence—a storm still days out, but already introducing itself.

Sable tightened her grip on the wrench and did not lower it.

"Start talking," she said.
