## 5. Landfall on Serena

**Sable**

The squall hit them forty minutes out from Isla Serena like a drunk uncle at a wedding—sudden, loud, and entirely too familiar.

"Rain's coming sideways," Ilo said from the bow, holding the rail with one hand and his hat with the other. He'd been wearing that hat since Puerto Hondo, a soft-brimmed thing the color of wet sand, and in three days of passage he had not once taken it off. Sable had begun to suspect it was load-bearing.

"It's not rain," she called back over the wind. "It's the sky rinsing itself before it does something worse."

She had the Mariposa's wheel in both hands and her eyes on the gray smear of the island ahead. Serena didn't look like much from this distance—a green thumbprint on the edge of the world, ringed by white water where the reef broke. Her mother's chart said otherwise. Her mother's chart said *ask about the quiet ones*, inked beside a circle drawn so hard it had nearly torn the paper.

"You've been quiet since dawn," Ilo said, coming aft to stand beside her. He had to shout to be heard over the engine and the weather, which Sable privately considered an improvement over his usual conversational volume.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a kettle is quiet," he said. "There's pressure building."

Sable almost smiled. Almost. "I was thinking about my mother."

That shut him up for a full minute, which told her something. Most men would have rushed to fill the silence with comfort or questions. Ilo just stood there in the rain, letting her have the space, watching the island grow the way she was—like it might bite.

"She came here once," Sable said finally. "Or near here. The chart stops about six miles short of Serena. Pencil lines just... trail off."

"People stop drawing maps for two reasons," Ilo said. "They found what they were looking for, or they stopped looking."

"And which do you think?"

He adjusted his hat against the wind. "I think you already know which, or you wouldn't have asked."

The squall passed as quickly as it had come, leaving the air scrubbed and trembling, and then Serena was right there in front of them: a horseshoe bay, a beach of crushed coral, and behind it a scatter of canvas that could only be the survey camp.

Sable cut the engine and let the boat drift the last hundred meters, listening.

No voices. No generator hum. No hammering of tent stakes being adjusted after a storm. Just the water slapping the hull and, faintly, carried across the flat morning air, a sound she couldn't place at first—a thin electronic chirp, rhythmic as a metronome.

"That's a radio," Ilo said quietly. "An automated check-in cycle."

"At a camp nobody's answering from?"

"At a camp nobody's answering *to*," he corrected, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before. Not fear exactly. Recognition.

* * *

**Ilo**

Ilo had stood on enough beaches in his life to know the difference between an empty shore and an abandoned one. An empty shore felt open. Abandoned felt held—held by whatever had emptied it, the way a room holds the shape of the person who just left.

This beach was abandoned.

The landing skiff from the camp sat pulled above the tideline, dry-rotted rope still looped around a coral nub. A pair of sandals stood side by side at the edge of the vegetation, toes pointing inland, as if their owner had stepped out of them to walk somewhere soft and meant to come right back.

"They left in a hurry," Villalobos said, wading ashore beside him with the surveyor's economy of motion he'd been admiring—and distrusting—for three days.

"No," Ilo said. "Look again. They left unhurried. That's what worries me."

She frowned at him, saltwater dripping off her braid.

"Hurried people drop things," he explained. "Scatter things. Take the wrong bag." He nodded down the beach toward the camp. "Everything here is *arranged*. Sandals together. Skiff tied off properly. That's not panic. That's people who expected to return in an hour."

Villalobos said nothing, but he saw her hand drift to the dive knife at her hip, and he counted that as agreement.

The camp itself sat in a clearing among the sea grapes: four tents, a cook shelter, a folding table buried under instruments, and a solar array angled toward the pale sun like a field of black flowers. Everything neat. Everything running. A laptop screen glowed under a plastic cover. A weather station ticked and recorded. And from a radio unit lashed to a tripod, every ninety seconds, that patient electronic chirp.

Ilo crossed to the radio and read the placard taped beneath it. *Scheduled check-in 0600/1200/1800 local. If no response, continue cycle.*

"When did they last answer?" Villalobos asked, appearing at his shoulder.

He checked the log display. His stomach did something slow and cold. "Eleven days ago."

"Eleven—" She looked around the camp, at the meals half-eaten under mesh covers, at the laundry line strung with shirts gone stiff with salt. "Eleven days and nobody's come looking?"

"Somebody sent me," Ilo said. It slipped out before he could weigh it, and he watched her turn that fact over with the same careful hands she used on salvage.

"Sent you," she repeated. "By whom?"

"The less you know right now, the more honestly you can tell me what you see." He met her eyes. "I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like a man who's practiced saying it."

He almost laughed. There it was again—that blunt, unsentimental clarity that made working with her feel like standing next to an open window. "Fair," he admitted. "Ask me again tonight. On the boat. I'll give you what I have."

"Tonight," she agreed, and turned away to search the camp, which was its own kind of promise: she wasn't leaving, but she was keeping score.

* * *

**Sable**

The tents told stories, if you knew how to listen, and Sable had spent her whole life learning to listen to things that couldn't speak—wrecks, engines, her mother's silences.

Tent one: sleeping gear, rolled and tied. Nobody planned to leave.

Tent two: a cot with a paperback facedown on the blanket, spine cracked to hold the page. Chapter nineteen of something with a submarine on the cover. She allowed herself a grim little laugh at that.

Tent three was the mess of supplies—spare batteries, ration cases, a first aid kit opened and neatly repacked. Disciplined people, these. Government people, or people pretending hard to be.

Tent four stopped her cold.

Inside, pinned to the tent wall with four stones, was a map. A proper hydrographic survey map of the waters east of Serena, covered in pencil annotations in at least three different hands. Depth soundings. A reef line. And circled, twice, with the second circle pressed so hard the pencil had torn through—

Sable's breath went thin.

She knew that handwriting. Not the surveyors'. The oldest annotations, faded and half-smudged, were her mother's. The same forward-leaning script. The same habit of crossing sevens. The same little arrow pointing at deep water east of the reef with two words beside it:

*quiet ones*

Her mother hadn't just been near this island. Her mother had stood in this exact spot—or a tent very much like it, years ago—and marked the same water the survey team had come to mark.

"Sable." Ilo's voice from outside, carefully neutral. "You should see the galley."

* * *

**Ilo**

He'd found the meals first and understood them second, and by the time he called her over he'd arranged his face into something presentable.

Three plates under mesh covers on the cook table. Rice, congealed now, a day or three old when it was abandoned—no, longer, the mold said longer, maybe five days. Five plates' worth of food cooked. Three plates left. Two eaten down to scraping.

"Two people finished eating," Villalobos said, reading it faster than he had. "One didn't start. Where's the rest of the team?"

"Counting the tents, eight, maybe nine people." He lifted the mesh. Flies rose lazily, unbothered, which told him no one had died here recently enough to matter. Small mercies. "And they cooked a full meal and then walked away from most of it. You don't do that unless something interrupted you between the stove and the table."

"Something they all followed?" She was circling the shelter now, reading ground the way he'd seen her read water. "No scuffle marks. No dropped anything."

"No tracks at all past the eastern edge of camp," Ilo said. "I've walked the perimeter twice. They go about thirty meters into the tree line and just... end. All of them. Same direction."

"Together. Calm. Barefoot or shod?"

"Shod. Boot prints, consistent tread, heading east-northeast." He paused. "Toward the point."

Villalobos went still in the way he was beginning to recognize as her version of shouting. "The point is where the reef drops off," she said. "Where the water goes from twenty meters to two hundred in the length of a dive light beam."

"Yes."

"And your mysterious employers sent you here because...?"

"Because the team stopped answering," he said, which was true, and because of what the team had found, which was truer, and neither of which he was ready to say aloud with the flies buzzing and her mother's handwriting waiting in that tent like a loaded question.

She studied him for a long moment. Rain-wind moved through the sea grapes. Somewhere down the beach, the radio chirped its patient, idiot greeting to no one.

"Tonight," she said again. "On the boat. And Ilo—" she tapped the cook table, right beside the untouched plate, "—when you tell me, remember I found my mother's handwriting on their map. Whatever you're protecting me from, I'm already standing in it."

* * *

**Sable**

They worked the rest of the afternoon because work was the only language either of them trusted. Sable photographed everything worth photographing—the map in tent four most of all, shooting it from four angles with her salvaged camera while her heart knocked around inside her ribs like a loose fitting. Ilo pulled the data cards from the instruments, logging serial numbers with the fussy precision of a man who'd been audited before and hadn't enjoyed it.

The light was going amber when she heard it: the radio, mid-cycle, doing something new.

Not the chirp. A voice. Recorded, looping, a woman's voice with the flat patience of someone reading words written by committee:

*"Serena station, scheduled check-in. All personnel accounted for. Operations nominal. Next transmission at eighteen hundred."*

"All personnel accounted for," Sable repeated.

"Recorded eleven days ago," Ilo said from the tripod. "Same message, every cycle, since."

"So whoever hears this thinks everything's fine."

"Whoever hears this thinks everything's fine," he agreed.

She looked out past the point, where the water had gone from turquoise to ink as the reef shelf fell away, and felt the dread arrive—not understanding, not yet, just dread, the animal kind, the kind that lives below language. Eight or nine disciplined people had cooked dinner, set the table, and walked barefoot-calm into the trees toward the deepest water in the archipelago, and the machinery they'd left behind was cheerfully telling the world they were fine.

"My mother wrote 'ask about the quiet ones,'" Sable said.

Ilo came to stand beside her. For once he didn't adjust his hat.

"I think," she said slowly, "the quiet ones answered."

Down the beach, the radio chirped. Out past the point, the tide had begun, almost imperceptibly, to fall.
