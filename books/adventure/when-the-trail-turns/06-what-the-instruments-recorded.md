## 6. What the Instruments Recorded

The survey team had left their laptop behind.

That was the first thing that bothered Ilo about the whole arrangement. In his experience—and his experience was longer and uglier than he liked to admit—people who vanished took their electronics with them. People who were taken didn't get to choose. Either way, a laptop sitting open on a camp table, screen dark, power light still breathing its slow green breath, was either an oversight or an invitation.

He'd been staring at it for ten minutes when Sable came back through the treeline carrying two coconuts and an expression like a storm front.

"Generator's got fuel for maybe six more hours," she said, setting one coconut in front of him with the thunk of a man being sentenced. "Whoever ran this camp was tidy. Fuel drums sealed, water cans full, food stores inventoried in pencil on the inside of the mess tent flap. Nobody packs up in a hurry and leaves the inventory current."

"Nobody leaves at all," Ilo said. "That's the part I keep circling."

"Tracks?"

"End at the tree line on the north side. Six sets of boots walking out, none walking back. The ground's too soft there—should be prints all the way down to the rocks if they went that way." He turned the laptop toward her. "And nobody locked this."

Sable looked at the machine the way she'd looked at the wreck off the nameless atoll, he suspected—with the particular hunger of a woman who made her living on other people's sunken things. "Can you get into it?"

"I can try." He said it lightly, but his hands were already moving, and they moved with a certainty that he knew she noticed. He saw her notice. He watched her decide not to say anything yet, and he was grateful for it, and ashamed of the gratitude, in roughly equal measure.

* * *

The password fell in under four minutes, which embarrassed him. Not because it was hard—because it wasn't. The team's administrator had used the station name and the year. *Serena2024.* Somewhere, a security officer in a government office building was going to have a very bad week whenever anyone told him, and Ilo found he didn't much care. He'd spent enough of his life around people whose bad weeks never seemed to arrive on schedule.

"What are you, exactly?" Sable asked from the tent flap, where she'd stationed herself with the second coconut and a knife, watching the beach. "Passenger doesn't fit. You checked my tide tables before I did."

"Hired help," Ilo said.

"For whom?"

"That's the part I'm still deciding how much to tell you." He said it plainly, because he'd learned—late, expensively—that the lies you dressed up in soft language cost more than the ones you said straight. "Give me an hour with these files. Then I'll give you everything I've got, and you can decide whether to put me back on the boat or put me ashore."

She considered him over the rim of the coconut. She had salt drying in white lines at her temples and a small cut on her knuckle from the reef, and she looked, he thought, like someone deciding whether a stray dog was worth the fleas.

"One hour," she said. "Then everything. No editing."

"No editing," he agreed, and meant it, which surprised him.

* * *

The files organized themselves into the tidy taxonomy of bureaucracy: daily position logs, equipment calibration records, weather observations, personnel rosters. Reyes, M. — team lead. Okafor, D. — geophysicist. Lindqvist, A. — sonar tech. Chen, P. — dive supervisor. Baptiste, R. — medic. Ferro, J. — comms. Six names. Six sets of tracks ending at the trees.

Ilo read fast, and as he read, the ordinary record began to bend.

The first nine days were textbook. Bathymetric sweeps of the shelf east of the island, sediment cores, magnetometer runs that came back clean. Then, on day ten, the tone of the annotations changed. Lindqvist's sonar logs started including notes that weren't notes so much as arguments with herself:

*Anomaly E-4 again. Same bearing. Moved? Reclassified as fixed feature pending re-survey.*

Two days later: *E-4 is not a geological feature. Geometry too regular. Requesting guidance from HQ. HQ says continue routine survey. Continue routine survey. Fine. Routine.*

He could hear her voice in it—the specific exasperation of a technician being told to unsee what she'd seen. Ilo had heard that voice before, decades ago, in a different accent, on a different ship, from men who had also been told to continue routine. Some of those men had continued. Some of them had not slept properly again for the rest of their lives. He was one of the latter.

Day fourteen. The magnetometer file had been deleted, but deletion on a government-issue laptop was a courtesy, not a fact; the data sat in the free blocks like bodies under calm water. He pulled it with a recovery utility older than some of the people who'd written the survey protocols, and there it was: a mass reading centered four hundred meters offshore, at a depth of thirty-one meters, consistent with—his hands went still on the keyboard—a steel hull. Displacement estimate in the range of eight thousand tons.

There was no wreck of eight thousand tons on any chart of these waters. He knew, because he had helped make sure of it.

"You've gone quiet," Sable said from the flap. "Quiet like a man reading something he already half-knew."

"Your hour's nearly up anyway," he said. "Come sit down. This is better told sitting."

* * *

She came and sat cross-legged on the camp bench across from him, close enough that he could see the laptop screen reflected twice in her eyes, and he gave her what he'd promised: everything, no editing.

"The anomaly is a ship," he said. "Scuttled. Deliberately, in deep water past the shelf edge, except whoever did the math that night got it wrong, or the current disagreed, and she settled here instead—up against the reef, near a cave system, in water shallow enough that someday, someone with good sonar would find her. Someone did. Eleven days ago."

"Sunk how long ago?"

"Forty-one years." He let that number sit between them. "Cargo manifest classified. Mission classified. The men who crewed the scuttling operation signed papers they didn't fully read, and the ones who asked questions were reassigned somewhere very far away, and the ones who kept asking—" He stopped. Started again. "It wasn't munitions, Sable. That's the official line, if there's ever an official line. Munitions don't need manifests with names on them."

Her face did something complicated. He watched her arrive at it ahead of her words, the way divers do—reading the water before the surface shows anything.

"People," she said quietly.

"People." The word came out of him flat and old, worn smooth from forty years of turning it over. "My ship did the scuttling. I was twenty-two and very good at following orders and I told myself for three decades that I hadn't known what was in the hold, and then one day I admitted that I had known—I'd seen the loading, I'd heard the sounds—and that knowing and admitting are two different countries and I'd been living in the wrong one my whole adult life."

Sable didn't offer comfort. He noticed that, and appreciated it. She just waited, the way you wait at anchor, letting the thing swing around until it points true.

"So when your navy friend warned everyone away from Serena," she said finally, "he wasn't warning. He was clearing."

"He was mowing," Ilo agreed. "This team found the hull. They reported it. And within seventy-two hours, headquarters sent a 'resupply vessel'—which arrived at night, which carried no resupply, and which left before dawn. The morning after, this camp was empty."

"And you?" she said. "Where do you come in? Because a guilty conscience alone doesn't buy triple-rate passage on my boat."

Fair. He'd known it would be fair the moment he met her. "Three weeks ago I received a letter. Anonymous. Inside was a copy of the original scuttling order with my ship's name on it, and a note: *They're sending a new team to Serena. They'll find it. They'll be silenced. You owe the hold a witness.*" He spread his hands. "I don't know who sent it. I know I couldn't not come. So I hired the only captain headed this way, who happened to be angry, curious, and short on funds—which, in my experience, is the most reliable combination in the world."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her, brief and unwilling. "You paid me triple rate because I was broke."

"I paid you triple rate because you were the right person. The broke part just meant you'd say yes." He turned the laptop toward her. "But here's what matters now. Look at the last entry."

* * *

The final log wasn't a log at all. It was a text file, saved at 0317 local time, eleven days ago, in the last minutes the team had owned their own instruments. It was unsigned. It read like someone typing fast in the dark, and Ilo felt each line land in his chest like a dropped tool.

*Sonar contact confirmed. Hull intact. Cave system connects to shallows on north side—we can hear surf through the rock at low tide. Okafor thinks the stern section breached into the cavern. There are things in the water we can't account for. Lights below last night. Not bioluminescence. Regular intervals.*

*Someone is watching the site. Boat with no lights passed the point at 0200. We are moving our records off-site tonight. If anyone reads this:*

*It opens at low tide.*

The cursor blinked after the period, patient as a pulse.

"It opens at low tide," Sable repeated. She was looking past the screen, out through the tent flap at the lagoon, doing the arithmetic he could see her doing—the same arithmetic he'd done at dawn. "North side. Where the tracks end."

"Where the tracks end," he agreed. "They didn't leave, Sable. They went down. Or they went in. Into the cave, into the hull, somewhere the navy's sweep didn't think to look—or looked, and decided leaving them was cheaper than retrieving them."

"Eleven days," she said. "On whatever air pockets a flooded wreck offers. If any of them are alive—"

"Then every tide between now and forever is a door swinging both ways." He closed the laptop gently, as if the machine might wake the dead or the nearly-dead, and looked at her. Here it was, then. The moment he'd been dreading since Puerto Hondo, since the letter, since forty-one years ago. "I should tell you the rest. The part where I say you can take the Mariposa and go, monsoon or no monsoon, and I won't think less of you. I've rehearsed it. It sounded very noble in rehearsal."

"Don't say it, then," Sable said. "It wastes time and we both know you're lying."

He blinked. "You're not angry."

"Oh, I'm furious." She stood, cracking her knuckles one hand against the other, and there was a light in her face that he recognized from photographs he'd studied of her mother—the same restless, stubborn blaze, the inheritance she didn't know she was carrying. "I'm furious that someone paid me to forget a serial number last month. I'm furious that your anonymous letter-writer knows more than either of us. And I'm furious that six people walked into those trees and the ocean swallowed the evidence." She jerked her chin toward the lagoon. "Tide turns in five hours. Low water at 1640. If that cave opens at low tide, I want to be looking at it when it does."

Ilo sat very still for a moment, holding the strange, terrifying gift of being believed.

"There's one more thing," he said. "In the files. The personnel roster has a seventh name added by hand, in the margin. Just initials. *M.T.*"

Sable went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the heat.

"My mother's initials," she said. "Marisol Thackeray." Her voice came out steady, which he suspected cost her. "She dove these islands twenty years ago. Her chart has this island circled. She wrote *ask about the quiet ones* next to it, and I thought it meant the islanders." She looked out at the water, and Ilo watched her whole history rearrange itself behind her eyes. "It meant the ones who don't come back."

Neither of them spoke. Out past the reef, the first gray shoulders of the monsoon were assembling on the horizon, patient as debt.

"Five hours," Ilo said finally, and reached for the dive gear.
