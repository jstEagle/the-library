## 7. Tracks That End at Water

The anomaly sat a half-mile off Serena's northern point, in water the color of strong tea left out too long. Ilo marked it on the chart with a pencil cross so small I almost missed it.

"You're sure about these coordinates?" I asked, watching him fold the paper away like it owed him money.

"The survey team was sure," he said. "Their instruments were very sure. Their last log entry was surest of all."

*It opens at low tide.* I'd read the note three times last night by lamplight while Ilo pretended to sleep sitting up, which is a skill some men develop and none should be proud of. The tide was already falling when we dropped anchor over the spot, the Mariposa swinging her stern into the swell like an old horse turning its rump to the wind.

"Rules," I said, holding up fingers. "One: you stay on the surface with the line. Two: if I tug twice, haul me up and don't ask questions until my regulator's out. Three: if anything happens down there that I can't explain—" I paused, because rule three had started as a joke and turned into something else on the way out of my mouth. "If something feels wrong, we leave. No site is worth either of us."

Ilo looked at me for a long moment. He had that way of looking at people, like he was reading a document he'd been given clearance for but hadn't expected to receive. "Agreed," he said finally. "And a fourth rule: you come back up."

"That's not a rule, that's just diving."

"It's a rule," he said quietly, checking the buckle on my tank strap with hands that knew what they were doing. "I've buried enough divers in my head. I'd rather not add one I've actually met."

There are worse things a man can say before handing you your fins. I couldn't think of one at the time, so I bit back whatever smart remark was queued up and went over the side.

* * *

The first ten feet were ordinary. That's the thing about the sea—it doesn't announce its moods. You drop through warm, sun-stippled water where small fish scatter around your bubbles like gossip around a dropped tray, and everything says *nothing here, nothing here, move along*. The reef below me was healthy enough, brain corals and fan corals doing their slow stone business, a hawksbill turtle giving me the flat, unimpressed look turtles have perfected over a hundred million years of being unimpressed.

Then the bottom changed.

It happened between one kick and the next: the living reef gave way to dead rock, pale and scoured, as if something enormous had dragged itself across it and taken the color with it. Not recent damage—this was old, decades at least, the kind of scar the ocean grows over slowly and never quite heals. My mother would have called it a wound. She had opinions about wounds, most of them inconvenient.

I followed the scar downhill.

The light dimmed as I descended, that green-gray twilight where colors go to die and everything becomes a rumor of itself. Twenty meters. Thirty. The slope steepened, and the dead rock gave way to boulders the size of buses, tumbled in a jumble that no storm could account for. Storms arrange things. This looked *unarranged*, if there's such a thing—like the seabed had been dealt a bad hand and shoved the table over.

And then I saw the opening.

It was less a cave mouth than a wound in the hillside, a black slot maybe four meters wide at the top narrowing toward the base, framed by those impossible boulders like a door someone had tried to hide behind furniture. Sand funneled toward it in thin ribbons, drawn inward by a breathing current—the cave inhaling and exhaling with the tide, slow and patient, the way big things breathe.

My mother dove here.

The thought arrived without permission and settled behind my sternum like a swallowed stone. Her map, her annotations, her little penciled depth soundings that I'd assumed were from somewhere else, anywhere else, some other chapter of her long running-away life. But she'd stood—or swum—exactly where I was floating, and she'd known this hole in the world well enough to mark it.

"Ask about the quiet ones." I said it into my regulator, which turned it into a stream of useless bubbles. Some questions you need air to ask properly.

I swam closer.

The breathing current tugged at me, gentle but insistent, the way a dog tugs a leash—not asking, exactly. More like insisting on an introduction. I kept one hand on the rock face and let myself drift along the lip of the opening, running my glove over stone that was smoother than it should have been, polished in long vertical streaks.

Polished by rope. By lines run in and out of this mouth hundreds of times, wearing grooves into the rock the way water wears grooves into everything, given enough years and enough purpose.

Somebody had used this door. Somebody had used it a lot.

That was when I saw the boot.

It was wedged in a cleft of rock two meters below the lip, toe-down, sole facing me—a heavy rubber dive boot, the kind issued by governments and worn by people who fill out forms before they drown. The laces were still tied. Beside it, snagged on a nub of coral that had no business growing this deep, hung a scrap of jacket fabric, sun-bleached to the color of old bone, torn along a seam in a way that suggested urgency rather than accident.

I hovered there, my bubbles ticking upward past my face, and made myself look at the boot the way you make yourself look at anything true: all at once, no peeking through fingers.

A boot doesn't come off a foot by itself. Not underwater, not tied. Either the man who wore it pulled it off in a hurry—kicked free to swim faster, or to squeeze through something narrow—or the foot came out of it some other way, and the sea, which is tidy in its own terrible fashion, sorted the pieces.

I thought about the camp ashore. Tents pitched. Meals half-eaten. A radio faithfully calling into the empty morning. Five sets of gear, five cups, five everything—and not one track leading away.

Five tracks leading *here* instead.

Twice, I told myself. Tug twice and I'm out. But my hand stayed on the line and my body stayed in the water, because there's a thing that happens to salvage divers—we're all of us part cat, part accountant, and part fool, and the fool part had noticed that the torn jacket scrap was navy blue with a white stencil fragment visible along the tear. Letters. Maybe a name. Maybe the kind of proof that turns a strange story into a dangerous one.

I eased closer, working my dive light around to catch the stencil at an angle.

And that was when the light died.

No flicker, no warning dimming—just gone, mid-beam, like a candle pinched by a wet thumb. Total dark closed over me the way cold water closes over skin, immediate and total and intimate. I've dived dark water before; every wreck diver has. There's dark and there's *dark*, and this was the second kind, the kind with weight, pressing on my eyeballs like a thumb testing fruit.

Okay, Sable. Breathe. Rule two exists for a reason.

I found the guide line with my left hand—thank God and Ilo both for nylon—and got my right on the rock. I was orienting myself, counting my breaths the way you do, four counts in, six counts out, when the sound reached me.

Not a sound, exactly. A displacement. Something below me in the black moved, and the water told me about it before any ear could—the pressure shifted against my chest, a slow rolling push, the way the air moves when a large animal passes close in a dark room. Not fast. Not hunting-fast. Just *big*, and near, and unhurried, the way things are unhurried when they have never once needed to hurry.

My mother's voice showed up uninvited, the way it always does at the worst moments, calm as a weather report: *When the deep moves and you didn't, it wasn't the current.*

Thanks, Mom. Very helpful. Terrific timing.

I pressed myself flat against the rock face, every muscle doing that thing muscles do when the mind says *still* and the spine says *are you insane*, and I waited. The displacement came again—closer now, or maybe just lower, rising out of the throat of the cave in a slow spiral. My bubbles streamed upward and something down there seemed to notice them, seemed to consider them, the pressure shifting with a deliberateness that felt horribly like attention.

Whale, I told myself. Big grouper. Whale shark, even—they come into caves, don't they? Don't they?

Nothing that answers its own questions honestly ever likes the answer.

The movement stopped. The water held still around me, holding its breath, or making me hold mine—I genuinely couldn't tell anymore whose breath it was. And in that absolute silence and absolute dark, with a dead man's boot hanging a meter from my knee and something vast idling in the black below, I understood something with total clarity:

Whatever took the survey team hadn't taken them anywhere. They'd come *in*. People flee into holes in the world when what's outside is worse. The boot, the jacket, the radio calling to nobody—all of it pointed the same direction, and the direction was *down*, into the breathing dark, where five adults had decided a flooded cave was the safer option.

You don't choose a cave over open sky unless the sky is full of teeth.

The thing below exhaled—or the cave did, hard to say which—and the current shoved past me toward the surface, dragging a curtain of silt off the bottom. In the stirred murk, faintly, absurdly, my dead dive light caught a glint. Not from the water. From inside the cleft where the boot sat wedged.

Metal. Reflective tape, maybe. Or a buckle. Or—

The glint vanished as the silt settled. The pressure below began to move again, sliding, circling, patient as arithmetic.

Rule two, Sable. Rule two, rule two, rule two.

I tugged the line twice, hard, and kicked for the light above with the specific, undignified speed of a woman who has just learned something about herself, namely that her curiosity has limits after all, and they are located approximately one large unknown animal below a drowned man's boot.

* * *

I broke the surface gasping, and Ilo's hands were on my tank strap before I'd finished spitting out my regulator, hauling me to the ladder with a brisk efficiency that would have annoyed me under any other circumstances.

"Your light," he said.

"Died. Full dark, no warning." I got a leg over the gunwale and sat down hard on the deck, dripping, my heart still going like a bilge pump in a flood. "Also, there's something down there."

He went very still. "Define 'something.'"

"I can't. It was big. It moved like it had nowhere to be and all day to not be there. It circled under me in the cave throat." I pulled my hood back, wringing seawater out of my braid onto the deck, mostly to give my hands a job that wasn't shaking. "And Ilo—the boot. A surveyor's boot, tied, wedged in the rocks. Jacket scrap next to it. Navy stencil, partially legible. And the rock around the cave mouth is grooved smooth from ropes. Decades of ropes. People have been going in and out of that hole since before I was born."

Ilo crouched in front of me, and for once he didn't look like a man choosing his words. He looked like a man who'd run out of them. "The team went in," he said. "They weren't taken. They went in."

"They ran in. There's a difference, and the difference is whatever they were running from was worse than a pitch-black flooded cave with a monster in the basement." I looked at him, really looked, and the question came out before I could dress it up. "What did your navy put down there, Ilo?"

Something crossed his face—there and gone, quick as the glint in the rocks. "I don't know," he said. "But I think I know who does. And I think—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think your mother asked the same question. And I think that's why her chart stops where it stops."

The wind had come around while I was under. I'd felt it even at thirty meters, a low shudder through the water, the sea clearing its throat. Now it walked across the surface in long gray strides, and the barometer needle on the bulkhead clock—my mother's barometer, of course, because the woman never met an instrument she trusted anyone else to own—had begun its slow, sick slide toward the red.

Monsoon. Early. Because of course.

Ilo followed my eyes to the needle and said nothing, which told me plenty. We had maybe two days of decent weather. Possibly one. And somewhere below us, in a breathing hole in the seafloor, five people—or the evidence of five people—waited in the dark with something that circled.

"We'll need better lights," I said. "Redundant everything. And I want to know what that thing down there is before I go back."

Ilo nodded slowly. Then he reached into his jacket—the dry bag he never let out of arm's length—and drew out a waterproof case I hadn't seen before. He set it on the deck between us without opening it.

"Then it's time," he said, "I told you what I actually know."

Above us, the first thunder of the season rolled down off the empty island, and the Mariposa pulled at her anchor like a dog that smells rain coming.
