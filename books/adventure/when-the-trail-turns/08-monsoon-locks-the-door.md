## 8. Monsoon Locks the Door

*Sable*

The barometer on the Mariposa's bulkhead had been falling all afternoon like a stone dropped down a well, and by the time the first true gust hit, Sable had already stopped pretending the storm was going to be polite about its schedule.

"It's early," she said, standing in the cockpit with her hair whipping sideways. "The forecast said tomorrow night."

Ilo looked up from the anchor line he was doubling, his expression doing something complicated between apology and arithmetic. "Forecasts are opinions. Barometers are confessions."

"That's very poetic for a man who won't tell me his last name."

"Ilo is my last name. Also my first name. It simplifies paperwork." He hauled another fathom of line and cleated it off. "We should move her into the lee of that point before the wind backs around. If she drags here, she drags onto the reef shelf."

Sable had already been thinking it, which annoyed her slightly — she liked being the one to think of things first aboard her own boat. "Ten minutes," she said. "I want the dinghy secured and anything loose below lashed down. My mother lost a sextant overboard in a squall once because she left it on the chart table. She mourned it longer than she mourned two husbands."

"Your mother sounds like a woman of clear priorities."

"She was a woman of no priorities except the next horizon. It's why I'm like this." She didn't elaborate on what *this* was, and Ilo had the good grace not to ask.

They got the Mariposa moving in the last gray light, engine coughing through the first rain like an old man clearing his throat. The anchorage behind the point was shallower but sheltered, ringed by black volcanic rock and a fringe of mangroves that bent and hissed as the front came across the water. Sable set two anchors in a V — her good CQR off the bow, and the battered fisherman they'd pulled from the survey camp's abandoned gear locker, because the dead and the vanished have no use for ground tackle and the living are fools to be squeamish.

By full dark the storm had them.

It didn't arrive so much as *occupy* — wind first, then rain so hard and horizontal it sounded like gravel thrown at the cabin sides, then the sea getting up inside the anchorage itself, short steep chop that slapped the hull in irregular applause. Sable sat below with a hand lamp swinging from a hook, listening to the boat talk to her. Every vessel has a voice in bad weather, and after eleven years she knew the Mariposa's vocabulary: the groan of the rigging was complaint, the rattle of the galley drawer was grievance, and the deep structural sigh along the keel was philosophy.

What she heard at two in the morning was none of those things.

It was a new sound. A scrape, rhythmic, coming up through the sole boards near the head of the berth. Rock against wood. Rock against *her* wood.

She was on deck in oilskins before Ilo could wake fully, and when he stumbled up into the cockpit thirty seconds later, blinking against the rain, she was already hauling on the anchor rode hand over hand, reading it like a pulse.

"We're dragging," she shouted. "Both of them. Slowly. We're walking backward onto the coral shelf."

Ilo didn't waste breath on questions. He went forward, and together they fed out more scope on the CQR, paying line into a sea that wanted to take all of it and give back nothing. For twenty minutes that felt like twenty years they fought the boat's slow retreat — engine in reverse at half throttle, prop churning foam, the fisherman anchor resetting with a shudder Sable felt through her boots like a heartbeat finding its rhythm again.

And then the sea simply picked the Mariposa up and put her down wrong.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the terrible part. No movie-moment wave, no screaming. Just a lift, a slide, a grinding *shudder* along the starboard side forward of the keel — the sound of coral, which does not forgive, introducing itself to fiberglass, which does not forget.

Sable went below with the lamp while Ilo held the wheel. She knelt in the bilge, in three inches of water that should have been one, and watched it rise.

"Seam's opened," she called up, keeping her voice level the way you keep your hand level when a bird lands on it. "Forward starboard, near where we grounded. It's seeping, not pouring. We can pump it."

"How much pumping?"

"All night. All week, probably." She sat back on her heels in the wet dark and laughed — actually laughed, a short ugly bark of a thing. "You know what my mother used to say? 'A leaking boat just means the ocean wants to read your mail.'"

"Was your mother always this comforting?"

"She died out there somewhere, so draw your own conclusions."

* * *

*Ilo*

Ilo took the dawn watch, or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of wet slate. The storm hadn't peaked yet — he knew these systems, had watched them from navy decks in another life, had helped secure cargo hatches against winds exactly like this one. That memory arrived uninvited and he set it down again, the way you set down a hot pan.

He found Sable asleep sitting up in the pilot berth, one hand still resting on the bilge pump handle like a rider on a very small horse. Her face in sleep was younger and harder at once, and there was a crease between her brows that looked permanent, like a fold in a well-read map.

He worked quietly. Bailed, pumped, checked the anchors, made coffee on the gimbaled stove with the concentration of a man defusing something. When the smell woke her, she surfaced with the instantaneous alertness of someone who'd slept alone in strange harbors too long to do otherwise.

"Coffee," she said, suspicious. "You're trying to soften me up before asking me something."

"Yes. I'm going to ask how bad the hull is."

"And the coffee makes me answer honestly instead of optimistically."

"The coffee makes you answer at all. You strike me as someone who communicates primarily through sarcasm until properly caffeinated."

She took the mug. Drank. Considered. "It's bad enough that I can't take her to sea until it's glassed over, and I can't glass it until the sea stops trying to come in and inspect the work. So: days. Maybe a week if the storm stalls. And the fuel situation—" she nodded at the gauge, which sat at a quarter like a guilty dog. "We burned a lot holding position last night. And the radio died about four this morning. Not the antenna. The set itself. Water got in somewhere."

"So we're trapped."

"We're *staying.* There's a difference. Trapped implies somebody did it to us." She said it lightly, but Ilo heard the thing underneath it, the same thing he'd been carrying since Puerto Hondo: the sense of being selected. Of the weather, the wreck, the vanished team, the whole archipelago arranging itself around them like fingers closing.

He said none of that. Instead he said, "There's a cove half a mile south. I saw it yesterday before the front came in — high ground above it, and what looked like an overhang in the cliff face. If we're going to be marooned, better ashore than bailing."

"You've been scouting escape routes since we anchored."

"I've been scouting *options* since I was nineteen. It's kept me alive and friendless."

Something moved across her face — not quite sympathy, but the neighboring country. "Well," she said, draining the mug. "Let's go make friends with a cliff."

* * *

*Sable*

Getting ashore was its own small war. They ferried gear in the dinghy through surf that had opinions, Sable rowing and Ilo bailing with a bucket, both of them soaked past meaninglessness within the first thirty seconds. The Mariposa stayed on her double anchor, riding easier now in the settled swell, her bilge pump running off the battery bank with a patient, mechanical persistence Sable chose to interpret as loyalty rather than indictment.

The overhang was real — a shelf of basalt jutting out over the shingle beach, dry underneath, big enough for two people and their salvaged empire: a tarp, two sleeping bags, the camp stove, a case of canned food liberated from the survey team's stores (with Sable leaving a note under a rock listing everything taken, because even looting deserved bookkeeping), a first aid kit, and Ilo's mysterious aluminum case, which he carried personally and never let out of arm's reach.

By midday they had a camp. By afternoon they had a routine. And by evening, with the storm roaring overhead like a train that never finished passing, they had a conversation neither of them wanted to start and both of them needed.

"Three weeks," Sable said, poking the stove. "That's the minimum if the monsoon behaves. Six if it doesn't. We have food for ten days, fuel for maybe forty hours of engine, and a boat that drinks seawater."

"Then we ration, we repair, and we wait."

"'We.'" She looked at him across the flame. "You keep saying that word like it's settled. I picked you up as a fare, Ilo. Fares get dropped off. Fares don't help bail."

"No," he agreed. "But fares also don't decrypt government data logs, and this one doesn't leave a woman alone on an island where a survey team walked into the ocean." He turned his hands palm-up, an odd little gesture of surrender. "You could order me off. I wouldn't argue. But I'd only be in the next cove over, doing the same things, worse prepared."

Sable studied him for a long moment. Rain drummed the tarp overhead. Somewhere out in the dark, the Mariposa's pump thumped away like a distant heart.

"My mother had a saying about strangers," she said finally. "'A stranger is just a story you haven't been told yet.' She also married a man she met on a bus in Cartagena, so her judgment on the subject is historically questionable."

"I'll try to be one of the good stories."

"You'll try to be a *true* one. That's worth more." She threw him a can of sardines. "Dinner. And tomorrow you're helping me careen the hull in whatever calm window we get. If we're stuck here, we're stuck here with a sound boat, or as close to one as fiberglass and stubbornness can manage."

* * *

*Ilo*

He woke once in the black heart of the night to find Sable standing at the edge of the overhang, looking out at the storm-lit water, her silhouette sharp against the occasional white flash of breaking surf.

"The cave's out there," she said, without turning. She didn't have to explain which cave. "Low tide opens it, the logs said. And the tide doesn't care that there's a monsoon sitting on top of us."

"No," Ilo said. "It doesn't."

"And the navy will come back for whatever's left in there. Before the weather clears, if they're smart."

"They were never smart. They were funded. There's a difference, but it goes further."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Get some sleep, Ilo. Whatever window the tide gives us, I want us ready for it. And I want this hull patched first, because I am not swimming back from that cave."

He lay back down and listened to the storm grind the island's edges rounder, and thought about how circumstances choose people — how a missing team, a dying radio, a cracked seam, and a falling barometer had conspired to lock two strangers behind a wall of weather with nothing but time, truth, and each other. He had spent thirty years avoiding exactly this kind of entrapment.

Outside, the surf boomed against the reef like a door being tested.

Four hours either side of low tide, the logs had said. *It opens at low tide.*

Somewhere out in all that violence, something was waiting to be opened.
