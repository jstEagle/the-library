## 9. Ilo's Confession

The rain had settled into a rhythm by nightfall—not the furious drumming of the storm's arrival, but a long, patient hammering, like someone determined to teach the roof of the world a lesson it refused to learn. Ilo sat near the fire they'd built in the lee of a fallen palm, close enough to feel it on his face, far enough that the smoke wouldn't find him. Old habits. On the *Almirante Reyes*—no relation to the surveyor, he'd checked, twice—the men who sat closest to the galley stove were the ones who got noticed, and being noticed was rarely a gift.

Sable was across the fire, mending something. She was always mending something. A strap, a net, a length of hose, her own patience. Tonight it was a dive glove, and she worked the needle through salt-stiffened leather with the concentration of a woman defusing ordnance.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive, but in my experience they usually are."

He almost smiled. That was the trouble with Sable Villalobos—she made smiling feel safe, and nothing about what he had to say tonight was safe. He'd been circling it for two days now, since the data logs, since *it opens at low tide*, since she'd come up from the anomaly site white as bone and said only, "There's a cave down there, and someone left a boot." He owed her more than a boot.

He fed a piece of driftwood to the fire and watched the flames take it.

"My name isn't Ilo."

She looked up at that. The needle stopped.

"It's Ildefonso. Ildefonso Marquez Vidal. But everyone called me Foncho, and then, when I got older and quieter, just Fon. Ilo is—" He turned his hands over, palms up, an offering or a surrender. "Ilo is what I made out of what was left."

"Okay," Sable said slowly. "Ildefonso."

"Foncho, if we're going to be friends. Nobody has ever trusted a man named Ildefonso. It sounds like a man who sells you insurance against your own funeral."

That earned him half a smile before she set it aside. "And why does a man named Foncho have a fake name and triple rate to spend on a boat ride to a haunted island?"

He had rehearsed this. In the weeks since Puerto Hondo, in the squalls, in the long grey hours of the storm, he had composed and recomposed the telling—short version, long version, version with dignity, version without. All of them collapsed now, sitting here, with the rain coming down and her eyes on him. So he told it the way it actually happened, which was badly.

* * *

"In 1979 I was nineteen years old and stupid in the specific way navies love. Strong back, good swimmer, no questions. They put me aboard the *Bautista*, a transport out of the eastern yards. Officially we carried engineering equipment. Dredging gear. Materials for coastal works." He paused. "You know how a lie works on a ship? Everyone knows it's a lie, and everyone agrees not to know it, and after a while the not-knowing gets so heavy the whole hull rides lower in the water."

Sable said nothing. She'd put down the glove. Good. He wanted her hands still for this, though God knew why—it wasn't as if she could catch the words and hold them.

"The cargo that season wasn't dredging gear. It was people. Forty-one of them. Political detainees, though the paperwork said otherwise, because the paperwork always says otherwise. The order came down from above my pay grade and above my imagination: scuttle the vessel in deep water off the archipelago. No witnesses. No record. The crew who handled the loading were rotated off before departure. I stayed because I was young and obedient, and obedience felt like virtue right up until the moment it didn't."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the surf worked the reef, patient as a debt collector.

"There was a hold," he said. "Compartment C. They sealed it from outside. I stood on deck while they did it. I could hear—" His voice did something he hadn't authorized. He waited it out. "I could hear knocking. At first. Then not knocking. Then a kind of sound I've spent forty years failing to describe, and I won't try tonight, because you'll hear it anyway if you go where I think you're going, and I won't have put it in your ears ahead of time. Some things should arrive unannounced."

Sable's face had gone very still. Not horrified—that would come later, he knew, in pieces, over months—but still, the way water goes still before it decides whether to freeze.

"We reported the position as twelve hundred meters, off the shelf edge," he went on. "That was the official log. The actual scuttling happened in shallower water—we had engine trouble, or the captain wanted it shallower, the stories changed depending on who was drinking. Shallow enough that a wreck survey might find it. Shallow enough that instruments might sing about it. For years I told myself the deep lie was mercy. Nobody would ever look there. The dead would keep their privacy."

"And then the navy sent a survey team," Sable said quietly.

"And then the navy sent a survey team." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry rusted thing. "Forty years I carried it, and the punchline is bureaucracy. Somebody digitized the old logs. Somebody cross-referenced. Somebody decided the site needed 'environmental assessment' before it became a hazard—and somebody else, higher up, realized what a hazard it actually was. To careers. To reputations. To the whole quiet architecture of things nobody wants named."

"So the team found it first."

"They found it first. And then, one night between check-ins, they vanished, and their government issued no missing persons report, and a civilian salvage diver got paid triple rate to forget a wreck whose serial numbers had been ground off." He met her eyes. "Yes. I know about your contract. I'm the reason the numbers were ground off, Sable. Or my service is. I helped write the manual they used."

* * *

The rain filled the silence for a while. Sable got up, crossed to the supply crate, and came back with two cups and the dregs of the coffee tin. She made it strong and bad, the way coffee tastes when it's mostly an excuse to hold something warm, and handed him one without ceremony.

"Go on," she said. "You're not done. Men who are done don't look like they're waiting to be shot."

"I could stop."

"You could. But then I'd spend all night inventing worse things than whatever you're not saying, and I promise you my imagination has better funding than your conscience."

He drank the terrible coffee. It helped, the way punishment helps.

"Afterward," he said, "I left the service. That's the part people call brave, leaving. It wasn't brave. It was arithmetic—I counted what I'd seen against what I could live with, and the sum didn't work. I went into marine surveying. Civilian. I got very good at finding wrecks, which is either penance or compulsion, depending on the day. I married. My wife, Elena, used to say I slept like a man listening for knocking." His throat tightened around the name and he let it, briefly. "She died eleven years ago. Cancer. Before she went she told me to go back. She said, *Foncho, you've been standing on that deck for thirty years. Either get off the ship or go below and open the hatch.*"

"That's a hell of a thing to leave someone," Sable said. There was no judgment in it. Just the flat wonder of a woman hearing another person's life laid open.

"She was like that. Elena believed guilt was only useful if it moved you somewhere." He looked into the fire. "So when the survey team's schedule crossed my desk last spring—anonymously; I have old friends who owe me and some who owe other people—I knew what it meant before I finished reading it. Coordinates within four kilometers of where I stood on that deck at twenty-two hundred hours with my hands over my ears. I hired the first captain who'd take me, which was you, which was luck, or providence, or the universe's sense of humor. You were the only skipper in Puerto Hondo whose boat was headed roughly toward the place I've been running from my entire adult life."

"And the map," Sable said.

Ah. There it was.

"When it fell from its case," he said carefully, "I recognized the hand."

"You've seen my mother's charts before?"

"No." He set down the cup. "I've seen *that chart*. Thirty-eight years ago. A woman came to the naval yard office asking about charter permits for the Serena group. Dark hair, sunburned, a laugh that arrived before she did. She had a half-finished map of the archipelago rolled up under her arm, and she asked questions—too many, too precise—and three days later the office received a directive that no civilian traffic would be licensed in those waters. Indefinitely. I remember her because she argued with the clerk for twenty minutes and won, somehow, and because when she left she looked at me—young idiot in a dress uniform holding the door—and said, *They'll tell you it's munitions. It's never munitions.*"

The fire cracked. Sable hadn't moved. Her breath had gone shallow in a way he recognized from divers, from men underwater trying to make a small amount of air do a large amount of living.

"What was her name?" he asked gently.

"Mara." Barely a sound. "Mara Villalobos. She dove. She surveyed. She—" Sable pressed the heel of her hand against one eye, briefly, hard, the way you press on a wound to slow the bleeding. "She disappeared when I was nine. Everyone said she ran off. Everyone said she was restless, that restlessness was hereditary, that I should watch myself." She lowered the hand. Her eyes were wet and furious and luminous in the firelight. "Her map has an island circled. One word written next to it. *Ask about the quiet ones.* I thought it meant locals. I thought it meant gossip."

"It might mean the dead," Ilo said. "Or it might mean the men who made them quiet."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. This was the thing he had feared most—not anger, not disgust, but this: the moment when a person recalculates everything you are, and you sit in the firelight waiting to find out what the new number is.

Sable picked up the dive glove. Turned it over. Put it down again.

"Say the rest," she said. "The part you've been chewing since the logs. I can hear it rattling."

He nodded. "The team found the hold. The data proves it—sonar returns inside the compartment, air pocket readings, and that final note. Which means they opened it. Which means they saw what I heard through forty years of steel plating. And then they vanished, and the navy's response was not a search. It was a patrol skiff sweeping the coast with searchlights, and crates being loaded at low tide." He watched her absorb that. "I need you to understand what I am, Sable. I'm not a rescuer who happens to have a past. I'm the past, come back to finish its sentence. Everything that's happening on that reef is happening because men like me buried it, and men like me are the only ones who know where the shovel is."

"You were nineteen," Sable said.

"That's not absolution."

"No," she agreed. "It's context. Different thing." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, and studied him across the fire with an expression he couldn't file under anything—he'd have to build a new drawer for it. "My mother spent her whole life being told she ran away. I spent mine being told I would. And you've spent yours being told—by yourself, mostly—that you're the cargo's jailer." She shrugged. "Seems to me the navy's very good at writing people out of their own stories. Grounds the serial numbers off them, too."

He blinked. Of all the responses he had rehearsed defenses against, metaphor had not been among them.

"Elena would have liked you," he managed. "She also weaponized metaphors at tired men."

"Somebody has to. You people just sit there being tragic otherwise." But her voice caught on the end of it, and she looked away, out at the rain, and when she spoke again it was quieter. "The boot I found. Wedged in the rocks at the cave mouth. Survey-issue. Torn jacket nearby." She swallowed. "If my mother left markers in that cave—if she was diving these waters before I could walk—then she knew. She knew what was down there, and she kept coming back, and then she stopped coming back anywhere."

"Yes."

"Then we're not salvaging a wreck tomorrow, Foncho." She said the name deliberately, testing it, and something in his chest shifted like ballast settling. "We're finishing two sentences. Hers and yours."

* * *

The fire burned down to coals and they fed it again, and the talk turned practical, because grief can only carry so much weight before it demands a task list. Air supply. Tide tables. The narrow window when the storm's eye-wall passed far enough north to flatten the swell. Sable sketched the cave mouth from memory in the ash at the fire's edge, and Ilo corrected her angles from a memory forty years old, and between them, drawn in charcoal and guilt and stubbornness, a route began to take shape.

It was nearly midnight when she finally banked the fire and paused, one hand on the tarp.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking at him, "the knocking sound you couldn't describe. You don't have to. I've heard it once—off a wreck in the Sunda basin, hull gas escaping, everybody said. Everybody said." She ducked out into the rain, and her voice drifted back, half-lost in the drumming. "Get some sleep, Foncho. Tomorrow we ask the quiet ones ourselves."

Ilo lay back and listened to the storm work the island, and for the first time in forty years, the sound beneath the sound—the sea moving against stone, patient, insistent—did not feel like accusation.

It felt like a door, opening at low tide.
