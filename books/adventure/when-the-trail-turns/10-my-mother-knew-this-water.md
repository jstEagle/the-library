## 10. My Mother Knew This Water

The rain had settled into that monsoon rhythm that wasn't quite a storm and wasn't quite mercy—a steady gray drumming on the Mariposa's cabin roof, the kind of weather that made a person either go quietly mad or get very honest with herself. I chose honesty, mostly because going mad required energy I didn't have after two days of bailing.

Ilo sat across the chart table from me, drying the survey team's data drive in a nest of rice like it was an injured bird he'd found. He'd been at it for an hour, turning the grains over with a patience that struck me as either professional or pathological.

"You're staring at that chart again," he said without looking up.

"I'm studying it."

"You've been studying it since yesterday. At some point studying becomes brooding."

"There's a difference?"

"Brooding has more sighing." He glanced up. "You're due."

I ignored him, which was my right as captain, and spread my mother's chart flat under the lamp, weighting the corners with a coffee mug, a wrench, a tin of biscuits Ilo kept eating from despite my warnings, and a coil of shock cord.

Here's what I knew about my mother's map, or thought I knew: she'd drawn it over years, maybe decades, in that cramped left-slanting hand of hers, the letters leaning like they were walking into wind. She'd marked depths in fathoms where charts said meters. She'd noted currents with little arrows and comments in the margins—"lies," "sulks," "runs mean here"—as if the water were a cast of characters she'd interviewed personally. And she'd circled one island. Isla Serena. With four words beneath it: *ask about the quiet ones.*

For twenty years I'd assumed the circle was a destination she never reached. That was the story I'd built my whole restless life on—the half-finished map, the half-finished woman, the daughter who inherited the unfinished part. It was a good story. It explained why I never stayed anywhere longer than a contract. It even explained why I took jobs salvaging things other people wanted forgotten; you can't be haunted by a place if you never give it time to learn your name.

But last night, unable to sleep with the rain playing drums on the deck above, I'd done something I should have done years ago. I'd overlaid her chart on the modern hydrographic survey Ilo had pulled from the team's files, holding them up to the lamp, and I'd seen it.

Her annotations around Isla Serena weren't guesses. They weren't rumors collected secondhand in some port bar. They were *soundings*. Precise ones. Depths to the inch along the eastern reef shelf, exactly where the sonar anomalies had been recorded. Current timings matched to tide tables from a specific year—Ilo had checked; 1997. And there, in the margin beside the anomaly site, in pencil so faint I'd needed to wet the paper with a fingertip to raise it:

*entrance opens 0.4m below MLW — line runs true from the split rock*

A route line. A *route line* meant someone had laid one. Route lines don't come from imagination. They come from swimming a passage with a reel in your hand, paying out line into the dark because the dark doesn't care how brave you are.

My mother hadn't been headed here. She'd *been* here.

* * *

"You could say something," I said, "instead of watching me have a crisis."

"This is a crisis? It looks like reading."

"It's not reading. It's archaeology. Of my own childhood." I tapped the faint pencil note. "She dove this site, Ilo. Before the navy ever parked a survey team on this island. Before whatever they buried got buried—or after. She knew about the cave entrance. She ran a line into it."

Ilo set down the rice nest with great care, the way he did everything, as though the world might startle. "Are you certain it's her hand?"

"It's her hand. She wrote 'sulks' next to the outer current. Nobody else writes 'sulks' about a current." My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the family trick. "And look at the date reference. Ninety-seven. I was fourteen. She told me she was doing hull surveys out of Cebu that summer. She told me a lot of summers' worth of things that summer."

He was quiet a moment. Outside, the rain thickened, then thinned, breathing. "What did she tell you about this island?"

"Nothing. That's the point. There's nothing. No letter, no logbook page, no story. The map just stops." I turned the chart toward him. "Except now I'm looking at it with different eyes, and it doesn't stop. It stops *deliberately*. See? Everything east of Serena is finished. Detailed. Obsessive, honestly, the woman annotated a jellyfish bloom. But west of the island—" I swept my finger across the blank space, the empty water where the cave mouth waited under its thirty feet of green. "Blank. Not unexplored-blank. *Erased*-blank. You can see ghost impressions where something was rubbed out. She erased her own work."

"Why would she do that?"

"That's the question I've been circling for twenty years, and I think I've been asking it wrong. I always asked why she left. Why we moved. Why she never talked about before." I pressed my palm flat on the chart, over the blank water. "I should have been asking who made her stop talking."

* * *

We went in at slack low water the next morning, because of course we did. I want it noted that I planned to wait—properly wait, plan the dive, check the swell—and then I woke at dawn with the chart's ghost-erasures printed on the inside of my eyelids and Ilo already checking tanks, and neither of us said the thing we were both thinking, which was that waiting had never once run in my family.

The split rock was exactly where her note said it would be, a black wedge of basalt standing off the reef like a doorpost, wearing a wig of barnacles. From its seaward face, at forty centimeters below the waterline, a fissure opened into the reef's shoulder. I went down the anchor line first, Ilo on the surface hose—he wasn't dive-certified by any paper I'd ever seen, but he handled gear like a man remembering rather than learning, and I'd stopped pretending I didn't notice.

Thirty feet down, the green light turned to blue glass. The fissure widened into a slot, and there, hooked through a natural eye of coral rock just inside the lip—

A line. Old nylon, furred with algae, gone stiff as wire, but still there. Still tied off in a bowline my hands recognized before my brain did, because I'd grown up tying that exact bowline, my mother's fingers guiding mine, saying *the rabbit comes around the tree and down the hole*, and every diver in the world ties bowlines but only one person in the world tied them with a doubled tail "so the sea can't unpick your mistakes."

I hung there in the blue, regulator hissing, and let the feeling move through me. It wasn't grief exactly. Grief I knew; grief was an old crewmate by now. This was something stranger—like finding a letter addressed to you in a bottle, except the bottle had been sitting in your own house for twenty years and you'd been using it as a doorstop.

*Ilo,* I signaled, and waved him down the line. He came through the slot awkwardly, all elbows, and when he saw the route line his eyes changed. I watched him read the bowline the way I had. Watched him understand whose it was.

We followed it in.

The tunnel ran level for ten meters, then kinked left and rose, and the water rose with it, thinning from blue to silver, and then we broke the surface into air—a chamber the size of a chapel, hollowed out of the island's bones, lit by a single thread of daylight falling through a crack in the ceiling like the world's most patient spotlight. Stalactites came down in pale curtains. The air smelled of stone and salt and old rope.

And against the far wall, on a natural ledge above the high-tide line: a tin.

I knew the tin. God help me, I knew the tin. A biscuit tin, rusted orange at the corners, with a faded picture of a sailing ship on the lid—the same tin that had lived under the bunk of every boat my mother ever owned, the one I'd emptied into a donation box in Puerto Hondo three years ago because it held nothing but dried-up pens and a fuse and I'd wanted, God forgive me, to travel lighter.

I waded to the ledge and lifted it. Inside: two waterproof matches, a spare dive-light bulb wrapped in wax paper, a stub of pencil, and a folded page torn from a notebook, soft as cloth from damp cycles.

I didn't open it right away. I sat on the ledge with the tin in my lap like a kid with a report card, and Ilo stood dripping in the shallows and gave me the room, because somewhere between the storm and the rope work and the dinghy he had learned exactly how much room to give me, and I hated how grateful I was for that.

Then I opened it.

Her handwriting. Of course her handwriting, leaning into the wind.

*If anyone finds this, the hold is real. Manifest copies are—*

And it stopped. Mid-sentence. Mid-*word*, almost—the pencil stroke dragging off the edge of a letter like a hand pulled away.

Below it, in fresher ink, different pressure, a second line had been added at some later date, smaller, hurried:

*They watch the entrance. Come at low tide. Don't trust the port.*

No signature. No date. Two visits, minimum. Two women—or one woman twice, changed—standing on this same ledge with the same tin, writing notes to whoever came next.

Me. The notes were to me. Or they were to no one, and I had spent twenty years being no one's daughter in exactly the way she'd feared.

"She came back," I heard myself say. My voice bounced around the chapel of stone. "After ninety-seven. After she erased the chart. She came back anyway." I looked at the unfinished sentence. "She was copying the manifest, Ilo. She found the hold. She found what's down there, and she started documenting it, and then—" I stopped, because finishing the sentence out loud would make it a fact, and facts have weight, and I was already carrying enough ballast to sit low in the water.

"And then she stopped," Ilo said quietly.

"People don't stop mid-sentence."

"No," he said. "They don't."

* * *

We sat in the chamber while our tanks rested, passing the biscuit tin back and forth like a peace pipe nobody believed in. Ilo told me, in his sideways way, a little more—not the confession yet, I could feel it banked in him like a fire kept under ash, but the edges of it. That he'd served on a vessel in these waters. That the word "cargo" had a different weight depending on which side of the hatch you stood on. That he'd spent thirty years being paid to forget things and had recently discovered forgetting was a skill that rotted your teeth.

"My mother used to say the sea keeps better records than governments," I said. "I thought it was just something divers say."

"It's something survivors say." He turned the tin lid over, studying the faded ship. "Sable. If she was silenced—if someone made her stop—it means they knew about her. Which means they may know about the map. About you."

"They've had twenty years to come find me. I've been diving their wrecks the whole time. If they cared, I'd have had an accident by now."

"Or they didn't know the map existed until the survey team found the same cave and started asking the same questions." He let that sit. "The team vanished the night after their last log entry. You arrived two days later. You're now sitting inside the thing they vanished for, holding a document trail that leads to a dead woman who was investigating the same crime."

When he put it that way—and Ilo always put things that way, flat and numbered, like a man reading a damage report—it sounded less like destiny and more like a trap snapping shut with excellent timing.

"So what you're saying is I inherited a mystery, a target, and a boat with a leaking seam."

"I'm saying you inherited more than you knew. Which is either very good news or very bad news, and the tide will decide."

I laughed. It surprised both of us. But there it was—somewhere under the fear, under the cracking-open feeling of learning my mother hadn't abandoned this place but had been *taken out of it*, under the vertigo of realizing that every restless year of my life had been built on a story I'd told myself to survive a silence—somewhere under all of it was a fierce, ridiculous relief.

She hadn't run. That was the thing. All those years I'd resented her for running—for hauling us from port to port, never anchoring, never explaining—I'd been resenting a woman for fleeing a fire I never saw. She wasn't restless. She was *watching her six*. And she'd left me the map anyway, in a house full of people who couldn't read it, betting that someday the daughter would grow gills too.

"Well," I said, standing, tucking the note into my dry bag with the tenderness I usually reserved for regulators and engines. "She left a route line, a tool kit, and homework. That's the most maternal thing I've ever heard of."

"You're taking this remarkably well."

"I'm taking it remarkably *wet*. Ask me again when we're on the boat." I looked up at the thread of daylight, at the dark water below it where the tunnel dropped toward the hold and whatever names waited down there in the dark. "Tomorrow, at the next low tide window, we go deeper. We finish her sentence."

Ilo nodded slowly. Then he cocked his head, listening, and held up a hand.

Above us, muffled by stone and water, came a sound I knew from bad contracts and worse harbors: engines. More than one. Running slow, running quiet, the way boats run when they don't want to be heard.

Through the ceiling crack, the patient thread of daylight flickered—as something passed between it and the sky.

I killed my dive light, and in the sudden dark we listened to the navy come looking for what my mother had found first.
