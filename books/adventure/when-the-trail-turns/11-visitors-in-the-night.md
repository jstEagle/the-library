## 11. Visitors in the Night

*Sable*

The fire had been Ilo's idea, and to his credit it had been a good idea right up until the moment it wasn't.

They'd built it small—a nest of driftwood in the lee of a fallen palm, screened by rock on three sides—because the nights after the storm came down cold and wet and the kind of miserable that made a person stupid. Sable had been sitting close enough to feel it on her shins, working a whetstone along her dive knife while Ilo read the survey team's decrypted logs for the fourth time, when he went suddenly still.

"Kill it," he said.

She didn't ask why. That was one thing she'd learned about him in the weeks since Puerto Hondo: he didn't waste warnings. She kicked sand over the coals and pressed them flat with her boot heel, and in the sudden dark she heard what he'd heard—a low diesel burble, out past the reef line, where no honest boat had any business being at half past midnight.

"Skiff," she whispered.

"Navy." He was already moving, folding the tarp over their gear with hands that knew exactly how fast quiet could be done. "Get the Mariposa under way. Mangroves, north end. I'll bring the rest."

"The rest is two crates and a radio that doesn't work."

"Then you'll be quick."

She almost smiled at that, which was its own kind of madness, because her heart was going like a bilge pump and her mouth still wanted to joke. Her mother's restlessness again—the Thackeray inheritance that turned fear into motion instead of freezing it. She slipped down the beach to where the Mariposa sat at anchor, and as she swung herself aboard she looked back once at the water beyond the reef.

The searchlight came on like an accusation.

It swept the coast in a long white arc, slow and thorough, the way a man pats down a room he already knows has someone hiding in it. The beam crawled across the cave entrance below the headland—*their* cave entrance, the one her mother's route line led into—and paused there, patient as a held breath, before moving on.

Sable started the engine at the lowest idle the old girl would tolerate and ghosted north along the shore, running dark, running shallow, steering by memory and moon-shadow because running lights were a confession. Behind her the light swept again. Ahead, the mangroves stood up out of the black water like a crowd of people who had decided, all at once, to mind their own business.

She nosed the bow into the channel between the roots and killed the engine. The boat drifted the last few meters and kissed the mud with a sound like a kiss itself—soft, apologetic. She tied off to a root thick as her waist and crouched low in the cockpit, and then there was nothing to do but wait, which was the part of salvage work nobody put in the brochures.

Ilo came through the trees ten minutes later carrying everything they owned, which fit in two arms, which said something about their lives that Sable chose not to examine just then.

"They're at the cave," he breathed, settling beside her among the roots. He handed her the binoculars without being asked. He'd learned that too—that she wanted to see things herself before she believed them.

She raised the glasses.

* * *

*Ilo*

He watched her watch, and tried to read the shape of her silence.

Through the gap in the mangrove canopy, maybe six hundred meters across the black water, the navy skiff sat at anchor off the headland with its searchlight pinned on the rocks below the cave mouth. It was a rigid-hull inflatable, the kind the service used for interdiction work—fast, shallow-drafted, armed with a mounted gun that was currently pointed at nothing, which somehow made it worse. Four sailors in the boat. Two more wading at the cave entrance with the water up to their thighs, passing objects hand to hand.

Crates. Sealed crates, olive-drab, stenciled with markings Ilo could not read at this distance but did not need to. He knew those crates. He had loaded their grandfathers onto a different ship in a different decade, back when he was twenty-two years old and very good at not asking questions, and the Navy had thanked him for it with a commendation and a lifetime of dreams he couldn't say out loud.

"They're cleaning up," Sable said quietly. Not a question either.

"Yes."

"After the monsoon. They waited for the weather so no civilian boats would be out here to see—" She stopped. Adjusted the focus wheel with a small, furious precision. "Look at them. They're being *careful*. They're handling those crates like they're full of eggs."

"Because whatever's inside matters more than the men who carried it here," Ilo said. "That's the arithmetic. It's always been the arithmetic."

She lowered the binoculars and looked at him, and even in the dark he could feel the weight of the look. Weeks ago that look would have been pure suspicion—the salvager sizing up the stranger who paid in questions. Now there was something else under it, worn smooth by shared storms and shared secrets: partnership, or the early scaffolding of it.

"You knew they'd come," she said.

"I hoped they wouldn't. I expected they would." He kept his voice level. "A team vanishes. The Navy sends a patrol to look for them. The patrol finds the cave. The patrol reports up the chain. And somewhere up the chain, someone does the math and realizes the survivors—if there are survivors—are worth more dead than found." He made himself say the next part plainly, because she deserved plain. "This is how it was done the first time, Sable. A ship, a sealed hold, a crew ordered to forget. Nothing new. Just better boats."

In the mangroves around them, the crabs went on with their small loud lives, indifferent to the history repeating itself six hundred meters away. Somewhere overhead a night heron complained once and thought better of it.

On the skiff, a sailor's torch blinked twice toward shore. The wading men passed up another crate. Ilo counted them as they went—one, two, three, four, five—and felt his stomach drop through the bottom of the boat.

"That's five," he murmured. "Reyes' logs listed nine crates recovered from the hold before the team went missing."

"So four are still in there." Sable's jaw set. "Or the count's wrong."

"Or some of what they found, they hid." He allowed himself the smallest grimace. "Survey teams are trained to duplicate records. If Reyes suspected what she'd stumbled onto—and she did, her last log entry makes that clear—she may have cached evidence somewhere the Navy won't think to look."

"Like inside a flooded cave behind a wreck only my mother ever mapped."

"Like that," Ilo agreed, "if we're lucky. And we have been neither lucky nor unlucky lately. We've been *busy*, which is different."

That earned him a huff that was almost a laugh. Progress. He filed it away, the way he filed everything now—carefully, guiltily, like a man saving receipts for a debt he intended to pay in full.

* * *

*Sable*

The loading took two hours, and Sable watched every minute of it, because watching felt like doing something and doing nothing felt like agreeing.

The sailors worked with the unhurried competence of men on a familiar chore. One of them smoked. Another checked his watch. At one point two of them laughed about something, and the sound carried thin across the water, ordinary as anything, and that was the part that made her knuckles go white around the binoculars. Monsters, in her experience, mostly laughed about lunch.

Four crates remained unaccounted for. Four crates, plus a missing survey team, plus a boot and a torn jacket wedged in the rocks at the cave mouth, plus her mother's unfinished note sitting in its tin like a sentence waiting for its verb. Sable laid the pieces side by side in her head the way she'd lay out salvage on a deck, looking for the pattern, the load-bearing piece.

The pattern said: *they're not done.*

"They'll be back for the rest," she whispered.

"Within days," Ilo said. "Maybe sooner. The monsoon window works for them same as it works for us—no witnesses on the water."

"And the team? If any of them are alive in there—"

"Then every hour we spend hiding is an hour they spend breathing bad air in the dark." He said it evenly, but she heard the cost of it in the flatness. He'd been doing this math longer than she had, carrying it like ballast. "We can't go tonight. A skiff with a mounted gun sits between us and the cave mouth, and I'm many things, Sable, but I am not a miracle."

"I wasn't suggesting we go tonight." She was, a little. She let it go. "Tomorrow, though. When they pull out."

"When they pull out," he agreed. "And until then, we are two mangrove crabs with excellent discipline."

She passed the binoculars back and settled deeper against the root, pulling her jacket tight. The rain had stopped but the air was still wet enough to drink, and the cold came up through the hull and into her bones. She should sleep—they both should—but her eyes wouldn't shut while that light was sweeping the coast, so she rationed herself: sixty seconds open, thirty closed, like a diver managing air.

Somewhere in the rationing, she must have slept anyway, because she woke to gray light and a hand on her shoulder and Ilo saying her name in the careful voice he used for important things.

"They're leaving."

She took the glasses. The skiff was underway, standing off the headland, riding low in the water with its cargo. As she watched, it turned south and opened up, the diesel note climbing, and in three minutes it was a smudge, and in five it was nothing.

But before it went, Sable caught one last detail, and it lodged in her chest like a splinter: a man at the stern, alone, facing back toward the island. Not scanning. Just looking. Standing at parade rest at the rail of a boat full of sealed crates, staring at the cave mouth the way you stare at a grave you dug yourself.

She knew that posture. She'd seen it in mirrors, in marinas, in the faces of people paying off debts to the sea.

"He knows," she said.

"Many of them know," Ilo said quietly. "Knowing and stopping are different muscles. I spent thirty years exercising the wrong one."

* * *

*Sable*

By midmorning the tide had turned and the mangrove channel had deepened enough to float the Mariposa out. They worked in silence, checking gear, topping off water, and the silence wasn't comfortable exactly, but it was *theirs*, which was new, and Sable found she didn't hate it.

At the channel mouth she paused, engine idling, and looked back at the headland. The cave entrance showed as a dark seam in the rock above the waterline, innocent as a shadow. Somewhere behind that seam, if the math held, four crates and possibly nine living people were waiting out the clock.

"My mother dove here," she said. It came out before she'd decided to say it, the way true things do. "Years ago. She left a route line inside, tools, a note she never finished. She wasn't passing through, Ilo. She was *here*, in this exact water, and then she left and never talked about it, and I spent my whole life thinking she was just... restless." She shook her head. "What if she wasn't restless? What if she was running?"

Ilo was quiet a long moment. Out past the reef, the sea lay flat and glittering and completely unbothered, keeping its secrets the way it always had.

"Then tomorrow," he said finally, "we finish what she started. And what the Navy started. And what Reyes started." He glanced at her, and for once there was no calculation in his face, only a tired, stubborn hope. "Everyone leaves something unfinished in these islands, Sable. The trick is deciding whose unfinished business becomes yours."

She pushed the throttle forward and turned the bow toward the headland, toward the dark seam in the rock, toward the four hours of low tide that would begin at dusk.

"Mine," she said. "It's mine."
