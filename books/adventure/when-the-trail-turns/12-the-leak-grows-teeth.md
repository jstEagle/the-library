## 12. The Leak Grows Teeth

The Mariposa leaked the way an old woman complains—steadily, bitterly, and worse when the weather turned against her.

Sable lay on her back in the bilge at dawn, cheek pressed to a frame slick with green scum, and listened to her boat confess. Drip. Drip. A longer drip, like a tap someone hadn't quite closed. Somewhere aft, where the coral had kissed her hull during the worst of the anchorage drag, seawater came through in a thread no thicker than sewing silk—and threads, she knew from long experience, had ambitions.

"Talk to me," she murmured, running her thumb along the seam until she found it: a hairline split in the caulking between two planks, maybe eight inches long, sitting just below the waterline on the starboard side. Repairable. Barely. If you could get epoxy into it while it was dry, which meant beaching her or careening her against something solid, which meant—

She closed her eyes and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for two days.

The monsoon had settled over Isla Serena like a guest who intended to stay through the holidays. Rain came down in gray ropes. The swell outside the reef broke in long white shoulders, and every few minutes a set rolled through that made the whole lagoon shudder. There was no sheltered cove here, no friendly careening beach with a winch and a cold beer waiting. There was only the reef shelf on the leeward side, where the water went from four fathoms to nothing in the space of a body length, and where—if she waited for the brief lulls between squalls—the surge might die down enough to let a diver work.

Or it might not. The sea didn't do guarantees. It did offers, take them or leave them, and sometimes it took your offer back mid-handshake.

"You're going to dive in this." Ilo stood at the companionway, holding two mugs of coffee like he wasn't sure whether offering one would be presumptuous. He'd been doing that a lot lately—standing in doorways, hovering at the edge of conversations, a man perpetually asking permission to exist. It was a strange quality in someone who'd spent thirty years in the navy, where presumption was practically a job requirement.

"I'm going to try," Sable said, taking the mug. "There's a difference."

"There is. One of them ends with a funeral."

"One of them ends with my boat sinking at anchor and both of us swimming." She blew across the coffee. "I've patched hulls in worse."

"Have you?"

She considered lying, decided against it. "In different worse," she said. "Worse with better visibility. Worse without a monsoon sitting on top of it." She tipped her head toward the bilge. "It's eight inches of caulking, Ilo. Maybe ten. I go down at the start of a lull, I pack it with epoxy putty, I brace a patch plate over it, done. Twenty minutes if the sea behaves. Forty if it doesn't."

"And if it misbehaves worse than forty minutes' worth?"

"Then I come up and we think of something else."

He looked at her for a long moment, rain drumming on the deck above them, and she watched him weigh something—some argument, some memory, some old guilt wearing a new coat. Finally he said, "Then I'm coming with you."

"It's a one-person job."

"It's a one-person job with a safety diver standing by on a line." He said it flatly, the way he said most things, but his eyes had that stubborn set she'd started to recognize. "You'll be on a tether. I'll hold the other end. If anything goes wrong, I pull. That's not negotiable, and before you tell me I don't know your boat or your reef, understand that I've spent more hours underwater than you've been alive, and most of them were in water exactly this angry."

She wanted to argue. The words lined up behind her teeth—*my boat, my dive, my risk*—all the old liturgy of self-sufficiency she'd been reciting since she was nineteen and buried her mother's ashes off a headland because there was no one else to say the words. But the bilge dripped its bitter little drip, and the swell boomed against the outer reef, and somewhere under all of it was the memory of yesterday's set that had picked the Mariposa up and walked her three meters sideways like a drunk carrying furniture.

"Fine," she said. "But you follow my instructions out there. Not the navy's way. My way."

"My way got people killed," Ilo said quietly. "Yours will do fine."

* * *

They spent the morning preparing, and preparing, Sable discovered, was its own kind of conversation—one conducted in rope and hardware instead of words.

Ilo knew knots she'd never seen, splices that looked like they'd grown rather than been tied. He rigged the tether line off the Mariposa's forward cleat with a friction hitch around a spare fender as a snubber, so the line would give gently when the surge pulled instead of jerking her off the reef like a hooked fish. He talked as he worked—not much, but more than usual—and Sable realized he was explaining each choice, teaching without announcing it, the way her mother used to explain weather by pointing at clouds.

"Signal code," he said, holding up the line. "One tug: everything's fine. Two tugs: coming up. Three tugs—" He paused. His hands went still on the rope. "Three tugs means pull. No questions, no hesitation. You feel three, you haul until my arms give out, and then you tie it off to the winch and let the machine do it."

"Three tugs," Sable said. "And if *you're* the one in trouble?"

A flicker crossed his face—surprise, maybe, that she'd asked. "Then you cut me loose and save yourself."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard, and I once heard a man try to pay me in goats."

"Sable—"

"If you go in after me, you're on your own line, clipped to your own point, and you signal me the same way. We are not cutting anybody loose. This isn't a navy op. Nobody here is expendable." She heard her own voice harden and didn't soften it. Some things deserved hardness.

Ilo looked at her the way he'd looked at the survey team's data logs—with the expression of a man reading something he half-hoped wasn't true. Then he nodded, once, and tied the second line.

* * *

The lull came at midafternoon, announced not by silence—for the rain never stopped—but by a change in the swell's rhythm. The big sets stretched further apart, five minutes, six, the lagoon's surface going glassy-dark and strange between blows, like the sea holding its breath.

"Now or never," Sable said, already pulling on her wetsuit. The neoprene had gone soft and sour-smelling from years of salt, and putting it on felt like shaking hands with an old adversary. She checked her tank, her regulator, her knife, her light. Epoxy putty in a canvas bag slung across her chest, patch plate and stainless screws in another. Everything double-clipped. Nothing loose to catch.

At the swim step, Ilo pressed the tether line into her hand. His grip was dry and steady, and for a moment neither of them moved.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "Forty if it doesn't behave. And Sable—" Something almost cracked through the flat calm of his voice. "Come back up."

"Wasn't planning on relocating," she said, and rolled backward into the green.

* * *

Underwater in a monsoon was a different country from underwater in fair weather. The visibility, usually twenty feet on this reef even on bad days, had collapsed to a brown murk shot through with drifting curtains of sand. Every surge shoved her bodily sideways; every recede dragged at her fins like a hand trying to steal her shoes. She worked by feel and memory, counting fin kicks from the anchor line to the hull, following the Mariposa's shadowed curve down until her gloves found the familiar geography of planking and seam.

There. The split. She could feel it with her fingertips before she could see it—a lip of displaced caulking, a whisper of cold water pushing through against her touch like a small animal breathing.

She braced herself in the lee of the keel, wedged her knees against two frames, and got to work. Knead the putty warm between her palms. Press it into the seam, working from the ends toward the middle so no air pocket stayed trapped. Her breath rasped loud in her own ears, bubbles streaming away overhead into the churn. Eight inches. Ten. The putty went in gray and obedient, and she began to believe—

The sea changed its mind.

She felt it first as a sound: a deep, rolling boom transmitted through the rock of the reef itself, felt in her teeth and sternum before it reached her ears. Then the water around her went still—not calm, but still the way a drawn bow is still—and every instinct she owned screamed at her to let go of the hull and get clear.

Too late. The surge hit.

It didn't push her. It *folded* her—slammed her against the reef shelf beside the hull with a force that knocked the regulator half out of her mouth and drove the air from her lungs in a silver burst. The world became pressure and tumbling sand and the grinding crunch of her tank hitting coral. She got the regulator back, sucked a mouthful of half-water, coughed, got a cleaner breath, and then the surge withdrew and tried to take her with it, dragging her across the jagged shelf, and she did the only thing there was to do: she grabbed the reef and held on while the water tried to peel her off like a label.

Her left arm found a coral head and locked around it. Fire lanced from wrist to elbow—she'd opened her forearm on something sharp, she could feel blood dissolving into the water in a dark ribbon—but she held. The next surge came and flattened her again, and again she held, and now she understood, with a clarity that arrived colder than the water, what was actually happening.

The surge was pinning her. Each wave slammed her against the shelf and the recede couldn't lift her because her own grip and the geometry of the rock held her fast—and between surges, in those few seconds of relative slack, she couldn't move. Her legs were wedged, her bag of tools had torn free and gone tumbling into the murk, and the coral head she clung to was on the wrong side of a channel of churning water from any path back to the anchor line.

She was, in the oldest and simplest sense, stuck.

*Think.* Her mother's voice, calm as tide tables. *Panic spends air faster than work does. Assess.*

Air: half a tank, maybe less after the coughing fit. Time between surges: perhaps fifteen seconds, and shrinking as the lull died. Options: wait for a gap in the sets and sprint for the anchor line—except the sets weren't gapping anymore, they were stacking, the lull closing like a door. Or—

Her hand found the tether line, still clipped to her harness, still trailing away upstream into the murk. Still attached to the cleat. Still attached to Ilo.

She pulled. Once. Twice. Three times, hard, the line singing taut.

*Pull,* she thought. *No questions. No hesitation.*

* * *

Above, in a world of rain and gray light, Ilo felt the three tugs come up the line like a scream delivered by telegraph.

He didn't think. Later, Sable would understand that this was the gift he gave her in that moment—all his thinking, all his hesitating, all his careful navy arithmetic about acceptable losses, set aside in the space between the third tug and his hands closing on the rope. He cleated the line, threw the winch handle, and the motor took the strain with a whine that climbed as the load came on.

The line came tight. Came tighter. Held.

Down below, Sable felt the tether snap taut against her harness and understood what it meant: someone up there was hauling, blind, trusting her position, betting her life on geometry he couldn't see. The line ran from the bow cleat down past the hull to her—and the angle was wrong, dragging her sideways across the coral instead of up and clear. She felt the harness straps bite, felt herself shift an inch, two inches, the coral head tearing from her grip—

—and then the line angle changed. Ilo, reading the strain through his hands the way she read water through hers, had run forward and re-cleated to the midship winch, shifting the pull from sideways to vertical. The new tension lifted her, peeled her up off the shelf like a cork out of a bottle, and the sea, furious at losing, sent one last surge to slam her back down.

It missed. By the time it broke against the reef she was rising through the murk, the Mariposa's keel sliding past above her like the belly of a whale, and then the surface hit her face and the rain hit her face and Ilo's hands hit her arms, hauling her over the gunwale onto the deck, where she lay gasping like landed fish while the storm shouted around them both.

"You—" she wheezed. "Re-cleated. Mid-ship. How—"

"The line told me," Ilo said. He was kneeling over her, checking her harness buckles with quick, rough hands, and his composure had finally cracked clean through—he was breathing like he'd been the one underwater. "Thirty years of pulling on lines. They talk." His eyes found the blood sheeting down her forearm and his jaw tightened. "Hold still."

"It's a scratch."

"It's a scratch that's going to get cleaned and wrapped anyway." He was already moving, fetching the first aid kit from the wheelhouse, and Sable lay back against the deck boards and let the rain fall on her face and laughed—actually laughed, a shaky, disbelieving sound that the wind tore away as fast as she made it.

Because here was the thing, the thing she kept turning over like a coin she'd found in the sand: for those endless seconds pinned on the reef, she hadn't been alone. Not in the way that mattered. Someone had held the other end. Someone had *come for her*, without question, without calculation, and the knowledge settled into her chest with a warmth that had nothing to do with the wetsuit.

She had spent her whole adult life being her own safety diver. It had worked. It had kept her moving, kept her free, kept grief at the length of an arm's reach. And it had nearly killed her today, on an ordinary reef, doing an ordinary repair, because the ocean doesn't care how independent you are—it only cares whether anyone's holding your line.

* * *

Evening came down purple and wet. Sable sat in the cockpit with her arm freshly bandaged, watching Ilo check the bilge pump for the third time in an hour. The epoxy had held—she'd gone back down at the next true lull, shorter and angrier, and finished the job in eleven flat minutes with Ilo on the line the whole time, and when she'd surfaced he'd hauled her aboard with the same fierce efficiency, as though once clearly wasn't enough to prove the point.

"We should talk about the cave," she said.

Ilo glanced up. "Now?"

"Tide tables don't care that I almost drowned today. The window opens in—" she checked her watch, "—about fifty hours. Low tide, storm lull, all the stars aligning, whatever's left of the survey team's luck rubbing off on us. And I can't do it alone."

She said it plainly, and watched him absorb it. Because that was the real confession, wasn't it? Not the near-drowning—that was just weather and bad timing. The confession was the sentence she'd just spoken, the one she'd sworn since she was nineteen never to need.

"I can't do it alone," she repeated, softer. "Inside that cave, with whatever's down there, with the navy coming back—I need someone on my line who'll pull. And Ilo?" She met his eyes. "I don't want to do it alone. That's the part I'm still getting used to saying."

For a long moment he said nothing. Rain ticked on the cockpit canopy. Out beyond the reef, the monsoon grumbled through its nightly inventory of thunder.

"When I was on the Verano," he said at last—his old ship, the one that had carried its terrible cargo to this water, "we had a saying. *No one dives alone.* It was regulation. Everyone mocked it." His hands were quiet in his lap. "I have spent thirty years learning why it wasn't a rule about diving."

Sable reached over and touched the tether line, still coiled on the cleat, still smelling of salt and strain. "Then it's settled," she said. "You're mine, and I'm yours. Line partners." She managed a crooked smile. "Try not to make it weird."

Something that was almost a smile moved across his face too—there and gone, like the sun through monsoon cloud. "Fifty hours," he said. "We'd better plan."

But Sable was already looking at the chart spread under the cockpit lamp, at her mother's faded pencil marks converging on the circled island, at the route line that stopped mid-stroke decades ago—and at the fresh red circle she'd drawn around the cave mouth, waiting for its own line to be finished.

Fifty hours. And this time, she wouldn't be diving alone.
