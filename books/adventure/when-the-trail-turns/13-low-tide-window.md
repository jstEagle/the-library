## 13. Low Tide Window

I had spent eleven years of my naval service learning to read tables—fuel consumption tables, ammunition expenditure tables, casualty projection tables—and I had come to hate them all with a quiet, professional loathing. So there was a particular irony in finding myself at three in the morning, hunched over a salvaged tide almanac by the light of a camp stove, doing arithmetic I actually cared about.

"Say it again," Sable said. She was sitting across from me with her knees drawn up, a mug of instant coffee she wasn't drinking cradled in both hands. "Slower. And without the part where you apologize for the math."

"The spring low comes at 0640 tomorrow," I said. "The one after that is thirteen hours later, but by then the storm surge will be stacking water into the cave mouth like a man stuffing laundry into a duffel bag. We get one real window. Four hours, give or take."

"Give or take how much?"

"Twenty minutes either way, if the barometer holds. Which it won't."

She nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd already suspected about the universe's sense of humor. Outside, the monsoon had dropped from a scream to a sullen mutter—the eye of it, or the edge of it, or whatever the sea offered us as a courtesy. The Mariposa sat on her mooring lines fifty meters out, listing slightly to port where the coral had opened her hull, and every few minutes a wave slapped her flank like a creditor reminding us of a debt.

Four hours. In four hours we had to enter a flooded sea cave that the navy had spent decades pretending didn't exist, follow a route line my dive partner's dead mother had rigged years before either of us touched this water, and find out what had happened to five government surveyors who had vanished so completely that their own agency had sent me—a retired man with bad knees and worse secrets—to sweep them under the same rug as everything else.

And we had to be back out before the tide turned, because the cave mouth closed like a throat.

"You're smiling," Sable said.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's a terrible smile. It's the smile of a man reading his own obituary and noticing they spelled his name right."

I put down the almanac. "My name isn't Ilo."

"I know," she said, and drank her coffee at last, grimacing. "But it's the name you gave me, and I've decided it fits you better than whatever's on your discharge papers."

* * *

We spent the morning turning the survey camp inside out.

The tents still stood where the team had abandoned them, twelve days ago now, though the monsoon had begun to eat at the guy lines and one dome tent had collapsed into itself like a deflated lung. The instruments were the treasure. Reyes' team had come equipped the way government money equips people—generously and without much thought—and they'd left nearly all of it behind when they ran.

Sable carried a dive computer out of the equipment tent, turned it over twice, and held it up to the gray light. "Sealed unit. Depth rated to a hundred meters." She looked at me. "These people were surveying a reef, not planning a funeral."

"No," I agreed. "The reef was the cover story. They were mapping the cave whether they knew it or not."

There were two more computers in a waterproof case, along with three dive lights—one dead, two alive—a spool of guide line, a slate with a grease pencil, and a first-aid kit whose oxygen bottle was half full. Sable laid everything out on a camp table with the reverence of a surgeon arranging instruments, and I watched her hands move and understood, not for the first time, that she had been born to exactly this work. There was a steadiness in her that her restlessness couldn't quite disguise. Her mother had drawn maps; the daughter read water the way other people read faces.

"We need weights," she said. "Belts or otherwise. And a second regulator."

"There's a gear locker under the radio tent. I checked yesterday while you were sleeping."

"You checked while I was sleeping."

"You snore," I said. "It seemed polite to be elsewhere."

"I do not snore."

"You snore like a small engine failing to start. Repeatedly. With conviction."

She threw a bungee cord at my head, which I caught, mostly because I'd been expecting it since the word *snore* left my mouth.

The gear locker yielded two weight belts, a backup regulator that would need testing, and—folded into a dry bag with a care that made my chest ache—a woman's wetsuit, size small, with *M. OKONKWO* inked on the collar in permanent marker. Okonkwo was the one who hadn't made it to the cave. We had found nothing of her except a single boot at the waterline and, wedged in the rocks near the anomaly site, the torn jacket Sable still hadn't let me bury properly because she kept saying there'd be time after.

Sable held the wetsuit for a long moment.

"She was smaller than me," Sable said finally. "It'd fit wrong in the shoulders."

"It might fit right enough."

"That's not—" She stopped. Folded the suit back along its original creases. "We'll bring it out with us. Her family should have something that isn't a form letter."

"Yes," I said, because there was nothing else to say that wouldn't have been a lie dressed up as comfort.

* * *

By noon the storm had settled into its working rhythm—long bands of rain marching in off the open sea, punctuated by lulls that felt less like mercy than like the ocean catching its breath between arguments. We used the lulls. Sable tested both regulators in the shallows off the mangroves, submerged to her neck in churning green water, and came up shaking her head.

"Second stage free-flows if you tilt past forty degrees. Usable, but you'd want to keep your head level, which—" she gestured at the sea, which was doing its impression of a washing machine, "—will be hilarious."

"Hilarious," I repeated.

"Don't patronize me, old man. You haven't been below ten meters since before I was born."

That was true, and it landed harder than she probably intended, because I went quiet, and she noticed, and for a moment neither of us did anything but listen to the rain drumming on the mangrove leaves.

"Ilo." She waded closer. Water streamed off her. "I wasn't—"

"You were exactly right," I said. "And I've been thinking about it all morning, so you may as well hear it. My last dive was thirty-one years ago. The Marisol. Two hundred tons, riding low, hold sealed from the outside. I was twenty-four and I told myself the people going into that hold were cargo, because the alternative was to refuse an order, and I was twenty-four, and refusing orders had a specific price that I could not afford." I looked out at the Mariposa, riding her damage. "So yes. My body remembers the motions. Whether it remembers them well enough is another question entirely."

Sable wrung out her hair and considered me with an expression I was beginning to know well—the one where she decided not to say the kind thing because the useful thing was more honest.

"Then you stay shallow," she said. "You run the line from the cave mouth to the first air pocket. That's eight meters, maybe ten. I go deep. If anything goes wrong down there, it goes wrong with me, not with the man who knows what the navy does when it finds witnesses."

"And if something goes wrong with you?"

"Then you'll have four hours to figure out how sorry you are," she said, and grinned, and the grin was terrible and bright and I understood why her mother had circled this island on a map and written *ask about the quiet ones.* Some inheritances aren't property. They're instructions.

* * *

The plan, when we finally wrote it out on the slate, took up most of the surface and looked, in Sable's assessment, like "a shopping list written during an earthquake."

0615: kit check, both divers, on the beach.
0620: enter water at the anomaly site, surface swim to conserve gas.
0630: descend on the mooring line Sable had tied to the rock shelf two days earlier.
0640: slack low. Cave mouth open. Enter on my mother's route line—Ilo to the first air pocket, tie-off, wait. Sable continues solo to the hold.
0730: hard turnaround for Sable regardless of what she has found. No exceptions. We wrote NO EXCEPTIONS on the slate and underlined it twice, and then Sable added a third underline, and then drew a small skull next to it, in case I missed the point.
0840: both divers out. Cave closes.
0900: Mariposa moved to the secondary anchorage behind the reef, engines cold, tarps on. Invisible.

"And if the navy skiff comes early?" I asked.

Sable tapped the last line. "Then it comes early and finds an empty island and a boat that looks like it's been abandoned since the war. We'll have left the camp looking wrecked. Tents down, gear scattered. Let them think the storm did their work for them."

"They'll dive the cave anyway."

"Not at high tide, they won't. Their own hydrographic data says the mouth's impassable above half-flood—we read it off their laptop before the battery died. Whoever planned this operation built the schedule around the same tides we are." She smiled thinly. "Which means somewhere in that navy there's a planner who did the exact same arithmetic we just did, and I find that weirdly comforting. Like having a pen pal."

"You have a gift," I said, "for making horror sound like correspondence."

"My mother's gift, probably. I got the map gene and the gallows-gene in the same envelope."

* * *

We spent the afternoon rehearsing.

Not diving—there was no point burning gas or nerve in water like this—but the mechanics. Sable rigged the guide line between two palms on the beach and walked me through the tie-offs blindfolded, my gloves on, until my fingers could find the knots without my eyes. Twice she changed the sequence without telling me, and twice I got it wrong, and the third time I stopped her.

"If the line's different down there than it was up here, I need to know immediately, not politely. Say 'change' and describe it. Don't test me. Test results are for people with time."

"Change," she said promptly. "Line runs left around a spur instead of right. Your call."

"Stop. Feel for the spur. Confirm direction by touch, not memory. Then continue." I pulled the blindfold off. "Like that."

"Like that," she agreed, and for the rest of the afternoon she drilled me with the merciless cheer of someone who had clearly once taught novices and enjoyed it more than she admitted. By dusk I could rig and read the line in the dark, in gloves, with rain hammering my hood, and my hands only shook a little, and I chose to believe the shaking was the cold.

We ate rice and tinned fish and half a jar of pickled vegetables Sable had liberated from the survey camp's stores, sitting under the tarp with our backs against a fallen log, watching lightning walk the horizon in slow silver strides.

"Can I ask you something," Sable said, "that isn't about tomorrow?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends entirely on how tired I am."

"What was her name? The ship. You said it once—Marisol—but you said it like a man saying a word in a language he's trying to forget."

I was quiet for a while. A gecko somewhere in the darkness made its ticking sound, absurdly domestic against all that weather.

"There were a hundred and fourteen names on the manifest," I said. "Marisol was the ship. But when I dream about it—not often, anymore, but when I do—it's never the ship. It's a shoe. One shoe, floating in the hold water, when we cracked the hatch to scuttle her. Small. A child's shoe, maybe, or just a small person's. Thirty-one years, and the navy gave me a pension and a new posting and eventually a new name, and none of it covered the shoe." I turned the tin plate in my hands. "Tomorrow, if there's a manifest in that hold—if anyone wrote down who they were—then those hundred and fourteen stop being my secret and start being the world's problem. That's all I want. To put it down."

Sable reached over and took the plate from me, set it down, and did not say anything comforting, which was the most comforting thing available.

"You'll put it down," she said instead. "But you should know—I'm not carrying it out of that cave for you. Or for the navy, or for Reyes, or even for the families, though God, yes, for the families. I'm carrying it because my mother came here, and dove here, and started writing something here that somebody made her stop writing. Whatever's in that hold, it's the second half of a sentence she never finished. I'm going to finish it."

"And then?"

"And then," she said, "we'll see if the truth floats."

* * *

I did not sleep. I don't think she expected me to, and she didn't pretend to sleep either; we lay in the dark under the tarp listening to the storm work through its moods, and around two in the morning she said, conversationally, into the black:

"The barometer's climbing."

I had been thinking the same thing for an hour. "Half a millibar since midnight. Maybe a fluke. Maybe the back edge."

"Or maybe the window widens."

"Or maybe it's the false lull before the storm's last argument. Monsoons lie, Sable. It's the one thing they do well."

"Mm." A pause. Rain on canvas. "If it widens, we take the wider window. More bottom time. I could go past the first pocket, map more of the route—"

"You could also die with extra documentation. The plan holds. Four hours. NO EXCEPTIONS. Skull."

"You drew the skull."

"I stand by the skull."

She laughed—quietly, so the sound wouldn't carry across the water, but really laughed, and I lay there in the dark feeling something I hadn't felt in thirty-one years, which was that I was exactly where I was supposed to be, doing the thing I was supposed to do, beside the person I was supposed to be beside. It was a dangerous feeling. Hope always is. But I was too old and too tired to pretend I didn't recognize it.

At 0430 the rain thinned to a drizzle and the wind swung fifteen degrees north, and we stood on the beach in the pre-dawn gray and watched the sea begin, very slowly, to lie down.

"Barometer's up another two points," Sable said. She was already pulling on her wetsuit, moving with the economy of someone who had made her decision and filed it away. "Window's holding. Maybe widening."

"Stick to the plan."

"Sticking to the plan." She zipped up, then paused, looking east, where the sky had gone the color of pewter and the first suggestion of dawn was bleeding through the cloud deck. "Ilo. Look."

I looked. Beyond the reef line, far out across the steel-colored water, a shape was standing away from the horizon—a low, dark silhouette, running without lights.

A patrol skiff. Coming in fast, straight toward Isla Serena, hours ahead of any schedule we had calculated.

Sable lowered the binoculars very slowly.

"So much for the plan," she said.
