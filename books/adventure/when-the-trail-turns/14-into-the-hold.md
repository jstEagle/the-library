## 14. Into the Hold

The cave mouth opened like a held breath.

From above, at slack water in the gray hour before dawn, it was nothing—a darker seam in a dark reef, the kind of shadow a diver catalogs and forgets. But Sable had been raised on her mother's habit of looking twice, and looking twice at that seam told her what the survey team's instruments had already screamed: the rock didn't end where the water began. It continued, downward and inward, a throat.

"Four hours," Ilo said, checking his watch for the third time in a minute. "The window closes fast. If we're not out by the turn, the surge in that passage will be—" He stopped, as if the sentence were a weight he couldn't lift.

"As bad as you keep not saying," Sable finished.

"Worse. I keep underpromising so you'll still get in the water."

She laughed, because the alternative was to let the fear set up housekeeping in her chest, and she'd spent her whole life evicting it. "Then let's go before I get smart."

They went down together, Ilo's light and hers cutting twin cones through water the color of strong tea. The reef fell away beneath them in shelves, and the cave mouth grew from a seam into an archway, and the archway grew into something vast. Sable had entered wrecks before—cargo holds, engine rooms, the sad interiors of ships that had died badly. She had never entered a place that felt like a mouth about to close.

The passage narrowed. She turned sideways, exhaling slowly, feeling the rock brush her tank on one side and the open water on the other. Her light caught the walls: coral, then bare stone, then something that stopped her heart for one full beat.

A line.

Nylon, old, furred with a decade of marine growth, but unmistakable—a route line, knotted at intervals in the particular pattern her mother had used on every wreck they'd ever worked together. Two half-hitches and a bowline. Sable had tied that knot ten thousand times without knowing she was inheriting it.

Her mother had been here.

She'd known it in her head since the night she'd found the tin of tools and the unfinished note in the outer chamber—*the manifest is the whole point, don't let them tell you it isn't, if you're reading this then I didn't*—but knowing a thing in your head and meeting it with your hands in forty feet of black water were different animals entirely. Sable wrapped her glove around the line and held on for a moment, letting the current push her gently against the rock, and said nothing, because there was no one to say it to and no word for it anyway.

Ilo's light found her face. He didn't gesture, didn't hurry. He just waited, and then pointed down the line, and they went on together.

* * *

The passage opened into the hold.

She knew it was a hold before her light resolved any detail, because her body knew holds—the way the echo changed, the way the water went still and cold, the particular hush of a big empty room that had been full of something once. The ship lay on its side, wedged into the cave the way a body wedges into a narrow bed, and the hold gaped above them, half-flooded, half-air, its ribs rising like the carcass of some enormous animal.

Her light swept across the bulkhead and she forgot how to breathe.

Tallies.

Scratched into the steel, four groups of four with a slash through each—prisoner's marks, castaway's marks, the arithmetic of someone counting days they weren't sure they'd live to see. There were dozens of them. Then hundreds. Her light followed them down the wall, row after row after row, and then, near the waterline where the air pocket must have been thinnest, they stopped. Mid-row. A single unfinished stroke, the scratch trailing off like a sentence that ran out of breath.

Sable hung in the water and looked at them, and something in her chest that had been clenched since Puerto Hondo finally, quietly, broke open.

* * *

She'd expected, in the way you expect things you don't let yourself expect, to find horror here in its usual costume—bones, rust, the impersonal wreckage of a bad night at sea. What she found instead was worse, because it was intimate.

The hold had been a hold for people.

Her light found a child's shoe, leather gone stiff as bark, wedged in a rib of the hull. A comb. A rosary whose beads had fused into a single dark rope. A tin cup, dented, and beside it—arranged, deliberately, the way you arrange things when arranging them is the last act of dignity left to you—a small heap of personal effects. A wedding ring. A pair of spectacles folded closed. A photograph in a waterproof sleeve, the image bleached to ghosts, but you could still see the shape of it: a family, four of them, arms around each other, squinting into a sun that had been gone for fifty years.

"They were passengers," Sable said. Her voice came out strange in the regulator, muffled, private. "Not cargo. Someone's mother packed that bag."

Ilo's light was very still. When he spoke, his voice was the flattest she'd ever heard it, the flatness of a man holding a door shut against something enormous. "The manifest will have the names."

"The manifest." She turned. "You keep saying that like you know it's here."

"I was on the ship that brought them, Sable. I've known what's in this hold for forty years. I just never—" His light wavered, steadied. "I never had the courage to come look at it."

She wanted to ask him everything—the ship's name, the year, how a man who'd been aboard a scuttling crew was now diving the wreck with her at dawn—but the hold was patient and the tide was not, and her mother's note had been explicit about priorities. *The manifest is the whole point.*

She found it in the captain's cabin, or what the charts of wrecks like this called the captain's cabin—a small compartment aft, half-collapsed, its desk still bolted to the deck at an angle that defied gravity and sense. Her mother's route line ran directly to it. Of course it did. Her mother had never once in her life failed to find the paperwork.

The manifest was in a steel document tube, the kind surveyors and navy men both used, and it was sealed with wax that had gone the color of old teeth. Sable cracked it with her dive knife, working carefully, the way you open something you already know is going to change your life.

The pages inside were water-stained but legible. Typed columns. A ship's name that had been struck through and retyped. And then the list.

Names.

Not cargo codes. Not the euphemisms she'd half expected—*personnel, materials, units*. Names. Whole families listed together, which is how you could tell they were families: the same surname, the same port of origin, ages running from sixty-two to four months. The four-month-old had a name. *Ana Belén Ruiz.* Someone had typed it. Someone had entered a four-month-old into a manifest as though she were freight, and the terrible mercy of it was that whoever typed it had also, in the remarks column, written *infant—handle with care*, as if care had been an option anyone aboard had intended to provide.

Sable read the names. All of them. She didn't let herself skim, because skimming felt like the first small betrayal, and these people had been betrayed enough for fifty years. Two hundred and eleven souls, the manifest said, and then, in a later hand, in pen, someone had amended it: *214 at boarding. 3 died in transit.*

She read every name aloud in her head, giving each one the two seconds of attention that was all she had to give and all, she suspected, they had ever wanted. *Ana Belén Ruiz.* *Tomás Iriarte.* *The Mercado family, six.* On and on, a litany typed in triplicate by a clerk who had probably gone home that night and eaten dinner.

When she finished, she sealed the tube again and stowed it inside her buoyancy vest, against her chest, where she could feel it with every breath.

* * *

They had maybe ninety minutes left in the window, and her mother's note had said there was more—*evidence in the strongbox, and the tallies tell you how long*—so Sable did what her mother would have done, which was to keep looking.

The tallies, it turned out, were only the beginning of what someone had left on that bulkhead.

Near the unfinished stroke, in the same scratched hand, there was writing. Letters this time, laborious, gouged into steel with something harder than fingernails—a belt buckle, maybe, or a spoon handle. Sable brought her light close and read them one at a time, because the salt and the rust had half-eaten them and because her eyes had gone unreliable.

*WE WERE HERE. REMEMBER US.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic, as though its author hadn't wanted to presume:

*MY NAME IS ELENA. IF ANYONE FINDS THIS, TELL MY SISTER IN—*

The port name was gone, eaten by rust, a hole in the steel exactly the size of the word that had lived there.

Sable pressed her glove against the bulkhead, over the hole, over all of it. The steel was cold through her glove. Somewhere behind that steel, fifty years ago, a woman named Elena had scratched her name into the world with a spoon handle because she understood, with the terrible clarity of the dying, that being remembered was the only survival left on offer.

"Well," Sable said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack. "We found it, Elena. It took us a while. But we found you."

Ilo had drifted to the far side of the hold, to a sealed strongbox bolted beneath a bunk frame—her mother's note had mentioned it, and there it was, exactly where the note said, which meant her mother had opened it, which meant her mother had stood in this exact water and read these exact names and then gone out into the world and been silenced for it. The thought arrived in Sable not as grief, exactly. Grief was an old lodger by now. This was something hotter. This was the understanding that her mother hadn't been running from this place all those years. She'd been running *toward* it, carrying it, and the world had closed its hand around her before she could finish the sentence.

Sable was going to finish the sentence.

"Strongbox?" Ilo asked.

"Later. It's not going anywhere, and neither is the manifest." She checked her gauge. "We've got maybe fifty minutes, and I want to document the tallies before we go. All of them. Every mark."

Ilo looked at her. In the twin lights, in the drowned cathedral of that hold, his face did something complicated. "Why the tallies?"

"Because someone counted." She got her slate out, the waterproof one she used for survey work, and began, methodically, to photograph and copy the marks. "Whoever scratched these woke up every morning in a flooded hole in the ground and counted another day, and the navy has spent fifty years making sure nobody would ever see the count. So we see it. We copy it down. We take it out of here and we put it somewhere the whole world has to look at it." She glanced up. "That's the salvage job, Ilo. This is the cargo."

He didn't answer right away. Then he got out his own slate, and together, in the blue-black hush of the hold, two divers copied the last testament of two hundred and fourteen people onto plastic slates by dive light, stroke by stroke, tally by tally, while the tide outside began, without malice or mercy, to turn.

* * *

They were three rows from done when Sable's light caught something at the edge of the beam that made her go absolutely still.

The tallies stopped mid-row. She'd seen that. But she'd assumed the hand that scratched them had simply run out of time, out of air, out of everything. Now, following the wall down toward the flooded lower section of the hold, her light found more marks—fresher ones. The steel there was less rusted, the gouges sharper, and the arithmetic was different. These weren't day counts.

These were tallies of a different kind. Grouped. Recent. And beside them, scratched in a hand that shook but didn't quit, three words:

*SOME OF US WAIT.*

Sable's heart went from slow and heavy to hammering in the space of one breath. She turned to Ilo, and even through the masks, even through forty years of his guilt and twenty-eight years of her restlessness, she saw that he'd read it too.

The passage beyond the hold—the one her mother's route line didn't enter, the one the survey team's final note had pointed toward—breathed. Sable felt it against her cheek before she understood it: a faint, rhythmic movement of water, in and out, in and out.

Not current.

Breathing. Or something that moved like breathing.

"Some of us wait," Sable said into her regulator, and pointed her light into the dark of the inner passage, where the water shivered, and something—or someone, her mind insisted, *someone*—waited on the far side of the tide.
