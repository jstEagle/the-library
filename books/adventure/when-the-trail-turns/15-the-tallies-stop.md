## 15. The Tallies Stop

The route line ran out of light before it ran out of cave.

Ilo kept his hand on the guide rope—Sable's mother's rope, nylon gone stiff as old sin but still holding—and counted his breaths the way he'd taught himself to count them forty years ago, when he was nineteen and standing watch on a ship whose cargo hold he'd been ordered never to open. In through the nose. Out slow. The regulator Sable had rebuilt from survey-team spares rasped against his jaw like an old man clearing his throat.

Ahead of him, her dive light swept the passage in patient arcs. She moved through water the way some people moved through grief: carefully, and without any intention of stopping.

They had been in the flooded gallery for nearly two hours. The manifest was already in the dry bag strapped to Sable's chest—the names, the tonnage figures, the lie written in naval shorthand that said *agricultural equipment* where it should have said *people*. They had what they came for. Ilo had told her so twice, tapping his wrist gauge, and both times she'd held up one finger: *one more chamber.*

He understood why on the third chamber.

The tallies started at the bulkhead seam, scratched into the coral-encrusted steel with something sharp and desperate—a surveyor's rock hammer, maybe, or a dive knife worn to a nub. Four vertical strokes, a diagonal. Four vertical strokes, a diagonal. The groups marched along the wall in a line that rose and fell with the uneven surface of the rock, and then, near a shelf of stone just above the old high-water mark, they stopped.

Mid-stroke. The fifth line of the last group trailed off into a scratch no deeper than a fingernail.

"They stopped counting," Sable said. Her voice came through the water thin and strange, bent by the helmet's comm. "Why would someone stop counting?"

Ilo put his glove against the wall. Below the tallies, half-hidden by a curtain of pale sponge, there were letters. Small ones. Careful ones, at first, and then less careful, the way handwriting goes when the hand gets tired or cold or frightened.

*DAY 9. AIR GOOD IN POCKET 2 IF WE STAY CALM. M. SAYS THE TIDE WILL BRING FRESH WATER THROUGH THE CRACK AT DAWN. DAY 10. VESSEL OFFSHORE AGAIN. LIGHTS ON THE REEF. WE DO NOT GO OUT. DAY 11.*

Day eleven ended in a smear of carbon, the pencil pressed hard enough to snap, and below that, nothing at all.

"Eleven days," Sable breathed. "Someone lived down here for eleven days."

"Or died down here," Ilo said, because he had learned that lying to yourself underwater got you killed faster than lying to anyone else. "Eleven days ago would have been—" He did the arithmetic against the dates in Reyes' stolen logbook, and the number arrived in his chest before it arrived in his head. "Before the camp went quiet. Before we ever anchored off this reef."

Sable's light swung toward him. Even through the mask he could feel her looking at him, the way you can feel weather changing behind your back.

"So whoever wrote this," she said slowly, "might still be writing it."

* * *

The air pocket, when they found it, announced itself the way all good things announce themselves in a flooded cave: by sound. A hollow resonance ahead, a change in the way their exhaled bubbles broke apart—not streaming upward and gone, but gathering, pooling under a low ceiling of limestone that dipped toward the water like the roof of a mouth about to whistle.

Sable surfaced first. Ilo heard her spit out her regulator, heard the wet gasp of someone tasting air that was stale and mineral-sweet and, to a person who'd been breathing compressed gas for two hours, probably the finest vintage ever bottled.

"Ilo." Her voice echoed strangely, flattened by the low stone. "Get up here. Bring the spare light."

He came up into a space barely large enough for three people to sit shoulder to shoulder, their heads bent beneath rock the color of old bone. The air tasted of salt and pencil shavings and, faintly, impossibly, of a chemical light stick—that green ghost-glow that survey teams carried by the dozen, one of them wedged into a crack overhead, dying by degrees after who knew how many days.

And there were people in it.

Three of them, folded into the dark like letters crammed into an envelope too small. A woman with a beard's worth of stubble shadowing her jaw and a navy-issue blanket around her shoulders like a cape she'd stopped being embarrassed about somewhere around day four. A young man, barely more than a boy, asleep sitting up with his chin on his chest and a dive computer strapped to a wrist far too thin. And between them, propped against the wall with a rock hammer across her knees like a scepter, a compact gray-haired woman who looked at the two surfacing divers with an expression Ilo could only describe as *mildly annoyed to see them.*

"You're not navy," the woman said. It wasn't a question. It was an audit.

"No," Sable managed, pulling her mask up onto her forehead. Water streamed off her face. "We're—"

"Good," the woman said. "Because if you were navy, I'd have to hit you with this hammer, and my arm is very tired." She paused, considering. "Also I might miss. My depth perception has gone strange. But the sentiment stands."

Something in Ilo's chest unclenched for the first time in eleven days—or in forty years, depending on how you counted. He laughed. It came out cracked and half-drowned, but it was a laugh, and the sleeping young man jerked awake at the sound of it, eyes going wide and white in the chemical glow.

"Divers," the woman explained to him, gently, as if announcing a change in the weather. "Civilian ones. Possibly lost. Possibly idiots. We'll take either."

"We're looking for the survey team from Isla Serena," Sable said. "Are you—"

"Dr. Marisol Okafor-Reyes. Team lead." The woman said it the way other people recite their own eulogies, briskly and without ornament. "This is Tomás Okonkwo, our hydrographer, who is currently asleep because I made him sleep, and Dr. Priya Raghavan, who is—" She stopped. For the first time, the briskness failed her, and Ilo watched something move behind her face like a fish passing under ice. "Priya didn't make it into the pocket. There's a second air space deeper in, past the squeeze. She was hurt in the swim-in. A rockfall, when the navy's charges went off above us. We got her to the second pocket, but..." Her thumb moved over the head of the rock hammer, back and forth, back and forth. "She stopped breathing on the fourth day. We couldn't bring her body through the squeeze. So we stay close enough to talk to her, and we keep her air pocket clear, and we write down everything she taught us so it isn't wasted."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The chemical stick guttered. Somewhere beyond the stone, the sea breathed in its long slow rhythm against the reef, indifferent and enormous.

"I'm sorry," Sable said finally, and Ilo heard in her voice that she meant it in the specific, terrible way you mean it when you have recently read a man's name off a fifty-year-old manifest and felt it land in your own hands like ballast. "Dr. Raghavan's name will be in what we carry out. All of yours will be. That's why we came."

Okafor-Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Then at Ilo. Then at the dry bag strapped across Sable's chest.

"That's the manifest," she said quietly. "You found the hold."

"We found the hold."

"Then you know what's down there." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went flat and even, the voice of a woman who had spent eleven days deciding exactly what she would say if rescue ever came, so that no important word would be wasted on tears. "Four hundred and six people. Sealed in. Scuttled in international water so deep the navy thought no one would ever look. And when my team looked—when we *found* them—they sent men with crates and searchlights to make us un-find them." She drew a breath of the bad air and let it out. "Tomás saw them loading. We ran. We've been running in circles inside this rock for eleven days, eating survey rations and drinking condensation off the walls, and every tide I've sat here telling myself that if I could just get to a radio—"

"There's no radio," Ilo said gently. "There hasn't been a radio since the storm took the Mariposa's mast antenna. But there are four hours of low tide coming, a working regulator rig, and a boat hidden in the mangroves with two functioning lungs aboard." He looked at the sleeping boy, at the blanket-caped woman, at the hammer across the doctor's knees. "We came to carry people out, Doctor. Not papers. Papers we already have."

Okafor-Reyes stared at him. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, exhausted, ferocious thing, like a match struck in a wet room.

"Priya always said someone would come," she said. "Not the navy. Not the government. Just—someone. Some diver, some fool with a boat." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, quick and businesslike, the way you'd wipe grease off a gauge. "She said the sea keeps better ledgers than any ministry. I told her she was a romantic." Her voice caught on the last word and she cleared her throat hard. "She was right, of course. She usually was. It was insufferable."

* * *

Waking Tomás was a lesson in how much fear a body can hold and still function. The boy came up out of sleep swinging, connected solidly with Ilo's shoulder pad, and then froze, staring, breathing in great ragged gulps.

"Civilians," Okafor-Reyes repeated, with the patience of a woman who had given this briefing to herself many times. "Rescue. Real rescue. You can stop guarding the exit now, Tomás. Go on. Cry if you need to. Nobody's logging it."

He cried. Quietly, furiously, the way young men cry when they've been brave longer than they had fuel for, and Sable put a hand on the back of his neck and let him, saying nothing, which Ilo suspected was the exact right thing. He found himself watching her do it—watching the ease of it, the way she seemed to know without instruction that a drowning man doesn't need words, he needs something solid to hold—and he thought, not for the first time since Puerto Hondo, that he had hired a boat and gotten something else entirely, something he hadn't known he was short of until it was alongside him in dark water.

"The squeeze," Sable said, when Tomás had steadied. "Show me. If we're moving four people through it on one regulator rig, I need to see it before the tide turns."

"It's not a squeeze," Okafor-Reyes said. "It's an insult. Forty centimeters of gap between two slabs of limestone, flooded to within a hand's width of the ceiling, with a lip of rock halfway through that wants your tank. Priya called it the Letterbox. She went through first to prove it could be done." Her jaw tightened. "It could be done. Once."

"Once is all we need," Sable said. "Twice each, actually. But who's counting?"

"Everyone," said Tomás, with the hollow conviction of a man who had spent eleven days counting. "In here, everyone counts everything."

Ilo followed Sable back down into the black water to look at the Letterbox, and while she measured the gap with her hands and muttered calculations about tanks and buoyancy and the physics of hope, he drifted a few meters further, to the second air pocket, the small one, the quiet one.

Priya Raghavan lay where her colleagues had laid her, wrapped in the second blanket, arranged with care on a natural shelf above the waterline. Someone had tucked a survey notebook under her crossed hands. Someone had written on the cover, in pencil, in a careful student's hand: *DR. P. RAGHAVAN — SHE FOUND THEM FIRST.* Beside her, wedged upright in a crack of stone, stood a third chemical light stick, unbroken, saved.

Saved for eleven days. Saved for her.

Ilo hung in the water beside her and did the thing he had come forty years and half an ocean to do, though he had imagined it happening in a courtroom, in front of microphones, with lawyers present. He pulled his mask down and spoke to the dead woman in the ordinary voice of one diver to another.

"You found them first," he said. "I helped bury them. I'm sorry it took me so long to come back for the difference."

The light stick beside her glowed its steady green, and the sea breathed, and somewhere behind him Sable called his name—called it the way you call a name when you've decided it belongs on your crew list—and Ilo put his mask back on and turned toward the sound, toward the work, toward the four hours of falling tide that stood between six living people and the sky.

Behind him, in the small stone room where the tallies stopped, the last of the chemical light burned on, keeping its faithful, useless, beautiful vigil over the woman who had counted everyone.
