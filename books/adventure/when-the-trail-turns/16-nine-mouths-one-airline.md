## 16. Nine Mouths, One Airline

**Sable**

The air pocket at the back of the hold was the size of a walk-in closet and smelled like a walk-in closet that had been lived in by six frightened people for nine days. Sable broke the surface into it and the first thing she heard was a child counting.

Not a child. A grown man with a clipboard, counting in a whisper, the way you count when counting is the only prayer you've got left.

"Fourteen minutes since the last exchange," the man said. He had a gray-streaked beard, cracked lips, and eyes that had gone past fear into something more useful. "That's you, then. The diver."

"Reyes?" Sable said.

"Reyes." He didn't offer a hand. His hands were busy holding a waterproof case against his chest like it was an infant. "Ilo sent you."

"Ilo sent all of us, technically. He's topside rigging the line. It's a relay. One regulator, six of you, four hours of tide." She kept her voice level, the way you do when the math is bad and everyone in the room can do math. "We go one at a time. You breathe off the tank in the tunnel, pass it back, next person. Nobody panics, nobody sprints, nobody holds a breath going up."

One of the younger team members — a woman with a bandaged forearm, Okafor, according to the name tape — laughed. It was a thin, cracked sound, but a laugh. "One regulator. Six people. That's not a dive plan, that's a hostage negotiation."

"It's a salvage plan," Sable said. "I've run worse with better equipment and worse people."

Reyes looked at her then, really looked, the way a man looks at a rope when he's deciding whether to put his weight on it. "How many dives have you made where the equipment was held together with hope?"

"All of them," Sable said. "It's a family trait."

She got a second laugh out of Okafor, and out of the two others — a big quiet man named Delacruz and a wiry kid, Torres, who hadn't stopped shivering since she'd surfaced. That left the one who wasn't laughing. The one who wasn't there.

"Where's your sixth?" Sable asked, and immediately hated the question, because she already knew the shape of the answer. Nine days in a hole teaches you to read rooms. The room had a hole in it shaped like a person.

"Vasquez," Reyes said. "He didn't make the swim in." He said it the way he'd clearly said it every day since — flat, complete, a stone laid down and left. "We're bringing his tags."

Sable nodded. She'd been diving wrecks long enough to know that the dead came along or they didn't, and either way the living had to keep breathing. "Then we're five, plus tags. That's better math."

It wasn't better math. It was the same bad math with a smaller denominator. But Reyes almost smiled, and that was worth the lie.

* * *

**Ilo**

Ilo crouched at the tunnel mouth with the regulator rig laid out on a flat rock like surgical instruments, and he was aware — in the detached way he had learned to observe himself, the way he'd learned to observe everything after that ship, after that order, after thirty years of carrying it — that his hands were shaking.

Not from cold. From the arithmetic.

One tank. One regulator with a working second stage and one octopus he'd rebuilt from survey spares with tape and stubbornness. A guide line he'd run through the flooded passage himself, twice, because a line run once is a suggestion and a line run twice is a promise. Four hours of low-tide window, minus what they'd already spent.

Six — five — people who hadn't been underwater in nine days, whose air discipline would be rust, whose panic would be fresh.

"You're doing the thing where you stare at the equipment like it insulted you," Sable said, surfacing beside him, water sheeting off her hood.

"It's a coping mechanism."

"Does it work?"

"No. But it passes the time." He handed her the slate. He'd written the rotation out: order of divers, estimated air per leg, the two choke points where the passage narrowed and where the ceiling came down to less than a meter of clearance. "Okafor first. She's injured but she's calm, and calm is contagious. Delacruz second, he'll carry the spare tank. Torres third. Then the two researchers — what are their names?"

"Amara and Chen. They haven't stopped apologizing for being terrified, which I told them is the most normal thing they've done in nine days."

"Amara and Chen, fourth and fifth. Reyes last." He paused. "Reyes will want to go last anyway. He'll say it's so he can be sure everyone else is out."

"And is that the real reason?"

Ilo looked at the black water of the tunnel, at the line disappearing into it like a thread into a needle's eye. "No. The real reason is he doesn't trust anyone else to carry the case. And he's right not to. That case is the only reason any of this — " He stopped. Thirty years of not saying things; old habits. "The manifest has to come out. Whatever else happens today, that case comes out."

Sable studied him. She had a way of looking at him that made him feel like one of her mother's map annotations — something being read for what was underneath the ink.

"Then here's what we'll do," she said. "Reyes goes last. He hands the case to me at the last air pocket, and I carry it through. He can hold the line, count us out, do his clipboard thing. He gets his people out and his paper out, and nobody has to trust a stranger with the only thing that matters."

"He'll argue."

"Of course he'll argue. He's been arguing with the ocean for nine days and losing. Let him win one." She shouldered the tank. "Ilo. The choke point at the twelve-meter mark. The ceiling drop."

"I know."

"If someone freezes there — if someone panics in that gap with a regulator in their mouth —"

"Then you put your hand on the back of their head and you turn them toward the light and you push," Ilo said. "That's how it's done. That's how it was done on me, once, by a man who didn't come back for his own tank." He met her eyes. "Go. The tide's already thinking about turning."

* * *

**Sable**

Okafor went first, and the tunnel took her the way the ocean takes everything — completely, without comment.

Sable waited at the first air pocket, the little cathedral of trapped breath where the passage kinked, and counted. At four minutes, Okafor's bubbles arrived before she did, silver beads rising through the black like a string of broken rosary. Then her hand found the line, then her head broke water, and she gasped — one huge, tearing breath of the pocket's stale air before Sable pressed the octopus into her hands.

"Breathe it slow. It's not a milkshake."

"Everything you say sounds like an insult," Okafor wheezed.

"That's how you know I like you." Sable took the regulator, checked the gauge — a third gone on one leg, worse than she'd hoped — and passed the primary back down the line to Delacruz, who was already coming. "Two minutes here. Then you follow the line, left hand on it, feet up, and when the ceiling drops you exhale and flatten. You'll feel like the ocean is sitting on you. It is. Let it."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No. It's supposed to be true. Comfort is for people with options."

Delacruz came through like a barge with a plan — slow, deliberate, unstoppable — and Sable decided she liked him too. Torres came through like a ferret, fast and twitchy, and Sable had to catch his ankle at the choke point and hold him for three seconds while his breathing went ragged and then, blessedly, even. Amara and Chen came through together against all instructions, holding hands on the line like schoolchildren, and Sable decided that some rules were written by people who had never been trapped in a cave for nine days.

Then it was Reyes, and the case.

He surfaced into the last pocket with his lips gray and his eyes ferocious, and he held the case out to her the way you hand over something at a border crossing — reluctantly, watching to see what the officer does with it.

"Nobody carries this but me," he said. "That was the deal I made with myself."

"Deal's amended." Sable took it. It was lighter than she expected, which frightened her more than weight would have — all those names, all that tonnage of human cargo, reduced to paper and a hard shell. "You're last out. You'll be on fumes. A man on fumes drops things, and I don't drop things. Ask anyone. It's my single virtue."

Reyes stared at her. Somewhere in the black behind him, the tide made a sound like a door thinking about closing.

"You'll bring it up the ladder yourself," he said finally. "Not Ilo. You."

"Ilo gets a vote on that."

"Ilo gets whatever I say he gets," Reyes said, and there — for one second — was the man who had kept four people alive in a hole for nine days. "Now go. And Sable." He caught her wrist, his grip shockingly strong for a man who'd been eating survey rations and hope. "The tallies on the bulkhead. The ones in the hold. When this is over — someone reads them out loud. All of them. Every mark. That's not a request."

"It's a commission," Sable said. "I accept."

She went down into the tunnel with the case strapped to her chest and her mother's route line under her left hand, and she did not think about the tallies, because thinking about them underwater was a way of drowning, and she had people waiting in the gray light at the other end.

* * *

**Ilo**

They came up one at a time into the grey roar of the storm's held breath, and Ilo pulled each of them over the rocks like a fisherman who had been promised a catch and refused to believe it until his hands were full.

Okafor. Delacruz. Torres, who kissed the rock, which Ilo found entirely reasonable. Amara and Chen, still holding hands, still apologizing. And then the water went still, and the four-hour window became three, and then the stillness broke and Reyes came up last, coughing half the tunnel out of his lungs, and Ilo decided that if the man died now, after all of it, he would take it personally.

He didn't die. He lay on the rocks breathing like a bellows and staring at the sky as if he intended to file a complaint with it.

"Everyone," he croaked.

"Everyone," Ilo said.

"The case?"

Ilo looked at Sable, who was climbing the last few meters with the case strapped to her chest and her mask pushed up, and who was — he would swear to this later, under oath if it came to that — smiling.

"Ask the courier," she said.

Reyes sat up. He looked at the case, at Sable, at the grey sky, at his four living people huddled under the overhang with their tags and their frostbite and their impossible, unreasonable aliveness. And then the team leader of the Isla Serena survey team, the man who had held nine days of dark together with a clipboard and a stubbornness like rebar, put his face in his hands and cried — briefly, fiercely, the way men cry when they've scheduled it between tasks — and then stood up and said, "Right. Down to the boats. We're not out yet."

"He's right," Ilo said. "We're not out yet."

Because the tide was turning. Because the window was closing behind them like a mouth. And because, far out across the grey water, where the channel met the open sea, Ilo's eyes — old navy eyes, eyes that had spent thirty years pretending not to see things — had already found it and could not un-find it.

A shape on the horizon. Low, dark, deliberate.

A patrol skiff, coming back for its crates.
