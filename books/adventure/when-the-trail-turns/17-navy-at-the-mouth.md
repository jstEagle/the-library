## 17. Navy at the Mouth

The last survivor came up out of the water like something being born.

Sable hauled on his arm while Reyes pushed from below, and together they got him over the gunwale of the skiff they'd borrowed from the survey camp—a battered aluminum thing with a motor that coughed twice before it caught. The man was young, maybe twenty-two, lips gray, hands shaking so hard he couldn't grip the rail. His name was Okafor, and the first thing he said when he could say anything at all was, "I dropped the spare light."

"Welcome to the club," Sable said. "I've lost three this month."

He laughed, or tried to, and it turned into coughing, and she pounded his back and looked past him at the black mouth of the cave behind them, where Ilo's head broke the surface a moment later, then Reyes, then the last two—Vasquez and the quiet one everyone called Doc, though Sable had gathered she wasn't that kind of doctor.

Six survivors. Six people who had spent nine days in a flooded tomb breathing off air pockets and one salvaged regulator rig, eating nothing but emergency rations and stubbornness. They were alive. That was the miracle. Sable kept repeating it to herself because there was no room for anything else—not yet, not with the sky still bruised purple from the storm's last tantrum and the water around them flat as poured glass.

Too flat.

"Ilo," she said quietly.

He'd already seen it. He was standing chest-deep beside the cave mouth, holding the tow line, and his face had gone the particular shade of stillness she'd learned to read as *trouble*. He tilted his chin toward the horizon to the south, where the clouds were breaking apart, and where—far off, small as a fingernail clipping—a white shape sat against the darkening sea.

The patrol skiff.

"They're early," Reyes said. She'd climbed halfway up the rocks to get a better angle, and her voice came down flat and hard, stripped of everything but function. "They weren't due back until Thursday."

"Storm changed their schedule too," Ilo said. "Or they got impatient." He waded toward the aluminum skiff, water streaming off him. "How long to get everyone back to the Mariposa?"

"Twenty minutes if we run flat out," Sable said. "Fifteen if we don't care about waking the dead with the engine noise."

"And how long for them?" He nodded at the white speck.

Sable did the math she didn't want to do. "Twelve."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The survivors looked at each other, and Sable watched the hope that had carried them through the relay dive—the one regulator, the narrow passage, Reyes counting breaths like a miser counting coins—begin to curdle into something older and more familiar. Vasquez started to cry without making any sound about it, which somehow made it worse.

"No," Reyes said. It came out like a hammer strike. "We did not spend nine days in a hole in the earth to get taken on a beach. Sable. You know these waters. Tell me we have options."

Sable looked at the mangrove line to the north—a green wall along the shore, cut through with channels she'd scouted two days ago while repairing the hull, channels barely wide enough for the Mariposa, let alone a patrol skiff with a deep draft. She looked at the Mariposa herself, moored in the lee of the point, her patched hull holding, her fuel low, her whole tired self suddenly the most important object in the world.

Then she looked at Ilo, and found him already looking at her, and understood that they were having the same thought, which was either comforting or terrifying depending on how you counted.

"The dinghy," they said at the same time.

* * *

The dinghy was the Mariposa's tender, and it had not been well since the storm dragged it half under and filled it with seawater and shame. Its transom was cracked, its oars were gone—one lost, one broken—and it rode at anchor looking like a dog that knew it was about to be blamed for something.

"It won't float long," Sable said, dragging it onto the sand. "Half an hour, maybe less, before it takes on enough to sit low."

"That's all we need," Ilo said. "Low and moving."

The plan assembled itself the way plans do in emergencies—fast, ugly, held together with string. The flare kit from the Mariposa's locker: six flares, a signal pistol, and a length of fuse cord Ilo had been carrying since God knew when, because men like Ilo always carried things like that, and Sable had stopped asking. He rigged the flares along the dinghy's bench seats, wired to the fuse, wired to a cheap alarm clock he'd bought in Puerto Hondo and never once used to wake up, because Ilo woke the way cats did, instantly and without apology.

"When the clock closes the circuit, the flares go," he said, crouched over the tangle of wire with a soldering iron he'd heated over a cigarette lighter. "All six, staggered by a few seconds. From offshore, in the dark—" he glanced at the sky, where the last light was draining away like water down a plughole, "—it'll look like a running boat. Engine noise will help. We tie the dinghy's painter to a drift weight and let the current carry it south, toward the channel. Toward them."

"And when they catch it," Sable said, "they find an empty dinghy full of burned flares."

"After they've shot at it for a while."

She looked at him. "You're very calm about the shooting part."

"I'm extremely calm about the shooting part," Ilo agreed, and there was something underneath the calm that she recognized now, having heard his confession by firelight—the guilt of a man who had been on the other side of a navy skiff once, decades ago, and had done nothing. This was him doing something. She decided not to take it away from him.

Reyes appeared at her elbow, carrying the manifest wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, because she carried it everywhere now, slept with it, would probably be buried with it. "The survivors can't run the mangroves in the dark," she said. "Okafor can barely stand."

"They won't have to run far." Sable pointed. "There's a cut about four hundred meters north—deep enough for the skiff at half tide, and it opens into a pool behind the mangroves big enough to hide a fleet. We put everyone in the aluminum boat, run dark, no lights, hug the shoreline until the last possible minute. While they're busy chasing your ghost dinghy, we slip into the cut."

"And if they don't chase the ghost?"

"Then we're swimming," Sable said, "and I'll be honest, my plan after that is mostly swearing."

Something that might have been a laugh went around the group—thin, exhausted, but real. Even Okafor managed it. Sable had learned something in the last week about laughter: it wasn't the opposite of fear. It was what fear used to hold itself together.

* * *

They moved in the last ten minutes of light, fast and silent, a chain of soaked people passing gear hand to hand. Sable ferried the survivors to the aluminum skiff in two runs, the little motor idling so low it was more suggestion than sound. Vasquez had to be lifted. Doc climbed in under her own power and then took Okafor's pulse with two fingers, purely out of habit, and told him he was fine, and he believed her, because everyone believed Doc.

Ilo finished the rig with four minutes to spare, tested the circuit twice with a pencil lead, then stood and looked at the dinghy sitting there with its cargo of hidden fire.

"You know," Sable said, "my mother would have loved you. Terrible influence, both of you."

"High praise."

"The highest." She hesitated, then put her hand on his shoulder. It was the first time she'd touched him on purpose, and neither of them made a production of it. "Don't be on that beach when it goes. Whatever happens, don't be near it."

"I'll be in the mangroves before you are."

"Liar. You'll watch the whole thing."

"Yes," Ilo said simply. "I will. Someone should see it. Someone should remember exactly what they do."

And because Sable had spent her whole life around people who left—her mother first among them, always halfway out the door with salt in her ledger and one foot on the next tide—that sentence landed somewhere deep and stayed there.

* * *

The aluminum skiff ran north with its engine muffled by a blanket over the cowl, which was illegal, ineffective, and the best they could do. Sable steered by memory and moon-shadow, keeping the darker mass of the land on her right, counting seconds in her head. Behind them, the mangrove wall slid past, close enough to touch. In the boat, nobody spoke. Six survivors, one ex-navy man turned ghost, and the manifest riding in Reyes' jacket like a second heartbeat.

At 1941 by Sable's watch, the southern horizon lit up.

It started as a single bloom of red-white light, low over the water, and then another, and another, six in all, staggered just as Ilo had promised, painting the clouds from below in ugly festive colors. A heartbeat later the sound arrived—flat cracks rolling across the water, like the world's loneliest fireworks display.

"There," Vasquez whispered. "Look."

Far south, the white shape of the patrol skiff had come alive. Its searchlight swung wildly, caught the burning dinghy adrift in the current, and locked on. Even at this distance they could hear the engine note change as it came about, throttling up, closing on the decoy.

"They bought it," Okafor breathed. "They actually—"

"Hold on," Sable said, and turned the bow into the cut.

The mangrove channel swallowed them. It was narrower than she remembered, or the night was darker than she'd planned for; roots reached out of the black water on both sides like the fingers of drowned men, and twice the hull kissed them with a soft wooden knock that made everyone flinch. The air inside the mangroves was warm and thick and smelled of mud and rot and, improbably, flowers—some night-blooming thing hanging over the water, sweetening the swamp. Sable thought her mother would have written a poem about that, and hated it, and written it anyway.

They rounded the last bend into the pool and killed the engine, and Sable shipped the oar and poled them into the deepest shadow beneath an arch of roots, and then there was nothing to do but listen.

* * *

What came next lasted eleven minutes. Sable knew because she watched her watch the entire time, its dial turned down to nothing, feeling the minutes pass like stones dropping into a well.

First, the searchlight swept the coastline north of the decoy—methodical, patient, the way a man pats his pockets for keys he's sure are in his other coat. Then the skiff's engine slowed near the burning dinghy, and a voice crackled across the water, distorted by distance and a loudhailer, words unreadable but tone unmistakable: challenge, command, the grammar of authority.

Then the gunfire started.

It didn't sound like the movies. It sounded like someone tearing an enormous sheet of canvas, over and over, the reports stacking on each other across the water until they blurred into a single ragged roar. Muzzle flashes strobed the mangrove trunks orange. Something whined overhead—ricochet or wild rounds, impossible to tell—and Vasquez made a small sound and Doc's hand found hers in the dark.

Beside Sable, Reyes had gone rigid. Not afraid, Sable realized. Furious. She could feel it coming off the woman like heat off a stove, a fury so complete it had passed through rage and come out the other side as something cold and structural, load-bearing. This was what they had done to her team, Sable thought. This was the sound that had chased them into a hole in the ocean floor. And now it was tearing apart an empty dinghy, spending bullets on driftwood and flare casings, because the navy's answer to questions had always been volume.

The firing stopped. Started again, shorter. Stopped.

A long silence. Then the loudhailer again, closer now—they were pulling alongside the wreck—and Sable heard, faintly, over the idling engines, a sailor's voice, young and confused:

"Skipper... there's nobody aboard. It's just flares. It's—it's rigged to a clock, sir. Somebody built this."

Another voice, older, harder, saying something Sable couldn't make out. But she could imagine it. She could imagine it perfectly: the officer standing in his own searchlight, looking at a clock ticking in a gutted dinghy, understanding that he had been played, and doing the arithmetic of what that meant—whoever built this knew the patrol schedule, knew the waters, knew the cave, knew the team. Whoever built this had been inside the operation, or close enough to touch it.

The silence that followed had a new quality. Not confusion anymore. Hunting.

"Go," Sable whispered. "Go now, go now, go—"

The aluminum skiff backed out of the pool with the blanket still muffling its engine, and they ran north along the shore, low and dark, while behind them the searchlight began to sweep the mangrove line with slow, terrible thoroughness, and the loudhailer called out into the night in a voice trained to sound reasonable:

"This is a naval safety operation. All persons in the area are advised to remain clear. If you require assistance, signal immediately."

*Assistance.* Sable's hands shook on the tiller, and it wasn't only fear. It was fury, braided tight through the terror strand by strand until she couldn't tell which was driving. Nine days those people had survived on air pockets and ration crumbs while the navy searched the coast—not for them, never for them, but for loose ends. And now the same voice that had shot up an empty boat was offering *assistance* to anyone listening, in the warm reasonable tone of a man inviting you to step into a sack.

The Mariposa rose out of the dark ahead, moored in the lee of the point, and Sable felt her eyes sting and blamed the salt spray, knowing perfectly well it wasn't spray.

They got everyone aboard in under four minutes—hands hauling, Doc boosting, Okafor practically thrown over the rail—and then Ilo was there too, appearing out of the mangroves like smoke condensing, soaked to the ribs, alive, and Sable grabbed his wrist and checked his pulse like Doc would have, and he let her, and said nothing, which was its own kind of promise.

"Cast off," Reyes said. "Get us out of here."

But Sable stood at the wheel a moment longer, looking south, where the searchlight still swept and swept, patient as a lighthouse, patient as grief.

"They'll figure it out by morning," she said. "Empty dinghy, no bodies, no wreckage worth the name. They'll know we went north, and they'll know about the Mariposa eventually—she's registered, she's visible, she's mine." She looked at Ilo. "Every port within two hundred miles will have our description by tomorrow night."

"Then we don't go to any port," Ilo said.

"We have six people who need a hospital and a manifest that needs daylight," Reyes said sharply. "Where exactly do we go that isn't a port?"

Sable looked at the chart spread under the binnacle light—at her mother's penciled annotations, the half-finished lines, the small careful handwriting that had led her here—and an idea surfaced slowly, the way wrecks surface, stern-first and inevitable.

"My mother drew a route," she said. "Northwest, through the outer shoals. She marked a cove on Isla Canela—no port, no radio station, one fishing family she trusted enough to write a name next to. It's forty miles of open water and shoals no navy pilot bothers memorizing." She met Reyes' eyes. "Your contacts on the mainland. How fast can they move once they have the manifest in hand?"

Reyes was quiet for a moment. Then, slowly, the cold structural fury in her face shifted into something else—something that looked almost like hope wearing armor.

"Faster than the navy can type a press release," she said. "Get me to a working transmitter and twenty-four hours of head start, and this ends in headlines, not a classified file."

Sable put the wheel over. The Mariposa came about, groaning, her patched hull complaining, and headed northwest into the dark, toward the open water and the storm's last argument, with the searchlight shrinking astern like a dying star.

Behind them, faint across the miles, the loudhailer was still calling its reasonable offers to the empty night.

Nobody answered it.
