## 18. What We Owe the Dead

The sea cave they found was not on any chart, which by now struck Reyes as the only honest kind of geography left in this archipelago. It sat behind a curtain of falling water where a stream spilled off the cliffs, wide enough at the mouth to take the Mariposa's tender and deep enough inland that the storm's noise arrived muffled, like gossip through a wall.

Reyes had been hiding in worse places. He'd hidden in a drainage culvert outside a naval base once, for eleven hours, with a folder of documents taped to his ribs and a guard dog patrolling six feet above his head. That had been for a matter involving misfiled fuel invoices. This was considerably larger.

He counted heads the way he always did when he needed to feel useful: three of his own survey team—himself, Okonkwo, and Priya, hollow-eyed and salt-crusted but alive. Sable Villalobos, who moved like someone who had never once waited for permission. Ilo, whose real name was Ilián Ferro and whose file Reyes had read twice before ever boarding her boat, though he saw no reason to mention that yet. And the manifest itself, sealed in a waterproof case that Sable carried the way other people carried infants.

Six mouths. One airline, as the diver had put it during the relay out—a phrase so grimly funny that Priya had laughed until she cried, and then just cried, and Reyes had held her shoulders in chest-deep water while she got it out of her system.

Now there was firelight, such as it was. They'd built it small, behind a windbreak of stacked coral slabs, because cold was its own danger and because Ilo argued that smoke from a wet-driftwood fire would read as nothing more than fishermen sheltering from the monsoon. "Everyone on this coast is hiding from something this week," he said. "We'll be invisible by being ordinary."

Reyes crouched near the flame and warmed his hands and thought about how many times in his career he had used that exact logic to justify exactly the opposite.

* * *

It was Priya who started it, because Priya never could leave a question alone, even questions with teeth.

"So what happens Monday?" she asked. She was wrapped in two emergency blankets and one of Sable's spare shirts, and she looked, Reyes thought, about nineteen years old, which was unfair, since she was thirty-four and had a doctorate. "Hypothetically. We get to the mainland. We walk into an office. Whose office? Because I've been thinking about it, and every office I can think of has a flag over the door."

"Mine," Reyes said. "Technically."

"Technically," Okonkwo repeated, from the shadows where he'd been quietly re-taping his blistered feet. He said it the way you'd say *haunted*.

"I'm senior enough," Reyes went on, "that my report would go up the chain. And the chain—" He stopped. There was no graceful way to finish that sentence, so he didn't. The fire popped. Somewhere back in the dark of the cave, water dripped with the patience of a metronome.

"The chain swallowed your team," Sable said. Not cruelly. Just laying it on the table like a tool. "Four people walked into that camp. One came out of the water alive because he happened to be on the far side of the island taking sediment cores." She nodded at Okonkwo. "You were going to hand them a report?"

"I was going to hand them the truth," Reyes said, and heard immediately how it sounded.

"You were going to hand them a manila envelope," Sable said, "and hope they were having a good day."

He wanted to argue. He was, by training and temperament, an arguer; he had won promotions with it. But he'd also spent nine days in a flooded cave eating tinned peaches and listening to the tide decide whether he lived, and somewhere in there his faith in good days had taken on water.

"He's not wrong, though," Priya said, surprising everyone, maybe including herself. "About the risk. If we go public—if we just *give it away*—we lose control of it. Anyone who gets their hands on that manifest first gets to shape the story. Or bury it. Or sit on it for twenty years and release a redacted version with all the names gone." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I've read those releases. The names are always gone. The names are the whole point, and they're always gone."

"And if we go through channels," Sable said, "the names are gone too. Faster, and with better stationery."

Ilo laughed—one short, startled bark—and then looked apologetic about it. "Sorry. It's just that you two are arguing about which courier loses the package."

"Then what's your answer?" Reyes asked him. "You've had decades to think about it. You knew what was down there before any of us did."

That landed harder than Reyes intended. He watched it land—Ilo's face going still, the firelight finding new lines in it. Reyes had spent the last several days deciding how he felt about the man: ex-navy, self-appointed penitent, the kind of guilt that either curdles or clarifies. So far it seemed to have clarified. But trust was a different currency than sympathy, and Reyes was nearly broke.

"My answer," Ilo said slowly, "is that I spent thirty years believing the right filing cabinet would fix it. I wrote reports. I sent letters. I talked to a journalist once, in '09, and he called me back very excited, and then he called me back very quiet, and then he stopped calling." He fed a stick to the fire. "The cabinet doesn't fix it. But neither does shouting into the square, necessarily. What fixes things is when too many people know at once for it to be unwound. Families. Press. Same day. Before anyone can get between them."

"That's the plan you already described," Reyes said. "On the boat."

"Yes."

"It requires contacts I don't fully believe exist."

"They exist," Ilo said. "One of them owes me. Several of them think I'm dead, which makes the story better."

Okonkwo snorted. "Nothing says credibility like a source rising from the grave."

"It worked for Lazarus," Priya said, and then pressed her hand over her mouth, and then laughed anyway, helplessly, the way people do after fear—the laugh coming out sideways, carrying more than jokes. Even Sable smiled, briefly, like sun through squall.

But the smile faded, and she leaned forward into the firelight, and Reyes recognized the posture. He'd seen it in briefing rooms before the launch of operations that went wrong. It was the posture of someone about to say the thing everyone else was circling.

* * *

"Can I tell you what's in my head?" Sable said. "And you can all hate it."

"We're listening," Reyes said.

"There are forty-one names on that manifest." She said it flatly, without performance, and Reyes understood she'd memorized them. All of them. He'd read the manifest four times himself and couldn't have managed ten. "Forty-one people who were put on a ship as cargo, in hold two, and the ship was scuttled in shallow water close enough to shore that some of them might have heard surf. Some of them survived the sinking. We know because they scratched tallies on the bulkhead, and the tallies go on for days." Her voice stayed level. That was somehow worse. "Somebody's mother is still alive somewhere wondering what happened to her son in 1974. Somebody's granddaughter grew up with a photograph on a shelf and a story that ends with *lost at sea*, which is a lie somebody official signed off on."

Nobody spoke. The waterfall filled the silence.

"I've been paid to not know things before," Sable went on. "It's a whole industry. You salvage something, the client pays extra for discretion, and you take the money, because rent exists. And every time, there's this itch. Like wearing someone else's coat." She turned the waterproof case over in her hands. "I promised myself on that reef that I was done being the coat. So here's what's in my head: the families find out first. Not a ministry. Not a hearing. A woman in a kitchen gets a phone call, or a knock, or a letter, and hears her brother's name spoken out loud by a person, not a press release. Then the press, same hour, so nobody can make her crazy or make her disappear. Then, fine—then the hearings, the inquiries, whatever machinery wants to grind. Let it grind. But the names go home first."

"That's—" Priya began.

"Reckless," Reyes finished. "It's reckless. If we surface on the mainland and start making calls, we're identifiable within hours. Six people who vanished from a government survey, reappearing next to stolen classified material. They won't arrest us quietly. They can't afford to let us talk at all."

"Then we shouldn't be identifiable," Sable said. "You're the survey lead. You know the protocols. Dead drops, cutouts, public drop boxes. You people taught courses on this."

"We taught courses on protecting *data*," Reyes said. "Not on handing grandmothers the worst news of their lives gently."

"Gently isn't the assignment," Sable said. "Honestly is."

And there it was. Reyes sat with it. He thought about his own career, the long respectable arc of it: the careful memos, the findings that reached the third floor and stopped, the promotion that came six months after the report about the fuel invoices died in a drawer. He had told himself for thirty years that working inside was the responsible choice, that the system corrected itself if you kept feeding it accurate paper. He had believed it the way you believe a hull is sound—right up until the water came in.

"Can I say something cowardly?" he asked.

"Please," said Okonkwo. "It'll be a change of pace."

"If we do it your way," Reyes said to Sable, "some of us may not come through this free. Possibly not alive. I've read the internal manuals. I helped write one of them, God forgive me." He rubbed his face. "And my first instinct—the instinct I've been trained into for three decades—is to protect the operation. Protect the team. Get us ashore, get ourselves safe, and then fight."

"Your first instinct," Sable said, "is to protect the team. And the families aren't the team."

"No," Reyes admitted. "They never are. That's the whole problem with institutions. Everyone inside is somebody's team, and everybody outside is paperwork."

The fire cracked. Priya had gone very still, listening the way she listened to instruments—totally, hungrily. Okonkwo had stopped taping his feet.

"But," Reyes said, and held up a finger, because he was constitutionally incapable of surrendering without a rider, "I've also spent nine days in a hole watching tallies on a bulkhead, and I can't unread them. Forty-one names. And I keep thinking—" His voice did something embarrassing, and he pushed through it. "I keep thinking about the last tally. Someone marked the wall knowing no one would count it. Knowing the counting was the whole act. Witness for its own sake. And I thought: the least I can do is be the counter. Out loud. Where it costs something."

Sable nodded slowly. "So you're with me."

"I'm with the dead," Reyes said. "You happen to be pointing the same direction."

"Best kind of ally," said Okonkwo. "Agreement by conscience. Very durable. Doesn't dissolve in rain."

Priya wiped her eyes and laughed at the same time, which Reyes was learning was simply what survival looked like on this island. "Then it's settled? Families first, press same day, then we burn our own names down doing it?"

"Not settled," Reyes said. "Planned. Settled implies we voted, and I want it on the record that I resisted for a full ninety seconds before agreeing, for legal purposes."

"For legal purposes," Sable repeated, deadpan, "you resisted heroically."

"Ninety seconds is heroic, in committee terms."

That broke the room open—laughter, real laughter, ragged and loud in the stone throat of the cave, bouncing off wet rock. Reyes let himself have it. Fear and grief had been sitting on his chest for days, and laughter was the only crowbar that moved them, even briefly. He noticed Ilo laughing too, quieter, watching Sable with an expression Reyes filed away under *later*.

* * *

Later, when the others had curled into blankets along the wall—Okonkwo already snoring with the untroubled depth of a man who had decided the watch order was someone else's problem—Reyes found Sable standing at the cave mouth, looking out at the storm.

The rain had eased to a hard gray drizzle, and beyond it the sea ran in long confused swells, whitecaps shredded sideways by wind. The Mariposa rode her mangrove berth somewhere out in that murk, and Reyes thought about what it cost a person to love a boat in weather like this.

"You should sleep," he said. "You did most of the swimming today."

"So did you."

"I'm older. My suffering is traditional."

She huffed something like a laugh. For a while they stood together, two people watching a storm consider them.

"The plan needs a boat," she said finally. "Your contacts, Ilo's dead-drop theater, all of it—it happens on the mainland. Which means crossing that." She tipped her chin at the water. "In the Mariposa. With a patched hull I wouldn't trust to cross a harbor in calm."

"The forecast says the surge breaks tomorrow night. After that, a lull."

"A lull isn't calm. A lull is the ocean clearing its throat." She was quiet a moment. "There's a bar crossing at Puerto Hondo that will be ugly even in the lull. And the evidence locker weighs forty pounds and floats badly."

Reyes heard the rest of the sentence before she said it, because he'd spent a career listening for the sentence underneath the sentence.

"You're going to have to choose," he said. "Boat or locker."

"I know."

"Your mother's boat."

"I know." She said it steadily, but her thumb was moving over the waterproof case, back and forth, like a rosary. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me when the water's actually trying to kill me. Tonight I'd give you the noble answer, and tomorrow I need the true one."

"That's the healthiest thing anyone's said on this island," Reyes said. "Including me."

Out past the reef line, lightning stitched the horizon—once, twice—and thunder came rolling in late and lazy, like the storm clearing its own paperwork. And beneath the thunder, faint but unmistakable, Reyes caught another sound: an engine. Low, steady, wrong for fishing boats in this weather, wrong for anything civilian.

Sable heard it too. Her head came up. Down the coast, half-hidden by rain, a running light blinked—white, then red, then white again—moving with the unhurried confidence of something that owned the water it stood in.

"They're early," she breathed.

Reyes watched the light crawl along the coast toward the mangroves, toward the Mariposa, and felt the debate they'd just finished become suddenly, urgently academic.

"Wake the others," he said. "Quietly."
