## 19. Storm's Last Argument

The monsoon gave us three days of borrowed calm, and we spent them like gamblers who'd finally learned the value of a chip.

Reyes and his two survivors—Okonkwo, who hadn't slept a full night since the cave and probably wouldn't for a year, and a wiry young surveyor named Pell who kept apologizing for things that weren't his fault—came aboard the *Mariposa* along with everything that mattered: the manifest in its waterproof sleeve, Reyes' field notebooks, the photographs of the bulkhead tallies that Sable had taken with the survey team's own camera because some proofs you want in duplicate. The sealed evidence locker itself was a steel case the size of a sea chest, salvaged from the camp, its latch welded shut by Ilo with a torch he'd produced from a duffel bag I had decided, after much internal debate, not to ask about.

"Six people, one boat rated for four," I said on the morning we left Isla Serena, watching the mangroves slide astern. "That's not a crew complement, that's a dare."

"The sea respects audacity," Ilo said.

"The sea has never respected anything in its life. That's rather the problem."

But the sky held. Gray, bruised at the edges, holding anyway—the way a tired dog holds a sit when there's bacon involved. We ran south-southwest through the channel between Serena and the outer shoals, then turned west for the mainland crossing, forty nautical miles of open water that under ordinary circumstances would have been a pleasant day's run and under these circumstances was a coin toss with a two-headed coin.

Ilo took the first watch at the helm. Reyes sat in the cockpit going over his notes one more time, as if repetition could make the names heavier, harder to lose. Okonkwo slept sitting up, which is a skill I associate with soldiers and new parents. Pell helped me check the bilge pump for the fourth time, mostly, I think, to have something to do with his hands.

"You know," he said, "when I signed up for this survey, the brochure mentioned coral biodiversity. It did not mention—" he gestured vaguely at everything, "—all this."

"They never do. The brochures are written by people whose worst day at work is a paper jam."

"I used to have paper jams," he said wistfully.

"Hold onto that. When this is over, go find yourself a nice office. Jam some paper. In my experience it's underrated."

* * *

The barometer started lying to us around noon.

I say lying because that's what it felt like—a slow, dishonest sag, the needle drifting down like a man sliding off a barstool while insisting he's fine. By one o'clock the wind had backed two points. By two, the horizon to the northwest had gone the color of a bruise you get doing something you're ashamed of.

"That's not weather," Ilo said quietly. "That's an argument."

"It's the last argument," I said. "Monsoons always save their best material for the finale. Like opera."

We shortened sail—or would have, if the *Mariposa* had been a sailboat, which she isn't; instead we battened everything down, double-clamped the hatches, lashed the dinghy until it looked like a mummy, and put the sea anchor over the bow when the first real gusts arrived. I'd ridden out weather before. Everyone who works water has. But there's riding out weather in a harbor, and riding out weather offshore, and then there's this: being caught in the open with a damaged hull, six souls, and a cargo that half the world's navies would prefer at the bottom of the sea.

By four the waves had organized themselves into something with intent. Not chaos—chaos would have been almost restful. This was rhythm, long gray hills marching out of the northwest with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. The *Mariposa* climbed each one and slid down the far side with a shudder I felt in my teeth.

"She's taking water faster," I called down into the cabin. The bilge pump was running every ninety seconds now instead of every five minutes. The repair patch I'd laid over the coral gouge back in chapter eight—metaphorically speaking, chapter eight of my life, though it felt like an actual serialized novel by then—was holding, but holding the way a handshake holds between people who don't trust each other.

"How much faster?" Ilo shouted up from the helm, salt streaming off his hood.

"Faster than I'd like! Slower than catastrophic! Somewhere in the middle, where all the interesting decisions live!"

Reyes appeared beside me, gripping the rail, his face green but resolute. "What can we do?"

"Bail if the pump dies. Pray if the pump doesn't. Standard maritime procedure covers both."

* * *

It happened at dusk, because of course it did. Catastrophe keeps banker's hours.

A wave came out of the dark larger than its brothers—not a rogue, exactly, just the tallest kid in a tall family—and the *Mariposa* took it wrong. Not head-on, not beam-on, but in that awkward quartering way that makes a boat twist like a fish on a line. I heard the patch let go. Not saw, not felt—heard, a sound like heavy tape peeling off wet glass, transmitted straight through the hull into my boots, and I knew before the pump cycled what it meant.

The pump ran. And ran. And didn't stop.

"We're shipping too much!" Ilo's voice from below, stripped of everything calm. "Sable—it's coming in fast!"

I went down the companionway into a cabin lit by one swinging lamp, where seawater stood ankle-deep and rising, cold as a debt collector's hand. The patch had peeled from one end, leaving a gash maybe thirty centimeters long breathing through the hull with every wave. Ilo already had the collision mat pressed against it, both arms straining, water jetting around the edges.

"This buys minutes," he gritted. "Not hours. Minutes."

"Then spend them well." My mind was doing the arithmetic sailors do, the cold brutal kind. Distance to mainland: twenty-six miles. Speed in this sea: four knots, maybe five if we were lucky and the sea was feeling generous, which it wasn't. Time: five hours, minimum. Water coming in: faster than a bilge pump and two able-bodied bailers could manage for five hours. Conclusion: the *Mariposa* was dying, and we were all standing inside her.

Unless.

The evidence locker sat lashed to the port settee, eighty kilos of welded steel full of names. Names of the dead in the hold. Names of the vanished survey team. Names that three governments' worth of paperwork had been designed to make weightless, forgettable, gone. Eighty kilos of ballast we did not need, in a boat that needed every gram of buoyancy it could beg, borrow, or steal.

Ilo saw me looking at it. He went very still.

"Sable."

"If she goes down, she goes down with everyone aboard and the case goes down with her. If we lighten her—" I stopped. Because the other option wasn't lightening. The other option was the life raft, the little four-man valise raft packed under the cockpit seat, and the math there was even worse: six people, four seats, open ocean, night, monsoon. No. The raft was the last page of the book, not the next one.

"There's another way," Ilo said, and his voice had gone strange. "You know there's another way."

"I know what you're thinking, and no."

"The case. Over the side. Sealed steel, welded shut—it floats, or near enough. It drifts. Storms push debris ashore, that's how flotsam works, it's practically a delivery service—"

"And it also sinks, or gets found by the wrong beachcomber, or ends up in a navy warehouse by Thursday. Ilo, that case is the only reason any of this happened. Those tallies, that manifest—that's the whole argument. You throw it overboard and we're six witnesses with nothing but memories, and memories drown easier than steel."

"So we sink instead?" His voice cracked on it. "For a box?"

"For the box." I heard myself say it and was surprised by how certain it came out. "My mother spent years circling this island on a map nobody else could read. She died—maybe died—for whatever's in these waters. Reyes' team lost Marcela in that cave. The people in the hold lost everything, including their names, and the whole point of our entire miserable week is to give those names back. I am not throwing them overboard so my boat can float a few hours longer. The *Mariposa* is my mother's. But this—" I put my hand flat on the cold steel. "This is hers too. Hers and theirs. And it outranks me."

The cabin pitched. Water sloshed to mid-shin. Ilo stared at me across the lamplight, and something in his face shifted—the look of a man setting down luggage he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"All right," he said. "All right. Then we fight for both. Get Reyes and Pell bailing. I'll re-lay the patch with the epoxy and the canvas strips—we do it from outside."

"Outside. In that."

"In that." He was already pulling on his fins. "You taught me rope work in a surge, remember? Fair's fair. Besides—" and here he managed, God help him, a smile, "—the sea respects audacity."

"The sea respects nothing, I told you—"

"Then let's be disrespectful together."

* * *

What followed was the worst hour of my life, and I include the cave in that assessment, which is saying something, because the cave had skeletons in it. Literally. Well—not literally, but close enough.

Ilo went over the stern on a tether with the epoxy kit in a bag on his chest, and I fed line and watched his dive light strobe wildly in the black churn beneath the hull. Each time the boat rose on a swell he had a window—seconds—to slap canvas and resin over the gash and hold it while the cure began. Between windows he was underwater, blind, yanked around like a kite in a gale, and all I could do was count the seconds and haul him up when the tether went slack in the wrong way.

Inside, Reyes and Pell bailed with buckets and a cooking pot, and Okonkwo—awake now, silent, tireless—bailed like a man performing penance, which perhaps he was. Nobody spoke except to shout numbers. Gallons are a language all their own.

Twice the patch tore loose and Ilo had to redo it. The second time he came up gasping with a gash on his forehead where the hull had kissed him, blood diluting instantly in the rain, and he looked at me hanging over the transom and said, "Third time's traditional," and went back down before I could argue.

The third patch held.

Barely. Stubbornly. The way old boats and old people hold—out of habit and spite in equal measure. The pump settled back into a cycle we could almost keep pace with, and the *Mariposa*, lighter by nothing but sweat and pride, clawed westward through the dark at three and a half knots with her heart patched and her bones groaning.

I sat at the helm through the small hours, soaked to the soul, and I will confess something I've confessed to no one but this page: somewhere around three in the morning, with the wind screaming and the case lashed safe behind me and my mother's boat leaking under me, I laughed. Out loud. Alone. Because I had finally understood the joke, the one she'd been setting up my whole life with that half-finished map and that restlessness she'd left me in place of a house.

She hadn't been running from anything. She'd been carrying something. And carrying something, it turns out, looks exactly like running if you don't know what's in the pack.

* * *

Land came at dawn the way land does after a night like that—not dramatically, but shyly, a gray-green suggestion on the horizon that hardened by degrees into hills, then trees, then the honest brown smear of a river mouth. The storm's last argument had blown itself hoarse behind us, grumbling off to bother someone else's morning.

We came into the river channel with the tide, six ghosts and a wounded boat, and grounded her gently on the mud flats below the fishing village just as the engine coughed its final opinion and quit forever. The villagers came out to stare. Word travels fast in places like that; by the time we waded ashore, someone had already sent a boy running for the man with the truck.

Reyes stood beside me on the mud, the evidence locker slung between him and Ilo on a stretcher of oars and line, and looked back at the *Mariposa* listing gently against the falling tide.

"She saved us," he said.

"She leaked on us, is what she did. But yes." I touched her rail once, the way you touch a shoulder at a funeral. "She got us here. That's the whole job description."

"What happens to her now?"

"Now? Now she gets hauled out, dried, and argued with at length. I've sunk twenty years into this boat. I'm not starting a collection." I said it lightly, and mostly meant it, and the part of me that didn't mean it kept quiet, because that part was still learning what letting go actually weighed.

The man with the truck arrived—a broad, sunburnt fellow named Esteban who took one look at six salt-wrecked strangers, a battered trawler, and a welded steel box, and asked precisely zero questions, which either made him the wisest man on the coast or the most dangerous. Ilo shook his hand like they were old friends. Of course they were. Everything about Ilo was old friends.

"Where to?" Esteban asked.

Reyes and Ilo exchanged a glance over the top of the locker, the kind of glance that has a whole committee meeting inside it.

"There's a newspaper editor in San Cristóbal," Reyes said slowly. "An old classmate. If we can reach her before anyone official reaches us—"

"San Cristóbal is six hours by road," Esteban said, "and there is a navy checkpoint at the junction, because there is always a navy checkpoint at the junction."

I looked at the box. At the road. At the horizon behind us, where Serena was a smudge and the cave was a secret and the tallies on the bulkhead waited in the dark for the world to learn how to read them.

"Then we'd best not be official," I said. "Anyone official would take the main road."

Esteban grinned like a man who'd been waiting all morning for permission. "There is another road," he said. "It is not a good road."

"Wonderful," said Ilo. "Those are the only ones we're any good at."

We loaded the locker into the truck bed and covered it with fishing nets, and as the truck pulled out I took one last look back at the *Mariposa*, alone on the mud with the tide going out around her, patient as a promise. Hold on, I told her. I finished my mother's map. I'm not done finishing things.

The road to San Cristóbal unrolled ahead of us, bad as advertised, and somewhere past the first bend the sun finally broke through—thin, watery, but unmistakably on our side of the sky for the first time in a week.

Behind us, at the navy checkpoint at the junction, a radio crackled with orders to watch for a salvage diver, a former officer, and a steel case.

They were watching the main road.

We were not on it.
