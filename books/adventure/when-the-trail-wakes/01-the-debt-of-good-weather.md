## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The storm came up out of the southwest like something owed, and Ansel Yarrow drove toward it the way a man walks into a bank he knows will ruin him.

He had learned to read weather the way other men read ledgers — by what it promised and what it would actually pay. This one was honest, at least. A supercell with a mesocyclone turning fat and green-black over the flats, hail core glowing that sickly teal on radar that meant stones the size of fists. It would put on a show. Shows paid. And the Vane Syndicate paid for shows, which made the storm, in the narrow arithmetic of Ansel's life, good weather.

"Base is tightening," Pemba said from the passenger seat, his tablet balanced on one knee, stylus moving in quick precise strokes. "Rotation's dropped maybe two hundred feet in the last ten minutes. If you want the wall cloud before it ropes out, you've got about six miles of road and then dirt."

"Dirt's flooded," Odile said from the back, not looking up from the wheel bearing she was repacking in her lap, grease to her elbows like she'd been arm-deep in something born. She always rode with work in her hands, as if stillness were a debt too. "Alder crested last night. Teo saw it."

"I saw it," Teo said. He was wedged against the equipment rack, nineteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who belonged in a truck doing eighty toward a rotating thunderstorm. His knuckles said otherwise. "It was — really brown."

"It was really brown," Ansel agreed, and let it go at that. The kid would learn that rivers after flash flood weren't scenery; they were creditors too, taking whatever they wanted.

Five years. That was how long he'd been measuring his life this way — in miles instead of years, in footage sold instead of seasons lived. Five years since the flood took everything with a name attached to it, and he'd answered by making himself unnameable: a man in a truck, chasing weather across three hundred miles of nowhere, selling the sky's worst moods to people who'd never stand under them.

The Vane Syndicate bought storm footage the way they bought everything else — cheap, in bulk, and with terms you didn't fully understand until the interest bloomed. Ansel had signed with them two winters ago when the truck's transmission died outside Corvane and there was no one left to borrow from who wasn't already a creditor. The contract had been four pages. He'd read all of them. He'd understood perhaps two.

"You're smiling," Pemba observed.

"I'm concentrating."

"You're smiling at a tornado like it owes you money."

"It does," Ansel said. "Everything does."

* * *

They caught the storm where the county road gave up and became a suggestion. Ansel pulled off onto the shoulder with the practiced violence of a man who'd done it four hundred times, and the crew spilled out into air that tasted like copper and cut hay. The wall cloud hung ahead of them over the flats, lowering, breathing, dragging a skirt of rain that turned the horizon into wet charcoal.

"Tripod's yours," Ansel told Teo. "Stay behind me. If I say run, you don't ask which direction — you just match me."

"What if we get separated?"

"We won't."

"But—"

"Teo." Ansel looked at him, and the kid shut up. There was a version of this speech that involved kindness, patience, mentorship. Ansel had never found it. What he had instead was competence, and competence said: keep the rookie alive long enough for him to stop being one.

The wind arrived first, warm and wrong, smelling of ozone and torn earth. Then the rain came sideways, and then the sound — that freight-train roar people always described and never got right, because it wasn't a train, it was the sky tearing along a seam nobody had known was there.

Ansel filmed. He filmed the way he did everything now, with a focus so total it felt less like attention than anesthesia. The funnel touched down a half-mile north, a gray rope writhing against the green, and he tracked it smooth and steady while behind him Teo shouted questions the wind ate whole. Somewhere in the middle distance a barn lost its roof in sheets of tin that caught the light like fish. The tornado walked across the flats for eleven minutes, unhurried, a landlord collecting rent, and then it lifted, and the silence afterward rang worse than the noise.

"Got it?" Pemba asked, when Ansel lowered the camera.

"Got it." Eleven minutes of clean footage. At Syndicate rates, after their cut, after the interest payment due Friday, that was maybe nine days of staying ahead of the collectors. He didn't say the number out loud. Numbers spoken became real, and real numbers had weight, and weight slowed a man down.

Odile was already back at the truck, and she was standing very still beside the rear axle, which was never a good sign. Odile stillness ranked somewhere between a check-engine light and a funeral.

"What," Ansel said.

"U-joint," she said. "It's been singing for a week. Now it's screaming."

"How long can it scream?"

She wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth and gave him the look she reserved for questions that insulted them both. "To Corvane? Maybe. Past Corvane, into the hills? You're gambling."

"We're always gambling."

"Yeah," she said quietly. "That's kind of what I'm talking about."

* * *

They limped into the fuel stop at Mile 88 as the light went amber behind them, the storm walking away east with its pockets full of somebody's fences. Ansel topped the tank while Odile crawled underneath with a flashlight clamped in her teeth, and Pemba spread the day's take across the tailgate — camera cards, log sheets, the small bureaucratic harvest of terror.

"Vance called," Pemba said, without looking up.

Ansel's hand stopped on the pump handle. "Maro Vance called you?"

"He called the shop line. Odile's cousin took a message." Pemba finally raised his eyes, and there was something in them Ansel hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but its careful cousin. "He says your account's been flagged for review. He wants to meet. Not Friday. Sooner."

"Sooner meaning?"

"He said, and I'm quoting, 'before the weather turns.'"

Ansel looked west, where the Serrano Range stood blue and indifferent above the flats, already wearing its first suggestion of snow. Then he looked south, where the Alder River ran high and brown through the low country, swollen with the same storm system they'd just chased.

Before the weather turns. Vance had a collector's instinct for phrasing — he always made threats sound like favors, deadlines sound like mercy.

"Flagged for review," Ansel repeated. "I've missed nothing. I've paid every—"

"Every payment," Pemba agreed. "On time. Every one. Which is why it doesn't make sense." He folded the log sheets with unnecessary precision. "Unless they're not reviewing the account. Unless they're reviewing you."

The pump clicked off. Ansel stood there a moment with the nozzle in his hand, watching fuel drip, thinking about contracts with four pages and the two of them he'd understood.

Behind the truck, Odile's voice floated up from beneath the axle, muffled and grim: "Ansel. You're going to want to see this."

He crouched. Her flashlight beam lay along the frame rail, and bolted there — welded, actually, welded with a neatness no mechanic of theirs had ever managed — was a small steel plate stamped with a serial number and a single word:

**VANE.**

"They've been tracking us," Odile said. Her face, upside down in the flashlight glow, was perfectly calm, which was how he knew she was furious. "How long, do you figure? Since the contract?"

"Since before the contract," Ansel heard himself say. "You don't weld a plate onto a truck you haven't decided to own."

Above them the evening went dark, and far to the south, over the drowned lowlands, lightning flickered inside a cloud with no thunder attached — heat lightning, or the storm reloading, or just the world flashing its ledger open for one white instant before closing it again.

Pemba cleared his throat. "There's one more thing. The message. Vance left a number to call back." He hesitated. "And a second line, written smaller. It says: *We hear you found something in the river.*"

Ansel straightened slowly. They hadn't found anything in the river. Not yet. Not anything anyone could mean.

But the Alder was cresting tonight, and tomorrow it would drop, and it would leave things behind on its banks the way floods always did — and Maro Vance, forty miles away, already knew it.

"Get some sleep," Ansel told his crew, and heard the dread under his own steadiness, thin as ice over moving water. "Tomorrow we drive down to the Alder. I want eyes on our crossing before Vance decides to have some."

Nobody asked why a washed-out ford mattered. Nobody had to. They were a crew, and crews learn to read their driver the way drivers learn to read the sky — by what's coming, and how much time is left.

That night Ansel slept in the truck with the keys in his fist, and dreamed, as he always dreamed, of water rising over a road he knew by heart, and woke before it reached the house, the way he always woke. Five years of miles, and the past still gained on him every single day.

By morning, the river had given something back.
