## 3. A Dead Nation's Signature

The chest sat on Odile's workbench like a patient that had already died once and didn't intend to do it again.

She'd cleaned it through the afternoon while the storm's aftermath dripped off the shop roof in long, arrhythmic strings. River mud had gotten into everything — into the bronze stamping, into the seams of the lid, into a hairline crack along one corner where water had been getting in for years, maybe decades. She'd worked it with rags and soft brushes and finally her own thumbs, and what emerged from under the silt made her stop whistling.

Not a maker's mark. A seal. Pressed into the bronze like wax, like a signature: a mountain with three peaks, a river running beneath it, and around the rim of the impression, letters in an alphabet she didn't know. Angular. Deliberate. The kind of writing that had been designed by people who expected to be read for a thousand years.

"Ansel." She said it quietly first, then again when he didn't look up from the ledger he was pretending to balance. "Ansel. Come look at this."

He came over wiping grease off his hands with a rag that only redistributed it. Teo drifted in behind him, still damp from helping Pemba stake down the awning before the wind came back. Outside, the Alder River had dropped a foot since dawn but still ran brown and muscular, carrying somebody's fence post past the yard at a walking pace.

"That's not a shipping mark," Ansel said.

"No," Odile agreed.

"That's not anything."

"It's something." She turned the chest so the light from the work lamp caught the seal. "It's somebody's whole name, is what it looks like to me."

Teo leaned in close enough that his breath fogged the bronze. "Kestria," he read, then frowned. "Wait. Is that — I've heard that word. Have I heard that word?"

"You haven't," Odile said. "Nobody has. Not in forty years."

She knew because she'd spent twenty minutes with the shop's ancient atlas, the one with the frayed spine she used to prop up the short leg of the parts shelf. She'd gone looking for Kestria the way you'd check whether a stranger's face matched any wanted poster. It wasn't there. It wasn't in the index. And where it should have been — that wedge of territory between the Serrano foothills and the old imperial survey lines — the map showed nothing at all. Not unexplored territory, not disputed land. Just the cartographer's blank courtesy of mountains and a river with no towns marked on it, as if the country itself had been wiped with a wet cloth before the ink dried.

Nations didn't die politely. But they usually left ghosts: border disputes, refugee neighborhoods, stamps in old collections, a dish in someone's restaurant. This one had left less than that. This one had left a chest in a river.

"Lock's river-seized," she said, changing the subject because her hands wanted something to do. "Corroded, probably brass tumbler inside an iron housing, which is a stupid design unless you're trying to keep out exactly one generation of thieves." She held up her pick set, the good one, wrapped in oilcloth. "Give me ten minutes."

"Should we be opening this?" Teo asked.

Ansel looked at the chest for a long moment. Odile watched him do the arithmetic he always did — footage value, scrap value, what Maro Vance would pay, what Maro Vance would do if he found out they'd opened it first. Five years she'd watched him run that math behind his eyes, and five years it had come out wrong, and he kept doing it anyway.

"We're going to open it," Ansel said. "Because if Vance finds it sealed, he takes it sealed, and we never learn what we lost. If we open it, at least we know what we're selling."

"That's a grim way to put it."

"It's the accurate way to put it."

Odile bent over the lock. The picks felt good in her hands, the way they always did — the small honest conversation between steel and steel, tension here, feedback there, the tumbler telling you its whole life story if you listened. The river had done her a favor in one way: the corrosion had eaten the retaining pin, so once the tumblers gave, the whole mechanism would slide free rather than fight her.

It took eleven minutes. She counted them by the drip off the roof.

The lock clicked open with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and all three of them stood there a second, nobody wanting to be the one who touched the lid.

"I'll do it," Teo said, and reached forward, and lifted it.

* * *

The smell came out first. Old paper and cold metal and something faintly sweet underneath, resinous, like a church Odile had never attended. Inside, the chest was lined with oiled canvas gone stiff as bark, and nested in it were three things arranged with the care of a burial.

Ledgers first. Six of them, bound in gray leather gone green at the edges, their spines stamped with the same three-peaked mountain. Under them, a letter in a heavy envelope, sealed with red wax that had cracked but held. And beneath everything, cradled in a fitted wooden tray like an instrument in a surgeon's case:

A compass.

Brass, palm-sized, heavier than it should have been, with a hinged lid worn bright at the thumbpiece from years of being opened. Odile lifted it out and felt the weight settle into her hand with an authority that had nothing to do with ounces. Someone had carried this every day for a very long time. You could tell from the wear. Tools confessed their histories if you held them right.

"Careful—" Teo started.

"I'm always careful," she said, and flipped the lid.

The needle swung. That was normal; needles swung. What wasn't normal was that it kept swinging — a slow, deliberate arc, east to south, south to west, unhurried, like a man turning his head to look at each member of a room. Then it stopped. Pointed. Not north.

It pointed at Ansel.

Not at him exactly — at the space just past his shoulder, toward the door, toward the flats beyond, toward some direction only the needle cared about. Ansel shifted left. The needle followed. He shifted back. It stayed.

"Okay," Teo said. "Okay, that's — magnets, right? There's a magnet somewhere. The truck's got a magnet somewhere."

"The truck's outside," Odile said.

"There's magnets *everywhere*, Odile."

She turned the compass to face herself. The needle shivered, considered, and swung away from Ansel — and settled pointing past her, out the shop's side window, toward the rail yards where she'd learned to weld, where her father had worked until the works closed, where the crane she'd climbed at fourteen still stood rusting against the sky like a gallows that had forgotten its purpose.

Her mouth went dry.

She didn't say anything. She handed it to Teo instead, and watched the needle swing again, and settle — toward the hills north of town, where his mother's people had a farm he hadn't visited in two years, not since the argument, not since the letter he kept folded in his wallet behind his ID. She saw his face change. She saw him recognize the direction even if he wouldn't say it aloud.

"Pemba!" she called, louder than she meant to. "Pemba, get in here!"

* * *

Pemba came in shaking rain off his jacket and stopped dead three steps through the door, the way a man stops when he walks into a room where something sacred or illegal is happening and can't immediately tell which.

"What," he said.

"Read this," Odile said, holding out an open ledger.

He took it the way he took everything — carefully, both hands, like the object might be shy. He was the most careful person she knew and the least demonstrative, a man who navigated by stars and memory and never raised his voice, and she watched him go still over the page in a way that was different from his usual stillness. This stillness had a tremor under it.

"This is Kestrian," he said.

"You can read it?"

"Some." He wet his thumb and turned a page. "My grandmother — when I was small, she taught me letters from her stories. She said our people traded across the mountains with the Kestrians, before. She had songs with Kestrian words in them. I thought they were just words." He looked up, and Odile saw something in his face she'd never seen there: vertigo. "They weren't just words. They were a country."

"What does it say?" Ansel asked.

Pemba read slowly, his finger under the lines, translating in pieces the way you'd carry water in your hands. "This page is accounts. Grain. Timber. Payments to — I don't have the word. *Serravane.* Keepers of the passes? And this column here—" he stopped.

"What?"

"These aren't numbers. These are names. Long lists of names, with amounts beside them." He looked up. "This isn't a merchant's book. This is a treasury ledger. This is where a nation kept track of what it owned."

The shop was quiet except for the roof-drip and the river.

"And this," Pemba said, lifting the sealed letter with a reverence that made Teo shift his weight uncomfortably, "this is sealed with the state seal. The mountain and the river. My grandmother drew this shape once, in the dust, and told me it belonged to a place that was taken off the maps, and that someday the maps would owe it an apology." He almost smiled. "I thought that was a story too."

"Everything about this is a story," Ansel said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when the sky went green. "Which means everything about this is trouble. A dead nation's treasury doesn't wash down a river by accident. Somebody hid it. Somebody else probably wants it. And the somebody-else category includes—"

"Vance," Odile said.

"Vance," Ansel agreed. "And whoever Vance reports to, which is worse than Vance."

Teo had picked up the compass again — Odile hadn't seen him take it, which bothered her — and was watching the needle swing lazily between directions that meant nothing to him. "So who does it point to? For real? North is that way." He aimed it at the wall. The needle ignored the wall. "It's broken."

"It's not broken," Odile said quietly.

All of them looked at her.

"When I held it," she said, and found she had to make herself continue, "it pointed at the rail yards. Where my father worked. Where I learned everything I know about metal." She took the compass back from Teo and held it flat on her palm between all of them, a small brass thing under the work lamp, its needle trembling like a living creature deciding. "When Teo held it, it pointed north-ish, toward the hills. Toward his mother's farm, if I had to guess. When Ansel held it—"

"Don't," Ansel said.

"—it pointed out the door, southwest. Toward the valley."

"Odile."

"Toward Halloway Flats, Ansel. Where the flood was."

The silence got very large. Teo looked between them without understanding, and Pemba, who understood more than he ever said, put the ledger down gently and stepped half a step back, giving the moment room the way you'd give a wounded animal room.

Ansel's face did something complicated and then did nothing at all, which was worse. "It's a trick of the mechanism," he said. "Gimbal bias. Residual magnetization from the chest lining. There are four explanations and none of them involve magic."

"Sure," Odile said. "Probably."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it." She set the compass down on the bench between them, needle still swinging its slow, patient arcs, waiting for a hand to answer to. "But you should know that when I held it, it pointed at the only place I've ever been homesick for. So whatever it is, it's not random."

Outside, the light was going. The river kept talking to itself in the dark, carrying things downstream — fence posts, branches, topsoil, the ordinary debris of other people's lives. Somewhere up in the hills the last light caught the clouds and turned them the color of old brass.

Pemba cleared his throat. "There's a colophon," he said. "In the back of the last ledger. A note in the keeper's hand." He read it aloud, slowly, translating as he went: "*If these records survive us, let them find hands that will finish what we could not. The treasury is not gold. The treasury is the proof that we existed. Guard the proof.*"

Nobody spoke.

Then headlights swept across the shop's front windows — slow, deliberate, the speed of a car that wanted to be seen — and died. An engine shut off. Doors did not open.

Ansel moved to the window without touching it, angling his head to see through the gap in the curtain. Odile watched his shoulders change.

"Black sedan," he said quietly. "Two men. Sitting." He turned around, and the giddy, buried wonder of the last hour was already gone off his face, packed away somewhere deep, replaced by the arithmetic again. "That was fast. That was too fast."

"They can't know," Teo whispered. "We only opened it tonight."

"Rumors don't wait for locks to open." Ansel looked at the chest, at the ledgers, at the small brass compass still ticking through its patient arcs on the bench. "Odile. Kill the work lamp. Everybody think — right now — about every person who's seen that chest since we pulled it out of the river. Every single one."

Odile reached for the lamp switch, and in the second before the dark took the shop, she looked down at the compass one more time.

The needle had stopped swinging.

It lay steady now, unwavering, pointing straight at Ansel Yarrow — at his chest, his heart, the valley behind his eyes — as if it had finally decided, after forty years in the dark, whose memory it intended to keep.
