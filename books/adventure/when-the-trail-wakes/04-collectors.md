## 4. Collectors

The chest had been in the truck for two days when the weather turned wrong.

Not storm-wrong — Ansel knew storm-wrong the way he knew his own pulse, could read a sky's intentions from forty miles out, could taste hail before it fell. This was a different kind of pressure, one that lived in the low country of the gut rather than the high country of the clouds. It came in with the morning fog off the Alder flats, and it sat on the camp like a hand.

Odile felt it first. She always did. She'd been under the truck since dawn, and by nine she'd said nothing at all, which was how Ansel knew something was coming. Odile filled silence like a cistern; when she stopped filling it, the level was dropping somewhere dangerous.

Pemba noticed too. He stood at the edge of the camp with his coffee going cold, watching the road — the single gravel thread that ran along the river levee and connected them to everything they owed.

"Road's quiet," Pemba said.

"Roads are usually quiet," Ansel said.

"Not this quiet." He turned the compass over in his pocket — their compass now, the brass thing with the needle that ignored north — though he didn't say so aloud. They'd agreed, wordlessly, in the way crews agree, not to speak of it where the wind might carry it to town. But towns carried things faster than wind. Towns had telephones. Towns had men whose whole trade was listening.

Teo was asleep in the cab, still gray-faced from the river crossing two nights ago, from hauling the chest up the bank while the flood tried to take him as payment instead. The boy had earned his sleep. Ansel envied it.

He sat on the tailgate with the salvage paperwork spread across his knees — the permits he'd filed in Corvane, the receipts, the ledger of debts that the Vane Syndicate kept on him the way a bank keeps a mortgage: patiently, and with the understanding that the property would eventually be theirs. Five years of numbers. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and some dollars, accruing interest at a rate that felt less like arithmetic and more like weather. Every supercell he'd chased, every hour of footage sold, every dry season survived — all of it went into that number, and the number grew anyway, patient as rust.

The chest sat behind the seat. He could feel it there without looking, the way you feel a stranger standing too close in an empty room.

*Forty years erased,* Ilse Marchetti hadn't said yet — she wasn't due for another day, though Ansel didn't know that, couldn't know it, only felt the shape of her arrival approaching like a front on the horizon. What he knew was what the ledgers said, what Odile's picks had opened: a dead nation's signature, page after page of Kestrian script, and a treasury that existed somewhere beyond the Serrano Range if even half of it was true.

And rumors, already, moving down the valley faster than the floodwater had.

* * *

Maro Vance arrived at noon, exactly when the fog burned off, because Maro Vance believed in timing the way other men believed in God.

Ansel heard the car before he saw it — a long, expensive idle, the sound of an engine that had never been asked to work for its living. A black sedan came up the levee road trailing dust, unhurried, and parked thirty yards from the truck with the precision of a man who measured distances professionally.

Vance got out alone. That was its own message. Men like Vance traveled with muscle when they meant to take something and alone when they meant to offer you the chance to give it up yourself, and both options were the same option wearing different clothes.

He was smaller than Ansel remembered. Five years ago, when Vance had first come to collect on the burial loan — the money Ansel had borrowed to put three coffins in the ground after the Alder broke its banks and took the valley with it — Vance had seemed enormous, a figure cut from the same dark cloth as the debt itself. Now, in the flat noon light, he was just a compact man in a good coat, silver at the temples, with hands that stayed loose and open at his sides the way a card player's do.

"Yarrow." He smiled without warmth, the smile of a man acknowledging a colleague. "You look terrible."

"Flood season." Ansel didn't get off the tailgate. Getting up meant yielding ground, and he'd learned that much about Vance: the man collected ground the way he collected interest.

"So I hear." Vance walked a slow half-circle around the camp, cataloguing. The tarp over the instruments. Odile's tools laid out in surgical order. Teo's boots drying by the fire ring. His eyes touched each item and moved on, and Ansel understood that none of these items were why he'd come. "Nasty business, that flash flood. Took out the footbridge at Miller's Bend. Drowned a whole stretch of pasture. You lose equipment?"

"Some."

"Recovered some, too, I understand." Vance stopped walking. He turned, and his face arranged itself into an expression of mild, reasonable curiosity — the most frightening expression he owned, because it meant the knife was already out of the sheath and being held very casually. "Word travels. A fisherman downstream says he saw your rookie pull something out of a snag. Big item. River-stained. Heavy enough that it took two of you."

"The kid pulled my sonde array off a willow," Ansel said. "Cost me four hundred bucks to replace. I was grateful."

"Mm." Vance looked at the truck. At the cab. Behind the seat, the chest sat in the dark like a held breath. "That's not what I heard."

"Then you heard wrong."

The silence that followed had texture. Ansel had spent five years lying to creditors — small lies, structural lies, the load-bearing lies of a man keeping a roof over a crew — and he'd gotten good enough at it to survive audits. But this lie came out of him thin and cracked, and he watched Vance hear the crack, watch the man file it away with the small satisfaction of a collector finding a flaw in a coin.

"You know what I admire about you, Yarrow?" Vance drifted closer, hands still loose. "You're honest in your bones. It's only ever the surface that lies. I've watched you sign papers you hated, sell footage you were ashamed of, drive through weather that should have killed you, and every time, your face tells the truth while your mouth does the work. It's why I've never worried about you running. Running requires a certain appetite for falsehood. You don't have it." He paused by the fire ring, nudged one of Teo's drying boots with his toe. "So when you lie to me badly, I know it means the thing underneath is worth lying about. And that tells me everything I need to know."

Ansel's mouth was dry. Behind him, inside the shop tent, he heard Odile go very still over her work — heard the specific absence of the ratchet clicking.

"What do you want, Vance?"

"To talk terms." Vance finally sat, uninvited, on the far end of the tailgate, folding himself down with the ease of a man settling in for a long negotiation he'd already won. "But first, a history lesson. Free of charge. Do you know what Kestria was?"

"A country."

"A country," Vance agreed, "that owed debts. Forty-odd years back, when the maps got redrawn, Kestria didn't just disappear politically. It disappeared financially. Banks closed overnight. Accounts froze. And a great deal of movable wealth went into the mountains and never came down." He let that sit. "The Syndicate has spent forty years buying up Kestrian paper. Claims, deeds, promissory notes, inheritance lines. We hold more of that dead nation than any living entity. And recently—" he tapped the tailgate twice, softly, like knocking on a door "—a river gave up a sealed chest with a Kestrian treasury seal, in the possession of a storm chaser who owes us forty-one thousand dollars and change."

"Rumors," Ansel said.

"Rumors are just unpaid invoices with better stories." Vance smiled again, and this time there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "Here are my terms, Yarrow, and I'll only make them once. You give me the chest — contents intact, seals unbroken or broken, I don't care, we have people who read dead languages. In exchange, I burn your ledger. All of it. Forty-one thousand, the interest, the burial loan, everything. You walk away clean. First clean day in five years." He leaned back on his palms, gazing out at the flats as if admiring the view. "You know what that's worth? Not the money. The mornings. Waking up and owing nobody. I've never had one myself, but I'm told they exist."

The trap, Ansel thought, was beautiful. That was the worst part. It wasn't a threat — Vance hadn't mentioned the collectors, the spotter planes, the men who visited debtors who fled. He'd offered forgiveness, real forgiveness, priced fairly against a box of paper from a country that no longer existed. Any reasonable man would take it. Any reasonable man would hand over the chest and sleep that night like the dead.

Which was exactly how Ansel knew the chest was worth more than forty-one thousand dollars. Vance didn't forgive debts worth less than what he was buying.

"I don't have a chest," Ansel said.

"No?"

"No."

Vance nodded slowly, and for a moment he simply breathed, looking at the river, and Ansel watched the gentleness drain out of him like water out of a cracked hull. When Vance spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register Ansel remembered from five years ago, from a funeral week, from a kitchen table where a grieving man had signed his life away.

"My grandmother," Vance said, "was born in Vellmark. Kestrian province. She used to sing songs in a language I wasn't allowed to learn, because learning it marked you, and marked children get collected." He stood, brushed off his coat. "I am the last person alive who wants that nation's treasure in the Syndicate's vaults, Yarrow, and I want it anyway, because wanting is not a luxury I was issued. So understand me clearly." He turned, and his eyes were flat and patient as a ledger. "You have until I reach Corvane — call it six hours — to decide whether that chest is yours to give. If it isn't on the truck when I send for it, then it belongs to whoever holds your paper. And I hold your paper."

He walked back to the sedan. At the door he paused, half-turned.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope you run. Men who run are easier to find than men who hide, and far more interesting." He opened the door. "Give my regards to your crew. The tall one reads Kestrian, doesn't he? Grandmother's stories. Charming."

The sedan pulled away down the levee road, trailing dust, unhurried, and the fog of the morning came back in behind it like a tide.

Ansel stood a long time at the tailgate. Then Odile's voice came from the shop tent, low and tight:

"He knows about Pemba. He knows about the script. Ansel — *who told him?*"

And before Ansel could answer, Pemba pointed downriver, toward the bend, where a woman was walking up the levee road alone, coat buttoned to the throat, carrying a leather satchel like it held the last copy of a country — and moving with the certainty of someone who had been coming for them since long before Vance ever found the trail.
