## 6. Compass of Small Truths

Teo had never held anything older than his grandmother's sewing tin, so when Odile pressed the compass into his palm and said, "Hold it steady, rookie," his hands shook before the needle even moved.

They had made camp in the lee of a collapsed grain elevator outside Halden's Crossing, the truck backed against the concrete so the wind couldn't get at the tarps. The chest sat open between the tailgate and a campfire built low, its contents laid out on a wool blanket like organs removed from a body: three ledgers bound in green leather gone soft as skin, a sealed letter thick with wax, and the compass — a disc of brass the size of a saucer, its lid worked with a pattern of interlocking wheat sheaves that Pemba said was Kestrian for *plenty shared is plenty kept*.

The needle didn't point north.

Teo knew this because he had checked twice against Pemba's phone compass, holding the brass thing level the way Odile had shown him, elbow tucked, breath held. The phone said north-northwest. The needle said east, dead east, and held there with a confidence that made Teo's teeth ache, the way a lie told too smoothly does.

"It's broken," he said, and immediately hated how young his voice sounded.

"Nothing about it is broken." Odile crouched across the fire from him, her forearms on her knees, grease still in the creases of her knuckles from the day's work on the truck's rear axle. "Broken things don't have opinions. That thing has opinions."

"It's a toy," said Pemba, but he said it the way he said most things he didn't believe — carefully, watching the words to see if they'd hold weight. He sat cross-legged with the first ledger open on his lap, and he'd been on the same page for an hour, tracing the script with one finger without touching the paper. "My grandmother had a prayer book in this hand. She used to read it to us when the storms came through the tea fields. We thought it was just old words. Nobody told us it was a whole country."

"It's nobody's country," Odile said. "That's the whole problem. It's a country that got *deleted*, and this—" she nodded at the compass, the ledgers, the letter with its broken seal of wheat and river, "—this is the receipt."

Teo turned the compass over. On the back, scratched fine as hair, someone had engraved a line of that same script, and beneath it, in a rougher hand, in letters Teo could actually read: *FOR THE ONE WHO REMEMBERS WHERE.*

"Where what?" he asked.

Nobody answered. The fire popped. Out on the flats, the wind was changing its mind about the evening, dragging a smell of wet dust and far-off rain across the camp, and Teo felt the old prickle at the back of his neck that meant weather was thinking about them. Five months on the crew and that prickle was the only inheritance Ansel had given him so far, though he'd learned more from the man in five months than in nineteen years of living with anyone.

Ansel was at the truck's tailgate with the maps spread out, not looking at the compass. He had not looked at it since Odile picked the lock, Teo realized. He'd looked at the ledgers, at the seal, at the road atlas, at the sky. Never at the compass.

"Give it here," Odile said, and Teo passed it over, and that was when the needle moved.

It didn't swing. It *drifted*, slow and deliberate, like a fish changing its mind, and settled on a bearing somewhere south of where it had sat for Teo, trembling faintly, the way a compass does near iron — except there was no iron. Teo checked. He was the rookie; checking was his whole job.

"Huh," said Odile.

"What?"

"It moved when you let go." She held it flat on her palm, and the needle held its new bearing, steady as a struck bell. "It was pointing east for you. Now it's pointing—" she rotated, squinting along the needle's line, out past the fire, past the dead elevator, toward the darkening hills to the south. "Huh. That's toward Corvane. That's toward the shop where I apprenticed. First shop. Where I learned that engines are just arguments about fire."

"That's coincidence," Pemba said.

"Sure," Odile said. "Hand it to Pemba."

Pemba took it the way he took everything — with both hands, careful, as if the world had taught him early that things given freely could be taken back just as freely. He held it level. The needle drifted again, that slow fish-turn, and settled west. Pemba went very still.

"West," he said. His voice had changed. "There's nothing west. There's the pass road and there's—" He stopped. Teo watched him do the arithmetic he didn't want to do. "There's the valley where my mother's family farmed. Before the border closed. Before everything."

"Coincidence," Odile said softly, and for once it didn't sound like her at all.

* * *

They tested it the way you test anything you're afraid of: repeatedly, and in denial.

Odile had Teo walk the compass out fifty meters from camp, then a hundred, watching whether the needle held its bearing or corrected. It corrected. Whatever it pointed at, it pointed at from anywhere, the way north does — except it wasn't north, and when Teo held it again the needle swung back to his east with a small, eager shiver, like a dog hearing its name.

"East," Odile said, watching him from the fire. "What's east for you, Teo?"

"Nothing. The flats. The highway." He turned the compass in his hands. The brass was warm now, body-warm, which it shouldn't have been. "I grew up in a rail town east of here. Marrow Junction. Nobody's ever heard of it."

"Try it," Pemba said quietly. "Say the name while you're holding it."

Teo felt his face go hot. It was a stupid thing, a séance thing, and he was nineteen and had a reputation to not-build. But the needle was trembling against its stop like something impatient, and the wind was coming up, and there was a part of him — the part that had felt the prickle on his neck since he was a kid watching storms walk the horizon, the part that had gotten him thrown out of three foster placements for standing on roofs — that part wanted to know.

"Marrow Junction," he said.

The needle didn't move. It had already been pointing east. But it *stilled*, went perfectly, unnaturally quiet, the trembling gone, and Teo had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that the thing was not pointing at a direction at all.

It was pointing at a *place*. And the place was in him.

"Okay," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and he didn't care. "Okay, that's — how does it *do* that? There's no magnet in a memory. There's no — it's brass. It's a hundred years old. It was in a river."

"Forty years in a river," Odile said, "and it still knows where home is." She said it lightly, but her hands were busy, wiping themselves on her rag over and over though they were already clean. "That's more than most of us can say."

Teo looked at the needle pointing out of his chest toward a rail town he'd left at eleven and never once gone back to, and felt something open under his ribs like a trapdoor. The compass knew. Not the town — the town was just coordinates. It knew the *weight*. The platform where his mother had put him on a bus with a sandwich and told him to be brave, which was the last sentence she ever said to him that made sense. The needle was pointing at that.

"Give it to Ansel," Pemba said.

And the camp went quiet in a way that Teo felt in his ears.

* * *

Ansel had heard. Teo could tell by the set of his shoulders at the tailgate — that particular stillness he got when weather was about to turn, the stillness of a man reading something too far away to name yet. He came over slowly, wiping his hands on his thighs, and he didn't sit. He stood over the fire with the light moving on his face and held out his hand, and Odile put the compass in it, and for a moment nothing happened at all.

Then the needle moved, and it moved *fast*.

Not a drift. Not a fish-turn. It swung like a struck thing, like something released, and it hauled around to the northeast and slammed against its stop and *pulled* — Teo had never seen a needle pull, they point, that was the whole deal, they pointed — but this one leaned, strained, the brass disc shivering faintly under Ansel's hand as if the needle were trying to drag the whole compass out of his grip and go there without him.

"Northeast," Pemba said, and got out his phone, and his thumb moved over the map, and stopped. "Northeast is—" He looked up. He looked at Ansel. Teo saw him decide not to say it.

"Say it," Ansel said. His voice was flat. Weather-voice.

"Northeast is the Alder valley," Pemba said. "Upper reach. Halloway Bend."

Teo didn't know the name, but he knew what it was. Everyone in the chase world knew what it was, the way everyone knew the name of the hurricane that had taken their town, if they'd had one. The dam break. The flood that had come down the Alder valley one June night five years ago and erased four towns and nine hundred people, and made a name for itself in the hydrology journals, and made an orphan out of the best storm reader Teo had ever stood under.

Ansel Yarrow was from Halloway Bend. Teo had known it the way you know things about your crew — from a file, from a drunk night Odile had never let him forget he'd eavesdropped on. But he had never heard Ansel say the name of the place. Not once. Not in five months of driving through it, around it, within sight of it. They had rerouted twice, Teo realized now. Twice, without explanation, Ansel had redrawn a chase line that added hours, and Odile had just done it, no questions, the way you step around a grave.

The needle strained northeast, quivering, insistent, and Ansel stood there holding it the way you hold a snake — by the head, at arm's length, knowing it would bite.

"It's wrong," he said.

"It's not wrong," Odile said gently. "It's the only thing here that isn't."

"It points at memories. Fine. Mine are wrong. The valley's under forty feet of water and silt. There's no *there* there." His knuckles had gone white around the brass. "It should point at nothing."

"Maybe it doesn't point at places," Pemba said. He had closed the ledger. "Maybe it points at what the place left in you." He said it in the tone of a man reading scripture he didn't fully believe but couldn't put down. "My grandmother's prayer book. There's a passage — she'd touch my forehead with it. *The land forgets nothing; it only waits for someone to ask.* I thought it was about crops."

The wind came harder off the flats, flattening the fire, carrying the first fat smell of the rain that was still a day out but coming, Teo's neck said so, coming for sure. Ansel looked down at the compass. For a long moment the only sounds were the wind and the fire and the small dry tremor of the needle against its stop, and Teo thought — with the strange clarity that sometimes came to him in the last light before weather — *he's going to throw it in the river.* He could see the whole motion in Ansel's arm, coiled and ready.

Instead Ansel crouched. Slowly, like an old man, like a man kneeling at a rail. He set the compass on the flat of his palm and held it out toward the northeast, toward the drowned valley, toward whatever was left of Halloway Bend under the water and the years, and he let the needle strain against its stop, and he watched it, and his face did something that Teo had no name for and never wanted to see again.

"My father surveyed that dam," Ansel said. It came out of him like something lanced. "He flagged the seep lines. He wrote the memos. I found them after — I was seventeen, I didn't understand what I was reading, and by the time I did, the Syndicate's lawyers had made sure every copy in the valley was gone. Every copy but the ones I—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "The debt I owe Vane started the week I burned my father's files. They paid for the funerals. All nine hundred. And then they owned me."

Nobody moved. The needle pulled northeast, patient now, tireless, a thing built to wait out decades.

"It's not pointing at the valley," Ansel said finally, and his voice was almost steady, which was worse. "It's pointing at the part of me that's still standing on the levee at two in the morning listening to the water come down." He closed his fist around the compass, and the trembling stopped, muffled, alive, like a bird in a hand. "It's been pointing there since the river gave us the chest. I could feel it. I just told myself it was the truck."

"Ansel—" Odile started.

"Vance knows about the salvage," Ansel said, and the weather-voice came down over everything like a front. "He'll have heard by morning. And if that compass points at memory, then the Syndicate doesn't need it to find a treasury — they need it to find *leverage*, and it's holding more leverage in this camp than any vault in the mountains." He stood, and put the compass down on the blanket between them, needle still straining northeast under its closed lid, and looked at each of them in turn, and Teo felt the look land on him like a hand on the shoulder. "So we decide tonight. All of us. Because whatever this thing is, it's not cargo anymore. It's a compass, and it's pointed at all of us."

Teo looked at the brass disc lying in the firelight, at the needle he could feel straining even through the closed lid, and out past it at the darkening east where a rail town he'd never see again was keeping his mother's last sentence, and he understood, with a prickle at his neck stronger than any storm, that they were no longer a chase crew.

They were being chased — by something that knew exactly where each of them lived.

Far out on the flats, a pair of headlights came up the county road, moving slow, sweeping the ditches, and Odile was the first to see them, and she said only: "That's not a local's driving."
