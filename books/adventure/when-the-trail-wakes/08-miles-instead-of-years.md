## 8. Miles Instead of Years

The road east of the Alder was a scar that hadn't decided what it wanted to be — gravel in some stretches, cracked asphalt in others, and in one memorable quarter-mile, nothing at all but hardpan baked to the color of old bone. Ansel drove with his forearm draped over the wheel and let the truck tell him where the road went, because the road had opinions and arguing with them cost axles.

Teo rode shotgun with the compass on his knee.

That had become a thing without anyone deciding it. The boy held it the way you'd hold a sleeping animal, cupped, careful, thumb resting light against the brass rim. Every hour or so he'd glance down at it, then out the windshield, then back at Ansel, working up to something. Ansel knew the look. It was the look of a kid who'd been told storms were dangerous and wanted to be told they were also beautiful.

"Ask," Ansel said finally.

"The needle." Teo tilted the compass so the glass caught the morning light. "It's not pointing at the pass."

"No."

"It's pointing kind of... left of where we're going. Back the way we came, almost. Toward—" He stopped himself, and Ansel watched him decide whether to say it. "Toward the valley. Your valley."

Ansel kept his eyes on the road. A jackrabbit broke from the sage and ran parallel to the truck for fifty yards, ears flat, before peeling off into nowhere.

"It does that," he said.

"Does it do it for everybody? Pemba said when *he* holds it—"

"Pemba's grandmother's village, probably. Ilse gets her grandfather's letter. Odile won't say." He shifted gears as the hardpan gave way to washboard and the whole cab began to buzz like a trapped hornet. "It's not a compass, Teo. It's a mirror with a needle in it. You point it at yourself and it tells you what you already know."

"That sounds worse than a compass."

"It is."

Teo turned the instrument over in his hands, frowning at the caseback where someone had scratched a line of Kestrian script too fine to read at speed. Then, mercifully, he put it away in his jacket, and Ansel felt the cab breathe again.

Ahead, the Serrano Range stood up out of the earth like something that had been waiting for them all along — blue-black ridgelines stacked against a sky so clean it looked rinsed. Too clean. Ansel read the horizon the way other men read newspapers, and the newspaper this morning said: *dry air aloft, cap holding, but for how long.* The monsoon was coming. Everyone on the radio agreed about that much. The disagreement was about whether they'd beat it through the high country or become a footnote in somebody else's story.

"Okay," he said. "Lesson one."

Teo straightened like a student called on unexpectedly.

"Storm-reading. You wanted to learn, so learn. First thing: forget the sky. Everybody looks at the sky. The sky lies. Clouds build all day and die without dropping a drop. You want the truth, you watch three things." He lifted one finger off the wheel. "Wind. Feel it on your neck through the window gap. If it dies at midday, the cap's breaking. If it turns and starts sucking *inward*, toward the storm instead of away from it — that's the storm breathing. That's when you get serious." Second finger. "Dust. Ground-level dust moving in a column where there shouldn't be any wind means outflow. Means something upstream already collapsed and is throwing its weight around." Third finger. "Smell."

"Smell?"

"Rain hits hot ground, you get petrichor. But rain hitting ground that hasn't seen water in years — that's a different smell. Greener. Almost sweet. Old-timers call it the smell of trouble, because dry country floods worst. The ground can't drink fast enough, so everything runs." He glanced sideways. "You'll want to remember that part especially."

Teo wrote it on his forearm in pen, which Ansel chose to find endearing rather than alarming.

* * *

They stopped at noon in the shadow of a grain elevator gone rust-red with age, and while Odile chased a coolant leak and Pemba spread their maps across the tailgate weighted with lug nuts, Ansel climbed onto the truck roof with the binoculars and did the thing he did every day of his life: looked up.

The spotter plane found them an hour later.

He heard it before he saw it — a drone-note wrong for any bird, too steady, too patient. It came out of the northwest sun, a pale sliver with a long camera lens glinting beneath the fuselage, and it made no attempt whatsoever to hide what it was doing. Syndicate aircraft didn't need stealth. They needed only to be seen.

"They've been flying that grid since dawn," Pemba said, not looking up from the maps. "Box pattern. They know we're in the box. They're just narrowing it."

"Vance doesn't have enough men to take us on the open road," Odile said from under the truck, voice echoing off the undercarriage. "Not after Corvane. He needs us stopped somewhere. Somewhere with walls."

"So we don't stop anywhere with walls," Ansel said.

"We have to sleep sometime."

"Then we sleep stupid places. Culverts. Ruins. Places with two exits." He swept the binoculars east, following the plane's arc, and felt the old familiar tightening in his chest — five years of being hunted by weather had taught him its rhythms, and being hunted by men was turning out to be humiliatingly similar. Watch the pattern. Find the gap. Move during the gap.

The plane banked, caught the light, and turned back toward the mountains.

Ansel lowered the binoculars and found Teo watching him with that expression again — the one that wasn't quite a question yet.

"You said your family were farmers," Teo said carefully. "Before."

The tailgate conversation died. Odile slid out from under the truck with a smear of coolant on her jaw and didn't pretend she wasn't listening.

"They were," Ansel said.

"And then the flood."

"And then the flood."

He climbed down off the roof because it was easier to say things at eye level, or at least easier to not say them there. He picked up a lug nut, set it down again. Behind him the range stood waiting, patient as debt.

"There was a dam," he said, and heard how flat his own voice came out, the way it always did, sanded smooth by five years of telling it short. "Upstream of our valley. Big one. I was nineteen, working my first survey contract — measuring seepage rates, piezometer readings, the boring arithmetic of water pressure. My father farmed below it. My mother kept bees. My sister—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a night in the spring melt when the readings went wrong. Not dramatically wrong. *Slightly* wrong. Half a meter of extra head, seepage running cloudy. I flagged it. I flagged it in writing, and my supervisor told me the numbers would settle, and I was nineteen and he'd been doing it thirty years, so I let him be right."

Nobody moved. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"It didn't settle. At four in the morning the face came apart and forty million tons of water came down a valley that had eleven minutes' warning, and most of that warning was the sound." He looked at the mountains because looking at people was impossible. "I was on the ridge taking night readings. That's the whole story. That's why there's a me instead of an us. I was above the waterline because I was doing my job, and my job was the thing that failed."

"Ansel," Odile started.

"The Syndicate owned the dam. Vane Consolidated, before it was the Vane Syndicate — same money, newer name. They paid out settlements fast and quiet, and mine came with a paper asking me to sign off that the failure was 'an act of extraordinary natural circumstance.' I signed it. I took the money, I buried my family in a cemetery that the insurance paid for, and I got in a truck and drove until the odometer became a personality." He laughed, and it came out wrong, like a tool breaking. "Five years. You want to know how long five years is? It's nine hundred thousand miles. I checked. I keep checking. Because miles are easy. Miles don't ask you anything. You drive them and they're gone behind you and none of them are the eleven minutes."

Teo was very still. When he finally spoke, his voice was young and unguarded in a way that made it land harder than any comfort could have. "The compass has been pointing at your valley since the river."

"Yeah."

"What's in your valley?"

Ansel looked east at the Serranos, at the passes they hadn't reached, at the snowline they were racing, and understood suddenly that he didn't know the answer — that the question had been circling him for days like the spotter plane, narrowing its box.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I think it wants me to find out."

* * *

They drove the afternoon in the plane's blind spots, which meant driving the ugly roads — creek bottoms and fire cuts, the truck's suspension taking a beating that Odile narrated with increasingly creative profanity. Twice the drone-note returned and twice they sat motionless under rock overhangs, engine ticking, watching the shadow of the aircraft slide across the canyon wall like the hand of a clock counting down to something.

During the second wait, wedged in the shade with dust sifting down off the stone, Teo asked quietly, "Did it hurt? Telling us."

Ansel considered it. Above them the plane droned past, patient, incurious.

"Yes," he said. "And lighter. Both. Like lancing something."

"My dad used to say grief is just love with nowhere to go."

Ansel looked at the kid — nineteen, maybe twenty, same age he'd been on the ridge — and thought about the flood of things he'd never said to anyone, backing up behind his teeth for five years like water behind a bad dam.

"Yeah," he said. "Your dad was right."

When the shadow passed, they rolled out of the cut and made another twenty miles before dark, and camped in a ruined stagecoach station with two exits and no walls worth mentioning, exactly as promised. Pemba took first watch. The stars came out hard and cold over the black bulk of the range, and somewhere beyond those ridgelines, snow was already falling on the high col.

Ansel lay on his bedroll under the truck's flank — old habit, the vehicle between him and whatever was coming — and listened to the crew breathe. Odile muttered torque specs in her sleep. Teo slept like the dead, the compass tucked in his jacket an arm's length away, and even from here Ansel could feel it, faint and persistent, like a pulse through the ground.

*Toward the valley. Always toward the valley.*

He closed his eyes and let the memory come for once instead of fighting it, and it arrived the way the flood had arrived: quietly first, then all at once. His mother's hives on the south slope, white boxes in the evening light. His sister laughing at something on the porch, the screen door banging. The smell of rain on ground that hadn't seen water in years — greener, almost sweet, the smell of trouble — rising off the fields that last afternoon while he drove up to the dam with his clipboard full of numbers that would have been enough, that should have been enough, if anyone senior had listened to a nineteen-year-old with muddy boots.

Eleven minutes. Forty million tons. Nine hundred thousand miles.

He woke before dawn with the word *petrichor* in his mouth and the strange certainty that the dream had been less a haunting than a summons. East, the sky over the Serranos had gone the color of a bruise, and the wind on his neck had died completely, and turned, and begun to suck inward, toward the mountains, toward the storm that was breathing behind them.

The monsoon, early. Days early.

He stood there in the gray light reading the sky he'd taught Teo to distrust, and the sky told the truth this time: they had hours, not days, to reach the foothills — and halfway up the track behind them, small and patient in the first gusts, a plume of dust that was not weather.

Headlights. Two vehicles, running dark, closing fast.

Ansel put his boot down on the gravel softly and went to wake the others.
