## 9. The Last Honest Town

Corvane smelled of diesel and wet wool and, underneath it all, the river — that green mineral breath that clung to everything east of the Serrano foothills. Odile noticed smells before she noticed anything else; a mechanic's nose was her livelihood, tuned to burning wiring and failing seals the way Pemba's ear was tuned to weather. So she clocked Corvane's particular perfume from two miles out, window down, rain still beading on her forearms, and thought: *town that has given up pretending.*

The main street ran along the old ferry landing like a spine. Buildings leaned into each other for warmth. A grain elevator stood at the north end with its top third dark, dead bulbs in the floodlights, and half the storefronts wore plywood over their windows with hand-painted signs still visible beneath: *HARDWARE — OPEN*, *BAKERY — CLOSED FOR GOOD*. The town had been dying politely for thirty years, and now here came four strangers in a storm-chase rig with a dead nation's chest strapped under a tarp in the bed, asking it to keep one more secret.

"Fuel first," Ansel said, pulling under the single working canopy. "Then food. Then we're gone before dark."

"You said that about the last three towns," Teo said.

"And we made it out of all three."

"We made it out of two. We *fled* the third."

Odile let them argue and went to work on the fuel pump, which was the kind of antique that required persuasion — a palm-flat slap on the housing, then a slow crank, then the gurgle and cough as forty-year-old gasoline found its way up. She liked machines like this. They were honest about their age. Everything in Corvane was honest about its age except the people, who watched the truck from doorways with the flat, patient attention of herons watching water.

She felt those eyes on her back the whole time the tank filled.

* * *

They ate at a diner called the Landing, because it was the only place with lights on. Vinyl booths split at the seams, mended with tape gone amber. A radio behind the counter played something with too much fiddle in it. The waitress was maybe nineteen and moved like someone conserving energy for a longer night.

Pemba ordered for everyone in his quiet, list-making way — he'd been doing that since the ford, since Calloway's men put Teo in the river and the crew pulled him out gasping, since they lost half their supplies to the water and the running. Odile watched him count heads, count plates, count exits. He always sat facing the door now. So did she.

It was Ilse who saw the notice board.

She'd gone up to pay, or to pretend to pay — money was getting thin, and they all knew the arithmetic nobody spoke aloud — and she stopped so suddenly that Odile heard her boots squeak on the linoleum. Then Ilse crossed back to the booth fast, low-voiced, and set a torn piece of paper in the middle of the table like a card turned face-up.

Four descriptions. Typed, not handwritten, which meant a printer, which meant planning. *Woman, forties, dark hair, foreign accent, may travel with three others. Man, thirties, storm-chaser build, scar through left eyebrow. Boy, late teens. Older man, Himalayan features.* And beneath, in the same type: *Substantial reward. Inquire with M. Vance, room above the feed store. No questions asked.*

"He's been here," Ilse said. "Hours ahead of us, maybe less."

Ansel took the paper. His thumb moved once across the scar, an old habit Odile had catalogued years ago the way she catalogued every sound his engine made. "He's not hunting us yet. He's fishing. Posting bait and waiting to see what bites."

"The waitress read this board," Pemba said quietly. "Everyone in this town reads this board."

The fiddle music seemed very loud all of a sudden. Odile became aware of the counter again — the cook who had stopped chopping, the two men at the far table who had stopped talking, the wet umbrellas dripping by the door. Nobody was looking at them. Everybody was looking at nothing very hard.

"Walk," Ansel said. "Casual. Truck. Now."

They got eleven steps.

* * *

The trouble started at the door, the way trouble usually did — small, almost polite. A man in a canvas coat stood between them and the street with his hands in his pockets, and behind him, drifting out of the hardware store across the way, came two more. Not Syndicate muscle; Odile knew that breed, all shoulders and cheap suits. These were locals, which was worse. Locals had reasons.

"That your rig with the mast on it?" Canvas Coat asked. "The chase truck?"

"It's a truck," Ansel said.

"Man upstairs at the feed store says there's five hundred dollars for word on where you're headed." He said it without malice, almost apologetically, the way you'd mention rain coming. "Five hundred dollars is a lot of money in Corvane."

"I know it is," Ansel said. "I owe more than that."

Something flickered across the man's face — recognition, maybe, of a fellow drowning man. But the two behind him shifted, and down the street a door opened, and Odile did the math she always did: three against five, tired against fresh, and a chest in the truck bed worth more than the whole town. Her hand found the tire iron under her coat, mostly for comfort. Irons didn't win fights anymore. They just made them memorable.

Then the diner door opened behind them, and a voice like a boat hull scraping gravel said:

"Hal Brannick, if you stand in these folks' way one second longer, I'll tell your mother what you did to her fence in '09, and I'll do it at church."

Canvas Coat — Hal Brannick — went stiff as a fence post. "Miss Sorrel—"

"Go home. All three of you. And take that paper off the board while you're at it. Vance can pin his own laundry."

Odile turned. The woman in the doorway was seventy if she was a day, built low and wide like a bollard, with hands that had clearly spent decades around rope. Silver hair cut short and practical. Eyes the gray-green of the river itself. She looked at each of them in turn the way Odile inspected a used engine block — no sentiment, just assessment — and whatever she found, she apparently decided to keep.

"My place is two streets down," she said. "You'll want to move before the rest of the town finishes its supper and gets ambitious. Follow me. Don't follow me obviously."

* * *

Her house sat where the ferry road met the water, a long low building that had been something else once — Odile spotted the wide doors, the roller tracks bolted into the foundation, the hoist beam still jutting over the bank. A boathouse, converted. Inside smelled of pitch and coffee and old canvas, and every wall held river things: oars, depth gauges, framed photographs of a ferry crowded with people, dozens of them, standing shoulder to shoulder on a deck too small for hope.

"Ferry house," Ilse said softly. She was staring at the photographs.

"Sorrel Vane—" the woman began, and stopped, and something crossed her face like weather. "Sorrel Marsh, when I ran the crossing. Thirty-one years on the Alder. Carried everything that needed carrying." She put a kettle on a wood stove that had been fed so long its iron had gone white. "Including some cargo the county preferred I hadn't."

Ilse went very still. "Kestrians."

The woman — Sorrel — didn't answer right away. She measured coffee with the deliberation of someone who had learned never to hurry anything that mattered. "Forty years ago, when your country stopped being on maps, its people didn't stop being on my dock. They came down out of the hills at night with what they could carry and papers that weren't theirs, and the law said turn them back, and I said the river doesn't check passports." She glanced at Ilse. "You've got the look. North-valley Kestrian. Am I wrong?"

"No," Ilse said. Her voice had gone rough. "You're not wrong."

"Thought not." Sorrel set out cups — mismatched, chipped, generous. "Sit down. All of you. You look like a crew that's been eating gas-station food and lying to each other about how bad it is."

Teo laughed — a short, startled bark, the first real laugh Odile had heard out of him since the ford — and then looked embarrassed, and then didn't, because Sorrel was smiling at him with something almost like approval.

* * *

Odile ended up in the corner by the workbench, because she always did, because corners had sightlines and workbenches had tools. She cleaned the truck's air filter by lamplight while the others talked — Ansel laying out just enough truth to buy trust, Ilse correcting him with historian's precision, Pemba watching the windows. Sorrel listened the way old ferrymen listen: to what the river carried, not what it claimed.

When Ansel finished, she was quiet a moment. Then: "Vance. Maro Vance. Tall, gray at the temples, polite like a knife is polite?"

"You know him?" Ansel asked.

"I know the name. Vane Syndicate ran freight contracts on this stretch before your time. Cheap rates until they owned you, then rates you couldn't refuse." She poured coffee. "And I know the surname, because it's mine. Distant branch. Bad branch." She said it flatly, but Odile saw her knuckles whiten on the pot handle. "Half this country's related to money somewhere in the family tree. Doesn't mean you have to visit that limb."

"So why help us?" Odile asked. It came out blunter than intended, but she'd never seen the point of sanding a question. "Five hundred dollars is five hundred dollars. You said yourself the town reads that board."

Sorrel looked at her for a long moment, and Odile braced for anger, and instead got something worse and better: honesty.

"Because in '83 I ferried a woman across at midnight with a baby in her arms and soldiers coming down the north bank," Sorrel said. "I cut my own mooring line to make the current, and I lost the crossing license for it, and I'd do it again tonight. That woman's granddaughter runs a bakery in a city I won't name, and every year she sends me bread I don't need, and every year I eat it anyway." She set the pot down. "Your chest has Kestria's seal on it. I saw the tarp shift when you loaded up outside the diner. I'm eighty pounds past being able to fight anyone, but I can still choose whose side of the river I stand on. That's all this is. That's everything this is."

Nobody spoke. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the monsoon's outer bands had arrived — Odile could hear them arriving, a soft vast pressure of rain beginning on the roof, the first real taste of the season they were racing.

"There's a back road," Sorrel said at last, businesslike now, pulling a rolled map from under the bench like she'd been waiting all evening for permission. "Old ferry supply track, follows the high bank. No checkpoints, no board with your faces on it. It puts you on the mountain road forty miles north of where Vance expects you to be." Her finger traced the line, then stopped at a notch in the range. "But hear me. Once you're past the treeline, no road I know will follow you, and the snows don't care whose bloodright anything is. Whatever you're carrying to that col — carry it fast."

* * *

They slept in shifts in the boathouse, and Odile took the last watch, which suited her. She liked the hours when the world stopped performing. Rain on the tin roof. The stove settling. Teo asleep sitting up, the way he'd slept since the river tried to keep him. Ilse awake too, of course, at the photograph wall, touching the glass over a picture of the ferry deck — all those faces, all those saved lives, packed together on thirty feet of wood.

"They knew," Ilse murmured, not turning around. She must have known Odile was awake; mechanics breathe loud. "All these years, somebody always knew. Somebody kept a light on."

"Somebody always does," Odile said. "You just don't meet them till you're sinking."

Ilse smiled faintly and didn't argue.

Near dawn, Odile stepped out onto the old hoist platform to check the sky, and that was when she saw the light. Across the river, up on the ridge road, a vehicle's headlights swept once — slow, deliberate, a search pattern — and went dark. Then, faintly, the smell of a cold engine drifting over the water.

Someone had found the ferry house. Someone was deciding.

She was back inside in six steps, hand on Ansel's shoulder, already whispering: "Get up. We're leaving now — and Miss Sorrel, I think you'd best come with us, because whoever that is knows exactly whose side of the river you stand on."
