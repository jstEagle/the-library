## 11. The Rival Ring

Teo learned the river before he saw it.

Pemba had been reading the map with his lips moving, the way he did when he was doing arithmetic in his head, and Odile had gone quiet at the wheel — and Odile going quiet was worse than any alarm. The truck's engine note dropped as she eased off the throttle, and through the windshield the valley opened up like a hand uncurling: gray scree on both sides, a thread of alder scrub going gold at the edges, and below it all, filling the whole floor of the gorge, a river running brown and fast and full of the monsoon's first stolen water.

"That's not a river," Teo said. "That's a flood with ambitions."

"It's the Serrano's front door," Pemba said. "And it's the only ford for forty miles either direction."

Ilse stood up through the roof hatch, one hand braced on the roll bar, her hair whipping. She looked upstream, then downstream, then at the sky — which had gone the color of wet slate, low clouds dragging their bellies on the peaks — and Teo watched her do the same arithmetic Pemba had been doing, and watched her arrive somewhere worse.

"The Calloway team will know about this ford too," she said.

"Everyone knows about this ford," Odile said. "That's the problem with fords."

Ansel was in the passenger seat with the compass open on his knee. He'd been holding it more and more these last two days, Teo noticed — not navigating with it, exactly. Consulting it. Like a man checking a wound to see if it was still bleeding. The needle lay steady, quivering faintly, pointing not north but off to their left, into the high country where the passes waited under their first skins of snow.

"Half a day behind us," Ansel said quietly. "Maybe less. They've got two vehicles and no conscience." He closed the compass. "We cross now, fast, and we're on the far bank when they arrive. Water buys us time."

"Water also eats trucks," Odile said.

"So we don't let it eat the truck."

That was Ansel all over, Teo thought. Stating the impossible like it was a checklist item. Five weeks ago Teo had been a kid who fixed radios in Corvane and lied about his age to anyone who'd listen, and now he was riding in a truck full of stolen national treasure with people who talked about flooded fords the way other people talked about weather. He still wasn't sure how it had happened. He suspected it had something to do with being the only person who'd ever said yes when Ansel Yarrow asked for help.

The truck ground down the switchbacks toward the water. Teo braced himself against the chest — the Kestrian chest, roped and tarped in the bed, heavy as a sin — and tried to read the river the way Ansel had taught him to read storms. Look for structure. Look for what the chaos is organized around.

The chaos here was organized around a gravel bar midstream, a pale tongue of shallows that split the current in two. On the near side the water ran shin-deep and friendly. On the far side it piled against the cutbank in a single brown shoulder of current, deep enough to lift a tire, fast enough to shove one sideways.

"Near channel's fine," Ansel said. "Far channel is the fight. We hit it straight on, second gear, no stopping. Odile?"

"No stopping," Odile said. "I heard you the first time, boss."

"And if we stop," Pemba said, not looking up from the map, "we become a very expensive rock."

* * *

They almost made it.

The near channel was everything the gravel bar promised — a long hissing sheet of ankle-deep water, spray fanning off the tires, and Teo laughing despite himself, actually laughing, because there was something ridiculous and wonderful about a truck swimming. Then the far channel took them and the world turned to brown violence.

The current hit the driver's side like a fist. The truck lurched, slewed, and Odile swore in three languages and fed it fuel, and for one glorious second the wheels bit and they were driving on the bottom of a river, which Teo would have said was impossible, except everything about the last month was impossible. The water climbed the doors. It came over the hood in a brown cataract and broke across the windshield, and through it Teo could see nothing but churning silt and the ghost of the far bank, impossibly far away.

Then the rear end stepped out.

He felt it before he understood it — the whole vehicle pivoting downstream, the current getting its teeth into them, Odile fighting the wheel with both arms and her whole furious shoulders. Ansel was shouting something. The engine screamed. And the chest — roped, tarped, lashed to the bed rails — shifted.

Teo saw it happen in pieces, the way bad things happen. The rope on the downstream corner fraying against the bed rail. The tarp peeling back. The chest sliding an inch, two inches, grinding toward the tailgate. And himself — because he was closest, because he was the rookie, because nobody had time to think — already moving, climbing over the seatback into the bed, dropping to his knees on the pitching steel, throwing both arms around the chest's cold bronze-stamped flank.

"Hold it!" Ansel roared, half turned in his seat, rain and river hammering the glass between them.

"I've got it!" Teo shouted back, and discovered that this was only half true. The chest weighed as much as a dead man. The truck bucked beneath him like something trying to be born. Every time the current shoved the vehicle, the chest shoved back, sliding, grinding, wanting the river the way rivers want everything.

The rope snapped.

It went with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the chest surged, and Teo went with it, arms locked around it, boots skidding on the wet bed steel until his heels caught the tailgate lip. For one endless moment he was a boy lying flat on his back in a flooding truck bed, hugging a nation's treasury while a river tried to take them both, and the sky above him was gray and rushing, and he thought, with strange clarity: *this is how I die, and it will be in the company report as a cargo loss.*

Then the tires found the far gravel.

It happened all at once — the lurch, the bite, the sudden blessed drag of solid ground as the truck clawed up the shallowing water and out onto the far bank in a fan of spray and stones. Odile didn't stop. She drove them fifty yards up the bank, into the alders, and only then did she stand on the brakes, and the truck rocked to a halt, dripping, steaming, alive.

Teo lay in the bed with his cheek against the chest, breathing like a landed fish.

"Teo." Ansel was already out of the cab, hauling himself over the tailgate, hands finding Teo's shoulders, patting him down with rough efficiency — arms, ribs, head, like checking a crate for damage. "You idiot. You absolute — are you hurt?"

"I'm fine," Teo said, and found that his voice shook, and hated it. "I'm fine. I just — the rope—"

"You went into the bed," Ansel said. "In moving water. Tied to nothing."

"Somebody had to hold it."

Ansel stared at him. Rain dripped off the brim of his hat between them. Behind him the others were piling out — Odile already circling the truck looking for damage, Pemba standing hip-deep in the shallows staring back across the river, Ilse with both hands pressed flat on the chest as if taking its pulse.

"You could have been swept," Ansel said. "Do you understand? That channel would have taken you under the cutbank and you'd have come out a mile down or never. For a box."

"For *the* box," Teo said.

"That's not the same thing as your life."

"It felt the same," Teo said. "Sitting in the cab watching it go. It felt exactly the same."

Something moved across Ansel's face then — quick, complicated, gone before Teo could name it. Ansel looked away, at the river, at anything else, and clapped Teo once on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, and said, "Never again," in a voice that meant *thank you*, and climbed down out of the bed without another word.

* * *

They took inventory in the lee of the alders, and the inventory was ugly.

The river had gotten into the bed during the crossing and stayed there, sloshing, and it had taken its tithe. Two of their fuel jerry cans were gone — torn loose, downstream, somewhere in the Serrano's throat. The food box had been lashed better but not well enough; the lid had worked open and the river had taken the flour, the rice, most of the dried meat, leaving them with oats, tea, a sack of onions Odile refused to surrender, and half a case of tinned peaches that Pemba held up like a relic.

"Half our supplies," Pemba said. "Call it half. Maybe more."

"How far to the next resupply?" Odile asked.

"Corvane is behind us. Ahead—" Pemba spread the map on the tailgate, weighting the corners with stones. His finger traced the line up through the foothills, past the treeline, to the col. "Nothing. There's a shepherd hut marked here, but it's a mark, not a promise. After that, the archive, if it exists, if Ilse's letter isn't lying."

"The letter doesn't lie," Ilse said, in the voice she used when someone doubted Kestria in her presence.

"The letter is sixty years old," Pemba said gently. "Rivers change. Huts fall down. People starve between marks on maps."

Teo sat on the tailgate eating a tin of peaches with his fingers, listening to them do the math of survival, and noticed that nobody had said the obvious thing yet. So he said it.

"We could turn back."

Four heads turned. He kept his eyes on the peaches.

"There's a town behind us. There's a road. We sell the chest to whoever pays, we split it, everybody lives. Vance gets paid off, Calloway gets nothing, and I go back to fixing radios." He shrugged, aiming for casual and hearing the crack in his own voice. "I'm just saying what everyone's thinking."

Silence. The river filled it, talking to itself in the distance.

Then Odile laughed — short, harsh, not unkind. "Kid, I've known this crew eight years. You know what we did the last time the math said turn back?"

"What?"

"We didn't." She jerked a thumb at Ansel. "This one drove us into a hailstorm once because the radar showed a hook echo and he wanted the shot. Pemba navigated us across a salt flat at night by the stars because the road washed out. I rebuilt a transmission in a parking lot in Corvane with parts I bought from a man I'm fairly sure was a war criminal." She counted them off on grease-black fingers. "We are not a crew that turns back. We're a crew that runs out of luck eventually, but we haven't yet, and I've got a feeling about this week."

"You always have a feeling," Pemba said.

"My feelings have a record."

Teo looked at Ansel, because Ansel was the one who decided, whatever the voting rituals they pretended to have. Ansel was standing apart, looking back across the river at the near bank, where the road they'd come by curved away south toward debts and flatlands and every version of himself he was trying to outrun. The compass was in his hand again. Teo hadn't even seen him take it out.

"We go up," Ansel said finally. "We ration. We walk when the truck can't. And when Calloway comes to this ford and finds our tracks going the wrong way for them—" a thin, tired smile, the first Teo had seen from him in days— "they'll have to decide whether to follow us into winter. Rivers buy time. We just spent half our supplies buying more of it."

"And if they follow anyway?" Teo asked.

Ansel closed the compass. "Then they learn what Odile's feelings can do."

* * *

They camped that night in the alders, cold-camped, no fire because smoke carried in a gorge. Teo drew first watch, which he suspected was a kindness disguised as a chore — the watch nobody wants, right after dark, when you can't sleep anyway.

The river talked all night. That was the thing about crossing one; it stayed in your ears afterward, a low constant argument of water on stone. He sat with his back against a tire, wrapped in the blanket Odile had thrown at him without comment, and listened to the gorge breathe.

Across the water, far downstream, he saw it: a light. Brief, bobbing, gone. Then another. Headlamps, maybe. Or fires. Moving along the near bank the way people move when they're searching a riverbank for a crossing.

Calloway. A day behind, maybe less. Finding the same gravel bar, doing the same math.

He should wake Ansel. That was protocol; that was the whole point of a watch. But he sat there instead, heart tapping, and watched the lights, and did his own math: how many hours of darkness left, how many miles of switchbacks, whether the truck could climb the first ridge in the dark, whether fear was heavier than supplies.

A boot scraped behind him. Ansel lowered himself onto the tailgate beside him without a word, following his gaze across the river, and after a while he said, quietly:

"You saw them."

"Yeah."

"Good eyes." Ansel was quiet a moment. "When you grabbed that chest today — I want to tell you something, and then I want you to forget I told you." He turned the compass over in his hands, not opening it. "Five years ago I'd have let that box go. Would have told myself it was the smart call. Cargo versus crew — any fool does the arithmetic." He looked at Teo, and in the dark his face was mostly shadow and the gray gleam of the river. "Today I watched a kid nearly drown for a box, and my first thought wasn't the box. It was you. And I've been trying to work out all evening when that happened — when the arithmetic changed." He pocketed the compass and stood. "Wake Pemba for second watch. Don't mention the lights till morning; let them sleep while they can. Tomorrow we climb."

He walked back to his bedroll. Teo sat a while longer, watching the far lights move, feeling something settle in his chest that he didn't have a name for yet — warm and terrifying and new, like the first breath after coming up out of deep water.

Belonging, maybe. He'd find out later that's what it was.

Above the gorge, unseen behind the cloud deck, the snowline waited, and somewhere on the near bank, men were pulling boats from a truck bed, and the monsoon was gathering itself over the plains like a debt coming due.
