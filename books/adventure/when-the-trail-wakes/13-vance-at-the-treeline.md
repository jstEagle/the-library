## 13. Vance at the Treeline

The snowcat's engine reached them before its headlights did — a low diesel growl rolling up the valley like a throat being cleared by something enormous. Odile heard it first because she always heard engines first; twenty years of listening to machines tell on themselves had tuned her ears finer than any of Pemba's instruments.

She was elbow-deep in the truck's rear axle when the sound came, grease to her wrists, a stubborn bearing half-free in her grip. She went still the way a hare goes still, and Teo, who'd been holding the work light, saw her face change and killed the lamp without being asked.

"Odile?"

"Listen."

Above the treeline, where the pines gave up and the world turned to scree and gray sky, the growl swelled, then faded, then swelled again. Circling. Something up there was looking for them the way a hawk looks for mice — wide, patient loops, burning fuel it could afford because someone else was paying for it.

"Snowcat," she said. "Big one. V-8 or better." She wiped her hands slowly down her thighs, thinking. "Nobody rents those for sightseeing."

Pemba emerged from the tent with the compass cupped in his palm like a small animal he was afraid to squeeze. "How far?"

"Two ridges. Maybe three miles as the crow flies, but that thing doesn't fly. It has to come around through the saddle." She stood, knees cracking, and looked at the map spread across the tailgate, weighted at the corners with lug nuts. The route they'd chosen threaded between two walls of rock and climbed through a stand of black spruce before breaking into open country above the treeline. Good cover for a truck. Terrible cover for a truck being chased by something with tracks instead of tires.

Ansel came back from scouting the switchbacks with frost in his eyebrows and bad news in his posture before he said a word. "Fresh tracks," he said. "Sno-cats, plural. And boot prints. At least six men, maybe eight. They came up from Corvane side."

Ilse stood apart from all of them, arms crossed against the cold, the chest's documents wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "Then we're done talking about whether he found us," she said. "We're only talking about what happens next."

* * *

They spent an hour that felt like a day preparing, and Odile hated every minute of it, because preparation meant admitting the fight was coming, and some stubborn mechanic's superstition in her believed that if you never said a war out loud, sometimes it forgot your name.

It didn't forget this time.

They cached what they couldn't carry — spare parts, the heavy tools, water they could melt from snow later — under a cairn Pemba built with a care that bordered on prayer. They loaded the chest into Ansel's pack because he was strongest and Ilse insisted on walking beside him, one hand never far from the straps. Teo got the flare gun and instructions to use it only if things went truly wrong, which Odile privately translated as: when things go truly wrong.

"We go up through the trees," Ansel said, tracing the line on the map. "Quiet, single file, no lights. If we can reach the col before dark, we're ghosts to them."

"And if we don't?" Teo asked.

Ansel didn't answer, and Odile loved him a little for not lying to the kid.

She took point. It was her right by seniority and her burden by temperament — she walked angry, and anger made good pace. Behind her came the soft crunch of five sets of boots and the muffled bulk of the pack, and above them the spruce closed ranks, branches heavy with last week's snow, sifting white down their collars with every touch. The air smelled of resin and cold iron. Somewhere behind, faint as a conscience, the snowcat kept circling.

They were forty minutes into the climb when the flares went up.

Not theirs. Two red stars burst over the ridge to the east, and the whole mountainside lit up like a wound, shadows swinging long and wrong across the snow. Shouts followed — human voices, amplified by cheap megaphones, bouncing off the rock walls until it was impossible to tell how many men there were or where any of them stood.

"Odile Yarrow!" a voice called, and her own name in a stranger's mouth made her skin crawl worse than the cold ever could. "Maro Vance would like a word!"

"Keep moving," Ansel hissed. "Don't answer. Don't stop."

But the mountain men were already among them.

They came down through the trees on skis and snowshoes, big quiet shapes in white, and the first warning was a rope snapping taut across the trail ahead — a tripwire rigged with a road flare that burst in a blinding bloom of magnesium. Odile threw up an arm, blind, and heard Teo cry out, heard Pemba shouting directions in three languages at once, heard the unmistakable wet crunch of a body hitting snow.

She swung blind and connected with something solid — a shoulder, a jaw, she'd know the feel anywhere — and the man went down grunting. Then hands were on her, gloved and professional, wrenching her arms back, and she fought the way she did everything, with her whole weight and no elegance whatsoever, driving her heel into a shin, throwing her head back into a nose she felt give with a satisfaction that shamed her even mid-fight.

"Odile!" Ansel's voice, raw, somewhere to her left. "The chest — don't let them—"

She got one arm free and drove her elbow into the ribs of the man holding it and spun, and in the dying red glare of the flare she saw the whole ugly picture at once: mountain men everywhere, six of them at least, and Maro Vance standing apart beneath a spruce with his hands in his coat pockets, watching the way a man watches weather he ordered.

And she saw Pemba.

He'd gone down near the trail's edge, where the slope fell away steep and slick toward the creek drainage, and one of the hired men had him by the collar of his parka while another bound his wrists with something that gleamed — zip ties, plastic, cheap and final. Pemba wasn't struggling. That was the worst part. He was looking past his captors at the rest of them, calm as a navigator plotting a course, and he mouthed something she couldn't read in the light.

"Pemba!" Teo screamed it, and started forward, and Odile caught him around the chest and hauled him back as a rifle butt whistled through the space the boy had occupied.

"Enough." Vance's voice carried without effort, the voice of a man who had never once needed to shout. "Nobody dies tonight. Mr. Vance is very clear about that." He stepped closer, boots whispering on the snow, and his eyes found Ansel across the chaos. "You have something of mine, Yarrow. Well. Of my employers'. I'm prepared to be generous one more time. The chest, and the woman walks away unharmed, and you all walk away period. That's the offer. It expires."

"It expired chapters ago," Ilse said, and her voice shook with fury rather than fear. "You don't even know what you're carrying for them. You're a Kestrian name selling Kestrian bones, and someday you'll have to live in that knowledge with nowhere to hide."

Something flickered across Vance's face — fast, quickly buried, but Odile had spent a lifetime reading faces over engines, over poker tables, over the hood of a truck at midnight, and she caught it. Doubt. A hairline crack in good masonry.

Then it sealed over, and he snapped his fingers, and the man holding Pemba began dragging him backward up the slope toward the waiting snowcat, its headlights now blazing through the trees like a pair of cruel yellow eyes.

"No—" Teo lunged again. Ansel grabbed him. For one suspended second the whole crew strained toward Pemba like compass needles toward a pole, and Vance watched them do it, and Odile understood with sick clarity that this was the actual plan — not the chest, not yet, not entirely. Take the navigator. Split the crew. Make them chase.

"The chest stays with you for now," Vance said pleasantly. "Consider it collateral. You'll bring it to me when you want your friend back. You have until the pass closes. After that—" he shrugged, a small elegant gesture obscene against the violence around it, "—the mountain keeps what the mountain takes. So does the Syndicate."

They dragged Pemba into the trees. His eyes found Odile's one last time, and he smiled — actually smiled, that infuriating gentle smile — and mouthed it again, slow enough this time for her to read:

*Go up. Don't follow.*

* * *

They stood in the wreckage of the flare light for a long time after the engines faded. Snow sifted down through the spruce, filling boot prints, erasing evidence, indifferent as bureaucracy.

Teo sat in the snow with his head in his hands. Ilse held the strap of Ansel's pack in a white-knuckled fist, as if the chest might levitate away without her anchoring it. Ansel stared up the slope where Pemba had vanished, and his face was doing something Odile had never seen it do in five years and four thousand miles: nothing at all. Blank as a gauge with the needle pulled out.

It was left to her, then. It was usually left to her.

"We move," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended, scraped raw. "Now. While they think we're broken. Broken people sit still, and sitting still is dying up here."

"They have Pemba." Teo's voice cracked down the middle. "We can't just—we have to go after him. Right now. Tonight."

"With what?" She rounded on him, and the fury she'd been banking all night finally broke loose, hot and shameful and necessary. "With what, Teo? Six of us against eight guns and a snowcat? You want to end up tied next to him? You want me carrying *you* out of here in a duffel bag?" She was shaking. She made herself stop. "Vance counted on exactly this. On us being stupid with love. That's why he took Pemba and left the chest — because he knows we'll trade sense for a person every single time."

"Is that wrong?" Teo asked quietly.

And there it was, the question with teeth, and Odile had no honest answer, so she gave him the true one instead. "I don't know. But dead people can't rescue anybody. We go up. We make the col. And then—" she looked at Ansel, who had finally moved, finally blinked, finally come back from wherever he'd gone, "—then we figure out how to get our navigator back without handing Vance the one thing that matters more than any of us."

Ansel nodded slowly. "Split," he said. "Ilse and Teo push for the col with the chest. Odile and I go down after dark, track the camp, get Pemba out."

"That's insane," Ilse said.

"Yes," Odile agreed. "Get used to it. It's been the plan since the river."

* * *

They left messages for each other at the cairn — Pemba's cairn, built that morning with such care, now a mailbox for the living. Ansel wrote in block letters on waterproof paper: *Col by tomorrow night. Then we come to you.* Odile added a line of her own, because superstition demanded it and grief permitted nothing less: *Hold on, old friend. Nobody gets left. Not on my watch.*

Then the crew split in the blue hour, and Odile watched Teo and Ilse dwindle up the trail into the spruce shadows until the mountain folded them away, and the loneliness of it hit her like altitude — sudden, crushing, stupidly physical.

She and Ansel descended through the trees as the dark came down full, moving on the snowcat's track, following the churned trench of its passage like a scar leading straight to the heart of the trouble. Twice they froze as searchlights swept the lower slopes. Once they hid in a culvert with meltwater soaking through to their skin while voices passed close enough to count teeth.

Near midnight, from a granite shelf above the tree line's ragged edge, Odile looked down and saw it: Vance's camp, a cluster of dome tents glowing amber, the snowcat parked with its engine ticking as it cooled, and beside it a smaller vehicle — a cargo sled — and figures moving between the fires.

And there, seated on a supply crate with his hands still bound but his spine straight as a surveyor's rod, sat Pemba. One of the hired men offered him something — food, maybe, or a flask — and Pemba inclined his head with perfect courtesy and declined, and even at this distance, even in this dark, Odile could see that he was watching everything. Cataloguing. Counting guards, timing rotations, mapping the camp the way he mapped passes.

Her eyes burned, and she told herself it was the wind.

"He's fine," Ansel breathed beside her. "Look at him. He's *working*."

"Yeah." Odile wiped her face with the back of a greasy hand — she'd never washed the grease off, and was suddenly, fiercely glad of it. "Course he is. Question is whether we're good enough to match him." She settled onto her belly in the snow, cold biting through her jacket, and began counting guards aloud, low, methodical, a woman doing what she knew how to do. "Four on watch, rotating every twenty minutes. Two by the sled. Vance's tent is the big one, and I'd bet my left arm there's a radio inside it."

Below, a fire popped. Above, the clouds were building again, fat and black out of the southwest, the monsoon's vanguard arriving to bury every escape route.

"Twenty minutes," Ansel murmured. "That's our window each rotation."

"Then we'd better not waste the first one," Odile said, and started down.
