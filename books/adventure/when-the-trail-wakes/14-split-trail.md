## 14. Split Trail

The cairn stood where the trail forked below the treeline, a stack of gray stones no higher than a man's knee, and it had been there long enough that lichen had stitched its seams together. Someone had built it before any of them were born — some herder, some pilgrim, some nameless traveler marking the one place in the Serrano Range where a choice could be made and unmade.

Ilse Marchetti stood before it with the chest strapped to her back and the wind pulling her braid sideways, and she thought: *this is where everything I have left gets divided in half.*

Below them, the monsoon had arrived like a country annexing another. The foothills had vanished under a bruise-colored ceiling, and the rain came down in ropes, in sheets, in the long gray curtains of a storm that intended to stay for days. Every pass east of them was already closing. Every hour they stood here arguing was an hour the snows gained on them from above.

"The col is forty hours if the weather holds," Pemba had said, before they took him. "It won't hold. So it's fifty. Maybe sixty. We go light or we don't go at all."

That had been the morning. Then Vance's men had come down off the ridge at dusk with Pemba's pack and Pemba's boots and no Pemba, and the arithmetic had changed.

Now it was night, and the four of them — what was left of them — stood in the rain around the old cairn, and Odile was saying what everyone already knew.

"They'll move him up-valley to the mine road. Vance wants the chest, but he wants it *clean* — no Syndicate blood on it, no witnesses. He'll keep Pemba alive as long as Pemba's useful, and Pemba's useful exactly as long as we believe he's alive." Odile's voice was flat, the voice she used for engines that couldn't be saved. "Which means we have maybe three days."

"Then we go after him," Ansel said.

"And who goes to the col?" Ilse turned on him, rain streaming off her jaw. "The archive doesn't wait. The vault seals for winter — my grandfather's letters are explicit. If we miss it, we don't come back in spring and knock. The location dies with the season. Everything — the treasury, the names, forty years of *proof* — buried under an avalanche slope until someone else finds it, and the someone else will be Calloway, or worse, the Syndicate."

"So we split."

The words came out of Teo, and everyone turned. The kid stood with his hood down, rain running over his face, seventeen years old and shaking — but not from cold, Ilse realized. From the effort of having said it.

"We split. Two to the col, two to the camp. It's the only play that doesn't lose everything."

"It's the play that loses *half* of everything," Odile said.

"It's the play that keeps half," Teo said. "That's more than we've got standing here."

Nobody answered that. The rain filled the silence, hammering the canvas of the chest, drumming on stone, and Ilse looked at the cairn — at the small, patient pile of rocks some dead traveler had left as a promise that *someone passed this way* — and understood that they were about to do the same thing. Leave proof. Leave trust. Leave a trail of words in a place where words were the only thing that could cross the distance between them.

* * *

**The first letter, in Ilse's hand, wedged under the third stone from the top:**

*Ansel —*

*If you're reading this, the fork is behind us and the rain has already tried to erase the trail, so I'll keep this short and I'll keep it honest, because I've decided that's the currency between us now.*

*Teo is holding. He doesn't complain, which worries me more than complaining would. He reads the compass at every halt without being asked, and he's stopped flinching when the needle moves. I think the boy is becoming the kind of person who walks toward things instead of away from them, and I think you did that, and I think you should know it.*

*The pass is worse than the maps promised. We lost the trail twice above the second lake and found it again by following the cairns the old Kestrian herders left — small ones, waist-high, spaced a day's walk apart. My grandfather's generation built them. Forty years and the stones still remember the way. I keep touching them as we pass, like shaking hands with the dead. Teo pretends not to notice. He's kind that way.*

*The chest is heavier every day. Not the chest — the knowing of it. Every switchback I think: this is the weight my grandfather carried up a mountain so that a country could be born twice. And then I think: what if the country it's born into is only me, and a boy, and a brass compass?*

*Don't die in Vance's camp. If you die, I'll have carried this thing over a mountain for a man who owed a debt, and I refuse to have the story end that way. Come to the col. Both of you. That's not a request. I've spent my whole life having things taken; I'm done asking politely.*

*— I.M.*

*P.S. The needle points at you. Did you know that? Not at your valley — at you. Teo checked. Whatever the compass wants from you, it hasn't finished wanting it.*

* * *

The mine road climbed the eastern wall of the valley in a series of switchbacks cut into scree, and Ansel Yarrow climbed it in the dark with rain in his collar and Odile's silhouette ahead of him, moving with the economy of a woman who had spent thirty years crawling under things.

They'd left the truck at the fork, camouflaged under branches. They'd left Teo with Ilse, which had been the hardest thing Ansel had done in five years of hard things, harder than lying to Vance, harder than wading the Alder — because Teo had looked at him with that open, unguarded face and said, "You'll come back," and Ansel had said, "Yeah," and both of them had known the word was a coin tossed into deep water.

Now there was only the road, and the rain, and the thought of Pemba's boots sitting empty in Vance's camp.

"Smoke," Odile said, an hour before dawn. She crouched at the edge of a cut in the hillside, and Ansel crouched beside her, and there it was below them: a bowl of a valley where the mine road leveled out, and in it, three structures — a canvas mess tent, a equipment shelter, and a snowcat parked under a tarp like a sleeping animal. Two fires burned under a lean-to. Figures moved between them. Four that he could count. Maybe five.

"Pemba?"

"Can't see. The shelter's got no windows facing us." Odile chewed her lip. "Guard rotation's sloppy. They're mountain men, not Syndicate. Vance hired locals — look at the gear, mismatched. He's spending his own money now, not the company's. That means the Syndicate's cut him loose or he's cut himself loose."

"Which is worse?"

"Which is worse for *us*, or worse for *him*?"

Ansel didn't answer. He was watching a figure come out of the shelter — tall, moving carefully, favoring one leg — and even at this distance, in the firelight, the way the man held his head was unmistakable. Chin up. Shoulders squared. The posture of a man who had decided that if he was going to be a prisoner, he would be a prisoner the way mountains were prisoners: slowly, and on his own terms.

Pemba.

Something in Ansel's chest unclenched for the first time in two days.

* * *

**The second letter, in Ansel's hand, left at the cairn on their way back up-valley — because Odile had insisted they pass the fork again, had insisted on the ritual of it, had said, "Words are rope, Ansel. You leave them anchored on both ends or the whole line goes slack."**

*Ilse —*

*He's alive. We saw him. He walked out of that shelter like he owned it, and I wanted to laugh and I wanted to break something, both at once. They're feeding him. He's limping but the limp looks like a sprain, not a break — Odile agrees, and Odile knows what broken looks like better than anyone I've ever met.*

*Here's the strange part, and I'm writing it down because I don't trust my own head at this altitude: Vance isn't treating him like leverage. He's treating him like a* guest *who can't leave. There was a plate. There was a second plate. Vance sat across from him for an hour last night, talking. I couldn't hear what they said. But Pemba didn't look afraid. Pemba looked like a man listening to a confession.*

*I don't know what that means yet. I'll know soon. We go in tomorrow before first light, while the fog sits in the bowl. Odile's already got a plan for the snowcat that I'm not going to write down in case you're captured and this letter is, because I've decided to be paranoid now, at the end, when it's finally too late to be anything else.*

*Teo — tell him — I don't know. Tell him the sky over the mine road did that thing at sunset, the green-under-gold thing, and I thought of him pointing at clouds and naming them like they were horses. Tell him the storm's coming up the valley behind us and it's the biggest I've ever read and I wish he were here to read it with me. That's not sentiment. That's a weather report.*

*Keep the chest dry. Keep yourself drier.*

*— A.Y.*

*P.S. The needle. I know. I've known since the cave. I haven't told you what it's pointing at because what it's pointing at isn't a place anymore. I think it's a thing I have to do. When it's done, I'll tell you everything. That's a promise made in writing, at altitude, in the rain, which in my family is the most binding kind.*

* * *

They went in at gray light, under fog so thick the world shrank to twenty feet in any direction, and it went wrong in the way Ansel had learned, over five years, that all plans go wrong: not catastrophically, not all at once, but in small betrayals of detail.

The guard on the north perimeter wasn't where the rotation said he'd be. He was closer. He was smoking, and the cherry of his cigarette floated in the fog like a signal fire, and Odile took him down from behind with a length of climbing sling and an apology she actually whispered — "sorry, friend, nothing personal" — and they stashed him behind a stack of drill casing with his own gag in his mouth and his own pride, presumably, bruised.

The shelter door wasn't locked. That was the first thing that went *right*, and it went so right that Ansel's skin prickled, because in his experience doors that should be locked and aren't are doors someone wants you to walk through.

Inside: crates, rope, a cold stove, and Pemba, sitting on a cot with his ankle wrapped in a clean bandage, reading. Actually reading — a paperback, spine cracked, held in both hands like a man with all the time in the world.

"You're late," Pemba said.

"We came as fast as—" Ansel stopped. "You're *reading*."

"Vance lent it to me. Kestrian poetry, translated badly." Pemba set the book down, and his face, in the gray light through the canvas seam, was something Ansel had never seen on it — not fear, not relief, but a kind of terrible thoughtfulness. "Ansel. Before we run. You need to hear what he told me."

"We don't have time—"

"We have exactly as much time as it takes for you to understand that the man who took me prisoner is not the man you think he is, and that if you run out of here and leave him to the Syndicate, you will be doing the same thing the Syndicate did to Kestria." Pemba stood, testing his ankle, wincing. "Sit down. Five minutes. Then we burn his snowcat's fuel line and walk out of here together, and you can hate me for the delay the whole way up the mountain."

Outside, the fog pressed against the canvas like a living thing. Somewhere in it, Odile was working on the snowcat with a multitool and thirty years of grudges against machinery. Somewhere above all of it, on the far side of the range, Ilse and a seventeen-year-old boy were climbing toward a col that was closing like a door.

Ansel sat down on the crate across from Pemba, and Pemba began to talk, and everything Ansel believed about the man who had hunted them across half a country came apart like wet paper.

* * *

**The third letter was never written.**

Ilse left the cairn behind at dawn on the third day, chest on her back, Teo a half-step behind her on the knife-edge of the ridge, and the col ahead of them wrapped in cloud. The snows had started early — fat, wet flakes that melted on contact and then, as they climbed, stopped melting, and began instead to *accumulate*, silencing the trail, erasing the cairns one by one behind them like a tide going out.

At the last herder's cairn below the col, Teo stopped and dug in his pack and pulled out paper and a pencil stub gone soft with rain.

"You're leaving one," he said. Not a question.

"I don't have anything to say that they don't already know."

"Write it anyway." The boy's face was raw with wind, his lips cracked, his eyes steady in a way that no seventeen-year-old's eyes should have to be. "It's not a message. It's a rope. You said — you told me your grandmother said — a rope anchored on both ends."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Then she knelt in the snow, shielded the paper with her body, and wrote four lines, and folded them into the cairn's cold heart:

*We are above the clouds. The col is open. Whatever you carry back to us — Pemba, or the truth about Vance, or nothing but yourselves — carry it fast, and carry it whole.*

*The mountain is keeping our place.*

Then she stood, and shouldered the weight of a dead nation, and turned toward the pass — and did not see, far below and behind, at the fog-line of the mine road, a second column of smoke rising into the gray morning, thin and black and wrong, the kind of smoke that comes from burning canvas and burning plans and burning bridges of every kind.

The storm took the smoke. The storm took the letters' answers before they were ever written. But the rope held — anchored at the cairn, anchored at the col, strung taut across forty miles of falling snow — and somewhere along its length, in a camp full of borrowed fires, Ansel Yarrow was learning that the man he'd been running from for five years had been running too.
