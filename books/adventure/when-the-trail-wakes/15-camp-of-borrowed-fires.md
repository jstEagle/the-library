## 15. Camp of Borrowed Fires

The snowcat's tracks told the story before we saw the camp. They ran up the drainage in a lazy switchback, confident as a signature, and Odile read them the way she read engine noise — with her whole body tilted toward the sound of trouble.

"Two machines," she said, crouching where the treads had chewed a bank of wind-slab. "Maybe three days old. He's not hurrying."

"He doesn't have to hurry," I said. "He has Pemba."

She didn't answer that. She'd been not answering it for two days, since the treeline scattered us and we lost sight of everything except each other's boot prints. Now it was just the two of us climbing through a forest of black spruce bent like old men under the snow load, following a man I hated toward a fire I could already smell.

Woodsmoke and diesel. That combination meant Vance. The Syndicate burned whatever was cheapest, but Maro Vance had always kept his camps warm. I remembered that from the survey years, before the dam, before the flood, before I learned that warmth could be a thing men sold to each other at interest.

We moved until full dark, then stopped and let the cold have us for an hour while we listened. Below us, maybe four hundred meters down the slope, the camp glowed against a rock wall like a coal someone had cupped in their palm. Two tents, the snowcats, a cook fire throwing shadows that jumped when the wind gusted. Three figures moving. Four if you counted the one sitting apart, hands bound in front of him with cord, back against a boulder.

Pemba.

Even at that distance I knew the set of his shoulders. Five years on the road teaches you to read your people the way Pemba read contour lines — not their shapes but their meanings. And what those shoulders said was: *I am still here. I am still mine.*

"They'll have a watch rotation," Odile whispered. "Two up, one sleeping, probably."

"Vance sleeps," I said. "He always sleeps well. It's how he wins. Men who can't sleep start making mistakes out of guilt."

"And you slept fine, back then?"

I didn't answer that either. There were too many answers and none of them would fit through the cold.

* * *

We came down through the trees on the north side, where the wind had scoured the snow thin over bare rock, because Odile said a quiet step beats a fast one every time. She went first. I followed, counting my breaths the way my father taught me when I was small enough to be afraid of thunder — in for four, hold for seven, out for eight — except now I was counting toward something worse than thunder.

The sentry nearest us stood at the edge of the light with a rifle slung loose and his collar up, watching the storm build in the west. The monsoon's outer bands had reached even here, and the clouds above the pass were churning like something being boiled. Every few minutes the wind carried up a smell I hadn't smelled in five years: wet stone, high country, the specific green-black breath of a storm deciding where to die.

Odile took the sentry without violence. She simply stepped out of the dark, put a wrench against the base of his skull hard enough to fold him, and caught him on the way down so he wouldn't make noise against the rocks. Then she zip-tied him to a spruce, gagged him with his own scarf, patted his cheek twice — an apology, or a promise — and looked at me.

*One,* her eyes said.

We took the second sentry the same way, near the snowcats. That left the cook, an older man stirring a pot with the absent patience of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and Vance himself, standing at the fire with his hands out to the heat.

And Pemba, bound at the boulder, watching me come out of the dark with an expression I will carry to my grave: not surprise, not relief, just a slow nod, as if I'd arrived exactly on schedule and he'd been keeping my seat.

"Yarrow." Vance didn't turn around. "Your tread pattern. Left rear tire, patched sidewall. I've been reading your tracks for three days." He turned then, and the firelight did its work on his face, carving the familiar planes deeper. "You always did drive like you were apologizing to the road."

"Untie him," I said.

"In a minute." He nodded past me, and I realized the cook had set down his pot and picked up a shotgun with the calm of a man choosing a spoon. "Sit by the fire, Ansel. You're half-frozen and it's embarrassing to watch. We'll talk like professionals."

"We're not professionals. You're a collector. I'm a debtor. That's the whole relationship."

"That was the whole relationship." Vance crouched and fed a branch to the fire, and for a moment the light climbed his face and I saw something I had never seen on Maro Vance in eleven years of knowing him at a distance and two years of fearing him up close. Hesitation. "Things got complicated."

"Complicated." Odile laughed, low and ugly. "You mean you took our navigator and dragged him up a mountain."

"I mean," Vance said, "that I need to show you something before you decide what I am. And then you can shoot me, or tie me, or walk away, and I'll live with whichever you choose." He glanced at Pemba. "Him especially. He's earned the right to decide."

Pemba's voice came out rough from cold but level. "Show them the letter, Maro."

*Letter.* Not *boss*. Not *the man who tied me to a rock for three days*. Something passed between them that I couldn't read, and it made the hair rise on my arms worse than the cold did.

Vance reached inside his coat and brought out an envelope, yellowed at the edges, soft as cloth from handling. Kestrian script covered one side in a hand that shook slightly, the ink browned with age.

"My grandmother wrote it," he said. "Before they closed the border. Before the maps changed. Her name was Amalie Vance before it was Amalie Vansko — did you know that? Half the Syndicate families are Kestrian names worn smooth like river stones. Vane itself. Vanescu. Vance." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion. "We erased ourselves to survive, and then we built the machine that erases everyone else. That's the family business. That's *my* family business."

"You're lying," Odile said flatly.

"I'm a lot of things, but I've never needed to lie about this." He held the letter out to Pemba, who took it with his bound hands and read it in silence, lips moving over the old script the way he'd moved them over the chest's ledgers weeks ago, in another life. When he looked up, his face had changed.

"It's real," Pemba said quietly. "It's her hand. It matches the letter in the chest — the same phrasing, the same—" He stopped, swallowed. "Maro. What is this?"

"The price list." Vance sat down heavily on a supply crate, and suddenly he wasn't a Syndicate enforcer anymore, just a tired man in a borrowed coat beside a borrowed fire. "You want to know why I took you instead of killing you? Why I've been herding you up this mountain instead of calling in the ring's spotter planes? Because my family are bond-servants, Yarrow. Has been since the Syndicate absorbed the Kestrian debt after the annexation. Three generations. My mother works a loom in a mill town you've never heard of, and my sister's contract passes to her daughter when she dies, the way property does. The treasury buys them out. All of them. Every Kestrian name on the Syndicate's ledger — and there are more than you'd believe. We didn't disappear into the Syndicate. We were *folded into* it, like a bad debt."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, thunder rolled down the Serrano Range like furniture being dragged across heaven.

"So you sell us to them," I said. My voice came out strange. "You hunt the one thing that could free your people, and you sell it to the people who enslaved them. At interest."

"At the only rate they offer!" For a moment the enforcer flashed through, all teeth and fury, and then it guttered out. "You think I haven't done that arithmetic every night for twenty years? You think I don't know what I am?" He looked at his hands, which were scarred and steady and suddenly shaking anyway. "I told myself it was mercy. Sell the treasury back cheap, buy out the bonds, save what I can reach. A ransom paid in other people's heritage. And then I caught up to you at the treeline and watched your mechanic try to kill me with a wrench, and your rookie stand in front of the chest like a door, and your navigator look at me with *pity*, Yarrow. Pity. And I thought: this is what my grandmother felt. This is what it is to be the thing other people run from."

Silence, except the wind and the fire and the storm gathering its strength beyond the pass.

"Why tell us now?" Odile asked. Her voice was still hard, but her hands had come off the wrench.

"Because tomorrow the pass closes, and Calloway's scout is somewhere behind you with the chest, and the Syndicate's second team lands at Corvane tonight. After tomorrow there's no more choosing. So I'm choosing now, in front of witnesses." He stood, and crossed to Pemba, and cut the cords off his wrists with a knife that trembled only slightly. "Take me to the archive or leave me here. Testify against me or testify for me. But hear this first: I never touched the boy. I never gave the order to hurt anyone. I've spent my whole career making sure the Syndicate's collectors were feared instead of lethal, and I don't know if that makes me better than the rest of them or just a coward with a conscience, but it's the only thing I have left to be."

Pemba rubbed his wrists, flexing his fingers, studying Vance with the long patience he gave to maps. Then he looked at me.

"Ansel. The compass."

"What about it?"

"When they searched us at the treeline, they took everything. Everything except—" He smiled, tired and fierce. "Except what was sewn into my coat lining. Because Maro told them to search gently. Because Maro knew what it was. Because Maro's grandmother wrote letters in the same hand."

My heart stopped, started again wrong. "You have the compass."

"I have the compass." Pemba drew it from inside his jacket, brass dull in the firelight, and set it on the crate between us all. The needle swung once, twice — and settled, steady as a plumb line, pointing west. Toward the ridge above Harrow Bend. Toward my father's survey kit. Toward home.

Toward the archive, Ilse would say, because for her it had always pointed both ways at once.

"Then we move tonight," I said. "Before the pass closes. All of us — and Vance, you walk in front where Odile can see you."

Vance nodded slowly. Then he paused, head cocked, listening to something beneath the wind — and his face went gray.

"That's not thunder," he said. "That's the second team's snowcat. Coming up the south drainage. They landed at Corvane early."

Odile was already snuffing the fire with snow, moving, alive. Pemba pocketed the compass. And far below us in the dark, engines growled closer, carrying men who had no grandmothers' letters, no debts of blood, nothing in their hearts but a ledger — and orders to leave no witnesses on the mountain.
