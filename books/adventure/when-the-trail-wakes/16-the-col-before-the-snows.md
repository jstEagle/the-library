## 16. The Col Before the Snows

Pemba had counted his steps for three days, and somewhere past four thousand he had stopped counting and started simply climbing, one boot in front of the other, the way his grandmother used to knead dough — without thought, without mercy, until the work became a kind of prayer.

The rope between him and Ansel Yarrow sang in the wind. Not a musical sound; more like a wire fence humming before a storm, that thin metallic complaint of something stretched past its intention. Pemba had learned to read ropes the way other men read weather. This one said: *we are all still here, we are all still holding.*

Below them, the monsoon made landfall.

He had watched it come for two days — a bruise on the southern horizon that widened into a wall, then into a country of cloud so vast it seemed less like weather than like geography, a new sea laid down over the lowlands they had crossed a lifetime ago. Now it arrived beneath them, filling the valleys like gray water filling a basin, and the thunder did not crack so much as *roll*, long and continuous, a sound with no beginning that Pemba felt in his back teeth and in the soles of his boots and in the old scar along his ribs where a yak had once kicked him.

"Look at it," Teo breathed. The boy stood at Pemba's shoulder, roped in, hood thrown back despite the sleet because he was nineteen and nineteen-year-olds believed weather respected youth. "It's like the whole world below us is drowning."

"It is drowning," Odile said from behind. She had Ilse's pack strapped over her own, because Ilse's shoulder still wouldn't take weight after the treeline fight, after Vance's hired mountain men, after the day everything scattered and Pemba spent nine hours zip-tied in a snowcat's cargo bed listening to Maro Vance talk about his mother's hands. "That's what monsoons do. That's why we're above it instead of under it."

"That's why we're *dead* if we stay on this face much longer," Ansel said.

He hadn't stopped moving while he said it. None of them had. That was the thing about the last three days that Pemba kept trying to explain to himself and failing: there had been no stopping, not once, not even to sleep — only the slow grinding rotation of hours in which they climbed and then climbed, and someone always held the rope, and someone always watched the slope, and the chest rode in its sling against Ilse's back like a sleeping animal none of them dared wake.

The slope in question rose ahead of them at forty degrees, maybe fifty, a field of old snow sitting wrong on the mountain's bones. Pemba had been watching it since dawn. He had grown up below mountains like these, in a village where the word for avalanche was also the word for grief, and he knew the sound a snowpack made when it was thinking about letting go. It was making that sound now. A deep, subterranean settling, like a house shifting on its foundation, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and deciding whether to wake.

"We go around," he said.

Everyone looked at him. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"The face is loaded," Pemba went on, and heard his own voice come out flat and certain, the voice of his grandfather reading the sky off the eastern ridge. "One more hour of this sleet and it comes down on whoever is standing under it. We traverse east to the ridge spine. The knife."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The knife ridge was the thing they had been avoiding saying out loud for two days: a half-mile of exposed crest, no wider than a hallway in places, with air on both sides and the storm coming up behind them like a tide.

"Ilse?" Ansel asked.

Ilse Marchetti sat against a boulder with her arm bound across her chest, her face the color of the granite, and she looked up at the face, then east at the ridge, and Pemba saw her do the arithmetic that exiles learn young — the arithmetic of what can be carried and what must be left.

"The archive is beyond the col," she said. "My grandfather's letter says the vault sits under the north face, and the col is the only approach that isn't a cliff. If we don't cross today, we don't cross. The pass closes tonight." She paused, and her voice, when it came again, was quieter. "Kestria waited forty years. I can wait one more hour. But the mountain won't wait at all."

"Then we move now," Ansel said, "and we're on the spine before the face decides anything." He turned, and for a second his eyes found Pemba's, and there was something in them Pemba had never seen there before — not the restless measuring look of a man counting miles, but something steadier. Gratitude, maybe. Or recognition. "Lead us, navigator."

* * *

They traversed in silence, because silence was the price of the slope.

Pemba went first, testing each placement, driving his axe in shallow and shallow again, feeling through the shaft for the answer the snow gave back. Solid, solid, hollow — a pocket of depthless nothing under two feet of crust, and he skirted it wide, and behind him the rope paid out and took up and paid out, five lives strung together like beads on a wire.

He thought about his grandmother's stories. He had thought about them almost constantly since the night Odile picked that lock in the truck bed and the smell of forty-year-old paper came out of the bronze-stamped chest like a breath from a sealed room. Kestrian script, she'd said, touching the ledger pages with one finger, careful as touching a burn. His grandmother had written letters in that script, letters to cousins in a country that stopped answering, and when Pemba was small she had told him stories of the red-roofed towns and the bell foundries and the treasury that would rebuild everything someday, someday, *someday* — the way poor families say *someday*, meaning never, meaning *keep hoping anyway because hope is free*.

He had never believed the stories were a place you could walk to.

Now the place was strapped to a wounded historian's back, forty feet of rope behind him, and the country it belonged to was a wall of cloud swallowing the world below, and Pemba's legs burned with a tiredness that had gone past pain into something almost holy.

A sound came down the valley. Not thunder. Thunder rolled *over* you; this rolled *under*. A long groan out of the mountain's throat, deep as a ship's hull, and every hair on Pemba's arms stood up inside his sleeves.

"Move," he said, and did not turn around, and did not need to. He heard the rope go taut. He heard five sets of crampons bite at once. Behind him, faintly, Teo made a small sound — not a word, just a note of fear, honest as an animal's — and then they were all moving, fast and smooth, the way frightened things move when they have decided together not to panic.

The face let go thirty seconds later.

It did not come for them. It came off the slope a quarter-mile south, a whole meadow of snow standing up and walking, and the sound of it was the sound Pemba had heard once as a child from a safe valley floor and never forgotten: not loud so much as *total*, a white river going by at the speed of a falling body, carrying boulders the size of trucks in its teeth. The powder cloud reached them a full minute after the mass had gone, a cold soft slap of displaced air that smelled of stone and ancient ice, and then the mountain was quiet again, quiet the way a room is quiet after a door slams.

They stood roped together on the traverse, breathing.

"Navigator," Odile said finally, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word, and she laughed, one short bark, "navigator, I am going to buy you a drink the size of a bathtub."

"Save it," Pemba said. His hands were shaking now that it was over, shaking the way hands are allowed to shake only afterward. "Spine first. Drinks after."

* * *

The knife ridge was worse than he had promised, which was the navigator's burden: you told the truth about terrain and people remembered it against you.

It rose out of the traverse like the backbone of something buried, a crest of wind-carved ice and dark rock no wider than a kitchen table, dropping away on the left into the drowned valleys and on the right into a cirque where the storm was already arriving — spindrift streaming off the crest in banners, the wind coming up the right side in gusts that hit like open hands. Beyond it, dim through the streamers, the col itself: a saddle of gray light between two black towers, the last door before the far side.

Pemba went first because he had been first all day and because, he suspected, nobody else could bear to look down on both sides at once.

The trick, he had learned from a Sherpa cousin on a different range in a different life, was to stop looking down and start looking *along* — to treat the ridge as a road and refuse to acknowledge it was also an edge. So he looked along. He placed his feet on the rock ribs where the ice had scoured away, hooked his axe over the crest when the gusts came, and moved in the small hunched shuffle of a man crossing a bridge that happened to be made of sky.

Halfway across, the wind decided to test them.

It came in a series of gusts, each one bigger, each one separated by just enough calm to make the next one feel like betrayal. On the fourth gust Pemba dropped to one knee and hooked his axe and felt the whole rope-line shudder behind him, five bodies leaning into the same invisible fist. Someone shouted — Odile, maybe, or the wind wearing Odile's voice — and then the gust passed and the world resumed and they kept moving, because on a knife ridge the only direction is forward.

It was Teo who faltered.

Not from weakness. From the look of the thing — from a gap in the crest where the rock had collapsed, a three-foot notch with nothing below it but a thousand feet of cirque, requiring a step across that any of them could make on a sidewalk and none of them could quite believe in here. Pemba crossed it, anchored, and watched the boy arrive at the edge and stop, and saw in Teo's face the exact expression of a man who has suddenly understood how high up he is.

"I can't—" Teo started.

"You can," Pemba said. "Step where I stepped. The rock is good. The only thing across that gap is the rest of your life."

And then Ansel's voice, from further back, calm as a man discussing freight rates: "Teo. Remember the ford. Remember Calloway's scout and that water. You didn't freeze there."

"I almost drowned there."

"You didn't." A pause, wind screaming through it. "None of us let you. That's the whole arrangement. That's the entire deal we've all got. Nobody drowns, nobody falls, nobody gets left — not because we're strong, but because we don't allow it. Now step."

Teo stepped.

Later, on the far side, safe in the lee of the first tower, the boy sat down hard in the snow and put his face in his mitts and shook, and nobody teased him, because every one of them wanted to do exactly the same thing. Odile put a hand on his hood, briefly, the way you'd steady a lantern. Ilse leaned against the rock with her bound arm and her impossible chest and closed her eyes.

Pemba looked back along the ridge they'd crossed. The spindrift banners streamed off it, gorgeous and indifferent, and below on the left the monsoon country churned, lightning flickering inside the cloud like thoughts inside a closed skull.

His grandmother had a word for weather like this. Not a Kestrian word — an older one, from before, from the mountain villages that had never belonged to anyone. The word meant roughly: *the sky does not hate you; the sky has simply not noticed you.* And standing there with his crew — and that was the word he found himself using now, *crew*, though six weeks ago they had been four strangers and a debt — Pemba understood that this was the whole secret of surviving indifferent things. The sky would never notice you. The mountain would never love you. But the people on the rope could, and did, and that was the only shelter there had ever been.

* * *

The col, when they reached it, was anticlimax shaped like a saddle.

Gray light. Wind funneled through it with a constant moan, like breath across a bottle top. Snow lay smooth on the far side, unbroken, descending toward a north-facing bowl where — according to a letter written forty years ago by a man who had hidden a nation's memory in the rock — a vault door waited under stone.

They had perhaps an hour of light. Maybe less; the storm was eating the horizon in visible bites.

Ansel stood at the top of the descent, compass in his bare hand, and Pemba watched the needle swing — not north, never north, but toward something down in that bowl, something that made Ansel's jaw tighten in a way Pemba had learned to read as *memory*. The compass had been dragging the man toward his dead for days now, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, because some cargoes you carry by pretending they aren't there.

"Down and across the bowl," Ansel said. "Fast, spaced out, one at a time on the steep pitch. The snow's stable if we don't insult it." He looked around at them — Odile with her burned hands wrapped, Teo hollow-eyed and upright, Ilse pale as paper with her chest and her papers and her grandfather's dream, Pemba with his aching legs and his grandmother's stories come true. Something moved through Ansel's face, quick as the lightning below. "I've chased weather my whole life," he said. "This is the first time I've ever outrun it to something instead of from something."

"Sentimental," Odile said. "Save it for the vault."

But she was smiling, or nearly, and Pemba was smiling too, or nearly, and for one moment at the top of the world, five exhausted people stood together in the wind and let themselves believe the worst was behind them.

Which was when Teo, facing back the way they'd come, said:

"What's that?"

Pemba turned. Far down the knife ridge, tiny with distance, movement — figures on the crest, roped, moving with the practiced speed of professionals. And behind them, lower on the flank they'd escaped that morning, a machine: a snowcat's running lights, crawling toward the col like a slow yellow star.

Odile raised her binoculars. A long moment. Then she lowered them, and her voice came out flat and cold as the ice under their boots.

"Calloway's colors. And they've got our scout with them." She paused. "The one from the ford. The one we fed."

The wind screamed through the col, indifferent, carrying nothing either way. Pemba tightened his grip on his axe and looked at the smooth unbroken snow of the far side — the last descent, the vault, the end of forty years of waiting — and understood that the mountain had saved its cruelest lesson for last:

There is no finish line. There is only the next rope-length, and who is holding the other end.

"They'll reach the col in an hour," Ansel said quietly. "We cross now, or we stand here and meet them."

Ilse pulled the chest higher on her back. "Then we cross," she said, "and we pray the snow remembers whose side it's on."

Pemba turned downhill, drove his axe in, and led.
