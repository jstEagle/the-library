## 17. Betrayal at the High Col

The col had a sound before it had a name. Teo heard it first as a whisper under the wind — the hiss of snow grains migrating across the knife ridge like sand across a desert floor, millions of them, all going somewhere without him. Then Pemba named it, shouting over the gale: *the Col of the Last Honest Wind*, some old herder's name from his grandmother's stories, and Teo thought there was nothing honest about any wind that cold. It went through his parka like water through a net.

They had been climbing since before dawn. Fourteen hours on the ridge, roped in pairs, the chest strapped to Ansel's pack because Ansel refused to let anyone else carry it — *it's my debt,* he'd said, as if a bronze-stamped box could be weighed in dollars. Ilse moved ahead of them all with a steadiness that seemed almost mechanical, planting her ice axe at metronome intervals, and Odile brought up the rear muttering about rope wear in a voice too low for comfort.

Teo was tied to Ansel. He had been tied to Ansel since the ford, since the river tried to take him and the crew refused — refused, actually argued about it, shouted at each other over whether to go back in after him, and then went back in anyway. He still didn't fully understand what had happened there. In his old life, in the salvage yards outside Corvane where he'd learned to pick locks and hotwire generators, nobody came back for anybody. You were useful or you were gone. But these people had waded into brown floodwater for a rookie who'd been with them six weeks, and ever since, Teo had been carrying something strange in his chest, something he didn't have a word for yet. It felt like vertigo. It felt like standing on a ledge and realizing the drop wasn't behind you anymore but inside you, and that falling into it might not kill you.

He was thinking about that — about the word he couldn't find — when the scout made his move.

* * *

His name was Dario. That was what he'd told them at the ford, anyway, spitting river water and shaking, half-drowned himself, claiming Calloway's team had cut him loose when he objected to their methods. A survivor, he'd said. Just trying to get over the range before the snows. And they'd believed him, because grief makes people generous and cold makes them stupid, and because Odile had checked his gear twice and found nothing but a man's ordinary misery.

He had walked with them nine days. He'd shared tea with Pemba, listened to Ilse read Kestrian script by headlamp, helped Teo patch a torn tent fly. He'd laughed at Teo's jokes. That was the part Teo kept snagging on afterward, turning it over like a stone with something alive underneath: the laughing had been real. Or it had sounded real. He would never know now which, and maybe that was the whole cruelty of it — that betrayal doesn't require a monster, just a man willing to be warm right up until the moment warmth becomes expensive.

The col narrowed where a serac had calved away years ago, leaving a gap crossed by two fixed lines — an old climbing rope on the left, frayed and sun-bleached, and their own newer line rigged on the right. Beyond the gap the ridge widened again toward the descent slopes, and beyond those, somewhere below the cloud deck, the archive waited. Ilse had stopped at the gap to re-tie her crampon strap. Ansel was unclipping from the fixed line to transfer to their own. The chest rode high on his back, and the wind hit it broadside, and for three seconds — three seconds, Teo would count them forever — every hand on the mountain was busy.

Dario moved like he'd rehearsed it against the dark for nine nights.

Later, reconstructing it, Teo realized how much rehearsal it must have taken: the way Dario's axe swung not at a person but at the anchor, one clean strike to the piton holding their running line; the way his other hand already had the chest's strap, cutting it free of Ansel's pack with a blade that must have lived in his sleeve since the ford; the way he'd chosen the exact moment when Ansel's weight was off the rope and onto the rock. Not improvisation. Choreography. Nine days of watching them, learning their rhythms, waiting for the col to offer him its one narrow door.

The line parted with a sound Teo felt through his boots more than heard — a wet snap, like a bone.

"NO—" Odile's voice, raw, from the rear.

Dario shoved past Ilse — she grabbed for him and got a fistful of sleeve, and then he was through the gap and moving down the far side with the chest slung across his back, fast, impossibly fast, a man who had trained his whole life for exactly this slope and no other. The severed end of their rope whipped away into the cloud like something fleeing.

And Teo, standing on the near side of the gap, felt the world tilt.

Because the old fixed line — the bleached one, the one they hadn't trusted, the one Odile had called *a widow-maker with delusions* — that line had been anchored to the same serac wall. And when Dario's axe strike cracked the ice around the new piton, the vibration traveled. Teo saw it happen in terrible slow motion: a seam in the blue ice opening like a mouth, a fist-sized block tilting, sliding, dropping —

— taking the old anchor with it.

Ansel was clipped to the old line.

* * *

There is a moment — Teo would try to describe it to Ilse much later, in a low room in a rebuilt town, and fail, and try again — when you watch someone you love start to fall and your body decides things your mind hasn't voted on.

Ansel dropped maybe four meters before the old rope caught on a horn of ice and jerked taut, and he swung out over the void with the broken anchor groaning above him, and everything in Teo went silent and simple. The chest was gone. The treasury — the gold, the ledgers, the names of the erased, forty years of a dead nation's hope — was receding down the mountain on a traitor's back. Every rational argument, every debt, every reason they had climbed fourteen hours through a storm said *go after the chest.*

Teo went after Ansel.

He didn't decide it. His hands decided it — gloves off, bare fingers on frozen rope, feeding line toward the gap while Odile screamed his name and Pemba threw himself flat to anchor the remaining line across Teo's waist. The wind took his breath and gave it back sharp. Below him Ansel hung like a pendulum, boot tips scraping blue ice, and above him the cracked anchor shed little white teeth of snow, and Teo understood with perfect clarity that he was about to die, and understood equally clearly that this was not the relevant question.

"TEO!" Odile again, closer now, her voice shredded by wind. "THE ANCHOR WON'T HOLD —"

"I KNOW!" He didn't know anything. He knew the rope was burning through his palms even through the gloves. He knew Ansel was looking up at him with an expression Teo had never seen on the man's face — not fear, worse than fear, the naked horror of a man watching someone else choose his death. "Cut me loose!" Ansel shouted. "Teo! Cut it! The chest —"

"To hell with the chest!"

The words tore out of him before he could weigh them, and he saw them land — saw Ansel's face change, saw five years of measuring life in miles crack open like lake ice in spring. And in that same instant the anchor finished dying. The horn of ice sheared, the old rope sang once and went slack, and Ansel fell.

Teo fell with him.

Not all the way. Pemba's line caught them both — caught Teo, who caught Ansel, one arm hooked through the other's harness in a grip that would leave bruises shaped like fingers for weeks. They slammed the wall together, swung, slammed again. Snow smoked off the rock. Somewhere above, three people were hauling with everything they had, and the rope hummed a note Teo felt in his molars, and the world reduced itself to this: weight, friction, breath, the astonishing animal fact of another human heartbeat hammering against his own through two layers of synthetic fill.

"Hold," Teo gasped, to Ansel, to himself, to the mountain. "Hold, hold, hold."

"I've got you." Ansel's voice, wrecked, right against his ear. "Kid — I've got you —"

"You don't let go either."

"I'm not letting go."

They hauled them up. It took eleven minutes. Teo counted, because counting was a thing his mind could do instead of screaming. Eleven minutes of Odile's arms and Pemba's arms and finally Ilse's, all of them leaning back against the storm, walking the rope hand over hand, until Teo's knee found the lip of the ridge and he crawled over it dragging Ansel behind him, and the four of them collapsed into a tangle of rope and limbs and ragged breathing on the lee side of the serac, alive, absurdly, stupidly alive.

For a long time nobody spoke. The wind filled the silence with its grain-whisper, its migration of small white lies.

Then Odile sat up, looked down the far side of the col where Dario had vanished into the cloud deck with a nation's inheritance, and said, very quietly, in the voice she usually saved for engines that had betrayed her:

"We're going to kill him."

"No," said Ilse.

Everyone turned. She was kneeling in the snow, and her face was wet, and whether it was melt or tears Teo couldn't tell and suspected she couldn't either. Her eyes were fixed on the empty gap, on the space where the chest had been.

"No," she said again, softer. "We're going to follow him. Because he doesn't know what he's carrying." She pulled the sealed letter from inside her jacket — the letter they'd opened at the foothill camp, the letter her grandfather had written in a hand like iron filings. "He thinks he's stealing gold. He isn't. He's carrying proof of the dam failure, and the names of every family the Syndicate erased, and if he sells it to Vance, it doesn't just disappear." Her jaw set. "It burns. All of it. Every page. That's what the Syndicate does with paper that remembers."

Pemba translated the wind for a moment, then said, "The monsoon hits the valley tonight. The passes close behind us tomorrow."

"Then we don't sleep," Ansel said.

Teo looked at him. Ansel was sitting up now, working blood back into his fingers, and there was frost in the stubble of his beard and something else in his face — something rearranged. He looked at Teo the way a man looks at a compass needle that has finally settled.

"You went over the edge for me," Ansel said. "With the chest getting away."

"It wasn't a decision."

"I know." Almost a smile. "That's what scared me."

* * *

They descended the far side into the teeth of the coming storm, four ropes, four lamps, four small stubborn flames against a wall of weather. The trail below the col was Dario's — fresh crampon marks filling with snow, a discarded glove, the arrogance of a man descending fast because he believed no one was behind him.

Teo walked last, as he always did now, and watched the marks fill, and thought about the word he'd been carrying since the ford. He had it now. It had come to him on the rope, in the eleven minutes, in the space between falling and being caught.

It wasn't *family.* He wasn't ready for that word; it was too big to fit through the door of what had happened today. But it was adjacent to it. It was the room next door. It was the knowledge that when the rope broke, four people had leaned back into the storm for him without a vote, without a calculation, without asking what he was worth — and that he had gone over the edge for Ansel the same way, and that neither act had been a transaction.

Below them, the clouds were turning the color of bruised iron. The monsoon's leading edge had reached the foothills; he could feel it in the pressure, a heaviness in his ears like the whole sky leaning down to listen.

Ahead, faint in the failing light, the crampon tracks bent east — not down toward the Syndicate's roads, but along the flank of the mountain, toward a route only one person among them had ever studied. Toward the archive's back door, if Ilse's grandfather's letter meant what they thought it meant.

Dario wasn't running for the Syndicate.

He was running for the vault.

"He knows the letter," Ilse breathed, staring at the tracks, and her voice went thin and cold. "He's known it since the ford. He's been listening since the ford."

The first rain of the monsoon arrived then, fat and warm and wrong at that altitude, striking the snow around them like applause.

Ansel shouldered his pack — lighter now by the weight of a nation — and looked back at his crew, at the strange family the mountain had welded out of strangers, and said the only thing left to say:

"Faster, then."
