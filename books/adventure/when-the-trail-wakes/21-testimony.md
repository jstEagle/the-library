## 21. Testimony

The storm came down the mountain with us, as if it had been waiting at the archive door to walk us home.

We descended in the grey half-light of a monsoon morning, the Serrano Range dissolving behind us into a wall of rain that had no top and no bottom. Ilse led, because she knew the old Kestrian pack trails the way other people knew their own kitchens. Teo came second with the empty chest strapped to his back — empty now, its ledgers and its gold and its forty years of names left in the vault with the exiles, but he carried it anyway, the way you carry a thing that has changed what you are. Odile took the rear with her wrench still hanging from her belt like a sidearm she no longer believed in. Pemba walked beside me, bruised from Vance's camp, saying nothing, which from Pemba was a kind of sermon.

And Maro Vance walked among us.

Not at gunpoint. That was the strange part. Odile had offered, actually offered, to tie him, and he had looked at her with those flat grey eyes and said, "You'd have to catch me first, and I'm tired." So he walked free in our midst, a man unburdened by the pretense of being our enemy anymore and not yet anything else. His snowcat lay dead on the treeline where Odile had killed it. his hired mountain men had melted into the weather like they were made of it. All he had left was himself, and a ledger entry he wanted struck, and — though I wouldn't understand this until later — something that looked, from certain angles, like shame.

The rain found every seam in my coat. I let it. There was a strange cleanliness in being soaked all the way through, as if the mountain were rinsing five years off me, and I kept thinking about the compass, which rode in my inner pocket with its needle finally still. It had pointed me to my father's survey kit in that crevasse. It had pointed me, all along, to the thing I'd buried deepest. Now it pointed nowhere, and neither, I thought, did I. For the first time in five years, nowhere felt like a place I could stand.

We came down out of the clouds near dusk and found shelter in a shepherd's hut — dry-stone walls, a roof of sod, a hearth that Odile coaxed alive with kindling and blasphemy. We hung our clothes to steam. We ate Pemba's rice and Ilse's dried Kestrian figs, which tasted like honey and smoke and a country that no longer existed. And then Vance sat down across the fire from me, and said the sentence I had been dreading since the vault.

"They'll want it in writing."

I looked at him. "What?"

"Your testimony." He warmed his hands like a man with no claim to warmth. "The dam. The survey. The flood. You understand what I'm telling you, Yarrow. The evidence Ilse has is paper — engineering reports, correspondence, the Vane family's own signatures. Paper convicts a company. But a company is made of people, and people are convicted by witnesses. You were there. You signed the survey. You were nineteen years old and they put your name on a lie, and then they paid you to bury your own family under the silence." He looked into the fire. "I was there too. Lower down the ladder than you, but there. If you testify alone, they'll call you a grieving man with a grudge. If I corroborate, they'll have to call us both liars, and they'll have to do it in a courtroom, under oath, where the Vane Syndicate's lawyers have never once had to stand."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the rain kept up its work on the world.

"And what do you get?" I asked him. It came out harsher than I meant, but he didn't flinch. He never flinched. That was the thing I'd hated most about him, back when hating him was simple.

"My name out of their ledger," he said. "Same as we agreed. And one thing more." He reached into his coat — Odile's hand went to her wrench, old habits — and drew out a folded paper, soft with handling, and laid it on the stone between us. "The servitude contracts. My family. Three generations of Vanes who were never Vanes at all — my grandmother's name was Vaskel. Kestrian. They changed it when they took her family's debt." He said it evenly, the way you recite something you've said to yourself so many times it's gone smooth as river stone. "The Syndicate bought the treasury to keep buying people like her. I was going to help them do it. I want that on the record too. Not just what they did. What I did."

I looked at the paper a long time. The names on it were written in two scripts, one I knew and one Pemba had grown up hearing in his grandmother's stories, and they were the same names, twice, like a person photographed from two sides.

"Tomorrow," I said. "We go down to Corvane tomorrow. There's a magistrate there who owes Odile a favor and a telegraph line that reaches the coast. We do it properly. Sworn, witnessed, sealed." I looked up at Vance. "If I'm going to burn five years of silence, I'm going to burn them all at once."

* * *

Corvane in monsoon season was a town holding its breath under a grey sky, gutters running like rivers, awnings sagging under the weight of water. The Last Honest Town had gotten less honest since we'd passed through — Vance's posters were still on the telegraph poles, our faces rain-blurred, REWARD printed beneath in letters that had begun to peel like sunburned skin. Teo pulled one down without being asked. He crumpled it, then stopped, smoothed it out, and folded it into his pocket instead.

"For the record," he said, when I looked at him. "Evidence."

The kid was learning.

Magistrate Halloway was a broad, slow woman with a magistrate's patience and a ferryman's hands — she'd run the river crossing before the bridges, Odile told me, and Odile had rebuilt her engine twice for free, and debts like that don't drown. She cleared her back room, sent her clerk home, swore us in one at a time on a ledger book because Corvane's courthouse had flooded and its Bible was drying on a line somewhere. The rain hammered the roof like a fist that never tired. A single lamp burned on the table between us. And I put my hand on a ledger full of other people's debts, and I told the truth.

It's a strange thing, telling the truth after five years of not telling it. You'd think it would come out clean, like water from a tap. It didn't. It came out the way the flood had come — in surges, in silences, in pieces of debris I hadn't known I was carrying.

I told them about the survey. I was nineteen, fresh out of technical school, and the Vane Syndicate paid better than any outfit in the valley, and I was grateful, and gratitude makes you blind in exactly the shape of the thing you're grateful for. My job was instruments — levels, transits, the patient mathematics of where water wants to go. My father had taught me. He'd been a surveyor too, before the Syndicate bought up the valley, and he'd warned me about them at the dinner table with the specific weariness of a man who'd been cheated by them once already. I told them I took the job anyway. I said the word *anyway* and heard, for the first time, how much of my life had lived inside it.

I told them about the readings. The seepage line climbing the dam face a foot higher every month. The pressure gauges that didn't match the reports. The memo I drafted — I still remembered its number, I said it out loud and my voice didn't shake, which surprised me — flagging the spillway capacity as insufficient for a hundred-year event. And I told them about Corben Vane's project manager, a soft-spoken man named Aldous Retch, who read my memo in front of me, slowly, the way you read something you intend to remember in order to destroy, and thanked me for my thoroughness, and had me reassigned to a culvert project forty miles south before the month was out.

"They didn't fire me," I said. "That's what I want understood. Firing me would have created a record. A disgruntled ex-employee is a risk. A promoted employee is a defense." My hands were flat on the table. I watched them as if they belonged to someone else. "They moved me up. More money. A crew of my own. And in the spring, the rains came, and the spillway failed exactly the way my memo said it would, and the valley went under in forty minutes."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Halloway's pen scratched. She was writing everything down in that slow ferryman's hand, and I was grateful she didn't look up, because looking up is a kind of mercy and I didn't deserve mercy yet, not in the middle of it.

"My family lived eleven miles downstream," I said. "My father. My mother. My sister, who was fourteen and wanted to be a pilot." I had not said my sister's name aloud in five years. I said it now. "Renna Yarrow." The name sat in the room like a third witness. "The Syndicate's investigators ruled it an act of God. Unprecedented rainfall. They paid settlements — small ones, fast ones, with clauses. I took mine. I signed the clause. And then I took the debt, the real debt, the one with the Syndicate's own seal on it, because a man who's just sold his silence needs somewhere to put the money, and I put it in the ground over my family's names, and when that was gone I put the rest into a storm-chase rig and five years of driving away from the sound of water."

I stopped. The rain filled the silence for me.

"That's my testimony," I said. "Ansel Yarrow. All of it. I'll sign whatever you put in front of me."

* * *

Vance went second.

I expected him to be smooth. I'd watched him lie to my face in a diner over bad coffee and never blink, and I think we all braced for the performance. What came instead was something flatter and stranger and more damning: a recitation. He gave names. Dates. Ledger numbers. He described the internal file the Syndicate kept on the dam failure — not the public report, the real one, the one marked *Legal Hold* — and he described who had access to it, and what it contained, which was my memo, the original, kept for five years in a fireproof cabinet in the Vane family's private offices because Corben Vane was too careful to destroy leverage even when the leverage was against himself.

"I saw it," Vance said. "I was sent to retrieve it in the spring, when the rumors about the Kestrian treasury started, because Corben wanted all his paper in one place. The memo was in the same cabinet. I read it. I've read everything I've ever been sent to fetch — it's how you survive in that house, knowing where the bodies are filed." He folded his hands. "I can testify to the existence and location of that document. I can testify that Corben Vane knew the dam was unsafe before the failure, and suppressed the engineering finding, and settled the survivors' claims with non-disclosure clauses. And I can testify," and here his voice did something I had never heard it do, something almost gentle, "that the woman who raised me signed a servitude contract with that same house when she was sixteen, and that her name before they changed it was Vaskel, and that she was Kestrian, and that she died still owing them for the privilege of existing."

Halloway's pen had stopped moving. She was looking at Vance with the expression of a woman recalculating a river crossing she'd made a thousand times.

"You understand," she said, "that what you're both describing is not a debt dispute. It's manslaughter. It's conspiracy. It's the kind of thing that ends companies."

"It's the kind of thing that ends companies," Vance agreed.

"And you," she said, turning to me, "you understand that your debts — and I've seen the paper, Yarrow, the Syndicate's claims against you would drown this whole town — you understand they don't vanish because you've told the truth. Truth isn't legal tender."

"No," I said. "But it's collateral." I slid the folded pages across the table — my sworn statement, Halloway's clerk-copy, signed and sealed with the Corvane magistrate's stamp, which was a lopsided ferry-wheel pressed in wax. "You send that to the coastal courts, and you send a copy to every newspaper that will print it, and you send a copy to the Vane Syndicate's own board of directors, because corporations have a weakness, Magistrate. They're run by optimists. And an optimist who reads a document like this has to make a calculation: what's the treasury of a dead nation worth, weighed against the cost of defending a live one?" I tapped the wax seal. "They'll fold. Not because they're sorry. Because the arithmetic changed."

Vance made a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly killed. "He talks like them now," he said to Halloway. "Five years of chasing storms and he's learned to price things. That's my fault too, probably. Put it in the record."

She put it in the record.

* * *

We walked out of the magistrate's back room into a Corvane washed clean and shining, the rain stopped at last, the streets running gold with lamplight, the mountains to the west holding the last of the storm against their shoulders like a burden being set down. Odile had the truck running — she'd spent the day on it, and it idled with the deep contented sound of an engine that has been argued with and won. Pemba was asleep in the cab. Teo was on the tailgate, and when I came out he held up the crumpled poster, our faces on it, REWARD in peeling letters, and then he tore it in half, and then in half again, and let the pieces fall into the gutter where the water took them south, toward the coast, toward the courts, toward whatever came next.

"Did it work?" he asked. "The testimony. Do you feel —" He stopped, hunting for the word. The kid was always hunting for words, and I loved him for it, because I'd spent five years refusing to hunt for mine.

"Lighter," I said. And then, because it was true and because he'd earned it: "And heavier. Both at once. Like setting down a pack and picking up a country." I looked at the wet street, the gold light, the truck. "You get used to the weight. That's the trick nobody tells you. You get used to it, and then one day you set it down and you can feel the air on your back, and it's terrible, because the air remembers everything the pack was hiding."

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "Renna," he said. "Your sister. You said her name in there."

"I did."

"I wrote it down." He tapped his chest pocket, where he kept his little weather notebook, the one where he recorded cloud types and wind speeds and, apparently, now, the names of the dead. "So it's in two records now. The magistrate's and mine."

I put my hand on his shoulder and couldn't speak for a second, and he had the grace to pretend he didn't notice.

Vance came out last. He stood on the magistrate's step in the lamplight, a man with no snowcat, no hired men, no ledger entry, no name he could trust, and for the first time since the flats he looked — not free, nothing so kind as free — but unassigned. A storm without a heading.

"The board will have my statement by wire tonight," he said. "Corben will deny everything by morning. By afternoon the optimists will start counting." He looked at me. "You understand they'll come for you anyway. Men like Corben don't forgive arithmetic. They just change the equation."

"Let them come," I said, and heard, in my own voice, a man who meant it.

Odile leaned out the driver's window. "Are we standing around feeling things, or are we driving? Because I rebuilt this carburetor today and it wants to be driven, and I have earned a drink, and Corvane has exactly one bar that will serve us."

We got in. All five of us — because Vance stood a moment longer on the step, and then, without asking, without being asked, opened the passenger door and folded his long grey self into the seat beside Pemba, who woke, looked at him, and went back to sleep, which was its own verdict and its own pardon.

The truck pulled out of Corvane under a sky scrubbed down to its first stars. Behind us the Serrano Range stood black against the west, holding its archive, holding its treasury, holding the names of the erased, holding — somewhere under the ice, in a crevasse no compass would ever need to find again — my father's survey kit, and everything it had been waiting to tell me.

I didn't check the rearview. For five years I had measured my life in miles, and miles were just distance wearing the costume of time, and I was done with the costume.

Ahead of us the road ran flat and open toward the coast, and the needle of the compass in my pocket lay still as a held breath, and somewhere a wire was crossing the dark country toward a boardroom full of optimists who were about to learn what my father always said at the dinner table, the sentence I'd carried out of the valley like a splinter: *the water always keeps its books.*

By morning, so would they.
