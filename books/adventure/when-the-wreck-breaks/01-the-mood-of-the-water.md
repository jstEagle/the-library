## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Kestrel River had moods the way old women had opinions—freely, loudly, and with no particular interest in whether you agreed.

Orsen Ravensworth had spent eleven years learning to read them. This morning's mood was sullen but honest: a low gray sky pressing down on brown water, a wind out of the northwest that smelled like rain that hadn't committed yet, and a current running just fast enough to make the sandbars lie about their positions. She stood at the wheel of the *Vesper's Wake* with one hand on the brass rail and the other holding a chipped mug of tea gone lukewarm, and she watched the water the way other people watched a card game they'd already won.

"There," she said.

Pell didn't look up from where he was coiling rope on the foredeck. He never looked up when she said *there*. That was the arrangement between them. She spotted; he trusted; neither of them wasted breath on confirmation.

"Left side or right?" he asked, which was not confirmation so much as bookkeeping.

"Left. The one pretending to be a shoal."

"Ah." Pell finished his coil, hung it on its peg with the neatness of a man who had once been paid to be sloppy and found he hated it, and ambled back toward the engine housing. "The liar at Marker Nine?"

"The very one."

"She moves every spring."

"So do I."

Pell made a sound that in a more expressive man would have been called a laugh. In Pell it was a sort of hydraulic exhalation, brief and warm, like a stove door opening for half a second before it clanked shut again. He was sixty if he was a day, built like something the river had decided to keep rather than sink—a barrel chest, hands like knotted dock line, and a face so weathered that gulls occasionally mistook him for driftwood and tried to land on him. He'd been her deckhand for six years, which made him the longest relationship of her adult life, a fact she tried not to think about too hard because thinking about it made it feel like something that could be lost.

The *Vesper's Wake* herself was forty feet of stubborn oak and patched iron, a stern-wheeler built for cargo and converted—badly, then competently—for passengers who could be counted on one hand. Her paint was the green of river moss not because anyone had chosen it but because that was the color the last coat had faded into, and Orsen had decided years ago that fading further was cheaper than repainting. Her paddle wheel turned with a sound like a slow heartbeat heard through a wall. Her name was painted on the stern in letters that had once been gold and were now the color of old honey, and if you asked Orsen what *Vesper's Wake* meant, she would tell you it meant nothing, that she'd bought the boat off a drunk widow in Corvane Landing who couldn't remember either, and that names were like paint jobs—something you inherited and stopped asking questions about.

This was a lie. It was also none of anybody's business, which made it a good lie, the most durable kind.

The Kestrel ran two hundred miles from the snowmelt country of the Corvane Range down through the gorge lands and the farm bottoms to the sea port of Marrow Bay, and Orsen ran it end to end and back again, season after season, hauling freight and the occasional passenger and never, ever asking anyone where they were going or why. The river didn't ask. The river just carried you. That was the whole appeal of it.

She took the boat down past Marker Nine with three inches to spare on the lying sandbar—not showing off, exactly, though there was no one aboard to appreciate it except Pell, and Pell had long ago exhausted his capacity to be impressed by anything short of divine intervention—and swung wide around the drowned willow at Cooper's Bend that everyone else marked on their charts as a hazard and Orsen thought of as an acquaintance. It leaned out of the water at an angle she recognized, its roots clutching the bank like an old man refusing to leave a bar at closing time.

"You're leaning worse this year," she told it.

"Don't talk to the trees," said Pell, without heat, from below. It was an old exchange. They had perhaps four hundred exchanges total, worn smooth as river stones, and they rotated them according to some schedule only the two of them understood.

The morning settled into its rhythm. Tea, wheel, water. A kingfisher staked out the pilings at Tallow Reach. Somewhere upstream a farmer was burning stubble, and the smell came across the water sweet and sharp. Orsen felt the particular contentment that was the closest thing she let herself have to happiness: the boat answering the wheel, the river behaving exactly as badly as she'd predicted, and nobody aboard needing anything from her except competence, which she had in surplus.

Competence was the trick. That was the whole secret, if you wanted it. People didn't pry into a woman who could read a river like a ledger. They didn't ask her about her family, her history, the eleven-year-old question of why a person with her accent—which was educated, southern, the kind of accent that came with pianos in the front room—was running a mud-boat up and down the Kestrel for coin. They just handed over their freight and their fares and got where they were going faster and drier than anyone else could take them, and they left her alone. Competence was armor. Attachment was a hole below the waterline. She'd learned the difference young, and expensively, and she saw no reason to re-test the lesson.

Marrow Bay hove into view by early afternoon, all tar smoke and fish stink and the forest of masts that made the harbor look like a dead winter wood. The syndicate warehouses lined the quay in their brick bulk, flying the gray pennant of the Harbor Combine, and Orsen docked at the far end, away from their offices, out of habit and policy both. She backed the *Wake* in against the fenders with a touch so gentle that the mate on the barge next door tipped his cap, and cut the engine, and the silence that followed was the good kind—the river's hum replaced by harbor sounds, gulls and hammers and somebody's terrible accordion three piers down.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually did, with a person standing on her dock looking like they belonged there.

He was a clerk type, ink-stained, with a leather folder under his arm and the smile of a man who had been trained to smile at people whose cooperation he required. He waited until Pell had gone off to argue with the freight agent about tarpaulins, which could take hours, and then he stepped aboard with the confidence of someone who had checked first and confirmed that the captain was alone.

"Captain Ravensworth."

"No passengers," Orsen said.

"I haven't asked for—"

"You were going to." She kept wiping down the wheel with an oiled rag. It didn't need wiping. It needed her to have something to do with her hands that wasn't throwing him off the boat. "Nobody comes down the quay to my berth with a folder unless they want passage, and I don't carry passengers this run. Cargo only. You can put yourself in a crate if you like. Rates are posted."

The clerk's smile held. Credit where due; it was a well-built smile. "It's a single passenger. Northbound. As far as Coldharbour, if you go that far."

Orsen's rag paused for exactly one second, which she hated herself for, because a pause was information and she'd just given him some. Coldharbour was the mountain port at the head of navigation, the last stop before the Corvane passes, and she did go that far, twice a season, when the water allowed. Nobody asked for Coldharbour casually. Coldharbour was where you went when you were going over the mountains, or meeting someone who was, or hiding from something that hadn't thought to look that high yet.

"Rates are posted," she said again. "I'm not going north this run."

"When is your north run?"

"Whenever I say."

"Then consider this an advance booking." The folder opened. Inside was paper—good paper, the heavy laid stock that institutions used—and a sum written on it that was roughly four times what the whole run would earn her. "My principal requires discretion and speed. You come recommended on both counts."

"By whom?"

"That's rather the point of discretion."

Orsen looked at him. He looked back with the pleasant blankness of a man being paid by the hour. And she felt the old arithmetic start up in her head unbidden—that sum, divided against hull repairs she'd been putting off, against the winter stores, against the simple animal comfort of having money—and she shut the arithmetic down the way she always did, because the arithmetic was how they got you. Not with threats. With sums. Any fool could refuse a threat. It took discipline to refuse a number.

"Here's my discretion," she said. "I don't know your principal, your business, or why a man with institutional paper wants to get to Coldharbour ahead of the calving traffic, and I intend to keep not knowing. Find another boat."

"There aren't many boats willing to run the upper Kestrel this season."

"Lucky me."

"Captain." The smile finally flickered, and underneath it was something harder, and she noted it and filed it. "My principal doesn't enjoy being refused."

"Then your principal should try being less refusable." She set down the rag. "Dock's that way. Mind the gap in the planking; I've been meaning to fix it since the war and I'd hate for you to sue me over it."

He went, eventually, with the unhurried dignity of a man adding her name to a list somewhere. Orsen watched him go and told herself the small cold feeling in her stomach was relief. Refusal was habit. Habit was safe. She'd refused a hundred fares like this one—too much money, too many questions, too much interest in where she was going instead of how fast—and every refusal had cost her coin and bought her another year of being nobody in particular, which was the only thing she owned outright.

Pell came back down the quay at dusk with the tarpaulin situation resolved and a sack of oranges nobody remembered buying. He climbed aboard, looked at her face, and performed his entire emotional range, which consisted of one raised eyebrow.

"Clerk," said Orsen.

"Mm."

"Wanted passage to Coldharbour. Big money."

"Mm."

"I told him no."

"I know," said Pell. "You've got the face on."

"What face?"

"The one you get when you've done the smart thing and you're mad about it." He sat down on the cargo hatch and began peeling an orange with a knife that had probably been illegal in three countries. "There's supper on. Stew. It's mostly turnip."

"It's always mostly turnip."

"Turnip keeps." He handed her half the orange, unpeeled, which was as close as Pell came to tenderness, and looked out at the darkening harbor where the syndicate lamps were coming on one by one along the quay, gray pennants going soft in the dusk. "Weather's turning," he said. "Barometer's dropped since noon. We'll get a blow tonight."

"Good," said Orsen. "A blow stirs things up. River'll be different tomorrow."

"Different's not always better."

"Different's interesting."

Pell chewed his orange and said nothing, which from Pell was a full paragraph of disagreement, and Orsen went below to eat her mostly-turnip stew and tell herself the night was ordinary.

* * *

It was not ordinary. But she wouldn't know that until morning, when the storm had passed and the river, in its sullen generosity, gave something back.

She woke before dawn to the particular silence that follows hard weather, went up on deck with her tea, and stopped.

Something was bumping against the hull. Gently. Rhythmically. The way a hand knocks on a door.

Downstream of the berth, wedged between the *Wake*'s flank and the pilings, rode a chest. Dark wood, swollen with water, trailing weed like a drowned man's hair. And stamped into its lid, still sharp beneath the grime, was a crest she hadn't seen in eleven years and had hoped never to see again:

A mountain over crossed keys. The seal of Vhalen.

Orsen stood very still with her tea going cold, and the river lapped at the hull, and the chest knocked softly, patiently, like something that knew it had found the right door.

"Pell," she said.

From below, muffled: "If it's the tree again, I don't care."

"It's not the tree."
