## 2. What the Flood Gave Back

The storm came up the valley at dusk like a debt collector who'd finally found her address.

Orsen Ravensworth had been reading weather for eleven years on the Kestrel, and she knew this one's handwriting: the air gone thick and sweet an hour before sunset, the birds flying low and stupid, Pell's bad knee aching so badly he'd taken to sitting on his crate with both hands pressed to it like a man praying. She'd tied the *Vesper's Wake* down with four lines instead of two, doubled the fenders against the pilings at Miller's Bend, and told herself that was enough.

It nearly was.

The storm hit around midnight with a sound like the whole river being torn in half, and Orsen lay in her bunk listening to the boat groan through it — not the frightened groaning of a vessel in trouble but the deep complaining of an old woman made to sleep through a party. The *Wake* was thirty years old and had opinions about everything. Orsen had learned to tell the difference between her complaints and her cries for help, which was more than could be said for most of the men who'd owned her before.

She slept in patches. Around three she dreamed of water rising over a doorstep, and woke with her heart going hard, and lay there telling herself it was just the storm talking. It was always just the storm talking. That was the arrangement she'd made with her own head years ago: you don't ask me about before, and I won't keep you up at night.

By dawn the rain had quit. The river, though — the river was another animal entirely.

Orsen came up on deck with coffee in one hand and stopped so fast the coffee slopped over her knuckles.

The Kestrel had come up six feet in the night. The willows along Miller's Bend stood waist-deep, their green heads bobbing like swimmers. Whole trees went by — not branches, *trees*, root-balls the size of cottages, spinning slowly downstream with terrible patience. A fence post. A section of somebody's roof, shingles still attached. A drowned hen, wings spread, riding a whirlpool with what Orsen chose to read as dignity.

"Huh," said Pell, appearing beside her. He said it the way other men said *the end is nigh*.

"Flood crest'll pass by noon," Orsen said. "We sit tight till then."

"Mm." Pell was looking at something else. Orsen followed his gaze and felt her stomach do a slow, unwilling turn.

There was something against the hull.

Not driftwood. Driftwood knocked and scraped and moved along; this thing sat against the *Wake*'s starboard bow with the settled insistence of a cat that has decided whose lap it lives in now. It was dark and square-shouldered, half-submerged, and as the current pushed it gently against the planking, Orsen heard the sound it made — a low wooden knock, knock, knock, like a visitor at a door.

"Log," she said.

"Logs don't have corners," Pell said.

They watched it together for a while. The rain-swollen river heaved past them, brown and muscular, carrying away the wreckage of somebody's spring. The chest — because by now they were both admitting it was a chest — rose and fell against the hull, patient as tide.

"Could be anything in it," Pell said. "Could be dead man's clothes. Could be snakes."

"Snakes don't seal chests."

"Dead men's things bring dead men's trouble. You know that rule."

Orsen did know that rule. She'd written that rule, practically, in the years when she was learning what the river cost people who reached for floating things. A pilot who grabbed at every prize the water offered ended up overboard. It was the first lesson and the last one, and she'd paid full tuition.

"Get the boathook," she said.

Pell looked at her. Pell had a face built for looking at people — long, mournful, framed in gray hair he cut himself with a knife, so that it always looked like an argument between him and the knife that the knife had won. Right now the look said, plainly, *you know better.*

"I know better," Orsen said. "Boathook anyway."

* * *

Getting it aboard took the better part of an hour and most of Orsen's patience. The chest was heavier than it looked — waterlogged oak, iron-bound, its lid held shut by a hasp that had rusted into a single piece of metal. It left a wet ring on the deck like a stain from a glass nobody remembered drinking. Up close it smelled of river mud and old varnish and, underneath that, something faintly resinous, like a church.

And there, stamped into the lid — worn shallow, but legible once Orsen crouched and wiped the silt away with her thumb — was a crest.

A mountain split by a river. A crown above it, tilted, as if falling.

Orsen went very still.

"You see it?" Pell asked quietly.

"I see it."

"That's Vhalen."

"I know what it is."

Vhalen. The name sat in the air between them like a dropped glass nobody would pick up. Every child on the Kestrel grew up on Vhalen stories the way they grew up on flood stories — the mountain kingdom that wasn't a kingdom anymore, swallowed whole a generation back by treaty and rumor and a war nobody quite admitted had happened. Maps still printed the shape of it out of habit, the way old men still spoke of dead wives in the present tense. Half the traders who worked the upper river claimed Vhalen blood when it suited them and denied it when it didn't. The country itself had gone quiet as a sunken bell.

"What's a Vhalen chest doing in the Kestrel?" Pell asked.

"The flood brought it. Floods bring things down from everywhere. High valleys, side streams—" Orsen heard herself doing it, the thing she did when she wanted to make a problem small: explaining it into harmlessness. "Somebody's attic got wet. Somebody's grandfather kept souvenirs. It's a box, Pell."

"It's a box with a dead nation's seal on it."

"It's a *box*."

Pell said nothing. Pell's silences were more articulate than most men's speeches, and this one said, *you're arguing with me, but you're really arguing with yourself, and I've seen you do it before, and I'm going to stand here until you lose.*

Orsen straightened up and looked at the chest, and the chest looked back, if a chest can be said to look, which hers certainly seemed to.

The honest truth — the truth she kept below her waterline, where she kept most things — was that her hands wanted to open it. Not her head. Her head was issuing sensible bulletins about dead men's trouble and syndicate eyes and the wisdom of letting rivers keep their secrets. But her hands had already memorized the weight of the thing, the give of the swollen wood, the exact resistance of the rusted hasp. Her hands were traitors. They'd been traitors her whole life; it was how she'd ended up a pilot instead of a farmer's wife, how she'd ended up alone instead of sorry.

"Pry bar," she said.

"Orsen."

"Pell."

He fetched the pry bar. He was, after all these years, constitutionally incapable of refusing her anything except conversation.

* * *

The hasp gave with a crack that sounded obscenely loud in the morning hush. Inside, packed in wool gone gray and sodden, lay papers.

Dozens of them. Bundles of them, tied with ribbon that had rotted to string. Ledgers with swollen covers. Letters, sealed and unsealed, their ink feathered by damp but mostly legible. And on the topmost page, pressed flat under the weight of everything above it, a document bearing the same crest as the lid — mountain, river, falling crown — rendered this time in wax, deep red, intact.

Orsen didn't touch it. Not right away. She stood over the open chest with the pry bar still in her hand and felt something in her chest do a thing she didn't have a word for. It wasn't greed. She'd felt greed before, at the dock, over cargo shares, and it had a hot quick flavor. This was slower. Colder. This was the feeling of standing at the edge of deep water and knowing, in your body, before your mind agrees, that you are going in.

"Throw it back," Pell said.

She glanced at him. "You want to throw it back?"

"No," Pell admitted. "That's why I want *you* to throw it back. One of us has to be the sensible one, and I've seen your sensible one, and she's asleep."

Despite herself — despite the chest, the seal, the cold slow pull of it — Orsen snorted. Eleven years, and Pell could still get a laugh out of her at the worst possible moment. It was one of maybe four things she liked about him, and she'd never told him which four.

"Look at it," she said. "Ledgers. Correspondence. That's not treasure, that's *paperwork*. Nobody kills anybody over paperwork."

"People have started wars over less than that wax stamp."

"People start wars over nothing. That's what wars are *for*." She crouched, finally, and lifted the top bundle free. The paper was good stock — heavy, expensive, the kind bought by chancelleries and banks, not farmhouses. The handwriting on the first letter was small and precise, and even though it was written in Vhalen script, which she couldn't read, she could read the pressure of the pen, the confidence of it. Someone important had written this. Several someones important, many times, about something that mattered enough to seal and ship and sink.

Or sink partway, and float eleven years, and choose her boat.

Because that was the thought arriving now, unwelcome as the storm: of every hull on every bend of the Kestrel, the river had set this thing against *hers*. Rivers didn't choose. Rivers didn't mean anything. She'd built her whole life on the certainty that rivers didn't mean anything — that meaning was a story people told afterward to make their luck feel like fate. And yet.

"Could be worth money," she said, and hated the sound of it coming out of her mouth, because it was greed dressed in work clothes, and Pell would recognize the outfit.

"Could be worth a hanging," Pell said. "You know who works this river now. Harbor syndicate's got men at every landing from Corvane Ford to the sea. Anything old and Vhalen comes up, they hear about it inside a week. Then it's not a chest anymore, it's a *debt*, and we're the ones holding it."

"So we sell it first."

"To who? The kind of buyer who pays fair for a dead country's secrets?" Pell shook his head slowly. "Orsen. Put it back in the water. Let the river finish the job. Some things come up so they can go back down."

Orsen looked at the papers. At the red seal, waxy and perfect, holding its shape after a generation underwater the way certain stubborn things do. She thought about the dream from last night — water over a doorstep — and pushed it down where she pushed everything.

Here was the moment. She would understand that later, in the way you understand the exact plank that broke. Here was the moment when the sensible thing was still cheap. Toss the chest over the rail, watch the river take it, spend an hour scrubbing the mud ring off her deck, and by supper it would be a story — *remember the flood, remember the crazy box* — and by midsummer it wouldn't even be that.

Her hands were on the rail. She actually had it half-lifted, the weight of it swinging out over the brown water, Pell watching with something that was almost relief.

And then the wind turned the top page.

Just that. A breath of morning breeze off the floodwater, lifting the loose corner of the topmost letter, turning it over with a dry whisper — the only dry sound in a soaked world. And on the back of the page, in a different hand, in plain trade-common letters anyone on the Kestrel could read, someone had written a single line:

*If this reaches no one, then no one is left. If it reaches one, tell them we were here.*

Orsen stood there a long time.

The river went by, hauling its wreckage south. The hen, or a hen very like it, circled twice in an eddy and gave up. Somewhere upstream a tree struck the pilings of the old ferry crossing and cracked like a rifle shot, and Pell flinched, and Orsen didn't.

"We should throw it back," she said.

"Yes," said Pell.

"We should."

"Yes."

Neither of them moved. The chest sat on the deck between them, dripping, patient, smelling of churches and mud, and Orsen Ravensworth — who had spent eleven years learning exactly how much of herself to give away and to whom, who had refused passengers and favors and friendships with the smooth efficiency of a lock turning — stood holding a dead country's mail and could not make her arms let go.

"Below decks," she said finally, gruffly, as if the decision had been practical all along. "Before the deck boards swell. Wrap it in the spare canvas."

Pell exhaled through his nose — the closest he came to argument — and bent to help her lift.

They got it down the companionway and stowed it in the hold behind the spare line, under an oilcloth, wedged where it couldn't shift. Orsen told herself it was temporary. A day, two days, until she figured out the smart play. She was good at figuring out smart plays. It was practically her trade.

But that night, making her rounds with the lantern, she found herself stopping at the hold hatch and standing there longer than any round required, listening to the river settle, thinking about a hand small and precise enough to write *tell them we were here*, and wondering — with a curiosity that felt less like a crack in a wall than like water finding one — who on this river was left to tell.
