## 3. A Girl With a Dead Country's Eyes

Tamsin had learned three things about following people in her fourteen years, and the first was that you didn't follow them. You arrived where they were going and pretended you'd always been there.

The second was that hunger made you stupid, so you ate before the hunt, even if what you ate was half a heel of bread stolen from a baker's cooling rack and a fistful of river mussels boiled in a dented tin cup over somebody else's abandoned fire. The third was that everyone, no matter how careful, told you exactly who they were if you watched their hands instead of their faces.

The pilot of the *Vesper's Wake* had hands that never stopped moving. Tamsin had watched her for two days now—watched her check lines she'd already checked, run her thumb along the rail like she was taking its pulse, count coins twice at the chandler's and then count them a third time just to be sure the world hadn't cheated her while she blinked. A woman who trusted nothing to stay put. A woman, Tamsin thought, with something worth losing.

That was the kind of person you could work with.

The tavern was called the Drowned Gull, which Tamsin found either very honest or very stupid, depending on how much you loved the river. It sat on pilings above the mudflats at Marrow's Bend, and it smelled of tar, fried fish, and the particular sourness of men who had argued about the same things for thirty years. She slipped in through the kitchen door because front doors were for people with money, and she stood in the shadow of the flour barrels until she found her mark.

Orsen Ravensworth sat alone at a corner table with her back to the wall—not facing it, Tamsin noted approvingly, *angled* toward it, so she could watch both the room and the door—and a bowl of stew in front of her that she was eating slowly, like someone who had learned that fast eating invited company, and company invited questions.

Tamsin waited until the woman had taken a mouthful. There was an art to interrupting someone mid-bite; they swallowed rather than speak, and the swallow bought you three seconds of silence in which you were already talking.

She crossed the room like she owned it. Chin up. Shoulders back. She had practiced this walk against the reflection of a flooded quarry, and the quarry had said she looked ridiculous, but the quarry was a liar.

"You have my chest," she said.

Orsen Ravensworth did not choke. Tamsin gave her points for that. She simply finished chewing, set down her spoon with the deliberate care of a woman putting down a weapon she didn't need yet, and looked up.

Tamsin had prepared herself for a lot of faces. What she hadn't prepared for was the eyes. They were gray, river-gray, the color of water over stone, and they went through her the way a current goes through a wading bird—quick, thorough, and utterly without malice, cataloguing everything: the patched coat, the cracked boots, the hollows under her cheekbones, the knife at her belt that was more threat than tool because its edge had been sharpened down to a sliver.

"Sit down," Orsen said, "before someone trips over your courage."

"I'll stand."

"You'll stand somewhere else, then. That table's mine." She nodded at the bench across from her. "This one's empty. Your choice."

It was not, Tamsin understood, really a choice. She sat. Sitting meant she wanted something, and wanting something put you below the table, but she'd been below tables her whole life and knew all the ways out from under them.

"The chest," she said again. "The one the flood left against your hull. It has the Vhalen seal on it. It's mine."

"Is it." Orsen picked up her spoon. Took another bite. Made Tamsin wait through an entire, agonizing mouthful of stew. "Funny thing about property," she said. "Usually when it's yours, you know what's inside it."

"I know what's inside it."

"Do you." Another bite. This woman, Tamsin decided, should be outlawed. "Because I opened it. Carefully. Sealed things rot from the inside when they sit in a river for a generation, and I wasn't going to haul a box of soup around on my deck." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "There are papers in there. Documents. And one of them has a name written on it that I'd bet my boat you can't say."

Tamsin's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. She had known—or she had believed she knew—but belief and knowledge were different animals, and one of them had teeth.

"Say it, then," she said, and hated how young her voice came out.

"No." Orsen leaned back, arms crossing. "Here's what I think. I think you saw the crest on that chest—I'd wager there's not ten people on this whole river who'd recognize it, which means you're one of the ten, which means you're Vhaleni or you're a thief who does her research. And I think you tracked me here through two days of rain because you want what's in that box badly enough to be brave about it. All of which is fine. Bravery's cheap. But you don't get to stand in my light and claim things with a hungry look and no proof."

"I don't need proof. It's my birthright."

"There it is." Something flickered across the pilot's face—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, the way a fish ghosts under the surface. "Birthright. You know how many people come down off the hills claiming birthrights? Half of them are lying and the other half are worse off for telling the truth."

"And which half are you?"

The question came out before Tamsin could stop it, sharp as a fishhook, and she watched it land. Watched the gray eyes go still and flat, the way water goes still right before something rises under it.

"Neither," Orsen said quietly. "I'm the half that doesn't claim anything."

* * *

They stared at each other across the stew. Around them the tavern roared on—a card game turning ugly by the window, the kitchen bell clanging, some old man near the hearth insisting to anyone who'd listen that the spring flood of '61 had been higher than this one and he had the waterline scratched into his doorpost to prove it.

Tamsin made herself breathe. She had rehearsed this conversation eleven times on the road from Corvane Landing, and in nine of the rehearsals she had been magnificent. In the other two she had cried, which she had sworn not to do, and so far she was winning that particular battle, though only barely, and only because crying took moisture and moisture was food and she had budgeted both.

"My mother's name was Sera Vael," she said. "She came down out of the mountains the winter I was six. She never talked about Vhalen except once, when she was fevered, and she said a name over and over." Tamsin kept her eyes level. Kept her hands flat on the table where the pilot could see them, because hands told truths and hers were going to tell this one. "The name was Aldous Vael. And if you open that chest—if you actually read those papers instead of just counting them—you'll find his name, because he was my grandfather, and the seal on that chest is the seal of his office."

All of which was true. Every word of it true, which was the best way to lie: build the lie a house made entirely of true rooms, and let the visitor assume the whole building was sound.

What she did not say—what she would not say, not to this stranger with the counting hands and the armor made of competence—was that Aldous Vael had not merely held an office. That the papers in that chest were not merely heirlooms. That somewhere in that waterlogged bundle was a page that named names, and one of the names, if her mother's fever-words and three years of listening in taverns and ferry offices and customs sheds had taught her to read the pattern right, belonged to a man still living, still respected, still wearing a surveyor's license like a clean shirt over a rotten wound.

And that Tamsin had not come for her birthright at all.

She had come for justice, or vengeance, or whatever you called it when you were too small and too poor for either and had to make do with stubbornness.

Orsen Ravensworth studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she pushed the bowl of stew across the table.

"Eat," she said. "You've got the look of someone arguing with a meal and losing."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're starving. I know the difference. Hungry people talk about food. Starving people pretend they aren't thinking about it every four seconds." The gray eyes held hers, and there was something in them now that hadn't been there before—something that wasn't pity, thank every drowned god, because Tamsin would have walked out over pity, but recognition. Terrible, unwelcome recognition, like catching your own face in a dark window. "Eat. Then we'll talk about your chest."

Tamsin ate. It was the hardest thing she'd done all week, harder than the mussels, harder than the walking, because accepting food from a stranger was a debt and debts were chains, and she had sworn on her mother's grave—which was a cairn of seven stones on a hillside above a burned valley—that she would owe nobody ever again.

But the stew was hot and thick with barley, and her body overruled her pride with the ruthless efficiency of a body that had been running on willpower and river water for too long.

When the bowl was empty, Orsen spoke again, and her voice had changed. Lower. Almost gentle, in the way that a river is almost gentle right up until it isn't.

"Who else knows you're looking?"

"No one."

"Liar," Orsen said, without heat, without accusation, just stating it the way she might state the depth of a sandbar. "You didn't find me by accident. Someone pointed you at Marrow's Bend. Someone told you a pilot named Ravensworth pulled a sealed chest out of the flood. Which means someone's watching me, which means whoever they are, they know what's in that box too, or they wouldn't care who comes asking." She stood, dropping coins on the table—counted twice, Tamsin noticed, even now. "I'm going to my boat. You're going to finish being mysterious somewhere that isn't next to me. If you're still on my dock at dawn, we'll see whether you're worth the trouble."

"And if I am?"

Orsen paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the gray morning light, and for one unguarded moment Tamsin saw past the armor—the tiredness underneath it, the years of it, a woman who had been holding the world at arm's length so long her arms must ache.

"Then God help us both," she said, and was gone into the rain.

Tamsin sat alone at the table, in the warmth of the Drowned Gull, with a full belly and a plan full of holes and a name burning behind her teeth like a coal she couldn't spit out.

Dawn was eight hours away. She intended to be on that dock in seven.

But first—she touched the folded scrap of paper hidden inside her coat, the one she'd carried for three years, the one with a name copied out in her mother's shaking hand—first she had one more lie to get exactly right.

Outside, the river rose against the pilings, patient as everything else that had come out of Vhalen, and waited.
