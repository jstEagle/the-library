## 4. Buyers at the Dock

The morning the syndicate came calling, Orsen Ravensworth was elbow-deep in the Vesper's Wake's bilge, cursing a pump gasket with the fluency of a woman who had learned every swear word on three rivers and invented several of her own.

"You've got visitors," Pell said from above, his voice pitched in that particular flat way he had that meant *visitors you won't like*.

"Tell them I'm dead."

"Third time this month. They stop believing it around the second week."

Orsen sighed, wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands, and climbed the ladder into daylight she immediately regretted leaving. The Kestrel was running high and green with snowmelt, slapping against the hull in a rhythm she found more honest than most conversation. Tied alongside at the dock, her boat looked exactly as she liked her to look: unremarkable, work-worn, the kind of vessel a man's eye slid over without catching.

Three men stood on the planking. Two were dockside muscle — broad through the shoulders, narrow through the imagination, wearing matching expressions of professional patience. The third was different. He was small and neat as an accountant's ledger, with a gray coat brushed free of harbor dust and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face did, like he'd sent it ahead to scout the terrain.

"Mistress Ravensworth." He inclined his head. "Merrick Voss. Harbor Syndicate, Contracts and Acquisitions."

"Contracts and Acquisitions," Orsen repeated. "That's a long way of saying you buy things."

"And occasionally," Voss said pleasantly, "things become available."

Behind him, at the far end of the dock, Tamsin sat on a bollard pretending to eat a heel of bread but actually watching everything with those dark eyes of hers — eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. She'd been sleeping aboard for two nights now, curled in the cargo hold like a cat who had decided the house was hers and simply hadn't informed the owner yet. Orsen hadn't formally agreed to anything. She kept telling herself that. She kept not quite believing it.

"I don't have anything available," Orsen said. "I have a leaky pump and a debt to the chandler."

"The chest."

And there it was. Orsen felt the word land in her stomach like a dropped anchor. She kept her face still — eleven years of river poker had seen to that — but inside, something cold began doing arithmetic.

"Don't know what you mean," she said.

Voss's smile widened by one tooth. "Mistress Ravensworth. Two nights ago, a storm came down the valley and rearranged the river's furniture. Yesterday morning, you pulled a sealed chest out of the driftwood against your hull. You carried it aboard under a tarp. You've been seen since at the chandler's buying wax and at the ropewalk buying cord, which is what careful people buy when they intend to lash something valuable down where it won't shift." He spread his neat hands. "I don't need to know what's in it. I only need to know that you have it, and that the Syndicate pays better than anyone else on this river for things that wash up out of old countries."

*Pell,* Orsen thought, *you useless wonderful man.* He'd wanted to throw the thing back the moment they'd hauled it dripping over the rail — waterlogged oak, iron-banded, stamped with a crest neither of them had recognized until Tamsin had gone white as river foam looking at it. The crest of Vhalen. A nation drowned under treaty and rumor before Tamsin was old enough to remember it standing. Pell had said, in his flattest voice, *Rivers give back what they want sunk. Best habit is agreeing with them.*

She should have agreed with him. She'd known it then. She'd hesitated anyway — just a moment too long, just long enough for curiosity to get its fingers under the lid of her self-preservation — and now here was a smiling accountant on her dock pricing her hesitation.

"Even if such a chest existed," Orsen said slowly, "why would I sell it to you? I haven't heard a number yet."

"Ah, now we're speaking the same language." Voss produced a purse from his coat. It made a sound Orsen knew intimately: the heavy, muffled clink of real coin, not the tinny rattle of river-town promises. "Two hundred crowns. For the object, unopened, no questions asked, no records kept. That is four times what this vessel would fetch at auction, Mistress Ravensworth, and I say that with respect, because I've seen her auction listing."

Beside her, Pell made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been *take the money, you madwoman*. Orsen ignored him. Two hundred crowns was a year of good runs. Two hundred crowns was the chandler paid off, the pump rebuilt, the hull recaulked stem to stern, and a winter spent somewhere warm with no debts and no name worth tracing.

Two hundred crowns was also, some quiet voice observed, the price of letting the Syndicate decide what happened to whatever — whoever — that chest belonged to.

Her eyes went, despite herself, to the bollard. Tamsin had stopped eating. She was watching Orsen with an expression Orsen recognized because she'd worn it herself once, years ago, on a dock much like this one, waiting to see whether the world was going to hand her over or let her keep walking. It was the look of someone calculating, with terrible precision, how much they were worth to the people around them.

Orsen hated that look. She especially hated seeing it aimed at her.

"Here's my problem, Master Voss," she said. "You said 'no questions asked.' But you knew about the chest within a day. You knew what I bought at the chandler's. Which means either your people watch this dock closer than the harbor master does, or somebody talked." She shrugged. "Either way, if I sell to you, the next visitor won't be so polite. And the one after that will be less polite still. A chest like that doesn't make me rich. It makes me a waypoint on other people's maps. I've spent eleven years staying off maps."

Something flickered behind Voss's pleasant expression — respect, maybe, or its close cousin, disappointment. "A sensible woman."

"I'm told it's my worst quality."

"Then let me be equally direct." The smile stayed; the warmth drained out from under it, the way sun leaves a deck when a cloud crosses. "The Syndicate has asked. Politely. If asking fails, the request becomes an offer, and if the offer fails—" he gestured gently at the river, at the town, at the whole wide world of flammable and sinkable things "—well. Boats are fragile things. So are reputations. So are people who sleep aboard them."

There it was. Not even dressed up. Orsen felt her jaw tighten and made herself loosen it, because men like Voss collected reactions the way she collected sandbar charts.

"So that's the shape of it," she said. "Sell it to you and hope you're the last hungry mouth on the river, or keep it and wait for my hull to develop opinions."

"I'd frame it more generously, but yes."

"Well." Orsen looked past him, upriver, north, toward the gray smudge of mountains at the edge of sight where the Kestrel narrowed into gorges and the charts went thin and hopeful. Toward Coldharbour, the mountain port, three weeks' hard steaming upstream if the water cooperated and longer if it didn't. "Then I'll tell you what I'm going to do, and you can carry the news back to whoever writes your contracts."

Voss waited.

"I'm taking it north myself. To Coldharbour. There are buyers there who pay in imperial scrip and don't burn boats they might still want to charter." It was a lie. She had no buyer in Coldharbour, no plan beyond *away*, but lies delivered with confidence were just navigation by instruments nobody else could read. "So the Syndicate can either wish me good luck, or explain to your superiors why their acquisition sank in a gorge with two witnesses who saw your men do it. Gorges talk, Master Voss. Echoes carry names a long way."

For a long moment the only sound was the river slapping the hull and, distantly, a gull arguing with another gull about something neither would ever concede.

Then Voss laughed — a short, genuine bark of a laugh, quickly folded away again. "You know what I admire, Mistress Ravensworth? A woman who understands that the best threat is one you never have to collect on." He tucked the purse away. "I'll report that the item is mobile and the seller unreasonable. Both accurate. But I'll tell you freely, as one professional to another: you're being watched now. Every lock from here to the headwaters. If I were hauling something precious north—" he tipped an imaginary hat "—I'd want extra hands aboard. Hands I trusted. Hands that came cheap."

He turned, and his two shoulders turned with him, and the three of them walked back up the dock into the morning crowd like ink dispersing in water.

* * *

"Well," Pell said, after a suitable interval. "That went well."

"That went terribly."

"Aye. Well." He handed her the rag she'd abandoned, which was somehow both an act of kindness and an accusation. "We throwing it back now?"

Orsen looked at the hold hatch. Under the tarp, under the cord and wax, the chest sat with its drowned crest and its secrets, patient as only objects can be. Patient as only trouble can be.

"No," she said, and heard herself say it, and wondered briefly who had taken over her mouth.

"No," Pell repeated. "Right. Because the smart move was so obviously boring."

"Pell—"

"I'm not saying throw it back. I'm saying I *said* throw it back, and history should record that I said it." He leaned on the rail beside her, squinting upriver. "Voss wasn't bluffing about the watching. There was a skiff off the point this morning, sitting on its oars. Same skiff was there yesterday. Somebody's counting our smoke."

"I know."

"And you told him Coldharbour. Out loud. To a Syndicate man."

"Because he already knew I wasn't selling, and a man who thinks he knows your course stops looking for it." She said it with more certainty than she felt. "Besides. North was always the play. Coldharbour's got archivists, brokers, people who deal in old-country paper without asking whose blood dried on it. We sell it there, split it, done. Three weeks. In and out."

"Three weeks," Pell said, in the tone of a man reading a sandbar and not liking its face. "With the girl."

Orsen didn't answer immediately. Down the dock, Tamsin had risen from her bollard and was approaching with the wary, deliberate stride of someone who had heard every word — of course she had, the dock carried voices like a drumhead — and had decided to walk into the conversation rather than pretend otherwise. Orsen was beginning to suspect that pretense, for Tamsin, was a tool used only when nothing sharper was at hand.

"You're taking me north," Tamsin said. Not a question. She'd heard.

"I'm taking the *chest* north," Orsen corrected. "You happen to be attached to it. Like barnacles."

"Barnacles don't pay."

That startled a laugh out of Orsen before she could stop it — a short, rusty sound, like a winch unused all winter. Pell raised his eyebrows at her in mild alarm, the way he did whenever she produced an emotion in public.

"Fine," Orsen said. "Terms. You ride to Coldharbour, you eat from my galley, you pull your weight — lines, scrubbing, whatever Pell puts you to. When we arrive, we find a broker together, because frankly, girl, you know what's in that chest better than I do and I want your knowledge at the table, not lurking in my hold. We sell. We split. My share covers the run and the risk. Yours buys you whatever future you think a dead country's signature can purchase." She held out a hand. "That's the price. Take it or swim."

Tamsin looked at the hand. Then up at Orsen, and for a moment something moved behind those dark eyes — something that wasn't calculation at all, something younger and hungrier and more frightened, quickly buried like a body in a shallow grave.

"Fifty-fifty," Tamsin said.

"Forty-sixty, my favor. I own the boat."

"Sixty-forty, mine. It's my birthright."

"It's my *hull* it washed against, and my neck it's currently costing. Fifty-fifty, and I don't charge you for the bread."

Tamsin's mouth twitched. She spat in her palm — mountain manners, Orsen noted, not river — and clasped. Her grip was stronger than her wrists suggested, and dry as paper.

"Fifty-fifty," Tamsin agreed. "And when we're done, you take me wherever I ask next."

"One destination. Within reason."

"Define reason."

"Anywhere I'd go anyway."

They glared at each other, and Orsen had the distinct, unsettling feeling of having just lost a negotiation she'd thought she'd won.

* * *

They cast off on the afternoon tide, the Vesper's Wake turning her blunt bow north into the current with the groaning reluctance of an old woman asked to climb stairs. The town fell away astern — the chandlery, the ropewalk, the tavern where Orsen had refused a passenger a lifetime ago, which was to say five days. On the point, the skiff was gone. Orsen didn't trust its absence any further than she could throw it.

Pell took the wheel while she stood at the rail, charting the first sandbars of the upper reach by eye, reading the water's faces the way she always did: the smooth lie of deep channel, the wrinkled frown of a bar shifting, the nervous ripple where something waited under the green. Behind her she heard Tamsin asking Pell questions — about the ropes, the blocks, the wheel — and Pell answering in his spare, grudging syllables, which was, Orsen knew, his version of enthusiasm. By evening he'd have her splicing. By midweek he'd have her believing she was crew.

*Not crew,* Orsen corrected herself, watching the mountains grow by inches. *Passengers. Cargo with opinions. This is a delivery run.*

But the chest sat in her hold like a second heartbeat, and somewhere behind them a skiff was surely finding its oars again, and ahead lay three weeks of narrowing gorges and widening trouble, and when she caught her reflection in the black water alongside — brief, broken, reforming with each slap of the current — she noticed she was almost smiling.

She stopped immediately.

Some habits, she told the river, were load-bearing.

The river, being a river, declined to promise anything.
