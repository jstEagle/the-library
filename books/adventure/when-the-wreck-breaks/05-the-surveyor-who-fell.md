## 5. The Surveyor Who Fell

Davor Hale had been waiting on the Coldharbour road for three days when he finally understood that the river was not going to come to him.

This was, he reflected, entirely in keeping with his luck. Rivers came to other people. Rivers came to men who had not spent eleven years being politely uninvited from every survey office, garrison mess, and respectable tavern between the coast and the Corvane foothills. Rivers came to men whose names did not appear, in certain circles, followed by the word *traitor* in a hand so neat it might have been engraved.

He sat on his one good trunk—his only trunk, if he was honest, the second having been sold in Kestrelmouth to pay for the first winter of his disgrace—and watched the water slide past below the levee grass, brown and patient and utterly indifferent to him.

"You'll catch your death sitting in that draft," he told himself, because there was no one else to tell, and because his mother had said it every morning of his childhood and it seemed a shame to let the habit die with her.

The Vesper's Wake came upriver an hour past noon, riding low and mean, her paint gone the color of old pewter, her smokestack trailing a plume like a question mark. Hale had seen her twice before from a distance—the famous Orsen Ravensworth, who took no passengers, carried no letters, answered no hail, and had once, according to a lock-keeper at Tarn's Bend, thrown a customs man into the river for asking about her cargo. The story was probably embellished. Most stories about women who wanted to be left alone were.

But she was the only boat running north this week, and the pass would open soon, and Hale had run out of weeks.

He stood up. His knees objected; they had been objecting since Gray Landing, with increasing eloquence. He brushed off his coat—the good coat, the last coat, dove-gray once and now the gray of things that have been rained on and dried too many times—and arranged his face into what he hoped was dignity.

What he actually managed, he suspected, was something closer to a man begging at a church door who has forgotten which pocket holds the coin.

* * *

The Wake came alongside the landing stage with a grace that surprised him. He had expected barge-handling; he got surgery. The hull kissed the pilings soft as a closed book, and there at the wheel stood Orsen Ravensworth herself—tall, weathered to the color of her own deck, with eyes that went through him like a sounding line through shallow water and found nothing worth anchoring in.

"Passage north," Hale said. "As far as the mountain port."

"No passengers."

"I can pay."

"Still no passengers." She was already turning back to her wheel.

"Miss Ravensworth." He heard his own voice crack on the name and hated it. "Please. I know the Corvane passes. All of them. I mapped them—I mapped them *first*, before any chart you've ever bought or stolen was a rumor. I can read ice, I can read snowfall, I can tell you where the glacier will calve and when the window closes, and I am telling you now: it closes early this year. There's a storm system coming down out of the northeast that every pilot on this river should be worried about, and none of them are, because none of them look up."

That stopped her. Not much—a half-turn, the way a cat stops when it hears a mouse but hasn't decided whether the mouse is worth the trouble—but enough.

Behind her, at the rail, a big quiet man with forearms like mooring cable paused in coiling a line to watch. And at the rail's end sat a girl, thin as a whittled stick, with dark eyes that had gone very still and very bright.

Hale knew those eyes. He had seen them across a treaty table twenty years ago, on the faces of the Vhaleni delegation, the day before everything ended. A dead country looking out at him through a living girl.

His stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

"The mountain port," Orsen said. "Not the pass."

"Not the pass," he agreed quickly. Too quickly. He saw her register it.

"And why does a man who knows the passes need a *boat* to get to them?"

"Because," Hale said, and here was the moment, the one he had rehearsed on the levee grass for three days, the speech about credentials and fair payment and mutual advantage—and what came out instead, God help him, was the truth—"because I have been walking away from something for eleven years and my legs are tired, and yours is the only boat going where I need to be seen arriving."

Silence. The river slapped the pilings. Somewhere upstream a loon made its idiot beautiful noise.

"That," said Orsen Ravensworth, "is the worst sales pitch I have ever heard."

"Yes," Hale admitted.

"It doesn't even make sense. You want to be *seen*? By whom?"

"By whoever is still angry enough to look."

Something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of one survivor taking the measure of another. He had seen it before, in mirrors, and never liked it there either.

"Deckhand," she said without turning. "Opinion?"

Pell—the big man—considered Hale with the unhurried attention of a man judging timber. "He's lying about something," Pell said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "But he believes what he's saying. Mostly."

"That's the most honest assessment anyone's given me in years," Hale said.

"He talks a lot," Pell added, to Orsen, as though Hale were weather.

"He does," Orsen agreed. She looked at Hale for a long moment, and he watched her do the arithmetic—passage against payment, risk against knowledge, the chest he could see lashed under canvas amidships against whatever it was he represented. He did not yet know about the chest. He would learn. Everyone learned about the chest eventually; it was the kind of object that arranged itself at the center of any room it entered.

"Forty crowns," she said. "You sleep on deck. You eat what we eat. You touch nothing that isn't yours, and if anything on this boat goes wrong because of you, I put you off at the next landing, wherever it is, whatever the weather. Are these terms clear?"

"Perfectly."

"And you'll tell me the truth when I ask for it. Not all of it. Just the part I ask for."

Hale hesitated. It was a strange bargain—truth by the inch, metered out like rope—and some tired honest corner of him wanted to refuse it, wanted to say *you shall have all of it, the whole rotten ledger*, the way penitents always imagine confession will feel before they've actually tried it.

"The part you ask for," he said. "On my honor."

She snorted—an inelegant, genuine sound—and turned to her wheel. "Pell, take his money and his trunk. We cast off in ten minutes."

* * *

The first day upstream taught Hale that he had made a serious miscalculation about rivers.

He had thought of them as roads. Roads were his trade—passes, trails, the grammar of terrain—and roads, however cruel, were legible. A road told you what it was. A river lied constantly, cheerfully, and with tremendous force. The Kestrel narrowed into gorges where the current ran backward along the banks and forward in the middle, where whirlpools opened and shut like breathing things, where Orsen Ravensworth stood at her wheel reading the water the way he read contour lines—except that his lines stayed put, and hers moved.

"Sandbar ahead, port side, under the cutbank," she said at one point, to no one, forty seconds before a sandbar became visible to anyone else. Hale watched it emerge from the murk exactly where she'd said, exactly shaped as she'd described—"like a sleeping dog, long snout downstream"—and felt something he hadn't felt in years: professional awe, clean and uncomplicated.

"You read faces," he said, when he'd worked up the courage to stand near the wheelhouse without touching anything.

"Hm."

"The bars. You said once—they're like faces."

"Everything's like a face if you look long enough." Her eyes never left the water. "River's just a lot of faces that change their minds."

"I mapped the Corvane Range for nine years," Hale offered. "I never once trusted a map I made. Terrain shifts slower than water, but it shifts. Every surveyor knows his charts are obituaries written in advance."

Now she glanced at him. Briefly. "That's the first sensible thing you've said."

"I'm capable of others. They tend to arrive late."

A sound escaped her that in a more generous woman might have been called a laugh. In Orsen Ravensworth it was more of a structural event—a single exhalation, quickly suppressed, like a leak patched mid-voyage. Hale counted it a triumph and pretended he hadn't noticed it, which he understood was the price of ever hearing another.

* * *

It was the girl who undid him.

Tamsin. That was the name Pell used, in the few words Pell spent per day. She kept to the bow, mostly, practicing knots with a length of line the deckhand had grudgingly lent her—grudgingly, Hale gathered, in the way of a man who pretends not to care while leaving the coil deliberately within reach. She learned fast. Mountain-born, Hale guessed, watching her hands: she handled rope like someone who'd grown up with goats and gantries, not streets.

And she watched him. All day. With those eyes.

On the second evening, moored in a gorge where the walls rose so sheer the stars came down a slot like coins into a well, she finally approached him. She sat on the hatch cover opposite, unwinding and rewinding her knot practice, and said:

"You knew Vhalen."

It wasn't a question, and he was too tired and too old to pretend otherwise. "I surveyed its northern border. Three seasons. Before."

"Before," she repeated, tasting the word.

"Tamsin." He set down his cup. "Whatever you think I am—whatever you've been told—"

"I don't think anything. Yet." She tied off a bowline without looking at it, which annoyed him professionally. "Yet. But I've got time, and you've got a face like a man doing sums he doesn't like. So start talking, surveyor. What were the passes like?"

And that—that—he could answer honestly. That he could give her whole.

"They were the most beautiful country I ever walked," he said, and heard his voice go soft and strange. "The high valley especially. South-facing slopes, terraced orchards gone wild since the old wars, stone bridges built by people who expected to be building for a thousand years. The light in autumn came down the valley like honey poured slow. I mapped every trail, every spring, every defensible ridge. I wrote reports. I drew the maps. And then—" His hands had started doing something without his permission, turning his cup, turning it. "Then the imperial army came through the Serpent's Tooth pass in November. Through snow I had certified impassable after the first week of the month. Because I had certified it. My maps, miss. My measurements. They marched four thousand men through a door I opened."

"The treaty said the passes were closed," Tamsin said quietly.

"The treaty said a great many things." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "You know what the worst of it was? I was decorated. Commendation for cartographic excellence. There's a plaque somewhere in the Surveyor-General's hall with my name on it, and beneath my name, in smaller letters, the number of days saved by the route. Days. As if it were a delivery schedule. Four thousand men, and a nation, and they printed the *days*."

The gorge was silent except for the river muttering to itself in the dark below.

"My father died in the fall of Vhalen," Tamsin said.

Hale looked up. She wasn't looking at him; she was looking at her hands, at the rope.

"I don't remember him. I was small. But everyone says I have his eyes, and his eyes came from a country that doesn't exist anymore, so I suppose I have its eyes too." She stood, coiling her line with a snap of finality. "I'm not forgiving you, surveyor. I just wanted to hear you say it out loud. Some people can't."

"I can say it," Hale said. "Saying it is the one thing I can still do."

She paused at the rail. For a moment he thought she might say something else—something that might have been mercy, or its opposite. Instead she said, "Your knot work is terrible, by the way. If we're crossing ice, you'll want to fix that," and was gone aft to where Pell was pretending not to teach her splice work by lamplight.

Hale sat alone in the gorge-dark with his cold cup and found, to his considerable surprise, that he felt lighter. Not forgiven—nothing so comfortable. But *heard*. It was astonishing how heavy a secret became when nobody asked you to carry it aloud, and how much lighter, even unburied, even bleeding, once it lay open in the air.

Guilt, he decided, was like ballast. Useless unless you could convert it to something that moved you forward.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the storm system arrived early.

He felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the light on the eastern peaks, the particular hush that mountains produce when they're about to say something. He climbed to the wheelhouse uninvited, which he had learned was tolerated, and stood beside Orsen looking north.

"There," he said, pointing to a smudge above the farthest white shoulder. "That's your window closing. Cirrus off the range, moving south-southwest, thickening from the top down. Three days, maybe four, before it reaches the high passes. After that, the glacier route shuts for the season—shuts *hard*, avalanche season, whole corridors gone."

Orsen studied the sky with the same attention she gave her sandbars. "You're sure."

"I have been wrong about many things, Miss Ravensworth. Weather is not among them. It's the only mistress I've kept faithfully."

"Don't call me that."

"Ravensworth, then."

"Don't call me that either." But there was no edge in it; there was almost, almost, humor. She turned the wheel a degree. "If we make the mountain port tomorrow night—"

"We won't, at this pace. Not with the low water at Gray Landing. You'll ground on the Graveline shoals if the river's dropped another hand-span, and it has; I can see it on your banks."

Her jaw tightened. He had just told a river pilot about her own river, and they both knew it, and both understood that the knowing changed something between them permanently.

"Gray Landing," she said at last, "is a mudflat with ambitions."

"Gray Landing," Hale agreed, "is also where I saw syndicate riders two days ago, asking after a boat matching this one's description. Which is why I mention the shoals. If they're waiting there—"

He stopped. Not because he wanted to. Because Orsen Ravensworth had gone very still, in the way of deep water, and he understood that he had just handed her a piece of information that rearranged her entire map.

Behind them, unnoticed until now, Tamsin stood at the wheelhouse door with the wind pulling her hair sideways, and her dead country's eyes had gone bright as signal fires.

"Then we'd better not ground," she said.
