## 6. Crew of Convenience

The Kestrel narrowed north of Marrow Bend the way a river does when it stops being polite about it. The banks drew in close enough that Orsen could have thrown a biscuit to either shore, and the current turned from a suggestion into an argument. She stood at the wheel with her boots planted wide and her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was enjoying herself and would deny it under torture.

"Starboard quarter," Pell said from beside her, not looking up from the rope he was coiling.

"I see it."

"You always say you see it."

"And I always do."

"One day you won't."

"One day I'll die," Orsen said. "Until then, I see it." She eased the Vesper's Wake left, and the boat leaned into the turn like a woman who'd been doing this long enough to have opinions about it. The gorge walls rose on either side, gray stone streaked with rust-colored water stains, close enough in places that the paddlewheel's wash came back at them as a second, smaller wave. Overhead, a strip of sky no wider than a road.

Three days out of the tavern where this whole mess had started, and her boat was carrying two passengers, one sealed chest with a dead country's stamp on it, and more trouble than cargo space. She'd told herself it was for the money. Tamsin had paid half up front—coin counted out with suspicious precision—and Hale had paid the other half in the only currency he had, which was promises and a head full of maps. Orsen had taken both payments and told herself the ledger balanced.

The ledger did not balance. The ledger was lying to her, and she knew it, and she steered anyway.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble aboard a small boat always does, at breakfast.

Orsen's galley was four feet of counter, a stove that sulked if you looked at it wrong, and a set of rules posted on the wall in Pell's careful hand: *Wash your own dish. Do not touch the coffee unless invited. Do not discuss politics, religion, or drowned nations before noon.*

Tamsin was reading the rules aloud when Hale came down the companionway, ducking his tall frame through the hatch like a man entering a room he expected to be ambushed in. Which, given the girl's expression, was reasonable.

"'Do not discuss drowned nations before noon,'" Tamsin read. "That's aimed at us."

"It's aimed at everyone," Hale said mildly. "It's a good rule."

"You wrote it?"

"Pell wrote it. After our first evening aboard." Hale settled onto the bench across from her, unfolding his long limbs with the care of a man who'd learned that sudden movements near Tamsin were ill-advised. "I believe the phrase he used was 'never again.'"

"He's not wrong," Orsen said from the stove, where she was performing the morning ritual of threatening the coffee pot into cooperation. "First night out, you two went at each other over Vhalen's grain tariffs until midnight. Grain tariffs. I've run gunrunners who argued less."

"They weren't tariffs, they were *levies*—" Tamsin started, and then caught Orsen's look and shut her mouth around whatever came next, though her eyes stayed lit like banked coals.

Hale poured himself coffee without asking, which was either courage or death wish; Orsen hadn't decided. "For the record," he said, "I wasn't arguing about grain. I was arguing about accuracy."

"You were arguing about being right."

"There's a difference."

"Not on this boat there isn't." Tamsin tore her bread in half with unnecessary force. "You spent three days mapping passes you never walked, and then your empire used them to walk armies through, and now you sit there drinking coffee like history is a debate you can win on points."

The galley went quiet in the way galleys do—the stove ticking, the river muttering against the hull, Pell's boots overhead pausing mid-stride because even he could hear the temperature drop.

Hale set his cup down. Slowly. "You think I don't know what my maps did?"

"I think knowing and paying are different things."

"Yes," Hale said quietly. "They are. And I've been paying for eleven years, girl, in ways you can't count yet because you haven't lived long enough to owe anything that big." He rose, ducked back out the hatch, and was gone.

Tamsin stared at the hatch curtain. Her ears had gone red. "I didn't mean—"

"You meant every word," Orsen said, sliding a plate of eggs in front of her. "That's what worries me. Eat. Arguing on an empty stomach is how people fall overboard."

* * *

By afternoon the gorge had tightened further and the wind had turned traitor, funneling down the rock corridor straight into their teeth. Orsen took the Wake up the throat of it in short, hard pushes, reading the water the way she read everything—by feel, by the color of the boils, by the little lies the surface told. The passengers kept clear of the pilothouse, which suited her. The pilothouse was the one place on the boat where nobody needed her to be anything but competent.

Except that around the fourth hour, she noticed something happening on the foredeck that made her forget, briefly, to be annoyed by the wind.

Pell was teaching Tamsin knots.

He did it the way he did everything: wordlessly, mostly. He'd tie one—slowly, hands moving like they were pouring something invisible—and hold it out. Tamsin would study it, frown, try it, fail, swear under her breath, try again. Pell would take it back, untie it, and start over without a single syllable of commentary. No sighs. No corrections delivered like verdicts. Just the same patient demonstration, again and again, as if time were something the two of them had an endless supply of.

Orsen had known Pell nine years. In nine years she had seen him teach exactly nothing to exactly no one. He tolerated passengers the way a dockpost tolerates gulls—with stillness and quiet contempt. And here he was, spending the better part of an hour teaching a half-starved girl the bowline, and the cleat hitch, and something complicated with two loops that Orsen herself had never bothered to learn because Pell handled all the fancy rope work and she handled the steering and that division of labor had served them fine, thank you.

She watched Tamsin get the bowline right on the sixth try. The girl held it up, and Pell examined it, and nodded once—a single nod, worth more than most men's applause—and Tamsin grinned like she'd been handed a medal. It changed her whole face. For a moment she looked exactly her age, which Orsen realized she'd been guessing at wrong; the hunger and the sharpness made her seem older, but the grin was seventeen if it was a day.

Orsen turned back to the wheel and found she was smiling, and stopped immediately, the way you'd snatch your hand off a hot stove.

*This,* said the old voice in her head, the one that had kept her alive and alone for eleven years, *is how it starts. First knots. Then meals together. Then somebody's sick and you sit up with them, and then one day you look around and there are people on your boat whose absence would leave holes, and holes are just places where the water gets in.*

She put the thought away. There was a sandbar shelf ahead, unmarked, the kind of thing that ate hulls, and she had real problems to attend to.

* * *

*

The truce, such as it was, lasted until supper.

Orsen cooked—her one domestic act, performed with the grim efficiency of a woman feeding a crew she hadn't agreed to have. Stew, bread, the last of the good cheese. They ate in the main cabin because the deck was awash with spray, four of them crowded around a table built for two, knees bumping.

It was Hale, this time, who broke the ceasefire. Orsen suspected later that he'd done it deliberately—that underneath the guilt and the gray beard and the careful manners there was a stubborn streak a mile wide, and he'd decided that if the girl was going to hate him, she'd at least hate him accurately.

"The pass we're taking," he said, tearing bread. "The Corvane glacier route. You know why it opens at all?"

"Because the ice moves," Tamsin said. "Calving season. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows the words. Do you know what's actually happening?" He waited. Tamsin's chin came up, which Orsen had learned was the girl's version of *no, but I'll die before admitting it.* "The glacier advances all winter. Come spring, the meltwater undermines the toe of it, and great slabs shear off into the valley. For a few weeks, the rubble clears and the route through is open. Then the melt slows, the ice stabilizes, and the pass closes until next year." He paused. "My survey mapped that timing to within four days. Four days, across forty miles of ice. That part of my work was true. Whatever else was done with it."

"Whatever else was *done with it*," Tamsin repeated. "Listen to him. Like the maps walked down the mountain on their own."

"Tamsin," Orsen said.

"No, I want to hear this. Go on. Tell us how the empire just *found* your maps. Tell us how they happened to use the one route through the Corvanes, the one nobody else had charted, to come at Vhalen from behind while the treaty talks were still sitting at the table." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Orsen saw it—the thing underneath, the thing the girl was lying about or hiding or carrying. Grief so fresh it hadn't finished becoming anger yet.

"My maps were seized after the expedition," Hale said. His voice had gone very flat and very controlled, the voice of a man walking carefully across ice he knew was thin. "I filed a protest. I was court-martialed for it. Stripped of rank, stripped of license. I have spent eleven years watching my name attached to the worst thing the empire ever did, and I have documents in my own hand proving I objected, and do you know what those documents are worth, girl? Nothing. Less than nothing. Because history doesn't want the man who objected. History wants someone to blame, and I'm conveniently shaped."

"So am I!" Tamsin was on her feet now, and the cabin felt suddenly small. "So is everyone who came out of that valley! You lost a *rank*. We lost—"

She stopped. Just stopped, mouth open, the rest of it caught behind her teeth like a fish she'd decided not to land.

"We lost what we lost," she finished, quieter. "And you'll never know what that is, because the ones who could tell you don't talk to empire men."

She went up on deck. A moment later, spray rattled the windows, and Pell's boots crossed overhead to stand near hers—not close, just near. Orsen had seen him do it twice now. Standing watch over the girl without being asked, without acknowledging it, in the manner of a man who would deny it under direct questioning.

Hale sat with his head bowed over his untouched stew for a long moment.

"She's right," he said finally. "About the shape of it. I lost a rank. She lost a country. Those aren't the same weight, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise to win an argument."

Orsen looked at him—really looked, for the first time since the dock. At the gray in his beard and the tremor in his hands and the way he sat like a man who'd been carrying something heavy for so long he'd forgotten it could be set down.

"Eat your stew," she said. "Argue tomorrow. Before noon, per the rules."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through him. "Your deckhand's rules are the best governance I've encountered in years."

"Don't tell him that. He'll post more."

* * *

*

Two days later, the gorge opened into the long lake country, and the wind died, and for one golden evening the Kestrel ran smooth as poured glass.

Orsen came off her watch at dusk and found all three of them in the stern. Tamsin at the rail, practicing her knots with a length of line, quick fingers, no more swearing. Hale beside her, pointing out birds—some small brown diving thing—and Tamsin actually listening, actually asking questions, the war between them suspended like a held breath. And Pell at the tiller line, keeping her steady, saying nothing, watching the girl's hands with an expression Orsen had never seen him wear and couldn't have named if you paid her.

Nobody asked her to join them. That was the thing. Nobody turned to make room, nobody called out, nobody performed inclusion. They just... continued, in her presence, as if she belonged to the picture whether she consented to it or not.

Orsen stood at the edge of the lamplight with her arms crossed and understood, with the cold clarity of a pilot reading water, that she was in danger.

Not syndicate danger. Not rocks or sandbars or flood-chests. The other kind. The kind she'd spent eleven years and a great deal of money outrunning. The kind where a boat stops being an escape vehicle and starts being a home, and a crew stops being ballast and starts being a reason, and reasons are the heaviest cargo there is, because you can't jettison them when the water gets rough. You just go down holding them.

*Tamsin laughed at something Hale said.* Actually laughed. And Hale smiled like a man surprised by his own face, and Pell almost—almost—cracked, the corner of his mouth twitching toward something that in another man would have been called warmth.

Orsen went forward to check the chest lashings she'd checked an hour ago.

Under the tarp, the Vhalen seal stared up at her, wax dark as dried blood. She crouched beside it in the dark and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the tavern: that the girl had lied to her about something—Orsen had watched enough survivors to smell it, and Tamsin smelled like a story with pages torn out. That the surveyor's guilt ran deeper than he'd confessed, or differently. That the syndicate gunboat they'd spotted two nights back, hanging on the horizon like a bad memory, hadn't given up.

And that none of it—not one bit of it—was making her turn the boat around.

She lashed the tarp down tight and sat back on her heels and listened to her crew laughing in the stern, and hated how much she wanted to go stand in the middle of it.

Ahead, far up the darkening lake, lightning flickered inside a cloud bank stacked on the northern horizon like masonry. Silent, for now. But Orsen had read weather her whole life, and the sky was telling her the same thing the river always did:

Everything downstream comes due eventually.
