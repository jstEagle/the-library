## 7. Syndicate Smoke

Pell had a theory about smoke.

Eleven years running the Kestrel with Orsen Ravensworth had taught him to read the river the way she did—by feel, by smell, by the particular way water lied—but his own private education had been in smoke. Woodsmoke meant breakfast or a cold night coming. Coal smoke meant a steamer working upstream against the current, all patience and money. And thin, greasy, bitter smoke—the kind that smelled like someone burning oil they hadn't paid full price for—meant trouble with a flag on it.

He smelled that third kind on the second evening out of Gray Landing, just as the sun was going down behind the western bluffs and turning the whole river the color of an old bruise.

He didn't say anything at first. He'd learned that too. You didn't walk up to Orsen Ravensworth and say *trouble*, because she'd look at you with those flat gray eyes and say *I know* in a voice that made you feel like you'd wasted her time by confirming it. So instead he climbed up onto the roof of the pilothouse where he could see downstream without looking like he was looking, and he watched the bend below Cutter's Shoal, and sure enough, there it was: a smudge of gray hanging low over the water where no honest boat would be making smoke that thick.

The girl saw him watching. Tamsin had taken to sitting on the cargo hatch in the evenings, wrapped in a blanket three sizes too big for her, doing something with a piece of cord—knots, mostly, the ones Pell had been teaching her, tied and untied until her fingers knew them better than her mouth knew words. She followed his gaze downstream, squinted, and said nothing either. Which was its own kind of education. Three days aboard and she was already learning the Vesper's Wake's first language, which was silence with opinions.

Hale, of course, said plenty.

"Smoke," the surveyor announced from the deck below, in the tone of a man who believed observation was a gift he was bestowing upon the world. "Downstream. Heavy, oily. That's a syndicate packet boat, if I'm any judge. The *Kestrel Queen* class, twin boilers, shallow draft for river work but not as shallow as—" He stopped, because Orsen had come out of the pilothouse and the temperature on deck had dropped several degrees.

"Thank you, Master Hale," she said. "For the shipwright's report."

"You're welcome," Hale said, missing it entirely, which was more or less his natural condition.

Pell watched Orsen look downstream. She didn't squint the way Tamsin did; she just went still, the way she went still over sandbars, over weather, over anything the river was about to do. Stillness was how she listened. Then she turned to Pell and said one word:

"Supper?"

"Stew's on," Pell said. "Rice'll be done in ten minutes."

"Good." She looked back at the smoke, then at the sky, where the first stars were starting to show through the bruise-color. "Fog tonight. Cold water under warm air off the sloughs. We'll eat, and then we'll go visit some old friends of mine."

"Friends?" Tamsin asked.

"Sandbars," Pell said. "She means sandbars. Don't ask. It's worse than it sounds."

* * *

They ate stew while the fog came down the river like something poured.

Pell had seen a hundred fogs on the Kestrel and never two alike. This one came off the side sloughs in long white ropes first, drifting across the channel in slow ribbons, and then the whole river seemed to exhale at once and the world simply ended about forty feet from the hull. The lantern light turned into a soft yellow ball around them. The water went silent except for the Wake's own engine, ticking and muttering to itself below decks like an old man complaining about his knees.

Orsen stood at the wheel with the throttle lever in her left hand and the wheel in her right, and she wasn't using charts. Pell knew this because he'd seen the chart case latched shut, and also because he knew her. Charts were for other people. Orsen carried the river in her head the way other folks carried their mothers' names.

"Master Hale," she said pleasantly, which anyone who knew her understood to be a warning label, "you mapped these waters once."

"Surveyed them, yes. In '41. The reach above Cutter's Shoal is well documented, soundings every hundred yards, I took most of them myself—"

"And is there a channel through the shallows east of Gull Island?"

Hale paused. Pell heard him set down his bowl. "There's no channel east of Gull Island. There's a marsh, some deadfalls, and a great deal of mud. I wrote as much in my report. *Impassable.*"

"Mm." Orsen turned the wheel a few degrees. "You surveyed in August, didn't you. Low water year."

"...Yes."

"Flood years are different." She said it the way a priest might say a scripture. "Flood years, the river remembers being bigger. It leaves itself little doors. Come spring, when the water's high and the fog's thick and somebody's chasing you—" the wheel turned again, smooth as thought, "—the doors open."

Tamsin had come forward to stand near the pilothouse window, blanket abandoned, knots forgotten. "Somebody's chasing us," she said. Not a question.

"Probably," Orsen said. "Could be a late freighter. Could be fishermen. Could be men who offered me a very polite sum of money for a chest we're currently sitting on top of, and who got told no."

"I told you we should've thrown it back in the river," Pell said. He said it every day. It was practically grace before meals now.

"Yes, dear," Orsen said, which was what she always said, and which meant *no*.

The engine note changed. Orsen eased the throttle back until the Wake was barely moving, sliding through the white like a knife through milk. Out in the fog somewhere behind them—downstream, past the dark—they heard it: a steam whistle, one short blast, then another. Not the friendly two-blast greeting of boats passing. One blast, repeated. A question asked twice by someone who already knew the answer.

"They're asking if we're here," Tamsin whispered.

"They know we're here," Orsen said. "They're asking if we're going to be polite about it." Her hands were easy on the wheel. Her voice was easy too. But Pell had spent eleven years learning the difference between Orsen-easy and actual easy, and this was Orsen-easy, which was the kind of calm a person builds on purpose, plank by plank, over deep water.

The whistle blew again, closer. Underneath it now, faint, came the chuff-chuff of paddles, and beneath even that—a sound Pell felt in his teeth more than heard—the deep bass note of a big boiler working hard.

"She's faster than us," Hale said quietly. All the lecture had gone out of his voice. "In open water, she runs us down inside an hour."

"That's why we're not giving her open water," Orsen said. "Pell. Bow lamp off. Fenders down along the port side, all of them. We're going to get intimate with some reeds."

"Aye," Pell said, and went to do it, and noticed as he passed the cargo hatch that Tamsin had picked up the mooring hook and was holding it like she'd held things before, things with edges. The girl kept surprising him. Most surprises on a boat were bad ones. He was beginning to suspect she might be the other kind.

* * *

What happened over the next hour, Pell would remember for the rest of his life, and he would tell it badly, because some things don't survive telling.

Orsen took the Vesper's Wake east of Gull Island.

There was no channel east of Gull Island. There was a marsh, some deadfalls, and a great deal of mud, and a man named Davor Hale had written *impassable* across it in a government hand so fine it looked printed. And Orsen threaded her boat into it in fog so thick Pell couldn't see the bow from the wheelhouse, at night, at speed—or what passed for speed in six feet of water—reading the river by ear and by the pull of the current against the hull and by eleven years of loving a woman made entirely of stubbornness and sandbars.

Pell stood at the bow with a boathook and a lantern he wasn't allowed to light, calling depths by the color of the boil off the prow. Dark water: fine. Brown water: getting shallow. Green-brown with a certain oily swirl: *now*. He'd done this maybe four times in his life, each time convinced it was the last, each time swearing off, each time back aboard within the month because the alternative was a life without her cooking in it, and a man has to have standards.

"Hard to port!" he called, as a deadfall loomed up like the ribs of something drowned.

"Port," Orsen agreed, and the Wake swung so close to the snag that Pell heard bark whisper along the hull, and then they were past it and into a lane of reeds taller than the rail, bending and hissing overhead, and the world was nothing but white fog and green stalks and the steady, unhurried voice of Orsen Ravensworth saying *ease her* and *hold* and *there*.

Behind them, out in the real river, the syndicate boat's whistle blew again—and this time it was confused. Two blasts, then one, then a long frustrated bellow. They'd lost the channel marks in the fog. They were reading charts. Charts, Pell thought with a savage little joy, drawn by a man standing on our deck right now holding a rope like his life depended on it.

"Mr. Hale," Orsen called, without taking her eyes off the water. "Your report says impassable."

"It does," Hale called back. His voice was shaking, but he was grinning—Pell could hear the grin. "It appears my report was written by a man who had never been chased."

"Most reports are." She eased the throttle up a hair. "That's how empires end up surprised."

And then—this was the part Pell would tell badly, the part that never came out right—the syndicate boat, blind and furious out in the main channel, ran hard aground on Cutter's Shoal with a crunch that rolled across the marsh like distant thunder, and her whistle let out one long descending note of pure humiliation, and in the pilothouse, lit by the single dim binnacle lamp, Orsen Ravensworth almost smiled.

Almost. It was the corner of her mouth, lifting, for perhaps half a second, before she put it away like something breakable.

Pell saw it. He was the only one positioned to see it, and he saw it, and it frightened him more than the gunboat had.

Because he knew what that almost-smile cost. He'd watched her pay for it before, years ago, back when there had been other people aboard—people whose names she never said anymore, whose absence lived in the empty cabin aft that she kept swept and locked. Every time she let herself be glad of something, the river eventually sent her a bill. He'd watched her learn the arithmetic. Gladness divided by time equals grief. So she'd stopped dividing. Stopped letting herself hold anything long enough for the current to take it.

And now here was a girl on her deck with a dead country's eyes, and a broken surveyor laughing shakily over a rope, and a chest full of other people's sins riding in her hold, and Orsen had almost smiled, and Pell stood in the reeds with the boathook in his hands and thought: *oh, captain. You're doing it again. You're buying in.*

The river always collected.

* * *

They came out of the marsh an hour later, quiet as a prayer, into a stretch of black open water under clearing stars, and tied up to a cottonwood on a nameless bank where the only sounds were frogs and the engine cooling. Tamsin sat down on the deck planks and discovered her hands were trembling, and looked personally offended about it.

"Adrenaline," Hale said kindly, wrapping her fingers around the warm mug Pell handed her. "Perfectly normal. The body spends its courage whether or not the mind approves of the purchase."

"My body can keep it," Tamsin muttered, and drank the tea anyway, and Pell noticed she sat where she could see both the bank and the boat, the way survivors sit.

Orsen came out last. She stood at the rail a moment, looking back the way they'd come, toward the distant glow where a grounded gunboat was presumably still cursing its charts. Then she looked at her crew—one girl, one ruin of a surveyor, one deckhand who'd stayed too long to pretend it was employment—and said, in the voice she used for navigation notes:

"We make Coldharbour in five days if the weather holds. After tonight, assume the syndicate knows exactly where we are and what we're carrying. They'll stop sending boats." She paused. "Boats are the polite part."

Nobody asked what the impolite part was. Nobody needed to.

Pell banked the stove and checked the lines, and as he came back past the pilothouse he found Orsen still at the wheel, though the boat was tied fast and going nowhere. She was looking at the chart case—latched shut, useless, unnecessary—and her hand was resting on it, lightly, the way you touch a door you're deciding whether to open.

"Pell," she said, without turning around.

"Captain."

"The girl's hands shook."

"Yes."

"Hale laughed."

"Yes."

A long pause. The frogs filled it. Somewhere far downstream, very faint, the syndicate boat's whistle sounded one last time—not a threat now, just a boat admitting it was beaten, and small, and far from home.

"When we get to Coldharbour," Orsen said, "I put them ashore, take the fee, and burn the chest. That's the plan. That's been the plan since Gray Landing."

"I know it has," Pell said.

She turned then, and looked at him, and her face was the flat gray mask it always was, except for the corner of her mouth, which was still not quite behaving.

"So why," she said, "does it keep sounding like a plan for somebody else?"

Pell didn't answer. He'd learned that too. He just nodded goodnight and went aft, and lay in his bunk listening to the river move against the hull, and worried, quietly and without any hope of stopping, about the bill that was surely already in the mail.

Two days later, at the fuel dock below Coldharbour, a boy in a syndicate cap handed Pell a folded paper and pedaled away without a word. Pell read it once, then stood very still for a long time, and then walked slowly up to the pilothouse, where Orsen was studying the mountains with a spyglass, and held it out to her without a word of his own.

The paper said only: *We know what's in the chest. So does everyone else. Ask your surveyor what he signed.*

Orsen read it. Then she lowered the paper, very slowly, and looked down the deck at Davor Hale—who was teaching Tamsin a bowline knot, patient and gentle, his scarred hands moving over hers—and Pell watched his captain's face do something he had never once seen it do in eleven years.

It closed like a trap.
