## 8. The Chest Speaks

Tamsin had been counting the days since she'd lied to Orsen Ravensworth, and the number had grown heavy enough to carry like a stone in her coat pocket.

Eleven days. Eleven days of eating Pell's terrible river stew and pretending it was fine. Eleven days of watching Davor Hale's hands when he thought no one was looking—long, precise hands that drew little maps in spilled tea on the deck boards. Eleven days of Orsen at the wheel, shoulders set like a woman who had decided exactly how much of the world she would allow near her, and Tamsin standing just outside that line, smiling her best smile, holding her secret shut.

The trouble with secrets, Tamsin had learned young, was not that they were hard to keep. It was that they got heavier every day you carried them, and nobody ever offered to help lift.

Tonight the river ran wide and slow through a country of dark pines, the Vesper's Wake chugging north with her lamps lit against the early dusk, and Tamsin sat on the cargo hatch with the chest between her knees, because she had finally run out of days.

* * *

It started, as most disasters aboard the Wake did, with Pell's cooking.

"Salt pork again," Hale said, not unkindly, turning his tin plate as though a different angle might reveal lamb.

"Salt pork is *reliable*," Pell said from his crouch by the little deck stove. He was a long, folded-up man who spoke maybe forty words a day and spent them all wisely. "Pork doesn't lie to you. Pork shows up every evening and does its job."

"That's more than I can say for some passengers," Orsen murmured from the wheel. She wasn't looking at any of them, but Tamsin felt the words land anyway, gentle as a thrown pebble.

"I've paid my fare," Tamsin said.

"You've paid half your fare."

"And I'm worth the other half."

Orsen glanced back then, just briefly, and there it was—the thing Tamsin had been cataloguing for eleven days like a naturalist studying some rare bird: the almost-smile. It lived at one corner of Orsen's mouth and came out only when she thought no one was watching, and it made her look ten years younger and infinitely more dangerous, somehow, because a woman who could still almost-smile after whatever had made her like this was a woman with something buried deep enough to kill for.

Tamsin looked down at the chest between her knees. The Vhalen seal stared up at her from the lid, that drowned nation's crest—a river and a mountain inside a circle—and she felt the stone in her pocket get heavier.

"Hale," she said.

"Mm?" He was scraping his plate with bread.

"When you mapped the Corvane passes. How many people were on your expedition?"

The scraping stopped.

That was the thing about Hale: he was quick everywhere except where it mattered. Quick with numbers, quick with languages, quick with a joke when the mood took him. But ask him a question that touched the wound, and he went slow as river ice forming.

"Why do you ask?" he said carefully.

"Curiosity." She smiled. She was aware, even as she did it, that her smile had teeth in it now, and that everyone at the stove had gone quiet in the particular way of people who have heard a change in the weather.

"Fourteen," Hale said. "Myself, six surveyors, four guides, two cooks, and a mule named General who hated all of us equally."

"And when you came back down—how many of those fourteen knew what the maps were for?"

"Tamsin." Orsen's voice from the wheel had changed. Not sharp. Worse. Flat. "What are you doing?"

"What I should have done eleven days ago." Tamsin stood up. Her legs were shaking, which annoyed her; she'd faced down harbor syndicate men with steadier legs than this. But those men hadn't mattered. These people—these ridiculous, stubborn, salt-pork-eating people—mattered, and that was precisely the problem. "I'm going to open the chest properly this time. All the way. And everyone's going to hear what's actually in it."

"We've seen the documents," Orsen said. "Charters, ledgers, correspondence. Vhalen's official papers."

"Yes." Tamsin knelt and worked the lock—it had surrendered to her hairpin on day three, though she'd relocked it every time, a small daily treachery. "You've seen what I *showed* you. The top layer. Charters and grain ledgers, dry as ash. Enough to make the chest valuable. Enough to make it dangerous. Not enough to make anyone ask why a girl with no shoes walked into a tavern and claimed it."

She lifted the false bottom.

Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay the rest: a bundle of letters, a slim book bound in gray leather, and a single sheet that even now, after all these months, made her hands want to shake.

* * *

"This is treason," Hale said quietly. He hadn't moved from his place by the stove, but everything about him had gone still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "Whatever that is, reading it out loud on an imperial-licensed vessel—"

"It's history," Tamsin said. "Which around here amounts to the same crime."

"Tamsin." Orsen had lashed the wheel and come aft. Up close, without the lamp behind her, her face was all angles and patience worn thin. "I hauled you north on the strength of a story. Girl born under a dead seal, chest is her birthright, take me to Coldharbour. I didn't believe half of it, but I believed *you*, which is worse, because believing a person costs more than believing a story. So before you open whatever that is—you're going to tell me what I've actually been carrying."

"The truth?"

"The whole truth. Starting with why you flinched every time Hale mentioned his expedition."

Tamsin looked at Hale. He was watching her with an expression she'd seen once before, years ago, on the face of a village elder the night before soldiers came: the look of a man who already knows and is only waiting for the world to confirm it.

So she told them.

"My name isn't just Tamsin. It's Tamsin Vael-Karrowe. My grandmother was third cousin to the last Speaker of Vhalen, which makes me nothing, officially, because officially Vhalen doesn't exist. Officially it signed a treaty of accession twenty-six years ago and its people scattered so thoroughly that the empire stopped bothering to count them." She unwrapped the oilcloth as she talked, layer by layer, like peeling a bandage. "Officially, Vhalen opened its mountain passes to imperial troops because its own leadership chose peace over slaughter. That's the story in every schoolbook south of the range. Peaceful accession. Bloodless transition."

"And the truth?" Pell asked. Forty words a day, and he'd spent three of them on her.

"The truth is in the letters." She lifted the bundle free. "Correspondence between the Speaker's council and the imperial governor, in the year before the treaty. And in the ledger book—" she touched the gray leather without picking it up, the way you'd touch a snake you weren't sure was dead— "payments. Sums disbursed. To named individuals. For services rendered in facilitating the, and I quote, 'orderly resolution of the Vhalen question.'"

"Paid traitors," Orsen said.

"Paid patriots, the empire called them afterward. Men who sold the passes, sold the granaries, sold the names of every family that might resist. My grandmother's family was on a list in this book. We were supposed to be rounded up in the first week. Someone warned us instead—one of the bought men, at the end, having second thoughts too late. Half my family ran north. The other half—" She shrugged, because the other half needed no shrugging explanation. Everyone on this boat knew what happened to the other halves of families.

Silence held the deck. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere upstream a loon called, long and lonely, like the country itself asking after its own name.

Then Hale said, "Show me the ledger."

And Tamsin, who had rehearsed this moment a hundred times in the dark of the fo'c'sle—angry versions, tearful versions, a version where she threw him overboard—found that none of the rehearsals fit. Because she didn't feel angry. She felt sick.

She handed him the book and watched him turn pages with those long precise fingers, watched his lips move faintly over names, and waited for the page. She knew the page. She'd known it since the night she first read it by candlelight in a smuggler's attic, and had spent eleven days hoping she'd remembered wrong.

Hale reached it. She saw him reach it.

His thumb stopped on the entry, and for a long moment he didn't breathe, and then he did the worst possible thing, which was nothing. No denial. No outrage. Just a man going very quiet over a name written in faded brown ink.

"Read it aloud," Tamsin said. Her voice cracked on *aloud*, which she hated. "Since we're being honest tonight. Read the entry for the fourth of Frostmonth."

"Tamsin—" Orsen started.

"*Read it.*"

Hale read it. His voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "'Fourth of Frostmonth. To D. Hale, surveyor, Corvane expedition, the sum of four hundred crowns, in consideration of completed surveys of the northern passes, with gratitude of the Crown for services rendered.'" He lowered the book. "That's my signature below it. That's my hand."

"You took money," Pell said. Not a question. An accusation delivered in the flattest tone imaginable, which from Pell was a shout.

"I took *payment for maps*," Hale said, and now he was on his feet, and the stillness had broken into something rawer. "Fourteen of us went up into the Corvane Range on a commission to chart trade routes—we were told *trade routes*. When I came down and learned what the surveys had armed, I went to the magistrate, I went to the press, I went to three different ministers. Do you know what happens to a surveyor who accuses his own commission? You lose your post. Your credentials. Your name ends up in a file marked *unreliable*, and twenty-six years later you're begging passage on a riverboat because it's the only work left to a man whose maps killed a country!"

"You didn't know?" Tamsin laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "That's the defense? The great Davor Hale, who can calculate a glacier's drift to the yard, couldn't work out what passes and supply routes were *for*?"

"I was twenty-four years old and ambitious and I didn't *want* to know! Yes! There's your confession, girl—write it in your ledger!" He was breathing hard. "But I did not sell Vhalen. I built the knife and someone else swung it, and I have spent every year since trying to—"

"To what? Atone? Is that what this is? Begging aboard my boat, offering your precious glacier route—is that penance or is it the last page of the ledger, making sure nothing else surfaces with your name near it?"

The words landed harder than she'd meant them to. She saw it hit—saw it go in under his ribs the way only true things can—and part of her wanted to take it back immediately, and a larger, older, hungrier part of her stood there and let it bleed him.

Because here was the thing Tamsin had never said out loud, not to anyone: she had spent her whole life with nothing. No country, no house, no name anyone respected, half a family in the ground and the other half scattered like seed. The chest was the first thing that had ever been *hers*. The anger in it was hers. The grief in it was hers. And grief hoarded turns to poison, and she had been drinking it for eleven days straight, and tonight she had poured a cup for everyone.

"Tamsin." Orsen's voice cut across the deck like a cleaver. "Enough."

"He—"

"I heard what he said. I heard what you said. Both of you sit down before I put you both ashore, and I don't care if it's the swimming kind of ashore." Orsen turned to Hale. "You. Did you know the chest existed before she found you?"

"No."

"Did you know what was in it?"

"No."

"Were you sent by anyone—syndicate, ministry, anyone—to watch it?"

A pause. A real pause, the kind a lying man fills instantly and an honest man has to search. "No," Hale said. "I came to you because you were going north and I know the pass, and because—" his voice caught and steadied— "and because I thought if I could stand on the ground my maps betrayed, carrying the proof of what they did, it might weigh less. It doesn't. In case anyone was wondering. It weighs exactly the same."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed. Pell fed another stick into the stove, which was his way of speaking.

Orsen turned last to Tamsin, and her eyes were the color of the river in November, and Tamsin braced herself for the verdict.

"You let me sail eleven days with a lie sitting in my hold," Orsen said. "You let syndicate men threaten my boat over cargo I didn't understand. You let that man eat Pell's pork"—a flicker, there and gone, of the almost-smile, gone before it finished arriving— "while you sat on the thing that could hang him. That's done. I won't forget it." She held out her hand. "Give me the ledger."

Tamsin's arms closed around the gray book before her mind agreed to anything. "No."

"Give it to me, girl. I'm the captain. It's my hold."

"It's not yours. It was never going to be yours. That's the whole point of it—that's the point of *me*. Everyone wants to carry it, and keep it, and decide what happens to it, and it's mine. It's the only thing that's ever been mine."

"Is it?" Orsen said softly. "Because from where I stand, it looks like it owns you."

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of a gravel bar, because nobody trusted anybody at the wheel, which meant Orsen took first watch alone and dared anyone to comment. Tamsin sat in the bow with her knees drawn up, the chest locked again beneath her, the ledger wrapped against her stomach inside her coat like a second heartbeat.

Footsteps, soft on the deck. Pell lowered himself onto the hatch beside her without asking, which was very like him. For a while he said nothing at all, and the silence was almost comfortable, which confused her.

"You could've thrown him off at Gray Landing," he said eventually.

"I could have."

"Could've thrown the book in the river, too. Then nobody's guilty and everybody's clean."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "The book is the only proof. Without it, Vhalen stays dead on paper forever. With it—" She gestured at the dark northern sky, where somewhere past all that blackness the mountains waited, and beyond the mountains, if the old stories were true, the valley where the ones who ran north had endured. "With it, there's a case. A reckoning. Maybe a country."

"Mm." Pell turned a stick over in his fingers, whittling at nothing. "Man on my mother's island used to say, you can carry a grudge across an ocean and back, and at the end of it you'll have strong arms and empty hands." He stood, joints cracking like green firewood. "Stew's on. Salt pork."

He shuffled aft. Tamsin stayed in the bow a while longer, listening to the river slide past in the dark, and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, that the stone in her pocket had a shape now. It had always had a shape. She'd just never let herself look at it directly.

The ledger against her ribs felt less like a birthright tonight and more like a brick she'd chosen to swallow.

And then lantern-light bloomed downstream—two lights, then three, riding low and fast against the current, running without running-lamps, the way boats run when they aren't fishing.

Tamsin was on her feet before she'd decided to move. "Orsen," she called, low and urgent. "Orsen. Lights on the water."

From the stern, flat and grim, the captain's voice: "I see them. Everybody below. Now."

The stone in Tamsin's pocket had gotten heavier still—but that, she suspected, was about to become the least of their problems.
