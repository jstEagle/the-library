## 9. Marooned at Gray Landing

The river ran out of ambition at Gray Landing.

Davor Hale had watched it happen all morning—the Kestrel thinning from a broad brown muscle into a sulking ribbon, gravel bars surfacing like the backs of sleeping animals. By noon the *Vesper's Wake* had grounded herself on a shoal with the deliberate, offended air of a lady sitting down in the middle of an argument and refusing to rise until it was conducted properly.

"Low water," Orsen said, in the tone other people reserved for plague reports. She stood at the bow rail, arms crossed, staring at the sandbar ahead as though it had insulted her mother. "Seasonal. Should have thought of it."

"Should have *told* us," Tamsin said.

"I told the river. It didn't listen either."

Hale kept to the stern, where he'd kept himself for six days now, ever since Tamsin had stood in the middle of the deck and said the word *signature* in a voice like a drawn knife. Six days of meals eaten standing up, of conversations that stopped when he approached, of Pell's broad back turned with the precision of a fortress gate. Six days of sleeping in the wheelhouse because his old berth now required walking past people who looked at him the way one looks at a stain.

He had been a surveyor for the Imperial Corps of Roads and Passes for nineteen years. He had endured blizzards, rockfall, a bear with strong opinions about his cookfire, and the slow bureaucratic murder of his career. None of it had prepared him for the particular cruelty of being marooned on a sandbar with three people who had every reason to hate him and nowhere for anyone to walk away to.

The sandbar was perhaps forty yards of gray gravel and scrub willow. Gray Landing itself—a dock, a leaning warehouse, a ferryman's hut with a chimney that had given up smoking sometime last decade—sat on the north bank, marooned in its own right, since the channel between had gone shallow enough to wade. A ferryman, ancient and unbothered, sat on his porch mending a net and watching them with the idle interest of a man who had watched rivers strand better boats than this.

"Could push her off," Pell offered. It was the first thing he'd said to Hale's vicinity in two days, and he said it to Orsen, not to the air generally, which Hale noted was an improvement of sorts.

"With what? The water's dropped a foot since dawn." Orsen crouched, laid her palm flat against the hull, and read it the way Hale had once read snowpack—by touch, by instinct, by some private liturgy he'd never been permitted to learn. "We wait for the evening release from the upriver dams. Maybe she floats at midnight. Maybe tomorrow."

"And if the syndicate gunboat finds us sitting here like a goose in a courtyard?" Tamsin demanded.

"Then we're a goose in a courtyard," Orsen said. "Get the chest off the deck. If we're stuck, we're stuck lightly."

Hale stepped forward to help, and Tamsin's glare arrived before he'd crossed half the distance. He stopped. Set his hands in his pockets. Watched them carry the chest below like a coffin, which, he supposed, in a way it was.

* * *

The evening release, it turned out, was a rumor the river declined to confirm. By dusk the *Wake* still sat on her shoal, listing slightly, wearing her grounding like a sulk. Orsen cooked—she cooked the way she did everything, efficiently and without a single wasted motion—and the four of them ate on deck in a silence so complete Hale could hear the willows scraping the gravel downstream.

It was Tamsin who broke it, because Tamsin could no more endure silence than a kettle could endure fire.

"You could at least say something," she said, not looking at him. "You've been a ghost for six days. It's worse than if you argued."

"Arguing with you is how I got here."

"No. *Lying* is how you got here. Arguing is just what I'd prefer to the ghost."

Hale set down his tin plate. He had rehearsed this confession, in the dark of the wheelhouse, in perhaps forty versions. The short version. The dignified version. The version with maps drawn in the dirt to prove his honesty. Now, with the river gone quiet and the first stars pricking through, every version evaporated and what remained was simply the truth, which was heavier than all of them.

"Then I'll say it," he said. "All of it, once, and then you can decide what I am."

Orsen didn't tell him to stop. That was something. He took it.

"I was the senior surveyor on the Corvane expedition, eleven years ago. Fourteen of us. We spent three summers mapping the high passes—the glacier route, the calving windows, every stable crossing and every deathtrap. It was the best work of my life. I was proud of it." His voice caught on the word *proud*, and he let it. "The maps were commissioned for trade. That's what the writ said. Open the passes for trade with the northern valleys. And then Vhalen fell, and the imperial army came over the Corvane Range in the middle of calving season, through the one window my survey said was stable, and they burned a country that had never seen them coming because their generals had my maps in their saddlebags."

The willows hissed against the gravel. Somewhere downriver a night bird called, twice, and thought better of it.

"I didn't draw them a road to Vhalen," Hale said. "I drew the mountains. The mountains don't take sides. But I've had eleven years to learn that's no defense at all, because a knife doesn't take sides either, and it's still the knife's fault in the end, isn't it? Everyone says so."

"It wasn't your fault."

Every head turned. It was Pell who had spoken—Pell, who had not addressed a full sentence in Hale's direction since the chest came aboard, sitting with his back against the rail, turning his tin cup slowly.

"Maps are tools," Pell said. "I've watched Orsen read this river her whole career. If someone took her soundings and used them to run a blockade, you wouldn't blame the soundings."

"That is," Hale said carefully, "the most generous thing anyone has said to me in a decade, and I'm not sure I deserve it."

"You don't," Tamsin snapped. Then she stopped, and Hale watched her wrestle with something—watched the anger in her face collide with the exhaustion underneath it, and lose, narrowly. She was nineteen and carrying a dead country in a locked chest, and she was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "My grandfather was in the northern militia," she said, quieter. "He used to say the empire didn't conquer Vhalen. It *purchased* it, and the purchase order was signed by one of our own. That's what the papers say. That's what the signature says. So don't tell me about knives, surveyor. Tell me about the man who sold us."

"I can't," Hale said. "I never learned who it was. But—" and here was the part he had hoarded for six days like a miser, the part he'd been afraid to spend because spending it made him useful, and useful men were kept, and kept men could be hurt again, "—I can tell you who *paid*. The expedition was funded through the Harrowgate Trading Concern. Which is the Harrowgate Trading Concern still, today, except now it calls itself the harbor syndicate and owns half the docks from Kestrelmouth to Coldharbour."

Silence again, but a different silence—the silence of a lock turning.

Orsen set down her cup. "You're telling me the men chasing us up this river are the same men who bought a country."

"I'm telling you they're the same *money*. Whether the same men, I can't swear. Money wears new coats every generation." He looked at the chest's outline through the deck boards, as if he could see through wood and into the truth. "But I know their ledgers' habits. I mapped their trade routes for years before the war. If there's a signature in that chest tied to Harrowgate gold, then whoever sold Vhalen wasn't a traitor in the empire's pay. He was a businessman. And businessmen keep records, and records can be read."

"And you can read them," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question. It was a recalculation—he could see it happening behind her eyes, the ledger of his worth being rebalanced, guilt struck through and utility entered in its place. He should have felt relieved. Instead he felt like a tool on a shelf, which was, he supposed, at least a shelf with better company than he'd had in years.

"I can read them," he said. "Old mercantile script, imperial cipher marks, the lot. I spent three years transcribing Harrowgate contracts. I know their abbreviations the way Orsen knows her sandbars."

Orsen was looking at him across the small circle of lamplight, and Hale had learned enough of her in six days to know that her silences had textures, and this one was the texture of a woman weighing a man on scales she didn't entirely trust. He waited. Waiting was a surveyor's whole trade: you did not hurry the mountain; you let the mountain tell you when it was ready.

"Here's what I know," Orsen said at last. "Six days ago I had a boat, a deckhand, and a passenger I didn't want. Now I've got a grounded boat, a deckhand who talks more than he used to, a girl who lies about her age, and a disgraced surveyor who just told me the syndicate that's hunting us built its fortune on a massacre." She rubbed her eyes. "And the worst part is that I believe you. Which means I can't put you off at Gray Landing, because if the syndicate finds you here, they'll kill you, and if Tamsin's right about what's in that chest, you're the only person on this river who can prove it."

"So I'm useful," Hale said.

"You're *alive*, and useful, and those aren't the same thing, and I'd thank you not to confuse them." She said it without heat, which somehow made it land harder. "I've been useful to people before. It's a terrible way to be kept."

* * *

They sat up past midnight, because nobody could sleep, and the confession loosened things the way a jammed lock loosens—suddenly, all at once, with everyone startled by the noise.

Hale talked about the Corvane. He couldn't help it; eleven years of guilt had compacted into eleven years of silence, and now that the seal was broken the mountain came out of him in slides. He told them about the calving season, how the glacier pass opened for perhaps five weeks in high summer when the meltwater dropped and the ice towers stood stable in the cold nights, and how the window had been narrowing year by year as the climate soured. He told them about the tollhouses the old Vhaleni had built along the route, stone shelters with iron stoves, some of them still sound. He told them about the serac fields, where the ice stood in towers the height of church spires and fell without warning, and how you crossed them at dawn, when the cold still held everything together, and you did not stop, and you did not raise your voice.

"Like a library," Tamsin said.

"Like a library," Hale agreed, "built by a librarian with a temper."

Pell, of all people, asked about the tollhouses—asked whether they had water, whether their doors still hung, whether the syndicate would know them. And Hale realized, with a small cold drop in his stomach, that Pell wasn't asking as a curiosity. Pell was asking as a man planning a route.

"You're thinking of taking the pass," Hale said.

"I'm thinking," Pell said, "that the syndicate has boats and we don't, and the mountains don't care about either of us. Seems fair."

"The mountains don't care about anything," Hale said. "That's their whole charm. But the pass is doable, if we make Coldharbour before the window opens and cross in the first stable week. Late crossers get caught by the calving. Early crossers get caught by the melt." He paused. "I've never crossed it myself. I mapped it from the ridgelines and the old Vhaleni accounts. I'll be honest about that now, since we're apparently being honest: I know the pass the way a man knows a woman from her letters."

"And Tamsin knows it the way a man knows his grandmother's kitchen," Tamsin said. "Mountain-born, remember? My mother's people came over the Corvane the year of the fall. The routes are in the family. Songs, mostly. Songs and grudges."

"Between the two of us," Hale said, "we might make one competent guide."

"Between the two of us," Tamsin said, "we might. Don't flatter yourself into thinking that means forgiven."

"I wouldn't dream of it. Forgiveness is a country I've been denied entry to for eleven years. I've learned to live in the borderlands."

Orsen, who had been silent through all of this, said: "Borderlands flood, too."

Hale looked at her. She was watching the river, her face half-lit, and for a moment he saw something in it he hadn't seen before—not the armor, not the competence, but the thing the armor was for. A woman who had also been denied entry somewhere, and had also learned to live in the borderlands, and knew exactly what the borderlands cost.

"Yes," he said. "They do."

* * *

The river rose at 3 a.m.

Hale was awake to feel it—a shudder through the hull, a long groan of timber, the particular sensation of a grounded boat remembering she was a boat. He was on deck before he'd fully decided to be, and found Orsen already there, barefoot, one hand on the rail, listening to the water come back with an expression he could only call tenderness, though he suspected she'd deny it under oath.

"She floats," he said.

"She floats." Orsen didn't look at him. "Get the others. We'll warp her off the bar while there's water under us. I'm not sitting here at dawn with the syndicate's gunboat for a neighbor."

They worked in the dark, all four of them, hauling the kedge line out to the anchor Pell dropped in the returned channel, hauling until the *Wake* sighed off the gravel and swung free, and the river took her back the way a grudging host offers a chair. Nobody spoke much. It was work, and work, Hale had found, was a language everyone on this boat could speak without lying.

It was as they were coiling the last of the line that Tamsin came to stand beside him at the rail. The sky was going gray in the east. The chest sat below them in the hold, and the signature sat in the chest, and the money that had moved the signature sat in Coldharbour, two hundred miles north, wearing its newest coat.

"Harrowgate," she said. "You're certain of the name."

"Certain enough to bet my life on it. Which, I note, I already have."

"Then when we reach Coldharbour, you're going to teach me to read their script. All of it. Every abbreviation, every cipher mark." Her jaw was set in a way he recognized from photographs of people he'd never met—Vhaleni officers, Vhaleni holdouts, the whole stubborn lineage of a stubborn country. "Because if the syndicate wants this chest badly enough to chase us up a river, then it's worth more than papers. It's worth leverage. And I intend to know exactly how much leverage I'm holding before anyone takes it out of my hands."

Hale looked at her—this half-starved, fierce, unkillable girl with a drowned nation in her eyes—and felt, for the first time in eleven years, something that was not guilt. Purpose, maybe. Or its poor cousin, usefulness, worn honestly at last.

"First lesson at dawn," he said. "Bring the chest."

They were three miles upriver, the channel narrowing into the first gray throats of the gorge country, when Pell came down from the masthead and said, in his flat, unhurried way, that there was smoke behind them. One column. Moving fast.

Gunboat smoke.

Orsen looked back once, then north, at the walls of the gorge rising ahead, and Hale watched her do the arithmetic every fugitive does—speed against distance, fuel against fear—and then she opened the throttle, and the *Vesper's Wake* ran for the mountains with the syndicate's own sins chasing her up the river.
