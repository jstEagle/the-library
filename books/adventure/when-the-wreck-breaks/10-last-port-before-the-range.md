## 10. Last Port Before the Range

Coldharbour announced itself the way a debt collector does—first with a smell, then with noise, then with the sinking certainty that you were about to be handled.

Orsen smelled it before she saw it: woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular sour tang of mule. Then the river bent around a shoulder of granite and there it was, stacked up the mountainside like a child's idea of a city—three tiers of buildings clinging to the slope, rope bridges sagging between them, and above everything the gray wall of the Corvane Range filling half the sky, wearing its snows like a man wears a coat he hasn't decided to put on yet.

The port itself sat on a shelf of stone where the Kestrel gave out. Beyond Coldharbour the river was a rumor—white water tumbling down from the glaciers, good for nothing but drowning in. Everything that went north from here went on four legs or two.

"End of the line," Pell said. He said it the way he said most things, which was to say he barely said it at all, just let the words fall out of him like ballast.

"That's the arrangement," Orsen said.

She heard how it came out of her mouth. Flat. Final. A door closing. She'd rehearsed it for six days, all the way up through the gorges, and it still sounded wrong, and she couldn't have told you why.

Tamsin stood at the rail with the wind pulling at her hair, looking up at the mountain like it owed her money. "It's bigger than I thought."

"They always are," Orsen said, and immediately regretted saying anything at all, because Tamsin turned and looked at her with those eyes—the dead country's eyes, gray as slate and twice as patient—and Orsen felt something shift in her chest that she chose to interpret as indigestion.

Hale emerged from below decks blinking, his maps under one arm, looking like a man who had slept badly and deserved it. "Coldharbour," he said. "I haven't been here since—" He stopped. Everyone on this boat stopped their sentences at the same places lately. It was becoming a habit, like a family of stammerers.

"Since your maps worked," Tamsin supplied helpfully.

"Tamsin," Orsen said.

"What? I'm being supportive. He needs to face it."

"He needs to *walk*, is what he needs. All of us do. Dock's ahead."

* * *

The harbor master of Coldharbour was a woman named Brakka who looked like she'd been carved out of the same granite as the mountain and had considerably better manners than most stone. She took Orsen's mooring fees without counting them, which meant she'd already counted them by eye, and jerked her chin toward the upper town.

"Mule trains leave when the pass opens," she said. "Pass opens when it opens. Ask me again tomorrow and I'll tell you the same thing, and the day after that too, so save us both the breath."

"And syndicate men?" Orsen asked, keeping her voice bored. "They asking after the pass too?"

Brakka's eyes did something small and knowing. "Three of them. Been here a week. Buying drinks, telling stories about how generous the syndicate is these days." She slid the coins into a drawer. "I don't drink with them myself. Bad for the digestion."

"Where are they now?"

"The Salt House. Where else? It's the only tavern with chairs that don't wobble, and men who want to feel important need steady furniture." She looked at Orsen a moment longer. "You're the one with the boat from downriver. The one they're asking about."

"I'm nobody," Orsen said.

"Mm," said Brakka, in the tone of a woman filing that away under fiction.

* * *

They took a room at a place called the Anvil's Rest, which was cheaper than the Salt House and smelled honestly of hammer-scale instead of dishonestly of perfume. Orsen paid for two rooms and pretended not to notice that within an hour the arrangements had reorganized themselves without her permission—Pell claiming a corner of the larger room "to watch the gear," Hale spreading his maps across the floor like a man laying out a body for burial, Tamsin sitting cross-legged in the middle of it all sharpening a knife she'd acquired from somewhere Orsen didn't ask about.

The chest sat under the window. Orsen had carried it up herself. She noticed she'd done that—carried it herself, waved off Pell's offer—and she filed the observation away next to the indigestion.

"So," she said. "Here's how this ends. Tomorrow I find a buyer for that chest. Somebody with more muscle and less curiosity than we've got. I take the money, I split it fair, and everybody goes wherever people like us go when a job's done."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"That's the plan," Pell said.

"It's a good plan," Hale said, in the voice of a man reading someone else's eulogy.

"It's a *plan*," Tamsin said. "That's different from good. That's different from anything. It's just words in a row."

"It's words in a row that get us paid and keep us alive," Orsen said. "Which is more than most plans manage."

Tamsin looked at her. Not angry. Worse—disappointed, the way you're disappointed by weather. "You said north to the mountain port. You never said what happened after."

"There was no after. There's never an after. That's the whole trick of it."

And Tamsin nodded slowly, like Orsen had confirmed something sad she'd suspected all along, and went back to her knife, and Orsen stood there feeling like she'd kicked a dog that had been sleeping against her leg, except she hadn't kicked anything, she'd just stated terms, plain terms, *fair* terms—

She went downstairs to find a buyer.

* * *

The Salt House was warm and loud and full of exactly the kind of people Orsen had spent eleven years avoiding: mule drivers flush with pay, prospectors lying about their luck, and in the corner booth by the fire, three men wearing good boots and bad smiles.

She knew the type before she knew the faces. Syndicate men dressed down still dressed wrong—the cloth too fine to fool anyone, the hands too soft, the way they watched a room like they were pricing it. The big one in the middle had a scar through his eyebrow and the relaxed posture of a man who had never once needed to hurry toward violence because violence always waited for him.

Orsen bought a beer she didn't want and made herself visible, because that was the job tonight. Be seen. Make the offer happen on her terms, in public, with witnesses. Get it done before the thinking could start.

It took twenty minutes. Of course it did. Men like this enjoyed being sought.

"You're the pilot," the scarred one said finally, waving her over with two fingers. "Ravensworth. Vesper's Wake. We hear you've been carrying cargo upriver that isn't yours."

"I carry whatever pays."

"We know." He smiled. "That's why we like you. Simple transactions. So let's make one." He set a purse on the table. It clinked with the heavy, honest sound of real silver. "For the chest. Tonight. And a bonus for forgetting you ever saw it."

Orsen looked at the purse. It was a lot of money. It was, in fact, roughly triple what the chest could possibly be worth to anyone operating legally, which told her everything about how illegal this was going to be, and how thoroughly the syndicate intended for there to be no loose threads afterward. A bonus for forgetting. People who wanted you to forget usually planned to make sure you couldn't remember.

"Triple rate," she said. "Generous."

"We're generous people." He pushed the purse an inch toward her. "Well? You haul things. This is a thing. Haul it."

And here was the moment. Six days of river, one ambush, one confession, one girl with a dead country's eyes, and here at last was the exit, paid for in advance, clean and simple. Take the purse. Hand over the chest. Walk back to the Anvil's Rest, split the money, point the Wake downstream, and be nobody again by spring. That was the plan. That had always been the plan. She'd said it out loud not three hours ago, words in a row, a door closing.

Orsen reached for the purse.

Her hand stopped.

Not hesitated—*stopped*, the way a hand stops when it finds a stove is hot, some reflex older than decision. She looked at the silver and all she could see was Tamsin's face this morning, nodding slowly, confirming something sad. And Pell teaching her knots on the deck without ever once explaining why, just standing closer each day until closeness was a fact. And Hale on his knees among his maps, saying *I can show you the route* like a man offering the only coin he had left.

"Well?" the scarred man said.

"The chest isn't mine to sell," Orsen heard herself say.

Her own voice sounded surprised.

The smile left his face by degrees, like a tide going out. "Whose is it, then?"

"A client's. I deliver. I don't divest."

"Pilot." He leaned forward, and the room's noise seemed to lean with him. "You're a sensible woman. Sensible women retire rich. Unsensible women—" he spread his hands, apologetic, "—well. Rivers are dangerous. Glaciers especially. So many ways to fall."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a weather report." He tapped the purse once, leaving it on the table. "Keep that. Buy your crew a meal. Think it over. Generous people give second chances." He stood, and his companions stood with him, unhurried. "But only the one."

They left. Orsen sat there with the purse in front of her like a dare, and realized her heart was going hard, and realized something worse: she wasn't afraid for herself. Eleven years of self-preservation and the fear had finally found a door she'd left open somewhere, and it had walked right in and started rearranging the furniture.

She picked up the purse. She'd need the silver—just not for what they thought.

* * *

Back at the Anvil's Rest, the room was lamplit and quiet. Tamsin had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-argument apparently, her knife still in her hand and her head tipped against the wall. Hale was asleep too, sprawled across half his maps like a scholar felled mid-sentence. Pell sat by the window, awake, watching the street, because Pell always watched the street.

He glanced at her. Took in her face. Took in the purse.

"They found you," he said.

"They found me." She dropped the purse on the table between them. "That's their opening offer. Triple rate plus a bonus for amnesia."

"And?"

"And I told them the chest wasn't mine to sell."

Pell was quiet a long moment. Outside, a mule train bell clanked somewhere in the dark, going nowhere until morning. "Eleven years," he said finally. "You ever turn down money before?"

"Constantly. Money comes with attachments."

"This money came with a way out."

"I know what it came with."

"Do you?" He turned back to the window. His voice stayed level, but Orsen had known him eleven years and could hear the seam in it. "Because I've been watching you not-sell that chest since Gray Landing, and I'll tell you what I see. I see a woman building a case against her own plan. Every day another reason. First it was the price wasn't right. Then it was the buyers weren't safe. Now it's the chest isn't yours to sell." He shook his head slowly. "You could've sold it any time. You keep finding doors and not walking through them, and calling it bad luck."

Orsen sat down across from him. Downstairs, the innkeeper's dog barked once at nothing and gave up.

"When did you figure it out?" she asked.

"Figure what out?"

"That I don't have a plan anymore."

Pell almost smiled. On Pell's face it was a seismic event. "About the same time you did," he said. "Which was somewhere around the fog banks, if I had to guess. Maybe the shallows. Hard to pin down. These things creep up on a person."

Above them, the floor creaked—Tamsin turning in her sleep, or getting up, or listening; impossible to say. Orsen looked at the chest under the window, at the drowned seal of Vhalen stamped into its lid, at the whole stubborn weight of it sitting in her life like a passenger who'd refused every port.

"Syndicate riders are already ahead of us," she said. "Brakka will know. Someone always knows. And the pass opens when it opens, which means we're stuck in this town with three men in expensive boots and nothing to do but wait for them to get creative."

"So we don't wait," Pell said. "We get permits. Legal crossing papers. A mule train contract. Paper is armor, up here. Even syndicate men think twice about killing people with documents."

"Permits need leverage. Leverage costs."

"We've got a chest full of leverage."

Orsen looked at him. "You want to trade pieces of it."

"I want us alive on the other side of that glacier. You want—" he paused, choosing, "—whatever it is you want, which you'll tell me eventually or you won't. Either way, we need paper."

From the floor, without opening her eyes, Tamsin said: "I know an archivist."

Orsen froze. "How long have you been awake?"

"Since you came in smelling like other people's beer." Tamsin opened her eyes, gray and unbothered. "And I've been thinking. The syndicate offered you triple rate, which means they're scared, which means the chest is worth more than they're pretending, which means we shouldn't sell *any* of it—we should spend it. Carefully. Like coin. One page at a time." She sat up, knife disappearing into her belt with the ease of long practice. "There's an archivist here named Master Ellery who handles crossing permits for the mountain clans. He's also the kind of man who'd trade his teeth for a document with the Vhalen seal on it. My mother told me about him."

"Your mother," Orsen said carefully, "told you a lot of things."

"My mother," Tamsin said, "told me true things. That was mostly her problem."

She got up, crossed to the window, and looked out at the mountain looming black against the stars, snowline faintly visible even in the dark, like a border drawn on the world.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we go see a man about a country."

Orsen should have corrected her. Should have said *tomorrow we go see a man about permits, and permits are all this is.* Instead she heard herself say:

"First we buy breakfast. Real breakfast. Eggs, if this town has eggs."

Tamsin turned, and for a second—just a second—she grinned, quick and bright and young, and Orsen felt that thing in her chest again, the one she'd been calling indigestion for six days, and understood, with the calm of a woman reading water she'd sworn not to enter, that it had never been indigestion at all.

Upstairs, in the dark, Hale slept on top of his maps. And far to the south, Orsen would learn soon enough, the Vesper's Wake sat quiet at her mooring with strangers' hands already on her lines—but that grief hadn't arrived yet. Tonight there was only the mountain, the snow coming down off it earlier than any year Brakka could remember, and a purse of syndicate silver that Orsen intended to spend making their lives difficult.

"Get some sleep," she told the room, her crew, whatever they were. "Tomorrow we buy a country breakfast and forge some paperwork."

Nobody argued. That, too, was new.
