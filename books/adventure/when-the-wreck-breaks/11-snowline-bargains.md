## 11. Snowline Bargains

Coldharbour smelled of mule dung, pine pitch, and other people's money.

Tamsin had been in mountain towns before—she'd been born in one, or near enough that the distinction didn't matter—but she'd never been in one that smelled like it was *bracing itself*. The whole port seemed to lean northward, toward the white line on the peaks above the rooftops, the way a dog leans toward a door it knows will open soon. Mule trains clattered up the switchback road in a steady procession, loaded with iron ingots and salt pork and coils of rope, everything a body needed to cross the Corvane Range before winter shut the pass like a book. Everything except the paper that let you cross at all.

"Permits," Orsen said, in the tone of a woman reading her own death warrant aloud for practice.

They stood at the foot of the permit office's steps, all four of them, and Tamsin watched the queue ahead: miners with frostbitten knuckles, a wool merchant arguing with his own ledger, two men whose coats were too good for this street and whose eyes were too busy. Syndicate men. She'd learned their look on the river—the way they stood like furniture that expected to be sat upon.

"Permits," Pell echoed, and spat, artistically, into the gutter.

"It's not the fee I mind," Orsen said. "It's the questions. Name, business, destination, next of kin." She said *next of kin* the way other people said *gangrene*.

"You could lie," Tamsin offered.

"I could. But the syndicate owns half the clerks in this town, and the other half owe them money. A lie is just a truth wearing a disguise, waiting to be recognized at an inconvenient moment." Orsen rubbed the bridge of her nose. "We need leverage. Real leverage. Something worth more to somebody here than whatever the syndicate pays them to remember our faces."

Tamsin felt the chest's weight before she remembered she wasn't carrying it. That was the trouble with traveling with Orsen Ravensworth—you stopped noticing what your hands were doing because hers were always already doing it. The chest rode in the Vesper's Wake's old strongbox now, under a false floor of tarred canvas, in a rented room above a chandlery that smelled of hemp and mouse. But Tamsin carried it anyway. In her ribs. She suspected she always would.

"There's the archivist," Hale said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He had a way of saying things quietly that made them land louder than if he'd shouted—that was a surveyor's trick, Tamsin decided. Men who measured mountains learned that the small precise thing outlasts the loud vague thing.

"The provincial archive," Hale went on. "Third building past the customs house, the one with the copper roof gone green. There's a man named Ellery Voss who keeps it. He was there when I was. Before." His jaw did the thing it did when he stepped near the word *before*, like a man testing ice. "He collects Vhalen material. Obsessively. Illegally, some would say—he buys from deserters, smugglers, grave-robbers. If anyone in Coldharbour would trade permits for a page of the old country, it's Voss."

"And how," Orsen said, "do you know so much about a man you claim you haven't spoken to in fifteen years?"

Hale looked at the mule train grinding past, at the snowline stitched bright against the blue sky, and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. Tamsin had catalogued Hale's silences the way he must once have catalogued ridgelines. This one meant: *because some things you never stop watching, even when you've sworn you're done.*

Orsen read it too. Tamsin saw it cross her face—not softness exactly, but the recognition of a fellow professional. Two people who understood that watching was a form of love, and also a way to avoid it.

"Fine," Orsen said. "We talk to the archivist. Nobody promises anything. And nobody—" her eyes found Tamsin's and held there, "—nobody gives away anything we can't afford to lose."

*Anything we can't afford to lose.* Tamsin nodded and smiled and agreed, and inside her chest, where the crucial page lived folded against her heart in an oilcloth sleeve, something small and cold and very old whispered: *we'll see.*

She wasn't proud of it. She was learning that pride and strategy were different animals that happened to share a den.

* * *

The archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of cedar—Vhalen cedar, Tamsin realized with a jolt, from the document cases lining the walls. Her grandmother had kept letters in a box made of the same wood, and the smell hit her like a hand laid flat between the shoulder blades. Steadying. Claiming.

Ellery Voss turned out to be a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and spectacles repaired with wire, who received them in a back room stacked floor to ceiling with papers that no empire had any right to still exist. Maps. Tax rolls. A christening gown pressed flat under glass. A nation in exile, filed alphabetically.

"You're the river people," Voss said, before anyone introduced them. "The ones with the chest. Half the town's talking about it. The other half has been paid not to."

"Then we'll be brief," Orsen said.

"Briefness is a luxury of people without archives." Voss took off his spectacles, polished them on his cuff, put them back on. "Ask me what I want."

Tamsin had planned to let Orsen do the talking. Orsen was better at it; Orsen could make silence do half the work. But Voss's eyes had gone past her, past Hale, and settled on Tamsin with the particular hunger of a man who had spent years collecting the ashes of a fire and had just seen someone walk in still warm from it.

"You have her chin," Voss said. "The Grand Duchess Maren. You have her exact chin. There's a portrait in the third case there, water-damaged, but the chin survived."

Tamsin's heart performed a maneuver she chose not to dignify by naming. "I get that a lot," she said. "Big family. Lots of chins going around."

It was the wrong thing to say and the right thing to say, both at once, and she knew it, and Voss knew she knew it, and something passed between them that was almost respect. A liar recognizing craftsmanship.

"What I want," Voss said, settling behind his desk like a man taking his seat at a card table he'd built himself, "is the correspondence. The autumn dispatches. Every collection I've ever bought has fragments—a signature here, a seal fragment there. But the dispatches, the actual letters between the Chancellery and the northern garrisons in the last month before the treaty—if those exist, they don't just prove Vhalen existed. They prove *who killed it*. Names. The names are what matter. Dead countries stay dead until someone says the murderer's name out loud."

The room got very quiet. Hale had gone the color of old paper himself. Tamsin kept her face pleasant and empty as a hired room.

"And what," Orsen said carefully, "would a man pay for such a thing?"

"Pay?" Voss laughed, short and dry. "I can't pay coin. I can pay access. Permits for four across the Corvane, stamped and sealed, no questions asked—which, given that the syndicate has agents asking questions at every checkpoint, is worth more than coin right now. I can pay lodging through the calving season. I can pay silence—I keep better secrets than any priest on this river, because priests answer to gods and I answer only to the filing system." He leaned forward. "And I can pay in kind. I have documents too. Things a girl with that chin might find... useful. Family things."

There it was. The hook, baited with exactly the right bait, dropped into exactly the right water. Tamsin felt it set behind her teeth.

*Family things.*

She thought of the chest in the strongbox above the chandlery. She thought of the dispatches inside it—eleven letters, and among them the one that mattered, the one with the name, the one she'd moved to the oilcloth sleeve three nights ago while Pell snored and the river muttered against the hull. She hadn't decided then. She'd told herself she was only *reorganizing*.

Liar, said the small cold voice. Strategist, she corrected it. Same animal, better manners.

"We'll consider your offer," Orsen said, in the voice she used when a deal was already dead but she was too polite to bury it in front of witnesses.

"Consider quickly," Voss said. "The pass opens early this year. Everyone will want through at once, and permits will get scarce as honesty." He smiled at Tamsin, thin and knowing. "Come back alone, girl with the chin. Some bargains go easier without the crew."

* * *

"He wants the dispatches," Orsen said that evening, pacing the rented room like a cat deciding whether the furniture was beneath her dignity. "All of them. Which means handing the whole truth to a stranger in a town crawling with syndicate ears, and trusting that green roof and a filing system to keep us alive."

"He keeps Vhalen cedar," Tamsin said. "He presses christening gowns under glass. Men like that don't sell what they love."

"Men like that don't sell what they love. Men like that *hoard* it. Hoarding is selling with extra steps, once the price gets high enough." Orsen stopped pacing. "What did he mean, come back alone?"

"He meant he'd rather bargain with me than with you."

"Why?"

"Because I'm cheaper," Tamsin said, "and easier to frighten, and he thinks I'm desperate. He looked at me and saw a starving girl who'd trade her grandmother's bones for a warm bed."

"Were you insulted?"

"A little."

"Good. Insulted means thinking clearly." Orsen sat down heavily on the room's single chair, which complained. "Here's my position, and I'll say it once. We give him copies. Pages that matter less. Enough to satisfy him, enough to buy the permits, and the rest stays with us over the pass. That's the whole offer. Take it or leave it."

And there it was—the moment Tamsin had been walking toward for three days without letting herself look down.

Because the pages that mattered least were easy to copy. She'd done it already, hunched over the chandlery counter with a stolen pen, reproducing two garrison letters in her careful schoolroom hand while pretending to write a letter home. Home. The word had made her laugh into her sleeve. But the page that mattered most—the dispatch bearing the signature, the name that detonated halfway through Hale's old expedition roster—couldn't be copied convincingly. The watermark ran wrong. The ink aged wrong. Voss would know within a minute, and Voss would tell whoever paid him most, and the name would be in syndicate hands before they reached the snowline.

So: give Voss the true copies of the lesser pages, and keep the original of the one that mattered—and let everyone believe the originals had all changed hands.

It required lying to Orsen. Not loudly. Just by letting her believe a thing that was almost true.

Tamsin looked at Orsen—at the woman who'd pulled her out of the Gray Landing shallows by the collar without being asked, who'd stood between her and syndicate guns in the fog, who refused passengers at docks because refusal was armor and armor was all she had. Orsen, who had said, exactly once, in the dark after the chase, *you eat first, growing girls need it,* and had pretended afterward it hadn't happened.

If Tamsin told her the plan—all of it—Orsen would forbid it. Or worse, Orsen would agree to it, and then Tamsin would owe her, and debts were how the syndicate owned people, and Tamsin had sworn on a drowned country's grave that no one would ever hold paper on her again.

If Tamsin didn't tell her, and it worked, everyone crossed the pass free and clear, and the secret stayed a small private stone in Tamsin's boot where it bothered no one but her.

Secrets multiply, her grandmother used to say. Like mice in a granary. You never end up with one.

"I'll take it to him tomorrow," Tamsin said. "Alone, like he asked. Copies of the lesser pages. He won't know the difference."

Orsen studied her for a long moment, and Tamsin felt the look go through her like a sounding line through dark water, touching bottom, finding it deeper than expected.

"All right," Orsen said finally. "But Tamsin—" and here her voice did something rare, something unarmored, "—whatever you're carrying that you think protects us, remember that I've run cargo my whole life. I know what hidden weight does to a boat in rough water. It doesn't protect anything. It capsizes her."

"I'm not hiding anything," Tamsin said, and was impressed by how steady it came out.

"Mm," said Orsen Ravensworth, who had read sandbars like faces for eleven years and was, Tamsin realized with sinking certainty, currently reading hers.

* * *

The exchange with Voss went the next morning like a well-oiled lock. He spread her copies under his lamp, ran a loupe over the watermarks, hummed, frowned, hummed again—and Tamsin stood with her heart hammering a rhythm she hoped read as eagerness rather than guilt, and told herself the lesser pages were real enough, aged enough, *true enough*, because wasn't a copy of the truth still mostly truth? Wasn't she keeping only the one page, the necessary page, the page that named the man whose name would otherwise buy their deaths?

Voss signed the permits. Four crossings, no questions, stamped with a seal older than the province. He slid them across the desk, then held one finger on the topmost sheet.

"For the record," he said mildly, "the watermark on the second letter runs wrong. Whoever copied it pressed too hard on the downstrokes. A nervous hand." He looked up over his wire-mended spectacles. "I'm not asking what you kept, girl. I'm telling you I know you kept *something*, and that I find it interesting, and that interested men can be useful allies or expensive enemies, depending entirely on whether the person who fooled them ever needs a favor again."

Tamsin's mouth was dry. "Then let's hope I never need one."

"Oh, you will." Voss smiled and released the permits. "Everyone crossing the Corvane this season needs a favor eventually. The mountain sees to it. Ask your surveyor—he'll tell you."

She walked out into the cold bright street with the permits against her palm and the crucial page against her heart, and she felt—there was no honest word for it but *triumphant*, and triumphant felt filthy, and filthy felt like winning. She'd done it. She'd played the archivist, protected the name, secured the road. Secrets multiplying like mice, and every one of them hers, and wasn't that what survival was?

She was three streets from the archive, rehearsing the innocent version of events for Orsen, when she heard the bells.

Not church bells. Harbor bells—the alarm pattern, the one Pell had taught her during the fog chase, tapping out *riders in sight* across the water. Except Coldharbour had no harbor to speak of, just a frozen landing and the mule road, which meant the bells weren't for boats.

They were for the pass.

People were pouring out of doorways, pointing north. Tamsin followed their fingers to the peaks, where the snowline lay sharp and white against the morning—and where, above it, a plume of cloud was rising off the glacier saddle, fat and fast-moving, dragging a gray skirt of weather behind it.

A storm. Coming early. Coming *now*.

And threading through the crowd toward her, moving fast, faces grim, she spotted Orsen, Pell, and Hale—and behind them, at a distance that was trying very hard to be casual, two men in coats too good for this street.

Syndicate men. Walking their way.

Tamsin clutched the permits and the secret page and started to run.
