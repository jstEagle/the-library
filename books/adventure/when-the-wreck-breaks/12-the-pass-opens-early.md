## 12. The Pass Opens Early

Davor Hale had spent the better part of thirty years learning to read mountains, and in all that time he had never once been thanked for it. Surveyors were like lighthouse keepers: essential until the moment they were noticed, and blamed for everything the light failed to prevent. So when the boy from the archivist's office came pounding up the stairs of the Salt and Cinder with news clutched in his fist like a live coal, Hale's first instinct was to check whether the blame had finally caught up with him.

It hadn't. It was better than that. It was worse.

"The pass," the boy gasped, bending over his knees. "The glacier route. Spotters on the ridge say the ice bridge at Corvane Saddle is standing solid—earliest opening in nine years. The assessor's office is posting permits tomorrow at dawn."

Hale set down his tea. Around him, the common room of the Salt and Cinder went quiet in that particular way mountain towns go quiet—not silent, but listening, every ear tilted toward the news like flowers toward sun.

"Nine years," someone said reverently.

"Storm system behind it," the boy added, and the reverence curdled. "Coming down out of the north. The assessor says the window could be half what it usually is. Maybe less. Maybe four days of safe crossing instead of ten."

Four days. Hale did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four days meant leaving tomorrow or not leaving at all. Four days meant no more dithering in Coldharbour's warm taverns, no more telling himself they'd decide once the mule prices settled, once Tamsin's cough cleared, once Orsen finished pretending she wasn't going to hand off the chest and walk away from all of them.

He looked across the room to where the others sat. Pell was in the corner sharpening a knife with the patience of a man who found peace in repetition. Tamsin was hunched over a table with the archivist's ledger open before her, copying something in her careful, secretive hand. And Orsen—

Orsen was staring into her cup like it owed her money.

Hale gathered the boy's notice, pressed a coin into his palm, and carried the news to their table the way a man carries a diagnosis.

* * *

"You're certain," Orsen said. It wasn't a question so much as a dare. She dared things to be wrong. It was, Hale had come to understand, how she loved the world: by challenging it to disappoint her.

"I'm certain the boy was certain," Hale said. "And I'm certain about the storm. I can smell it." He tapped his nose. "Thirty years of surveying will do that to you. You stop trusting instruments and start trusting your own face. The air's gone flat. There's a mineral edge to it, like a struck flint. Somewhere north of us there's a wall of weather leaning against the sky, and when it tips over, it'll tip over fast."

"And the ice bridge?"

"Standing now. That's the trap of it." He pulled a chair around backward and sat, arms folded on its backrest. "A calving season opens early when the winter was cold and dry. Sounds like a gift. But an early opening under a storm system means the meltwater starts moving before the ice has finished settling. The bridge at the Saddle holds while it's frozen through. The moment the sun gets three good days at it—or one bad storm loads it with snow—it lets go. We wouldn't get the usual grace period. We'd get the window, and nothing after."

Tamsin looked up from her ledger. "How long to cross, if everything goes right?"

"Six days to the far side, if we move well. Eight if we're cautious. Nine if someone falls in a crevasse and we have to fish them out." He said it lightly. He'd learned long ago that the only way to speak about crevasse rescue in front of people who hadn't seen one was to make it sound like fishing.

Nobody laughed. Pell didn't even look up from his knife, but the whetstone paused for exactly one stroke, which from Pell was the equivalent of dropping a glass.

"So," Orsen said. "Tomorrow or never."

"Tomorrow," Hale corrected gently, "or we winter here. With the syndicate agents who've been counting our boots since the day we arrived. With the harbor men who know exactly what we're carrying and exactly which room it sleeps in." He let that sit. "I've been on the wrong side of a siege before, Captain. Sieges are just ambushes with worse food."

Orsen turned her cup a slow quarter-turn on the table. Hale watched her do it and understood he was watching a woman negotiate with herself. Eleven years, Tamsin had told him, running the Kestrel. Eleven years of keeping the world at oar's length. And now the river was frozen, the boat was docked, and the only road out of Coldharbour led straight through the country whose ghost she'd been hauling around in a chest.

"We'd need mules," she said at last. "Four, minimum. Rope. A proper tent—the one we have leaks like a politician. And someone in this town has to sell us all of it tonight, because tomorrow morning every outfitter between here and the Saddle is going to triple his prices."

"They already have," Pell said quietly. "I checked at noon."

Orsen closed her eyes briefly, as if asking some private deity for strength and knowing better than to expect it. "Of course you did."

* * *

They spent the afternoon in the particular frenzy that precedes all irreversible decisions: the frenzy of shopping.

Hale took the rope and hardware, partly because he knew cordage better than any of them and partly because bargaining with outfitters was the one social arena where his disgrace worked in his favor. Merchants trusted a ruined man. A ruined man couldn't afford to cheat you; his reputation was already ash, and ash burns only once. By mid-afternoon he had sixty fathoms of good hemp, two ice axes, a bundle of pitons he'd forged himself in a previous life, and a merchant's handshake that cost him nothing but the story of his downfall, told twice, with self-deprecating flourishes.

Tamsin took the provisions list and attacked it with the ruthlessness of someone who had been hungry and intended never to be again. She weighed dried meat in her hands like a jeweler assessing stones. She rejected an entire wheel of cheese on grounds Hale couldn't follow—"it smells like optimism"—and bought twice the hardtack anyone thought reasonable.

"It's not too much," she informed him, catching his look. "You've never crossed a pass. On a pass, food is morale. Morale is warmth. Warmth is life. I did the sums."

"She did the sums," Hale agreed solemnly. "The girl has done the sums."

Pell took the mule problem, because Pell had a way with animals that he entirely lacked with people. He stood in the livery yard for the better part of an hour saying nothing to four different mules, and the mules, one by one, decided they liked him. He came back leading a string of four with the smugness of a man who has won an argument without having one.

Which left Orsen with the hardest purchase of all, and she knew it, and everyone knew she knew it, which was why she'd assigned it to herself rather than admit it frightened her.

Permits.

Hale found her at the assessor's office an hour before dusk, standing at the counter with her jaw set like a woman biting down on a splint. Behind the counter, a clerk with ink-stained fingers was explaining, with the serene cruelty of bureaucracy, that crossing permits for the Corvane route required proof of purpose, letters of sponsorship, and a fee assessed per head plus per beast plus, apparently, per hundredweight of cargo.

"Cargo," Orsen repeated dangerously.

"Sealed freight carries additional liability, madam. If your party is lost on the ice, the crown must fund recovery operations—"

"If my party is lost on the ice," Orsen said, "the crown is welcome to send whoever it likes to recover us, and I promise you they'll be recovering considerably less than a hundredweight of anything."

Hale stepped up beside her and laid his old surveyor's seal on the counter. It was brass, worn nearly smooth, stamped with the crest of the Imperial Cartographic Service—a crest that had opened doors for him once, then slammed them, and now hung somewhere in between, ambiguous as everything else about him.

"Davor Hale," he said. "Formerly senior surveyor, Corvane Range expedition. I'm escorting this party as route consultant. My credentials should satisfy your proof-of-purpose requirement. And I'll sign personally for the cargo."

The clerk looked at the seal. Then at Hale. Then, with the delicacy of a man defusing something, back at the seal.

"Hale," the clerk said. "The Corvane Hale."

"There's only one," Hale said pleasantly, "and I'm doing my best to keep it that way."

There was a long moment in which Hale felt the whole weight of his history balance on the counter between them—the maps, the betrayal, the drowned nation, the inquiry, the disgrace, all of it compressed into a clerk's raised eyebrow. Then the clerk pulled out a fresh form.

"Consultant status waives the cargo surcharge," he said. "Sign here, here, and initial there."

Outside, Orsen exhaled through her teeth. "You enjoyed that."

"No," Hale said, and found, to his surprise, that it was mostly true. "But I'll take useful over enjoyable. Useful keeps me on the boat."

"You don't have a boat."

"I'm working on it."

* * *

That night the Salt and Cinder filled up with every climber, trader, and fool in Coldharbour who'd heard the same news, and the talk was all of the pass. Hale sat with his back to the wall—he always sat with his back to the wall—and listened to the room the way he used to listen to weather.

Most of it was ordinary greed and bravado. But threaded through it, thin as wire, was the other thing: syndicate men, three of them, at a table near the fire, drinking slowly and talking less. They weren't discussing the pass. They were watching the door. And every time one of Hale's party moved—Pell going out to see to the mules, Tamsin fetching water—they watched that too.

Hale leaned toward Orsen and pitched his voice under the room's noise. "Our friends by the fire."

"I see them."

"They're not here to drink."

"Nobody in this town is here to drink. Half these climbers are syndicate eyes. The other half are broke and would sell us for boot leather." She turned her cup that quarter-turn again. Her tell, Hale had catalogued it. "If we leave tomorrow, we leave in the dark. Before first light. Mules loaded the night before."

"They'll expect that."

"They'll expect it and they still won't like the cold enough to follow immediately. Men who watch doors are men who wait for orders, and orders come from warm offices." She glanced at him sideways. "You've crossed the Saddle before. In bad conditions. Tell me honestly—can you get five amateurs and four mules over that ice alive?"

Hale considered lying, considered softening it, and discarded both, because she'd hear either one.

"I can get us to the Saddle," he said. "After the Saddle, it's the crevasse fields, and there's no honest answer to those except rope discipline and luck. I've buried friends in that ice, Orsen. I won't pretend it's a road. It's a wager. But it's the best wager on the table, and the house closes in four days."

Across the table, Tamsin had stopped writing. She was looking at the fire with an expression Hale recognized from mirrors: a young person discovering that the past is not a story you read but a place you can walk into.

"My mother crossed that pass," she said quietly. "When Vhalen fell. She was seventeen. She came down the far side alone."

The room went on around them—dice, laughter, the syndicate men's slow glasses—but at their table it had gone very still.

"Then she left you a map of a kind," Hale said. "Not drawn on paper. Drawn in the fact that she made it. Seventeen years old, alone, in the worst year of her country's life, and she came down the other side. Whatever else the ice takes from us, it can't take that precedent."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. "You say things like that," she said finally, "like a man who's practiced apologizing."

"I've had practice," Hale admitted. "Thirty years of it. Some of it earned."

Something passed across her face—not forgiveness, not yet, but a stay of proceedings. From Tamsin, after chapter eight of their shared history, that was practically a pardon.

* * *

They were loading the mules by lamplight in the livery yard when the storm announced itself properly: not wind, not snow, just a sudden pressure in the ears and a smell like iron, the whole northern sky faintly luminous though no stars showed. Hale stood in the yard with his face tipped up and felt thirty years fall away. He knew that sky. He had mapped beneath that sky. It was the sky of the last expedition, the one that ended with his name on the wrong page of history.

"Beautiful," he murmured.

"It wants to kill us," Pell said beside him. Pell moved quietly for a large man; Hale was getting used to being startled by him.

"Everything beautiful does. That's not a reason to stop looking." Hale checked the lashings on the second mule's pack, tightened a knot Pell had deliberately tied loose for him to find—an old deckhand's test, and Hale suspected he'd passed it weeks ago without noticing. "You could stay, you know. Nobody would think less of you. The boat's docked for winter regardless. You could sit out the season right here and meet us on the far side come thaw, if we make it."

Pell was quiet for a while. Down the yard, Orsen was arguing with a mule about a saddle strap and losing.

"She'd stay," Pell said at last. "If we stayed, she'd find a reason. She's been looking for reasons to stop for eleven years. This town is full of them—warm rooms, syndicate men, a chest she could sell tomorrow and be free of all of us." He picked up the lead rope. "So we go. Not because of the pass. Because of her."

Hale watched him lead the mule toward the gate and thought, not for the first time, that the quiet ones always saw the shape of a thing first. The loud ones—himself included—were still arguing about details.

* * *

They gathered one last time in the common room, past midnight, over a map Hale had drawn himself from memory: the Corvane approach, the Saddle, the serac field, the crevasse country beyond. His hand shook slightly drawing the contour lines—not age, he told himself, though it might have been; memory, more likely, because the last time he'd drawn these lines, men had died following them.

"Here's the truth of it," he said, tapping the map. "The window is four days, maybe five. Day one, easy trail to the high camp. Day two, the Saddle—we cross the ice bridge at dawn, when it's coldest and strongest. Day three and four, the crevasse fields, roped at all times, no exceptions, no heroics. Day five, the descent. If we're not off the ice by the evening of day five, the storm finds us up there, and the storm doesn't negotiate."

"And if someone can't go on?" Tamsin asked.

Hale met her eyes. "Then we carry them. That's the whole of mountaineering ethics, girl, and it fits in one sentence: nobody gets left on the ice."

Orsen studied the map a long time. Hale watched her finger trace the route—down, along, up, across—and saw her arrive, visibly, at the point of no return. She'd been circling it for days. Commitment, for a woman like Orsen Ravensworth, was not a decision. It was a surrender she had to dress up as tactics.

"First light," she said. "We're on the trail before the syndicate's had its breakfast. And if any of you change your minds overnight—" she looked around the table, at each of them in turn, and something in her face almost softened, almost, before the armor came back down— "don't. Because I'm not saying this twice, and I'm not waiting."

She stood, drained her cup, and went up to bed like a woman marching to a execution she'd personally scheduled.

Tamsin grinned after her. "She's terrified."

"She's ready," Hale said. "In my experience those look identical from the outside."

Pell said nothing at all. But he collected the mule leads and set them by the door, ready for morning, and that was its own kind of speech.

Hale banked the fire and stood a moment in the darkening room, looking north through the window at the luminous, iron-smelling sky. Somewhere up there, the glacier was groaning through its calving season, opening a door it would slam shut behind them. Tomorrow they walked through it, and whatever waited on the far side—Vhalen's valley, the truth in the chest, the syndicate, the storm—they would meet it roped together, for better or worse, which was the only way anyone had ever survived anything worth surviving.

He blew out the lamp.

Behind him, upstairs, he heard Orsen's boots hit the floor, and then—faintly, unmistakably—Tamsin laughing at something, and Pell's low rumble answering, and the small domestic music of people becoming, against all their intentions, a crew.

The clock started now. Hale smiled in the dark, and went up to pack.
