## 13. Switchbacks and Old Wounds

The first thing Orsen Ravensworth learned about mountains was that they lied.

Rivers lied too—she'd built a career on knowing exactly how—but rivers lied in ways she understood. A sandbar shifted its shoulders overnight, a current told you one thing with its surface and another with its pull beneath. But a river never pretended to be still when it was moving. Mountains did it constantly. They stood there looking solid as a promise, and then the ground under your boot simply *decided* to be a slope.

She discovered this on the morning of the second day, forty minutes out of the last waystation above Coldharbour, when the trail crossed its first patch of old ice shaded under an overhang of granite. Her left foot went forward with all the confidence of a woman who had walked decks in gale-force spray for eleven years, and the mountain said, *no.*

She went down hard enough to see stars that weren't stars. Her tailbone filed a formal complaint. Somewhere behind her, the mule carrying their gear made a sound that could only be described as editorial.

"Don't get up fast," Tamsin said.

Orsen was already getting up fast, which is how she went down a second time, this time catching herself on both palms against ice so cold it burned. She lay there a moment, cheek nearly touching the frozen trail, considering the geological strata of her own humiliation.

"I'm fine," she announced to the mountain.

"Nobody said you weren't," Tamsin said. "I said don't get up fast."

Pell appeared at the edge of her vision, offered a hand without comment, and hauled her upright with the easy strength he used for everything, including breathing. He looked at her boots. He looked at the ice. He looked at her with an expression she'd seen him give cargo that had been loaded wrong.

"They're river boots," she said defensively.

"Yep."

"They've been fine for eleven years."

"Yep."

"You could offer some actual words, Pell."

He considered this. "Ice is slicker than deck," he said, which from Pell constituted a soliloquy, and moved off up the trail leading the mule.

Tamsin crouched by the patch of ice and knocked her knuckles against it. Up here, away from the river country, the girl moved differently than she had aboard the *Vesper's Wake*—looser, quicker, like something that had been kept in too small a bottle and was only now remembering its shape. On the boat she'd been a stowaway-shaped problem. Here she was plainly in her element, and the difference was startling.

"This is glacier melt," Tamsin said. "It refreezes every night and sweats loose every afternoon. So we cross the icy stretches in the morning, before the sun gets at them." She glanced back at Orsen, and there was something carefully neutral in her face—the particular neutrality of a nineteen-year-old working very hard not to laugh at someone she needed. "You'll learn it. You read water better than anyone I've ever seen. This is just water that forgot how to move."

"It didn't forget," Orsen muttered, testing her weight on the ice with the exaggerated caution of the recently betrayed. "It's sulking."

That got a real laugh out of the girl, quick and surprised, gone almost before it arrived. Orsen found herself absurdly pleased about it and immediately distrusted the pleasure. Attachment was a current, and currents took you places you hadn't agreed to go.

They climbed.

* * *

By the third day Orsen had catalogued her failures with a pilot's thoroughness. She could not walk on scree; scree was gravel that had ambitions. She could not judge distance across a valley, because mountain air was honest in a way river fog wasn't, and things that looked a stone's throw away were half a morning's climb. She could not sleep at altitude—the silence woke her, vast and airless, no current muttering under the hull, no rope creak, none of the thousand small voices a riverboat used to tell its pilot it was still alive. She lay in her bedroll listening to nothing and felt, absurdly, like a helmsman whose wheel had been removed mid-crossing.

Worst of all—and she admitted this to no one, least of all herself—was the switchbacks.

The trail above the tree line clung to the mountainside in long hairpin legs, each turn revealing more of where they'd been and less of where they were going. A river taught you patience in a straight line: bend after bend, but always forward, always the same water carrying you. Switchbacks were patience folded over itself. Every twenty minutes of climbing delivered you directly above ground you'd already covered, close enough to spit on, and then turned you away from it again. It was like being taunted by geography.

On the fourth day, halfway up a leg of switchback with the sun hammering the rock and Hale wheezing behind them—he was healing from the wound at Coldharbour, but altitude taxed him worse than any of them would say aloud—Orsen stopped, put her hands on her knees, and said, "This is a scam. Someone sold us this trail twice."

"We're making good time," Tamsin said.

"We're making *circular* time."

"That's how mountains work." The girl had stopped a few paces above her, and she was looking down the slope they'd just climbed with an expression Orsen couldn't read. Then, quietly: "My mother used to say the switchbacks are the mountain being kind. It could just go straight up. It gives you these instead. Room to breathe. Room to look back."

"Did your mother climb much?"

"She carried salt over this range twice a year from the time she was fourteen until—" Tamsin stopped. Recalibrated. "Until she didn't."

The gap where Vhalen's name should have been opened between them, as it did several times a day now, familiar as a bad tooth. Below them, tiny with distance, Hale sat on a rock pretending his lungs weren't filing grievances, and Pell stood beside the mule with the patience of a fence post.

"Room to look back," Orsen repeated. She squinted down the slope. From here she could see the whole lower valley they'd crawled out of, the gray thread of the river they'd followed north, and—far off, a brown fleck on the water at Coldharbour's docks—a shape she refused to let her eyes linger on. She'd been looking for it anyway, every switchback, every day. Checking whether the *Vesper's Wake* was still tied where they'd left her. Knowing, in the place where pilots keep the knowledge they don't say out loud, that syndicate agents had been thick as flies in Coldharbour, and that a boat left alone was a boat already lost.

"You keep checking," Tamsin said.

"Hm?"

"The boat. Every time we come around a turn you look downstream. You think I haven't noticed?" The girl shrugged, shouldering her pack higher. "I used to do the same thing in Harrowgate. Look toward the border every morning. Like if I watched long enough, the country might come back on its own."

Orsen said nothing. The wind came up the slope and pushed at them both, patient as tide.

"It doesn't work," Tamsin said. "Looking back. But my mother was right about one part—you have to keep doing it anyway, or the leaving eats you different. Worse." She started walking again, sure-footed on the broken rock, and threw the last of it over her shoulder like it cost her nothing: "Come on, pilot. The ice fields start tomorrow, and you're going to need every hour of practice you can get."

* * *

The practice began that evening and was, unexpectedly, Tamsin's idea.

They made camp early in a hollow below the ridge, sheltered from the wind, and while Pell saw to the mule and Hale dozed propped against his pack with his wound-wrapped side carefully arranged, Tamsin cleared a stretch of gravel near the fire ring and stamped it flat.

"Boots off," she said.

Orsen stared at her. "It's freezing."

"That's the point. Cold makes you careful." Tamsin was already unlacing her own boots, peeling off wool socks, standing barefoot on the frozen gravel like it was a summer beach. "You trust your feet on a deck because the deck moves and you move with it. Ice doesn't move. That's what's killing you—you keep waiting for it to shift under you like water does, and it won't, so you never actually commit your weight. You hover. Hovering is what drops people."

"I don't hover."

"Pilot. I have watched you cross three streams and a snowpatch. You hover like a heron."

From across the camp, without opening his eyes, Hale murmured, "She does hover. I assumed it was a nautical affectation."

"Betrayal," Orsen said. "From my own passengers."

But she took her boots off.

The first ten minutes were misery. The cold bit through her soles and up into her ankles, and her feet—feet that had spent eleven years reading the tremor of a deck through leather and oak—went stupid and rigid, reporting nothing but *cold, cold, cold.* She stood on the flattened gravel feeling like a fumbled parcel.

"Now walk," Tamsin said. "Slow. Heel first, then roll. Don't look at your feet—look where you're going. Your eyes pick the line, your feet just follow. Same as reading water, isn't it? You don't watch the bow. You watch the far bank."

And that—that was the key turning in the lock. Because it *was* the same. Orsen had spent so many years teaching green deckhands that you never stare at the obstacle right in front of the hull; you look past it, downriver, and let your hands find the course. She'd simply never imagined her own body was a vessel that could be steered.

She walked the length of the gravel. Then back. The third pass, something loosened in her ankles. By the fifth, she was almost—not quite, but almost—rolling her weight the way Tamsin did, committing instead of hovering, trusting the ground to hold its promise.

"Cold helps," Tamsin said, watching her with her arms crossed, satisfied as a sailing master after a clean tack. "Hurts enough to keep you honest. Tomorrow on the ice you'll remember it. Your feet will remember it even when your head is panicking."

"My head doesn't panic."

"Your head panics *beautifully.* Very quiet about it. Very dignified." Tamsin grinned, and for once didn't bother hiding it. "Like everything else about you."

Orsen sat back down by the fire and worked her thawing feet into dry socks, and found, to her considerable irritation, that she was smiling. She killed the smile. It came back. She let it live, briefly, the way you'd let a stray cat onto the deck—one night only, she told it. One night.

Across the fire, Pell was watching her with an expression she chose not to interpret.

* * *

The ice field proper began at dawn on the fifth day, and it was worse and better than anything the gravel lessons had prepared her for.

Worse, because it was enormous—a pale tongue of ancient ice spilling down between two shoulders of black rock, hummocked and ridged, glittering under a sky like polished tin. Better, because Tamsin stayed at her elbow the whole way across, and never once made her feel foolish for needing her there.

"Step where I step," the girl said, and it wasn't condescension; it was navigation. "See how the ice goes blue in the hollows? Blue means deep, means old, means it'll hold. White ice is new, full of air, can crumble at the edges. And where you see grit and stones sitting proud on the surface—walk there. Debris ice. Grips like a deck."

Orsen walked where Tamsin walked. Heel, roll, commit. Her feet remembered the frozen gravel. Her eyes picked lines down the far side of each hummock instead of staring at the crest of it. Twice her boot skated and twice she caught herself—not gracefully, but caught—and the second time Tamsin's hand was already there at her elbow, light as a mooring line, taking just enough weight to make the difference.

"Sorry," Orsen said, the second time.

"For what?"

"Being cargo."

Tamsin snorted. "You hauled me two hundred miles up a river when every sensible person told you to sell me out to the syndicate. You're not cargo. You're ballast." She paused, considering. "Ballast is important. Keeps the whole ship from tipping."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's whatever you want it to be, pilot."

Halfway across, they came to a place where the ice had split in some ancient winter and healed again, leaving a seam of frozen meltwater, glass-clear, and beneath it—far down, impossibly far down—a darkness with a faint blue-green glow, like looking through a porthole into the sea's dream. Orsen stopped without meaning to.

"How deep?"

"Nobody knows. The old climbers say the glacier swallows whole valleys and keeps them." Tamsin crouched at the seam's edge, unbothered, and studied the depth with the frank appraisal of someone raised where such things were neighbors. "My mother said the mountain keeps what falls in it safe. Nothing rots up here. Everything just waits."

"Waiting isn't the same as safe."

"No," Tamsin said softly. "It isn't." She stood, and for a moment the two of them stood together over that blue dark, a riverwoman and a mountain girl, each recognizing in the other the same thing: people who had spent years keeping themselves moving specifically so nothing could catch up to them.

"Tamsin." Orsen heard herself speak before she'd decided to. "On the boat. When you first came aboard. You said you'd been to Vhalen's valley. That you knew the way over the pass because your mother drew it for you." She kept her eyes on the ice seam. "That was true. But it wasn't the whole truth, was it."

The wind moved across the glacier with a sound like slow breathing.

"No," Tamsin said. "It wasn't."

Orsen nodded slowly. She thought about pushing. She thought about how a pilot reads a river—not by demanding answers of it, but by watching where the debris drifts, where the current wrinkles, where the water lies suspiciously smooth. Whatever the girl was carrying, it wasn't ready to surface. Pushing now would only drive it deeper.

"When you're ready to tell it," Orsen said, "tell it to all of us. Not just me. Crews break on secrets kept in ones and twos."

Something flickered across Tamsin's face—relief and guilt tangled together, quickly mastered. "You sound very sure we're a crew."

"I'm very sure I'm standing on a glacier because a nineteen-year-old told me to take my boots off," Orsen said. "At my age, that's practically a blood oath."

The girl laughed, and the sound went ringing off the black rock, and they crossed the rest of the ice field heel-roll-commit, step for step, and Orsen only fell twice more, and only counted one of those times.

* * *

They made the far side by late afternoon and camped in the lee of a moraine, a long rampart of stone and shattered rock the glacier had chewed out of the mountainside and spat along its flank. While Pell rigged the shelter tarp and Hale tended the small stove with the concentration of a man who had decided cooking was easier than conversation, Orsen climbed the moraine's spine to look back the way they'd come.

The habit again. Looking back.

Below, the valley they'd left that morning lay in blue shadow. Beyond it, faint with distance, the thread of the river. Beyond that—

Her breath stopped.

There was smoke on the southern horizon. Not one plume. Several, thin and orderly, spaced like fingers of a hand, rising from the direction of Coldharbour. Campfires, or signal fires, or—her mind supplied it with a pilot's cold efficiency—burned boats.

She stood a long time in the failing light, counting the smoke, reading it the way she'd read weather all her life. Too far south to reach tonight. Too organized to be travelers. Moving north, if they were moving at all, along the same corridor of passes they themselves had climbed.

Behind her, boots crunched on scree—sure-footed, unhurried. Tamsin came up the moraine and followed her gaze, and Orsen watched the girl's face do the arithmetic and arrive at the same sum.

"That's not hunters' smoke," Tamsin said quietly.

"No."

"Syndicate?"

"Syndicate doesn't travel in five tidy columns." Orsen's jaw tightened. "Syndicate travels in one column, loud, and hires the other four."

Down at the camp, Pell had noticed them standing there, and had gone still in the way he went still when the river changed its voice. Even from the moraine's spine, Orsen could see him set down the cook pot and turn to look south, reading the sky the way she'd taught him without ever once saying a word of instruction.

"Well," Tamsin said. She pulled her coat tighter. In the last light her eyes had gone the color of the ice seam—deep, patient, keeping something safe down in the dark. "How fast can this crew of ours move tomorrow?"

Orsen looked north, where the pass climbed toward the high col and the glacier beyond, and thought about the weather signs she'd been collecting for days without naming them: the tin-bright sky, the wind swinging east, the cold that had teeth in it now where last week it had only had manners.

"Faster than we've ever moved," she said. "Because I don't think that storm on the horizon is the only thing racing us to the top of this mountain anymore."
