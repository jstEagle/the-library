## 14. The Frozen Tollhouse

Pell had a theory about mountains, and the theory was this: they were rivers that had stopped pretending to be reasonable.

A river, at least, told you what it wanted. It showed you its sandbars, grumbled about your draft, and if it meant to kill you, it did so with water, which was honest. The mountain just sat there being cold and enormous, hiding its holes under snow like a card sharp hiding an ace, waiting for you to bet your life on a surface that looked solid.

He kept this theory to himself. He'd learned years ago that Orsen didn't want to hear his theories unless they involved rope tension or the price of tar. But he turned it over in his head as they climbed, because turning things over in his head was what he did instead of talking, and he was very good at both.

The trail out of Coldharbour had been three days of switchbacks and mule stink, and now, on the fourth morning, the wind had changed its mind about being merely hostile and committed to murder. Snow came sideways. Tamsin moved through it like she'd been born in it — which, as far as anyone knew, she might have been — while Hale limped along on his bandaged side making the particular sound of a man who refused to complain and wanted very badly for someone to notice.

Orsen walked point. She was terrible on ice — genuinely, almost impressively bad, a woman who could read a river's mood from fifty yards but couldn't stand upright on a frozen puddle — and she hated that everyone knew it. So she walked point anyway, arms out slightly for balance like a heron in a gale, refusing all offers of help with a look that could strip paint.

Pell watched her go down for the third time that hour, get up, and keep walking, and felt something in his chest do the thing it had been doing since Coldharbour. Not worry exactly. Worry would have been simpler.

* * *

They found the tollhouse by accident, which was how they found most good things that winter.

It crouched in a fold of the trail where the mountain pinched narrow between two shoulders of rock — a squat stone building with a slate roof half-buried in drift, its door hanging open like a jaw gone slack. Once it had collected fees from travelers crossing into the high country. Now it collected snow.

"Door's broken," Tamsin said, which was obvious, so nobody answered her.

"Hinges are broken," Hale corrected, squinting. "The door is merely optimistic."

Pell went in first, because going in first was his job and one of the few parts of his job that hadn't changed in eleven years. Inside: a single room, stone-floored, dominated by an old iron stove, a table, two benches, and a wall of shelves that some long-dead tollkeeper had stocked with the optimism of a man who expected civilization to come back for his pickles. There were no pickles. There were three jars of something dark and unidentifiable, a crate of candles gone soft with age, a stack of wool blankets that smelled of mouse but looked dry, and enough split firewood to make the room, briefly, feel like a place where people had once been warm on purpose.

"We're stopping," Orsen said. It wasn't a question, but Pell heard the question underneath it, the way you hear the current under ice.

"The light's failing," Tamsin agreed. "And the pass proper starts past the notch. You don't want to be on the glacier when the temperature drops."

"I don't want to be anywhere when the temperature drops," Orsen said. "That's not a plan, that's a philosophy."

Pell got the stove lit while the others argued about watches. He was good at fires. It was, he reflected, a strange résumé he'd built: fires, ropes, silence, and the ability to know where Orsen was at all times without looking. Eleven years of that last one. He sometimes thought it was the only skill he actually cared about.

* * *

It was Tamsin who found the riders' sign, and she found it because she was nosy, which Pell counted among her virtues.

She'd gone out to check the drifts before full dark — mountain habit, she said, reading snow the way Orsen read water — and came back in with her chin tucked and her eyes doing the thing they did when she'd decided not to say something yet. Pell had catalogued that look early on. It meant trouble with a delay on it.

He waited until Orsen was fussing with the stove and Hale was asleep sitting up, which was apparently a skill surveyors learned along with mapmaking. Then he stepped out into the blue dusk after the girl.

"Well?" he said.

Tamsin glanced at him. She still wasn't entirely sure what to make of him — he'd caught her studying him sometimes, like he was a knot she hadn't learned to tie — but she'd learned that he noticed things, and lately she'd started bringing him the things she noticed, the way you bring a stray dog evidence of other strays.

"Tracks," she said. "Under the new snow, coming up the trail from the south. Four horses, shod. They stopped here." She nodded back at the tollhouse. "Day before yesterday, maybe. They bedded down inside — there's straw behind the east bench."

Pell stood very still, letting the wind push at him, thinking.

Syndicate horses. Shod, which meant money; four, which meant confidence. And ahead of them, not behind. Which meant the syndicate hadn't given up at Coldharbour, hadn't waited for word to come back down the mountain. They'd sent men up the pass before Orsen had even finished arguing with the harbormaster.

"They're ahead of us," Tamsin said. "On the glacier somewhere. Waiting for us to walk into them, or watching the far side of the pass."

"You're sure they're syndicate?"

"One of them dropped this." From her pocket she produced a small brass token, stamped with the coiled-rope mark of the Kestrel harbor syndicate. Toll tokens — the syndicate had bought up the old toll rights on the northern routes years ago, another way of owning the river without ever getting it wet. "They'll have shown it at the far toll stations. Bought their way through clean."

Pell took the token and turned it over. Coiled rope. He'd spent his whole working life around rope, and he'd never once thought of it as something that could coil around *him*, but there it was, stamped in brass.

Inside, the stove ticked and settled. Through the fogged window he could see Orsen's shadow moving against the firelight, checking the flue, the way she checked everything, twice, because the world had taught her that anything unchecked would eventually betray you.

He went back in and told her.

* * *

She took it the way she took most news: standing very still, hands flat on the table, eyes fixed on the middle distance where she did her deciding.

Hale woke up halfway through the telling, which was convenient, because he immediately began narrating. "Four riders can't hold the whole pass. The glacier route is nine miles of crevasse field with maybe three safe lines through it. They can watch the exits, but they can't watch the ice."

"They don't have to watch the ice," Orsen said. "They can watch the far toll station. We come down off the glacier, we come down into their laps."

"So we don't use the main line." Tamsin was already crouched by the fire, drawing in the ash with a stick. "There's a high route. My mother talked about it — the shepherds' line, over the shoulder instead of through the notch. Longer. Colder. But it comes down north of the station."

"Colder," Hale repeated, with feeling.

"You've seen what I eat," Tamsin said. "I'm mostly insulation at this point."

Pell let them talk. He'd learned that the first ten minutes of any crisis belonged to everyone else, and his job started after, when the plans ran out and somebody had to say the thing nobody wanted said.

He said it when the fire had burned down and the arguing had worn itself into circles.

"Orsen." He waited until she looked at him. "We should turn back."

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when everyone had been thinking the same thing and hoping someone else would carry it.

"Back to Coldharbour?" Hale said carefully.

"Back down. All the way down, if we have to. Winter in Coldharbour, or lower. Wait out the season, come up in spring when the syndicate's forgotten—"

"The syndicate doesn't forget," Tamsin said. "The syndicate *files*."

"—or we find another route, or we put the chest somewhere and come back for it. Anything except walking nine miles onto a glacier with armed men at the bottom of it." Pell kept his voice level. This was the speech he'd been building for two days, and he'd built it well, and he hated every word of it. "The boat's gone. Fine. The chest isn't worth four lives. Nothing in it is worth four lives."

"It's worth mine," Tamsin said quietly.

"That's the problem with it being worth yours," Pell said. "You don't get to spend the rest of ours on it."

"Pell." Orsen hadn't moved. Her voice was the one she used on the river when the water went wrong — low, unhurried, final. "Say the rest of it."

"There's no rest of it."

"There's always a rest of it with you. You just keep it below decks." She came around the table and stood in front of him, close, the way she did when she wanted to see whether he'd flinch. "You think I haven't noticed? Three days you've been walking like a man carrying something heavy. Say it."

And because eleven years of silence is its own kind of habit, and habits break ugly, Pell heard himself say: "This road ends for you. That's the rest of it. Every road does, eventually, and this one ends on ice with syndicate guns, and I've been on boats that sank, Orsen, I know what the last mile feels like, and this is it. This is the last mile. Turn back and I'll row you anywhere you want for the rest of your life. Go forward and—" He stopped. His hands had made fists without asking him first.

The fire popped. Outside, the wind worried at the loose door hinge, patient as water.

Orsen looked at him for a long time. And then she did something he had never once seen her do in eleven years.

She smiled. Small, crooked, tired — but real, and aimed at him like a lamp.

"You're right," she said. "Every road ends. I've known that since I was nineteen years old, and it's why I bought a boat, because a boat lets you pretend the road is yours." She turned to the others. "But here's what I figured out on the river, and it took me eleven years and a sunken boat to figure it out: you can pick where a road ends, or you can pick who's standing next to you when it does. I spent my whole life picking the first thing. I'm done."

She picked up the brass token from the table and set it down in front of Pell.

"They sent four men to take one chest from one woman," she said. "That's not confidence. That's fear wearing boots. Somebody upstream is scared of what's in this box, and scared men make mistakes, and I intend to be standing next to them when they do." She looked around the room — at Tamsin with her ash-drawn maps, at Hale with his ruined side and stubborn knowledge, at Pell with his fists finally opening. "We go forward. High route, if the girl's shepherds' line is real. We leave at first light."

"And if it's not real?" Hale asked.

"Then we'll be lost together," Orsen said, "which is more than I've ever been."

* * *

Pell took first watch, because he always did, and because he needed the cold.

He sat in the doorway with a blanket around his shoulders and the broken door propped beside him, and he watched the stars come out over the notch like nails driven into black wood. Behind him, the others slept: Tamsin curled tight as a fist, Hale snoring with the dignity of a man who denied snoring, and Orsen on her side facing the door, because she always faced the door, because the world had taught her.

He'd meant it, what he'd said. Every word. Turn back, and he'd row her anywhere, forever, and ask nothing, because asking nothing was the deal he'd made with himself the day he signed onto the Vesper's Wake: her distance in exchange for his loyalty, her armor in exchange for his silence. It had been a good bargain. It had kept them both alive.

But she'd smiled at him tonight. Smiled and chosen forward, chosen *them*, and the terrible thing — the thing he sat with in the cold and turned over like a stone — was that some small traitorous part of him had been glad. Glad she was done running. Glad to be standing next to her at whatever end this road had in it, even knowing what it might cost.

He pulled the blanket tighter and thought about his theory. Rivers that stopped pretending to be reasonable.

Except that wasn't quite it anymore, he decided. A river you read. A mountain you crossed. And a crew — well. A crew you belonged to, whether you'd agreed to it or not, and the belonging had gotten into him somewhere back on the water, quiet as bilge water, and there was no pumping it out now.

Somewhere below them in the dark, four shod horses were waiting at the bottom of the world. Somewhere above, the glacier groaned in its sleep, shifting its weight, counting the days until it closed.

First light was five hours off. Pell fed the stove one more log, and settled in to keep watch over the people he loved, and began — silently, methodically, the way he did everything — to plan how he was going to keep them alive anyway.
