## 15. Ambush at the Serac Field

The serac field announced itself the way a bad debt does—quietly at first, then all at once.

Orsen had been reading ice for four days now, badly, and she knew it. On water she could tell you what lay under the surface by the way a current wrinkled; here on the glacier she was functionally illiterate. The ice towers rose around them in blue-white spires, some tall as her old masthead, leaning at angles that made her stomach clench with a pilot's instinct for things about to fall over. Meltwater trickled somewhere beneath them, a sound like the river whispering through a hull seam, and every hour of daylight the whole field groaned and ticked as the sun worked at its foundations.

"Cheerful place," Pell said, falling into step beside her.

"It's honest, at least. The river lies about what it'll do to you. This just stands there looking like the end of the world."

"That's not cheering me up the way you intended."

"It wasn't intended to."

Tamsin was ranging ahead, as she had since dawn, picking their line through the towers with the easy confidence of someone who'd grown up where the ground fought back. Hale limped behind her, his frost-nipped ankle improving by stubborn degrees, and he kept up a running commentary on glacial structure that nobody had asked for and Tamsin, to Orsen's surprise, occasionally answered. The two of them had reached an armed truce since Gray Landing—not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but a working agreement built on shared expertise and mutual exhaustion. It was more than Orsen would have managed.

She checked the sky instead of thinking about that. The storm system Hale had read in the cloud shapes two days back was still out west, a bruise along the horizon, but it was closer. They had maybe a day and a half of window left before the pass squeezed shut, and after that—

A whistle from Tamsin. Two short, one long. Not *come look at this*. The other signal.

Orsen's hand found her boat hook—it had become a walking staff, then a probe, and now, without her quite deciding, a weapon again—and she dropped behind a serac the color of old bottle glass. Pell folded down beside her without a word, which was eleven years of partnership compressed into a single motion.

"What is it?" she breathed.

Tamsin came back low and fast, snow spraying off her knees. "Riders. Four. Coming up our trail." Her face was pale under the windburn, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when someone is holding themselves together with both hands. "Syndicate colors on their coats."

"How far?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less. They've got mules and we've got feet."

Hale arrived last, breathing hard, and looked at each of them in turn. "They wouldn't attack on the glacier," he said, but his voice said he didn't believe it. "It's too dangerous. A fight up here, the noise alone could bring down—"

"They followed us across three hundred miles of mountain," Orsen said. "I don't think they're worried about noise." She peered around the edge of the serac, back down the trail they'd climbed. The ice field spread below them like a frozen city, towers and canyons and alleys, and far down at its mouth, small as toys, four riders and two pack mules were picking their way upward. Even at this distance she could see the black-and-silver sashes. The harbor syndicate hadn't gotten rich being merciful, and they certainly hadn't gotten rich leaving loose ends on mountains.

"The chest," Tamsin said. Her hand went to the pack strap across her chest, an unconscious gesture Orsen had watched a hundred times since Coldharbour. Everything the girl was—the name, the country, the dead father whose seal had floated down a river into Orsen's life—rode in that canvas wrap against her heartbeat.

"We could run," Pell said. "Cut east through the towers, lose them in the maze."

"And come out where?" Hale asked. "East is the cornice line. You'd be crossing avalanche slopes with four armed men behind you."

"West, then."

"West is the crevasse field. We cross it tomorrow, when the cold's got it locked up tight. Today, this early—" He shook his head. "The bridges won't hold a cat."

"So we stand and talk to them," Tamsin said, with a bitterness that made clear exactly what kind of talking the syndicate had offered before.

Orsen looked at the three of them—Pell crouched and calm, Tamsin fierce and frightened, Hale already fumbling at the straps of his surveyor's kit for the flare pistol he'd carried since another life—and felt something settle in her chest that she recognized from the worst nights on the river, the nights when the sandbar shifted under her keel and there was nothing left to do but steer into it. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, an awful clarity.

"All right," she said. "Here's how it goes. We don't run—we can't outrun mules on this footing, and running tells them we're prey. We don't gather in a bunch, because I've seen what one well-thrown rock does to a clustered target. We spread through the towers, we make them come to us, and we make them come to us stupid." She met Pell's eyes. "You're the best thrower I know. Anything heavy, anything with an edge—you're our artillery. Tamsin, you know this ground better than any of us. You're our eyes. Hale—"

"I'm a liability," Hale said quietly. "My ankle's still—"

"You're the only one of us who's ever been shot at by professionals. Tell us what they'll do."

He blinked. For a moment the disgraced surveyor looked like a man handed back a piece of himself he'd thought forfeit. "They'll split," he said. "Two to flush, two to cut off retreat. Syndicate field teams always work in pairs—it's in their training doctrine, I mapped supply routes for their northern operations for six years, I know how they think." His jaw tightened. "They'll want the girl alive. The chest intact. Everyone else is negotiable."

"Charming," said Pell.

"Then everyone else has an advantage," Orsen said. "Nobody's trying to take us alive."

* * *

It went wrong almost immediately, which Orsen supposed was the traditional way of these things.

The first pair of riders came up the main trail at a walk, unhurried, professional, leading their mules—a confidence that told Orsen they'd done this before and never once had it gone sideways. She watched them from behind a leaning tower forty yards upslope, Tamsin pressed beside her, both of them breathing shallow. The lead rider wore a gray coat with silver braid and had the broad, patient face of a man who enjoyed his work.

"Ho there!" he called up the trail, pleasant as a dockside greeting. "Travelers! We're out of Coldharbour, checking permits!"

"Liar," Tamsin whispered.

"Oh, absolutely. But it's polite of him."

The plan was simple: let them commit to the narrows between two great seracs, then Pell would drop a chunk of ice from above—nothing lethal, just enough to spook the mules and break their formation—and in the confusion they'd back away deeper into the maze and simply... keep being difficult until the storm or the cold or plain arithmetic discouraged pursuit. It was not a battle plan. It was a stalling plan. Orsen had spent eleven years avoiding fights by being faster, quieter, and harder to find, and she had intended to continue that tradition at altitude.

But the second pair of riders hadn't waited for the first pair to draw them out. They'd swung wide, west, through the towers, and they were good—good enough that none of them heard the crunch of boot on snow-crusted ice until a voice said, from ten feet away:

"There. The girl and the woman. Leave the old ones."

Everything after that happened in the space of a held breath.

Orsen turned and swung the boat hook on pure river instinct, the same motion she'd used a thousand times to fend off drifting logs, and caught the nearest man across the forearm hard enough to spin him. He grunted, staggered against a serac, and came back with a short blade already out, and Orsen discovered that fighting on ice was like fighting on the deck of a listing ship except that the ship hated you personally. Her boots skated. His did too. They grabbed at each other like two drunks in a doorway while somewhere very close Tamsin shouted and a pistol cracked and the sound went ricocheting around the ice field like the world's angriest bell.

The second rider came around the tower at a run, and he was heading for Tamsin—of course he was, the girl alive, the chest intact—and Orsen saw it happen the way pilots see disasters: clearly, completely, and too late to stop it. The man had a hand out for the girl's collar. Tamsin stumbled back on the slick ground, off balance, arms pinwheeling.

And Davor Hale—limping, fifty-three years old, a man who had spent fifteen years apologizing to a dead country with his whole body—stepped between them.

"Down!" he roared, and shoved Tamsin aside, and took the rider's blade high in the meat of his shoulder rather than let it find her throat.

The sound he made was not dramatic. That was the worst part, Orsen thought later. It was small and surprised, a man interrupted mid-sentence, and then he folded down onto the ice with blood already soaking dark through his coat, and the world went red at the edges for Orsen in a way it hadn't gone in eleven careful, unattached years.

What happened next, she would remember in pieces.

Pell appearing out of nowhere with a fist-sized chunk of glacier ice and putting it into the first rider's face with the accuracy of a man who'd spent a decade throwing mooring lines in gales. The man going down and staying down. Tamsin screaming—not fear, fury, a sound with a whole drowned nation in it—and driving her ice axe into the snow in front of the second rider's mount so the mule reared and dumped him. Orsen herself doing something with the boat hook that she declined to examine afterward, something that put the blade-man down gasping beside his friend.

And then the field itself decided the matter.

Whether it was the pistol shot, or the shouting, or simply the sun finishing its long argument with a serac that had been leaning toward ruin for a century, no one could say. But the great tower above the western trail—the one the second pair of riders had been using for cover as they moved to flank—shivered, groaned like a ship's timbers in a storm, and came down.

It did not fall so much as pour. Tons of ancient ice went down the slope in a white avalanche roar, and the two flanking riders, caught flat in the open, had time to turn their mules and nothing else. The mules survived. The men did not. When the thunder finally rolled away down the valley and the powder settled, the trail where they'd stood was simply gone, buried under a fresh face of blue ice, and the silence afterward was so complete that Orsen could hear her own heart slamming.

The remaining two riders scrambled onto their surviving mule and fled down-trail without a word. Nobody fired after them. Nobody had the breath.

* * *

They got Hale into the lee of a fallen block and cut his coat open, and Orsen's hands were steadier than she expected them to be, which surprised her. Eleven years of patching sails and stitching torn canvas, of splinting Pell's fingers and boiling river water—it turned out all of it counted. The blade had opened his shoulder in a long ugly line rather than sinking deep. He'd live. He'd ache until spring, and probably until summer, but he'd live.

"You idiot," Tamsin said. She was kneeling in the bloody snow with both hands pressed over the wound, and she was crying, furiously, as though the tears were a personal failing. "You absolute—I had it. I had my axe. I didn't need—"

"No," Hale said. His voice was thin. He smiled anyway, a crooked, exhausted thing. "You didn't. But I did."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I've been paying a debt for fifteen years, child, and I finally found a currency somebody would take." He looked up at Orsen, and his eyes were wet too, though he'd deny it later. "I told you once I wanted to be useful. I'm told usefulness isn't the same as worth. I've been hoping to learn the difference."

"Well," Orsen said, and found her voice had gone rough. "You're learning it the expensive way."

Pell came back from scouting the collapsed trail, his face grim in a new way. "Those two under the ice aren't getting up," he reported quietly. "And the other two will reach Coldharbour by tomorrow night if they push. They'll report we killed syndicate men. Doesn't matter that the mountain did it. On paper, we're killers now."

No one said anything for a moment. The wind moved through the towers, making its low mournful notes.

"Before today," Tamsin said slowly, "we were carrying a chest and arguing about history. Now—" She looked at her hands, still tacky with Hale's blood. "Now we've fought together. Bled together. There's no version of this where we go back to being strangers sharing a trail."

"No," Orsen agreed. "There isn't."

She should have felt trapped. That was the strange part. Every instinct she'd honed for eleven years—the refusal at the dock, the empty cabin, the careful distance—every one of them said *this is how it starts, this is how they get hooks in you*, and here she was with three hooks in her and bleeding freely. And yet when she looked at Pell, who had known her longest and loved her most quietly and was watching her now with an expression of resigned sorrow, as if to say *I knew this day would come*—when she looked at Tamsin with her dead country's eyes and Hale laid out gray-faced among the ice towers, Orsen felt something she hadn't felt since she was young enough to still believe in harbors.

Responsible. Willingly, gladly responsible. It was terrifying. It was also, she suspected, the opposite of drowning.

"Then we don't go back," she said aloud. "We go forward. Over the pass, into the valley, all the way to the end of this. Whatever's waiting there, we meet it as what we are now."

"And what are we now?" Tamsin asked.

Orsen looked at the blood in the snow, at the buried trail, at the storm building in the west like a promise being kept.

"Crew," she said. "God help us all. Crew."

Behind them, far down the glacier, the ice groaned again—a long settling sigh, as if the mountain had heard her and approved. Ahead, the pass narrowed toward the crevasse field, and the light was already turning the color of tarnished brass.

They had until tomorrow's cold. After that, the mountain would close its one door, and whatever was coming out of the west would arrive to find them either safely through—or wintering exactly where the syndicate knew to look.
