## 16. What Survival Costs

The dead men weighed less than Tamsin had expected.

That was the first thing she learned about corpses on a glacier: cold thinned them somehow, pared them down to their essential argument. The two syndicate riders lay side by side where Orsen and Pell had dragged them out from under the fallen serac, and they looked—there was no kinder way to say it—like luggage someone had abandoned at a station.

"We should say something," Hale said. He was sitting propped against a boulder with his arm bound tight against his chest, gray-faced but upright, because Davor Hale apparently considered fainting a form of dishonesty. "Words. Over the graves."

"They tried to kill us," Pell said. He was digging anyway, chipping at the frozen gravel with the boat's ice axe in short, economical strokes, as though grave-digging were simply another chore on a long list that included mending sails and scraping barnacles. "I can say *sorry about the hole* if you like."

"Pell." Orsen's voice carried the particular flatness she used when she was preventing an argument rather than starting one. She stood at the edge of the little clearing, arms crossed against the wind, watching the ice towers down-valley for signs of anyone else coming. She had been watching for an hour. Tamsin suspected she would watch until her eyes froze in their sockets before admitting she wanted to sit down.

Tamsin knelt beside the nearer body and went through its coat pockets, because sentiment was a luxury and information was not. A tin of tobacco, gone soft. Three coins, southern mint. A folded paper—a commission, signed by a harbor syndicate factor whose name she made herself memorize—and beneath it, wedged into the lining, a small oilcloth packet containing nothing but a strip of dried meat and a lock of hair tied with thread.

She sat back on her heels.

"What is it?" Hale asked.

"Nothing." She put the lock of hair back where she'd found it, gently, the way you'd return something borrowed. "He had family."

Nobody said anything to that. The wind filled the silence for them, moving through the serac field with a sound like a crowd murmuring just below hearing. Somewhere under their feet, deep in the glacier's blue interior, water ticked and shifted—the calving season working at its seams, the mountain deciding what it meant to let go.

Tamsin thought: *these men came up here to take a box away from a girl who hadn't been born when the country on that box's seal stopped existing.* And then she thought, more honestly: *and I climbed a mountain to keep it.*

* * *

They buried the riders in a crease of moraine where the gravel was loosest, marking the spot with two stones and, at Pell's insistence, one of the men's own belt buckles hung on a stick, "so if anybody comes looking, they find a grave and not a mystery."

Hale said words after all. He kept them short, which Tamsin appreciated; long prayers over enemies always struck her as a kind of showing off. He spoke of men who had been somebody's sons, which was true of everyone and therefore cost him nothing, and then he spoke of Vhalen, which cost him considerably more.

"I knew the country," he said, standing awkwardly with his good hand on his hat. "I mapped its passes. I never met its people until—" he glanced at Tamsin, and did not finish, and did not need to.

Tamsin looked at the two stones and felt the page against her ribs.

That was the thing nobody warned you about secrets: they got heavier the longer you carried them, and they were shaped exactly wrong for carrying, all corners and edges, so that every step pressed them somewhere new. The page she'd taken from the chest in Coldharbour—the page she'd traded an archivist a decoy to keep—was folded now into a square no bigger than a playing card and worn soft as cloth from handling. It named names. It was, as far as she could tell, the single most important document in the chest: the signature of the man who'd sold the Corvane passes to Vhalen's conquerors, written in a hand she'd come to know by heart because she'd stared at it through half of one sleepless night and all of another.

It was not Hale's name.

That should have mattered more than it did. For three days after the chest spoke—after she'd laid the truth out on the deck of the *Vesper's Wake* like a fish gutted on a cutting board—she had told herself the page proved Hale innocent, and keeping it was prudence, insurance against the day someone claimed otherwise. Prudence. Survivors' word for greed when greed wears sensible boots.

But there was a second line beneath the signature. A smaller hand, a note in the margin, and Tamsin had read it enough times that she could recite it against the inside of her eyelids: *Payment rendered in full — surveyor's maps confirmed viable — remainder owed upon occupation.*

The surveyor's maps. Hale's maps. Confirmed, sold, used. And the margin note meant there was a *remainder*—a second payment, promised, perhaps still collectible, perhaps already collected by someone whose name sat above that line in handwriting Tamsin didn't recognize yet but recognized the style of, because it was the same confident slant as the factor's signature on the dead rider's commission.

The syndicate hadn't stumbled onto the chest. The syndicate had come to collect an old debt—or to make sure no one else ever read it.

And she had known this for days and said nothing, because saying something meant explaining why she'd stolen the page in the first place, and explaining that meant admitting she'd planned, from the beginning, to trade Vhalen's truth piece by piece for whatever bought her closest to home.

*Home.* The word had always meant the valley. The hidden valley, the survivors, the elders who would look at her bloodline and see a country standing up out of a girl. She had walked toward it her whole life the way water walks toward a low place.

She looked up from the graves and saw Orsen watching her—not the valley-watching she did for danger, but the other kind, the quick flick-and-away, the look of a woman who read faces the way she read sandbars and had just noted a shoal forming where none had been that morning.

Tamsin smiled at her. It was, she reflected later, the first outright lie she'd told the crew since Gray Landing, and it landed badly, like a stone skipped onto thin ice.

* * *

Camp that night was a hollow behind a leaning slab of granite, wind-sheltered, fire small. Hale slept propped upright with his arm strapped across his chest, breathing in careful increments, refusing the tea Pell kept trying to press on him because "if I drink any more of that I'll be honest with you, and nobody wants that."

"He's joking," Pell said to Tamsin, gravely. "Mostly."

Orsen took first watch, which meant Tamsin was supposed to sleep, which meant she lay awake listening to the glacier groan and feeling the page press its corner into her ninth rib like a small patient knife.

After a while she gave up and crawled out of her bedroll to sit by the fire, and Orsen, without turning from her watch, said, "Sit where the smoke goes, not where it comes from. You'll freeze your back otherwise."

"You can't even see me."

"I can hear you breathe wrong. Mountain people breathe shallow when they're chewing on something."

Tamsin moved to the smoky side. It was absurd how much better it felt, warmth against her spine, and she hated that Orsen was right, and loved it, and hated loving it.

"My mother," Tamsin said, because the fire made talking easier the way dark made lying easier, "used to say the valley keeps us alive and the seal keeps us *Vhalen*. Two different jobs. She said most people only manage one."

Orsen was quiet long enough that Tamsin thought she wasn't going to answer. Then: "My mother ran a ferry. Small crossing, muddy water, nothing worth stealing on either bank. She died when the river took the ferry, and I spent eleven years learning that everything I love floats, so the smart move is to love nothing that stays." She turned her head slightly. Firelight caught the edge of her face. "You're doing the arithmetic, aren't you. Country or crew. I can hear it."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Tamsin." Just the name, flat and patient, the way she called soundings.

Tamsin looked into the fire. The honest answer rose in her like floodwater. *I have a page that could clear Hale completely and I've hidden it because hiding it also protects whoever took the rest of the money, and I've told myself it's strategy but it's fear, because the moment I show it, I stop being the heir with a claim and start being a girl who stole from her own country's chest, and I don't know which one the valley will want, and worse, I don't know which one I want to be.*

What she said was: "When we get there. When the elders read the documents. What happens to the four of us?"

"That depends on the documents, I expect."

"And on me."

"And on you."

"And if the documents say things the elders don't like? If they say things that make trouble for people who helped us?" Tamsin kept her voice level, curious, a girl asking about weather. "Do we get a say?"

Orsen finally turned fully around. She studied Tamsin for a long moment with those river-pilot's eyes that took depth readings of everything, and Tamsin had the distinct, sinking sensation of being charted.

"When you know what you're carrying," Orsen said quietly, "you'll decide whether to set it down or throw it to us. Either way, I'd rather you decided before the current decides for you." She turned back to her watch. "Go sleep. You dig a poor grave when you're tired, and I'm not doing that twice."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the smoke with the page against her ribs and did the thing she had been avoiding since Coldharbour, which was to imagine, precisely, the scene in the valley: the elders gathered, the chest opened, the documents read aloud in that slow ceremonial cadence old people use when words are weapons being counted. Her name spoken as heir. And then—because she had read the margin note eleven times and memory is a cruel archivist—the moment when some sharp-eyed reader asked why one page was missing from the sequence, and the answer arrived, and it was her.

An heir who rationed the truth. An heir who'd held back the one page that named the traitor's paymaster, the page that might have warned them years ago, might have—

Might have what? Saved a country that had been dead twenty-five years? No. That was the lie she'd been telling herself, dressed up in grief. The page couldn't save Vhalen. Nothing could. All it could do, here, now, was this: prove Hale clean, point at the syndicate, and burn her own hands in front of the only people who might have made her belong.

Or—she turned it once more, the way you turn a stone looking for the cool side—it could stay hidden. She could walk into the valley with a secret in her shirt and trade it, carefully, privately, for position. Come out of the reading with leverage instead of scars. Be the heir who *knew things*. Countries were built by people who knew things.

Countries were also built on graves, said a voice that sounded unpleasantly like the dead rider's belt buckle glinting on its stick.

Tamsin pulled the page out.

In the firelight it was unremarkable: cheap paper, brown ink, a signature and a margin note, the whole architecture of a nation's murder fitting in her palm. Twenty-five years she'd been raised on the idea of this—on seals and bloodlines and the sacred weight of paper. Her mother had died within sight of the mountains and made her promise the promise, and the promise had been the only warm thing in a cold childhood, and she had crossed a range on the strength of it.

And the paper itself? The paper didn't care. The paper had floated down a river in a box while a country drowned, and it would outlast her mother's memory and Hale's guilt and Orsen's boat and every person at this fire, calmly preserving its little inked betrayal for whoever dug it up next. Legacy was just survival with the love removed. She saw that suddenly, wholly, the way you see the bottom of a river when the light hits right: the elders would honor her for the page, and the honoring would be a wall between her and everyone she'd bled beside, and she would spend her life inside it, safe and sealed, a country of one.

"No," she said aloud.

Pell stirred. "Wha's happening?"

"Nothing. Sleep."

She fed the page to the fire corner-first, and it resisted, the way paper does, curling black at the edges and flaring, and for one heartbeat she nearly snatched it back—her mother's face, the promise, the seal—and then the margin note caught flame and the *remainder owed upon occupation* bloomed orange and was gone, and Tamsin Nakamura-of-the-line-of-Vhalen sat in the smoke with empty hands and found, to her enormous surprise, that she could breathe deeper than she had in weeks.

The ash lifted on the draft and scattered among the ice towers. Somewhere down-valley the glacier boomed, long and rolling, a sound like a door closing or a door opening; with glaciers you never knew which until morning.

She stayed by the fire until her eyes burned, memorizing the camp: Hale's careful breathing, Pell's blanket-humped shape, Orsen's silhouette against the stars, motionless except for the small turn of her head every hundred breaths, sweeping the dark, keeping watch over people she'd sworn she wouldn't keep watch over.

*A country of one,* Tamsin thought. *Not anymore.*

She was still warm-handed from the burning when the dawn came up gray and fast, and Orsen stood, stiff-kneed, and pointed north with her chin.

"Storm's moving faster than the scouts said," she said. "We've got maybe a day of good travel, less if the pass narrows. After that, the window shuts for the season—with us on whichever side of it we happen to be standing." She looked at each of them in turn, and her gaze rested on Tamsin a half-second longer than the others, and Tamsin understood that Orsen had seen the fire last night, had seen the paper, had said nothing, and was saying nothing now.

"Then we'd best not stand," Tamsin said, and broke camp.
