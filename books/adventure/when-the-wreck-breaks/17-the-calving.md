## 17. The Calving

Davor Hale had spent the better part of his life believing that mountains were patient things. That was the first lesson they taught you in the Imperial Survey Corps—*the stone does not hurry for you*, old Master Corvick used to say, tapping his brass alidade against the lecture slate until the whole room rattled. *You hurry for the stone.* Hale had repeated that maxim to a hundred green surveyors over the years, back when he still had students to teach and a reputation worth teaching them with.

The glacier disabused him of the notion somewhere around dawn on their fourth day above the snowline.

It began as a sound he felt before he heard it—a deep, tectonic groan rolling up through the soles of his boots, through the packed snow of the campsite, through the frame of the tent he'd been sleeping in. He was awake and halfway out of his bedroll before his mind finished translating what his body already knew: *that is not wind. Wind does not come from below.*

He crawled out into blue half-light and stood there with his breath freezing in his beard while the glacier spoke.

The tongue of ice they'd been following—the great white river that poured down from the Corvane saddle, the one his own maps had named the Vhalen Approach twenty years ago—was moving. Not creeping, the way glaciers are supposed to move, an inch a day, a patient inch a century. Moving the way a thing moves when it has decided to be somewhere else. Far below them, where the ice dropped away in a series of blue-green cliffs toward the meltwater lakes, something the size of a cathedral leaned forward, sighed, and let go.

The crack when it broke was like nothing Hale had ever heard, and he had spent two years of his life listening to ice. It started low, almost musical, a note held too long, and then it became a roar, and then it became a sound like the world's largest door slamming, and then there was only the echo of it, bouncing off the valley walls, going on and on long after the falling had stopped.

A plume of snow-dust rose from the calving face, catching the first pink light of sunrise, glowing like a signal fire.

Behind him, inside the tent, Tamsin said, "That's early."

"That's early," Hale agreed.

"How early?"

He did some arithmetic he didn't like. "By my maps? Three weeks."

Tamsin emerged from the tent with her hair full of frost and her expression full of murder. "Your maps," she said, "are twenty years old."

"My maps," Hale said, with the particular dignity available only to a man standing in his stockings at four degrees below freezing, "were the best maps ever made of this range, and I will thank you to remember that the last time you insulted them, we nearly walked off a cornice."

"The last time I insulted your maps we *did* walk off a cornice. Pell caught me."

"Pell catches everyone. It's his defining feature." He squinted east, where the light was coming up gold behind the jagged teeth of the Corvane crest. The calving face was still shedding—smaller pieces now, house-sized instead of cathedral-sized, but shedding all the same, each collapse sending its own smaller thunder rolling up the valley. "Warm night," he said, mostly to himself. "Cloud cover came in low around midnight. Held the heat in. Ice doesn't need much of an excuse once it starts thinking about spring."

"Orsen said the river's been running high since the flood." Tamsin had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was actually frightened, which was a rare enough occurrence that Hale made a point of noticing it. "She said everything downstream is a week ahead of itself. Meltoff coming early."

"Then the mountain got the same letter we did." Hale rubbed his hands together and watched the frost fall out of his beard. "The pass is closing, Tamsin. And it's closing from both ends."

* * *

Orsen took the news the way she took most news: standing at the front of the group with her arms crossed, looking at the problem as though it were a sandbar she could read.

They'd struck camp by then—all of them moving faster than they'd moved any morning yet, because nobody needed the danger explained twice—and stood in a loose cluster on a shelf of rock above the ice, looking north to where the route bent westward into the crevasse field Hale had been dreading since Coldharbour.

"The Séracs de l'Aube I can route us around," Hale said, unrolling the oilskin copy of his field map across a boulder. His hands were steadier than he'd expected them to be. "We cut high along the eastern moraine, cross the ice bridge at the narrows—that bridge was sound in my day, three wagons wide, and ice that thick doesn't thin in twenty years—"

"It thins in twenty years if the summer thaws have been eating it from underneath every one of those years," Orsen said.

"—and then we're through the field by midday tomorrow and climbing the saddle by evening." He looked up. "If we move now. If we move *clean*. No camp tonight except what shelter we can dig. We sleep on the far side or we don't sleep."

Pell, who had been silent as usual, crouched beside the map and studied it with the expression he otherwise reserved for knots. "How wide is the field?"

"Two miles of broken ice. Crevasses everywhere, most of them hidden under snow bridges. You rope up, you space out, you step where I tell you to step, and you do not—" he looked at each of them in turn, "—under any circumstances, wander."

"I never wander," Pell said.

"You wandered into a river once," Tamsin said.

"I was fishing."

"You were *in* the river."

"The fish were in the river."

Hale felt something in his chest loosen slightly—a laugh trying to happen and being strangled at birth, which was, he'd found, the correct way to handle laughter on a glacier. But the humor did its work anyway; he saw Orsen's shoulders drop a quarter inch, saw Tamsin's mouth twitch. Fear was a load, and a crew could carry it better distributed.

Orsen straightened. "Rope order," she said. "I want Hale on point. He knows the ice. Tamsin behind him—I've seen her read snow, she's second-best eyes we've got. Then me. Pell anchors. If anyone goes in, anchor holds, everyone else drops flat. We've drilled it."

"We drilled it twice," Tamsin said.

"Twice is once more than most people get." Orsen rolled up the map and handed it back to Hale, and for a moment her hand rested on his wrist—not a gesture she made often, and not one she seemed aware of making now. "You said the bridge was sound in your day."

"It was."

"And you're sure about today."

Hale thought about lying. He considered how easy it would be, how kind, how useless. "No," he said. "I'm sure about the arithmetic. Sound ice plus twenty years of thaw equals ice that needs testing. I'll test every step of it myself before anyone else puts weight on. That's the best certainty I can sell you, and I won't inflate the price."

Orsen nodded slowly. "Fair terms," she said. "You always did talk like a contract."

"I was a surveyor. Contracts were the only poetry we were allowed."

* * *

The crevasse field earned its name within the hour.

From the moraine it had looked almost gentle—a broad white apron sloping down from the saddle, wrinkled here and there like bedsheets, blue shadows threading between the wrinkles. From the ice itself it was a different country entirely. The wrinkles were seracs, towers of compressed ice the height of church spires, leaning at angles stone could never survive, balanced on foundations no one could see. And between them the crevasses opened: cracks in the world, some narrow enough to step over, some wide enough that when Hale leaned over the lip and dropped a pebble, he counted—one, two, three, four, five—before the pebble clicked off ice far below and kept going.

"Six seconds," he murmured, marking the spot with a wanded pole. "That one's a hundred fathoms if it's a foot."

"Don't say 'if it's a foot,'" Tamsin said behind him. "Don't say feet. Don't say fathoms. Just say 'deep' and keep walking."

"Deep," Hale amended. "Keep walking."

The cold was the strange mercy of it. Calving season meant the air had warmed enough to wake the glacier—but up here on the ice, in the shadow of the seracs, the temperature had dropped instead, the way a fever breaks wrong. The surface underfoot was iron-hard, squeaking under their boots, and the sky above was a hard enamel blue without a single cloud to soften it. Perfect crossing weather, Hale knew. Stable cold. The ice bridges frozen solid, holding their loads.

Perfect crossing weather—for exactly as long as it lasted. Because stable cold in calving season was a debt, and glaciers paid their debts the way rivers did: suddenly, and all at once.

He tested the first snow bridge with his ice axe, swinging the pick in a flat arc, listening. A healthy bridge answered with a dull *whump*, the sound of solid mass absorbing a blow. A rotten bridge rang, or hissed, or—worst of all—said nothing at all, because there was nothing beneath the crust but air and a long education waiting to happen.

The first six bridges sang true. The seventh hummed.

"Hold," Hale called, and the rope went taut behind him, four bodies becoming one body, everyone dropping to spread their weight the way they'd drilled. He lay on his stomach at the bridge's edge and reached out with the axe, probing, and the crust gave under the pick like the skin on old milk. Beneath it: a gap two feet wide, bridged by six inches of wind-packed snow that had looked, from three steps back, exactly like the other six bridges that had held.

"Reroute," he said, and crawled backward, and they cut left through a lane between two seracs, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that the axe hadn't already said.

But Hale noticed that after that, the spacing on the rope increased. Nobody discussed it. The rope simply grew longer between them, ten feet becoming fifteen, fifteen becoming twenty, the way a school of fish spreads when a shadow passes overhead. And he noticed something else, something that lodged under his ribs and stayed there: Tamsin, directly behind him, had begun talking. Quietly. Constantly. To him, to herself, to the mountain.

"—and the bakery in Coldharbour had those honey rolls, the ones shaped like knots, and Pell ate six, and the baker's wife told him if he ate a seventh she'd charge him double, and he looked at her so sadly that she gave him the seventh free—"

"Tamsin."

"Mm?"

"Why are you telling me about honey rolls?"

A pause. The crunch of boots on iron ice. Then, smaller: "Because if I stop talking I'm going to start listening, and the mountain's saying things I don't want to hear."

Hale looked back at her. She was nineteen years old and carrying a dead nation in a canvas satchel, and her knuckles were white on the rope, and she was telling him about honey rolls in a voice that only shook a little.

"When I was mapping this range," he said, resuming the lead, "there was a boy in our party—younger than you—who sang. All day, every day. Terribly. Our head guide told him if he stopped singing, the guide would stop guiding. Said a quiet man on a glacier is a man listening to his own fear, and fear echoes." He stepped, tested, stepped again. "So sing, Tamsin. Talk. Recite the whole history of honey rolls if you must. I'll listen to every word."

And she did. For two hours she talked—about the boat, about Pell's knots, about Orsen's terrible singing voice (which Hale had, in fact, witnessed once, on the third night out, and which had driven a ptarmigan off a ledge), about the taverns of the Kestrel and the mule drivers of Coldharbour and the exact shade of angry Orsen's face turned when someone touched her helm without asking. And Hale listened, and led, and probed, and found that his own fear had grown quieter too, worn smooth by the rhythm of her voice, the way water wears stone.

* * *

They were a mile into the field, near the narrows, when the mountain cleared its throat again.

This time there was no mistaking it for anything else. The groan came up through the ice itself, through their boots, through their teeth—a vast grinding exhalation, the sound of a living weight shifting in its sleep—and then, from behind them, southward, back the way they'd come: the crack. Longer than the dawn one. Deeper. Followed by a second crack, overlapping the first, harmonizing with it, and then a rumble that didn't stop so much as fade, the way thunder fades, reluctantly, keeping a piece of itself in reserve.

Everyone turned. Nobody had to.

Far to the south, at the calving face they'd camped above that morning, another plume of snow-dust climbed into the blue. And beyond it—Hale narrowed his eyes, doing the geometry, hating the answer—the shoulder of the glacier itself had changed. A section of the ice wall flanking the approach, the wall they'd walked beneath not six hours ago, had slumped inward. The route behind them was narrower now. Or gone.

"The pass," Tamsin whispered. "Is it—"

"Closing," Hale said. "Not closed. Not yet. But the door's swinging, and we're on the wrong side of the hinge to watch it comfortably." He forced his voice level. "It changes nothing. Forward was always the only direction. Now it's simply also the urgent one."

Orsen had gone very still, the way she went still when reading water. When she spoke, it was to the ice, quietly, as though negotiating. "How long?"

Hale did the arithmetic again, honestly this time, because these people had earned honest numbers. "Stable cold holds the bridges. Storm breaks the cold. There's a storm coming—I've smelled it building since yesterday, pressure falling, that halo around the sun this morning. Two days, maybe three, of crossing weather left. After that, the bridges rot under new snow, the crevasses open wide, and this field becomes a graveyard with excellent acoustics."

"So we cross it today."

"We cross it today," Hale agreed, "or we learn to live in it."

Pell, at the rope's end, spat carefully into a crevasse—an offering, maybe, or a review. "Never liked this glacier," he said. "Talks too much."

* * *

The narrows came upon them in the early afternoon: a place where two tributary glaciers had collided a thousand years ago and ground each other into a chaos of blue towers, leaving a single corridor of survivable ground barely a wagon-width across, walled in by seracs leaning so far overhead that they nearly met, filtering the sunlight into a dim underwater glow. Hale had named it the Cathedral on his original map, partly for the light and partly because a young man alone on the ice will name things whatever keeps him company.

The ice bridge was at the corridor's end. He saw it from fifty yards and his stomach dropped.

In his memory, the bridge had been a broad white arch, confident as a cathedral roof, spanning the crevasse where the two glaciers pulled apart. In reality—or in the reality twenty years of summers had carved—it was a spine. A narrow ridge of ice, perhaps four feet wide at its crown, sagging in the middle, its flanks eaten through so that daylight showed *through* it in places, blue light streaming through holes in the arch like light through stained glass, if stained glass were the only thing between a traveler and a hundred fathoms of dark.

Nobody spoke. Hale realized they were all looking at him.

"I'll test it," he said. "Alone. Rope's anchored to that horn—if I go in, don't be heroes. Be useful. Mark the hole and find another way, because there's always another way, and dead rescuers rescue no one."

"You rehearsed that speech," Orsen said.

"I've had twenty years to draft it."

He crossed the moraine to the anchor horn, set the rope, and went out onto the bridge alone.

It was worse close up. The surface was sound enough—his axe fell and answered with a solid *whump*—but the sound underneath, the sound the ice made when the wind gusted, was a faint harmonic ringing, like a wet finger on a glass rim, like something enormous humming to itself. He crawled the first third on his belly, distributing his weight, listening with his whole body the way he'd taught a hundred students to listen. At the midpoint, where the arch sagged lowest, he stopped and pressed his palm flat against the ice.

Cold poured up through his glove. And beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat, he felt it: movement. Not cracking. Not failing. Just *moving*—the whole bridge flexing, breathing, alive, a living thing carrying him over the dark and asking nothing except that he not linger.

He crossed. Stood on the far side. Turned.

Four faces watched him from the near edge, and Hale—who had lost a career, a country's trust, and twenty years of sleep over what his maps had done—felt something rise in his chest that had nothing to do with fear. He waved them across, one at a time, rope paying out, calling the rhythm: *weight on, weight off, weight on.* Tamsin came first, chattering about honey rolls at double speed. Then Orsen, moving with a river-pilot's balance, reading the flex of the bridge the way she read current. Then Pell, who crossed the entire span without appearing to notice it existed, glancing once into the blue depths with the mild interest of a man checking whether the fish were biting.

When all four stood on the far side, Hale coiled the rope with hands that shook only a little, and allowed himself one breath that was almost a laugh.

"One mile remains," he said. "The worst is behind—"

The mountain interrupted him.

The sound came from everywhere at once—from below, from the walls, from the sky—and it was not a groan this time but a *crack*, a single report like the world's largest mast snapping, and every head turned south. On the flank of the glacier above the route they'd just descended from, a serac the size of a watchtower shuddered, tilted, and began, with horrible unhurried grace, to fall.

"FALLING ICE—MOVE!" Hale was already running, hauling Tamsin by the arm, the rope abandoned, everything abandoned, four people sprinting across the hard blue ice while behind them the serac struck the glacier's shoulder, shattered, and came down the slope as a white avalanche of house-sized blocks, roaring, devouring the distance—

The ice shelf they stood on bucked like a deck in a storm. A crack shot past Hale's boot, splitting the surface, racing ahead of them toward the narrows—and the world tilted. Not much. Ten degrees, maybe fifteen. Enough.

Enough to send Tamsin's feet out from under her, enough to send her sliding toward the fissure that had opened at the shelf's edge, a black seam yawning wide, and Hale dove for her rope and caught it and the rope snapped taut and burned through his gloves and he slid, boots carving twin furrows, toward the same edge—

—and stopped. Something had hold of the rope. Something impossibly strong and utterly calm.

Pell stood braced against a serac with the line wrapped twice around his back, boots planted, face showing the mild concentration of a man tying off a dinghy. Behind him, Orsen had the anchor end over her shoulder, dug in, heels planted, a river pilot holding against a current no river had ever dared throw at her.

"Haul," Pell said.

They hauled. Tamsin came up out of the seam in a shower of snow, gasping, alive, and the four of them collapsed in a tangled heap on the tilted ice, breathing hard, while somewhere behind them the avalanche settled into silence and the dust drifted gold through the light of the Cathedral.

For a long moment, nobody spoke.

Then Tamsin, face-down in the snow, said, muffled: "The bridge. Did the bridge—"

Hale rolled over and looked. Where the ice bridge had spanned the narrows, there was now a gap, clean-edged, forty feet wide. The bridge was gone—dropped whole into the crevasse, or shattered to gravel at its bottom. The route behind them was closed. Completely, irrevocably, closed.

The pass had shut behind them with the soft finality of a door.

"Well," said Pell, into the silence. "No turning back now."

Hale started laughing—he couldn't help it, it came up out of him like water from a cracked aquifer, twenty years of dread converting all at once into something lighter—and Orsen was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen her wear, half fury and half the beginning of a smile, and Tamsin lifted her head and said, "What's funny?" and Hale wiped his eyes and pointed north, past the last mile of broken ice, to where the saddle gleamed above them, close enough now to touch.

"What's funny," he said, "is that for the first time in twenty years, I know exactly where I stand. There's only one road left, friends. It goes over the top of the world—and the storm's coming up behind us fast."

Above the eastern peaks, far off, a line of cloud had begun to build—high, white, anvil-shaped, beautiful, and hungry.
