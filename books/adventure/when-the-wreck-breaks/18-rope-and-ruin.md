## 18. Rope and Ruin

The glacier did not care that Pell was the best of them.

That was the thing about the ice, Orsen had learned in four days on the Corvane — it was not cruel, and it was not kind. The river pretended, sometimes, to have moods. The river sulked and teased and forgave. The glacier simply *was*, vast and patient as arithmetic, and when it opened a mouth under a man's feet, it did not do it out of malice. It did it because a thousand years of snow had decided that spot was hollow, and it had never once cared who walked over the decision.

They were three hours into the crevasse field when it happened.

The morning had been, by mountain standards, almost pleasant. The cold had a clean edge to it, the sky the hard blue of a kiln, and the snow underfoot squeaked like new leather. Tamsin had gone ahead on the rope with Hale, reading the bridge snow the way Orsen read sandbars — a slight sag in the white here, a blue-green shadow under the crust there, a hollow *whumf* under a probe pole that said *not today, friend*. Orsen came third, hauling the sled with the tent and the cook pot, and Pell took the rear because Pell always took the rear. Eleven years on the Vesper's Wake and the man had never once walked in front of anything. Orsen suspected he considered it a moral position.

"Bridge looks good here," Tamsin called back, her voice flattened by the wind into something almost cheerful. "Wide. Solid."

"Define solid," Orsen said.

"Solid enough."

"That's what the river says about every sandbar right before it eats a barge."

"Then trust me," Tamsin said, grinning over her shoulder, "I'm not the river."

Orsen was composing a reply — something dry, something that would make Pell snort the way he did, one syllable through the nose, his entire emotional range expressed in a single exhalation — when the glacier spoke instead.

The sound was not loud. That was the horror of it. No crack, no roar, no drama. Just a soft *whump*, like a fist thumping a mattress, somewhere behind her and below, and the snow under Pell's boots simply stopped being a floor and became a suggestion.

He did not even shout. He made a small sound — *ah*, as if he'd miscounted change — and then he was gone, and the rope between them snapped taut and hauled Orsen two staggering steps toward the hole before her boots found the edge of the bridge and her whole body became a single argument against physics.

"Pell!" Her voice came out of her like something torn. "PELL!"

Silence. Wind. The rope humming with his weight, sixty feet of it vanishing into a slot of blue shadow no wider than a coffin stood on end.

* * *

Later — much later, when she could think about it at all — what Orsen remembered was not the fear. It was how *quietly* competent everyone became. Tamsin dropping her pack and anchoring the rope around her ice axe in three motions she'd clearly done a hundred times in some other, colder life. Hale, gray-faced, wound and all, crawling flat to distribute his weight and shouting the angles in his surveyor's voice, that clipped imperial diction that had once mapped passes for conquerors and was now, at last, being used for something decent. And Orsen herself, hauling, hand over hand, her shoulders screaming, her mind doing the only thing it knew how to do with terror, which was to turn it into work.

"Talk to me!" she shouted down the slot. "Pell! Say something so I know you're not — say something *stupid*, I don't care!"

A pause. Then, drifting up out of the blue dark, thin and echoing and so perfectly, unbearably *Pell*:

"Could be worse. Could be the syndicate."

Orsen laughed — a cracked, awful, half-sobbing sound — and hauled harder.

He had landed on a shelf forty feet down. Not the bottom; the crevasse went deeper, the bottom lost in that particular glacier-blue darkness that seemed to swallow lantern light out of spite. But the shelf held him, and he was alive, and one leg was bent in a way legs were not meant to bend, and when they finally got the spare rope down to him — coiled, weighted, lowered on the second anchor while Tamsin held the first and Hale talked them all through the arithmetic of it — he tied it off with the same unhurried knots he used on the Wake's bowline, as though he were mooring at a dock instead of hanging in the throat of a glacier with a shattered shin.

"Hauling three people's worth of dead weight up a wall of ice," Hale said grimly, checking the anchor for the fourth time, "with two good arms between us. The physics are against us."

"The physics are always against us," Orsen said. "That's never once stopped the river."

They hauled. It took the better part of an hour, and it nearly killed all of them, which was, Orsen supposed, the traditional way of these things. Tamsin's anchor held by what she later admitted was "mostly faith and frozen rope." Hale's wound tore open somewhere in the middle of it and he kept hauling anyway, quietly, until Orsen noticed the red on the snow and made him stop. And when Pell finally came up over the lip — snow-caked, gasping, one leg wrong, his face the color of the ice itself — the first thing he did was look at the anchor, then at Tamsin, and exhale through his nose.

She'd earned the snort. Orsen could see she knew it, too. The girl sat down hard in the snow and put her face in her mittened hands and shook, and Pell, propped against the sled like a felled mast, reached over and patted her boot twice, which from Pell was a eulogy, a love letter, and a medal for valor, all in one.

They made camp two hundred yards on, on solid blue ice Tamsin probed like a suspicious aunt. They got the tent up. They got the little stove going. They splinted Pell's leg as well as they were able, which was not well, with a ski pole and a tent pole and Hale's surveyor's tape, and Pell bore it all with the stoicism of a man who had once watched Orsen run the Kestrel Narrows at flood stage and had apparently concluded that nothing in life would ever again qualify as an emergency.

"Boat's gone, you know," he said.

Orsen looked up from the stove. "What?"

"The Wake." He said it the way he said everything — flat, unhurried, a man reading a shipping notice. "Been meaning to tell you since Coldharbour and kept... not." His eyes went to the tent flap, where Tamsin's shadow moved, then back. "Didn't want it to be true out loud."

The stove hissed. The wind worried the fabric. Orsen found that she had stopped moving entirely, the way animals do when they hope the larger thing will pass them by.

"Say it," she said.

"Man at the chandlery told me. Day we left." Pell's voice was gentle, which was somehow worse. "Syndicate agents. Scuttled her at her moorings. Opened her seams and let her down at the dock like a — " he paused, searching, and Orsen watched him decide against kindness — "like a slaughtered thing. Harbor master's men raised her. Said she was past saving. Sold what was left for scrap." He looked at his splinted leg, then at her. "I'm sorry, Orsen. I should've said. I was a coward about it."

The strange thing — the thing Orsen would turn over in her mind for years afterward — was that there was no roar of grief. She had braced for a roar. She had spent eleven years building the Vesper's Wake plank by plank and payment by payment; she had slept on her, bled on her, run her through water no sane pilot would attempt; the boat was not a boat, she was the shape of Orsen's entire survival, the hull around the only life she'd allowed herself. And now she was scrap at the bottom of a Coldharbour slip, and Orsen sat by a hissing stove on a glacier and felt —

— clear.

That was the word that came, unbidden and terrible. Not empty. *Clear*. The way a river runs clear in a hard frost, every stone visible, nothing left to hide behind. Eleven years she had told herself she could always go back. Back to the Kestrel, back to the Wake, back to the water where a woman could be no one in particular and no one in particular could be hurt. And the whole time, the door had already been shut behind her. The syndicate had closed it in Coldharbour while she'd stood in the chandlery buying rope, and the world had not ended, and she had not even known.

There was nothing to go back to. There never had been, not really — not since the debts, not since the names she wouldn't say, not since the morning she'd cast off and told herself distance was the same thing as safety. The boat had just been the furniture of the exile. The exile itself she had built with her own hands, plank by plank, and she had built it so well she'd forgotten it was built at all.

"Orsen." Pell's voice, careful. "Say something, so I know you're not — say something stupid, I don't care."

She laughed. It came out wet. "You stole my line."

"Stole it fair. You were using it wrong."

* * *

Tamsin brought her tea. That was how Orsen knew the girl had understood — not the details, but the shape of it. Tamsin, whose whole country was at the bottom of a treaty and a rumor, whose birthright was a sealed chest and a dead nation's crest, sat down beside her without a word and handed her a tin cup of tea that was mostly hot water and ambition, and said nothing, and the nothing was exactly right.

"She was a good boat," Tamsin said at last.

"She was a *miserable* boat. Leaked from the day I bought her. Drafted like a barn door. The tiller pulled left in anything over a light breeze and I never did fix it." Orsen stared into the tea. "I loved her like you love a bad knee. Constantly, and with complaints."

Tamsin smiled — small, real. "What'll you do?"

"I don't know." And it was the strangest thing of the whole strange day: she meant it, and it did not frighten her. For eleven years every decision had been made inside a box the size of a riverboat, and now the box was at the bottom of Coldharbour harbor, and the sky in every direction was enormous, and somewhere in the enormousness was a valley full of strangers who might, if the chest said what Tamsin believed it said, be willing to call a river pilot *kin*.

"I used to think going back was the plan," Orsen said slowly. "Any plan. Whatever happened — the chest, the syndicate, the pass — at the end of it I'd point the Wake south and be nobody again. It was the whole architecture. Everything I did was built on top of being able to leave." She turned the cup in her hands. "Turns out the foundation was already gone. I've been standing on air since Coldharbour. And I'm still standing."

Pell, from his nest of blankets, exhaled through his nose. It was, Orsen thought, the warmest sound he'd ever made.

* * *

They did not sleep much. Wounded men and broken legs and glacier cold see to that. Somewhere in the small hours Orsen crawled out to check the anchors — a habit now, Tamsin's habit, catching — and found the girl already there, sitting with her back to a snow wall, watching the ice field under the stars.

The crevasse field glittered to the horizon like a frozen sea, and beyond it, black against the stars, the shoulder of the summit ridge. The storm they'd been outrunning since Coldharbour sat on the southern horizon like a sleeping animal — a smudge, a bruise, a promise. Hale had looked at it that evening with his surveyor's squint and said, quietly, "Two days. Maybe three. After that, this pass is a tomb until spring."

Orsen lowered herself onto the snow beside Tamsin. Below them, faint through the tent canvas, Pell was snoring with the deep contentment of a man who had fallen into the throat of a glacier and been pulled out by people who loved him, and who had apparently decided that was all sorted.

"He almost died today," Tamsin said. "And the first thing I thought — the *first* thing — was that if we lost him, I'd have lost the only person on this ice who never wanted anything from me." She hugged her knees. "Not the chest. Not Vhalen. Just... him."

"He snorts," Orsen said. "Hard to want much from a man whose whole vocabulary is one nostril."

Tamsin laughed, then sobered. "Orsen. The pass. Two days, maybe. And Pell can't walk."

Orsen looked at the summit ridge, black and patient. At the storm, sleeping on the horizon. At the sled, and the ski poles, and the rope coiled by the tent flap, and the four of them — a crippled deckhand, a wounded surveyor, a girl with a dead country's eyes, and a woman with no boat, no door to go back through, and, for the first time in eleven years, nowhere she wanted to be but here.

"We'll build him a litter," she said. "We'll take turns. We'll do the arithmetic Hale does and then we'll ignore it, the way the river ignores physics." She stood, and the cold came up through her boots, and she found that she was not afraid of it. "We go up. Tomorrow. All of us."

Tamsin looked up at her, starlight in her face. "And if the storm catches us on the ridge?"

Orsen thought of the clear river running over every visible stone. She thought of the Wake, down in the dark harbor water, her tiller still pulling left somewhere in the cold.

"Then it catches us *together*," she said, "which is more than it ever caught me before."

Behind them, muffled by canvas, Pell snorted in his sleep.
