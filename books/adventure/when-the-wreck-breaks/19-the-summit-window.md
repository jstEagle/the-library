## 19. The Summit Window

The storm told them it was coming two days before it arrived.

Orsen felt it first in her teeth—a pressure behind her back molars like the river pressing on a hull before a squall. She'd spent eleven years reading water, and she'd learned, somewhere in the last week of ice and switchbacks, that the sky spoke the same language. Clouds stacked themselves the way floodwater stacked against a weir. The wind swung around the way current swung before a sandbar shifted. The mountain was just a river running uphill, she decided, and once she'd made that translation, she stopped feeling quite so much like a drowning woman.

"It'll close by tomorrow night," she said, crouched at the mouth of their shelter—a shallow overhang with a floor of old avalanche debris and a ceiling of stone the color of dirty wool. "Maybe sooner. That cloud over the western peaks is moving wrong."

Tamsin looked up from rewrapping Hale's bandages. "Wrong how?"

"Too fast for its height. It's being pushed from underneath. Warm air rising off the valley." Orsen squinted westward, where the sky had gone the pale, bruised yellow she'd only ever seen before the worst storms on the Kestrel—the ones that ate docks whole. "Same thing happens over the river in spring. Warm air off the flats shoves under the cold, and everything stacks up until it has nowhere to go but down."

"Snow doesn't behave like water."

"Everything behaves like water if you watch it long enough."

Tamsin considered this with the flat, unblinking seriousness she brought to everything, then nodded once, as though Orsen had passed some private examination. "Then we climb today. All the way to the saddle if we can manage it. Once we're through the notch, the storm can do what it likes—it'll spill down the far side and miss us."

"And if we don't make the notch?"

"Then we winter where we stand." Tamsin said it without drama, the way you'd report the price of nails. "Which is to say, we don't."

* * *

Hale was worse than he pretended to be, which was itself a kind of progress; three days ago he'd been pretending not to be dying, and now he'd settled for pretending not to be useless. The wound in his side—taken at the serac field, taken shielding Tamsin, though he'd described it four separate times as "a misunderstanding with some ice"—had gone hot around the edges. He could walk, leaning on Pell's shoulder and his own stubbornness in roughly equal measure, but every hundred yards cost him something he wasn't going to get back on this mountain.

"We could leave me," he offered at the first rest stop, cheerful in the way of a man testing a door to see if it's locked. "I'd make an excellent landmark. Future surveyors would thank you. *Here lies Davor Hale, who mapped these passes honestly and was rewarded with a knife.* Very instructive."

"Shut up, Davor," said Pell.

It was the longest sentence Pell had volunteered in a week, and it landed with such perfect timing that Tamsin snorted into her mittens and even Hale laughed—which turned into a wince, which turned into him gripping Pell's arm while the color drained out of his face and came slowly back.

Orsen watched them arrange themselves for the next stretch: Pell taking Hale's weight without being asked, adjusting his grip higher when the trail steepened, the two of them falling into a rhythm that hadn't existed a month ago. Pell, who had served on the *Vesper's Wake* for six years and spoken perhaps six hundred words in all that time, had somehow become a man who carried people. And Hale, who had come aboard as a stranger begging passage with nothing but guilty knowledge to trade, had become someone they adjusted their pace around—not because they had to, but because leaving him was no longer a thing any of them could imagine doing, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, because saying it would have embarrassed them all terribly.

*This is what a crew is,* Orsen thought, watching Tamsin scout the switchbacks above. *Not people you hired. People you rearrange yourself around.*

The thought should have frightened her. It didn't, and that frightened her more.

* * *

They climbed through the morning in the strange amber light that precedes weather. The glacier pass opened ahead of them like a throat—the Corvane notch, a V-shaped cut between two walls of blue-shadowed ice, still holding its calving-season quiet. Somewhere below and behind them, the glacier groaned at intervals, long tectonic complaints that Orsen had stopped flinching at and started counting, the way she used to count the seconds between lightning and thunder.

"Groan's getting shorter," she told Tamsin at the midday halt. "Ice is moving faster. Warmer than it wants to be."

"You count the groans?"

"Don't you count anything?"

"I count steps," Tamsin admitted. "Since the tollhouse. Eleven thousand and change."

"To where?"

"Nowhere. It's just—" Tamsin flexed her fingers inside her gloves, and for a moment she looked less like a girl carrying a dead nation's birthright and more like exactly what she was: nineteen years old, half-starved until recently, very tired. "On the river, after my mother died, I counted planks. On the dock at Gray Landing there were forty-one, and I'd walk them every day so I'd know the world was still the same world. Counting is how I know I'm still here."

Orsen thought about the sandbars of the Kestrel, each one memorized like a face, checked and rechecked against memory every single voyage. She thought about how she'd called it competence, all those years, and how it had really been the same thing Tamsin did with planks: proof, plank by plank, bar by bar, that the world held still long enough to be known.

"Eleven thousand steps," she said. "Good number. Keep counting."

* * *

The trouble came at midafternoon, where the trail crossed a rib of bare rock between two snowfields, and the wind arrived all at once like a door kicked open.

It hit them sideways, freighted with ice crystals that stung like gravel, and Orsen heard Hale cry out—not from the cold but from the shove of it, his bad side taking the brunt as he staggered toward the drop. Pell caught his collar one-handed and slammed him gently against the rock face, and for a moment the four of them stood pinned there like laundry, breath ripped away, the world reduced to white noise and white light.

"Tamsin!" Orsen shouted into the wind, and pointed up-trail, where the route bent around a shoulder of stone. "How far to shelter?"

"Twenty minutes! There's a boulder field past the shoulder—big ones, house-sized!"

"Can Hale make twenty minutes?"

Tamsin looked at Hale. Hale, flattened against the rock, gray-lipped, looked back at her with an expression of furious honesty and shook his head slowly: *probably not.*

"He says probably not," Tamsin reported.

"He's lying," said Pell. It was nearly a speech, for Pell. "He lies about walking. Always has."

And there it was—the moment Orsen would remember afterward, in the valley, in the spring, whenever anyone asked her when exactly the *Vesper's Wake* had stopped being a boat and started being a crew. Because Pell, who never argued, argued. Tamsin, who trusted no one, immediately believed him. And Orsen, who had built eleven years out of refusing passengers and outrunning debts, heard herself say:

"Then we make him a sledge."

* * *

They made it from the rope, the oilskin groundcloth, and two tent poles lashed into runners, and it took eleven minutes they did not have, and it worked badly, and it worked. They loaded Hale onto it like cargo—his protests lasting approximately thirty seconds before the relief of not walking shut him up—and took turns hauling, two abreast on the rope, Pell always on the downhill side because he was heavier and because none of them discussed it.

Orsen hauled and read the sky.

That was the thing she kept coming back to, the thing that made the climbing almost bearable: she could feel the storm the way she felt the river through the deck of her boat. The pressure in her teeth. The taste of the wind—iron and ozone, the same taste the Kestrel carried before a spring flood. The clouds overhead had organized themselves into long parallel bands, combed eastward, and beneath them the air had gone unnaturally still and clear, a hole punched in the middle of the weather.

"That clear patch is the eye of it," she called to Tamsin. "We're inside the storm's own schedule. It'll hold maybe ninety minutes, then the back side comes down hard. Snow you wouldn't believe."

"How do you know?"

"Because rivers do the same thing. Flood crests, drops, looks finished—and then the second crest comes down from upstream and kills everyone who came out to look at the first one." She set her shoulder into the rope. "Ninety minutes. Move."

* * *

The boulder field, when they reached it, was better than shelter—it was architecture. House-sized blocks of granite, dropped by some ancient avalanche, stood tilted against one another in configurations that left caves and corridors, and Tamsin threaded them through it with the certainty of someone who had grown up playing in exactly such places. She found them a hollow beneath a leaning slab, deep enough to lie down in, high enough to sit, with a wind shadow in front of it wide enough for the little stove.

Pell got Hale inside and got the wet boots off him, working with those big careful hands, and Orsen got the stove lit in the lee of the rock, cupping the flame with her body while the wind tried to steal it. Tamsin melted snow. Nobody spoke much. There was a particular silence a group falls into when it has been working as one body for hours—the silence of oars shipped after a long pull, everyone breathing together without meaning to.

Hale broke it, naturally, because Hale could not bear a silence the way other men couldn't bear a debt.

"I want it noted," he said from the depths of the hollow, muffled and dignified, "that I have been transported across a glacier on a sledge made of tent poles, like a lady of quality, and that I intend to describe it that way in my memoirs."

"Noted," said Pell.

"'The men carried me tenderly,'" Hale went on, warming. "'The women refused to abandon me despite ample provocation. The girl counted my pulse with the sternness of a harbor master.' Tamsin, is that accurate? Am I being sternly harbormastered?"

"Your pulse is fast because you're feverish and you talk too much," Tamsin said, but she was smiling, there in the stove-light, and her hand stayed on his wrist a moment longer than the counting required.

Orsen watched them from the stove and felt something in her chest that had no name she wanted to give it. Eleven years she had kept her cargo dry and her heart drier, and here she was, huddled in a rock cave on a drowned nation's doorstep, warming snow for a disgraced mapmaker and a girl with a dead country's eyes, and the terrible truth—the truth she'd been circling since the flood gave back that chest—was that she didn't want to be anywhere else. Survival bought alone was just a slower way of drowning. Somebody had to say it eventually. She suspected, with grim resignation, that it would end up being her.

* * *

The back side of the storm arrived ninety-three minutes later, and Orsen knew it to the minute, and when she called it—"Now. Here it comes."—and the wall of snow descended on the boulder field like a curtain yanked across a stage, Tamsin looked at her with an expression Orsen had never seen on her before: open, undisguised awe.

"You called it," Tamsin said. "To the minute."

"Rivers," Orsen said, and shrugged, and discovered she was pleased, enormously, childishly pleased, and that there was no longer any point pretending otherwise.

They rode out the worst of it in the hollow, packed together for warmth, Hale in the middle because he was the coldest and complained the least about being crowded. The wind screamed over the top of their slab and the snow came down so thick that the world beyond arm's reach simply ceased, and in that small warm pocket of lamplight and shared breath, Pell did something unprecedented: he talked.

Not much. A few sentences, doled out like coins from a tight fist. About a sister, upriver, he hadn't seen in nine years. About why he'd signed onto the *Vesper's Wake*—because Orsen never asked questions, and that had seemed like heaven at the time. About how asking questions had started to seem less like heaven lately.

"Is that so," said Orsen carefully.

"Mm." Pell pulled his cap down. "Boat's gone anyway."

The words landed in the hollow and sat there. Everyone had known it since Coldharbour—the syndicate agents, the scuttled hull, the *Vesper's Wake* sitting in the mud of the harbor with her seams opened like a gutted fish—but nobody had said it aloud in front of Orsen, and Orsen realized, with a strange floating sensation, that they had been protecting her from it, and that she had let them, and that the grief when it finally came was real but not bottomless, because the boat had been armor, and armor you outgrow doesn't count as lost.

"She was a good boat," Orsen said at last.

"She was," said Pell.

"She leaked."

"She leaked," Pell agreed, and something that was almost a smile moved under his beard, and Tamsin laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and even Hale managed a wheeze of amusement from his nest of blankets, and the storm howled on above them, and none of it, Orsen thought, none of it could touch them tonight.

* * *

They climbed out of the boulder field at first light into a world scoured clean and blinding, and the notch stood above them, close now, close enough to see the wind pluming off its lip like smoke off a chimney.

The last mile was the hardest. The storm had filled the upper trail knee-deep in powder over ice, and Hale could not walk it, and the sledge could not be dragged up it, so they did the thing Orsen would once have called impossible and now merely called Tuesday: they took turns carrying him. Pell and Orsen traded his weight every fifty yards, Tamsin breaking trail ahead, calling holds—"rock, left foot, trust the rock, not the snow"—her mountain voice steady as a bell. Orsen, river creature, slipped twice and was steadied both times by Tamsin's grip without a word being wasted on it, and understood that this too was the new arithmetic: vulnerability passed hand to hand like a tool, no shame in it, everyone's turn coming round.

And then, near noon, with the wind rising again and the window visibly narrowing—the plume off the notch thickening, bending, beginning to stream sideways instead of up—they came over the last rise, and the pass lay open before them.

Beyond the notch, the land fell away southward into a long hidden valley, blue with distance, veined with a frozen river, terraced and green-remembered even under snow. Smoke rose from somewhere in its depths—thin threads of it, dozens of chimneys' worth, rising straight in the cold air.

Smoke meant hearths. Hearths meant people. People meant Vhalen.

Nobody cheered. It was too big for cheering. Tamsin stood at the notch with the wind pulling at her hair and her hand pressed flat against the chest strapped to her back, and she was crying without any of the noises that usually accompany crying, just tears freezing on her cheeks, and Pell put one enormous hand briefly on her shoulder, and Hale, slung between them, whispered something in a language none of the rest of them knew, soft as a prayer.

Orsen looked at the valley, and then at the sky, and her stomach dropped.

Because the sky over the southern approach—the far pass, the one the syndicate riders would use—was doing something wrong. Clouds stacking. Warm air pushing underneath. The same signature she'd taught Tamsin to read two days ago, written now over the enemy's road.

"They're coming," she said quietly. "Through the southern pass. A day behind us, maybe less—and they'll have the same weather window we did."

Tamsin wiped her face with the back of her glove, and when she turned, the tears were already freezing forgotten and her eyes had gone hard and bright as the ice around them.

"Then we'd better get down there first," she said, "and be waiting when they arrive."
