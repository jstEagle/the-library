## 20. Descent Into Vhalen's Valley

The mountain let them through the way a miser lets go of a coin—slowly, grudgingly, and with every sign it might snatch the bargain back.

Tamsin felt it in her boots before she saw anything: the trail tilting, at last, toward down instead of up. Eleven days of switchbacks, of wind that peeled the skin off her lips, of carrying Hale's weight in shifts while he insisted, hoarsely, that he could walk fine on his own leg if only his own leg would agree to exist. And now the grade softened underfoot, and the pines came back—stunted, wind-bent things at first, then taller, then a forest proper—and the air lost its knife-edge and turned green-smelling, and Tamsin had to stop twice on the same stretch of trail because her eyes kept filling up and she refused to weep in front of Orsen Ravensworth until she'd had a chance to do it privately.

"You're leaking," Orsen said behind her. Not unkindly. The way she'd say *you're listing to port*.

"Altitude," Tamsin said.

"Mm." Orsen looked past her, down the slope, where the trees opened onto a long green shoulder of land and beyond that—a haze. Not cloud haze. Smoke haze. Woodsmoke, hearthsmoke, the smoke of chimneys. "That's not altitude."

Pell said nothing, which from Pell was a shout of joy. He'd been quiet since the crevasse—the bad one, the one that had nearly taken him and very nearly took Orsen after—but his hand found Tamsin's shoulder as they stood there, one squeeze, brief and hard, and that was its own kind of speech.

Below them, threaded through the valley like a vein of silver, ran a river.

Tamsin knew rivers now. She'd spent a season of her life learning their moods from a woman who read water the way priests read scripture. But this river stopped her heart anyway, because she knew its name without being told. Everyone born of Vhalen's scattered blood knew the name of the Vessa, even if they'd never seen it, even if the maps sold in imperial cities showed the valley as blank white labeled *UNSETTLED — GLACIER RETREAT*. Her mother had sung about it. Badly. Off-key, into the dark of whatever cellar or attic or borrowed room they were hiding in that month, songs about a river that ran cold and clean through a country that no longer existed.

Except it did exist. It was right there. Chimney smoke and all.

"Hale," Orsen called back over her shoulder. "You seeing this?"

Hale came up the line leaning on a staff cut from a pine, his bandaged leg held stiff, his face gray with pain and eleven days of being carried by people he was certain didn't like him. He looked down at the valley for a long moment. Then he took off his ruined hat, which was the most emotional thing Tamsin had ever seen him do.

"The survey maps called it a cirque," he said quietly. "A dead-end bowl of ice. I drew it myself. I drew it *empty*." His voice caught on something. "They were already here when I mapped it. They watched me draw their valley off the face of the world, and they let me, because a blank spot on an imperial map is the safest wall ever built."

"They hid inside your lie," Tamsin said.

"I hid them inside my lie," Hale corrected, and there was something in his face—not pride exactly, but the next thing over from pride, the thing pride becomes when it's been dragged through twenty years of shame and comes out the far side humbled. "Best work I ever did. Worst thing I ever did. Same map."

Orsen snorted. "Welcome to my entire philosophy of river piloting."

And because none of them knew what else to do—they were four exhausted, half-starved, grieving people standing on a ridge above a country come back from the dead—Tamsin started walking downhill, and the others followed, and that was how the heirs and outcasts and river-rats of Vhalen came home: single file, limping, arguing mildly about who would cook dinner.

* * *

The valley received them the way deep water receives a stone: without fuss, but with total awareness.

Tamsin noticed the watchers before anyone announced them. A figure among the pines, still as a trunk. Another on the far slope, moving parallel. By the time they reached the first terraced field—barley, impossibly, growing at the roof of the world—there were a dozen of them, and they made no effort to hide anymore. They were dressed in wool dyed the gray-green of the valley itself, and they carried themselves with the unhurried confidence of people who had spent a generation learning exactly how much space they owned and not one inch more.

An old woman stepped out from between two barley rows as if she'd been waiting there since morning, which, Tamsin suspected, she had.

She was small and straight-backed, with white hair braided in the old style—coiled at the nape, pinned with what looked like a fishbone—and eyes the color of thaw-water. She looked at Tamsin for a long time. Not at Orsen, not at the tall wounded foreigner, not at the enormous silent deckhand. At Tamsin. At her face, specifically, the way you'd compare a face against another face kept somewhere behind the eyes.

"You have your grandmother's chin," the old woman said, in Vhaleni-accented Common. "And your mother's terrible habit of arriving unannounced."

Tamsin's whole body went hot and cold at once. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew everyone's mother. I'm Maren. I keep the rolls." She said it the way other people said *I command the garrison*. Then her gaze slid past Tamsin and sharpened. "Davor Hale."

Hale stiffened. "Madam Keeper."

"You've gotten old."

"You've gotten *found*," Hale said. "I spent twenty years assuming the valley was empty because I wanted it to be empty. Empty meant safe. You're the reason I could sleep at night, and I never knew your names."

"We knew yours," Maren said, without warmth or chill. "We argued about it every winter solstice for fifteen years. Whether the mapmaker was a traitor or a tool." She looked at his bandaged leg, at the four of them—muddy, bloodstained, snowburned, carrying each other's gear without appearing to notice they were doing it. Something in her expression shifted, some ledger entry amended. "The argument appears to be settling itself. Come. There's broth. You'll want to sit down before you hear the rest, because the rest involves titles, and titles are best absorbed sitting."

* * *

The hall was low-beamed and warm and smelled of pine resin and barley bread, and it was full.

Not crowded—full. Every bench occupied, every face turned toward the door when they entered, and Tamsin felt the weight of those faces like weather. Old people and young ones, children peeking around their mothers' legs, a blacksmith with forearms like hams, a girl of maybe ten staring at Pell with open worship, presumably because Pell was the largest object the child had ever seen indoors.

They ate first. That was the Vhaleni way, Maren explained, or rather didn't explain, simply enforced: no council, no questions, no decisions of any weight until everyone at the table had eaten and drunk and been seen to. So Tamsin sat between Orsen and Hale and ate barley soup with dumplings that tasted like every homesick dream she'd ever had, and watched Orsen Ravensworth—woman of the open river, refuser of passengers, keeper of distances—gradually relax by degrees so small you'd need instruments to measure them, until her shoulders came down from around her ears and she accepted a second helping without pretending she hadn't wanted it.

Then the benches were cleared, and an old man rose, and the room went quiet, and Tamsin understood that the meal had been the easy part.

He was introduced as Elder Corvin, though half the room seemed older than he was. He spoke in Vhaleni first, and Tamsin understood perhaps one word in three—her mother had taught her kitchen-Vhaleni, lullaby-Vhaleni, the language of hiding—and then again in Common, for the guests.

"A generation ago," Corvin said, "our country was sold. Not conquered—sold. The passes were opened from within; the garrisons were fed false musters; the fleet at Vessa-mouth was ordered to stand down by men whose signatures we have spent twenty years trying to recover." His eyes went to the chest, which sat between Tamsin's feet like a sleeping dog. "And now a girl walks out of the world carrying those signatures in a box."

"It's not a box of signatures," Tamsin said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "It's proof. Of who did it. Of who paid them. Of—" She glanced at Hale. "Of who didn't."

"We know what it is," Corvin said gently. "We have known since the flood sent word ahead of you. We have people on the Kestrel, child. We have always had people everywhere. What we did not know was whether anyone would be fool enough and stubborn enough to carry it through the pass." He looked around the room, and the room looked back, and Tamsin realized with a jolt that they were all looking at *her*, and that this had been arranged, and that she hated it. "So. The question before this council is an old one, asked new. The royal house of Vhalen ended in fire, but blood does not end. It wanders. It hides in cellars and sings off-key. And sometimes it walks home through a glacier pass with a chest and three strangers."

"There's a condition coming," Orsen murmured beside her. "There's always a condition. Ask me how I know."

"Shh," Tamsin said.

Corvin spread his hands. "The council offers you what remains of your inheritance. Recognition. Standing. The seat of the heir—the heir presumptive, until the elders confirm, but the seat nonetheless. A people. A purpose. A country, such as it is—one valley, four hundred souls, and a great deal of barley."

The room was absolutely silent. Tamsin's heart was doing something complicated and painful.

"And the strings?" she said.

Maren smiled thinly, from the side. "She asks about strings. Good. Her mother asked about strings too, and then married a man with a whole harness."

"The conditions," Corvin acknowledged, "are these. First: the documents remain here, under council seal, to be read aloud publicly when we choose the moment. Not before. Timing is armor; premature truth is just noise."

"That's fair," Tamsin said slowly.

"Second: the syndicate will come. They may already be coming—we have reports of riders on the southern approach. When they come, this valley answers as one body. No private wars. No personal debts settled at the valley's expense." His gaze moved, deliberate as a searchlight, across Orsen, Pell, and Hale. "Which brings us to the third condition. The heir's household must be *clean*. No fugitives. No disgraced imperial officers. No smugglers of unknown registry. Those who stand beside the heir must stand openly, under their own names, answerable to this council—or they must go."

The silence that followed had a texture. Tamsin felt it against her skin.

"They saved my life," she said. "All of them. Repeatedly. Usually against their will."

"Then they will be treated generously on their way," Corvin said. "There is coin. There is escort to the lowlands. The river pilot's boat, we understand, is lost—"

"It was scuttled," Orsen said. Flat. Level. Tamsin heard what it cost her to keep it level, the way you hear a rope creak under load. "Your people on the Kestrel might have mentioned that too."

"—and can be replaced," Corvin finished smoothly. "The valley has funds. The valley has gratitude. But gratitude and residence are different ledgers, child. This is not cruelty. It is survival. One informer among us, one compromised friend of the heir, and four hundred people burn. You of all people know what compromise costs. Your whole family burned of it."

Tamsin looked down at her hands.

She thought about the serac field, and Hale throwing his body sideways with a sound like tearing canvas, taking the crossbow bolt meant for her. She thought about Pell going down into the blue dark of the crevasse and the rope going taut and Orsen's voice—Orsen's voice, cracked clean through, screaming *hold, HOLD* like a prayer with the religion stripped out. She thought about a fog bank on the Kestrel and a woman threading shallows no charted pilot would dare, because a half-starved girl with a dead country's seal had asked her to.

Belonging. Here it was, offered on a plate, warm bread and all. A people. A name spoken without flinching. Everything she'd crawled through snow for.

With strings that ran straight through the chests of the only three people who had ever chosen her without checking her bloodline first.

"I need to walk," Tamsin said, and stood up, and walked out.

* * *

She found the river, because of course she did. Some instincts are inherited.

The Vessa ran shallow and quick over stones, loud in the evening hush, and Tamsin sat on the bank with her knees drawn up and tried to think like Orsen instead of feeling like herself, because feeling like herself wasn't working.

Orsen would say: *read the water*. Where's the current actually going? Not where the surface claims. Underneath.

Underneath, the council wasn't wrong. That was the miserable part. Four hundred people hiding for a generation, alive because they trusted no one—that wasn't cowardice, that was engineering. Tamsin had spent her whole childhood watching her mother pay rent in suspicion. She knew exactly what one loose thread cost.

But underneath underneath, deeper than the engineering, was the thing Corvin hadn't said out loud: *the strangers are temporary. Blood is permanent.* They'd offer her a throne built on the graves of her family and expect her to be grateful that they'd leave her friends alive and funded on their way out of it.

Footsteps on the bank. She didn't turn. She knew the tread—light, deliberate, a woman who'd spent eleven years reading sandbars and couldn't help reading riverbanks too.

"If you tell me to read the water," Tamsin said, "I'll throw you in it."

"Wouldn't dream of it." Orsen sat down beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to pretend she wasn't. For a while she said nothing, which was Orsen's native language, fluently spoken. Then: "For what it's worth, I told them no."

Tamsin turned. "What?"

"While you were storming out. Corvin asked—politely, the way empires ask—if the pilot and her crew would consent to withdraw north with an escort and a purse. I told him the purse was insulting and the escort was worse, and that we'd stay exactly as long as you needed us and not one day less, and that if he had a problem with the company you keep, he could take it up with the company." She picked up a flat stone and skipped it, badly, three hops. "Probably set negotiations back a week. Worth it."

Tamsin laughed, wetly, disgustingly. "You're supposed to want to leave. You've been wanting to leave since the dock."

"Yeah, well." Orsen skipped another stone. Two hops. She frowned at it like it had failed her personally. "Turns out I lie about some things too."

They sat. The river talked. Somewhere up the bank, Pell's laugh rolled out—rare as summer snow, unmistakably at something Hale had said—and Tamsin felt it land in her chest like a coal.

"They offered me everything," she said quietly. "My name. My country. My grandmother's chair, probably. All I have to do is trade the crew for the crown."

"What are you going to do?"

"I don't know." And then, because lying to Orsen had become impossible somewhere around the fog banks: "Yes I do. I know exactly what I'm going to do. I just don't know how to do it without getting everyone killed, including possibly me, and definitely the four hundred people in that hall."

Orsen nodded slowly, the way she nodded at a stretch of water that was going to be trouble and was worth respecting. "Well," she said. "Good news. Getting everyone killed is sort of our specialty. We've gotten remarkably good at *not* managing it."

Before Tamsin could answer, a shape came down the bank at a jog—young, one of the watchers from the pines, breathing hard. He sketched a hurried bow to both of them.

"Beg pardon. Keeper Maren sends word." He caught his breath. "Riders on the southern approach. Syndicate colors. Six of them, maybe eight, moving fast—and they're flying a parley banner, but they're also flying the chest-claim writ, sealed at Coldharbour." He hesitated, glancing between them. "They're asking for the heir by name, ma'am. And for the pilot."

Tamsin and Orsen looked at each other.

"Well," Orsen said, standing, brushing off her trousers, and Tamsin heard it in her voice—the old river-pilot calm laid over something that wasn't calm at all, the voice of a woman reading water right before the rapids. "Every debt comes due eventually. Might as well be owed to family."

They walked back up the bank together, toward the lit windows of the hall, where the council waited, and the chest waited, and the future came up the southern trail wearing syndicate colors.
