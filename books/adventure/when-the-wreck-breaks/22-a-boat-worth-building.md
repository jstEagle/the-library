## 22. A Boat Worth Building

The ice on the Kestrel broke up in the third week of March, and Orsen heard it from a hundred miles away.

That was the thing about a river—you never really left it. It lived in your ears, in the way you woke before dawn without an alarm, in how you read weather in the smell of the wind off the water. She'd spent eleven years telling herself the Vesper's Wake was her home, and eleven years lying, because a boat was not a home. A boat was a way of staying in motion so you never had to find out what you were when you stopped.

She found out in Vhalen's valley, in a guesthouse with a crooked door and a hearth that smoked unless you fed it just so, and it turned out that what she was, when she stopped moving, was tired. Deeply, bone-deep tired, the kind you can only feel once you're safe enough to feel anything at all.

She slept for three days. Nobody woke her. That alone told her more about where she'd landed than any speech the elders could have given.

* * *

Spring came up the valley like a rumor spreading. First the willows went gold along the streambeds, then green. The terraces above the village turned from brown to a green so bright it looked wet. Tamsin's people—her people now, though they'd been careful to make clear she could refuse—came down from the high pastures with the first lambs, and the whole settlement smelled of dung and milk and woodsmoke and thawing earth, which Orsen decided was the best smell in the world, better than tar, better than rain on hot deck planks, and she would have said so out loud except Pell would have given her a look.

Pell had opinions about everything these days. He expressed them mostly by fixing things.

He'd arrived in the valley half-frozen and hollow-eyed after the crevasse, wrapped in rope burns and frostbite and the particular silence of a man who had nearly died and hadn't yet decided how he felt about being alive. For two weeks he barely spoke. Then one morning Orsen came out of the guesthouse and found him in the elder Maren's woodshed, repairing a chair that had not needed repair, and after that there was no stopping him. He fixed the mill gate. He fixed the smokehouse hinges. He re-hung every crooked door in the village, including the guesthouse door, twice, because he claimed it still stuck, and Orsen suspected he simply liked having something to argue with that couldn't argue back.

"You're nesting," she told him one evening, watching him plane a board with the concentration of a man defusing a trap.

"I'm being useful."

"You're nesting. You've got shavings in your beard."

He wiped his beard with the back of his hand, considered the shavings, and went back to planing. "You're one to talk. You've been standing at the stream fork for an hour."

Orsen looked downstream, where the meltwater ran fast and loud over stones the color of old iron. She had been standing there a while. Downstream meant out. Downstream meant the Kestrel, eventually, if a body followed the water far enough—and the syndicate's writ ran along the Kestrel like rust along a hull, even with their agents exposed, even with the elders' testimony traveling south with the spring caravans. There would be hearings. There would be men in good coats saying *the Vesper's Wake was lost, regrettable, an act of nature.* There might even be restitution, someday, in the way that institutions pay debts: slowly, grudgingly, and to someone else.

Her boat was at the bottom of Coldharbour harbor, scuttled in the dark by men who thought they were ending something.

"They didn't," she said aloud, not meaning to say it aloud.

Pell set down the plane. "Didn't what?"

"End anything." She turned from the water. "Come on. We're going to go bother Maren."

* * *

Maren received them in the longhouse, in a room that smelled of cedar and lamp oil, surrounded by maps that were older than the treaty that had drowned her country. She listened to Orsen's proposal with her hands folded and her face doing nothing at all, which Orsen had learned was how Vhaleni people listened when they were very interested indeed.

"A vessel," Maren said. "Built here. Crewed by—" she glanced at Tamsin, who was sprawled in a chair pretending not to be part of the conversation and fooling nobody, "—us."

"Riverboat. Shallow draft, stern paddle, tough as sin. I know a builder in Gray Landing who owes me his life twice over and money besides. Timber we can cut here; there's black pine on the north slopes that'll steam fair. And the valley needs a way in and out that doesn't depend on the pass staying open or the syndicate's goodwill." Orsen leaned forward. "You've got a country again. Countries need doors."

"And you need a boat," Maren said gently.

Orsen sat back. "I need work."

"Of course." Maren's eyes crinkled. "We'll call it work."

* * *

It took six weeks to fall the pines and another four to pit-saw them into planks, and Orsen discovered that building a boat was like running a river in reverse: all the decisions you made blind, made visible at once, stacked in ribs and stringers where everyone could see your mistakes forever. Hale loved it. He'd taken to the valley like ink to water, mapping everything—the terraces, the stream gauges, the old trade paths under the moss—and the elders had given him a room in the archive tower, where he worked with the patient ferocity of a man paying down a debt he'd carried twenty years. He drew the keel template himself, full-scale, in lime on the floor of the new boathouse, and then stood over it for an entire afternoon checking his own arithmetic against charts he'd drawn in another life.

"Your curves are wrong," Tamsin told him, wandering through with an armful of nails.

"My curves are *correct*. They're the same lines I used for the survey barges."

"Which sank."

"The *barges* did not sink. The *route* did." But he crouched, squinting at the lime line, adjusting it a finger's width, and Tamsin grinned and kept walking, and Orsen watched the two of them and felt something in her chest do a slow, unfamiliar turn.

They bickered like family. That was the word her mind supplied, unasked, and she let it stand.

* * *

Keel-laying day was the first of June, chosen by the elders for luck and by Pell because the timber had finished curing and he refused to wait on superstition. The whole village came out. Somebody's grandmother brought honey cakes. Somebody else's uncle brought a fiddle, and somebody's opinion about the fiddle.

The keel timber lay on skids in the boathouse yard—a single black pine log, sixty feet if it was an inch, squared and true, dark as river mud. Orsen had picked it herself off the north slope, and when she'd laid her palm against the stump afterward she'd felt briefly, absurdly, like apologizing.

Maren spoke first, in Vhaleni, low and rolling, words Orsen didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand the shape of a blessing regardless of its language. Then she switched to the river tongue. "A keel is a promise," she said. "Everything else hangs from it. So choose what you hang carefully."

Tamsin went first. She'd grown over the winter—not taller exactly, though she'd shot up some, but filled-out, fed, her dead-country eyes gone bright instead of hungry. She knelt at the head of the timber and drove a silver coin into the wood with three blows, the old Vhaleni rite, and when she stood up she was crying and pretending very hard that she wasn't, and nobody pretended to believe her.

Hale drove in an iron bolt from the old survey expedition—one of the few things he'd salvaged from that ruin of a career. "For honest work," he said, "finally done honestly."

Pell drove in a spike he'd pulled from the wreck of the Vesper's Wake itself, dredged up from Coldharbour harbor by a salvage crew the elders had hired, along with her bell, her wheel, and her nameboard. They sat in the boathouse now, cleaned and waiting, and Orsen had not been able to look at them directly for weeks.

Then it was her turn.

She'd planned to drive a simple spike. She'd had one in her pocket since morning. Instead she found herself walking the length of the keel first, slow, her hand trailing along the grain, and the crowd quieted, because everyone knows the walk of a person talking to a ghost.

"Eleven years," she said, to the timber, to the boat that wasn't there, to the woman she'd been at the wheel. "You kept me alive. That's not nothing. Most days it was everything." Her voice caught and she let it. "But I kept you between me and everybody else, and that's not living, that's just drowning slower. You taught me to read water. Turns out I needed somebody to teach me to read people, and I was too proud to learn until the river took you away." She took out the spike—the one from the wreck, the one Pell had saved for her without saying so, because Pell saved things without saying so—and set it against the oak. "So this one's not for keeping afloat. This one's for coming home."

She drove it home in four blows. The sound rang out across the yard like a bell, and the fiddle struck up, and the honey cakes made their rounds, and it was done.

"What do we call her?" Tamsin asked, appearing at Orsen's elbow with cake crumbs on her chin.

Orsen looked at the nameboard leaning against the boathouse wall, the old letters flaked but legible. *VESPER'S WAKE.*

"Same name," she said. "New wake."

"That's not clever, that's just sentimental."

"I'm allowed. I'm old."

"You're forty-two."

"*Old,*" Orsen repeated with dignity, and stole the rest of Tamsin's cake.

* * *

They launched her at the end of August, on a high bright morning with the meltwater running strong. Orsen took her out alone for the first hour—tradition, and also necessity, because a new hull talks to you and you have to listen—and the Vesper's Wake answered the current like she'd been born to it, quick and sure, riding light. When Orsen finally brought her alongside the landing, the whole valley was on the bank, and Tamsin was already scrambling aboard before the line was thrown, and Pell was already complaining about her boots on his fresh deck, and Hale was already asking whether anyone had thought to bring the charts, and Orsen stood at the wheel with the sun on her face and realized she wasn't scanning the horizon for exits.

She was just looking at them.

"Where to, Captain?" Tamsin asked, hand on the rail, grinning like the whole river belonged to her.

Orsen considered. Downstream lay the Kestrel, the hearings, the syndicate's lawyers, the long slow work of making a country's enemies answer for it. Upstream lay the headwaters, unmapped, Hale practically vibrating at the possibility. Somewhere in between lay sandbars she'd read like faces for eleven years, and faces she'd read like sandbars for eleven years, and she knew now those had always been the same skill.

"Home first," she said. "Then anywhere."

And for the first time in eleven years, Orsen Ravensworth stopped moving—not because stopping was safe, but because, for once, she had somewhere worth being still.
