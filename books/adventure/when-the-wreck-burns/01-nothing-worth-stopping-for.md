## 1. Nothing Worth Stopping For

The thief's tracks told the truth his mouth never would have. Marguerite Coldharbour read them the way other people read letters from home — slowly, hungrily, with a finger tracing each line.

Left hind shoe thrown. He'd know it by now, would be listening for that hollow triple-beat on hardpan, and there was nothing he could do about it but pray for soft ground. He'd cut north off the wagon road around midnight, which meant he'd seen her campfire and panicked, and panic made men stupid in predictable ways. They went where the land looked easiest instead of where the water was.

There was no water out here. That was the whole point of the Ashfall Flats.

She sat her dun mare, Peso, at the crest of a rise and let the animal blow while she swept the country ahead with her field glasses. The flats ran silver and dead to the horizon, cracked clay glinting like spilled coins, heat shimmer standing everything up on end. A mile out, maybe a little more, a dark seam broke the white — an old dry well with a stone apron and a windmill skeleton leaning over it like a hanged man. The only shadow in ten square miles.

A smart man would ride past it. A tired man would stop.

Calloway had been running for two days. He wasn't smart anymore. He was tired.

Marguerite had been chasing him for those same two days, and she knew exactly how tired felt, because she'd been running longer than that. Eleven years of it. She'd stopped once, outside of Perdition Bend — taken a room above the assay office, hung a coat on a nail, learned the name of the woman who sold oranges at the corner. Eight months. Then the fever came through and took the orange-seller, and Marguerite had stood at the graveside understanding something simple: every place you stay, the staying costs somebody. Stay long enough and you'll bury them or they'll bury you, and either way you're the one left holding the shovel.

So she didn't stay. She rode contracts up and down the territory — stolen stock, skipped bondsmen, the occasional murderer if the fee held — and she kept her kit packed and her bedroll rolled and her heart in the same condition as the flats: nothing growing, nothing worth watering.

The horse thief was just work. Two hundred dollars of work, plus expenses. She repeated that to herself as she put Peso down the slope at a canter, angling wide so the well's shadow wouldn't see her coming until it was too late to matter.

* * *

He heard her at three hundred yards. Of course he did. There was no cover and no wind and the dun's hooves rang on the clay like a dinner bell, so Calloway did the only thing left to him: he stepped out from behind the windmill skeleton with his hands raised and his hat hanging off one knee, and he waited.

Marguerite reined in at fifty feet and sat very still, letting him look down the barrel of her rifle without hurrying about it.

"You're the ranger," he called. His voice carried flat and clear across the hardpan. "Coldharbour. I figured you'd quit."

"Figured wrong."

"I figured everybody quits eventually." He shifted his weight, easy, conversational, and she noted the Colt riding low on his hip and the way his thumb drifted toward it without quite committing. "You've been behind me since the Salt Lick. That's a long way to chase one horse."

"It's a good horse."

"It's a *fast* horse," he said. "That's different."

"Same thing out here."

He laughed — actually laughed, a short bark of genuine amusement, and she hated him a little for it, because it was the first laugh she'd heard in weeks and it landed somewhere under her ribs and stuck. "You always talk this much?"

"Only when I'm working."

"And this is work?" He spread his hands wider, showing her the whole sad theater of himself: dust-caked coat, three days of beard, eyes red-rimmed and bright. "One horse? Ranger, there are men upstream killing people over water, and the company pays your salary through taxes, and you're spending the territory's money chasing me across a salt flat for one bay gelding."

"The gelding belongs to a widow," Marguerite said. "Her name is Ada Tull. She needs that horse to get her kids to school and her crop to market, and she cried when she told me about it, and then she apologized for crying, which is worse. So yes. This is work."

Something moved behind Calloway's face — not guilt exactly, but its cousin. "I was going to sell it back to her," he said. "Half price. I do that sometimes."

"That doesn't make you a good man. It makes you a cheap one."

"Fair." He nodded slowly, and his thumb settled onto the hammer of the Colt, not drawing, just resting, the way a man rests his hand on a fence rail. "Here's my problem, Ranger. If I give up, I go to jail in Corriander Flats, and Consolidated runs the jail there, and I've seen what happens to men who go into Consolidated jails with debts unpaid. So I'm going to need you to believe me when I say I'd rather take my chances with you."

"With me?"

"With the rifle." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, all teeth and exhaustion. "You seem like you'd make it quick."

Marguerite had heard a hundred men talk their way toward a gun. She'd buried four of them herself and signed the paperwork on a dozen more. Every one of them had sounded reasonable right up until the moment they weren't, and she'd learned to stop listening to the words and start watching the hands.

His hands were steady. Too steady. A man that tired shouldn't have steady hands unless he'd already decided, and decided men were the most dangerous kind, because they'd already grieved themselves and didn't care what happened next.

"Calloway," she said. "Put it down."

"Give me a reason."

"You'll die thirsty otherwise. That well's been dry twenty years."

"So have I," he said, and started to draw.

* * *

Later — much later, in the part of her life where this all became story instead of memory — Marguerite would try to reconstruct those half-seconds and fail. What she remembered was the sun flashing off the Colt's barrel, and her own finger tightening, and then a sound from the rocks a quarter mile east that swallowed both of them whole.

The gunshot cracked off the flats and came back twice, and Calloway's hat spun off his knee into the dirt, and he was diving sideways behind the stone apron of the well before the echo finished, and Marguerite was off Peso and flat against the clay with dust in her teeth before her mind caught up to her body.

Second shot. Third. Both high, both wild, kicking spalls of stone off the windmill skeleton.

"Company men!" Calloway shouted from behind the apron, and there was no laughter in his voice now. "Ranger! That's a Consolidated patrol rifle — I know the sound!"

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I stole from them last week!" Another shot sang overhead, closer, and she heard him flinch against the stones. "Off their freight depot in Corriander Flats! Papers, Ranger! I stole *papers*, and they want them back, and they don't leave witnesses!"

Marguerite pressed her cheek to the burning clay and looked east, toward the rocks, where the muzzle flash had come from. Nothing moved. The shooter was patient, or careful, or both, and patient men on the flats had all day and all the water they could carry, while she had one canteen and a horse thief who'd just become the most valuable man in the territory.

Every place you stay costs somebody, she thought. And here I am, pinned down in the middle of nowhere, next to a stranger, and I can feel it already — the cost, coming due.

"Papers," she called across the twenty feet between them. "What papers?"

"Why?" Another shot; grit rained down on her shoulders. "You planning to arrest me?"

"Depends what's in them."

"Honest answer." He almost sounded pleased. "It's a ledger page. Names. Prospectors who went up the Ashfall and never came down, and dates, and payments marked *settled* next to every one of them. Settled, Ranger. Like debts."

Marguerite lay in the dust of the Ashfall Flats with a rifle in her hands and a bullet-buzz in her ears, and thought about Ada Tull crying and apologizing for it, and about the orange-seller's grave outside Perdition Bend, and about eleven years of keeping moving because stopping cost more than running.

Then the shooting stopped, and in the silence she heard hoofbeats — several horses, circling wide, cutting off the south — and Calloway said, very quietly, from behind his stones:

"So. Partners?"

She should have said no. Every instinct she owned, every scar, every grave she'd dug in other people's lives said *no, ride out alone, ride out now, ride out forever.*

Instead she thumbed fresh cartridges into her rifle and said:

"Don't make me regret it."
