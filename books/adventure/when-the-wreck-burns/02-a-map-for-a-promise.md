## 2. A Map for a Promise

The man in the rocks had been shot through the belly.

Marguerite knew it before she reached him — the way he lay, half on his side, one hand pressed flat below his ribs like he was trying to hold the day inside him. The other hand still gripped a scattergun with both barrels spent. The rocks around him were chalked with powder burns and dark spatter that the wind hadn't gotten to yet.

She'd heard the shot from two hundred yards out, rolling off the flats like a door slammed in an empty house. She'd been crouched behind the dry well with her rifle leveled at Calloway's chest, and the sound had broken their standoff like a stone through a window. Now Calloway stood ten feet behind her, hands still raised, watching her decide whether to walk toward danger or keep her gun on him.

"Go," she said. "You run, I'll shoot you in the back and feel fine about it."

"You're all heart, Ranger."

She left him there because she had no better option, and hated herself for the arithmetic of it. Then she crossed the shale in a low crouch, rifle up, reading the ground as she went. One set of boot prints coming down from the ridge, staggering. Two sets going back up, unhurried. Whoever did this wasn't running. They'd finished their work and walked away, which meant they weren't afraid of witnesses, which meant they were either professionals or fools, and the flats ate fools alive.

The prospector's eyes found her when her shadow fell across him. They were gray eyes, clear despite the pain, set in a face burned to the color of old saddle leather. Sixty, maybe older. A white beard gone yellow at the mouth. His shirt was soaked through, and the sand under him was soaking too, drinking it down the way the desert drank everything.

"Ranger," he said. Not a question. He'd seen the star.

"That's right." She knelt, pulled her canteen, wet his lips without asking. Water on a gut wound was a waste of good water, but some things you did anyway. "Who shot you?"

"Two men." He coughed, and the cough cost him something he didn't have much of. "Company men. Badges said Consolidated."

Calloway drifted closer, keeping outside arm's reach, and for once he wasn't performing. He looked at the dying man the way people look at weather they can't do anything about.

"I can get you to Corriander Flats," Marguerite said. "Doctor there. It's four hours if I—"

"No." He said it flat, final, the way men say no when they've already done the same math she had. "Save your horse. Sit down instead. You've got a camp?"

"A mile east."

"Then let's not spend my last hour on gravel."

* * *

They carried him between them, Calloway taking the heavier share without being asked, which Marguerite noted and filed away and refused to interpret. The prospector weighed less than he should have, the way men weigh when they've been living lean a long time. His name, when he gave it, was Ezra Vane.

In her camp — a ring of stones, a scorched coffeepot, a bedroll she'd slept on for six years of nights — they laid him near the fire and Marguerite did what little could be done. Whiskey for the pain. Her spare blanket folded under his head. The bullet had gone in below the ribs and out the back, which meant it had taken its toll on the way through, tearing up everything worth keeping.

Vane drank the whiskey and looked around the camp with an appraiser's eye. "Long time alone?" he asked.

"On and off."

"That's a lie," he said, almost gently. "Rangers don't stay anywhere long enough for on and off."

Marguerite fed the fire a stick of greasewood and didn't answer. The flames popped. Out past the firelight the flats went black to the edge of the world, and somewhere in that blackness were two company men walking back to wherever company men walked back to.

"I was upstream," Vane said. "Three weeks past the last checkpoint on the Ashfall. Where the river comes down out of the high country." He paused, gathering breath like a man gathering stones. "There isn't supposed to be anything up there. That's what the maps say. That's what the company says. Nothing but rock and snowmelt and nobody's business."

"But there is something."

"There's a valley." His voice dropped, though there was nothing within fifty miles to hear it but them. "Green as Ireland. Springs coming up out of the ground like the earth itself is bleeding fresh water. The whole Ashfall is born there — every drop of it. And the Consolidated Water Company has walled it off behind a dam you wouldn't believe. Not to hold water *in*, Ranger. To hold people *out*. They've got workers up there living behind wire. Men who signed contracts and found out the contract never ends."

Calloway sat forward. "Why would a water company strangle its own river?"

"Because Kell doesn't want to sell water." Vane's laugh turned into a wet rattle. "Kell wants to own thirst. You choke the headwaters, every ranch, every town, every farm downstream gets desperate, and desperate men sign anything. He's building a kingdom out of drought, and the river's just the lever."

Marguerite kept her face still. She'd heard conspiracy talk before, from heat-mad prospectors and grudge-carrying drunks, and most of it dissolved under daylight. But Vane didn't sound mad. He sounded tired, the specific tiredness of a man who has seen a thing and cannot unsee it.

"They killed you for knowing this," she said.

"They tried to kill me for having this." With the hand that wasn't holding his middle together, he fumbled inside his coat and drew out an oilcloth packet, wrapped tight and tied with waxed cord. He held it out to her. "Take it."

She didn't move. "What is it?"

"A map. My own hand. Every spring, every draw, every checkpoint on the company road, and the way into the valley that doesn't go through their gate. Three years of work. Three years of staying small and quiet and pretending to be a fool so they'd leave me be." His gray eyes fixed on hers. "And today they stopped pretending first."

"Give it to someone else."

"There is no one else." He pushed the packet against her knee. "I watched you come down off that ridge. You tracked that horse thief across open flats by his dust pattern — I saw you reading the ground before I ever saw you. You're the only law I've met in this territory who looks like she means it." A ghost of a smile. "So here's the trade, Ranger. The map for a promise. One promise, and I'll die easier for it."

"What promise?"

"Burn it or give it to someone worth trusting. Those are the only two futures that map gets. You don't sell it. You don't sit on it. You don't hand it to some territorial marshal who takes company money on the side. You burn it, or you find somebody with spine enough to use it." His breath hitched. "Swear it."

Marguerite looked at the packet on her knee. It weighed nothing. It weighed more than her rifle, her badge, her horse, her whole history of leaving places before they could cost her anything else.

"Ezra," she said slowly, "if what you're saying is true, then whoever carries this map is carrying a target. Those two men will report where you died. They'll know someone found you."

"They'll know a ranger found me." He closed his eyes briefly. "That's why it has to be you. Company men are careful with lawmen. For now."

"And if I swear and regret it?"

"Then you'll know how I felt for three years." He opened his eyes again, and there was humor in them yet, dry as the flats themselves. "Come on, Ranger. What's one promise to a woman who won't even promise herself a town?"

Calloway made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a warning. She ignored him.

"I swear," Marguerite said. "Burn it or give it to someone worth trusting."

Vane exhaled, long and slow, and something in his face unclenched — not peace exactly, but the end of a vigil. "Good," he said. "Now pour me another of that whiskey, and tell me about the thief. A man likes to know what kind of trouble he's leaving behind."

* * *

He lasted until well past midnight.

For a while he talked — about the valley, about the springs, about a woman in Corriander Flats who ran the telegraph office and could be trusted with a message but nothing heavier. His voice grew slower, the gaps between sentences wider, until the gaps became the conversation. Marguerite sat beside him and answered questions he only half asked. Calloway fed the fire and pretended not to listen and listened to every word.

Near the end, Vane gripped her wrist. His hand was cold now, the fire's heat unable to argue with what was happening underneath.

"The workers," he whispered. "Behind the wire. Some of them know. Some of them are the families of men the company buried. If the river dies, they die with it, quiet, and nobody files a paper." His fingers tightened. "Don't let it be quiet, Ranger."

"It won't," she said.

"I mean it. Paper's cheap. Kell owns paper." His eyes were losing their focus, drifting toward the fire. "Make it loud. Make it so loud they can't buy the echo."

Then he said something she couldn't catch, and then he said nothing at all, and the desert took him the way it took everything, without ceremony and without giving anything back.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin.

Marguerite dug the grave herself, in the hardpan east of camp where the caliche wasn't too deep for a shovel and a stubborn woman. Calloway helped, which surprised her, working in silence, sweating through his stolen coat. They stacked stones over the mound because there were no trees to cut and no carpenter to beg lumber from, and Marguerite scratched *E. VANE* into the flattest one with her knife, and beneath it, after a moment's thought: *HE FOUND THE WATER.*

"He'd have liked that," Calloway said, standing back, hat in his hands. He put the hat back on quickly, like grief was catching.

Marguerite sat on her heels and looked at the oilcloth packet in her lap. She'd slept with it under her bedroll, and had dreamed of nothing, which was its own kind of omen.

"So," Calloway said. "You gonna burn it?"

She didn't answer.

"Or the other thing." He crouched across the grave from her, forearms on his knees. "Because I've been thinking. Those company men walked north. North is Corriander Flats. And Corriander Flats is where anybody headed upstream resupplies, whether they're honest or otherwise. Which means if we're doing the other thing—" he tapped the packet "—we're going to need food, ammunition, and a boat, and we're going to need them in the one town in this territory where Consolidated badges outnumber cattle."

"We," she said.

He shrugged, and for once the shrug wasn't cocky. It was almost shy, if a horse thief could manage such a thing. "You swore an oath over a dead man, Ranger. Somebody ought to make sure you don't get yourself buried next to him. Consider it professional interest."

Marguerite looked at the grave, at the scarred stone, at the flats already shimmering with heat beyond it. Every place she'd ever stayed had cost her someone. She'd sworn she was done paying.

She tucked the map inside her coat, against her heart, where she could feel it like a second pulse.

"Corriander Flats," she said, and swung into the saddle.
