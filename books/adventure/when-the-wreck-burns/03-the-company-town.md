## 3. The Company Town

The trouble with Marguerite Coldharbour was that she watched everything and believed nothing, which made her the worst kind of person to steal alongside and the best kind to steal from. Calloway had done neither yet. He was still deciding.

They rode into Corriander Flats at dusk, when the light went the color of old brass and the town's one street lit up like a fuse. It was a company town, which meant it wasn't a town at all — it was a warehouse with pretensions. Every building stood in a row like soldiers at inspection, every roof the same tar-paper gray, every porch the same depth, and down the center of the street ran a water trough so clean it might have been polished. That was the tell. In a free town, the trough was green with scum and full of horse apples. In a Consolidated town, the water was company water, and company water was holy.

"Rules," Marguerite said, not looking at him. She was looking at the street the way a hawk looks at a field. "We're a married couple. I do the talking. You touch nothing."

"I never touch anything."

"You touched a horse."

"That horse touched me first."

She gave him the look — the one that had a rifle behind it — and swung down. Calloway followed, and the moment his boots hit the street he felt it, that particular company-town hum, the feeling of being inside a machine that was running fine and didn't need any parts from him. Men on the porches tracked them with their eyes but not their heads. A boy sweeping the boardwalk swept the same three feet of it over and over, because sweeping was the job and watching was the work.

The general store was called the Consolidated Mercantile, because Consolidated had never met a name it couldn't flatten. Inside, the shelves were full and the air was full too — full of the particular silence of a place where the clerk knew everybody and they weren't anybody.

"Supplies," Marguerite said. "Flour, salt pork, coffee, cartridges. Forty-five caliber." She laid coin on the counter, counted out, exact. A woman who paid exact was a woman who didn't want change, didn't want conversation, didn't want a receipt with her name spelled right.

The clerk counted it twice. "Passing through?"

"River road," Marguerite said. "Down from the flats."

"River road's company road." The clerk said it the way a man says *the floor is company floor*. "There's a toll at Brack's Narrows. And a permit office, if you're going to be on the water."

"We're not going to be on the water."

"Everybody says that." He smiled like a man who'd said it before and would say it again, to everyone, forever, and it was the only sentence he owned. "You folks have a good evening."

Outside, the lamplighter was working his way down the street, and Calloway noticed something that made the back of his neck prickle: the lamplighter was lighting lamps that already had oil in them, and he was lighting them in order, north to south, and at the third lamp he paused and looked down the street at them for exactly one second too long before moving on. Calloway had spent six years reading people the way Marguerite read terrain. That man wasn't a lamplighter. That man was a ledger with a match.

"Ranger," he said quietly, catching up to her. "We're being counted."

"We've been counted since the flats."

"Counted twice is a pattern. Counted three times is a report."

She glanced at him, and there it was again — that flicker, quick as a card trick, where she looked at him like he might actually be useful before remembering she'd decided he was a horse thief. "Where's the third time?"

"Give it a minute."

It took four. They were crossing toward the livery when two men stepped out of the shadow of the assay office, and they wore the badges — not star badges, not law badges, but brass ovals stamped with the Consolidated wheel, and they wore them the way the lamplighter wore his lamp pole, like tools issued from a store.

"Evening," said the taller one. He had a clerk's hands and a gunfighter's hips, which was a combination Calloway had learned to fear. "You folks on the river road?"

"Just passing through," Marguerite said. Her voice had gone smooth and pleasant, and Calloway nearly laughed, because he knew that voice. It was his voice. It was the voice of a person telling a story they'd told so many times it had worn a groove. "Heading south."

"South's the flats."

"South is south."

The tall one smiled. His partner, a short man with the build of a man who carried things for other men, drifted a half-step to the left, opening an angle on Calloway. Professional. "Lot of strangers on the road lately. Prospectors, mostly. You seen any prospectors?"

"Desert's full of them."

"Full of them," the tall one agreed. "Funny thing, though. Used to be fuller." He let that sit. "You come down from the north? Up past the Narrows?"

"Down from the flats," Marguerite said again, same groove, same worn place, and Calloway watched the tall man listen to it and heard what he heard: nothing. A story with no edges to grab. And Calloway felt something he wasn't expecting, something warm and unwelcome, rising in his chest like a swallowed coal.

Pride. He was proud of her. Damn it.

"Where's your permit for the road?" the short one asked.

"Didn't know the desert needed one."

"The desert doesn't. The road does." The tall one produced a little book, licked his pencil. "Names?"

Marguerite gave them. Not her real one — Calloway knew her real one now, had heard it in the dark when Ezra Vane died, heard her say it over a dead man's map like a vow — and not Calloway's, either. She gave names the way she gave coin: counted out, exact, and false. Mr. and Mrs. Harrow. The tall man wrote it down, and the pencil scratched, and Calloway understood that the scratch of that pencil was the most dangerous sound in the town.

"Mr. and Mrs. Harrow," the tall one said. "You have a good evening. Stay off the water."

They walked on. At the corner, out of earshot, Calloway exhaled. "You lie like a ranger."

"You lie like a ranger too. That's the problem with you."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in six years."

* * *

The livery was run by a woman named Tetch, who charged double and asked nothing, which made her the only honest person in Corriander Flats. She took their horses into a stall with a loose board in the back wall — "drafts come through, horses spook" — and Calloway understood that too. The loose board was the whole economy of the place. Tetch sold hay and silence.

They took a room above the saloon, because not taking a room would have been a report of its own. The room had one bed, one chair, one window overlooking the street, and Marguerite took the chair by the window and the rifle across her knees and told him to sleep, which meant she intended to watch, which meant he intended not to sleep either.

But the town had one more thing to give him, and he'd seen it on the way in: the Consolidated field office, dark now, set back from the street, with a window that faced the alley where the lamplighter's route ended. Calloway had robbed eleven banks, two trains, one riverboat, and a church, and he had never once robbed out of need. He robbed out of curiosity, which was worse, and he knew it, and he did it anyway. And the field office had a ledger. He'd seen the clerk carry it in at dusk — a big bound thing, green cloth, brass corners — the kind of book where a company writes down what it knows.

"You're going to tell me not to," he said.

Marguerite didn't turn from the window. "I'm going to tell you that if you get caught, I'll say I never met you."

"That's the spirit."

"I mean it, Calloway."

"I know you mean it. That's why you're going to leave the chair by the window unlocked and the lamp turned low, and why in about twenty minutes you're going to notice I'm gone and be furious about it." He was already working off his boots. "And then I'm going to come back through that window with something worth the fury, and you're going to look at it, and you're not going to be able to put it down. Same as the map."

She was quiet a long moment. The lamp hissed. Down in the street, the lamplighter made his last round, and his footsteps passed under the window and did not stop.

"Ezra Vane," she said finally, "gave me a map and a promise, and both of them are killing me a little more every day. You want to add to the pile?"

"I want to know what we're carrying." He eased the window up on its oiled sash — Tetch's work, or Tetch's luck. "There's a difference between a burden and a blindfold."

He went down the drainpipe the way water goes down a drainpipe, which is to say without any dignity and without any noise, and crossed the alley in the dark between lamps. The field office window was latched with a hook, and the hook was a joke. Inside, it smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular stale sweetness of paper that has been handled by too few hands. The ledger was on the desk where the clerk had left it, because in a company town, nobody stole, and therefore nobody locked anything.

He found it by feel, and then by the match he struck behind his hat brim, one second of flame, no more. The ledger was a payroll record — wages in, wages out — and payroll records are the most honest documents in the world, because companies lie with their mouths but their books tell the truth. He turned pages. Names, dates, amounts. And then, in the back, in a different hand, a tighter hand, a hand that pressed hard: a list. Not wages. Names of men, and next to each name a date, and next to each date a place — the Narrows, the Gorge, the Flats — and next to each place a word that recurred so often it stopped looking like a coincidence and started looking like a policy.

*Settled.*

Vane, E. — settled. Ochoa, R. — settled. Brandt, J. — settled. Eleven names. Eleven prospectors, all upstream men, all men who would have seen what was up there, all men whose wages stopped on the date they were *settled*, and not one of them marked dead, because dead men's wives ask questions, and settled men's wives just wait.

Calloway tore the page out along the spine, slow, so the binding wouldn't show the loss, folded it into his shirt, and closed the book. He was at the window when the match flared again in his memory — the tall one's pencil, scratching. *Mr. and Mrs. Harrow.* Somewhere in this town there was a book with their false names in it, and somewhere upstream there was a book with eleven true ones, and Calloway understood, crouched in the dark smelling of other men's ink, that the two books were the same book. Consolidated didn't write down what happened. Consolidated wrote down what was allowed to have happened.

He went up the drainpipe and through the window and Marguerite was standing with the rifle leveled at him before his foot cleared the sill, and then she saw it was him and didn't lower it, which was its own kind of greeting.

"Well?" she said.

He handed her the page. She struck a match, read it once, read it again, and he watched her face do the thing he'd been waiting for it to do — watched the armor crack along the seam where Ezra Vane's dying whisper had already started the split. Eleven names. She read them like a ranger reads a scene, and he could see her putting each one in the ground.

"Settled," she said. The word came out of her like a splinter.

"Companies don't kill people, Mrs. Harrow. They settle them."

She looked up at him, and for one unguarded second there was no rifle in her eyes, just the question underneath it: *what are you, that you knew where to look?* And he looked back with the answer he didn't say: *I'm you, with worse manners.*

"Get some sleep," she said, folding the page into her shirt next to the map, where it crinkled against the oilcloth. "We ride at first light. Upstream."

"Upstream's where they settle people."

"Upstream," she said, "is where they stopped settling Ezra Vane." She banked the lamp. "Somebody owes eleven men a witness. I'm tired of being the only one who knows it."

Calloway lay down on the floor with his saddle for a pillow and listened to her breathing change, slow and even, a woman asleep or a woman practicing. Outside, the lamplighter made one last pass down the street, and this time his footsteps stopped beneath their window, and stayed there, a long moment, in the dark.

Then they moved on. North. Upstream. Toward the Narrows, and whatever waited there counting strangers on the river road.
