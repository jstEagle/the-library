## 4. Upstream

Odile Brack kept her boat tied at the drowned pilings below Corriander Flats, and she kept her prices written on a plank nailed to the wheelhouse: *Passage upriver, negotiable. Questions, not for sale.*

Marguerite read the plank twice while Calloway stood behind her with his hands in his pockets, doing his best impression of a man who had never stolen anything in his life. It wasn't convincing. Nothing about Calloway was convincing, which was maybe the most honest thing about him.

"You're the ranger," Odile said, coming down the gangplank with a coil of rope over one shoulder. She was small and wiry, gray-streaked hair cut short as a man's, eyes the flat green of river water in shadow. "Heard you came through town asking after the Ashfall. Company men heard it too."

"Then we should leave before they finish their coffee."

Odile looked her over the way she'd look at a hull — checking for rot, checking for seams that wouldn't hold. Then she looked at Calloway, and her mouth twitched. "You travel with thieves?"

"He travels with me under protest."

"Good protest," Calloway said.

"Two hundred dollars to the head of navigation," Odile said. "Half now. And if Consolidated asks, I never saw either of you, because I'm a poor widow woman who runs salvage, not passengers."

"Are you a widow?"

"Ask me again when you've paid."

Marguerite counted out the money from Ezra Vane's belt — the dead man's dollars buying passage on the dead man's river — and tried not to think about what he'd say about it. Probably nothing. Dead men were economical that way.

The boat was called the *Spare Room*, which Odile explained was because her husband had built her a house once and then spent every night on the water instead. The boat had a shallow draft, a patched boiler, and a cargo deck stacked with salvage crates that made decent cover for two passengers who didn't want to be seen. By midmorning they were running upstream against the current, the canyon walls rising on either side like the pages of a closed book.

* * *

The first lesson happened on the second day, when Marguerite caught Calloway cleaning his pistol without checking the cylinder first.

"Stop." She held out her hand. He gave her the gun with the wariness of a man surrendering a knife at a card game. She broke it open, showed him the loaded chamber sitting next to five empty ones. "You'd have pulled the trigger on the one round you had and died reaching for cartridges."

"I'd have thrown it at them after."

"That's the spirit I'm trying to beat out of you." She handed it back. "Again. Cylinder out, thumb over the bolt, count your rounds like you're counting money. You're good at counting money."

"Allegedly."

"Allegedly," she agreed, and something happened in her chest that took her a moment to recognize as a laugh. It came out rusty, half-swallowed, like an engine turning over after a hard winter. Calloway grinned at her like he'd won something.

From then on it became a kind of trade. She taught him ranger's discipline — how to read sign along the banks, how to bank a fire so it left no glow, how to sleep in twenty-minute shifts with your boots on and your hand near your iron. He taught her, without appearing to teach anything at all, how to be less alone inside her own skull.

He did it with jokes, mostly. Bad ones. He named the river mules they passed after company men. He claimed the heron standing in the shallows owed him money from a game of faro in '09. He told a story about stealing a bishop's watch and returning it because the man wept so beautifully it felt like robbery twice. Marguerite found herself laughing at things that weren't funny, and catching Odile watching her do it with an expression the pilot probably thought was neutral.

One evening Calloway asked, casually, the way he asked everything, "Who'd you lose?"

She was oiling her rifle and didn't look up. "What makes you think I lost someone?"

"You flinch when the fire pops. You sleep facing the door. You said 'we' yesterday when there's only ever been you." He shrugged. "I'm a professional observer of people. Mostly their wallets, but the principle holds."

For a long time the only sound was the river and the boiler's slow heartbeat. "My sister," Marguerite said finally. "Lena. I stayed in one place three years because of her. Longest I ever stayed anywhere." She worked the bolt, oiled it, worked it again. "Company men came for our well rights. She argued with them. They came back at night." She snapped the rifle closed. "After that I stopped staying. Staying is where people die. Moving is just lonely."

Calloway was quiet for once, and she was grateful for it. Some gifts are silence.

But that night he took the first watch without being asked, and when she woke at dawn the coffee was already made, bitter and hot, waiting by her bedroll like an apology for all the questions he hadn't asked.

* * *

They cleared the first checkpoint at Miller's Bend by hiding in plain sight — Odile flying salvage colors, Calloway in a borrowed deckhand's shirt shoveling coal badly enough to be forgettable. The Consolidated inspector came aboard, a soft man with a soft voice and a badge polished bright as a coin, and asked his pointed questions while his eyes did the real talking.

"Headed upriver, Miss Brack?"

"Salvage run. There's a steamer wreck above the narrows worth stripping."

"Won't find much above the narrows. Water's low. Company's holding it back." He smiled. "You know how it is. Progress requires sacrifice."

"Always has," Odile said pleasantly, and the soft man tipped his hat and went ashore, and none of them breathed easy until the bend swallowed the checkpoint whole.

It was three nights later, camped in a willow brake below the narrows, that the ambush came.

Marguerite woke to the wrong kind of silence — no insects, no night birds, the river holding its breath. She was moving before her mind finished the thought, boot in Calloway's ribs, hissing *down*, and the first shot came off the bluff above them and clipped sparks off a stone not a foot from her head.

Three shooters, maybe four, spread along the high ground. Muzzle flash told her positions; the river told her the rest. There was no retreat downstream, not past those guns, and upstream was the direction she'd been hired — by a dead man — to go.

"The boat," she said.

"They'll have watched the boat," Odile said, already moving, calm as arithmetic.

"Then they'll watch us not use it." Marguerite pulled the map case tight against her ribs. "We go through the water. Shallow line, along the reeds. They're looking at the fire and the boat."

"And the fire?" Calloway whispered.

"Burns brightest right before you leave it." She fed the flames a double armful of deadfall, waited for the flare, and went into the cold black river with two liars and a pilot, shoulder-deep, boots dragging in silt, while bullets began stitching the abandoned camp behind them.

They came out a mile downstream, soaked, shaking, alive. Odile wrung out her braid and said, "That was the worst idea I've ever survived."

"It's going to happen again," Marguerite said. "Consolidated doesn't send four men to kill strangers. They sent them for the map. Which means someone knows what Ezra gave me."

Calloway, dripping, teeth chattering, still managed a grin. "Could be worse."

"How?"

"He could have given it to someone *less* worth trusting."

Marguerite laughed — really laughed this time, sharp and surprised — and then stopped, because upriver, faint through the dark, came a sound none of them expected: machinery. Pumps, or dredges, working the night shift somewhere the maps said there was nothing but empty canyon.

Odile went still, listening. "There's no wreck above the narrows," she said quietly. "And there's sure as hell no mine. Whatever they're building up there, they built it to keep people away."

Marguerite touched the map case at her ribs, wet leather and dead man's ink, and turned her face toward the sound.
