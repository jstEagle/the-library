## 5. What the Engineer Knew

The river told you things if you listened long enough, and Odile Brack had been listening for thirty years.

It was telling her now that the water was wrong. Too slow for this stretch, too warm, carrying a silt that didn't belong to the canyon walls around them. She stood at the tiller of the *Mudlark* with one hand on the wheel and the other trailing in the current, reading it like a ledger, and what she read made her want to turn the boat around and run for the delta.

"You feel that?" she said.

Marguerite came up beside her. The ranger had a way of standing that Odile was starting to recognize — weight even on both feet, eyes always on the far bank first, then the near one. A woman who checked exits before she checked views.

"Water's lazy," Marguerite said.

"Water's *starved*." Odile spat over the rail. "This time of year, the Ashfall should be shoving us upstream whether we like it or not. Instead it's barely got the manners to carry our hull. Somebody's drinking from this river before it ever gets here."

"Kell."

"Kell's downstream counting his money. Whoever's doing the drinking is upstream doing the work." Odile nodded at the bend ahead, where the canyon narrowed and the water went glassy and green. "And there's where they're doing it."

Around the bend, the mining camp appeared like something drowned and half-raised again. Odile knew its name from the old charts — Prosperity Bar, which was the kind of joke Consolidated liked to make — but the charts hadn't shown her the water lapping at the second stories of the bunkhouses, or the headframe leaning into the current like a drunk against a lamppost. The company had dammed the side canyon below the camp years back, and nobody had bothered to tell the river it wasn't welcome anymore. So the river had moved in. It owned the place now, room by room, window by window.

"Nobody's worked here in what, five years?" Calloway said from the bow. He'd taken up a habit of lounging against the cargo rail like he owned the boat, which Odile tolerated because he also took up a habit of spotting trouble before anyone else, which she valued more.

"Six," said a voice from the shore.

Odile's hand went to the tiller lock. Marguerite's hand went to her rifle. Both happened before the voice finished speaking, and that, Odile thought later, was the moment she understood what kind of crew she'd fallen in with.

A man stood on the collapsed loading dock, hip-deep in shadow, holding a lantern that he'd shaded with his coat so it threw light only at his own feet. Middle-aged, lean in the way of men who'd been eating badly on purpose, wire spectacles fogged at the edges. His clothes were good once — engineer's canvas, company issue, the brass buttons picked off so nothing caught the light.

"Six years since the last shift," he said. "I keep count. It passes the time."

"You're living in a drowned town," Marguerite said. "That makes you either a ghost or a fool."

"Both, most days." He raised the lantern slightly, showing his hands empty except for a leather tube clutched against his chest the way some men hold a Bible. "My name is Silas Renner. I was chief of hydrographic works for the Consolidated Water Company, and I would be very grateful if you'd point that rifle somewhere else while I explain why I'm about to beg you for a boat."

* * *

They let him aboard because Marguerite decided to, and by then Odile had learned that Marguerite deciding something was like weather — you could argue with it, but you'd lose.

Renner sat in the pilothouse wrapped in a blanket Odile kept for hypothermia cases, holding a tin cup of coffee he hadn't drunk. Up close he looked worse than he had on the dock: gray at the lips, hands trembling in a way that had nothing to do with cold. A man who hadn't slept a full night in a long time.

"I designed the dams," he said, without preamble. "Three of them. Upper Ashfall, Kettle Gap, and the big one at the source — the Kell Reservoir wall. I drew every cross-section. I calculated every load. I stood at the ribbon-cuttings and smiled while Ambrose Kell shook my hand and told the newspapers we were going to *harness* the river for the people of the territory."

"And instead?" Marguerite asked.

Renner laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as the flats. "Instead we strangled it. That's what the plans actually say, if you know how to read them. Every gate, every spillway, every intake — sized not to release water but to withhold it. The reservoirs aren't storage. They're a fist. We close them in a dry year, and every farm and town downstream has to buy from Consolidated or die of thirst. We open them just enough to remind people the river exists. That's the whole design. That was always the whole design. I just—" His voice cracked and he steadied it. "I told myself it was engineering. Numbers don't have morals. Water goes where you tell it. I was very good at telling myself that."

Calloway leaned in the doorway, arms crossed. "So what changed? Men like you don't grow consciences. They misplace them and go looking."

"What changed is the ledgers." Renner set down the cup and pulled the leather tube from inside his coat. "Prospectors started finding the upper canyons three years ago. Independent men, staking claims near the headwaters, close enough to see what the reservoir works were really doing. And those men started disappearing. I was asked to survey their abandoned camps. Officially, to assess mineral rights. Unofficially—" he swallowed, "—to confirm the sites had been cleared. All of them. Tools, papers, personal effects. Cleared and burned. You don't clear a site that carefully unless someone died there and left something behind."

He unrolled the tube's contents across the chart table: drawings, load calculations, a page torn from a company ledger with columns of names and dates, and a letter bearing Ambrose Kell's own signature, instructing that "the upper surveys proceed without further record-keeping."

Marguerite went very still. Odile had seen her stillness before, at the dry well, at the checkpoints, and she knew it wasn't calm. It was fury compressed until it fit inside a person.

"Ezra Vane," Marguerite said quietly. "Was he one of these?"

Renner looked down the list. His finger stopped. "Yes. Third from the top. Reported lost in a flash flood, fourteen months ago."

"He didn't drown in any flood. He was shot in the back and crawled six miles to die in my camp." Marguerite's voice didn't rise at all, which frightened Odile more than shouting would have. "He gave me a map to the source. He gave me a promise to keep. Now you're telling me the man who signed his death warrant put his name on this paper too."

"I'm telling you I carried the orders and called it paperwork." Renner took off his spectacles and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I didn't pull a trigger. I drew the blueprint that made the trigger worth pulling. There's no honest difference. I've had two years in this drowned town to look for one, and there isn't one."

"Why hide here?" Odile asked. "Man with your skills, you could've lit out for the coast, changed your name, found some canal company to hire you."

"Because they'd find me anyway, and because running is how Kell wins." Renner looked up, and behind the fogged lenses his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "These papers are the only complete set outside the company vault. If I die quietly in a flooded shack, they stay buried, and every name on that ledger stays a flood victim forever. I've been waiting for someone to come upstream who might actually do something. Rangers don't come up here. Pilots don't come up here." He glanced at each of them in turn. "But apparently a ranger, a thief, and a river pilot just did."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The water slapped the hull in the gentle rhythm of a river being held back somewhere above them, patient as a debt.

"He's asking to come with us," Calloway said. "You all hear that? He's asking. Not trading, not bargaining. Asking."

"I heard it," Marguerite said.

"For what it's worth," Odile put in, "I don't trust him yet. But I trust what Kell did to make him run. That part rings true. I've hauled freight for Consolidated ten years. I've seen what happens to their engineers who ask questions. There was a man named Aldous Ferry, ran the Kettle Gap gauges. Asked why the outflow numbers never matched the rain. They fished him out of the reservoir with his boots full of gravel." She looked at Renner. "You knew Ferry?"

"I wrote the eulogy the company made me read." Renner's jaw tightened. "'Drowned in the line of service.' I read it standing next to the man who ordered it."

Marguerite finally moved. She rolled the drawings back into the tube with careful hands, the way she handled everything that mattered, and held it a moment before giving it back.

"You'll carry these yourself," she said. "If we're caught, you're the one who swallows them or burns them or drowns holding them. I won't take that weight off you. It's yours."

"That's fair," Renner said. "That's more than fair."

"And when this is done — if we get to the source, if we get out — you testify. Publicly. Your name, your voice, no hiding behind documents. The papers prove it happened. You prove *who*."

Renner nodded slowly. "I've been dead in that town for two years, Ranger. Might as well stand up and say so."

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the drowned headframe, and Odile took the middle watch because she slept worst and knew it.

She found Renner awake when her shift began, sitting at the fire's edge with the leather tube across his knees, watching the black water slide past. She sat down without asking. That was the pilot's privilege.

"My daughter used to swim in the Ashfall," he said, after a while. "Before the upper gates closed. She'd stand in the shallows and dare the current to knock her down, and it never could, because the current was honest — it pushed exactly as hard as it meant to." He turned the tube over in his hands. "There's no honesty in the river now. It pushes as hard as a man in an office decides it should. I built that decision."

"You're building the opposite of it now," Odile said.

"I'm carrying paper. That's not building anything yet."

"It will be." Odile fed a stick to the fire. "I've run this river thirty years. You want to know what I learned? Nothing floats downstream unless something lets go upstream. You let go tonight, Mr. Renner. Rest of it's just current."

He almost smiled. It was a ruined thing, but it was a start.

Downstream, behind them, the dark held whatever Consolidated sent next. But upstream — upstream the river waited, starved and patient, and tomorrow they would follow it toward the gorge, toward the tolls and the checkpoints and the fortress-dam at the end of the world, four liars and a penitent in a flat-bottomed boat, carrying the truth in a leather tube like a live coal neither of them dared set down.

Renner cleared his throat. "There's something else you should know before the gorge," he said. "The toll-keeper at Widow's Pass. He doesn't take money."

Odile looked at him. "What does he take?"

Renner's grip tightened on the tube. "Maps. Any map worth having. And word is, he already knows one's coming."
