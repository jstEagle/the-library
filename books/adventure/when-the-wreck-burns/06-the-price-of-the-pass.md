## 6. The Price of the Pass

The gorge announced itself a full day before we reached it — a long gray seam in the mesa country, running north to south like somebody had taken an axe to the world and never bothered to clean up. By late afternoon we stood on its rim, looking down at the toll station wedged into the narrowest part of the crossing: a stone tower, a chain bridge, and a man on the parapet with a spyglass already pointed at us.

"That's Harrow," Odile said. She'd gone quiet in the way she did when she was reading water, except there wasn't any water here to read, just rock and heat and the far-off mutter of the river at the bottom of everything. "Runs the pass for whoever pays him best. Last I heard, that was Consolidated."

"Then we pay the toll and cross," Silas said.

"Consolidated doesn't take coin from people carrying what we're carrying."

I kept my eyes on the tower. Four years as a ranger teaches you that every problem looks simple from two hundred yards. It's the last fifty feet where things go wrong.

"We cross," I said. "Nobody gives up anything. Nobody improvises. Calloway—"

"I'm standing right here."

"You'll keep your hands visible and your mouth shut. Can you do that?"

He grinned at me, that easy thief's grin he wore like a hat. "Marguerite. I once talked my way out of a hanging by convincing the sheriff I was his own deputy's cousin. I can manage one toll-keeper."

"Your mouth is exactly what worries me."

* * *

Harrow met us at the middle of the bridge, which told me everything before he opened it. A man who trusts his visitors meets them at the gate. A man who doesn't makes you come to him over open air, four hundred feet of nothing under your boots, so you remember all the way across who holds the chains.

He was smaller than I expected. Neat. Gray beard trimmed close, spectacles, hands folded in front of him like a schoolmaster waiting out a slow student. Two guards flanked him with rifles held low but not carelessly.

"Four travelers," he said. "No boat. No pack animals worth mentioning." His eyes went down our line and stopped on Silas. "And an engineer."

Silas didn't move. "You know me?"

"I know faces that Consolidated pays bounties on." Harrow smiled without any warmth in it. "Yours has gotten thinner since the company newsletter ran your picture. But faces hold."

"We're just crossing," Odile said. "Passage for four. Name your price."

"The price is the same for everyone." He turned back to me, and I understood then that he'd known who led this little parade from the moment the spyglass found us on the rim. "Paper. Anything written. Maps, letters, ledgers, love notes. Everything goes in the box, gets read, gets returned or burned according to my judgment. That's the toll. The gorge is mine, and nothing crosses it that I haven't read first."

"And if we decline?" I asked.

"Then you climb down the south wall, swim the river at the bottom, and climb the north wall. People have tried. The river takes some, the rock takes the rest." He unfolded his hands. "Or you can pay the other toll."

"Which is?"

"The map I know you're carrying, ranger. Ezra Vane drew it. He passed through here eleven days ago, gut-shot and dying, and he paid me with a look at it before I could stop him bleeding on my floor." Something moved behind the spectacles — not greed. Fear, maybe. Or the memory of it. "Kell's men came through three days later asking after it. I told them nothing, because telling them something is how toll-keepers stop being toll-keepers. But they left a number. A very large number. And now here you are, carrying the very thing, walking straight into my hands."

The wind came up the gorge and pushed against all of us at once. Below, the river made its small patient sound.

"No," I said.

"Marguerite—" Calloway started.

"No." I said it again, quieter, which somehow made it heavier. "The map isn't mine to trade. It isn't even mine. I'm holding it for a dead man, and dead men don't renegotiate."

Harrow studied me the way Silas probably studied blueprints — looking for the load-bearing wall. "You'd risk four lives for paper."

"For a promise."

"Promises don't bleed."

"They're the only thing that keeps the bleeding from being for nothing."

He sighed, and for one strange moment he looked tired rather than dangerous. "I hoped you'd be reasonable. Kell's men will be back through here within the week, and if I let you cross and they learn of it, I'm a dead man either way. So understand my position." He snapped his fingers, and the guards' rifles came up. "You'll give me the map, or you'll give me something else worth the same trouble. Your supplies. The boat-woman's vessel, wherever she's stashed it. And the thief stays with me as surety until I decide whether selling you to Consolidated pays better than letting you go."

"Calloway doesn't stay," I said.

"Then nobody crosses, and I start shooting, and we see how fast reason finds you."

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way bad things do.

Calloway moved first — of course he moved first, he'd been raised on moving first — and I saw his hand slide toward the knife at his belt and I was already shouting no when the guard's rifle cracked and Calloway spun off the bridge rail like a doll on a string.

He caught the chain cable. Barely. One hand, swinging out over four hundred feet of gray air, boots kicking at nothing.

Everything after that I remember in pieces. Odile going for the second guard with a boat hook she'd carried forty miles for no reason I'd understood until that exact moment. Silas grabbing me around the waist because I'd started toward the rail and some part of him knew I'd have gone over it after Calloway without a thought. Harrow shouting orders that the wind shredded.

Calloway's other hand found the cable. He hung there, swinging, and looked up at me across twenty feet of bridge, and grinned — actually grinned, blood in his teeth, wind whipping his hair — and called out, "This is the part where you negotiate, ranger!"

So I negotiated.

I gave Harrow our supplies — most of a month's food, the spare ammunition, the medicine kit with the good morphine. I gave him Odile's boat, or the deed to it, moored thirty miles back downriver at the flooded camp; she signed the transfer papers with a face like carved stone and wouldn't look at any of us while she did it. And I gave him my word, spoken plain on his own bridge, that if Calloway died on that cable I would come back up this gorge alone and unarmed and I would not leave until one of us was done. Harrow had seen enough dying men to know when someone meant it.

He waved the guards back. Calloway hauled himself up over the rail and collapsed onto the planks, shoulder torn open, laughing and coughing blood-flecked spit into the boards.

"Best toll I ever paid," he wheezed.

"Shut up," I said, kneeling on his good side, pressing cloth against the wound. "Just — shut up and breathe."

* * *

We crossed the rest of the bridge in silence, all of us, even Calloway. On the north rim the land changed — redder, higher, the company country beginning in earnest with survey stakes and warning signs nailed to every fence post. We made two miles before dark forced camp in the lee of a fallen monolith, and there, in the firelight, the bill finally came due.

Not the supplies. Not the boat. Those were money, and money is replaceable. What came due was the look on Odile's face as she sat apart from us, turning her father's brass compass over in her hands — the one thing Harrow hadn't thought to ask for because it wasn't on any manifest.

"He took the *Marianne*," she said finally. "Thirty years my family ran boats on that river. My grandfather laid her keel." She looked up, and her eyes were dry, which was worse than tears. "For a piece of paper."

"Not for paper," Silas said quietly. "For what's on it."

"It's the same thing when you're hungry."

"Is it?" He fed a stick to the fire. "I spent nine years drawing dams for Kell. Nine years telling myself the numbers were just numbers. I know exactly what a thing is worth versus what it means. That's the whole education they give you."

Odile looked at him for a long moment, then away, and said nothing more. But she moved her bedroll closer to the fire. Closer to us.

Calloway slept propped against a rock with his arm bound and my coat over him, fever-bright and muttering about bridges. I sat awake beside him and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the canyon rim.

One map against a month of food. Against a boat. Against a man's shoulder torn open, a fever that could still take him, a pilot's inheritance gone. If I'd handed Harrow that paper, Calloway would be whole, Odile would still have her *Marianne*, and we'd be across the gorge with bellies full and nothing to show for any of it but our lives.

And Ezra Vane would have died in my camp for nothing. Every prospector in that ledger would stay missing. The river would keep shrinking, and Kell would keep his fortress, and someday some other dying old man would press a map into some other stranger's hands and whisper *burn it or trust someone*, and that stranger would be smarter than me, and it would end there.

I looked at the map's hiding place — inside my shirt, against my skin, where it had lived since the night Ezra died. It weighed nothing. Paper weighs nothing. It was the heaviest thing I'd carried in twenty years on the trail.

That was the truth of it, the one I couldn't say out loud yet: the map mattered more than any of us would admit. More than food, more than boats, maybe more than the people carrying it — and the moment I understood that, I also understood what a dangerous thing I'd become. People who believe a promise is worth more than lives will eventually spend lives. I'd nearly spent Calloway's on a bridge today, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and some cold quiet part of me knew it.

A ranger learns to protect people from the desert. Nobody teaches you how to protect them from yourself.

Toward morning, Calloway's fever broke, and he woke clear-eyed and asked for water, and drank it, and said, "We're close now, aren't we? To wherever this ends."

I looked north, where the red country climbed toward the company lands and the hidden valley beyond them.

"Yes," I said. "And Calloway — there's something I need to tell you all. About why I really won't give up that map. About what Ezra made me promise."

His eyes sharpened through the fire-smoke. "What did he make you promise?"

I fed the fire another stick and watched the flames take it.

"That I burn it," I said. "When we find what's at the source — I burn the map. No copies. No testimony on paper. Which means whatever we find up there, the only proof that survives is going to be *us*."
