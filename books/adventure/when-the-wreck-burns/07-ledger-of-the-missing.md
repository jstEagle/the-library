## 7. Ledger of the Missing

I'd been carrying the page for three weeks before anyone asked to see it.

That's the thing about stolen paper — it gets heavier every day you keep it folded against your ribs. By the time we made camp in the flooded mining camp's dry loft, with Renner muttering over his instruments and Odile smoking down by the waterline, that single sheet of Consolidated ledger felt like it weighed more than the boat we'd lost at the pass.

Marguerite asked for it the way she asked for everything. No ceremony. She just sat down across the fire from me, turned her hands toward the flames, and said, "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"Calloway." She didn't look at me. That was worse. "The page you lifted at Corriander Flats. I've watched you check on it every night like it's a sleeping child. Renner's been talking about missing prospectors for two days. Odile's cousin is on that river somewhere and hasn't written since spring. You've got something that matters, and you've been sitting on it because you're deciding whether it's worth more to us or to them."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the drowned camp, a timber settled with a groan, like the whole place was remembering how it died.

"You could just ask," I said.

"I just did."

So I took it out. Unfolded it on my knee, smoothed the creases with my thumb, and read it again for maybe the hundredth time. Names. Dates. Payouts. *Vane, E. — claim settled — 40. Hargrove, T. — claim settled — 40. Ostrander, P. — claim settled — 40.* Eleven names in a neat column, each one followed by the same two words, *claim settled*, each one followed by the same figure, forty dollars, and initials at the bottom I'd come to know by heart: *A.K.*

"It's a payroll sheet," Renner said, leaning in. He'd come up the ladder without a sound, the way engineers do, like men who've spent their lives in places where noise gets people killed. He put on his spectacles and read. Then he went very still. "No. It's not payroll. Payroll's a debit. These are credits. Forty dollars *paid out* per name." He looked up at me over the rims of his glasses. "Where did you get this?"

"Corriander Flats. Company office. I told you I lifted it."

"You told us you lifted it," Marguerite said. "You didn't tell us why a horse thief wants a page of accounting."

And there it was. The question I'd been dreading since the dry well, since the rocks, since the first night she'd bound my ribs and called it professional courtesy instead of what it was — the first kindness anyone had done me in years that didn't come with a bill attached.

"Because I was going to sell it back to them," I said.

* * *

The silence went on long enough that I could hear Odile's cigarette burning down by the river. I kept talking, because the only way out of a hole is through the bottom.

"Not the page. They'd have just killed me and burned it. The *secret*. You don't sell Consolidated a piece of paper — you sell them the knowledge that a piece of paper exists, and where, and in whose hands. Extortion, properly done, is a subscription. They pay you every month to stay quiet, and every month you get to decide if this is the month they decide a quiet grave is cheaper than a loud one." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I had a buyer. A man in Corriander Flats with a Consolidated badge and soft hands. Eleven names at forty dollars a head, and I was going to name my price for the twelfth."

"Vane," Marguerite said quietly. "Ezra Vane is on that page."

"First name on it. Claim settled. Forty dollars." I looked at her. "The man who gave you the map. The company paid forty dollars to settle his claim, and I'd bet everything I have left that the settlement was lead, delivered upstream."

Renner took his spectacles off. He does that when he's trying not to feel something. "The dams," he said. "I told you the headwater dams were built to strangle the river. This is the other half of it. Prospectors kept finding the source. Kept staking claims on the spring valley. So Consolidated bought the claims. Forty dollars a claim, and the claimants —" He stopped. Started again. "The claimants don't come down. The ledger calls it settled because as far as the company is concerned, a dead man's claim is the easiest kind to buy."

"Kell initials the payouts," Odile said from the bottom of the ladder. She hadn't come up. She was standing in the dark with her cigarette, and her voice was flat in a way I'd learned to be afraid of. "The founder signs for the murders himself. That's not a company killing people. That's a man killing people and a company paying for the ink."

I sat there with all of them looking at me — the ranger, the engineer, the pilot — and I waited for it. The moment where the thing I'd built over these weeks came apart, because that's the natural life cycle of anything I build. I steal. I get caught or I don't, I run or I don't, and either way I end up alone with whatever I've lifted, which is the only possession I've ever kept for long.

Marguerite stood up.

"Give me the page," she said.

I handed it over. What else was there. She held it up to the firelight, read all eleven names, and then she did something I didn't expect. She handed it back.

"You kept it dry," she said. "Three weeks on the river, through a checkpoint, through the pass, through a night ambush, and you kept it dry and flat. You could have sold it at any town we passed. You could've slipped away from me at the dry well and been rich by now." She sat back down. "Why didn't you?"

And that was the question underneath the question, the one I'd been avoiding since Corriander Flats. Because the truth was uglier than the theft. The truth was that somewhere between the well and the flooded camp, between her teaching me to read country the way she reads it and me teaching her to laugh at my terrible jokes, the buyer with the soft hands had stopped existing for me. Not because I'd grown a conscience. I want to be honest about that, since honesty was apparently the price of admission tonight. It wasn't conscience. It was that I'd started checking the page every night not to make sure it was still valuable, but because it was the first thing I'd ever stolen that I couldn't imagine giving up — and it wasn't the page I couldn't imagine giving up.

"Because of you people," I said. "Because I sat down at this fire and somewhere in the last three weeks it stopped being merchandise. That's not a defense. That's just what happened. I was a thief when you caught me and I'm a thief now, and if you want to hand me to the company at the next checkpoint, I'll even save you the rope by walking there myself. But I'm done lying to this fire. That's the whole confession. That's everything."

Renner looked at Odile. Odile looked at Marguerite. The river made its small noises below, and the drowned camp creaked, and I sat in the middle of it like a man waiting for a verdict with his hands flat on his knees.

"Eleven names," Odile said finally, climbing up into the light. She crouched in front of me, close enough that I could smell river water and tobacco. "My cousin Pilar went up the Ashfall in March with a prospecting license and a good mule. She's not on that page." She held up a hand before I could speak. "Which means either she's alive up there behind that dam, which the engineer says is a prison camp, or she's dead somewhere the ledger didn't bother to count. Either way, Calloway — you held the list of the people who might have killed her, and you were going to *invoice* them for it."

"I know."

"You know." She stood. "I want to hit you. I want you to know that. I'm not going to, because Marguerite's got a rule about crew and I've seen what happens when she keeps her rules." She turned away. "But you and me aren't square, and don't you forget it."

"I won't," I said, and meant it.

Renner was last. He folded his spectacles away and looked at me with those tired, guilty eyes of his, the eyes of a man who'd built the machinery and was only now counting what it was for. "I hid from them for two years," he said. "You sold to them for — how long?"

"Eight months. Off and on."

"Then we're the same animal, just different cages." He put a hand on my shoulder, brief and heavy. "But I'll tell you what I told myself the night I ran: the paper doesn't matter. What you do with what you know, that's the whole account."

* * *

Marguerite waited until the others had gone down to their bedrolls. Of course she did. She's the kind of woman who saves the real conversation for when the fire's burned down to the part where a man can't hide his face in the light.

"You could've told me at the well," she said.

"You'd have shot me."

"I'd have thought about it." She poked the coals. Sparks went up and died. "Here's what I can't figure, Calloway. You're good. You're the best I've ever tracked, and I've tracked professionals. You could be gone. Every night you could be gone, and every morning you're still here, checking on that page like it's going to confess to something. Why?"

I thought about lying to her, one last time, out of habit. Then I thought about Ezra Vane, dying in her camp, trading the only thing he had left for a promise. Trading it to *her*, because she was someone worth trusting, and I'd been listening from the rocks when he said it, and I'd thought at the time: what a fool, giving away your only asset for a word.

"Because you made a promise to a dying man," I said, "and I've never once seen you look for the exit out of it. I've spent my whole life finding the exit. Every job, every town, every — " I gestured at the fire, the camp, the three people sleeping twenty feet away who knew the worst thing about me and hadn't reached for a weapon. "Every one of them, I kept a door open. You don't keep doors. You just stay. And I wanted to know how that works."

"It doesn't work," she said. "You just do it and it hurts and then one morning it doesn't hurt as much." She looked at me across the coals, and her eyes had that flat desert light in them that means she's already decided something and is just being polite enough to inform you. "Here's how it's going to be. The page stays with you. You stole it, you carry it, and when we get to the source, you're the one who reads those eleven names out loud where Kell can hear them. That's your debt, and I'm not going to pay it for you."

"And if I run?"

"Then I'll track you," she said, without any heat at all. "I found you across forty miles of open flat with a two-day start. There's nowhere you go that I don't go."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Shut up, Calloway." But her mouth did the thing at the corner, the almost-smile, the one I'd spent three weeks teaching her and would deny to my grave that I was proud of.

She stood to go, then stopped. "One more thing. The man with the soft hands in Corriander Flats. The buyer. Does he know your face?"

"He knows my face, my voice, and the name I gave him, which wasn't Calloway."

"Then he knows someone's holding that page and hasn't sold." She stared into the dark, out toward the river, toward the dam we couldn't see yet but all of us could feel now, the way you feel a storm over the horizon. "Which means Consolidated's been counting the days since you went quiet. Which means they know someone's coming upstream."

I felt the cold go through me, slow, the way river water finds the seams in a boat.

"They won't just be watching the checkpoints," I said.

"No," Marguerite said. "They'll be watching for us. All the way to the source."
