## 8. Where the River Is Born

We climbed the last mile in the dark, and the dark was wrong.

I'd spent eleven years reading desert nights — the way they breathe, the way a canyon holds its cold like water in a cup. This night had no breath in it. The air came down off the ridge flat and dead, and beneath it ran a sound I couldn't name at first, a low continuous pressure against my ears like the world holding one long note.

Renner felt it too. He stopped on the switchback ahead of me, one hand braced against the rock, and said nothing, but his shoulders told me everything his mouth wouldn't.

"Say it," I whispered.

"It's the spillway," he said. "You're hearing the dam from six miles out. That's not possible."

"Then what am I hearing?"

He didn't answer. He just started climbing again, faster.

* * *

Calloway found the gap in the fence line an hour before dawn — a drainage cut where the wire sagged over scree, half-buried, the kind of neglect that happens when nobody's supposed to be looking anyway. He went through first because he insisted, and because Odile backed him with that flat look of hers that ended arguments before they started.

"I'm the thief," he said. "Let me be useful at the thing I'm actually good at."

He lifted the bottom strand and held it while we crawled under, one by one, into the country Consolidated Water had decided belonged to no one.

The valley opened below us as the light came up, and I forgot, for a full minute, how to breathe.

I had imagined the source of the Ashfall all my life without knowing it. Every ranger in the territory has. You grow up on a river that appears out of nowhere — rising full-grown out of the empty flats like something conjured — and you carry a private picture of the place it comes from. Mine had always been a spring. A green throat of a canyon, ferns, cold water pushing up out of limestone, small and holy.

What lay below me was a cathedral.

The spring valley sat in a bowl of red cliffs a thousand feet high, and out of its floor rose water — not seeping, *rising*, dozens of vents across a mile of meadow, boiling up silver in the early light, braiding together into a river wide enough to float a barge before it had run a hundred yards. Mist hung over the whole basin. Grass grew to a horse's belly. Birds I had no names for worked the reeds. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen in a life spent looking at beautiful, terrible country, and I understood in that moment why men would kill for it, and why they had.

Then I saw what they'd built on top of it.

The dam stood across the valley's only outlet like a fist closed over a throat. Not a graceful structure — Renner had shown us drawings of graceful dams, arches that leaned into their burden — but a fortress, a squat gray cliff of poured concrete with gun emplacements at the flanks and a work camp sprawled at its base like shed skin. Behind it, the reservoir backed up the valley for two miles, drowning the meadows, the vents submerged, the river's birthplace turned into a holding pond. The water behind the wall was still and brown-green. The water below it, where the Ashfall staggered out through the turbines, was thin as a creek.

"That's not a reservoir," Renner said. His voice had gone strange. "Reservoirs harvest floodwater. This is—" He took off his glasses, wiped them on his shirt, put them back on, as if the problem were his eyes. "This is a plug. They're not collecting the river. They're strangling it at the spring so they can meter it out downstream, every gallon, at Consolidated prices. The headwaters aren't being harnessed. They're being *held hostage*."

"How much flow is getting past?" Odile asked.

"Maybe a tenth. Maybe less." He was scribbling figures in his notebook now, fast, ugly little numbers. "At this drawdown the aquifer itself will start to fail within five years. The springs won't recover. There is no downstream after that. There's no *anything* after that."

Below us, a whistle blew, and the work camp began to move.

* * *

We watched the camp through Renner's field glasses passed hand to hand, and what we saw made the dam seem almost honest.

There were no free men down there. The workers moved between barracks and worksite in columns, counted at each gate by men with rifles slung casual as bedrolls. A wire perimeter ringed the whole basin — the same wire we'd crawled, though ours had been the lazy edge; here it was strung tight and humming. There were no women. No children. No saloon, no church, no store with a window you could buy hope through. Just rows of identical bunkhouses and a mess hall and men who walked like men walk when the ground belongs to someone else.

"They signed contracts," Renner said quietly. "Three-year terms, company housing, wages paid in scrip redeemable only at company stores — which don't exist up here. That's the design. You can't quit a job three days' ride from anywhere when your pay is paper that's only worth something back downriver." He lowered the glasses. "I drew the wage schedule. God forgive me. I thought the isolation premium was generous."

A column crossed the yard toward the turbine house, and one man stumbled. A guard said something. The column kept moving and the man picked himself up and limped on, and nobody looked back, because looking back is the first luxury a prisoner loses.

Calloway had gone very still beside me. "My brother worked a company camp outside Bitter Wells," he said. "Two-year contract. Came home weighing ninety pounds with a cough that killed him in the spring." He spat, deliberate and slow. "I stole horses from men like this my whole life and never once knew I had the right targets."

"Now you know," I said.

"Now I know."

Odile hadn't spoken in an hour. She lay on her stomach in the shadow of a juniper, glasses fixed on the reservoir's far shore, and when she finally did speak her voice was so quiet I had to lean in.

"Marge. Look at the north bank. Below the high-water mark."

I took the glasses. At first: rock, talus, the ordinary litter of a drowned shoreline. Then the shapes resolved. Timbers. Roof beams. A wagon bed. A fence line running into the water and stopping, the way fences do when the rest of the farm is under forty feet of green dead water.

"There were ranches up here," she said. "Homesteads. People lived on this river before the company owned it." She passed the glasses to Renner. "Where'd they go?"

Nobody answered. We all thought about Ezra Vane dying in my camp with a hole in his gut and a map in his shirt, whispering about men upstream who buried what they couldn't buy. We all thought about the ledger page in Calloway's pocket with its neat columns of names, each one followed by a date and a single word: *settled.*

The word had always read to me like accounting. Lying on that ridge with the drowned farms below me, it read like something else entirely.

* * *

We might have come down off the ridge clean. We should have. Renner wanted to — he'd gotten his proof, photographs and figures and the drowned homesteads documented in his notebook, and his plan was the engineer's plan: retreat, publish, litigate. But Calloway spotted the records office — a concrete blockhouse inside the compound, marked with the Consolidated star — and after the ledger page, none of us could look at a building like that and think of anything but names. Names that needed rescuing. So we stayed an extra day in the rocks above the valley, watching, waiting for the shift change that would thin the guards, and that extra day is when the sky changed.

It came out of the southeast at midmorning, a sound like a struck bell stretched into forever, and then the airship itself cleared the southern rim — a gray whale of hydrogen and doped canvas, engine pods slung beneath like fists, the Consolidated star painted on its flank large enough to read from our ridge without glasses.

It came over the reservoir low enough to ripple the dead water, and it moored at a tower on the dam's crest, and men came down the gangway in white linen, and the workers in the yard below stopped moving entirely, every one of them, the way a field stops when the hawk's shadow crosses it.

Renner's glasses were shaking. "That's Kell," he said. "That's Ambrose Kell himself. He doesn't come here. Nobody's seen him here in nine years. He runs the whole river from an office in Corriander Flats and lets the company be the face of it." He swallowed. "Why would he come now?"

And then I understood, cold spreading through my chest like water finding a crack, and I took the glasses myself and found the gangway and the man in white descending it — tall, unhurried, silver-haired, pausing at the bottom to say something that made the camp commander laugh the way subordinates laugh.

"He knows," I said. "Ezra Vane. The ledger page. The toll-keeper on the gorge. Somewhere between the flats and here, somebody told him a ranger was coming upstream with a map, and he didn't send guns." I handed the glasses back. "Guns make martyrs. He came himself."

"To offer you something," Odile said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen it too. Everyone who'd ever tried to stop me had learned eventually that you don't stop Marguerite Coldharbour with force. You stop her with a door opening instead of closing.

Down in the valley, the airship settled against its tower like a roosting bird, and the note I'd heard six miles out in the dark went on and on beneath everything — the dam, the spillway, the held breath of a stolen river — and I lay in the dirt above the most beautiful murdered place I'd ever seen and felt awe curdle slowly into fury, the way milk turns, gradual and total and impossible to reverse.

"We go down tonight," I said.

Renner looked at me like I'd proposed swimming the reservoir. "Marguerite—"

"He came to buy me. Which means tomorrow there'll be a price on my head instead. Whatever we're going to do, we do while he still thinks I can be bought." I checked the cylinder of my revolver, an old habit, comfort more than arithmetic. "Besides. He brought his records with him. Men like Kell always travel with their ledgers."

Calloway grinned, and it was not a nice grin. "Then let's go introduce ourselves."

Below us, the hawk-shadow of the airship slid across the drowned farms, and the river's true voice went on speaking under the concrete, patient, unheard, waiting.
