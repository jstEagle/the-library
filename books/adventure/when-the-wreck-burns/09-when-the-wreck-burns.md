## 9. When the Wreck Burns

I've run rivers that wanted to kill me. I've run rivers that did kill men I loved. But I'd never run a river that didn't exist yet — not until the night we broke Ambrose Kell's dam and taught the Ashfall how to be born again.

It started with Silas, of course. Everything with Silas started with a drawing.

He spread his plans across the floor of the cave above the reservoir, weighting the corners with stones, and traced the spillway with one cracked fingernail. "The gates are counterweighted," he said. "Iron on chains older than the war. You cut three links in the right order and the whole mechanism fails open. It'll take maybe four minutes to fall."

"And the water?" Marguerite asked.

"Forty feet of it behind those walls. Maybe more. It'll come down the valley like God changing His mind." He wouldn't look at her when he said it. "There are workers in the lower camp. Company men, indentured, mostly. They'll have minutes, not hours."

"Then we warn them first," she said, like it was simple. Like everything with her was simple if you just refused to make it complicated.

Calloway laughed from the mouth of the cave, where he'd been keeping watch on the fortress-dam below, its lamps strung along the crest like a carnival that hated itself. "Warn them. Sure. Walk down there ringing a bell. Hello, gentlemen, pardon me, we've come to drown your employer's life's work, mind the flood."

"We cut the fence on the east side," Silas said quietly. "Open the barracks. They'll run on their own. Men don't need convincing to flee a valley. They need a door."

Marguerite looked around at us — at Calloway still grinning but no longer laughing, at Renner hunched over his own guilt like a man warming his hands at it, at me. She didn't give a speech. That was the thing about her I came to love before I understood it: she never gave speeches. She just decided, and you found out afterward you'd decided too.

"Odile," she said. "Can you get us off this water?"

"I can get anything off any water," I said. "Question is whether there'll be water left to get off of."

"Then that's the plan. Doors open, chains cut, and we're downstream before the valley learns our names."

* * *

We went down in the dark before dawn, four shadows moving through sage and slag. The east fence was exactly where Silas said it would be, and Calloway went over it like he was born on the wrong side of fences, which I suppose he was. He opened the barracks gate and stood there in the lamplight, hat in hand, and told forty sleeping men that the river was coming home and they had best not be standing in its way when it arrived.

They thought he was mad. Then they heard the first chain link snap, high up on the dam face, ringing off the concrete like a church bell struck by a sinner, and they stopped thinking anything except *run*.

Silas and I took the gatehouse. That was my job — not the cutting, the timing. A pilot knows water the way a preacher knows scripture, and I knew that forty feet of reservoir would come down that gorge faster than any horse we owned. We had to be on the river, in the company's own longboat, before the front wave passed us, or we'd ride the crest all the way into the next world.

Marguerite took the records office alone.

I want that written plainly, because history will get it wrong otherwise. She didn't have to go. The map was proof enough; the ledger page Calloway lifted in Corriander Flats was proof enough; Silas himself, living and breathing and confessing, was proof enough. But Marguerite Coldharbour had spent a book's worth of miles learning that paper is how the powerful remember, and she meant to burn their memory down to the studs.

She went in through the window. I saw her silhouette against the lamp inside, calm as Sunday, pulling drawers, stacking ledgers, feeding contracts and deeds and death certificates into the iron stove Kell used to warm his clerks' coffee. And that's when the airship came.

* * *

You could hear it before you could see it — that insect drone, that arrogant hum. It came over the canyon rim with running lights blazing, because Ambrose Kell had never once in his life entered a room quietly, and it set down on the dam crest in a wash of dust and propeller-wind while his workers scattered below like startled quail.

I was at the longboat with Silas, cutting the last mooring line, when two of Kell's men came down the stairwell and put rifles against our backs.

"Mr. Kell wants the woman," one said.

"The woman," I said, "is busy."

They marched us up onto the crest anyway. And there it was, the scene I'll carry to whatever grave takes me: Marguerite standing in the doorway of the burning records office, soot on her sleeves, and Ambrose Kell descending from his airship in a white linen suit, unhurried, hands clasped behind him, surveying his drowning empire the way a man surveys a garden he's about to sell.

"You're cutting the counterweight chains," he said to her. Not angry. Interested. "That's Renner's work. I'd know the drafting anywhere." He glanced at Silas, and something almost tender crossed his face. "I never fired you, Silas. I let you resign. There's a difference, and someday you'll understand it."

"Let her talk, Ambrose," Silas said. "It's what she does instead of shooting people."

Kell smiled at Marguerite then, and I watched him do the thing that made him rich — the thing where a man makes your destruction sound like a gift. "Ranger Coldharbour. Do you know what you're standing in? The future. Every acre downstream, every town, every crop — they drink because I decide they drink. You've been chasing a thief and a dead man's map across two hundred miles of desert, and for what? To hand the river back to people who'll fight over it like dogs?" He stepped closer. "Or — stand with me. Name your price. Your badge back, if you ever lost it. Land. A house that doesn't move every time you do. Whatever it is you've been running from, I can build you a place where running isn't necessary anymore."

And here is the part I will tell my grandchildren, if the world lets me keep any: she didn't even hesitate.

Behind her, through the office window, the stove fire had climbed into the shelving, and the records were going up — decades of stolen water, bought silence, buried men — curling into black butterflies that rose past her shoulder into the night sky. She looked at Kell the way you look at a cardsharp who's shown you his whole hand and thinks you should thank him.

"Everything stopping has cost me," she said, "is people. And you're offering me more stopping. More land, more walls, more years sitting still while the world rots politely around me." She pulled the last ledger from under her arm — the master register, Kell's own handwriting on every page — and held it over the stove. "No deal, Mr. Kell. I promised a dying man I'd burn this or find someone worth trusting. Turns out I'm someone worth trusting."

She fed it to the fire.

Kell moved — I'll grant him that, he moved fast for a man in linen — but Calloway was faster, stepping into his path with that easy thief's grace, and the shot Kell's man fired went wide and took out a window instead of a ranger. Then the third chain link parted with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and the counterweights fell.

* * *

I have piloted through hail. I have piloted through fire on the water. Nothing — nothing — prepared me for the sound of forty feet of imprisoned river remembering what it was.

The gates failed open the way Silas swore they would, and the Ashfall came down the valley as a single moving wall, moonlit, roaring, carrying trees and fencing and one entire company barn ahead of it like offerings. The ground shook. The airship lurched on its moorings, and Kell was screaming orders at men who were already running, already done with him, and Marguerite was sprinting down the dam stairs toward us with Calloway covering her retreat and Silas hauling me into the longboat.

"In!" I roared. "All of you, now!"

They came over the gunwale one after another — Calloway first, then Silas, then Marguerite, soaked to the waist, soot-faced, alive — and I put the bow downstream and gave the boat everything she had.

The flood caught us at the bend. Of course it did. The river doesn't negotiate. It picked us up the way a mother picks up a child, and for one endless minute we were not steering, we were surviving — Calloway bailing, Silas praying to an engineer's god, Marguerite laughing, actually laughing, wild and soot-black and free, and me with both hands on the tiller riding the birth of a river.

Then the valley opened, and the water spread, and the roar became a rush became — incredibly — a current. A real one. Running clean and fast and free through country that hadn't seen it in twenty years.

We drifted in the gray light of morning, four wrecked people in a borrowed boat, watching the Ashfall run.

Below us, far downstream, the first towns would wake to a river they'd mourned. Above us, behind us, Ambrose Kell stood on a broken dam beside the ashes of his own records, and there was nothing left to prove and nowhere left to buy.

Calloway broke the silence first. "So," he said. "Anyone know how to steer this thing?"

"I do," I said. "Everyone hold on. We've got a story to carry downstream — and this time, nobody's buying it."

Marguerite looked back once at the smoking valley, then forward, and didn't look back again.

"There's a town called Corriander Flats," she said, "full of people who were told the missing men ran off. I reckon they'll want to hear different."

I put the tiller over, and the new river carried us toward them.
