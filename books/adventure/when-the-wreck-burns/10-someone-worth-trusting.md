## 10. Someone Worth Trusting

The testimony went downriver faster than the flood had.

That was the thing about water, Odile liked to say — it always found the low places, and so did truth. By the time our little crew limped into Corriander Flats on a borrowed skiff, three weeks after the spillway burned, there wasn't a saloon in the territory that hadn't heard Silas Renner's name spoken like a psalm. The engineer who'd turned. The ledgers he'd carried out of Kell's fortress-dam, half-singed, half-soaked, every missing prospector accounted for in cold company ink.

I'd given my own account to a federal marshal in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old coffee. Four hours. I told it all — Ezra Vane dying in my camp with a map pressed into his bloody hand, the toll-keeper's gorge, the reservoir strangling the headwaters, the workers kept behind wire. The marshal wrote fast and asked slow questions, and when I finished he sat back and said, "You understand what you've done, Ranger."

"I do," I said.

"You've made yourself famous and homeless in the same afternoon."

I almost laughed. Almost.

* * *

Kell's empire didn't crack all at once. It cracked the way ice cracks on a spring river — a sound here, a seam there, then all at once the whole sheet groans and gives. The Consolidated Water Company's stock collapsed in nine days. The territorial governor, who'd taken Kell's money for eleven years, resigned in twelve. Airships that once carried Kell's private guard now carried federal auditors upriver, their brass fittings catching the sun like beetles.

They never caught Ambrose Kell himself. Some said an airship lifted off the fortress-dam the night of the fire, running south with its engines coughing smoke. Some said he'd been aboard when the spillway let go. I didn't say anything at all. I'd learned by then which stories were mine to carry and which belonged to the river.

What I knew was this: the Ashfall ran again. Not full — it would be years before the canyons forgot what had been done to them — but running. Brown at first, then green, then clear as glass over the gravel bars where Odile swore she'd seen fish come back before anyone else believed it.

* * *

We wintered in Corriander Flats because we had nowhere better to be, and because Calloway got it into his head that the town needed honest men running its supply depot, and because none of us could bear to split up and none of us would admit it.

The depot had been Consolidated property. When the company dissolved, its assets reverted to the territory, and the territory, drowning in paperwork, forgot about a dusty warehouse on the river road. Calloway — reformed thief, current pillar of the community, a sentence that still made me snort into my coffee — simply started keeping the books one morning, and by the time anyone thought to object, he'd done it so well and so cheerfully that objection felt rude.

Silas stayed too. He'd tried going back east to testify before the commission and found he couldn't sleep in a city, couldn't breathe under roofs, kept waking at dawn listening for water that wasn't there. So he came back and took a room above the depot and spent his evenings drawing plans for irrigation ditches that would give the flats back their farms without bleeding the river dry. Redemption, it turns out, is mostly just showing up the next day.

Odile ran cargo downstream and back, her new boat christened the *Ezra Vane*, though she claimed she'd named it after a mule she'd once owned. She came upriver every few weeks with supplies and gossip and left again with timber and hides, and every time she left, something in my chest pulled after her like a rope gone taut.

And me? I kept the ranger's badge. The territory offered me a desk in the capital and I declined it so fast the clerk laughed. There was still country to ride. There were still people who needed finding. But I noticed, that first winter, that I'd stopped riding away from things. I rode out and I rode back, and the coming-back didn't feel like failure anymore.

* * *

It was Odile who finally asked me about the map.

We were on the ruined banks below the old fortress-dam — or what remained of it, a broken spine of concrete the feds had abandoned to the weather. She'd run me upriver on some pretext or other; I think we both knew there was no pretext good enough anymore. We built a small fire on the gravel where the flood had scoured everything down to bone and stone, and I took the oilskin packet out of my coat, the way I'd carried it for a year and a half through gorges and gunfights and grief.

Ezra Vane's hand-drawn map. The source of the Ashfall River. Every bend, every spring, every secret the canyon kept, inked by a dying man who'd trusted a stranger because she was the only stranger who'd stopped.

"Burn it or give it to someone worth trusting," I said. "Those were his terms."

"And you gave it to us instead," Odile said. "For a while there, I thought you'd decided we were worth trusting."

"For a while there, I hoped you'd decide I was."

She fed the fire another stick and waited. That was Odile's gift — she could wait like the river waits, patient and certain and utterly unbothered by how long it takes.

"The testimony carried the story," I said. "Downriver, to the marshals, to the commission, to every saloon from here to the sea. The paper doesn't matter now. The source is public knowledge. Kell's empire is ash." I turned the packet over in my hands. It weighed nothing. It had always weighed nothing. That was the trick of it — the things you can't put down are always the lightest ones. "But I promised him. And a promise isn't testimony. A promise is between you and one dead man, and you keep it or you don't."

"Marguerite." Odile's voice was gentle. "You know nobody's going to think less of you if—"

"That's not why I'm doing it."

"No," she agreed. "I know. I just wanted you to hear someone say it anyway."

I knelt at the fire's edge. The evening wind came down the canyon carrying the smell of wet stone and green willow, the smell of a river remembering itself. Somewhere upstream, water moved over rocks that had been dry for twenty years.

"Ezra," I said quietly. "I'm sorry it took so long. And I'm sorry I couldn't save you. But your river runs free, and everybody knows why now, and it's because of you. Rest easy, old man."

I fed the map to the flames.

The oilskin curled and blackened. The ink bloomed brown, then gold, then nothing. I watched until the last corner of Ezra Vane's careful handwriting became a fleck of ash that rose on the thermals and drifted up the canyon, upstream, against all sense, the way sparks do — as if even fire, sometimes, wants to go home.

I stayed kneeling a long time. Odile didn't rush me. When I finally stood, my knees cracked like pistol shots, and I discovered I was lighter than I'd been since before I could remember. Not empty. Lighter. There's a difference, and it took me forty years and one dead prospector to learn it.

* * *

They were waiting for me when I walked back up the bank.

Calloway sat on a driftwood log with his boots off, drying his socks over a second fire like a man with nowhere in the world to be, which was exactly what he was and exactly what he'd earned. Silas stood at the waterline sketching the broken dam in a leather notebook, because some wounds you have to draw before they close. Odile came up beside me and put her shoulder against mine, brief and warm, and then went to argue with Calloway about whether beans counted as a food group.

Nobody asked if I'd done it. Nobody needed to. They'd watched me walk down to the fire carrying the last weight of my old life, and they'd watched the horizon while I did it, because that's what family does — it guards the door while you grieve, and pretends afterward that nothing happened.

"Ranger," Calloway called, wiggling his bare toes at the flames. "Supper's in ten minutes. You're on coffee duty. House rules."

"We don't have a house."

"We have four fires and a boat and each other. That's a house with excellent ventilation."

Silas looked up from his sketchbook and smiled — a real smile now, not the guilty flinch he'd worn the first months. "There's a homestead act filing office in Corriander Flats," he said carefully, to the fire more than to me. "If a person wanted to file on land along the river. Say, near the depot. Say, four names on one claim."

The wind moved through the willows. Downstream, the Ashfall murmured over its gravel bars, steady as a heartbeat, going wherever rivers go when nobody's strangling them.

Four names on one claim. I turned the idea over the way I'd turned the map over, looking for the catch, the cost, the person I'd lose this time. And for the first time in my life, I couldn't find one.

"Coffee's ready," I said, and poured four cups, and sat down among them, and stayed.
