## 1. The Tide Keeps No Receipts

The tide went out farther than anyone in Port Sable could remember, and it took the town's good sense with it.

Oscar Oyelaran stood on the shingle at dawn with his hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had once been black and was now the color of a storm cloud's underbelly, watching the sea retreat like a debtor leaving town. The record tide had been all anyone talked about for three days — old men on the seawall shaking their heads, the harbormaster's daughter taking photographs, the fish-and-chip shop putting up a hand-lettered sign that said TIDE SPECIAL: EVERYTHING'S GOING CHEAP ANYWAY. The monsoon season was coming early this year; you could smell it in the air, a green metallic tang like a coin held too long in the mouth. But for now the sky was the pale gray of unwashed linen, and the water had pulled back past the channel markers, past the mussel beds, past anything sensible, exposing a mile of seabed that no living person had ever seen.

"Oyelaran." Dree Halloway came down the shingle road with her hands in her coveralls and her boots making that particular squelching sound of someone who has already been working since before light. She was sixty, built like a capstan, and she ran the only salvage outfit on the coast that would still hire him. "You're late."

"I'm early," Oscar said. "You're just unreasonable."

"That's what my first husband said. He drowned, so we'll never know who was right." She handed him a pair of gloves with two fingers missing and didn't apologize for it. "Storm debris. The council's paying by the ton for whatever the tide left us — timber, netting, dead appliances, the occasional sad rowboat. We start at the north point and work down. You take the waterline, I'll take the wrack line."

"You're giving me the wet job."

"You're the one who used to charge rich people money to fall off mountains. Consider this character building." She squinted at him. "You look terrible, by the way."

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

It was the kind of morning where humor sat thin on top of everything, like paint over rust. Eighteen months since the Kestrel Spire inquiry. Eighteen months since the word *negligent* got stapled to his name in every guide registry from here to the equator, and the Meridian Syndicate's lawyers made sure it stayed there. Eighteen months of hauling other people's garbage out of the sea because it was the only work left to a man whose profession was, officially, a danger to the public.

He didn't think about it. He'd gotten very good at not thinking about it, the way you get good at walking on a broken ankle — technically possible, deeply unpleasant, and only sustainable if you don't look down.

* * *

The seabed at low tide was a landscape from a bad dream. Kelp lay in long brown ropes across the mud like something drowned mid-struggle. Tide pools sat in the hollows, each one holding its own small stranded world — crabs, blennies, a starfish the size of a dinner plate going about its business with the calm of a creature that assumed the ocean would be back shortly and saw no reason to panic. Oscar worked his way along the waterline dragging a salvaged garden cart, filling it with the sea's leavings: a door, half a dinghy, forty meters of trawl net, a washing machine full of sand that weighed as much as a sin.

Dree worked the high line, and every so often they'd shout observations at each other across the flats, the way people do when silence would otherwise become conversation.

"That's the third piano leg I've found," she called at one point. "No piano. Just legs. The sea's mocking me."

"The sea doesn't mock," Oscar called back. "It just keeps records."

"Aye, well, let's hope it keeps them somewhere useful."

He laughed, and then stopped laughing, because he'd come around the shoulder of a headland that the charts — he'd memorized every chart of this coast during the long unemployed nights, the way some men drink — said was solid granite all the way down.

There was a cave mouth in it.

Not a crack. Not a slump. A proper cave mouth, tall as a doorway, maybe wider, its edges rounded by water that had been working at it for a very long time behind a wall of rock that should have been there. The record tide had stripped away the shingle bank that had sealed it — he could see the fresh scar of it, a pale wound of exposed clay and stone running down to the new opening — and now the sea had simply walked into a room that had been locked since before Port Sable had a name.

Oscar stood very still.

Here is the thing about luck: Oscar Oyelaran had stopped believing in it around the same time he stopped believing in the fairness of tribunals, which is to say completely, and with documentation. Luck was something that happened to men who hadn't yet learned better. What happened to Oscar was weather, and fees, and the slow grinding arithmetic of being broke in a town small enough that everyone knew exactly how far he'd fallen.

And yet.

He looked back over his shoulder. Dree was two hundred meters up the beach, bent over a tangle of netting, her back to him. The tide was still out. It would turn within the hour — a tide like this turned hard and fast, and the seabed would go from highway to graveyard in the time it took to have second thoughts.

He should have gone and told her. That was the correct thing, the procedure thing, the thing Mara Voss would have insisted on years later when telling this story — though he didn't know Mara Voss existed yet, and if you'd described her to him that morning he'd have assumed you were inventing a person specifically to complicate his life, which, in fairness, she was.

Instead Oscar did what broke men do. He stepped inside.

* * *

The cave went back farther than the light did.

His phone torch made a weak yellow cone that turned the walls into a moving shadow play — wet stone, old barnacle lines marking centuries of high-water marks like rings in a tree. The floor sloped gently upward, which meant the far end had been dry even at high tide, sealed behind its bank of shingle. Dry. For decades, maybe longer. The air tasted of salt and cold stone and something else underneath, something organic and papery, like the inside of an old book.

There were boats. That was the first real surprise. Two of them, pulled up on the slope, hulls rotted to lace but their shapes still honest — ship's boats, clinker-built, the kind you'd lower from a steamer. And near them, crates, most collapsed into pulp, their stenciling long dissolved. Whatever this place had been, it had been a cache. A storehouse. A place where someone had wanted things kept out of sight and out of the reach of weather and taxmen alike.

Oscar moved slowly, the way he moved on rotten snow slopes, testing each step. Old habit. The mountain had trained him well and then taken everything he loved and blamed him for the invoice.

In the deepest corner, tucked behind the ghost of a crate, sat a strongbox.

It was iron, or had been. Rust had gotten into it the way damp gets into a marriage — slowly, thoroughly, and without anyone agreeing to it. It was perhaps the size of a suitcase, and it was stamped on the lid with a crest: a meridian line through a globe, flanked by two dolphins, beneath the words MERIDIAN SHIPPING SYNDICATE.

Oscar knew that crest. Everyone on this coast knew that crest. It was on the harbor office plaque, on the pension fund building, on the little brass plate outside the law firm that had helped end his career. The Syndicate had owned this coastline for a hundred years — the ships, the insurance, the courts, the very stones of the seawall. When the *Coromandel* went down with all hands in '61, the crest had been on the wreath. When the guides' collective was broken up after the Kestrel inquiry, the crest had been on the letterhead of every letter that ended a man's livelihood.

Oscar stood in front of the box for a long time.

His heart was doing something complicated — not pounding so much as arguing with itself. Because this was the moment where a sensible man turns around and walks out and reports the find to the harbormaster and fills out forms in triplicate and waits six months while lawyers decide what happens to things found in holes in the ground. And Oscar had been a sensible man for eighteen months, and sensibility had gotten him a coat with holes in it and a job hauling washing machines.

But it wasn't greed that held him there. He wants you to know that, whoever you are, reading this later. It wasn't greed. It was the specific hunger of a man who has spent a year and a half being told, politely, in writing, that he imagined the things he saw on Kestrel Spire. That the rope failure was his fault. That the dead partner was dead because of *him*. Hunger like that doesn't want money. It wants the ledger balanced. It wants someone, somewhere, to open a book and say: *here. Here is the truth, in ink, in a hand older than your disgrace.*

He knelt. The rusted hasp gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically against a century of damp, were books. Ledgers. Dozens of them, bound in canvas, their spines hand-labeled in faded iron-gall ink. Dates. Voyages. Numbers.

The topmost one fell open to a page headed with a date Oscar recognized the way you recognize the date of a funeral.

Behind him, faint but unmistakable, came a sound from the cave mouth — gravel shifting, the scrape of a boot on stone.

The tide, he thought wildly, was still an hour out.

Someone else had come to see what the sea had uncovered.

Oscar closed the ledger quietly, and reached for the dark.
