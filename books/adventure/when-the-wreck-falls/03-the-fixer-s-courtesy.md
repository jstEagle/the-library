## 3. The Fixer's Courtesy

Mara Voss had catalogued eleven thousand documents in her career, and she could say with professional confidence that the most dangerous ones were never locked away. They were the ones sitting in plain sight, misfiled under something boring. *Miscellaneous correspondence, 1911–1914.* *Shipping manifests, damaged lots.* The Meridian Syndicate had hidden its sins the way all careful institutions did: inside paperwork so dull that no one would ever want to read it.

She wanted to read it very badly.

The tribunal's subpoena sat in her satchel, third folder from the top, wedged between a tide table and a half-eaten packet of ginger biscuits. It was a beautiful piece of paper, actually — heavy stock, embossed seal, language precise enough to make a lesser archivist weep with envy. *Recovery of original ledgers pertaining to the Meridian Shipping Syndicate, 1908–1921, for purposes of international adjudication.* Her name was typed at the bottom as designated recovery agent, which meant that if she failed, her name would also be typed into the tribunal's record of failures, and Mara Voss had spent nine years building a career whose entire architecture was designed to keep her out of records like that.

Disgraced archivists did not get second subpoenas. Disgraced archivists got remembered.

She stood at the window of the Harbourmaster's Office in Port Calloway and watched the rain come down in ropes. The monsoon was early this year — three weeks early, which the fishermen were saying the way fishermen say things, spitting and shrugging and re-tying knots that didn't need re-tying. Early monsoons meant closed passes, cancelled sailings, and men like Oscar Oyelaran running out of options faster than usual.

Oscar Oyelaran. She'd been reading his file for two days and it read like a tragedy written by someone who hated adjectives. Mountain guide, certified at nineteen. Eleven seasons on the glacier routes without a single client injury — an almost supernatural record, until the twelfth season, when an avalanche took three climbers and somehow, in the syndicate's insurance filings, became his fault. The blacklist followed within a month. His partner's family had publicly disowned him. And now he was here, on this sodden coast, hauling storm debris for salvage wages because no expedition company on four continents would touch his name.

Mara believed in files the way some people believed in scripture, and the file said: *scapegoat*. But files also said Oscar Oyelaran had taken a strongbox out of a sea cave three nights ago, and files did not say what he intended to do with it. That was the variable. That was the whole case, really, balanced on one man's intentions.

"You're staring at the Oyelaran fellow's lodging again," said Mrs. Adeyemi, the harbourmaster's widow, who ran the office and, by extension, the town. She set a cup of tea on the desk beside Mara without being asked. "He's not home. Been out since dawn, hauling planks off the north beach."

"I'm not staring at his lodging. I'm observing it. Professionally."

"Mm." Mrs. Adeyemi looked out the window at the sagging boathouse where Oscar rented a bunk. "Observing. In my day we called that watching a man's window, and it ended in weddings or restraining orders."

"It ends in neither," Mara said, with more certainty than she felt about most things. "It ends in a subpoena being served."

"Romantic," said Mrs. Adeyemi, and went back to her ledger.

* * *

Aldous Renk arrived on the afternoon ferry, and Mara knew him before anyone told her, because he was the only man on the boat dressed like the weather was a rumor he'd declined to believe. Grey suit, dry shoes, an umbrella held at the exact angle of a man who had never once been caught out. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the kind of pleasant, forgettable face that Mara suspected had cost the Syndicate a great deal of money to maintain.

He found her within the hour, which told her everything about how well the Syndicate's eyes worked in Port Calloway.

"Dr. Voss." He appeared at her elbow outside the chandlery, dry as paper while the rain tried its best. "Aldous Renk. I understand you're doing archival work on the coast."

"Recovery work," Mara said. "And you understand correctly, Mr. Renk, which means you've read the tribunal's public docket, which means we can skip the part where we pretend you don't know why I'm here."

Something flickered behind the pleasant face — appreciation, maybe. Predators tended to enjoy meeting prey that could count.

"I admire thoroughness," Renk said. "May I buy you lunch? There's a place up the street that claims its fish was swimming yesterday, which I find either reassuring or alarming depending on my mood."

"I'm working."

"So am I," he said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of the conversation I'd like to have."

They walked, because walking let her keep her hands free and her back to a wall of shopfronts, and Renk talked the way rain fell — steadily, gently, wearing grooves into stone. He talked about the Syndicate's history, its philanthropies, the lighthouses it had funded and the scholarships it still paid. He talked about legacy, and reputation, and the terrible weight institutions carried when they were asked to answer for the dead. He never once said the word *ledgers*, and Mara noted that the way she'd note a missing folio: by its absence.

Then, at the corner where the road bent toward the boathouses, he stopped being indirect.

"The man you're looking for," Renk said, "is a thief."

"He's a salvager. The tide brought those documents ashore. Under coastal law, that makes them recoverable property."

"Under coastal law." Renk smiled at the rain. "You're a rules woman, Dr. Voss. I can always tell. You people are the easiest to work with, in the end, because you want the process to be clean more than you want the outcome. So here is a clean process." He produced a card — cream stock, embossed, matching the umbrella and the shoes and the whole dry, expensive surface of him. "Mr. Oyelaran is going to receive an offer today. A generous one. Passage off this coast, funds sufficient to start again somewhere the blacklist doesn't follow, and silence, which costs him nothing he isn't already losing. If he takes it, your recovery becomes moot — the documents will be, shall we say, returned to private custody, and your tribunal file closes as *unable to locate*. No failure attached to your name. No scandal. You go home, you keep your career, and everyone's process stays clean."

"And if he refuses?"

Renk's smile didn't move but something underneath it did, the way ice shifts over deep water. "Then it becomes untidy. And I have found, Dr. Voss, that untidy things tend to stick to whoever touched them last." He tipped his umbrella slightly, a bow. "I do hope you'll consider advising him accordingly. You strike me as the only person on this coast he might listen to."

"I met him zero times," Mara said.

"Give it a week," said Aldous Renk, and walked away, dry to the last.

* * *

She served the subpoena that evening, because if she was going to be accused of influencing Oscar Oyelaran, she preferred to do it legally and on the record.

She found him on the north beach, hauling a waterlogged doorframe up the shingle, and her first unprofessional thought was that the file photographs hadn't done him justice — they'd made him look gaunt and bitter, when in person he was simply built like the work he used to do, all rope-muscle and weather, with eyes that assessed her the way he'd probably once assessed a snow slope: for load, for angle, for whether she was about to come down on top of him.

"Dr. Voss," she said, holding out the folder. "Tribunal recovery agent. This is for you."

He read it standing in the rain, which apparently was just what people did here, and his expression moved through several weathers of its own. Suspicion. Recognition of the Syndicate crest on the cover page. Something rawer, quickly buried.

"They sent their fixer to see me at noon," he said finally. "Man named Renk. Offered me money and a boat ticket, like I was a cat they were trying to rehome."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him I don't take charity from people who ruined my life." He folded the subpoena along its crease, precisely, and tucked it inside his coat. "He smiled at that. Smiled the way a tide smiles."

"He came to me after. He wants me to advise you to sell."

"Do you?"

The question landed harder than it should have. Mara thought about her father's photograph on her desk in another country — a Syndicate captain's uniform, a proud jaw, a man who drowned in 1913 when the *Cormorant Queen* went down with cargo the Syndicate had insured twice over. She thought about nine years of penance filed in triplicate.

"No," she said. "I think you should give them to the tribunal. Through proper channels. Which is what that paper says."

"Proper channels." Oscar looked out at the grey water. "Proper channels took eleven years to look at my file. Proper channels cleared the Syndicate's surveyor of negligence while my partner's mother died believing I killed her son." He turned back to her, and there was no anger in it, which was worse — just a tired arithmetic. "But I'm not selling them either. Not because I'm noble. Because the moment I sell them, I'm the man they said I was. Understand? That's the only thing they took that I can't buy back."

Mara stood in the rain and understood, entirely too well, and hated how much she understood.

* * *

At two in the morning, the glow woke her — orange light pulsing against her boarding-house ceiling, reflected off low cloud. By the time she reached the north beach, the boathouse where Oscar kept his bunk was burning like a signal fire, flames standing straight up in the windless rain as if the water itself had agreed not to interfere.

Oscar stood at the edge of the light, salvage hook in one hand, doing nothing, saving nothing, because there was nothing left to save. Mrs. Adeyemi materialized beside Mara with a blanket and the grim efficiency of a woman who had seen harbours burn before.

"No one hurt?" Mara asked.

"Boat was empty. He sleeps ashore on Sundays." Mrs. Adeyemi clicked her tongue. "Petrol, by the smell. Poured neat around the pilings. Very tidy."

Very tidy. Of course it was. Everything Aldous Renk did was tidy.

Oscar turned and saw her, and in the firelight his face was calm in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. "Well," he said. "There goes my deposit."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." But his knuckles were white on the salvage hook, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to something quiet and level, the voice of a man reading avalanche terrain. "He gave me until morning to reconsider. That was the message. Burn first, offer expires at dawn — polite as a calling card." He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly. "You said proper channels. Fine. But channels need a boat, Dr. Voss, and mine is currently a bonfire, and the monsoon's coming in early. So I'll ask you the question Renk was too polite to ask."

The rain fell. The fire hissed.

"What's the question?" Mara said, though she already knew.

"How fast does that research ketch of yours leak?"

* * *

Dawn found them at the harbour wall, assessing the ketch together — Mara pointing out the cracked hull plank with the resigned tone of a physician delivering a diagnosis, Oscar crouched to press his thumb against the split and grunting the grunt of a man who has fixed worse. Behind them, the ashes of his boathouse steamed in the early light, and somewhere down the quay, Mara was nearly certain, a grey suit was watching them with a pleasant, patient face.

"We leave with the tide," she said. "Before he decides tidiness requires witnesses."

Oscar slung his pack — ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against his spine — and managed something that was almost a smile.

"Then let's hope your boat sinks slower than my luck does."
