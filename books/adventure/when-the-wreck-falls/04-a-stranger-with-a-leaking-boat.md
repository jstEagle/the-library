## 4. A Stranger With a Leaking Boat

**Oscar**

The monsoon came eleven days early, the way debts do.

Oscar felt it before he saw it — a pressure change in his bad knee that had never once lied to him in twenty years of mountains. He was standing on the sea wall at Port Anselm with a paper bag of fried plantains going cold in his hand when the first drops hit like thrown gravel.

"Eleven days," he said to no one.

The fishmonger beside him laughed and began hauling her awning closed. "Eleven days early means eleven days more of it, guide-man. You want shelter, you find it now."

He had nowhere to be. That was the whole trouble with being blacklisted: your calendar went soft at the edges. He'd been planning to sleep in the ferry terminal again, but the ferry terminal flooded ankle-deep by four o'clock, and by five the wind was tearing the tin signs off the chandlers' row and using them as frisbees.

Which is how he ended up standing under the overhang of the Maritime Records Annex, watching a woman argue with a padlock.

She was soaked through, gray-eyed, furious at the building itself, shaking a key ring at a door that clearly wasn't hers anymore. A tribunal seal was pasted across the glass — *CLOSED PENDING REVIEW* — and beneath it someone had taped a newer notice: *EVACUATION ORDER, SECTOR 4.*

"The archive has a storm protocol," she said, not looking at him, in the tone of a person who had been talking to herself for hours. "It's a very good storm protocol. I wrote half of it."

"And?"

"And they locked me out of my own protocol." She finally turned. Her eyes did the thing he'd learned to dread in officials — the quick cataloguing, face to hands to boots, arriving at *blacklisted mountain guide, handle with tongs.* "You're Oyelaran."

"You're Voss." He'd seen her name on the subpoena notice Renk had waved at him like a menu. "Small coast."

"Small coast," she agreed. Then the rain found a gap in the overhang and poured down the back of her collar, and she said something in what he assumed was archival swearing, because it sounded Latin.

That was when the explosion of corrugated iron came off the roof across the street, and they both made the same decision without discussing it: the annex overhang was better than nothing, and nothing was coming.

They stood there an hour while the town drowned around them. She asked him three questions about the ledgers, each one phrased like a form. He answered none of them, each silence phrased like a wall. At some point she held out half a bar of chocolate from her coat pocket without comment, and he took it without comment, and that was the closest either of them came to diplomacy.

At nine, the harbor siren went — three long notes, the sound every coastal kid learns before their own name. *All boats out or all boats lost.*

"I have a boat," Mara said.

"Is it seaworthy?"

"It's a research ketch. It's *documented* seaworthy."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," she admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

**Mara**

The *Cormorant's Question* sat low in the water, which Mara chose to interpret as gravitas rather than leakage. She'd bought her with grant money seven years ago — forty feet of stubborn Norwegian steel, rigged for survey work, named after a question her father used to ask. The hull crack was new. It was also, she noted with professional detachment, exactly where the last survey report said the hull was thinning, which meant the report had been right and she'd been optimistic, and optimism was not a quality she could currently afford.

Oscar Oyelaran stepped aboard like a man boarding a stranger's kitchen. He looked at everything once, thoroughly, and touched almost nothing.

"Bilge pump?" he asked.

"Aft locker."

"Backup?"

"...There's a bucket."

"There's always a bucket." He found it, looked at the crack in the bucket, and set it aside. "Two buckets would be a fleet. One bucket is a metaphor."

Mara got them off the mooring while he bailed, and if she noticed that a disgraced guide handled a tiller better than most licensed skippers she'd chartered, she kept it to herself. The harbor mouth was a chaos of white water and running lights, fishing boats staggering out ahead of the worst of it, and then they were through, into the long gray heave of open coast, and the monsoon proper reached down and put its hand on them.

By midnight the bucket was losing.

"We can't run for the deep-water ports," Oscar shouted over the engine noise. "Renk will have eyes on every customs dock between here and Solvane."

"You think I don't know that?" She had the chart spread under a red lamp, weighted with a tin cup. "Fishing coves. Unregistered anchorages. There's a string of them downcoast — Halvard Sound, the old smokehouse jetty—"

"Halvard's a trap in this wind. The headland funnels it."

"It's also not on any syndicate manifest, which makes it the safest water on this coast tonight."

He was quiet a moment, watching her over the chart table, rain hammering the deck above like applause from a hostile crowd. "You've thought about how to hide documents before."

"I've thought about how to hide *myself* before. Documents are easier. They don't get seasick."

Something happened to his face then — not quite a smile, but the place where one used to live. "You're funny. Nobody warned me archivists were funny."

"Nobody warns anyone about archivists. That's our primary defense."

* * *

**Oscar**

Dawn found them anchored in a cove the charts barely bothered with, cliffs shouldering the wind aside, rain falling softer now, like it had gotten bored. The *Cormorant's Question* had taken on maybe eight inches of water overnight. Oscar had bailed most of it and dreamed, in the snatches of sleep between shifts, of strongboxes and padlocks and a dead man asking him why he hadn't called for the rope sooner.

Mara was already up, doing something surgical to the bilge pump with a screwdriver and a hair tie.

"That's not a repair," he said, handing her coffee from the spirit stove. "That's a hostage situation."

"It'll hold until we reach a yard." She drank, grimaced — he'd made it strong enough to strip varnish — and nodded thanks anyway. "We should talk terms."

"Terms."

"You have stolen documents from a sealed site. I have a tribunal subpoena requiring me to recover those documents through legal channels. We are currently sharing a bucket." She set the pump down. "So let's say plainly what we each think the other is going to do."

He respected that. He'd spent two years among people who only ever said things sideways. "You go first."

"I think you're going to sell them." No flinch, no apology. "A man who's lost his livelihood, his license, his name — holding the one object that could make him rich instead? Every incentive points at a buyer. And there are buyers. I know, because the tribunal has files on them."

"And you think I'm a thief."

"Aren't you?"

"The cave was open. The tide opened it. I didn't break anything except possibly my own ribs getting in." He turned his mug slowly. "Your turn to hear yours. I think you're going to bury those ledgers in procedure until everyone involved is dead of old age. I think 'pending review' is how the Syndicate wins without firing a shot — they just need the truth to arrive late, and you people are very good at late."

The words landed; he watched them land. But she didn't look away.

"My father was a Meridian captain," she said.

The rain filled the pause.

"He drowned off Cape Ferrow when I was nineteen. Official finding: navigational error, his fault, case closed, pension denied." She said it flatly, the way you recite something you've had carved into you so long it's smooth. "I became an archivist because records were the only weapon a nineteen-year-old girl could legally carry. And yes — I believe in procedure. Procedure is slow because it has to be *unbreakable*. If I hand a tribunal a ledger with a broken chain of custody, the Syndicate's lawyers will use that crack to discredit every page. Your revenge dies in committee. Mine too."

"Your father's in there," Oscar said quietly. "In the ledgers. The staged wrecks."

"I haven't read them."

"Why not?"

"Because if I read them alone, at night, on this boat—" her voice did something small and human, "—I'll find his name next to the word *expendable*, and I'll do something stupid. Procedure protects me from myself. That's not a weakness, Oyelaran. That's the design."

He looked at her a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere above, gulls argued with the weather and were losing.

"Here's my terms," he said. "Nobody sells anything. Nobody buries anything. We go through your channels — but *fast*. If the tribunal wants these papers, they get them inside the month, not inside the decade. And I keep copies until it's done. Not to sell. So that if your procedure gets slow, or gets bought, the truth doesn't die with the originals."

"Keeping copies is chain-of-custody contamination."

"So is drowning."

She stared at him, and then — he'd remember this later, the exact angle of it — she laughed. Short, rusty, surprised out of her like a bird flushed from cover.

"Fine," she said. "Copies. Contaminated, compromised, *witnessed* copies. I'll document that you hold them and why, so it reads as insurance, not leverage."

"You're going to file paperwork on our blackmail insurance."

"I'm going to file paperwork on our *mutually assured transparency*, yes."

"God help the tribunal."

"They won't know what hit them," she said, and there was that laugh again, and Oscar understood with a lurch that he was in genuine trouble — the kind no blacklist covered.

* * *

**Mara**

They raised anchor at noon for the run to Halvard Sound, and it was Oscar who spotted the boat.

Not a fishing boat. Fishing boats in this weather moved like drunks; this one moved like a bill collector. Gray hull, no name visible, sitting well off their stern and matching their course change for course change — including the little feint toward open water Mara made purely to test it.

"Fuel stop at the old cannery pier," Oscar said, too casually. "Next hour. Everyone stops there."

"Everyone stops there," Mara agreed, watching the gray shape. "Including us. Including, apparently, them."

"Could be coincidence."

"Do you believe that?"

He was silent long enough that the silence answered for him. Then he moved forward along the deck, checking lines with a sailor's economy, and said, without turning: "Get the ledgers somewhere that isn't obvious. And Mara — whatever happens at that pier, you stay with the boat."

"That's not how partnerships—"

"That's not a partnership yet," he said. "That's a bucket we're both holding. But if it becomes one, I'd like it to survive the week."

Ahead, the cannery pier slid out of the rain like something surfacing — and tied alongside it, engines idling, waiting with the patience of paid men, were two boats where yesterday there had been none.

Mara put her hand on the tiller and began, very calmly, to lie to the horizon about their heading.

"Oscar," she said. "How do you feel about swimming?"
