## 5. What the Ledger Doesn't Say

The cove had no name on any chart Mara Voss trusted, which was exactly why she'd chosen it. The fishermen who worked it called it something unprintable in three dialects and one language of pure gesture, and their boats sat at anchor around the *Kestrel's Folly* like patient dogs, nets drying from their rails in long amber sheets.

The monsoon had paused — not ended, never that, but drawn breath. Rain hung in the air without falling, a held note. Through the cabin skylight Mara could see Oscar Oyelaran on deck with his shirt off, re-caulking the seam above the waterline with a focus she found slightly offensive. Nobody should be that good at bailing and caulking simultaneously. It suggested a history of boats failing him, which fit everything else she knew about the man.

Which was almost nothing, she reminded herself. She knew he was blacklisted. She knew he carried a drybag of colonial ledgers worth more than every boat in this cove combined. She knew he slept with one hand near that drybag the way other men slept near a pistol.

And he knew precisely nothing true about her, because what was true about her would have sent him over the rail days ago, swimming for shore with the documents and no archivist to complicate his revenge.

She made tea instead of deciding anything. Tea bought time. Tea was the professional procrastinator's tool, and Mara had been a professional since she was nineteen years old and signed her first oath of custody.

"Your kettle whistles flat," Oscar said, coming below with sawdust in his hair and salt on his shoulders. He folded himself into the bench across from her, knees nearly to his chin; the *Folly* had been built for smaller, more optimistic people. "Like it's apologizing."

"It is. I've been meaning to fix it for six years." She poured two cups. "Everything on this boat has been meaning to be fixed for six years."

"That's not a boat, that's a memoir."

Despite herself — despite the ledgers in the hold, the tribunal subpoena in her bag, the man across from her being legally speaking evidence-in-transit — she laughed. It came out rusty. She noticed him noticing, and filed away the way his face changed when she laughed: something loosened behind the wariness, like ice going out of a river.

They drank tea while the cove breathed around them. A fisherman two anchors over was singing to his engine, coaxing it, the song older than the engine by centuries.

"I should tell you something," Mara said, before the tea could run out and cowardice could refill the cups.

Oscar set his mug down. Slowly. She watched him arrange his face into neutrality and fail at it; he'd never learned to hide things well, which was presumably how a mountain guide ended up blacklisted instead of quietly rich.

"My father," she said, "was Elias Voss. Master of the *Corinne B*, Meridian Syndicate coastal fleet, out of Halden Quay. Twenty-two years' service. Commendations. A photograph of him used to hang in the Syndicate headquarters lobby, next to the founder's portrait, until they took all the photographs down after the inquiries."

Oscar went very still. She'd seen stillness before — in witnesses, in defendants, in her own mirror — but his was different. His stillness was a man holding a door shut against weather.

"The *Corinne B* went down off Sker Point in the autumn of '09," she continued, because now that the door was open the wind had her. "Official finding: cargo shift in heavy seas. Insurance paid. My mother got a settlement and a form letter signed by someone whose signature was clearly stamped. I was eleven."

"Mara." His voice was careful. "Sker Point is in the ledgers."

"I know what's in the ledgers. I've read them twice." She turned her mug a slow circle on the table. "Page forty-one. Disbursement authorized, wreck staged, crew manifest reduced by one name prior to sailing. They told my father the run was routine. They wrote him into the numbers as a rounding error, and then they collected on his life the way you'd collect on freight."

The rain finally arrived, soft against the hull, as if it had been waiting politely outside the whole conversation.

"I became an archivist because archives are where truth goes when nobody powerful wants it," she said. "I told myself it was vocation. Calling. Love of the record." She smiled, and it hurt. "It was penance. Every document I catalogued, every chain of custody I maintained, every subpoena I answered within the hour — I was trying to be the kind of person who would have caught it. Who would have saved him, retroactively, through sheer procedural virtue. Duty as a religion. And religions are wonderful right up until they ask you to burn something."

"And the tribunal asked."

"The tribunal asked me to recover those ledgers through legal channels and hand them to people who will spend four years redacting them." Her jaw tightened. "I know how tribunals work, Oscar. Justice arrives, eventually, wearing a very nice suit, and by then everyone who suffered has died politely of old age. You want to burn the Syndicate down tomorrow. I understand it. God help me, some nights I want it too. But if we do it your way — sell them, leak them, torch them from a hilltop — half the pages become inadmissible and Renk walks, and my father stays a rounding error forever."

"So we do it your way and wait four years."

"We do it my way and *win*."

The silence between them filled with rain. Oscar looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something she didn't expect: he reached across the table and turned her mug so the handle faced her properly, a small absurd courtesy, an anchor dropped gently.

"You're not the only one carrying a dead man," he said.

* * *

He told it plainly, which was the only way he could have told it at all.

"His name was Teodor Aslan. We climbed together nine years. He taught me ice; I taught him to stop putting chili oil in everything. On the Karsk Ridge attempt — the one they revoked my certification over — Theo was leading the pitch when the cornice let go." His hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as though the table might otherwise go somewhere. "The official report said I ignored avalanche warnings. That I pushed the ascent for glory. There was a client on that rope, a Syndicate-linked logistics firm doing a 'team-building expedition,' and when the client's brother died, somebody needed to be guilty fast, and a blacklisted guide was cheaper than an inquiry."

"They framed you with the same pen they drowned my father with," Mara said quietly.

"Same ink." He almost smiled. "Theo's family got nothing. No pension, no acknowledgment. His mother sends me a letter every year on the anniversary asking when the mountain will tell the truth, and every year I write back that I'm working on it, and every year it's a lie, because what was I working on? Salvage jobs. Debt. Staying small enough that nobody remembered my name." He looked up. "Those ledgers aren't revenge, Mara. Revenge would be burning the headquarters down. Those ledgers are Theo's mother getting a letter that says *the mountain has told the truth*. It just happens that the same pages say your father wasn't a bad captain in bad luck — he was a murdered man on paper before he ever left the quay."

Mara discovered she was crying, which was professionally embarrassing, and that Oscar had passed her a rag without comment, which was somehow worse. She blew her nose with dignity.

"If we give them to the tribunal," she said, "your name gets cleared in a filing cabinet. Slowly. With footnotes."

"If we give them to the tribunal," he said, "they survive. I've spent nine years learning what it costs when the truth depends on one angry man keeping it alive. One storm, one fire, one fixer with a boat hook, and Theo's gone again. Paper in a system outlives paper in a drybag." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him visibly. "Doesn't mean I have to like the waiting."

"No," she said. "You get to hate the waiting. That's in the regulations. Section twelve."

"There are regulations for hating waiting?"

"There are regulations for everything. I wrote half of them." She wiped her eyes. "It's amazing what you can build out of grief if you're organized."

Outside, the light was going copper. The fishermen were lighting lamps, and the singing had stopped, and somewhere beyond the headland the sea was arranging tomorrow's weather with its usual indifference. Oscar gathered the mugs, washed them without being asked, and dried them with a thoroughness that suggested he needed his hands busy while his heart settled.

At the companionway he stopped.

"Mara. If it comes to it — if Renk corners us and there's a choice between the ledgers and you—" He shook his head, annoyed at his own sentence. "Forget it. Bad phrasing."

"I heard it anyway," she said.

"Yeah." He went up on deck to check the anchor, moving carefully, like a man who had just handed someone the last of his rations and wasn't sure yet whether that had been wise.

Mara sat alone in the lamplight with the tea going cold and her father's name sitting quietly in the air where she'd finally said it aloud. Penance, she thought, had always assumed it would be solitary. Nobody warns you that confession is contagious — that you trade wounds with a stranger in a leaking boat and come out the other side holding something that doesn't have a file number.

Then she heard his boots cross the deck overhead, quick, and stop.

"Mara," Oscar called down, and his voice had shed every ounce of warmth it had carried ten minutes ago. "There's a launch coming into the cove. Running dark. No lights, no registration, and it's not slowing down."
