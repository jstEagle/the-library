## 6. Renk's Long Arm

The fuel dock at Puerto Cabral was the kind of place that smelled like diesel and broken promises, and I'd have bet my last coin that Renk had a man on it before we ever tied off.

I told Mara as much while she coiled the spring line. She gave me the look she reserved for my paranoia — fond, exasperated, the look of a woman who had spent two weeks sharing a leaking ketch with a man who checked the bilge like other men check their pulse.

"Oscar," she said, "it's a fuel dock. There are four buildings and a dog."

"The dog worries me most."

"The dog is asleep in a fish crate."

"That's how they get you."

But I wasn't joking, not really. Two weeks of running downcoast had taught me something about Aldous Renk: he didn't chase. He waited. He was a man who understood that everything on water eventually comes to shore — for fuel, for food, for water, for hope. You could outrun a boat. You couldn't outrun needing things.

So we had a plan. Simple, ugly, effective. Mara would go ashore to pay and provision — a disgraced archivist with tribunal paperwork made a boring, forgettable customer — while I stayed aboard the *Tern*, keeping the ledgers strapped in their oilskin bundle against my chest under a foul-weather jacket, looking like exactly what I appeared to be: a broke salvage man waiting on his woman.

We'd rehearsed it. If anything went wrong, she'd drop the fuel nozzle. That was the signal. A dropped nozzle.

I want it noted, for the record of whatever court eventually hears this story, that the plan worked right up until the moment it didn't.

* * *

Mara went up the dock at half past nine, list in hand, hair pinned back, wearing the particular expression of a woman about to argue about the price of tomatoes. I watched her all the way to the fuel office. The dog did not wake up. The harbor lay flat and gray-green under a sky the color of wet slate, monsoon clouds stacked on the horizon like plates nobody had washed.

I was below, checking the bilge — because of course I was — when I heard footsteps on the deck above. Three sets. Heavy, unhurried, spaced like men who'd done this before.

Not Mara. Mara walked like punctuation.

I killed the cabin lamp and put my hand on the knife I kept by the companionway, which is not a thing I'm proud of owning but am glad I owned. The hatch darkened. A face came into it, square and sunburned, with the flat professional patience of a man who gets paid by the hour whether this goes easy or hard.

"Afternoon," he said. "Mr. Oyelaran. Mr. Renk sends his regards."

"He should send fuel money instead. We're nearly out."

The man smiled without warmth. "He's actually very generous. There's an envelope below with your name on it. Enough to make you comfortable in three countries. All he wants is the bundle you're sitting on top of."

"I'm sitting on a bilge pump."

"You're sitting on 1897 through 1904, is what you're sitting on." He said it lightly, conversationally, and that lightness scared me more than shouting would have. These were men who knew what they were taking. "Look. Nobody here needs to be a hero. The lady comes back from the chandlery, you hand over the package, everybody has a nice lunch."

"And if I say no?"

"Then it stops being a nice morning." He said it the way you'd read a shipping forecast. "No hard feelings either way. That's just weather."

Here's the thing about being blacklisted for six years: it teaches you exactly what your name is worth, and it isn't much. Here's the second thing: it teaches you what your word is worth, and if you've kept it clean, that's worth everything, because it's the only asset they couldn't take.

"No," I said.

He sighed, genuinely regretful, and reached past the hatch.

* * *

What happened next happened fast and badly, the way real violence does — nothing like the tidy choreography of stories. I got the knife hand up and caught his wrist on the companionway frame, and he was stronger than me, or better braced, and the whole struggle became a stupid grinding argument about leverage conducted at a range of eighteen inches. Behind him his two friends came over the rail, and one of them grabbed my ankle through the hatch, and I learned that a man can be in three fights at once and lose all of them simultaneously.

They got the jacket half off me. They got one strap of the ledger bundle. And then Mara's voice cut across the dock like a gull with opinions:

"HEY! That's MY boat!"

And she threw the fuel nozzle.

Not at anyone — she wasn't stupid — she threw it onto the planks, which was the signal, except the signal had been *drop the nozzle quietly and walk away*, and Mara Voss had apparently decided somewhere between the chandlery and the dock that signals were for people with more patience than her. Diesel arced across the boards. The dog woke up. Everything after that was improvisation.

She came down the dock swinging a canvas bag of provisions — I will never know what possessed her, except that I do know, because I've since fallen in love with her and understand completely — and caught the second man across the shoulders with what felt like twelve kilos of rice and tinned fish. He went down swearing. The first man loosened his grip for half a second, and half a second is a currency I've traded in my whole life.

I wrenched free, slammed the hatch, and threw the engine over while she was still scrambling aboard. The lines were still cleated. Something cracked — a cleat, a stanchion, possibly my future as a careful person — and the *Tern* lurched off the dock trailing rope and dignity.

"THE NOZZLE MEANT WALK AWAY," I shouted over the engine.

"THE NOZZLE MEANT WHATEVER I SAY IT MEANT," she shouted back, hauling in the trailing line while bullets of rain began to stitch the harbor. "Are you hurt?"

"Are YOU? You hit a man with groceries!"

"He was boarding us. It was proportionate."

It was not proportionate. It was magnificent. But there was no time to tell her so, because the third man had reached the end of the dock with a rifle, and because behind us, sliding out from behind the breakwater where it had been waiting all along the way I'd known it would be, came a gray-hulled launch with too much horsepower and no fishing gear whatsoever.

Renk hadn't chased us. He'd simply known that everything on water comes to shore.

* * *

The squall saved us, though I hesitate to call anything that nearly drowned us a savior.

The front came off the sea like a thrown blanket — no gradual building, just a wall of rain and wind arriving all at once, the way monsoons do when they're showing off. Visibility dropped to fifty meters. The launch behind us fired twice, the reports swallowed whole by the wind, and then even the sound of its engine dissolved into the roar.

I put the *Tern* on a heading I didn't entirely trust, into open water, away from the coast, away from every sensible option, because sensible options were all bought and paid for.

"We can't outrun them in this," Mara yelled, bailing with the bucket because the electric pump had died an hour outside Puerto Cabral and the manual pump was a rumor. "We can't outrun them in ANYTHING. This boat has the hydrodynamic profile of a bathtub with ambitions."

"So we don't outrun them. We confuse them."

"How?"

I told her. She looked at me like I'd suggested we pay our debts in seashells.

"That's insane."

"It's also the only idea I have, and I've been rationing ideas since Tuesday."

The plan was this: the ledgers went over the side. Not lost — never lost — but weighted, bundled inside the spare anchor bag with a marker buoy and sixty meters of line paid out on a drift sock, left to hang beneath us in the gray chaos while the launch closed in. Let them board us. Let them search. Let them find an empty oilskin jacket and a man who looked, for once in his life, exactly as desperate as he was.

"They'll tear the boat apart," Mara said.

"Yes."

"They might tear US apart."

"Also yes. But they won't find them, and Renk doesn't destroy evidence he hasn't confirmed exists. He's careful. Careful men check twice."

"And if the line parts? If the buoy drags? If—"

"Mara." I took her face in my hands, salt and rain between us. "Six years ago I watched the mountain take my partner because I trusted a route someone else certified. I have spent six years being careful and it bought me nothing but a blacklist and a bilge pump. Trust me on this one thing. Just this one."

She held my eyes for three seconds, which in a squall is approximately a geological era.

"If we die," she said, "I'm haunting you specifically."

"Noted."

* * *

They boarded us twenty minutes later, four of them this time, Renk's square-jawed friend from the hatch among them, and they tore the *Tern* apart with the thorough, joyless efficiency of customs officers and tax assessors everywhere. They pulled floorboards. They emptied lockers. They found my emergency cash and took it, which stung, and Mara's father's sextant, which stung worse, and I stood in the rain with my hands visible and my heart going like a drum in a parade and let them.

Renk himself wasn't aboard. Of course he wasn't. Renk never dirtied his hands; he sent weather forecasts.

"Nothing," the square-jawed man radioed, finally, and I heard the flat disappointment come back through the static, and then the launch stood off and was gone into the rain, and we were alone on a wounded boat in open water with a bucket for a bilge pump and a bundle of colonial sins hanging sixty feet beneath our keel on a length of line I prayed to every god whose name I could remember.

Mara hauled it up herself. Her hands were shaking. So were mine. Inside the anchor bag, wrapped in three layers of oilskin, the ledgers sat dry as sermons.

She laughed. It was not a sane laugh. It was the laugh of a woman who has been boarded at gunpoint and hit a man with groceries and is only now doing the arithmetic.

"You gave up the sextant," she said. "Your cash. Everything."

"Everything except the true thing." I touched the bag. "That's the trade. They can have the rest."

The *Tern* limped northeast through the dying squall, and I went forward to see how bad the damage was, and it was bad — a cracked seam weeping faster than any pump we had, a jury-rigged future measured in hours, not days. We needed a haul-out, materials, a harbor that wasn't on Renk's payroll.

Which left exactly one place. The fjord. The glacier pass. The route that had taken Tomas, opening for one short season and then closing like a mouth.

Mara came and stood beside me at the bow, reading the chart over my arm, and I felt her go still when her finger found the same mark mine had.

"There's no other way?" she asked quietly.

There wasn't. And ahead of us, faint through the clearing rain, the mountains stood up white and indifferent, waiting to see if I'd come back.
