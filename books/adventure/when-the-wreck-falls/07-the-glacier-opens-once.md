## 7. The Glacier Opens Once

*Oscar*

The fjord announced itself the way bad news does — quietly at first, then all at once.

For two days we'd motored up the coast in a boat that sounded less like an engine and more like an apology, the ketch's cracked hull weeping seawater into the bilge faster than the pump could lie about it. Mara bailed with a bucket when the pump faltered; I steered and pretended not to notice how tired her hands looked. Then the headland opened like a book, and there it was: a finger of grey-green water reaching inland toward mountains that had no business being that beautiful to a man about to climb them.

"Skarvholmen," Mara said, reading it off the chart the way other people read obituaries.

"I know."

"You've been here before."

"I've been here before." I cut the throttle and let the silence fill in around us. The water was so still it held the mountains upside down, and for a second I couldn't tell which way was up, which felt about right.

The last time I'd seen this fjord I'd seen it from above, from the shoulder of the pass, on my knees in the snow with Teodor's rope end slack in my hands. That was six years ago. The blacklist came down on me eleven days later, neat as a coffin lid. *Guide error. Improper route assessment.* The Syndicate's insurance investigators needed someone to blame for the wreck below the pass — their wreck, their staged storm damage, their dead crew — and a mountain guide with a foreign name and no lawyers was cheaper than the truth.

Now the truth was wrapped in oilcloth in the dry locker under my bunk, and the only way out of this country with it ran over the glacier behind those mountains. The pass opened once a year between the spring melt and calving season. According to the pilot's almanac Mara kept in the chart table, we had four days. Maybe five if the weather lied kindly.

We tied up at the fuel dock in Skarvholmen village, which consisted of a fish shed, a chapel, a fuel dock, and a woman named Signe who sold all three services and judged you as part of the transaction.

"Two jerry cans," I said. "And whatever coffee isn't a crime."

Signe looked at me for a long moment. In a village this size, faces are currency, and mine had apparently been spent here before. "You're the one who lost the man on the ice," she said. Not cruelly. Just the way you'd note the weather.

"That was me."

"They said you took the wrong line."

"They said a lot of things." I counted out coins. She took them but didn't move to fetch the cans.

"Pilots aren't flying this week," she said instead. "Or next week. Or until somebody tells them they can again. You want to know who tells them, ask yourself who's been buying diesel by the drum."

The cold went through me slower than fjord water but deeper. "Man in a grey coat? Smiles like he's doing you a favor?"

"He bought the chapel roof fund too," Signe said. "Generous fellow." She finally turned to get the cans, and over her shoulder added, "There's still the old route. Nobody flies it since your accident. Nobody's marked it either. Charts show what charts are paid to show."

Mara appeared at my elbow with the bucket, which she'd apparently decided was now a permanent accessory. She'd heard enough. Her face had gone into its archive mode — closed, catalogued, unreadable — which I was learning meant she was frightened and would rather die than file it under that heading.

"The old route," she repeated, once we were back aboard.

"It's not a route. It's a scar." I started coiling the mooring line just to have something to do with my hands that wasn't shaking. "It's the line Teodor and I were on when the serac came off. It's why they blamed me — because no sane guide takes that line, so taking it must mean incompetence. Except the sane line was closed by weather, and the Syndicate's own schedule pressure is why we were up there in the first place. None of which fit on an incident report."

"And it goes over the same glacier."

"Same glacier. Same icefall. Six years older and six years hungrier." I looked up at the pass, white and silent in the distance, beautiful the way a loaded thing is beautiful. "Four days, Mara. And every legal pilot within two hundred kilometers has been bought."

She was quiet for a while. The bucket sat between us on the deck, half-full of honest seawater, the most truthful thing on the boat.

"My father's ship went down in water like this," she said at last. "Calm. That's what always got me. Not a storm. Calm water, and a hole in the hull where no rock should be." She looked at the ledgers' hiding place, then at me. "Take the old route, Oscar. Take it because Renk thinks fear will do his work for him. Don't give him the discount."

I almost laughed. Almost. "You realize you're asking me to walk back into the worst day of my life."

"I'm asking you to walk through it," she said. "There's a difference. One of them ends on the far side."

* * *

*Mara*

He didn't sleep, and he thought I didn't know. I lay in the narrow berth listening to him re-rig gear in the dark — ropes checked twice, then a third time, carabiners opened and shut with small metallic whispers like a man rehearsing a language he'd sworn never to speak again.

I understood it more than he'd guess. Archives have their own icefalls. There's a box in the tribunal's sealed collection I have never been able to open without my hands going cold — the one with my father's last voyage manifest, the one with the freight figures that don't add up, the one that proves he signed on to a ship his own company intended to lose. Every time I open it, I'm twenty-six again, reading it for the first time in a records room that smelled of dust and formaldehyde, understanding that the tidy career I'd built on order and procedure was itself built on a lie I'd been hired to maintain.

Duty is how some of us grieve. I'd learned that about myself the hard way, and watching Oscar coil ropes at three in the morning, I recognized the shape of it in him.

At dawn the weather gave us its answer: high pressure sliding in from the east, cold and clear, the kind of window that opens like a door and closes like a trap. We beached the ketch at the head of the fjord, above the tide line, and I did what I could for her hull with epoxy and a prayer while Oscar packed the ledgers into a waterproof barrel harness that rode against his spine.

"You could leave them," I said. "Bury them here. Come back after."

"After what? After Renk finds them?" He shook his head. "They ride with me. They got me into this; they can carry their own weight out."

"That's not how barrels work."

"That's exactly how guilt works," he said, and shouldered the pack before I could argue with the physics.

The lower approach was kind — moraine gravel, then old moraine, then the glacier's snout, grey and dripping, a river being born under our boots. It was when the ice began in earnest that Oscar changed. I'd watched him for weeks now: a man who moved through the world like someone trying not to be noticed, economical, wry, always keeping a little of himself in reserve. On the ice, the reserve emptied. He became larger somehow, and sadder, his voice dropping into a register I hadn't heard — clipped, professional, the voice of a man reading from a script written in grief.

"Rope's ten meters shorter than standard," he said, checking it. "Teodor cut it that way. Said long ropes breed lazy spacing." He stopped. Looked at the coil in his hands. "First time I've said his name out loud in six years."

I put my hand over his on the rope. He didn't pull away. Above us the icefall stood in the morning light like a frozen cathedral mid-collapse, seracs leaning at angles that made my archivist's soul file itself under *structural failure, imminent.*

"We don't have to talk about him," I said.

"We'll have to talk about everything eventually. Might as well start where the ground is honest." He clipped in. "Stay left of the blue ice. Right side avalanches after noon. And Mara — if I say run, you run. Don't look back to see if I'm following. I'm always following. That's the whole job."

We climbed.

* * *

*Oscar*

By noon we were inside the icefall, and I knew every step before I took it.

That was the cruelty of it. Six years, and the mountain had barely redecorated. The same leaning tower of ice on our right — I'd flinched from it then, flinched from it now. The same crevasse bridge sagging like a bad argument. My body remembered the route the way a tongue remembers a chipped tooth, returning to the wound again and again without being asked.

Teodor had died forty meters above where we camped that first night. I knew it the way you know the location of anything you've tried to forget: precisely, permanently. I didn't tell Mara. But I pitched the tent thirty meters further along than prudence suggested, just so I wouldn't have to sleep on top of the memory.

He'd trusted me. That was the ledger entry I carried that no tribunal would ever read. He'd been the junior partner, fresh off his certification, and he'd said — I could still hear it, that easy grin, wind tearing the words apart — *your call, boss, I'm just the legs.* And my call had been the old route, because the Syndicate's charter schedule said we'd lose the season otherwise, because I was young and hungry and wanted the fee, and the mountain had collected the debt with interest compounded daily ever since.

Renk thought he'd cornered me into this route. He hadn't understood. I'd been walking this route every day for six years, in every country, on every job I couldn't get, at every interview that ended when they read the blacklist. The ice was just where I'd finally caught up with myself.

Mara cooked snow into water and made tea that tasted like the inside of a thermos, which is to say like mercy. She didn't ask questions. She sat close enough that our shoulders touched, and outside the nylon the glacier groaned — long, tectonic complaints, the sound of a thing shifting its weight in its sleep.

"It's talking," she said.

"It's always talking. Question is whether anyone's listening." I turned my cup slowly. "Tomorrow we cross the upper basin. Two hours of exposure under the big seracs. If the temperature holds cold, we're fine. If the sun gets into the ice—"

"Then we go early."

"We go early," I agreed. "And fast. And quiet."

She looked at me across the steam. "Oscar. Whatever happens up there tomorrow — whichever choice you have to make — I need you to hear this now, while it's calm. The ledgers matter. Justice matters. But I didn't come this far to watch this mountain take another person I—" She stopped. Recalibrated, the way she did, filing and refiling. "Just come across. Both halves of you. The guide and the man."

Above us, in the blue dark, something enormous let go — not near, not threatening, just the glacier clearing its throat, reminding us whose house this was. Mara's hand found mine in the dark and stayed there.

We slept in shifts. Mine, I spent listening to the ice learn our names.

At 3 a.m., an hour before first light, I woke her with tea already made and the rope already coiled.

"Early," I said. "Fast. Quiet."

And somewhere below us in the black fjord, faint as a struck match, a boat engine started that shouldn't have been there.
