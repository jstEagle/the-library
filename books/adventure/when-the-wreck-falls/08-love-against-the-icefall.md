## 8. Love Against the Icefall

The glacier had a voice, and it was not a comforting one.

It spoke all night through the fabric of our tent — a language of groans and cracks, of deep tectonic sighs, like an old house settling except the house was four hundred feet of ice and the settling could kill you. I lay awake cataloguing the sounds the way I used to catalogue manuscript discrepancies: *that one's normal, that one's concerning, that one we simply will not think about.*

Oscar slept like a man with a clear conscience, which I found both enviable and slightly insulting.

"Your glacier is talking," I told him at some black hour.

"It's not my glacier." He didn't even open his eyes. "And it's not talking. It's breathing. Everything breathes before it moves."

"That's supposed to be reassuring?"

"No," he said. "It's supposed to be true. Reassuring is a different department."

I should explain how we got here, because from the outside it must look like madness: two people with no business on ice, hauling themselves up a route that had already killed once, carrying documents worth more than both our lives combined.

Three days ago we'd reached the fjord and discovered that every pilot in the village had been bought — Renk's money in their pockets like sugar in tea. The ferry was closed to us. The road was watched. Which left the pass, and the pass left only one way up: the northeast couloir, the route Oscar had descended eleven years ago with his partner Tomas, and descended alone.

He'd stood at its base for a long time that first morning, saying nothing, looking up at the blue-white throat of it the way you look at a letter you know contains bad news.

"We can find another way," I'd said.

"There isn't one. There's this, or there's Renk." He'd adjusted his pack straps — the ledgers rode against his spine, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in a dry bag, the whole archive of Meridian's sins riding between his shoulder blades like a second conscience. "Tomas always said the mountain doesn't hold grudges. It just doesn't remember you were ever there."

"And what do you say?"

"I say he was being generous."

* * *

We climbed the way Oscar taught me to climb, which was slowly, and then slower than that.

He was a different man on ice. On the boat he was all jokes and superstition — refusing to whistle, naming the bilge pump after a saint, eating crackers in an order he claimed was strategic but looked like avoidance. Up here, the humor went quiet and something else came forward: total, unhurried attention. He read the slope the way I read provenance records, looking for the forgery, the weakness, the place where the story didn't hold.

"Screw your feet in properly," he said, for the fourth time. "Not politely. Like you mean it."

"I'm a maritime archivist. My feet are used to decks."

"Your feet are about to be used to falling, if you don't—"

"I'm doing it. Look. Meanly."

He glanced back and something crossed his face — not quite a smile, but the shape where a smile kept its coat. "Good. Now don't trust the ice. Trust the rope. The ice lies; the rope doesn't."

Above us, the seracs hung in their frozen cascade like a chandelier someone had abandoned mid-installation. Every so often the glacier exhaled and a fine powder of snow sifted down, catching the light, almost beautiful if you refused to think about tonnage.

"Tomas fell here?" I asked. I hadn't meant to ask. It came out anyway, the way questions do when the silence gets too heavy to carry alone.

"Not here. Below the icefall, on the descent. The route was rotten and we knew it, and we took it anyway because the weather window was closing and I wanted—" He stopped. Chipped a step. Started again. "I wanted the last climb of the season to count for something. A client was waiting. Money was waiting. So we came down fast, and the slope did what rotten slopes do." Another pause. "The inquiry said guide error. My error. The Syndicate made sure the inquiry reached that conclusion, because the client whose schedule I was protecting was theirs, and a dead partner makes a convenient footnote."

"You've carried that."

"I've carried worse. I've carried the part where everyone believed it." He looked back at me, wind-burned, utterly calm. "You asked once why I didn't sell the ledgers when Renk offered real money. That's why. It was never about the money. It was about one line in one report that says *Oscar Oyelaran, guide error.*"

"The ledgers say otherwise."

"The ledgers say everything otherwise." He turned and climbed on. "That's the whole problem with them. That's why Renk wants them burned."

We gained the shoulder of the icefall by midday, and that was where the mountain decided to have opinions.

* * *

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a door closing somewhere enormous, deep under our feet, followed by a crack that ran up the wall beside us like lightning deciding to be geological instead.

"Mara!" Oscar's voice, sharp as the air. "Move! Traverse right, now!"

I moved. That much I can say for myself. I moved exactly as trained and exactly wrong, because the shelf under my boots chose that moment to stop being a shelf. It didn't collapse dramatically. It simply resigned, quietly, the way old institutions do, and tilted, and I went with it — sliding, then falling, then caught short with a jerk that emptied my lungs, swinging on the rope over a blue gulf of crevasse with the whole chandelier of the serac field groaning above me.

Below my boots: forty meters of nothing, going down into light the color of bottled sea.

Above me: Oscar, braced across the slope, rope burning through his gloved hands, anchored to a picket he'd driven in minutes before — minutes, as it turned out, that would go down in my personal history as the most consequential minutes anyone ever spent screwing metal into ice.

"Hold!" he shouted. "Don't try to climb! Hold!"

"I'm holding!"

"Hold *still!*"

"I'm holding still-ly!"

Somewhere above us, the ice made its decision. I heard it gather itself — a long, tearing roar — and I looked up in time to see the serac release.

Not ours. Not directly. A tower of ice two hundred meters up and to the left, calving all at once, tons upon tons of it peeling off and thundering down the mountain in a white avalanche that hit the slope above us and exploded into a cloud of powder and debris.

And here is the thing I will remember until I die, the thing I have already told the tribunal and told no one else, because it isn't evidence of anything except a man:

Oscar did not move.

The avalanche roared, the slope shuddered, the rope went slack and taut and slack again as debris sheared past, and Oscar Oyelaran stayed anchored exactly where he was, hands locked on the line that held me, while the pack with the ledgers — the pack that was supposed to matter more than anything, the pack Renk had burned a building and bought a border patrol to get — began to slide off his shoulders.

I saw it happen. I saw him feel it happen. And I saw him let it.

He shifted his weight deliberately, deliberately, away from the pack, deeper into the anchor, giving the strap nothing to catch on, and the ledgers tore free and went cartwheeling down the slope in a spray of oilcloth and orange dry-bag, bouncing twice, disappearing into the debris field below in a puff of white.

"Gone," I screamed at him. "Oscar, they're gone, cut me loose and go after—"

"Shut up," he said, conversationally, through gritted teeth, hauling hand over hand. "You're heavier than paperwork."

"You're insane—"

"Probably. Brace on the wall — foot, now, there—" My boot found ice. His arm found my harness. And then, with a strength I would not have credited the lean salvage diver I'd met in a storm-battered harbor, he pulled me up out of the blue gulf and onto the slope, and we lay there side by side on the wrecked shelf, gasping, while the powder snow settled around us like the mountain apologizing without meaning it.

The silence afterward was total. Even the glacier held its breath.

Then Oscar started laughing. It was a strange sound — ragged, half-shock, entirely real — and it undid something in me, because I started too, lying on my back on a broken ice shelf with my hair full of snow, laughing at absolutely nothing and absolutely everything.

"They're gone," I managed. "The ledgers. They're gone."

"No," he said.

I turned my head. He was holding up his wrist, and around the wrist, tucked under his glove strap, was a flash drive on a lanyard — the copies I'd made in the fishing cove, the ones I'd insisted on, the ones he'd teased me for as archivist paranoia.

"You absolute—" I sat up. "You let the originals go. You let *Renk's prize* go, you let the only physical proof—"

"I let the thing strapped to my back go," he said, "because the thing strapped to my back was trying to drag me off my anchor while you hung over a hole in the world." He said it simply, without ceremony, wiping snow from his face. "Paper burns. Paper sinks. Paper gets stolen. I've watched enough wrecks to know the difference between cargo and crew."

"Cargo and crew," I repeated.

"Mm." He was already moving, checking the anchor, checking the rope, checking me — hands on my harness buckles, my wrists, my ribs, brisk and thorough, a man inspecting damage he couldn't bear to name. "You're intact?"

"I'm intact."

"Nothing broken?"

"My dignity. It slid forty meters."

"It'll recover. Dignity always does. It's the least honest thing either of us owns." He pulled me to my feet, and for a moment neither of us stepped away, standing close on the ruined slope with the whole white world tilting gently around us. His gloves were shredded where the rope had run through them. His palms underneath were bleeding.

"Oscar." I took his wrist, turned it over. "You held on with these."

"Somebody had to." He looked at me then — really looked, the way he'd looked at the couloir, reading for weaknesses, finding none he was willing to use. "Mara. When we get down off this ice, there's a conversation I'd like to have. Nothing dramatic. Just—"

The radio at his hip crackled.

We both froze. It was the little relay unit from the ketch, tuned to the fjord frequency, and it crackled again and resolved into a voice I recognized — smooth, unhurried, courteous as a knife laid on clean linen.

*"Miss Voss. Mr. Oyelaran. Aldous Renk. I'll keep this brief, since I understand your conditions are poor."* A pause, almost polite. *"My men met an interesting piece of debris coming down the mountain this afternoon. Orange bag. Very well wrapped. I do hope nothing important was inside — though of course, if it wasn't, you'll want to come down and tell me so yourselves. The north trail. Tonight. I've taken the liberty of arranging company."*

The radio clicked off.

Oscar and I looked at each other across the settling snow, and I watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes — the ledgers gone, Renk waiting below, the pass still a day above us and closing.

"He has the originals," I said quietly.

"He thinks he has the originals," Oscar said. And then, slower, as it landed on both of us at once: "He *thinks* he has the originals."

I felt my own mouth begin, helplessly, to smile.
