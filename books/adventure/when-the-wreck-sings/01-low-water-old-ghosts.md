## 1. Low Water, Old Ghosts

The Kestrel River has three moods, and I've learned to read all of them the way other people read weather. There's the morning mood, all silver and apology, fog sitting on the water like it's sorry for something. There's the afternoon mood, when the wind comes down from the north and turns the current into a argument. And then there's the mood nobody talks about much, the one where the river goes quiet and low and starts showing you things it meant to keep.

That was the mood on the day I found the cave.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and the river taught me better than that. You don't run a reach of water by skipping to the end of it. You take it bend by bend, mark by mark, the way it comes to you.

* * *

I was running cargo down from Harrow's Landing to Saltmarsh Quay—twelve pallets of roofing tin, four crates of something the manifest optimistically called *agricultural equipment* and which smelled, when you got close enough, like a distillery's apology—and I was doing it in the *Pelican's Regret*, which is a name I did not choose but have come to appreciate. A boat named after a bird that swallows more than it can carry and looks pleased about it. There are worse ways to describe a person.

Twenty years on the Kestrel. That's the arithmetic I let people have. Twenty years, cargo and passengers and the occasional coffin, because the Kestrel towns learned a long time ago that I don't ask what's in the crates and I don't ask what's in the past, mine or theirs. It's a good arrangement. It's the arrangement I built, plank by plank, the way you build anything you intend to keep.

"Captain." Dovie's voice came up through the deck plates from the engine room, pitched to carry over the diesel without actually being a shout, because Dovie considers shouting a failure of engineering. "Number two's running warm. Nothing dramatic. Just thought you'd want it in the log."

"I want it in the log and I want it cooler," I called back.

"That's two wants, Captain. I can do one."

"Do the second one first."

There was a pause, which in Dovie's language is a laugh. Then the thump of a hatch, and the *Pelican's Regret* settled into her work again, that deep satisfied thrum she makes when everything below decks is happy. A boat tells you the truth about itself if you listen. People are harder. People will lie to you with their whole face.

Rill was up on the foredeck with a gaff hook they weren't using, watching the banks go by. Nineteen, maybe twenty, all elbows and opinions, hired off the dock at Saltmarsh three months back because they showed up sober, on time, and willing to learn the lead line—which on the Kestrel is two out of three more qualifications than most. They had the restless look of the young, the one that mistakes stillness for stagnation. I remembered having that look. I'd worked hard to lose it.

"You ever get bored?" Rill asked, the way they ask things—like the question costs nothing.

"Of what?"

"Of this. Same river. Same towns. Same tin."

I watched a heron stand on a deadfall with the patience of a creature that has never once been in a hurry and has never once been wrong about it. "The river isn't the same river," I said. "That's the first thing it teaches you and the last thing anybody learns. You never step in the same water twice, and you never run the same reach twice either. The bar at Cooper's Bend was four feet further east last spring. The cut below the trestle silted two feet since the floods. The river is a document that rewrites itself nightly. You could spend a lifetime on it and never read the same page."

Rill considered this. "So it's not boring. It's homework."

"That's one way to put it."

"You put everything one way, Captain. Like you've got one way for everything and you don't let anybody see the others."

I didn't answer that, because there wasn't an answer I was willing to give, and Rill, to their credit, let it lie. That's another thing the river teaches: when to let a current carry something past you instead of fighting it.

* * *

We made Cooper's Bend by midday, took on water, delivered two pallets of tin to a man named Ostergaard who paid in cash and conversation, both of which I accepted in the quantities offered and no more. Ostergaard wanted to talk about the tide.

"Lowest I've seen it in nine years," he said, leaning on his dock rail with the air of a man about to make a point he'd already made to everyone else in the county. "Lowest since the drought summer. You mark it. River's showing her bones."

"Rivers don't have bones," I said, taking the receipt.

"Everything has bones, Captain. Everything. You just got to wait for the water to drop far enough."

I signed the paper and didn't think about it again for six hours, which is the last kind thing I will say about my own judgment in this account.

* * *

Below Cooper's Bend the Kestrel starts remembering it used to be tidal. The banks widen, the water goes brackish and brown-green, and the channel threads between mudflats that the charts—my charts, the good ones, the ones I've corrected in my own hand for twenty years—mark with the flat confidence of people who surveyed from a desk. The current slows. The air changes. You can smell the sea before you can see any evidence of it, a salt-and-rot sweetness that gets into the wood of the boat and never quite leaves.

I've run this reach perhaps four hundred times. I could run it blind, and have, once, in fog so thick the lead line was the only honest instrument aboard. So when the water started behaving strangely below the old lime kilns—falling when it should have been slack, pulling in long slow seams toward the north bank—I noticed. Not the way Rill noticed, which was to say not at all. The way you notice a wrong note in a song you've sung your whole life.

"Dovie," I said into the engine room voice-tube. "Ease back to half. I want to look at something."

"Looking at things costs money, Captain."

"Put it on my tab."

I brought her in slow, that easy half-throttle drift that lets the hull answer the water instead of arguing with it, and I watched the current. It wasn't an eddy. It wasn't a scour hole doing what scour holes do. It was a pull—steady, organized, directional—coming from a place where the chart showed nothing but mudflat and a stand of dead alders leaning out over the water like old men arguing at a funeral.

The tide was at the bottom of its drop. Ostergaard's nine-year low. And the mudflat that had been mudflat for as long as anyone had written anything down about it was, at the margins, becoming something else. The water was peeling back from the north bank in a long smooth sheet, and behind that sheet, where the alders ended and the rock began—

There was a mouth.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. I don't mean a hollow in the bank. I don't mean an undercut where the current had eaten the clay. I mean a mouth: an arch of dark stone, twice the height of my wheelhouse, opening into the hillside, with water running out of it in a slow clear tongue and the rock around it streaked pale with old tide lines. It was the kind of thing you'd see on a coast, on a chart, with a name and a caution note beside it. It was not the kind of thing you see on the Kestrel, four miles below the lime kilns, in a reach I have read like a psalm for twenty years.

"Captain?" Rill had come aft without me hearing them, which bothered me more than the cave did. They were staring at it too, gaff hook forgotten. "Is that on the chart?"

"No."

"Is that *possible*? For something that size to not be on the chart?"

"Everything's possible," I said. "The chart's just a bet about what's likely."

But my hands, on the wheel, were not as steady as my voice, and I noticed that too. Because here is a thing I have never said out loud, not to Dovie, not to Marisol, not to the river itself, which knows everything and tells nothing: I do not like it when the world shows me a page I haven't read. I built my life the way I built my charts—corrected, annotated, nothing left blank. No fixed address. No history discussed. A man—I use the word loosely, the way the river uses its banks—cannot be ambushed by his past if he keeps it in a box with the lid nailed down. That was Tam's teaching, though he never said it in those words. Tam said: *the river forgives, Casper-girl, but the land remembers. Stay on the water and you're only ever passing through.*

He'd been dead four years. He'd been my whole map for twenty before that.

"Should we go in?" Rill asked, and there was a hunger in the question that I recognized and distrusted, because it was my own hunger looking back at me from a younger face.

"No," I said. "We've got tin to deliver."

We delivered the tin. I want that on the record. Whatever else happened, whatever the cave cost me, on that first day I did the responsible thing: I ran my cargo to Saltmarsh Quay, I tied up, I settled accounts, and I told myself the mouth in the north bank was probably a limestone karst, probably mapped on some survey I'd never seen, probably nothing.

I told myself that for two days. I'm a good liar. It's a professional skill. But I've never once been able to lie to the river, and on the third morning, standing on the quay with the tide tables open on my knee, I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

The low that had exposed the cave hadn't been an ordinary low. It had been the leading edge of a spring tide series—the moon and the sun and the river all pulling the same direction, the kind of water you get maybe twice a year. And the tables said the next one, the true bottom of the series, the lowest water in nine years, would fall on the morning after tomorrow.

Which meant the mouth would open again. Briefly. Fully. And then the river would close its book for another season, and whatever was inside that hillside would go back to being nobody's business.

I stood there a long time, doing the other arithmetic too—the kind without numbers. The arithmetic of a person who has spent twenty years making sure nothing in the world could surprise them, standing in front of a surprise the size of a hillside.

Tam's voice, in memory, dry as old rope: *Curiosity is just greed with a library card, Casper. Know the difference before it spends you.*

But Tam had also taught me to read water. And the water was telling me something was in there.

* * *

I told Dovie I wanted to run light upriver on a "survey errand," and Dovie looked at me the way Dovie looks at a gauge reading that doesn't match the others—not accusing, just filing it away in that meticulous mental engine room where everything gets logged and nothing gets forgotten.

"Survey errand," Dovie repeated.

"Checking a new shoal below the lime kilns."

"Ostergaard says the water's the lowest in nine years."

"Ostergaard says a lot of things."

"Mm," said Dovie, and went below, and I was left with the distinct feeling of having been logged.

Rill I didn't tell at all. I just said I needed a deckhand for a morning run and watched them try not to look pleased about it and fail completely. Marisol was still upriver at her sister's; I hadn't called her, and the fact that I noticed I hadn't called her—that some part of me was already deciding who needed to know and who didn't—should have told me something. It did tell me something. I just wasn't ready to read it.

We went up on the morning of the spring low, in fog, because the river insisted on atmosphere the way a stage manager insists on dry ice. The *Pelican's Regret* ran quiet, half-power, Dovie's engine tuned to a purr you could barely feel through the soles of your boots. The lime kilns came and went as gray shapes. And then the north bank, and the dead alders, and the long smooth sheet of falling water, and—

The mouth was open. Wider than before. The low tide had peeled the river back another yard, and the arch stood over its own reflection like a door propped open by something patient, and the water running out of it was so clear I could see the pale ribs of the channel bottom, and the cold clean smell coming off it was not the smell of the Kestrel. It was the smell of deep stone and old salt and, underneath that, faintly, unmistakably, the smell of tar and old oak—the smell of a wooden boat that has been somewhere dark for a very long time.

"Captain," Rill said quietly. They weren't hungry anymore. They were scared, and their fear made my own fear legitimate, which is one of the secret services fear performs for us. "That's a cave. That's an actual cave. In the *river*."

"Keep her steady," I said, and brought the *Pelican's Regret* in toward the mouth, slow as a held breath, and the fog closed behind us like a curtain, and I remember thinking, with the last part of my mind still doing its responsible arithmetic: *this is the moment. Whatever you were before this morning, you're someone else after it.*

The arch passed over us. The sound of the river changed—the broad hiss of the open reach fell away, and what replaced it was a dripping, echoing quiet, the sound of a place that had not been visited in a very long time and knew it.

Inside, the cave opened into a low chamber lit gray-green from the mouth behind us. And there, sitting in the shallows with the patience of the drowned, was a shape I knew the way you know a face in an old photograph.

Timbers. Black with age, furred with silt, but timbers—frames and planking, a hull's bones, maybe sixty feet of them, lying on its side in the shallows like something that had lain down to rest and been forgotten by everyone except the water.

Rill made a sound. I didn't. I was reading the stern, where the planking rose out of the mud in a curve, and where something small and brass caught the gray light and held it.

I got the boat alongside. I stepped across. The mud took my boot to the ankle and let go reluctantly, the way the river always does—*mine, mine, mine*—and I knelt, and I wiped the brass clean with my thumb, and I read the name.

*M E R I D I A N.*

And below the name, bolted to what had been the transom, green with a century of growth but still shut, still stubborn, still waiting, a strongbox.

With two initials stamped into the lid, worn shallow but legible, legible as a voice, legible as a hand on my shoulder twenty years gone:

*T.B.*

Tam Brightwater. Who swore to me, once, over cheap whiskey in a cabin not so different from mine, that he'd never in his life sailed salt water.

The cave dripped. The tide clock in my chest started running.

I had until the water came back.
