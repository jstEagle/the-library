## 4. The Consortium Arrives

The trouble with a small river town is that it has exactly two speeds: asleep and talking. By the time I'd tied off at Pell's Landing that morning, the town had already chosen the second.

I knew before my boots hit the dock. You learn to read a town the way you read water—the way the loiterers cluster, the way conversation stops half a beat too long when you pass, the way old Mrs. Halloway, who hasn't bought bait since the Clinton administration, suddenly needs to stand at the end of the pier watching me tie a cleat hitch like it's performance art.

"Captain," she said, when I couldn't pretend not to see her anymore.

"Mrs. Halloway."

"There's a woman at the Saltgrass asking after tides." She said it the way other people say *there's a body in the slough*. "Unusual tides. Spring tides. She's got papers."

"Everyone's got papers," I said. "Half of them are receipts."

"She's offering money for stories."

"Then you'll be rich by Friday."

She sniffed, which from Mrs. Halloway is a full paragraph, and let me pass. But the damage was done. The word *tides* was out there now, attached to money, and in a town like this, information doesn't drift—it runs downhill fast and pools around whoever's willing to pay.

I did the math on the walk up from the dock. The cave had been open four days. In four days, someone had gone from *nobody knows that stretch of coast exists* to *a woman with permits is buying drinks and asking about spring lows*. Which meant either the consortium had better charts than I'd assumed—which was possible—or somebody local had talked. Fishermen talk. Crabbers talk. The tide itself talks, if you know its language, and a low enough spring tide shouts.

Either way, my quiet little secret had grown legs, and one of those legs was walking toward my boat.

* * *

Vesper Kline had been in Pell's Landing for eleven hours, and she already hated it, which was fine, because she'd hated every stop between here and the coast road, and hating places was a kind of fuel.

She sat in the Saltgrass with her back to the wall—old habit, good habit—and let the room come to her. It always came. People wanted to be asked about themselves, and Vesper Kline had built a career on being the person who asked, then being the person who quietly wrote down what they said, then being the person whose employer bought the thing they'd accidentally described.

The barman was named something forgettable. He'd told her about his knee, his ex-wife, and the best crab pots on the river, in that order, and somewhere in the middle of it he'd mentioned—casually, the way people mention things they don't know are gold—that the spring tide last week had been the lowest anyone could remember. Lowest in years. Low enough that old-timers were saying the river was showing bones nobody living had seen.

Bones. She liked that word. She wrote it down.

Her phone buzzed against the table. Director Ostrander, or rather Ostrander's office, or rather the machine that Ostrander fed and that fed on wrecks:

**Status.**

Two words. No question mark. She typed back: *Town confirms anomalous spring low. Local knowledge fragmented but present. One vessel operates this stretch regularly—cargo run, single operator, no registered home port. Digging.*

She looked at the reply for a moment before sending. Then she deleted *digging* and wrote *proceeding*, because Ostrander didn't pay for digging, Ostrander paid for results, and the distinction mattered to her in a way she'd stopped examining years ago.

Here was what Vesper Kline knew, and what she was very good at not showing: she was not a treasure hunter. Treasure hunters were romantics, and romantics died broke. She was an accountant with a boat. The Meridian was, on paper, a thirty-year-old insurance loss—a survey expedition that went into weather off the Kestrel delta and never came out, seven souls, hull insured twice over by men who later went to prison for unrelated fraud. Unrelated. That word had always bothered her. Fraud men insure their ships twice and lose them once, and everyone shrugs and calls it coincidence, and Vesper Kline had never once in her life shrugged at anything.

If the Meridian was findable, it was worth finding. If it was worth finding, it was worth finding *first*. And if some anonymous river rat had already found it—

Well. Then the river rat had a problem, and the problem was her.

The barman drifted back. "Refill?"

"Sure. And actually—" she smiled, and it was a good smile, calibrated, the kind that made people feel selected rather than targeted—"you mentioned the low tide. I'm doing coastal survey work. Hydrography. Boring stuff, permits, government-adjacent. I'm interested in anything unusual the river's been showing lately. Caves, shoals, structure. Happy to compensate for time."

The word *compensate* worked on him like a rudder. She watched him steer toward it.

"Well," he said, "there's talk."

"There usually is."

"Talk about a sea cave. Down past the Serrano flats, where the cliffs come down to the water. Nobody's ever seen it open. Old Man Pell used to swear his father saw it once, but Old Man Pell swore a lot of things." He leaned in, pleased with himself. "And talk about who's been running that stretch more than usual lately."

"Who has?"

"The cargo captain." He said it carefully, the way people say things they've been told not to say, which is to say he said it immediately. "Keeps to themselves. Good boat. Been running the lower river twenty years, give or take. Doesn't drink at the Saltgrass, doesn't gossip at the chandlery, doesn't have a house anybody knows of. Lives aboard." He shrugged. "River keeps 'em, more or less."

*No fixed address. No social footprint. Twenty years of perfect invisibility.* In Vesper's experience, people were invisible for exactly two reasons: they had nothing to hide, which was rare, or they had gotten very, very good at hiding, which was common and always interesting.

"What's the captain's name?" she asked.

"Casper Brightwater."

"And where would I find Captain Brightwater?"

The barman grinned. "On the river. Or the dock, mornings. But—" and here he lowered his voice as though handing over state secrets instead of directions—"fair warning. They don't talk much."

"Neither do I," Vesper said, and meant it as a promise.

* * *

She came to the dock at seven the next morning, which told me she'd done her homework, because seven was when I took coffee on the aft deck and pretended the day hadn't started yet. Anyone who shows up during your pretending hour wants something, and wants you agreeable when they ask for it.

She was maybe forty, dressed like someone who'd read about boats in a brochure—clean deck shoes, jacket too new to have ever been rained on—and carrying a leather folder the way lawyers carry leather folders, which is to say like a weapon with a handle. She stopped at a respectful distance from the gangway and smiled.

"Captain Brightwater? Vesper Kline." She didn't offer a hand across the water, which I appreciated. First smart thing she'd done. "I'm a scout with the Corvane Salvage Group. Do you have five minutes?"

"I've got coffee," I said. "Whether that converts to minutes depends on the pitch."

"Won't take long." She held up the folder. "Corvane holds recovery rights on several historical losses in this region. We're re-surveying the delta approaches. Your stretch of river is underrepresented in the charts—I'm told you know it better than anyone."

"Told by whom?"

"Everyone, apparently. You're famous for not being famous."

"That's the only kind of famous worth having."

She laughed, correctly, at the right volume, and I gave her credit for the mechanics even while every instinct I owned stood up and walked out of the room. I'd spent twenty years learning people the way I learned sandbars—one season at a time, by what they revealed at low water—and this one was all ballast and no cargo. Polished. Weighted. Built to sit steady in any weather and tell you nothing about what was below the waterline.

"Come aboard, then," I heard myself say, because refusing would have been louder than welcoming. "Mind the coaming. She bites."

She stepped over it neatly, which annoyed me. I'd hoped she'd trip.

I poured her coffee in the cabin, in the same mug Tam had drunk from for fifteen years, and watched her eyes do the room. People think captains' cabins are private. They're not—they're résumés. Everything in mine said *working boat, working person, nothing to see*. The strongbox wasn't aboard; it was buried under three feet of mud in a slough I trusted more than any bank. The letters were in oilcloth inside the engine room's spare-parts locker, because Dovie was the only soul alive who organized that locker, and no one on earth voluntarily opened it.

Kline sat, crossed her ankles, and opened the folder. Permits, licenses, a map of the lower river with survey grids penciled over it like a chess problem.

"Spring tides," she said. "You'll have noticed last week's low. Exceptional. These occur in cycles—we're entering another window in roughly nine days. Corvane is interested in what extreme lows expose along this coastline. Sea caves, submerged structures, that sort of thing. We charter local expertise. Well paid." She slid a figure across the table on a card, and it was a genuinely insulting amount of money, which is to say it was designed to make refusal feel like insanity.

I looked at the card for a long moment. Then I looked at her.

"Ma'am," I said, and put a slow, friendly dumbness into my voice like ballast, "I haul diesel drums and crab pot wire. Last week's tide was low, sure. River does that. What exactly do you figure gets exposed out there besides mud and old crab traps?"

"Structure."

"Cliffs are structure."

"Caves."

"Hell, there's caves all through that coast. Water's been chewing on it since before either of us was born. Half of them flood at slack, half of them are full of seals, and all of them smell like it." I scratched my jaw, letting the silence do work. "You want a guide to look at rocks, I can show you rocks. But I'll tell you free what the salvage outfits never want to hear: this river doesn't keep treasure. It keeps mud. Everything else it eats."

Something flickered behind her eyes—not doubt exactly. Recalculation. She was adjusting my weight in her ledger, deciding whether I was a fool or an asset pretending to be a fool, and I could see she hadn't decided, and I could see that not deciding bothered her.

"You're very certain," she said.

"I'm very old," I said. "It comes to the same thing."

She smiled again, closed the folder, and stood. At the gangway she paused, one hand on the rail, and delivered the parting shot I'd known was coming since she stepped aboard.

"For what it's worth, Captain—rumor says someone's been running the Serrano stretch at odd hours. Off schedule. A boat matching this one's description." She let that hang in the salt air between us. "Rivers are small towns. Talk travels faster than barges. If you hear anything unusual—caves, timbers, wreckage—you know how to reach Corvane. The number's on the card. The second number is for emergencies."

"Define emergency."

"Discovery," she said pleasantly, and went down the gangway without looking back, which was the third smart thing she'd done, and I was starting to keep score.

* * *

From the dock, Vesper allowed herself one long exhale.

Interesting. Genuinely interesting, which was rarer than the Meridian itself. Brightwater was either the best liar she'd met in a decade or the most contentedly ignorant captain on the coast, and the difference mattered enormously. Liars had tells, but the good ones only had absences—no home port, no history, no curiosity about a pile of money sitting on the table. Innocent people got curious. Innocent people asked questions. Brightwater had asked exactly one, and it had been *define emergency*, which was either a joke or a probe, and Vesper had built her whole career on never guessing wrong about which.

She pulled out her phone and typed: *Local operator uncooperative or uninformed. Possibly both. Running background. Town gossip places them near Serrano flats at irregular hours. Recommend accelerating survey barge arrival. Window in nine days.*

Send.

Then she stood a moment longer, looking at the boat—the *Kestrel Lark*, according to the transom, paint faded honest, lines kept true—and felt the particular itch she'd learned to trust above charts and sonar alike. Twenty years of anonymity on a stretch of river that had just produced an uncharted sea cave at a record low tide. Coincidence was a word accountants used for numbers they hadn't finished checking.

"Talk travels faster than barges," she murmured to herself, and went to buy a tide table, a chart of the Serrano flats, and dinner for the barman. Information was like crabs: you trapped it with bait, and you checked the trap every few hours, because things escaped, and sometimes things climbed in that you hadn't planned for.

By nightfall she had the tide tables spread across her bunk and a name from a marina clerk in her notes: *Marisol Vega, chandlery, friend of the captain.* Everyone had a door. Brightwater's just happened to be made of people.

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night, which was fine, because neither did the river.

I sat on the aft deck with the engine ticking itself cool and turned the day over the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath. Nine days. She'd said nine days, casual as a weather report, and my stomach had dropped through the hull, because nine days was the spring window, and she knew it, and she didn't know yet that I knew she knew it. That was the only card I held—that she thought I was holding none.

Twenty years I'd kept myself invisible, and it had taken one woman with a folder and a smile four hours to put a finger on the exact seam of it. *A boat matching this one's description.* The town was talking. Mrs. Halloway, the barman, the marina clerk—good people, ordinary people, people who'd sell my schedule for the price of a story and never once feel they'd betrayed me, because they didn't know they were selling anything. That was the thing about secrets: you didn't get to keep them alone. You just got to be the last one to find out they'd leaked.

Tam would have laughed at me. *You hid so good, kid, you forgot hiding's just lying with better posture.* He'd said that once, drunk on cheap brandy, staring at the river like it owed him money. I'd thought he was talking about himself. I'd been right, and I hadn't known it.

Below my feet, in the oilcloth, in Dovie's spare-parts locker, the letters sat in the dark. Ada. The photograph. The name Tam had buried deeper than any wreck.

Nine days. A barge coming. A woman with two phone numbers and a ledger where her conscience should be. And somewhere downstream, a cave mouth waiting at the bottom of the tide, keeping a dead man's testimony shut behind forty feet of cold green water.

I finished my coffee and went below to wake the chart table. If the consortium was going to arrive whether I liked it or not, then fine. Let them arrive. But they'd arrive on my river, on my schedule, following my lead line—and I intended to be the one who decided what the Meridian sang, and when, and to whom.

First, though, I needed a crew. And crewing up meant lying to the only people I'd never lied to.

That was tomorrow's problem. Tonight I just drew the route, and tried not to notice that my hand, tracing the channel past the Serrano flats, was as steady as Tam's had ever been.
