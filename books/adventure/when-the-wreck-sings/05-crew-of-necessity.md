## 5. Crew of Necessity

There are three ways to ask a person to lie for you. You can tell them the truth and trust them with it. You can tell them nothing and hope they don't notice the shape of the hole. Or you can build them a smaller, kinder lie to stand inside, like a boathouse in a storm, and let them call it home.

I'd spent twenty years on the Kestrel learning which lies float and which ones take on water. The one I was about to hand my crew was rated for calm weather only.

* * *

Dovie came first, because Dovie always came first. I found her in her shop behind the chandlery, elbow-deep in somebody's outboard, a cigarette burning down unattended in an ashtray shaped like a fish that had clearly been through some things.

"I need an engine man," I said. "Two weeks, maybe three. Survey work."

She didn't look up. "Whose survey?"

"Mine."

"Contracted by?"

"Me."

That got her attention. She straightened, wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being gray sometime last decade, and looked at me the way she looks at engines—like she could hear something wrong before she could name it. Dovie is sixty if she's a day, built like a capstan, and has forgotten more about diesel engines than most marine mechanics ever learn. She also has never once taken a job without knowing why, which is why I'd rehearsed this.

"Salvage survey," I said. "Wreck assessment. Insurance people want it documented."

"Insurance people." She said it flat, tasting it. "You've never done insurance work in your life. You told me once it was 'accounting with a boat attached.'"

"People change."

"People change their oil," she said. "What's the wreck?"

"A private matter."

"Casper Joubert." She said my whole name like a sentence being handed down. "You have no past, no address on paper, and a boat registered to a company that exists on exactly one document I have personally witnessed you burn a copy of. And now you want me to run engines on a secret wreck for two weeks of good money and no questions." She took a drag off the dead cigarette, found it dead, and lit another off its corpse. "One question. Just one, and then I'll say yes or no and we'll both pretend it was easy."

I braced. "Ask."

"Is anyone going to get hurt?"

The honest answer—the one I gave—was "Not by me." It wasn't quite the same as *no*, and we both heard the difference, and she nodded anyway, because that's the deal Dovie and I have always had: I don't insult her with polish, and she doesn't demand what I can't pay.

"Survey job," she said, turning back to the outboard. "Fine. But I'm bringing my own tools, and if I find out this is treasure hunting, I'm renegotiating. Treasure hunting attracts lawyers, and lawyers attract paperwork, and paperwork is how they got Al Capone."

"It's not treasure hunting."

"That's exactly what a treasure hunter would say." She waved me out with the wrench. "Be at the dock at six. And Casper—" I paused in the doorway. "Whatever hole you're digging, dig it deep. Shallow holes are how people fall in."

* * *

Rill was easier, which should have worried me more than it did.

Rill Okafor is twenty-four, all elbows and appetite, with a laugh that carries across three sloughs and a talent for finding work wherever money briefly exists. They'd decked for me on and off for two years—loading runs, net hauls, one memorable week dredging a sunken barge of fertilizer that made us both swear off salvage forever. Rill asks questions the way rivers ask questions of banks: constantly, cheerfully, and with eventual erosion.

I caught them on the dock at Grayling Bend, sitting on a mooring cleat eating a meat pie with the structural ambition of a collapsed bridge.

"Got work," I said. "Two weeks. Deckhand rates plus hazard."

They swallowed. "Hazard? What kind of hazard?"

"Tide work. Cave access."

"Cave." Their eyes went bright as new pennies. "What cave?"

"The kind you sign a paper about."

"You don't make people sign papers. You told me papers were 'a way of admitting you expect to be disappointed.'" They hopped off the cleat, pie in hand, walking backward ahead of me so they could watch my face while they talked, which is either rudeness or strategy depending on who's doing it. "Casper. A cave. On the river? What's in the cave?"

"Water, mostly. At high tide."

"And at low tide?"

"Less water."

They groaned like I'd personally wronged their ancestors. "You're doing the thing. The thing where you answer questions with weather reports. Marisol says you learned it from Old Tam."

The name landed harder than they knew. I kept my face where I wanted it—mild, amused, a captain mildly amused—and said, "Marisol talks too much."

"She does. That's why we keep her." Rill finished the pie in two bites and wiped their hands on their jeans. "Look, I'm in. Hazard pay, cave, mystery—I'm not made of reasons to say no. But I want to know one thing, and I want the real answer, not the tide table."

Here it was again. The river town's oldest game: everyone wants one true thing from you, and every true thing costs.

"Ask," I said, because apparently that was the word of the week.

"Why me?" They weren't joking now. Under all the elbows, Rill has a face that goes suddenly, disarmingly young when it's serious. "You've got half the dock who'd deck for you free. Why hire the one person on this river who can't keep a secret longer than a tide cycle? Even I know that about me."

It was a better question than they realized, and the honest answer—that I needed someone whose curiosity I could watch, someone too busy asking questions to go looking for answers elsewhere—was not an answer I could give. So I gave them a different true thing.

"Because you're the best deckhand I've had since Tam," I said. "And because whoever else I hired would talk, and you'll talk too, but you'll talk to me first. That's worth more than silence."

They stood there a second, chewing on it along with the last of the crust. Then they grinned, and the seriousness folded away like a chart.

"Hazard pay in advance," they said. "Half."

"Quarter."

"Third, or I start telling people there's a cave."

"There's always caves, Rill."

"Not ones *you're* hiding." They stuck out a hand, grease and pastry flakes and all, and I shook it, and that was that. Easy. Too easy. I walked back up the dock aware of a small cold feeling I couldn't name yet, the way you feel a current under a flat stretch of water—nothing visible, just your hull telling you something your eyes haven't caught up to.

* * *

Marisol was hardest, because Marisol doesn't take lies; she takes truths and holds them for you, which is worse, because then the lie is yours alone, sitting in her house like a guest who won't leave.

Her place sits above the bait shop on stilts older than the town charter, and she was on the porch shelling peas when I came up the stairs, which meant she already knew I was coming, because Marisol always knows, possibly through means she refuses to disclose.

"Survey job," I said, before she could greet me. "Two weeks. Cook's wages, cook's authority, and I need you aboard by Friday."

She kept shelling. Peas clicked into the bowl like small green coins. "Whose survey?"

"Mine."

"Mm." Click, click. "And the wreck?"

"Private."

"Casper." She set down the bowl and looked at me, and I want to be clear that Marisol Vega looking at you is a physical event, like weather. "I've known you nineteen years. You sat at my table the week Tam died and ate nothing and thanked me for it. You named your boat after his saying. You have never once in nineteen years asked me to work a job without telling me what it was. So either you've changed, or something's changed you, and I know which one I'd bet on."

The river breeze came through the porch boards and did nothing useful. Down below, somebody's dog was losing an argument with a gull.

"I can't tell you yet," I said. "Not because I don't trust you. Because I don't yet know what it is I'd be telling. There's a wreck. It matters. It matters in ways I'm still—"

"Still measuring," she finished quietly.

Tam used to say that. *Everything on a river gets measured—depth, distance, time. Trouble starts when you measure yourself.* He'd said it to me a hundred times, usually while doing something reckless, and Marisol had heard it enough through me that she owned it now too.

"Yes," I said.

She studied me a long moment. Then she picked the bowl back up and started shelling again, which from Marisol is a signature on a contract.

"Friday," she said. "But hear me, Brightwater. I'll hold your secret. That's what I do; it's the family business, near enough. But I won't hold it against the crew. If Dovie or Rill are standing in danger because you didn't tell them what you told me you couldn't tell me—" click, click, "—then the secret stops being yours to keep. Understand?"

"Understood."

"And eat something. You look like a heron."

* * *

We provisioned over two days, and I'll say this for lying to people you love: it makes you generous. I bought the good coffee. I bought the rope I'd been coveting for a year and pretending was a frivolity. I bought Rill's third of the hazard pay in advance, in cash, and watched them pocket it with a flicker of something that wasn't quite joy—a hesitation, half a heartbeat, gone before I could decide whether I'd seen it.

Thursday night the four of us sat in the cabin of the *Miles to Go*—named for Tam's other saying, the one he actually lived by, right up until he stopped living—and ate Marisol's stew and made plans out loud that were mostly true. Survey grid here. Anchor points there. Tide windows marked in pencil. Dovie talked engine hours and fuel margins. Rill talked about caves with the naked delight of a person who has never met a real one. Marisol fed everyone and watched me over the steam.

It was warm. That's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing that sat in my chest all night like ballast: it was genuinely warm. Lamplight, stew, the river slapping the hull, these three people laughing at each other's jokes in the small room that was the closest thing I had to a home. Chosen family, Tam called such things. *Blood's an accident,* he said. *Crew's a decision.*

And every laugh in that cabin was resting on top of a lie I'd built for them, load-bearing, rated for calm weather, and the storm I could feel coming upriver had no respect for ratings at all.

Near midnight, Rill lingered while the others turned in. They stood in the companionway with their hands in their pockets, working up to something, and I pretended to study charts so they'd have the room to do it.

"Casper," they said finally. "The cave. Is it the Meridian?"

Every clock in my body stopped.

"Because Theo Anand's been asking around," Rill went on, fast, the way they talk when they're scared of their own sentence. "The journalist. Asking about unusual tides, lost wrecks, whether anybody's seen anything odd at low water. And everybody knows you've been out at Grayling Bend at slack tide twice this month, which nobody does unless they're measuring something. So." They shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "Is it?"

I closed the chart book. I looked at this kid I'd hired precisely because their curiosity ran hot and open, and I weighed the truth against the tide tables, and the tide tables won—for now.

"If it were," I said, "what would you do?"

Rill blinked. Considered. And answered honestly, which is the cruelest thing about them: "I don't know. Probably tell somebody. Probably you first." A pause. "Unless telling you first was the same as telling everybody."

They said goodnight and went aft, footsteps light on the deck, and I sat alone with the lamp and the charts and the strongbox locked under my bunk, and I understood, with the particular clarity that comes at the end of long days, that I hadn't hired a crew.

I'd hired witnesses.

Outside, far downriver, thunder muttered—not rain yet, just the sky clearing its throat—and the tide turned beneath the hull, patient as everything else that was coming for us.
