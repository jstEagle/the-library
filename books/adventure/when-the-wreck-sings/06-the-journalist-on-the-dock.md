## 6. The Journalist on the Dock

The trouble with dockside cafés is that they have exactly one exit, and the exit is a dock.

I'd known this for years, of course. I'd chosen the Blue Heron's corner table precisely because it gave me sightlines to the water and the parking lot both—a habit I picked up from Tam, who used to say you should always know where the doors are, not because you're expecting trouble but because trouble doesn't send invitations. He'd say it over his coffee, that chipped enamel mug he refused to replace, and he'd smile like the advice was a joke when we both knew it wasn't.

So when the man in the linen jacket stood up from the counter and started working his way toward my table with the unhurried confidence of someone who has already decided the conversation is happening, I had time to do the math. One exit. It was behind him. The water was to my left, and while I can swim, my phone cannot.

"Marisol Vega?" he said, arriving at my table without being invited, which is a thing people only do when they think they're doing you a favor.

"That depends on who's asking."

"Theo Anand." He set a card on the table between us, face up, like a dealer showing his hand. Freelance journalist, it said, in tasteful serif font. Below that, an email address and nothing else—no outlet, no title, which told me he was either very good or very broke, and the linen jacket said very broke. "I write about lost ships."

"Then you've come to the wrong river. We mostly lose crab pots."

He smiled like I'd confirmed something rather than deflected. That's the thing about journalists—they treat every denial as a receipt. "May I?" He gestured at the chair across from me, and before I could say no, he was sitting in it, which is also a thing people only do when they think they're doing you a favor.

I looked at him properly then. Forty-ish, maybe younger worn hard by airports. Brown skin going gray at the temples, wire-rimmed glasses with one scratched lens, a notebook already out of his pocket though he hadn't opened it. His hands were still. That unsettled me more than anything else. Men who corner women in cafés usually fidget. This one waited, patient as tide.

"I'm not really the person you want," I said. "I paint boats. I don't sail them."

"But you crew for Casper Brightwater."

"Casper hires me for paint jobs. Seasonal."

"Mm." He turned the card ninety degrees with one finger, aligning it with the table edge. A tidiness habit. I know what tidiness habits mean; I live with a woman who coils rope so the ends all face the same direction. "Three weeks ago, the tide at the river mouth dropped nearly two meters below prediction. Hydrographic offices are calling it instrument error. Fishermen are calling it a bad moon. And one person filed a float plan that morning for a run downriver that nobody can account for—no cargo manifest, no destination logged, no return time. Just a name and a boat."

"And you think that's Casper."

"I think that's *someone*," he said mildly. "Names on float plans are like names on hotel registers. They tell you who signed, not who slept there."

I picked up my coffee. It had gone cold, but I needed something to do with my hands that wasn't wringing his notebook into the harbor. "Mr. Anand. Theo. Whatever I'm supposed to call you. River towns run on two economies: fish and gossip. You're not buying fish. So let's talk about what you're actually selling."

"Nothing. I'm asking."

"You're asking the way a crane asks a barge." I set the cup down. "Look. I'll save us both some time. Casper Brightwater runs cargo, patches hulls, and pays their bills on time, which around here makes them practically a folk hero. If they took a joyride three weeks ago, it was probably to outrun a bill collector or chase a sunset. There's no story there unless you invent one, and if you invent one, I'll spend my evenings telling everyone in this town you cried at a sea shanty."

Something flickered across his face—not offense. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a lock click open when he expected resistance. "You defend them well."

"I defend my friends well. It's not a skill, it's a reflex."

"And Tam?" he said. "Did you defend Tam too?"

The name went through me like cold water through a wetsuit.

I want to be clear: I'm not easily rattled. I grew up hauling nets before dawn and painting hulls in August heat; I have been yelled at by fishermen, cheated by brokers, and once spent six hours adrift in fog listening to my father explain everything I'd done wrong since birth. My resting heart rate is a rumor. But that name, out of a stranger's mouth, in this town, at this table—

Because here's what Marisol Vega knows that Theo Anand didn't yet know he knew: Tam used to tell stories. Not often, and never sober, and never twice the same way, but he told them. Stories about a ship called the Meridian that sailed into ice and never sailed out. Stories about a navigator who read stars like other men read street signs. Stories he'd cut off mid-sentence whenever Casper walked into the room, like a man snuffing a candle before it caught the curtains.

And I had always assumed those stories were just that—stories. Old man's embellishments, drunkard's epics, the kind of tale that grows a new mast every retelling. I'd laughed at them. I'd asked for them. Once, memorably, I'd asked whether the Meridian was real and Tam had looked at me with an expression I'd spent years filing under *drunk* and now, sitting across from a stranger with a tidy card and still hands, I refiled under *afraid.*

"Tam was our friend," I said. "He died. Leave him be."

"He died under a name that isn't in any record I can find," Theo said quietly. "No birth certificate. No service history. No property. It's as if Thomas Ambrose Kestrel—" and I flinched, because even the surname was wrong, Tam had made it up or stolen it, and hearing it spoken aloud by a stranger felt like watching someone wear your mother's coat— "appeared on this river fully grown, twenty years ago, with no past at all. People like that interest me. Either they're running from something, or they're running *toward* something they couldn't reach any other way. Both make good stories."

"Or they just don't like paperwork."

"Sure." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and I realized with a sinking feeling that he was enjoying himself. Not cruelly—he didn't strike me as a cruel man—but the way a man enjoys a chess game where the other player has finally stopped moving pieces at random. "Or they don't like paperwork. Can I buy you another coffee? Yours died about four minutes ago."

"No."

"Fair." He closed the notebook without opening it, which somehow frightened me more than if he'd filled it. "One last question, and then I'll get out of your hair. The Meridian. Have you heard the name?"

And there it was. The trap I'd walked into by walking into the trap. Because if I said no, I was lying, and he'd hear it—I could feel my own pulse in my ears—and liars make bad witnesses. And if I said yes, I was confirming that the legend had roots in this town, in this crew, in this family, and he'd dig until he hit bedrock.

I did what Tam would have done. What Casper would have done. What I hate myself a little for doing.

"Every wreck has a name," I said. "Half of them are Meridians. Sailors aren't original."

He studied me for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow, like a man marking a chart. "Thank you for your time, Ms. Vega. You've been very helpful."

"I've told you nothing."

"Exactly." He stood, tucked the card back in his pocket—no, wait. He left the card. He slid it across the table toward me with two fingers, deliberate as a man laying a bet. "In case you remember something. Or in case you decide you'd rather hear the whole story from me than watch it arrive secondhand. Those are usually the only two options, in my experience. Things either come to you first or they come to you last, and last is worse."

He put some bills under his saucer, enough for both coffees including the one I'd refused, and walked out the single exit onto the single dock, and I watched him go the whole way, and he knew I was watching, and he didn't hurry.

* * *

I sat there for twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. Long enough that the waitress refilled my dead coffee without being asked and shot me a look that said *trouble?* and I shook my head and she let it go, because in river towns you learn which questions to skip.

Here's the problem with loyalty: nobody warns you it has a curiosity clause buried in the fine print.

I love Casper. That's not sentiment, that's structural fact, load-bearing, like a keel. Twenty years ago I was a nineteen-year-old with a paint gun and a chip on my shoulder, and Casper was a deckhand with quiet hands and a way of looking at the river like it owed them money, and somewhere between a hundred jobs and a thousand shared meals they became the closest thing I have to family, and family on the water means you don't ask questions you're not prepared to help carry the answers to.

But Tam. God, Tam.

I keep circling back to the stories. The way he told them—the *specificity*. Drunk men generalize. Drunk men say *the storm was huge, the waves were mountains.* Tam said the wind came out of the northwest at forty knots and backed two points overnight, and the ice was the kind they call growlers because they growl in the swells, and the ship iced up so heavy her radar mast came down on the second night. Nobody invents details like that. You invent details like that from the inside.

And the woman. There was a woman in some of the stories, mentioned sideways, the way you mention weather. *She'd have known what to do,* he said once, staring into his mug. *She always knew what to do.* I'd assumed a wife. A ghost. The usual wreckage.

Now I wondered if she was a sister. A daughter. Someone whose name was written down somewhere, attached to a passenger list, attached to a ship that sailed into ice and didn't come out, attached to a navigator who read stars like street signs and survived when the ship didn't.

Which meant Tam hadn't just lied about his name. He'd lied about a *wreck*. He'd sat in our galley eating our food and telling our children's-children stories about a disaster he might have stood inside of, and watched us laugh, and told us more.

Unless he hadn't. Unless the stories were borrowed, secondhand, sailor's folklore passed along like a cold. Unless the Meridian was a hundred ships and none of them.

That was the fork in me, right there at the table with the card lying face-up like a tarot spread. Loyalty says: burn the card, warn Casper, batten down. Curiosity says: the man left the card *on purpose*, and men who leave cards on purpose want to be called, and men who want to be called have information, and information is the one cargo I've never been able to refuse.

I picked up the card. Good stock. Heavy. Expensive paper for a broke man's jacket, which meant somebody was paying him, which meant the story had a buyer, which meant it would run with or without us. That was the part that settled it, honestly. Not curiosity. Arithmetic. A story with funding behind it doesn't knock politely forever. Better to know what it says before it prints than after.

I texted Casper instead. *Coffee later? Nothing urgent.*

Two lies in nine characters. I hated how easy it was.

* * *

Casper met me at the boat at dusk, and I almost told them everything right there, standing on the deck with the gulls yelling at each other overhead. Almost. But Casper came up the gangway with that particular set to their shoulders I've learned to read over two decades—the one that says *the river's been talking and I don't like what it said*—and something in me folded. Not cowardice. Timing. You don't add weight to a boat that's already riding low.

We drank tea in the galley. Casper talked about engine hours and a fuel filter and the price of antifouling paint, normal things, safe things, and I answered in kind, and the whole conversation was two people carrying water buckets past a fire neither would acknowledge.

"So," Casper said eventually, casual as a knife going back in a drawer. "Anything happen today?"

And I looked at my oldest friend, and I thought about a card in my pocket, and a name in my memory, and a navigator who read stars like street signs, and I said:

"Not really. Some guy tried to sell me timeshare in Belize."

Casper laughed. It was a good laugh. It always is. That's the worst part.

Later, alone in my bunk aboard, I took out the card and turned it under the little reading lamp. Theo Anand. The email address. I thought about the woman in Tam's stories who always knew what to do, and I thought about Ada—because yes, fine, I'll say it, the name surfaced in my head the moment the journalist said *Meridian*, surfacing like something drowned rising when the tide goes out, and I don't know why I know that name, I don't know where Tam said it or when, but I know it the way you know a word in a language you thought you'd forgotten.

Ada.

I typed the email address into my phone. Deleted it. Typed it again. Saved it as a draft with no message in the body, subject line blank, a letter addressed to nobody, a door propped open with nothing but my own restlessness.

Outside, the river moved in the dark, keeping its own counsel, the way it always has, the way I was starting to suspect everyone I loved had learned to do from watching it.

Tomorrow, I'd decide. Tonight, I lay in the bunk and listened to the halyards tick against the mast, and I understood—with the clarity that only comes at two in the morning—that whatever the Meridian was, it had already taken one man from us whole. Tam had gone into the ground still holding his secret like a stone in his fist, and we'd buried the fist and never pried it open, and now strangers were coming up the river with funded notebooks and patient hands, ready to pry it open for us.

The question wasn't whether the truth was coming.

It was whether it would arrive as a guest or a flood.
