## 7. Charting the Impossible

I spread the charts across my cabin table at two in the morning, weighted down with a coffee mug, a brass letter opener Old Tam gave me for a birthday I never told him was mine, and a jar of bolts I use as a paperweight because it was closer than anything else. The cabin lamp swung on its gimbal with the river's breathing, and the light moved over the paper like something alive reading over my shoulder.

Here is what nobody tells you about tides: they are not weather. Weather you can argue with, hedge against, outrun if your engine's honest and your nerve is better than your sense. Tides are arithmetic. The moon does not care what you want. It pulls on schedule, indifferent as a debt collector, and all the wishing in the world will not move a spring tide by a single hour.

I had spent three nights confirming what I already knew in my gut, which is where most of my worst knowledge lives.

The cave mouth sits low in the bluff face—lower than any chart shows, because no chart shows it at all—and the water that covers it even at mean low has to drop another four feet before the black slot of an entrance breathes open. Four feet doesn't sound like much. On a river, four feet is a country. And the only time the Kestrel's lower reach gives up four extra feet is a spring tide, when sun and moon line up and pull together like two horses in double harness.

The almanac said it plain: nine days from now. One window. Maybe ninety minutes of access, maybe less if the bar outside shifted the way bars do after every blow. Miss it, and the next window was twenty-eight days out—a lunar month, the moon taking its sweet time coming back around like a landlord who knows you've got nowhere else to go.

Twenty-eight days was a lifetime. Twenty-eight days was Vesper Kline's survey barge sitting fat and patient off the point, running side-scan sonar until it found what I'd found, and then there would be no choosing anything. There would be permits, lawyers, floodlights, a salvage claim stamped and filed before I'd finished deciding how I felt about it. Consortiums don't hesitate. Hesitation is for people who own things they can lose.

I sat back and looked at the chart, and the chart looked back, and neither of us blinked.

* * *

The problem with being invisible for twenty years is that you get good at it, and then one day invisibility stops being armor and starts being a cage, and you notice the bars only when you try to stretch.

I'd built my whole life on a simple architecture: no fixed address, no past discussed, no name on anything but the boat's registration, and that under a company name so dry it could preserve fruit. *Kestrel Lighterage Co.* Nobody asks questions of a company called Kestrel Lighterage Co. It sounds like paperwork made flesh. It sounds like a man named Gerald.

Tam had taught me that. Or rather, Tam had lived it so thoroughly that I absorbed it the way you absorb an accent—not as a lesson but as air. Don't tell people where you sleep. Don't tell people what you want. Wanting is a handle, and handles are for pulling. For twenty years I'd kept myself so smooth and handle-free that I'd nearly convinced me too. Nearly. There was always the restlessness underneath, the feeling I'd carried since I was young enough to think it was ambition: that I was a man—or not a man, that was part of the restlessness too—living in a life sized for someone smaller, or larger, or simply someone else.

And now the river had gone and exposed a cave full of answers, and the answers had initials on them, and the initials were T.M., and every hour I sat still was an hour the world got closer to prying the box open without me.

I did the math again, because math is a comfort when nothing else is. Nine days to spring tide. The barge needed—I estimated, watching it work off the point through my binoculars that morning—maybe six days to finish its grid pattern over the lower reach before it started intersecting with the cove. Six days versus nine. Three days of margin, except margin isn't margin when the other side has money, helicopters, and a scout who looked at me in the Dockside like she was reading my manifest.

Vesper Kline didn't miss things. That was the whole trouble with her. Most people look at a riverboat captain and see a function. She'd looked at me and seen a variable.

* * *

Marisol found me on deck at dawn, which she does sometimes, bringing two cups of coffee and no conversation, which is why she's lasted thirty years as my closest friend. We stood at the rail and watched the fog burn off the far bank in ragged sheets.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I slept."

"Your eyes say otherwise."

"My eyes are liars," I said. "It's why I keep them."

She sipped her coffee. Marisol has the patience of driftwood—she'll sit in a current for years without complaint, and then one day you find her lodged somewhere permanent and immovable. "The consortium's barge moved again last night," she said. "Anchored off Gull Point. That's below the cove, Casper."

"I know where Gull Point is. I've poached oysters there."

"They're working a grid. Dovie heard it on the radio—their tender's been calling coordinates back to the mothership all week." She turned and looked at me, and I felt it like a searchlight. "You know what they're looking for."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult us both by denying it. "Maybe."

"Casper."

"Marisol." I set down my cup. Here was the moment where I could have told her everything—the cave, the strongbox, the letters sitting in my sea chest with their terrible neat handwriting, the photograph of the woman whose face I'd studied until I dreamed it. Here was the moment, and I want to be honest in this account even when honesty costs: I didn't take it. Not all of it. Some habits are load-bearing, and secrecy was holding up my whole house.

"There's a wreck," I said. "Old one. It matters to me personally, and it matters legally, and if the consortium gets to it first, both of those go up in smoke. That's what I can tell you right now, and I need you to trust that the rest comes later."

Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, maybe, or its cousin disappointment—but Marisol has known me since before I knew how to be known, and she read the shape of what I wasn't saying. "Later," she said. "You promise later?"

"On my boat."

"That's a real oath." She drained her coffee. "Then I'm in. Whatever it is. But Casper—" she tapped the rail twice, her old habit when she wanted a thing to stick, "—whatever you're planning, plan it fast. That barge isn't drifting toward Gull Point by accident."

* * *

I spent the middle of the day doing something I hated, which is pretending to be stupid in public.

Kline came to the dock at noon with two of her people and a tablet full of permits, and she found me re-caulking a seam that didn't need caulking, because a captain with his hands busy looks like a captain with nothing on his mind. She wore the same competent clothes as before, the ones that said *money, but practical*, and she smiled the smile of a person who has learned that smiling opens more doors than badges.

"Captain Brightwater." She crouched to look at my seam work. "That's a lovely hull. Older design?"

"She's older than me and twice as honest."

"Mm." She straightened. "We're expanding our survey south of Gull Point. Unusual tidal behavior down there—some of the old-timers talk about a cove that drains out entirely certain times of year. You'd know the water. Any thoughts?"

Every instinct I had said *nothing*. Give her nothing. But I'd spent the night with the charts, and the night had taught me something uncomfortable: silence was going to lose. Silence meant she found the cave herself with sonar and divers and lawyers, and I got to watch my inheritance—my burden, my truth, whatever it was—hauled up by strangers under floodlights while I stood on the dock being nobody.

There was another way. It was a bad way, a Tam way, and I recognized it the way you recognize your own face in a stranger. If I couldn't hide from the story anymore, then maybe I could steer it. A riverboat pilot who knows the water can lead anyone anywhere—including into a dead slough full of mud and reeds, forty miles from anything worth finding.

"Unusual tides," I said, wiping my hands slow, letting her see me think. "There's a slough past Gull Point floods deep on springs. Deeper than it looks. Old logging channel. If you're hunting anomalies, that's where I'd drag a magnet first."

Her eyes came up sharp and interested, and I watched her decide I was useful. "A slough. Would you be willing to show us? We pay well for local pilots. Very well."

"Well enough to make a man reconsider his schedule," I said, and let greed sit on my face where she could see it, because greedy men are legible men, and legible men don't get read closely.

"Captain." Her handshake was dry and firm. "I think this is the beginning of a profitable misunderstanding."

"It usually is," I said.

* * *

That evening I laid it all out on the chart table again, but differently now—not as a problem but as a plan, and plans have a smell to them, cold and clean, like the air before frost.

Step one: I would sign on as the consortium's pilot. I'd take their barge up the logging slough on the pretext of surveying it, feed their instruments a rich diet of interesting mud, and burn five, maybe six days of their clock. Kline was thorough but she wasn't psychic; she'd trust the water, because everyone trusts the water, and the water would lie to her on my behalf.

Step two: while I played shepherd to their barge, Dovie would prep my boat for a hard run—new impeller, fuel topped, the shallow-water gear checked twice. Rill would handle rigging without knowing why. Marisol would hold the tiller of the story, keeping Theo Anand and his notebook pointed at legends instead of facts.

Step three: on the ninth day, at slack before the spring tide peaked, I would leave the consortium mid-slough with a mechanical excuse and run for the cove like the devil himself was paying by the mile.

It was a good plan. It was also, I could see plainly now, written in Tam's handwriting. The false route. The convenient lie. The whole architecture of becoming unfindable. I'd spent twenty years telling myself I was nothing like him, and here I was at two in the morning again, drawing deception onto a chart with a steady hand, feeling that old familiar click of gears engaging—the click I'd heard in his voice a hundred times when he talked his way around a question.

The difference, I told the lamp, the chart, the ghost leaning over my shoulder, is that I'm lying to buy truth, not to bury it. I surface it in nine days. I put my name on it. His name too, whatever his name turns out to be.

The lamp didn't answer. Ghosts never do; that's their best trick.

* * *

I went up on deck near midnight to check the sky, which is half habit and half prayer. The stars were out hard and clear over the estuary, and the moon hung fat and rising, already fattening toward the alignment that would open the bluff like a door.

Nine days. I counted them like coins.

And that's when I saw the light. Out past the point, low on the water—a tender from the barge, running dark except for a single working lamp, moving slow along the shoreline below the bluff. Not surveying the open reach. Hugging the coast. My coast. The cove's coast.

They were looking for the cave on foot and by eye, not just by sonar. Which meant Kline hadn't swallowed my slough story whole. Which meant she was hedging, the way a smart gambler hedges, the way I would hedge.

I stood in the dark and let the cold resolve settle over me like ballast, low and steady, where it does the most good. Fine. Let her look. Let her spend her nights scanning bluffs she couldn't read. In nine days I'd be standing inside the Meridian's ribs with Tam's box in my hands, and she'd be forty miles up a dead slough learning what the Kestrel does to people who think they can buy it.

I just had to make sure that between now and then, nobody aboard my own boat sold me out.

Below decks, faintly, I heard Rill's phone chime.
