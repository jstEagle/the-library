## 8. Rill's Side Deal

There are three kinds of trouble on a riverboat, and I've been all of them: the kind you cause, the kind you inherit, and the kind that finds you because you were standing in the wrong place with your hand out.

This story is the third kind. Mostly. I'll get to the part where it's the first kind too. A person should be honest at least once a chapter.

* * *

Let me tell you about my debts, because they're the engine under everything, and engines are my business.

Fourteen thousand, six hundred and change, spread across three lenders like ballast nobody asked for. The biggest chunk belongs to a man named Ferro who operates out of the back room of a pawnshop two towns downriver, and Ferro does not believe in grace periods. He believes in arithmetic. Every week I don't pay, the number grows by an amount he explains to me slowly, like I'm a child or a tourist. The other two chunks belong to institutions, which sounds more respectable but behaves exactly the same—only they send letters instead of showing up on the dock with two friends and a patient expression.

I make decent money deckhanding for Casper Brightwater. I want to be clear about that. Decent money, steady work, a bunk that doesn't leak unless it rains sideways. What I don't make is fourteen-thousand-six-hundred-and-change money, not in any timeline where I also eat.

So when Vesper Kline stepped onto the dock in her city boots and started asking questions about unusual tides, I did what any sensible drowning person does.

I looked at the water and thought: how deep?

* * *

It started small, the way these things do. Small as a text message.

Kline had come through town twice before she found me. First time she was all permits and charm, buying rounds at the Saltgrass, asking the whole bar about tide anomalies and lost wrecks like she was writing a novel. Second time she was alone, and she found me re-tying a fender on the dock at dusk, and she didn't ask about wrecks at all.

"You're the one who works for Brightwater," she said. Not a question. Kline doesn't ask questions; she issues statements and waits to see if you correct them.

"Deckhand," I said. "Engineer's apprentice, if we're being generous."

"How generous are we being?"

"About sixty percent."

She smiled at that. She has a good smile, is the thing. Warm at the edges, cold at the center, like tea gone lukewarm. "My name's Vesper Kline. I work for Meridian Recovery Group." She let the name sit there between us, which I later understood was deliberate, though at the time I just thought it was a coincidence so neat it felt staged. "We're a salvage consortium. We locate historic wrecks, document them, recover what's recoverable. Legal, licensed, insured."

"That's a lot of nouns."

"I'm a lot of person." She looked out at the river the way tourists do, like it was scenery instead of a workplace with teeth. "Your captain knows something about the tides here. Something the charts don't show. I'd pay well for a conversation."

"Casper doesn't talk much."

"Everyone talks. It's a question of price." And then, casually, like commenting on weather: "You look like someone who's done some pricing lately."

I want it on the record that I said nothing. That I laughed it off, told her Casper was a closed book with a locked cover, wished her luck finding another river. I walked away clean.

What I didn't do was delete her number when she handed me the card.

That's the detail that matters, isn't it? The card stayed in my pocket. Then it moved to my toolbox, which is where I keep things I'm not ready to admit I'm keeping. A person's toolbox tells the truth about them long before their mouth does.

* * *

For nine days I was good. I want that noted too, since apparently I'm keeping a ledger of my own virtue now, and the entries are getting thin.

Nine days of hauling line and greasing fittings and saying nothing while Casper plotted courses on charts I wasn't supposed to study—and here's the thing, I studied them anyway, because that's who I am, a person who studies things he shouldn't. Dovie calls it curiosity. My mother called it nosiness. Ferro would call it an asset.

The charts were strange. I've been on the Kestrel my whole working life and I know its moods the way you know a dog that's bitten you twice—affectionately, with caution. But the route Casper was penciling in wasn't any route I knew. It bent up into the dead sloughs above Marsh Landing, waterways that haven't carried real traffic in thirty years, and there were little annotations in Casper's tight handwriting: *soundings unreliable*, *bar shifts E after flood*, and one note near a bend marked only with a small brass-colored dot and no label at all.

A dot like that means something. On a chart, everything means something.

And underneath the chart table, in the drawer that sticks, there was a strongbox. I didn't open it—I have limits, allegedly—but I saw the initials stamped on it once when Casper hauled it out at night, thinking I was asleep. T.A.M. Old Tam's initials. Tam who died before I ever signed aboard, Tam whose name comes up in crew stories the way dead captains' names always do, half reverence, half ghost story.

Casper sat up past midnight holding that box like it was a sleeping animal that might wake up angry.

I lay in my bunk doing the math I always do: what I knew, what it might be worth, what Ferro's arithmetic would do to me if I came up short again. And then I did the thing I'm not proud of, which is nothing. I lay there and let the math finish.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about betrayal: it's boring. It's paperwork. It's a Tuesday.

Day ten, Casper went ashore upstream to see somebody—Marisol covered the wheel, Dovie had the engine room, and I had four hours alone with the chart table while the boat swung at anchor off Cutter's Bend. Four hours. You could drive a barge through four hours.

I told myself I was just going to look. I actually said the words out loud, to the empty cabin, which should have been my first warning that I'd already decided otherwise. People who are just looking don't narrate.

The chart was still out from the morning's plotting. The camera on my phone is mediocre, but mediocre is enough for coordinates. I took eleven shots. The main route. The slough detour. The tide annotations. The page of Casper's notebook that was tucked under the chart's corner—the spring tide dates, the window, the words *cave opens 0640, closes fast* written in that same tight hand.

*Cave.* There it was, in ink, in Casper's handwriting. A cave.

Eleven shots took ninety seconds. Ninety seconds, and twenty years of a captain's careful secrecy folded up into my pocket next to my phone, lighter than a coil of rope.

Then I stood there feeling sick, which I recommend to no one, and did the second-worst thing of the day: I opened the message thread with Kline's number and sent the photos before the sickness could talk me out of it. No message attached. Just the images, naked, damning, sent.

The reply came in four minutes. Four minutes! She'd been waiting. That's what I understood then—that this woman had given me her card and then simply waited, with Ferro-like patience, for my arithmetic to finish. Her text said:

*Good start. This is worth 5. The rest—the actual location—is worth 40.*

Forty thousand. I read it maybe forty times, which felt thematically appropriate. Forty thousand dollars is Ferro paid in full, both institutions paid in full, my mother's roof fixed, and a year of not waking up at 3 a.m. doing division.

It is also, I noticed, in the part of my brain that was still honest, forty pieces of silver, and I am not even religious and that reference still landed.

* * *

I deleted nothing. Let's sit with that, because I've turned it over enough for both of us.

A smarter man deletes the thread. Scrubs the photos, wipes the sent folder, keeps his hands technically clean. I sat on my bunk with my thumb hovering over the delete option and I couldn't do it, and I've asked myself why maybe a hundred times since, and the answer keeps coming back the same ugly way: because deleting felt like lying worse than sending did.

Sending was a sin committed in a moment, by a scared man with bad math. Deleting would be a policy. It would mean I planned to keep doing it and just wanted no evidence. And some superstitious dock-rat corner of me believed—if you can call it believing—that if I left the evidence sitting there, plain as a chart annotation, then someday I'd have to face it. The photos were my confession, pre-written, addressed to whoever eventually made me write it out loud.

Also—and this is the smaller, sadder reason—deleting felt like pretending the debt didn't exist. The photos were worth five thousand against fourteen-six. I deleted them, I'd still owe the world everything. At least this way the world owed me something back.

Rationalization is a remarkable engine. Runs quiet, runs hot, burns anything you feed it.

* * *

Dovie caught me at it. Not the texting—she couldn't have, I checked the sightlines like a professional, which is to say like a guilty amateur who's watched too many heist films. No, she caught me at the *after*. Two days later, in the engine room, elbow-deep in the cooling pump, and she said, without looking up:

"You're checking your phone every eleven minutes. You check it like it owes you money."

"Everything owes me money."

"Mm." She handed me a gasket scraper. "When it's a person owing you money, you get twitchy. When it's you owing, you get *quiet*. You've gone quiet, Rill."

Dovie is seventy-some years old and has forgotten more about engines than I will ever learn, including, I suspect, the fact that people are also engines and she can read our gauges at a glance. I told her I was sleeping badly. Which was true. Which was also a lie. The two overlap more than you'd think.

"Sleep better," she said, and went back to her work, and let me keep my secret the way she lets an engine keep a rattle—filed under *will deal with it before it becomes a fire*.

I loved her for that and hated myself for needing it.

* * *

Marisol fed me that night, which is Marisol's solution to most problems, and possibly the correct one. Rice and beans and the good hot sauce she hides from the rest of us, and she talked about her garden on shore, and her sister's kids, and the book she was reading, ordinary human cargo, and I sat there across from her thinking: *these people would take a bullet for me, and I sold their captain's secrets for a down payment.*

Not even the full payment. Five thousand against forty. I hadn't even managed to betray anyone *competently*.

"You're somewhere else," Marisol said.

"Just tired."

"Liar." She said it fondly, which was worse. "Whatever it is, it'll keep till it won't. Eat."

So I ate. And the terrible thing, the thing I keep circling like a sandbar, is that the food was good and the company was warm and I felt—for one whole hour—like a person who had not done what I'd done. Memory is a slack tide. Everything settles, everything looks shallow and harmless, and you can almost believe the water will never come back for what you buried.

Then the tide turns. It always turns.

* * *

Kline texted again on day twelve. *Any movement on the rest? My buyers are impatient. Impatience makes offers shrink.*

I typed and deleted six replies. In the end I sent: *Working on it. Need time.*

Her response was instant, like she'd been holding it: *Time costs. Ask your captain about the spring tide. Clock's running on both of us.*

Which meant she already knew about the spring tide. Which meant either Casper's secrecy had more holes than my conscience, or my eleven photographs had told her more than I'd realized. Both possibilities made my stomach drop like a dropped wrench.

I stood at the rail that evening watching Casper at the wheel, silhouetted against a sky going copper, hands easy on the spokes, wearing twenty years of the river like a coat that fit. Casper had built a whole life out of not being known. No past discussed, no address fixed, a name chosen rather than inherited—I'd heard the stories, everyone had, and I'd always thought it was romantic, this self-made solitude, this captain who belonged to the water because the water asks fewer questions than people do.

And I had taken a phone picture of the walls of that life and mailed them to a stranger for five thousand dollars.

I didn't feel like a villain. That's the confession inside the confession. Villains feel powerful. I felt like a man bailing with a cup in a hull that was already taking water, and every cupful I threw over the side was one I'd punched through the planks myself.

* * *

That night I took out the strongbox question and turned it over one more time. Because here was my last exit, see. Here was the door marked *confess now, take the shame, keep the family*. All I had to do was walk into the wheelhouse and say: Casper, someone offered me money for your charts, and I took some of it, and I'm sorry, and I'll work it off, and I'll help you however you need.

I got as far as the companionway ladder. I actually had my foot on the bottom rung.

Through the wheelhouse glass I could see Casper at the chart table, head bowed over the strongbox, and the lamplight caught the box's brass corners, and Casper's shoulders were doing something I'd never seen them do in two years of watching that competent, contained figure—Casper's shoulders were shaking. Just slightly. Just enough.

And I thought: *they're carrying something that heavy, and I'd be adding mine on top, tonight, right before the hardest run of their life.*

Which is a beautiful piece of reasoning, and completely false, and I knew it was false even as I thought it, the way you know a sandbar chart is wrong but steer by it anyway because the truth requires a courage you don't have in stock.

I took my foot off the rung. I went back to my bunk. I lay in the dark listening to the river slap the hull—*you could have, you could have, you could have*—and I did not sleep, and I deleted nothing, and I confessed nothing.

In the morning, Kline's text was waiting:

*Last offer stands until Friday. After that I find my own way to the cave, and you find your own way out of debt. Choose.*

Friday. The spring tide was Sunday.

I stared at the screen a long time, thumb hovering, heart going like a badly balanced shaft, and somewhere below decks Dovie's engine turned over, steady and forgiving as ever, carrying us all toward water I'd helped make dangerous.

I typed: *What do you need to know?*
