## 9. Upstream Lies

The trick to lying on water, Old Tam used to say, is that the river does most of the work for you. People see a boat moving with purpose and they assume the purpose is honest. Nobody questions a wake. You just have to look like you know where you're going, and the current will carry your lie for miles.

I was counting on that.

* * *

The consortium's barge came up from the coast on Tuesday morning, all white steel and diesel arrogance, pushing a bow wave big enough to rock the fishing skiffs at their moorings. She was called the *Providence*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer, and she carried enough survey equipment to map the riverbed down to the last drowned beer can. Vesper Kline stood at her rail in a coat too clean for the Kestrel, watching our dock slide past like it was being appraised for purchase.

I'd agreed—grudgingly, publicly, with much visible reluctance—to guide them upstream. "Survey work," I'd called it, and the town had eaten it whole. Casper Brightwater, taking consortium money. There'd been talk at the bait shop. Marisol had said nothing, which was worse than if she'd said plenty.

What I hadn't told anyone—not Dovie, not Rill, not even myself until three in the morning when the truth arrived uninvited like a cat on the bed—was that I intended to waste their time so thoroughly they'd need a season to forgive me.

The dead slough branched off the main channel eleven miles upriver, past the old tannery pilings. On my charts it was marked as navigable to mile four. That marking was thirty years old, courtesy of a surveyor who had clearly been drunk, lost, or both. The slough silted shut after the flood of '94. It went nowhere now but into a wall of willow and reed, and it would take the *Providence* two full days of careful sounding to discover that, and another day to turn herself around without tearing her bottom out.

Two days. Maybe three, if the fog cooperated. Fog owed me favors; I'd spent twenty years earning them.

"Captain Brightwater." Kline's voice carried across the water between the boats, crisp as new money. "You're certain this channel shows consistent depth?"

"As certain as anything on the Kestrel," I called back, and let the river hear how steady I sounded.

It wasn't even a lie, technically. My chart did show depth there. Charts are just paper promises, though, and I was about to introduce the *Providence* to the difference between a promise and a fact.

Dovie had the engine idling sweet behind me, and I could feel her watching the back of my neck. She'd been quiet since we took the consortium's retainer—quiet in the way of an engineer who has noticed something wrong with the machinery and hasn't decided whether to mention it. I was the machinery. I knew it. I kept my eyes on the channel and my hands easy on the wheel and hoped she'd hold her diagnosis a little longer.

Rill was up forward, coiling lines that didn't need coiling. Rill had been coiling things for two days straight. Nervous hands find work; guilty hands find more of it. I filed that away next to everything else I was filing away these days—the strongbox under my bunk, Tam's initials cold as coins, the photograph of the woman whose face I still couldn't stop seeing when I closed my eyes.

We led the *Providence* up the main channel until the tannery pilings rose out of the water like black teeth, and then I swung hard to starboard and took us into the slough.

Behind me, I heard the barge's horn—a question mark in sound. I raised one hand, unhurried, the universal gesture of a pilot who knows something you don't. The horn stopped. The wake followed.

* * *

By noon we were deep in country no salvage barge had any business being in. The slough narrowed. Willows leaned over us, dragging their fingers across the deck. Herons watched us pass with the offended dignity of landowners interrupted. The water went tea-dark and slow, and the depth sounder began its long, mournful descent into fiction.

Kline came alongside in the consortium's rigid inflatable, riding our wake close enough to read my gauges. They climbed aboard without asking—of course they did—and stood at my shoulder while I conned us deeper into the lie.

"You know this water well," Kline said. Not a question. A measurement.

"Twenty years."

"Twenty years." Kline turned the number over like a coin they suspected was shaved. "You must have seen every inch of this river."

"Every inch changes every flood. That's the point of it."

"Mm." Kline looked out at the willows, at the herons, at the water so shallow ahead that a mullet was having second thoughts about it. "And unusual tides? Down near the coast? You'd have noticed anything... anomalous."

There it was. The word I'd heard them use in the dockside office, *anomalous*, wearing its permit like a badge. I shrugged with my whole body, the way Tam taught me—make the shrug do the work so your face doesn't have to.

"Tides do what tides do. I run cargo. Cargo doesn't care about anomalies."

"No," Kline said softly. "I suppose it doesn't." And then, turning to go back down the ladder: "Your engineer seems very thorough. She asked our sonar tech some interesting questions yesterday."

My hands stayed easy on the wheel. Inside, something dropped through cold water.

Dovie. Asking questions. Of course she was—she was rigging the boat for a real run, quietly, in the hours when she thought nobody counted her movements, and she'd been pumping the consortium's tech for specifications because that's what good engineers do: they learn the enemy's equipment before they learn to fear it. But Kline didn't know that. Kline saw a crew member gathering intelligence and thought maybe the intelligence was for sale.

Or maybe Kline hoped it was. Maybe Kline was casting lines everywhere, waiting for something to bite.

"Engineers ask questions," I said. "It's how you tell them from passengers."

Kline smiled with exactly half of their mouth. "Indeed." The inflatable dropped away, and I watched it fall in astern, and I did not breathe fully again for a quarter mile.

* * *

We anchored that night in a wide bend where the slough pretended to open up. The *Providence* threw lights across the water like a small dishonest city. I sat on my stern deck with coffee going cold and listened to their generators, their voices, their sonar pinging hopefully at mud.

Marisol came out and sat beside me without asking. Twenty years of friendship means you skip the asking.

"They're sounding ahead of us," she said. "Their launch went up past the bend before dark. They'll hit the silt bar by tomorrow noon."

"I know."

"You're going to let them find it."

"I'm going to let them spend a day and a half confirming it, then a day negotiating who's to blame, then a day getting turned around." I drank the cold coffee anyway. "Three days. Maybe four, if Kline argues with their own captain, which they will."

Marisol was quiet a moment. Out on the barge, someone laughed, the sound carrying flat across the water.

"And when they figure out you knew?" she said. "When they trace it back?"

"Then I'll be gone upriver with a head start and a spring tide coming."

"That's not a plan, Casper. That's a wish with a schedule."

I almost laughed. "Tam used to say something like that."

"Tam said a lot of things." She looked at me sideways, and there it was again—that gentleness with an edge on it, the way she'd been looking at me ever since Ada Voss's name first passed between us. "You've been different since the box. Moving like him. Talking like him sometimes. Did you know that? This—" she gestured at the dark water, the lying channel, the whole elaborate architecture of misdirection I'd built around us—"this is a Tam move. Leading people up a dead slough. He'd have loved it."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue.

What came out instead was quieter. "He learned it somewhere. I learned it from him. Maybe it goes further back than either of us."

"Maybe," Marisol said. "Just make sure you remember which side of the lie you're standing on. He forgot, near the end. Or he never knew."

She went below, and I stayed with the cold coffee and the generator hum, and I thought about Tam standing watches alone, keeping his own counsel, arranging the world so that no one could ever quite get a fix on him. I had told myself for twenty years that I was nothing like him. I had no fixed address, no past discussed, a name I'd chosen like a coat off a rack. I had built my whole life on the principle that the best place to hide is in plain motion.

And here I was, running a con on a salvage consortium, and finding—I hated this part, I found it in myself like a splinter working toward the skin—I was finding that I was *good* at it. Not grimly competent. Good. There was a pleasure in it, a clean bright pleasure, the same pleasure I got from threading a fog-bound channel, and I recognized it now for what it was: inheritance. Tam's blood ran in my habits if nowhere else, and his habits ran in the Meridian's wreck, and the wreck sat in a cave that opened twice a year like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

The tide window was shrinking. The storm system Theo Anand's teaser article had warned about was sliding down from the north, early and mean. Four days, Dovie's almanac said. Maybe less. And here I was spending them playing nursemaid to a barge full of people I was robbing.

* * *

Dovie found me at dawn, before the mist had finished lifting off the reeds. She came up the companionway with two coffees and handed me one, which was how I knew I was either forgiven or about to be fired.

"Their launch hit the bar at six," she said. "Forty minutes of horn-blowing. Their captain wants to warp off on the morning tide. Kline wants to push on in the inflatable, sound it by hand." She sat down across from me, knees nearly touching mine in the small cockpit. "That buys us till tomorrow, maybe the day after."

"Good."

"Casper." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "I've been rigging the boat. Spare fuel bladders lashed under the deck plates, the good prop, the spare sea cocks serviced, charts copied and hidden in three places. I've done it quiet, and I've done it well, and I haven't asked you one question." Her eyes came up, brown and level and older than her years. "But Kline thinks I've been selling. I can see it. They watch me like a hole in a hull."

"I know."

"So here's my question, and it's the only one: when we make the real run—who's aboard, and who gets left standing on a dock wondering?"

The question sat between us like a third person. Because that was the cost of the slough, wasn't it. Every day I bought with deception was a day I spent spending the trust of the only people who'd ever chosen me. Rill already had debts and hungry eyes. Marisol already knew too much and too little. And Dovie had just told me, in engineer's language, that she'd keep the secret as long as she was inside it—but not one minute outside it.

"Everybody," I said. "Everybody aboard, everybody told, before we run. No more lies inside the crew. The lies stay pointed outward or they don't sail."

Dovie studied me for a long moment, the way she'd study a weld she didn't quite trust. Then she nodded once and drained her coffee. "Then you'd better do it soon. Because Rill took the inflatable over to the barge an hour ago. Said they wanted to look at the sonar rig."

I felt it before I understood it—that cold drop through the water again, the same one Kline's words had started yesterday. Rill. On the consortium's barge. Voluntarily. With hungry eyes and coiled ropes' worth of nervous energy, standing on a deck full of screens and permits and cash.

Maybe it was nothing. Deckhands are curious; sonar rigs are shiny.

Maybe it was the hole in the hull, and I'd been watching the wrong gauge.

I stood up, set down the cup, and looked upriver toward the wall of willow where the slough died, and beyond it, two days south by the channels only I knew, a cave mouth waiting for the tide to open.

"Dovie," I said. "How fast can we be ready to leave?"
