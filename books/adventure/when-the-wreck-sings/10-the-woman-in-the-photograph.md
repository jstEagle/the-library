## 10. The Woman in the Photograph

The photograph had been sitting on my chart table for three days, and I'd been avoiding it the way you avoid a bill you can't pay. Every time I came into the cabin it was there—faded sepia, a woman on a dock with her hand shading her eyes like she was watching someone leave and knew they weren't coming back.

On the back, in Tam's careful draftsman's hand: *Ada. Harlow Bend, June.*

Harlow Bend was four hours upstream if the current cooperated, which the Kestrel never did out of charity. I told myself I was going up there anyway to pick up a generator part from Dovie's cousin, which was even true, in the way that most of my excuses were true—they just weren't the whole truth, and hadn't been since the tide went out and showed me a cave full of my mentor's secrets.

I left before dawn so I wouldn't have to explain myself to anyone. That was becoming a habit. So was noticing that it was becoming a habit.

* * *

Harlow Bend is the kind of town the river forgot to take with it when it changed course. The channel had migrated east over fifty years, leaving the old wharf high and dry and the town strung along a road that followed the ghost of the water. There was still a marina—a sad little thing, three houseboats and a fuel dock run by a man who looked like he'd been waiting since 1987 for someone to need gas.

"Ada Voss," I said.

He squinted at me. "You a reporter?"

"No."

"You selling something?"

"No."

He considered this. "Then yeah, she's up past the church. Blue house with the heron weathervane. She'll either talk your ear off or throw a broom at you. Coin flip."

The blue house sat at the end of a lane gone soft with moss, the heron on its pole rusted into something more suggestion than bird. A garden took up most of the yard—tomatoes, beans, a riot of nasturtiums spilling over a low fence like they'd escaped custody. An elderly woman was on her knees among the beans, and she heard me coming before I reached the gate because of course she did; people who live alone by rivers hear everything.

"If you're from the county about the drainage ditch," she said without turning around, "the answer is no, and the answer will remain no until the county learns what a culvert is."

"I'm not from the county."

She turned then, pushing herself up with a groan that was mostly performance and partly not. She was small and straight-backed, white hair cut short as a sailor's, eyes the flat gray-green of river water in February. She looked at me the way Tam used to look at weather coming downriver—not at what was there, but at what was behind it.

"Well," she said. "You've got his jaw."

My hand went to my face before I could stop it, which told her everything, which was probably the point.

"Come inside," Ada Voss said. "I'll put the kettle on. You look like a man who's been carrying something heavy and pretending it's light."

"I'm not a man," I said, mostly out of reflex, twenty years of correcting strangers.

She didn't blink. "Even better. Come inside."

* * *

Her kitchen smelled of rosemary and dust and the particular staleness of a house where one person has lived too long with their own thoughts. The kettle went on. She sat across from me at a table scarred with decades of rings, and she waited, hands folded, like she had all the time in the world and knew exactly how much of mine this was going to cost.

I put the photograph on the table between us.

She didn't reach for it. She looked at it for a long moment, and something moved through her face—grief, yes, but an old grief, the kind that's been renovated so many times it's mostly structural now.

"June," she said. "That was the last summer. He came up to say goodbye without saying goodbye. He was always good at that. Leaving sideways." She finally touched the photograph's edge, one finger, like checking whether it was hot. "Where did you get this?"

"In a strongbox. In a wreck." I heard how it sounded and kept going anyway. "The Meridian."

The kettle began its slow climb toward whistling. Neither of us moved to stop it.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

"The Meridian. The expedition ship. Lost—" I stopped, because I realized I didn't actually know the official story. Tam had never told it. That absence should have been louder than it was.

"Nineteen sixty-one," she said. "Off the Kestrel bar, supposedly. Eleven souls lost, one navigator among them. There was a plaque in the maritime museum for thirty years until the museum closed and somebody sold the plaque." Her voice was level, but her hands had gone still in a way that wasn't calm. "And you're telling me you found her. Found the wreck."

"And the strongbox. With his initials on it. And letters. And—" I pulled out the documents, the ones I'd read forty times and understood none of: discharge papers under a different name, a letter from a shipping company referencing a settlement, a naval commendation made out to *T. Voss*. "Tam told me he'd never sailed salt water. Twenty years, Ada. He told me he learned boats on the Kestrel, that the sea made him sick, that he never wanted anything but river mud under his keel. And every word of it was—"

"Lies," she said. "Yes. He was very good at them. It runs in the family, though I gave mine up earlier." She stood, took the kettle off before it screamed, poured two cups with the economy of someone who'd done ten thousand small tasks alone. "Do you know what the Meridian was carrying?"

"Cargo. Survey equipment. The manifest said—"

"The manifest lied too. It was carrying money. Sponsor's money, siphoned out through fake invoices for three years, and the final voyage was supposed to be the erasure—the ship 'lost,' the books closed, everyone who might ask questions conveniently at the bottom of the river." She set my cup down in front of me. "Except the navigator figured it out two days before they sailed."

I couldn't drink. My hands were doing something I didn't authorize, some fine tremor I hadn't felt since I was nineteen and burying the last of my own history.

"He was going to report it," Ada said. "He'd been raised better than that—our father was a harbor master, honest to the point of being unbearable about it. But Tam made the mistake of telling the sponsor's man what he knew instead of going straight to the authorities. And suddenly he wasn't a witness anymore, he was a liability, and the plan changed. The ship would be lost with all hands. Including the navigator who knew too much."

"So he ran."

"So he ran." She laughed, short and bitter. "Everyone thinks running is simple. Running is the hardest thing there is. You have to die convincingly. You have to let your sister go to your funeral—which was closed casket, empty, by the way, and I have hated that coffin for sixty years—and you have to become someone else so completely that even you start to believe it. He wrote me once a year. Unsigned, untraceable, postmarked nowhere useful. Just enough to let me know he was alive. Then the letters got shorter, and then they stopped, and I thought maybe he'd finally gotten careless, or tired, or dead for real." Her eyes came up to mine. "But he didn't stop, did he. He just stopped writing to me."

"He started writing to me," I said. "Sort of. He taught me the river. Twenty years. He never once—" My voice did something embarrassing and I steadied it. "Why? Why erase himself? If the sponsor falsified the loss, if there was embezzlement, he could have come forward. He had proof. He had—"

"Did you find the logbook?"

The question landed like a lead line hitting bottom. "How do you know about a logbook?"

"Because he told me, in the last letter, that someday someone would find the Meridian and that the truth was in the logbook, and that whoever found it should be careful, because the family that owned the shipping line didn't stay rich by letting witnesses breathe." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "He didn't come forward because coming forward meant proving he'd survived, which meant explaining where he'd been, which meant leading every vulture and creditor and possibly worse directly to the truth—and to anyone standing near him. Erasure wasn't cowardice, child. It was a wall he built and then lived inside so that the people he loved wouldn't catch what he caught."

"People he loved," I repeated. "He never mentioned a sister. Never mentioned any of it. I thought I knew him."

"You knew him the way the river knows a stone," Ada said. "You knew the shape of him, the part that shows above the waterline. The rest was his to keep." She studied me again, that weather-reading stare. "And what did you bury, I wonder? Because nobody gets that good at keeping still unless they've practiced."

I looked at my tea. Outside the window, a heron—the living kind—stood in the drainage ditch the county wanted to fix, motionless in that way herons have, like patience given feathers.

"That's not a story I give away free," I said.

"Good," she said, with sudden fierce approval. "Don't. He taught you that much, at least." She reached across and turned the photograph toward herself. "He was twenty-six here. He'd just gotten his navigator's papers. He stood on that dock in Harlow Bend—it was a real port then, before the river moved—and he held my hand and said, 'If anything happens, don't let them make me a monument.' I didn't understand him. I thought he meant it as a joke." She smiled, and it cracked something open in her face. "He meant: don't let them turn me into a story. Stories get owned by whoever tells them loudest."

* * *

We talked until the light went amber and long across the garden. She fed me soup I didn't ask for and would have been a fool to refuse. She told me about their father teaching them both to read tide tables before they could read novels. She told me about the funeral, the empty coffin, the sponsor's family sending flowers with a card that said *Deepest condolences* in typeset print, which she'd kept all these years as evidence of something she couldn't name until today.

And somewhere in the middle of it, the betrayal I'd been carrying since the cave shifted its weight. Not gone—nothing that heavy goes gently—but redistributed. Tam hadn't lied to me to use me. He'd lied to me to keep me outside the blast radius. Twenty years of silence wasn't a con. It was a man standing between a fire and everyone he cared about, holding the door shut with his body, for six decades, until his heart gave out in a wheelhouse on the Kestrel with his student at the helm.

It didn't excuse it. But it explained it, and explanation is sometimes the only mercy available.

"There's something else," Ada said, as I was getting up to leave. She went to a sideboard, opened a drawer with a key from around her neck, and came back with a thin envelope, yellowed soft as cloth. "His last letter. Fourteen years ago. I want you to read the second page."

I read it. His handwriting, unmistakable, the same hand that had labeled the strongbox:

*If the day ever comes that the river gives her back, find the person I taught. They'll know what to do. I couldn't finish it. Maybe stillness can.*

"'Her,'" I said. "The ship. He called the Meridian *her*."

"Of course he did," Ada said. "Sailors do." But she was watching me too closely, and I suspected she'd noticed that I'd flinched at *her* for reasons that had nothing to do with ships, and that she was polite enough—or cruel enough—to file it away without comment.

At the gate, she caught my sleeve.

"One more thing. Two weeks ago, a woman came through town. Sharp clothes, sharp questions, asking about unusual tides downstream and whether anyone local had ever mentioned the Meridian. I told her I was deaf and senile and sent her on her way." Ada's grip tightened. "She'll be back, or others like her. Whatever you found, whatever you're going to do about it—do it fast, and do it loud enough that no one can twist it after. Silence only protects you while nobody's looking. Once they start looking, silence starts protecting *them*."

I walked back down the lane to the boat with the letter in my jacket pocket and the evening coming on, and by the time I reached the fuel dock the radio was already crackling with the first reports: a storm system forming out in the gulf, tracking northeast, early for the season, moving faster than anyone liked.

Nine days to spring tide, the almanac had said.

The sky over the Kestrel had other ideas.
