## 11. Nine Days Becomes Four

There is a particular kind of silence a river town produces when you ask it the wrong question. It isn't hostility. It's more like a whole community leaning back from a table at once, chairs scraping in unison. I'd been hearing it for three days, and I'd learned to read it the way a sailor reads weather: not as a wall, but as a forecast.

My name is Theo Anand, and I write for outlets that mostly spell my name wrong. Freelance maritime history, salvage disputes, the occasional drowned-town feature. I came to Kestrel's Landing on a rumor the way everyone comes to Kestrel's Landing on a rumor, and like everyone, I assumed I'd be smarter than the rumor.

The rumor was the *Meridian*.

Every lost vessel has a gravity to it, and the *Meridian*'s was strange—light, almost. Twenty-two years gone, no bodies, no salvage claim, no widow's petition in any archive I could find. An expedition vessel that sailed up a river delta and evaporated. The official record said lost with all hands in a storm. The unofficial record said nothing at all, which is the loudest thing a record can say. Somebody had paid for that silence. I've been in this business long enough to know that silence has an invoice, and somebody paid it.

So I did what I do. I sat in the Saltgrass Tavern with a laptop I didn't open and a beer I did, and I listened.

* * *

The tavern keeper, a broad woman named Odile who polished glasses like she was interrogating them, gave me the town's collective biography in one sentence: "We ferry things downriver and we don't ask what they are." She said it with the flat pride of a town that has decided its discretion is a virtue rather than a business model.

But discretion frays at the edges, and I'm good at edges.

I learned that the tide had been strange this season—the lowest spring lows in a decade, old-timers said, though old-timers say that about every season. I learned that a salvage consortium with a name like a law firm—Corvane Maritime Recovery, which sounds less like a company and more like a diagnosis—had been asking about "unusual tidal exposures" up and down the coast. I learned that a woman with a corporate lanyard and a smile like a signed NDA had bought drinks for half the dockhands and gotten, by Odile's account, "nothing but weather talk."

And I learned—third beer, fourth listener, a deckhand with more loyalty to beer than to omertà—that a riverboat captain named Brightwater had been seen running the lower sloughs at dawn, alone, twice, in water no sensible pilot would risk.

Brightwater. I wrote the name on a napkin and looked at it for a while. Casper Brightwater. The name rang a faint bell, the way a name does when you've read it in a footnote and forgotten the footnote. I'd chase that later. Footnotes are where the truth goes to hide from people who only read the text.

Here's what I knew about the town's captain, assembled from tavern talk the way you assemble a bird from scattered feathers: twenty years on the Kestrel, ferried everything from timber to trouble, no fixed address, no history anyone could produce, and a mentor—Old Tam, dead five years now, a name spoken with the particular warmth people reserve for the dead and the very good. Nobody knew where Brightwater came from. Nobody asked. In this town, that wasn't rudeness. It was architecture.

Which meant that when a stranger started asking, the whole building noticed.

* * *

I filed the teaser on a Tuesday, because Tuesday is when editors are hungriest and fact-checkers are weakest.

I want to be honest about what I wrote, because writers who traffic in other people's secrets should keep a few of their own. The piece was four hundred words and it said almost nothing, which is the most I've ever said in four hundred words. *Sources close to the river trade suggest the final resting place of the Meridian, lost with all hands in 1979, may lie within the tidal reaches of the Kestrel system. The search area, one source says, is "narrowing." Corvane Maritime Recovery declined to comment. A spring tide in nine days may offer a window that comes once in a decade.*

"Narrowing." I love that word. It does all the work and carries none of the blame. It could mean the consortium was closing in. It could mean the river was closing in. It could mean nothing. It meant, functionally, that every person with a boat, a debt, or a dream between the head of tide and the bar would spend the next nine days looking at the water differently.

The piece ran under the headline THE RIVER KEEPS ITS DEAD—OR DOES IT? My editor wanted a question mark; I'd wanted a period. We compromised, which in journalism means the question mark won.

By Wednesday morning it had been picked up by two aggregators and one conspiracy forum with a user base that believed the *Meridian* was carrying Nazi gold, alien alloy, and—my personal favorite—the original master tapes of a lost blues album. By Wednesday afternoon, Corvane's press office had issued a statement so bland it qualified as a confession: they were "aware of speculation" and "committed to responsible engagement with any historically significant site."

Responsible engagement. I read that phrase four times. Corporations don't say *responsible* about things they don't intend to take.

By Thursday, I heard, they'd chartered divers out of Port Calloway. Commercial hard-hat crews, the kind that don't ask what they're recovering because asking costs extra. A survey barge was being fitted out. And a woman named Vesper Kline—whom I'd interviewed once, two years ago, about a wreck she'd stripped to the rivets off San Lucero while calling it "archaeological triage"—was in Kestrel's Landing, buying drinks and getting weather talk.

I'd come here to write about a ghost ship. I was rapidly becoming the thing that made the ghost ship real. There's a version of this where I feel guilty. I keep that version in the same drawer as my retirement plan. The truth is that the *Meridian* was always going to surface—tides like these come once in a decade, and secrets are just cargo waiting on a low enough tide. All I did was read the manifest out loud.

That's what I told myself, anyway, on Thursday night, when I walked the docks and saw the *Kestrel Queen*—Brightwater's boat, I'd learned her name by then, the way you learn the name of the one person in town who won't talk to you—riding low at her moorings, lights off, and felt the whole river holding its breath.

* * *

Friday, I made my one direct play. Marisol Reyes.

I'd been told to avoid her—told by Odile, in the universal language of barkeeps, which is a raised eyebrow and a slightly slower glass-polish. Marisol was Brightwater's oldest friend, ran the chandlery, and was, per the town's consensus, the captain's voice and right hand. Which made her either the worst person to approach or the only one. In my experience those are usually the same person.

She was reeving a block and tackle when I walked into the chandlery, and she didn't stop, and she didn't look up, and she let the silence do a full minute of work before she said, "You're the one writing the stories."

"Theo Anand." I put out a hand she didn't take, and I respected her for it. "I'm writing one story. Whether it's a good one depends on who talks to me."

"We don't talk to journalists."

"Sure you do. You just did." I smiled. She didn't. "Marisol. Can I call you Marisol? The *Meridian* was real. It's down there somewhere, and in nine days the tide's going to open the river like a drawer. The only question is who's standing there when it does. A consortium with a barge and a legal team? Or the people who actually live on this water?"

"Neither," she said. "Nobody. That's the answer you're not going to print."

"Nobody's not an answer. Nobody's just the consortium winning slowly."

She set down the tackle and looked at me then, and I'll give her this—she didn't perform anything. No wounded dignity, no townie scorn. Just a flat, measuring look, like I was a current she was reading for speed and depth. "You said nine days," she said. "Where'd you get nine days?"

"The tide tables. Spring tide, the big one, the once-a-decade low. It's public information."

"Then you should look at them again." And she went back to her tackle, and I stood there like a man who'd been handed a grenade with the pin already pulled and told it was a paperweight.

* * *

I looked at them again.

She was right, and being right cost her something to say, which told me everything. The storm system I'd been reading as a footnote—a low forming off the shelf, the kind of thing that makes a paragraph of weather copy—was tracking in early. Two days early, maybe three. And a storm surge doesn't just raise the water. It wrecks the window. It muddies the sloughs, shifts the bars, and turns a clean four-hour slack into a white-water argument. The once-a-decade tide wasn't nine days out anymore. It was four, maybe five, and it was going to come in angry.

I sat in the tavern with the tables spread out in front of me like a losing hand and did the arithmetic. Corvane's barge was fitting out at Port Calloway, a day's steam south. The divers were chartered. Kline was already in town. If the window was four days, then the race wasn't starting in nine days. It had started the moment I filed my teaser, and I had fired the starting gun on a race where I didn't know any of the runners, and one of them was a captain who had spent twenty years building a life out of not being found.

I thought about that napkin in my pocket. *Casper Brightwater.* I'd finally placed the bell it rang. Years ago, in a maritime archive in another life, I'd read a casualty report from an expedition called the *Meridian*—lost with all hands, 1979—and in the appendix, a list of the shore-side families notified. One of them was a Voss. Ada Voss, sister of the navigator. And in a newspaper clipping from the following year, a human-interest piece about river families, there had been a photograph: an old man at a tiller, and beside him a kid of maybe fifteen, sharp-eyed, holding a lead line like it was a sword. The caption named the old man—Tam—and the kid only as "his ward."

His ward. Nobody's child. Nobody's anything. A kid with no last name in the caption, raised by a dead man's ghost, who grew up to become a ghost too.

I'm not a sentimental man. Sentiment is what people use to avoid the story. But I sat there with my beer going warm and felt the story rearrange itself under my hands, the way a river rearranges a bar overnight. This wasn't a treasure hunt. It had never been a treasure hunt. It was a person who had spent twenty years standing guard over something, and I had just told the whole coast where the door was.

The question that matters—the one I'd be answering with my byline either way—was what the guard was guarding. Gold, or a grave? A crime, or a kindness? I'd spent two years reading Vesper Kline's press clippings. I knew what she'd do with either answer.

* * *

Saturday morning I walked the dock road early, before the town woke, because towns tell you their truths in the margins of the day. The river was low and brown and patient, the exposed mud giving off that cold iron smell, and the *Kestrel Queen* sat at her moorings with a new energy about her—Dovie's silhouette in the engine hatch, the deck already stacked with line and lift bags, the boat doing that thing boats do when they're being readied for something: leaning slightly forward, like a dog that's heard its name.

And there, at the end of the dock, talking to a deckhand with a smile that cost nothing and meant less, stood Vesper Kline.

She saw me see her. That's the thing about Kline—she never pretends she didn't see you. She excused herself from the deckhand, who suddenly found urgent business with a cleat, and walked toward me with her hands in her jacket pockets like the whole dock was a room she owned.

"Theo Anand." She pronounced it correctly, which unsettled me more than any threat could have. "I read your piece. 'Narrowing.' Nice word."

"Thank you. It's accurate."

"It's a fuse." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough to talk low, far enough to be deniable. "You know what happens when you light one. Everybody runs. Most of them run the wrong way." She glanced at the *Kestrel Queen*, briefly, professionally, the way an appraiser glances. "Some of them run the right way and pretend they didn't."

"Is that a warning or a job offer?"

"It's an observation." She smiled her signed-NDA smile. "The window's closing, Mr. Anand. Storm's coming in early—surely you've seen the tables. Four days, maybe less. My barge sails tomorrow night. When it gets here, this stops being a story and becomes an operation. Operations don't have bylines." She turned to go, then paused, and gave me the only honest sentence of the conversation, delivered like a tip on a horse: "Whoever you're protecting by writing around them—you should tell them the clock changed. They might not know yet."

She walked away down the dock, and I stood in the cold iron smell of the low tide with my hands in my pockets and my heart doing something I'd have called unprofessional if anyone asked.

Because she was right. That was the worst part. Brightwater might not know. The window had collapsed from nine days to four overnight, and the one person on this river with a reason to be first through that cave mouth was out there running sloughs at dawn, trusting a tide that no longer existed.

I could file the storm update and let the chips fall where the aggregators threw them. That was the job. That was always the job.

Or I could walk up the dock road, knock on a riverboat's hull, and tell a person who had spent twenty years not being found that their four days had just become four days—and watch them decide whether I was a messenger or just another tide coming in early.

I looked at the *Kestrel Queen*. Dovie's head was out of the engine hatch now, watching me watch the boat. Somewhere below, I'd have bet my last invoice, a chart was spread open and a pencil was moving, and the pencil was about to be wrong.

I started up the dock road.
