## 12. What the River Keeps

I've known Casper Brightwater for eleven years, and in that time I've learned exactly three things about their past: they don't talk about it, they don't write it down, and if you push, they'll change the subject with the smoothness of a river bending around a stone. So when I stood on the deck of the *Kestrel Queen* that gray morning, holding a scrap of paper with a name written on it in Theo Anand's terrible handwriting, I knew I was about to do something that would cost me.

The name was Ada Voss.

I'd gotten it out of Theo the way you get a splinter out of your own foot—slowly, painfully, and with a fair amount of swearing. He'd come to the dock café the evening before, all laptop and charm, buying me coffee I didn't want and asking questions I liked even less. He had a way of framing surveillance as friendship. *You've worked with Brightwater a long time, right? You must hear things. Old stories. River legends.* And then, casual as a man commenting on the weather: *The Meridian.*

I'd heard the name before, of course. Everybody on the Kestrel had, if they'd spent enough evenings listening to Tam tell his stories—though Tam had never once called it the *Meridian* in my hearing. He'd called it "the ship I never sailed on," which was his idea of a joke, delivered with that particular flatness that meant it wasn't one. Tam's stories were all about rivers. Never the sea. Twenty years I'd known the man, and not one story about salt water. I'd thought it was a quirk. Old men are allowed their quirks.

But Theo said *Meridian* and I watched his face when he said it, the way you watch a fisherman's face when he lies about the one that got away, and there was something under the charm. Something hungry. And then he'd mentioned—offhand, he thought, though nothing about Theo was offhand—that a woman named Ada Voss had been asking around upstream towns about a lost expedition vessel, and that Voss was a name connected to the *Meridian*'s original crew list, and wouldn't it be something if Brightwater had ever mentioned it?

I'd told him I had a shift. Which was true. I'd told him I didn't gossip about my captain. Which was also true, in the way that things are true right up until they aren't.

Because here's what I did after he left: I went back to my bunk on the *Queen*, where we keep a shoebox of Tam's things that nobody has ever had the heart to throw out—his spare pipe, a tide almanac from 1998, a photograph of a river regatta with half the faces gone soft from damp—and I sat there turning the name over. Voss. Voss. And then I remembered. Tam's box of letters, the ones Casper had shown me exactly none of, the ones I only knew existed because I'd walked into the cabin unannounced one evening three weeks ago and seen Casper's face do something I'd never seen it do before.

Casper had looked afraid.

Casper Brightwater, who once steered a loaded barge through a flood crest with a broken hand and a cigarette clenched in their teeth, had looked at a shoebox of old paper like it was a live grenade. And they'd closed the lid, and smiled at me, and said, "Old Tam's paperwork. Nothing interesting," in a voice that convinced me of exactly one thing: it was the most interesting thing on the river.

So that morning, when Casper came up on deck with two coffees and their usual expression of competent calm, I took the scrap of paper out of my pocket and set it on the rail between us.

"Ada Voss," I said. "Who is she?"

* * *

I want to tell you that Casper handled it gracefully. I want to tell you they laughed, or deflected, or did the river-bend-around-the-stone thing. They didn't. They stopped walking. The coffee in their hand did a small, slow tilt, and I watched them notice it and correct it, and in that half-second I saw the whole architecture of the lie—the one they'd been living, the one they were building now—and I understood that whatever was under the waterline of this boat, it was big.

"Where'd you hear that name?" Casper asked. Quiet. Careful. The voice they use on the radio when the channel's not secure.

"Theo Anand. The journalist. He came by the café last night buying coffee and asking about the *Meridian*." I kept my voice level. "He says a woman named Ada Voss has been asking about a lost expedition vessel in the towns upstream. He says Voss is on the original crew manifest. And I know, Casper, because I walked in on you with Tam's box three weeks ago and you lied to my face about it, that there are letters in that box, and I know that whatever's in those letters, it's why you've been sleeping four hours a night and checking the tide tables like a woman reading tea leaves."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere down the dock, Rill was hosing off the deck, the hiss of it filling the silence Casper left.

"You've been talking to Anand," Casper said at last.

"I've been *enduring* Anand. There's a difference." I folded my arms. "He's going to keep coming back. And he's not stupid, Casper. Hungry, but not stupid. And Kline's people are in town asking about unusual tides, and Dovie's rigged the engine for something she won't name, and Rill is jumpy as a cat on a hot plate, and you're planning *something*, and I am standing here on your boat holding a name you clearly hoped would stay buried at the bottom of the river with everything else Tam left behind. So. Ada Voss. Who is she?"

Casper looked at me for a long moment. And I'll say this for them: they didn't try the smile. They didn't try to make it small. They set the coffee down on the rail, and they said, "Come below. Not here."

* * *

The cabin smelled of diesel and old paper. Casper sat on the bunk across from me and put the strongbox on the table between us, and for a moment just rested their hand on the lid like you'd rest a hand on a casket.

"Tam's initials," Casper said. "On the box. In a sea cave, in the wreck of a ship Tam swore he'd never set foot on. I found it at low water, three weeks back. I've been sitting on it since."

"Three weeks." I didn't bother hiding how that landed. "You've been carrying this for three weeks and you told nobody."

"I was going to tell you." They opened the box. "I was working out how."

The letters were soft at the folds, the ink gone the brown of old tea. Casper handed them to me one at a time, and I read, and the cabin got very small.

They were from Ada Voss to a man named Tomas Voss—Tam, Tam, oh, Tam—and they were the letters of a sister writing to a brother who had died. That was the first thing. The letters started as grief. *They held the memorial in March. Mother lit a candle every Sunday until she couldn't anymore. I don't blame you for what they say you did, but I wish to God you'd let me know you're alive.* And then, further down in the stack, the letters changed. Because Ada had found out. There's a letter from 1997 that's just one line: *I know it's you. The handwriting gave you away, you always pressed too hard on your T's.* And after that, the letters are different—still a sister's letters, but to a living man, a man who had let his family mourn him and walked away into a river town and become Old Tam, keeper of stories about water he'd never sailed.

"He faked his death," I said. My voice came out strange. "Tam. Our Tam. He let them bury an empty box."

"He was the navigator on the *Meridian*," Casper said. "Final expedition. The official story is the ship was lost in a storm and the navigator's errors killed four people. Ada says that's not what happened. Ada says the sponsor—the man who funded the whole voyage—was running money through the expedition accounts, and when the audit got close, he needed a disaster. Tam saw the numbers. Tam knew the ship was being run into a storm it couldn't survive, or close enough, and he got himself off the manifest and let the sea take the blame." Casper's jaw worked. "He didn't drown. He just let everyone think he did. And then he spent thirty years ferrying cargo up and down a river, telling stories about water, and never once—never *once*—telling any of us who he was."

I sat with that. I looked at the photograph Casper took from the box—a young woman with Tam's same stubborn chin, standing on a dock, squinting into sun—and I thought about the old man who'd taught me to read water, who'd held my hair back when I was seasick on my first run, who'd died in a chair by the window with the river going by, and I felt the grief come back around again, but changed. Grief with a stone in it.

"Why?" I said. "Why not just come home? Why let his mother light candles for a ghost?"

"Ada says it was shelter, not spite. The sponsor's people were still looking for him. The only safe Tam was a dead Tam." Casper's voice did something complicated on the word *safe*. "She didn't learn he was alive until years after her mother died. She's been writing to him anyway, all this time, care of a poste restante in Halloway. The letters stopped coming back marked undeliverable after he moved here. She knew. She always knew where he was. And she never came." A pause. "Until now."

"Until the *Meridian* started surfacing," I said. "Literally."

"The tide dropped further this year than it has in decades. The cave mouth showed. And Ada Voss is an old woman who's run out of time to be careful." Casper looked at the letters, and then at me, and I saw the decision happen behind their eyes—not all of it, but the shape of it. "There's more in that wreck, Mari. I know there is. Tam kept the box sealed and he kept the cave off every chart he ever drew, and Tam didn't do anything without a reason. Whatever's down there, he wanted it found by someone. Or never found at all. I haven't decided which."

"And the consortium? And Anand? And whatever Dovie's rigged and Rill's jumpy about?"

"That part I'm not telling you yet." Casper said it plainly, no apology in it. "Not because I don't trust you. Because the fewer people who know the shape of it, the fewer people who can be made to give it up. Kline's people lean on people, Mari. They lean hard. I've seen what they do to a town. I want to be the only one who knows enough to hurt."

I should have been angry. Some part of me was—there's a particular sting in being loved and managed at the same time, and I know that sting like I know the sound of the *Queen*'s engine. But I looked at Casper's face, at the four hours of sleep and the three weeks of carrying it alone, and I thought about Tam, who'd carried his own secret for thirty years and died with it, and I understood that this was the family disease. This was what Tam had left us, along with the stories: the habit of carrying things.

"All right," I said. "Here's what you get. I'm in. I don't know the plan, I don't ask, and when it goes sideways—and Casper, it will, everything on this river goes sideways eventually—I'm standing on your deck when it does. That's the deal."

Casper exhaled, long and slow, like they'd been holding that breath for three weeks. "That's the deal," they said. And then, quieter, in a voice I'd only heard them use once before, at Tam's funeral: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." I stood up. My knees cracked; I'm fifty-two and the river takes its toll. "Because there's a journalist downstairs at the café right now, and he's going to come back, and I need to know what I'm allowed to tell him."

* * *

Theo found me at the café that evening, right on schedule, wearing the expression of a man who believes charm is a tool that works on everyone eventually.

"Marisol." He slid into the seat across from me uninvited, which was at least honest. "Any thoughts since last night?"

"A few." I stirred my coffee I wasn't drinking. Theo Anand, I'd decided, was best handled like a strong current: you don't fight it straight on, you angle across it and let it take you where you were already going. "I talked to my captain."

I watched him lean in, and I gave him the half-truth, the one Casper and I had built together on the deck that afternoon, word by word, like laying planks over deep water.

"The *Meridian* is a story Tam used to tell," I said. "Or half-tell. He'd get drunk on festival nights and talk about a ship he'd never sailed on, and everybody thought it was a joke. Casper thinks there might be something in it. Old man's stories, you know how they go. Casper's talking to a researcher upstream—yes, a woman, no, I'm not giving you her name—who knew Tam, about whether any of it's real. That's all. A maybe. A might-be. If anything comes of it, you'll hear it from us, because Casper would rather control the story than read it in your paper."

Every word of that was true. Not one word of it was the truth. I have never been prouder or more ashamed of a paragraph in my life, and I delivered it with the flat calm of a woman reading a cargo manifest, and Theo drank it down like it was coffee.

"A researcher," he said. "Upstream."

"Mm." I sipped. "And Theo? If you print a word before we're ready, you'll never get another one from anyone on this dock, and dock people talk to everyone on this river. Understood?"

"Understood." He smiled his laptop smile. But I saw his hand move on the table, the little involuntary twitch of a man who's already writing the headline in his head, and I knew I'd bought time and not much of it. Theo Anand didn't publish rumors. He published *teasers*. Somewhere in the next few days there'd be a piece, vague and hungry, about a legend narrowing, and every salvage man and treasure tourist within two hundred miles would come sniffing down the Kestrel like eels after blood.

I walked him to the end of the dock and watched him go, and then I stood there in the evening light watching the water, and I thought about Ada Voss pressing too hard on her T's, and Tam lighting no candles for anyone, and Casper carrying a strongbox like a casket, and I understood something that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone:

Secrets don't sink. That's the thing nobody tells you. Everybody says the river keeps what it's given, but the river doesn't keep anything—it just holds things under, and holds them, and holds them, until the tide drops far enough.

And the tide, I could feel it in my knees the way old sailors feel weather, was going out.

When I got back to the *Queen*, Dovie was standing at the engine hatch with a wrench in her hand and a look on her face I didn't like at all. She was staring down at something on the deck plating—footprints, or the lack of them, or maybe just the boat sitting wrong in the water—and when she glanced up at me, she said, in a voice as flat as Tam's old jokes:

"Marisol. You want to tell me what's going on with this boat? Because somebody's been in my charts, and it wasn't the captain."
