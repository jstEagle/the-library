## 13. The Mutiny of Small Doubts

I've been an engineer for thirty-one years, and in that time I've learned that boats tell the truth before people do. People will look you in the eye and lie with a straight spine and steady voice. A boat can't. A boat just sits differently in the water, and if you know how to listen, she'll tell you everything.

The *Pelican's Grace* was sitting wrong.

Not wrong enough for anyone else to notice. Wrong by inches, wrong by feel, wrong the way a room is wrong when someone's moved a lamp two feet to the left. I came aboard on the morning of the fifth day out from the Kestrel landing and stood on the dock for a full minute before I stepped across, just looking at her waterline.

She was riding light by the stern. Maybe two hundred pounds light, maybe less. Somebody had moved something. Or taken something off. Or put something on forward that I didn't know about.

Now, you have to understand what kind of boat the *Grace* is. She's a hundred and ten feet of old river steel that Casper Brightwater has kept alive through what I can only describe as stubbornness weaponized. I've been her engineer for nine years, and in nine years I have never once found something on that boat that Casper didn't know about. Not a spare shackle, not a case of oil, not a secret bottle of rye that Rill thought he'd hidden well in the forepeak. Casper knows every pound of that vessel the way I know my own hands.

So if she was sitting wrong, either Casper had changed something—and Casper tells me everything, that's the arrangement, that's the whole architecture of our working trust—or somebody else had changed something without telling either of us.

I went below and checked the hold first. Cargo was lashed where it should be. Then the lazarette. Then the forepeak, where I found Rill's rye, still hidden, still untouched, which meant Rill hadn't been down here in a while, which was its own small oddity, because Rill practically lived down here when he was avoiding work.

And then I found the thing that started the whole mutiny in my chest.

A crate. Maybe three feet long, strapped down under a false floor panel in the bow compartment, forward of where cargo ever goes, because that space floods in a hard chop and everybody knows it. Somebody had put a crate in the one part of the boat where you don't put anything you care about, and they'd lashed it down like they cared about it very much.

I didn't open it. That's the part I want on the record, if there's ever a record. I didn't open it. I put the panel back, I wiped my prints off the strap out of pure habit, and I climbed up into the gray morning with a feeling in my gut like a fouled injector.

Somebody on this boat had a secret. And it wasn't the secret I already knew about.

* * *

Here's the thing about the secret I already knew about: I'm not supposed to know it, and Casper doesn't know that I know, and I intend to keep it that way until it stops being survivable to keep it.

You don't spend nine years as somebody's engineer without learning to read their face the way you read a gauge. Casper's face has maybe four settings: *working*, *amused*, *tired*, and *the river is speaking to me and I am pretending it isn't*. But there's a fifth setting that only comes out when Casper thinks nobody's watching, and I started seeing it the night we left the landing. It's the setting I'd call *carrying something heavy up a ladder and hoping the rungs hold*.

The "survey job" was a lie. I knew it the way you know weather coming. Survey jobs don't get planned at midnight over charts that get covered up when you walk into the wheelhouse. Survey jobs don't make Casper Brightwater—Casper Brightwater, who once spent forty minutes explaining to a customs officer why our bilge pump was registered in two parishes—go quiet about destinations. Casper had told us a slough name I'd never heard of, for a client nobody could produce, and Dovie Okafor had smiled and said *sure, captain* and gone back to the engine room to think about it.

I didn't push. That's not false loyalty; that's engineering judgment. You don't disassemble an engine while it's running. You watch it, you listen to it, you note the vibrations, and you wait for the part that's going to fail to announce itself.

But then there was Rill.

Rill, who at twenty-three is the youngest thing on this boat besides the paint. Rill, who talks the way water runs downhill—constantly, and finding every low place. For the first three days of this trip, Rill had been *quiet*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Rill. Quiet. He'd stand his watch and answer when spoken to and then he'd go sit on the cargo hatch with his phone in his hands, hunched over it like a man praying into a small black altar, and when anybody came near he'd turn it face-down on his thigh with a speed that fooled absolutely no one.

Marisol noticed too. I saw her notice. She and I have a shorthand, Marisol and me, developed over card games and engine-room emergencies, and on the third evening she caught my eye across the galley while Rill sat in the corner not talking, and she did this tiny thing with her eyebrows that in our language means *you seeing this?* and I did the thing with my mouth that means *I'm seeing it and I don't like it*.

That night I lay in my bunk listening to the boat breathe and made a list. I'm a list person. It's how I keep from worrying, which is to say, it's how I worry in an organized fashion.

One: Casper is lying to us about the job, for reasons Casper believes are good. Probability of malice toward crew: very low. Casper would sooner cut off a hand.

Two: Rill is doing something with that phone that he's ashamed of. Probability of malice: unknown. Rill has debts—I know about the debts because Rill talks even when he's trying not to, and because a man who owes money to people like the people Rill owes money to develops a specific flinch when his phone buzzes.

Three: There is a crate in my forepeak that nobody told me about, and the *Grace* is riding two hundred pounds light by the stern, which means something else has also moved, and I haven't found what yet.

Four: We are going somewhere on this river that Casper needs to go very badly, and very quietly, and very soon, and the tide tables taped inside the wheelhouse door have been annotated in Casper's handwriting with dates circled in red, and the next circled date is four days out.

I lay there and I thought: *Dovie, old woman, you are on a boat with three secrets and you only understand one of them, and the river is going to be high and fast in four days, and you are the only person aboard whose job it is to imagine the disaster before it happens.*

So I did my job.

* * *

The life rafts on the *Pelican's Grace* are, officially, two: the standard canister mounts port and starboard amidships, inspected, tagged, and as decorative as most safety equipment on working riverboats, because in nine years we have never once needed them and everyone aboard has quietly agreed to believe we never will.

What is less official is that I maintain a third raft.

I'm not going to apologize for this. I came up on the Orinoco barges before I came up on the Kestrel, and I worked two seasons on a boat whose owner believed life jackets were an insult to his seamanship, and I watched a man I liked drown in water he'd crossed ten thousand times because the one thing nobody imagined was the one thing that happened. After that, I decided that paranoia, done properly, is just maintenance you do on people instead of machinery. I keep a raft—a real one, coastal-grade, twenty-man, hydrostatic release—wrapped in canvas under the workbench in the engine room, where it looks for all the world like a spare exhaust run. I service it every spring. I have never told anyone it exists. Not Casper. Especially not Casper, who would either be hurt that I plan for the *Grace* failing—which she never has—or would look at me with that fifth face and say, quietly, *good thinking, Dovie,* and somehow that would be worse.

On the fourth morning, while the others were at breakfast, I unwrapped her.

The canvas came away and the casing was sound, the hydrostatic release armed, the painter coiled the way I'd left it in the spring. I checked the gas bottle pressure, checked the light, checked the knife in its pocket on the casing. Then I sat back on my heels in the engine room's oil-smelling dark and asked myself the question I'd been avoiding for two days.

*What am I actually preparing for?*

And here's where I have to be honest, because this is the part of the story where the mutiny happened—not on deck, not in shouting, but in me, in the small civil war between the part of me that trusts Casper Brightwater with my life (correctly, nine years of evidence) and the part of me that has buried friends who trusted somebody too.

The truth is I didn't know what I was preparing for. That's what dread is, I've decided. Dread is fear without a gauge. Give me a temperature reading and a pressure drop and I'll tell you exactly what's about to break and how long you have. But this—Rill's hunched shoulders, the crate in the flood space, the red-circled dates, the way Casper stood at the rail last night looking downstream at the water like it owed them money—this was a reading with no needle. Everything slightly wrong. Nothing measurably broken.

I rewrapped the raft. And then, because I'm not a woman who does half a job, I did two more things.

First, I went through the lazarette and moved our real emergency kit—flares, the good medical bag, water, the handheld radio Casper doesn't know I keep charged—into the engine room, where I could reach it in ten seconds from my station. If something happened at speed, I would not be running forward through a boat full of people I might have to leave.

I hated writing that sentence even in my own head. *People I might have to leave.* But that's the arithmetic of a flooding boat, and I've done the arithmetic before, on the Orinoco, in water that took two of my crewmates, and I will not be caught without it again.

Second, I started a log. Not the official one. A little notebook, the kind I use for engine hours, and on the first page I wrote the date and under it: *Trim light aft approx. 200 lbs, cause unknown. Crate fwd, unknown contents, stowed in flood space. Rill: behavior altered since departure, phone use covert. Captain: destination inconsistent with stated job. Tide dates circled, next in 4 days.*

Then I sat with the notebook open on my knee and looked at what I'd written, and it looked exactly like what it was: a list of the people I love, itemized by suspicion.

* * *

Rill came down to the engine room that afternoon, which he never does unless the air conditioner in the crew cabin is acting up or he wants to avoid Marisol.

"Hey, Dovie." He leaned against the bulkhead by the workbench—right over the wrapped raft, which I noted with a small cold satisfaction—and tried to look casual, which for Rill is like a dog trying to look like it wasn't just in the trash. "You need a hand with anything?"

"You've never once in your life volunteered for engine work."

"I'm growing."

"You're hiding. There's a difference, and it's about four feet of steel and a lot of grease." I let him stand there in the silence for a while. Silence is a tool, in an engine room especially; everything down here is rhythm, and a human being who's lying can't find the rhythm. "Rill. You sleeping?"

"Sure."

"That's a no. You're doing the thing where you say sure like the word costs you money." I wiped my hands, slow, giving him time. "I'm not your mother and I'm not the captain. But I've been on boats a long time, and I know what a man looks like when he's carrying something he shouldn't be. It looks exactly like you."

The color went out of his face and then came back wrong. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." I turned back to the pump I was rebuilding, because sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a young man drowning in a small way is to stop looking at him. "When you're ready to say it, say it to the captain, not to me. I just turn wrenches."

He stood there another ten seconds. I could hear him deciding not to tell me. It's a specific sound, a decision like that—like a valve seating. Then he left, and I put down the wrench and pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes and felt the mutiny in my chest advance another inch.

Because here's what I hadn't put in the notebook, what I couldn't put in the notebook: when I said *say it to the captain*, Rill flinched. Not at the idea of confessing. At the idea of Casper. And I have watched Rill around Casper for three years, and that boy would take a bullet for that captain, and a flinch like that doesn't come from shame about a personal sin.

It comes from shame about something done *to* Casper.

* * *

I found Marisol on the back deck at dusk, mending a net she didn't need to mend, which is her version of my notebook.

"Two hundred pounds light by the stern," I said, sitting down next to her without asking. On the *Grace*, Marisol and I don't ask.

She didn't look up from the net. "I noticed Tuesday. Thought it was the water load."

"It's not the water load. I checked the water load. There's also a crate in the forward flood space that I didn't put there and I'm betting you didn't either."

That got her. Her hands stopped. "You opened it?"

"I looked at it. I didn't open it." I watched the water go by, brown and patient. "Here's what's eating me, Mari. If it was Casper's, Casper would've told me. Casper tells me everything that touches my boat—my engines, my trim, my weight distribution. So either Casper's changed in nine years, or it's not Casper's."

"Rill."

"Rill," I agreed. "And Rill won't look at the captain, and the captain's got red circles on the tide table, and nobody will say the word *Meridian* out loud even though the journalist in town said it to you loud enough that you came back to the boat looking like you'd seen a ghost with a press badge."

She set the net down. For a while she didn't say anything, and I let the river say it for both of us.

"Dovie," she finally said, "if I knew something, and I'm not saying I do, and I'm not saying it's mine to tell—what would you want me to do with it?"

"I'd want you to tell me whether we're in danger."

"That's not the same as telling you the thing."

"No," I said. "It's the only thing that matters."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she does when she's weighing a person, and whatever she saw made her jaw go tight. "We might be. I don't know how much. Ask me again in a day or two."

"A day or two," I repeated. "The tide's in four."

"I know when the tide is, Dovie."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I went down and stood my own unofficial watch in the engine room, which is where I do my best praying, which is to say my best swearing, which for me is the same discipline.

And around two in the morning, I heard footsteps overhead. Soft ones. Going forward, not aft. I gave it a minute and climbed the ladder quiet as a bilge rat and looked down the length of the deck, and there was Rill, crouched at the bow hatch, and the hatch was open, and the moon was enough to show me what he was doing.

He wasn't taking anything out of the crate.

He was putting something in. A flat, dark shape, wrapped in cloth, laid down with the kind of care you use on something either precious or damning. Then he closed the hatch, wiped his hands on his jeans, and stood there a moment with his head bowed like a man at a graveside.

I withdrew before he turned. I went back down my ladder and sat on my stool in the dark among my engines, and I made myself say the plain thing, the thing I'd been dressing up in procedure and lists and raft maintenance for two days:

Somebody on this boat is betraying somebody on this boat, and I don't know who, and I don't know how, and the tide comes in four days, and the water is going to be high and fast and full of places to die.

I got out my notebook. Under the last line I wrote: *Rill visited crate, 0200, added item, unknown. Behavior consistent with guilt, not malice. But guilt steers badly.*

Then, because I'm an honest woman even in a secret book, I added a line I didn't want to write:

*Consider: whatever Rill is carrying, Casper is carrying something bigger. Two secrets on one small boat, and the river rising. If they touch each other out there, no raft I own will be big enough.*

I closed the book. I checked the raft's painter one more time, though I'd checked it that morning, because that's who I am: the woman who checks twice, who keeps the third raft, who loves people and prepares for the day loving them isn't enough.

Up on deck, faint through the deck plates, I heard the wheelhouse door slide, and footsteps cross to the rail, and I knew without looking that it was Casper, standing the kind of watch you can't log, looking downstream at the dark water.

Four days to the circled tide.

And somewhere behind us, or ahead of us—I couldn't tell which, and that was the worst of it—something was already moving toward this boat, patient as the river, and I had the engineer's worst feeling in the world: that everything was running fine, and that this was exactly the moment to worry.
