## 14. Coordinates for Sale

There are three kinds of people who sell other people out, and I've bought from all of them.

The first kind sells because they're greedy. Easy to handle. You pay them once and they're yours forever, because greed is a leash the dog holds in its own mouth. The second kind sells because they're scared. Harder. Fear is a renewable resource but it burns hot and unpredictable, and a frightened seller will just as easily sell you as sell on you. The third kind—and this is the one that keeps salvage scouts like me up at night—sells because they love someone, or something, more than they love the truth. Those ones you can't buy. You can only break them, and breaking them costs more than the cargo's usually worth.

I sat in the survey barge's cramped office with my laptop open and a cup of coffee that had gone the color of river silt, and I decided that Rill Okafor was probably the third kind. Which meant trouble. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

The message came in at 9:47 p.m., which told me something already. Amateurs text at dinner. Professionals text during business hours so the transaction looks boring. Guilty people text late at night, when the house is quiet and the shame has room to stretch its legs. The photos arrived in a burst of four—crappy shots taken under bad light, angled like the photographer was afraid the camera itself would tattle: a hand-drawn chart of the lower Kestrel, a tide table for the coming fortnight, a page of Casper Brightwater's own handwriting with depth soundings, and a close-up of a circled point on the river's eastern channel where the ink had been pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

No note. No demand for money. Just the images and then, eleven minutes later, a single line:

*You didn't get these from me.*

I set down the coffee and laughed, alone, in a steel box on a barge full of sleeping men who thought we were in the business of dredging permits. *You didn't get these from me.* As if deniability were a garment you could hand someone else to wear. As if I hadn't spent twenty years in this trade learning exactly how much a sentence like that weighs—which is nothing, which is less than nothing, which is the sound a conscience makes when it's trying to negotiate.

But I liked the kid for it anyway. There was a decency buried in there somewhere, struggling to get out like a crab under a net. Decent people make the best informants precisely because they hate doing it. They don't double-cross you later. They just quietly fall apart, and falling-apart people are easy to steer.

* * *

Let me back up, because you deserve to understand what I understood, sitting there at 9:58 p.m. with a dead man's river chart glowing on my screen.

Vesper Kline, salvage scout, Kestrel River Salvage Consortium, third-generation wrecker on my mother's side and proud of exactly none of it. My job is simple to describe and miserable to do: find things worth taking before anyone else knows they exist, and secure the legal right to take them. I am very good at my job. I say this without vanity, the way a knife doesn't brag about being sharp. It's just what I'm for.

The Meridian had been my white whale for six years—not because of the legend, though legends are useful currency in my line, but because of the paper trail. The expedition's insurance settlement had been paid out suspiciously fast in 1971, against suspiciously thin evidence, to a sponsor company that folded two years later with its directors mysteriously solvent. I'd read the old filings the way other people read novels. Somewhere under that river—or wherever the Meridian actually died—was documentation that would either clear the record or hang somebody's grandfather. Either way, it was worth seven figures to the right buyer, and I knew three of them by their first names.

The problem was the river itself. The Kestrel doesn't give up its dead; it relocates them. Channels migrate. Bars build and dissolve like arguments. Every chart older than five years is a work of historical fiction. So when the consortium sent me upriver with a survey barge, a crew of twelve, and a budget that would embarrass a small nation, I did what I always do first: I stopped looking at the water and started looking at the people.

Towns along rivers are gossip economies. Information is the only commodity that appreciates. Within four days I knew which dockhand drank, which harbormaster took envelopes, which café waitress had opinions about everyone. And I knew about Casper Brightwater—or rather, I knew about the shape of an absence where Casper Brightwater should have been. Twenty years on the river and no fixed address, no history anyone could agree on, no photographs, no debts, no ex-spouses, no nothing. In my experience, people without pasts aren't mysterious. They're hiding. And people who hide usually know where the bodies—or boats—are.

So I'd been watching them. Watching the way they walked the dock at low water like a farmer checking fence lines. Watching them buy tide tables—actual printed tables, in a town where every phone had the data for free, which meant they wanted the annotations, the marginalia, the local corrections no app carries. Watching them ask, very casually, about spring tides.

And now here were their own charts, photographed by their own deckhand, showing a circled point on the eastern channel that matched nothing on any chart I owned.

* * *

I cross-referenced for two hours. This is the part of my job I actually love—the quiet forensic pleasure of it, laying documents side by side until they confess.

First, the coordinates themselves. The circled point sat off the eastern channel near a stretch called the Gullet, where the river braided through salt marsh before meeting the sea. Nothing marked there. No obstruction, no wreck symbol, no depth anomaly on the consortium's own sonar runs—which had covered that exact water twice. That should have told me the point was worthless. Instead it told me the opposite, because of the second document: the tide table.

Rill had photographed the wrong page. Or the right one—I still haven't decided if the kid was sloppy or secretly brilliant. It was the table for nine days out, the spring tide, and someone—Casper, in that tight, slanted handwriting—had written calculations in the margin. Times. Heights. A window bracketed in pencil: roughly ninety minutes, give or take, when the water would stand higher than any other moment that month.

Higher water. An unmarked location. A specific, narrow window when the location might be *reachable*.

I pulled up the bathymetric surveys again and looked at the Gullet with new eyes. And there it was—or rather, there it wasn't. The eastern channel ran deep, but just off it, behind a marsh point, the bottom rose into a shallow shelf that our sonar had logged as featureless mud. Featureless mud at low water. But add three meters of spring tide, and that shelf became something else entirely: cover. A drowned place that only existed, that was only *enterable*, when the river stood at its highest.

A cave. Or a cut, or a sink, or some hollow in the old shoreline that the charts had never recorded because no chartmaker had ever been there at the right hour with the right water. The kind of place a boat could hide in for fifty years while the whole world looked in the wrong channel.

The Meridian hadn't sunk. The Meridian had been *put away*.

I sat back and let that move through me, cold and clean as river water in February. Someone had known. Someone had always known. And that someone was currently feeding me a friendly smile across the fuel dock while offering to "guide" our barge upriver—for a fee, naturally—along a route they'd sketched out with great confidence and, I now suspected, great fiction.

Because here was the arithmetic that finished it. Nine days to the spring tide. Our barge, working the channels methodically north to south as our permits required, would reach the Gullet in eleven days. Two days too late. Unless, of course, we accepted Brightwater's generous offer to lead us up the western slough first—"good holding ground, likely debris field, worth a look"—which would cost us, by my calculation, almost exactly those two days.

They weren't guiding us. They were herding us. Buying time with our own momentum, spending our schedule like it was theirs, and doing it all with such pleasant competence that I'd actually thanked them. I'd shaken their hand over the deal. I could still feel the handshake—dry, firm, brief. The handshake of someone who had practiced not leaving fingerprints.

Old Tam's tricks. That was the phrase that surfaced, unbidden, from the research file in my head. Because I'd read everything ever written about Old Tam Voss, the legendary river pilot, and half of it was anecdote: how he could make a rival's boat lose a day without either captain knowing why; how he once talked a competitor into dredging a sandbar that didn't exist; how the man was a magician whose stage was other people's schedules. Brightwater had apprenticed under him. It showed. It showed like a family resemblance.

I should have been furious. Part of me was—a small, professional part that resents being played the way a chess master resents a pawn fork. But mostly what I felt was something I don't admit to often: respect. Cold, delighted, slightly alarmed respect. Casper Brightwater had looked at a consortium with a barge, a budget, and a battery of lawyers, and had chosen not to run, not to hide, but to *steer*. To make our own weight our weakness. That's not desperation. That's mastery.

It also meant they'd go for the wreck inside that ninety-minute window, nine days out, whether I approved or not. And whatever was in that strongbox of a cave—whatever proof, whatever paper, whatever ghost—they'd surface it and control the story before I could draft a single press release.

Unless I moved first.

* * *

Here's the thing about traps: the good ones aren't cruel. Cruelty is loud, and loud things warn people off. The good ones are kind. They offer exactly what the target wants most, wrapped in exactly the justification the target needs, so that walking in feels like wisdom.

I opened a fresh message thread to Rill Okafor's number and thought about the kid for a long moment. Twenty-three, maybe twenty-four. Deck hands don't earn enough to have debts, and yet Rill had the particular flinch of someone carrying numbers that grew faster than paychecks did. I'd seen the phone-bill anxiety, the way the kid checked messages like checking for verdicts. Somebody held paper on Rill Okafor. Somebody patient and predatory.

Which made Rill perfect. Not because the kid was weak—because the kid was *load-bearing*. Betray a loyal crew member and you learn nothing. Turn a guilty one, and you turn the whole structure they're standing in.

I typed:

*Rill. Vesper Kline. Received your photos — thank you. Before anything else: I want you to know I recognize what this cost you, and I don't think less of you for it. People in debt aren't traitors. They're just people with fewer doors.*

Then I deleted it, because flattery that obvious insults everyone involved. I tried again.

*Got your photos. Good work. The coordinates check out against our sonar — you've earned the balance of the finder's fee. One condition: I need the interior layout. Depths inside the cave mouth, the approach, anything Brightwater has annotated beyond what you sent. Meet me aboard the barge tomorrow, 1400, berth two, and we'll settle everything in person — cash, and the debt conversation stays between us.*

I read it three times. It was good. It offered money (what Rill needed), secrecy (what Rill feared losing), absolution (what Rill craved), and urgency (so there'd be no time to talk to anyone wiser). The meeting aboard the barge served triple duty: it got the kid away from Brightwater's influence, it put Rill physically inside my jurisdiction where conversations could be... extended, and it signaled trust, which is the most disorienting thing you can hand a guilty conscience.

I hit send at 11:31 p.m. and watched the little delivery arrow appear.

Then I did the rest of the night's work, because a trap with one door is a cage, and a trap with three doors is a plan.

Door one: I drafted a revised survey order rerouting the barge's official grid—on paper—to prioritize the western slough, matching the route Brightwater had sold us. If the kid confessed everything tomorrow, the paperwork would show we'd swallowed the false route whole. Innocence, documented.

Door two: I emailed our charter contact in the city and quietly booked two dive-certified contractors and a shallow-water ROV, arrival date eight days out, billed as "geotechnical support." The consortium's board thought we were dredging. The board would continue thinking that until there was something worth telling them, because boards are weather vanes and I don't share wind readings.

Door three—the one I lingered over—I wrote a single paragraph and saved it encrypted, addressed to no one yet: a summary of the embezzlement evidence I believed waited in that cave, framed as a consortium press release, dated nine days out. Not to send. To be ready. Because whoever surfaces the Meridian's story first owns it, and I had spent six years learning that story's every wrinkle while Brightwater had spent, what, three weeks? Let them find the wreck. Let them even open the logbook. Stories belong to whoever tells them best, and I had been rehearsing mine since before Casper Brightwater found a nameplate in the dark.

* * *

The reply came at 6:12 a.m., which meant the kid hadn't slept. I understood that completely. Guilt is a night-shift worker; it clocks in when the sun goes down and does all its heavy lifting in the dark.

*OK. Tomorrow 1400. Berth two. Just layout, right? Nothing else?*

*Nothing else,* I wrote. *You have my word.*

And here I'll confess something, since we've come this far together: I meant it, in the narrow sense. All I wanted from Rill Okafor was the layout, the last unknowns, the interior geometry that would let our divers move fast when the window opened. I wasn't going to hurt the kid. I wasn't even going to expose them—not unless exposure became useful. I keep my word the way a river keeps its channel: technically, and only until the flood comes.

I stood on the barge's deck with my coffee and watched dawn come up gray over the Kestrel, gulls arguing over something dead, the water running brown and patient toward the sea. Somewhere upriver, Casper Brightwater was probably doing the same thing—standing on a deck, watching the same water, counting the same days. We were playing the same game now, they and I, and the ugly truth was that I was enjoying it. It's rare, in my line, to meet someone worth the effort. Most targets are doors; you just find the lock. Brightwater was a river. You don't pick a river's lock. You have to read it, respect it, and strike exactly where it bends.

Nine days to the spring tide. Eight to my divers. One day to Rill's visit, after which I'd hold the map to the treasure and the leash to the traitor, and Casper Brightwater's beautiful, careful deception would become a museum piece—admirable, studied, obsolete.

At 6:40 a.m., my phone buzzed. Not Rill.

A news alert. Freelance byline, Theo Anand, picked up by a coastal wire service: *"Lost Expedition Vessel Meridian — Location 'Narrowing,' Sources Say."*

I read it twice, and the coffee went cold in my hand, and I started laughing again, alone on the deck, because of course. Of course there was a journalist. There's always a journalist. And a journalist with a deadline doesn't care about my nine-day clock or Brightwater's ninety-minute window—a journalist is a lit match, and we were all of us, every one of us on this river, standing in a dry field.

The tide tables said nine days. The storm systems rolling in off the coast said maybe less. And now the whole world was about to start asking questions none of us could put back in the bottle.

I went below, woke my dive contractors' contact, and moved the arrival up three days.

The noose doesn't announce itself. It just quietly finishes its knot while everyone's still admiring the rope.
