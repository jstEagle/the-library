## 15. The Run to the Cave

The fog came in off the estuary an hour before the tide did, which was the river's way of telling you it had opinions about your plans.

I stood on the deck of the *Kestrel Queen* at four in the morning with a mug of coffee going cold in my hand and watched the world erase itself. The dock lights went first, then the tree line, then the far bank entirely, until there was nothing left but the boat and the sound of water moving the way I'd asked it to. Somewhere out in that gray nothing was a sandbar that hadn't existed on Tuesday. Somewhere beyond that was a cave mouth that would open for exactly ninety minutes, and then close for another month, and then maybe never again if the storm chewing its way up the coast arrived early and rearranged the whole delta like a child dumping a puzzle box.

"Barometer's falling," Dovie said from the engine hatch, her voice carrying that particular flatness she reserved for facts she expected me to ignore. "Falling fast."

"I heard."

"You didn't *hear* anything. You were standing out here doing your brooding captain thing."

"That's not a thing."

"It's absolutely a thing. Tam used to do it. You do it. It's hereditary." She wiped her hands on a rag that had been black since before either of us owned it. "Engine's ready. She'll give you everything she's got, but she's going to complain about it, and so am I."

"Noted and overruled."

Dovie grunted—the sound of a woman filing something away for later—and disappeared back into the engine room. That was the arrangement between us: she told me the truth about machinery, I told her the truth about navigation, and we both pretended the other truths on this boat didn't exist. Rill's empty bunk. Marisol's silence at dinner. The charts I kept rolled up and weighted under my arm like a man carrying a confession.

I unrolled them now, though I didn't need to. I could have drawn that channel blindfolded, which was fortunate, because in forty minutes I might be navigating it blindfolded.

* * *

Marisol came up the companionway at half past four without a word, took the lead line off its peg, and coiled it over her shoulder the way she'd done a thousand times. Then she looked at me across the fog-wet deck.

"Theo called again," she said. "Twice. Left messages."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you were running a survey job upriver and wouldn't be back till Thursday." She paused. "It's even true, if you squint."

"Squinting is how I've survived twenty years on this river."

"Casper." She said my name the way she only did when she meant business—flat, no warmth in it, all weight. "Last night you showed me letters written by a dead man who wasn't dead. This morning we're stealing a wreck out from under a consortium with lawyers. And you're standing there making jokes."

"I'm standing here making jokes *because* of those things. It's load-bearing humor, Mari. Take it out and the whole structure comes down."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she hefted the lead line and nodded toward the bow. "Then let's go commit some crimes before the fog lifts and I come to my senses."

We cast off at 4:47. No lights. No radio traffic. Just the *Kestrel Queen* sliding off the dock into a world the color of wet ash, with Dovie bringing the engine up to a low, muttering idle, and the tide beginning its long pull beneath us, dragging the whole river seaward like a hand drawing back a curtain.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fog on a tidal river: it doesn't hide the danger. It hides the *edges*. On a clear day you can see the sandbar forming, see the water going glassy and wrong over it, see the current line kinking where deep water meets shallow. In fog, all of that information arrives through your feet instead of your eyes—a change in the boat's vibration, a hesitation in the wake, a feeling like the river has stopped breathing underneath you. By the time your feet tell you, you're usually already on top of it.

Which is why I had Marisol on the bow with the lead line, calling depths into the gray like a woman reading scripture.

"Six fathoms."

"Mark six," I acknowledged, and turned the wheel two degrees.

"Five and a quarter."

"Mark five and a quarter." Two more degrees. The current here ran hard against us on the ebb, and the channel bent around a point that the charts—my charts, the ones I'd spent nine days redrawing from memory and soundings and one illegal night of exploration—showed as a lazy S. Rivers don't draw lazy S's. Rivers draw lies. Every bend in that channel was a place where the current had changed its mind recently enough that no government surveyor had caught it.

"Four fathoms," Marisol called. "And there's debris. Log, port side."

"I see it." I didn't see it. I heard it—a soft knocking somewhere in the fog, wood against wood—and put the wheel over to starboard and trusted the sound. Twenty years of this. Twenty years of trusting sounds.

Dovie's voice came up the engine tube. "You want me to say it, or are you going to make me hold it in?"

"Say what?"

"That we're running this channel at flood tide, in fog, ahead of a storm, to beat a barge full of people with better equipment than ours, based on a route you memorized during one night run nine days ago."

"That about covers it."

"Just checking we're all agreed on the level of sanity involved." A pause, and then, quieter, through the tube, in the voice she saved for things that mattered: "Tam would've loved this."

That landed harder than she probably intended. I gripped the wheel and said nothing, and the *Kestrel Queen* carried us both forward through the gray.

Because that was the thing I hadn't said out loud to anyone—not to Dovie, not to Marisol, not to Ada Voss in her kitchen upstream with her brother's photograph on the wall. The thing was: I knew exactly why Tam had run. Not the embezzlement testimony, not the faked death, not the sponsor who'd drowned thirty people in a lie and called it weather. Those were reasons. But the *reason*, the one under the reasons—I knew it now, felt it in my hands on this wheel, in the grin I couldn't keep off my face while my stomach tried to climb out through my ribs. He'd run because once you've been truly alive on the water, in the bad water, the water that's trying to kill you and almost managing it—everything on land tastes like paper.

He hadn't faked his death to escape the sea. He'd faked his death because the sea was the only place he'd ever felt real, and the Meridian had made that impossible everywhere else.

I was doing the same thing, I realized. I just hadn't needed to die first.

* * *

The channel narrowed at the old oyster stakes, and the fog thickened until Marisol's voice seemed to come from inside my own skull.

"Three fathoms. Three and a *half*—" Her voice lifted. "Casper, the bar's moved. It's not where your chart says."

"How far off?"

"Fifty meters? Maybe less. It's built out past the second stake."

Of course it had. Nine days of ebb tides dragging sand off the point, and the bar had grown a new finger reaching straight across the channel like a hand testing a doorknob. On my chart it was open water. Under our keel, in about ninety seconds, it would be a very expensive noise.

"Dovie," I said into the tube. "I need everything she has, right now, and I need you to not ask why."

"She's already there," Dovie said, and the engine note climbed from a mutter to a snarl, and the deck began to thrum through my boots like a struck string.

There's a maneuver for this. There's always a maneuver; the river keeps a library of them and charges admission in hull plating. You can't go around a bar that spans the channel, and you can't go over it at displacement speed, so you do the thing every river pilot swears they'll never do and does anyway: you put the bow at the shallowest spot, you pour on power, and you surf the boat up the bar's shoulder and down the back side before she has time to think about grounding. It works because a planing hull rides on water, not in it. It fails because sometimes the water isn't deep enough on the other side, and then you find out at fourteen knots.

"Marisol!" I shouted. "When I say now, swing that lead and tell me the moment you feel bottom!"

"Now?"

"Not yet!"

The fog tore past. The stakes whipped by, white scarves of mist streaming off them. Somewhere behind us, faint as a memory, I thought I heard a horn—a barge horn, low and patient—but I couldn't afford to believe in things I couldn't see, so I filed it away and kept my eyes on nothing.

"NOW—"

The line sang out in her hands. "Two fathoms—one and three-quarters—"

Full power. The bow rose. For one long, impossible second the *Kestrel Queen* was flying—actually flying, propeller biting thin water, spray coming over the rails in sheets, the whole boat trembling like a living thing asked to be more than it was—

"—one fathom—"

—and then the bar's shoulder caught us, lifted us, and dropped us down the far side with a slam that rattled my teeth and sent Dovie's toolbox singing across the engine room floor. The depth gauge spun. Marisol whooped—an actual whoop, high and wild, the first honest sound I'd heard from her in a week.

"One and a half! One and three-quarters—two! TWO FATHOMS, WE'RE THROUGH!"

I eased the throttle back before my heart could finish its argument with my brain, and for a moment none of us spoke. The fog closed in behind us over the bar like a door.

"Well," Dovie said over the tube. Her voice was carefully, elaborately casual. "I've decided not to quit today."

"Appreciated."

"But I'm keeping the option open."

* * *

We came around the last point at 6:12, and the fog began to lift—not clearing, just rising, peeling up off the water in ragged layers like gauze being pulled from a wound. And there it was.

The headland. Black rock streaked white with bird lime, the pines leaning over it at the angle wind had bullied them into over centuries. And at its base, where the chart showed solid cliff, a seam of darkness—narrower than it had been nine days ago, the tide not yet high enough, the sea still holding the cave mouth shut like a fist.

"Twenty minutes to slack," Marisol said, checking her watch against the tide table I'd taped beside the wheel. "Maybe fifteen."

"We'll wait outside. Idle. Watch the water."

I brought us around and held station off the headland, engine at bare steerage, and for a few minutes the three of us just watched the seam in the rock. It widened by inches. Water that had been surging white against the cliff face began to smooth, to still, to slide inward with a sound like slow breathing. The fist opening, finger by finger.

That was when I saw the lights.

Downstream, back the way we'd come, out in the fog that hadn't fully lifted—two lights, then three, moving. Not fishing boats. Fishing boats don't run in formation, and fishing boats don't carry floodlights mounted on masts. The beams swept the water in long, methodical strokes, working the shoreline, working the points, patient as men searching a house they have a warrant for.

The consortium. Kline's barge, or her fast tender, or both. They'd found the false route sooner than I'd hoped—or never believed it at all—and they'd spent the night beating up the real channel, and now they were here, and between us and the open sea, and the tide was still rising, and the cave was still opening, and there was nowhere in the world I wanted to be except exactly where I was.

"Casper," Marisol said quietly. "Those lights—"

"I see them."

"They're between us and home."

"No," I said, and put the wheel over, and felt the old joy rise up through the fear like heat through a floorboard. "They're between us and *boring*. Dovie—all ahead. We're going in."

The *Kestrel Queen* surged toward the dark seam in the rock, and behind us the searchlights swung, and found us, and held.
