## 16. Inside the Meridian

**CASPER**

Slack water is a lie the river tells twice a day, and I've never trusted it. But there are maybe forty minutes when the cave stops breathing—when the surge goes quiet and the water inside goes clear enough to see your own hands—and we had forty minutes, and no more.

I stood on the deck of my own boat in a wetsuit I'd bought used off Dovie's cousin, holding a dive light like a candle at a séance, and I said, "Nobody goes in past the main deck. We look, we photograph, we come out. We are not treasure hunters. We are not heroes. We are two idiots with a flashlight and a deadline."

Marisol, already suited up, adjusted her mask and said, "You always give the most inspiring pre-dive speeches."

"It's a gift."

Dovie ran the compressor line and the timer from the deck, Rill was conspicuously absent—locked in the wheelhouse with instructions to touch nothing, which, given recent history, felt like the correct amount of trust, which is to say none—and the cave mouth yawned ahead of us, black water going briefly silver-green as the tide turned.

The Meridian had been in there a long time. I could feel it before I saw it, the way you feel a big animal in dark water—not a sound, exactly, but a displacement, a presence. Then my light found her.

She was lying on her port side at maybe eight meters, settled into the sand like a woman asleep on a train who'd missed her stop by sixty years. The hull was furred with barnacles and pale silt, but the lines were still there, still proud—the sweep of her, the rake of her stem. Expedition vessels have a particular posture. They're built to go where maps give up, and they carry it in their bones even after the bones give out.

I swam the length of her keel and my chest did something complicated. Twenty years I'd run cargo for people who measured worth in tonnage, and here was a ship that had been worth something else entirely, rotting in the dark because the world above had decided she'd never existed.

The nameplate was still legible. MERIDIAN, in flaked gilt. I put my glove against it, just for a second. Tam had stood on this deck. Tam had stood on this deck and then he had walked off it, into a new name, a new river, a new life, and he had never once—not in twenty years of card games and engine grease and the particular silence of two people who don't talk about themselves—given me a single true sentence about it.

I was angry about that. I want to be honest about that before I tell you what we found, because the finding changed the anger, and I don't want the record to show I went down there forgiving anybody.

Marisol tapped my shoulder and pointed. The main hatch had collapsed inward at one corner, leaving a gap a slim person could enter. She looked at me. I looked at the gap. The gap looked like a bad idea.

I went in anyway. So did she. I told you we weren't heroes. I never said we weren't idiots.

* * *

**MARISOL**

Inside the wreck, the water was the color of weak tea and the light didn't so much travel as suggest. Casper went first, which I knew meant something, because Casper Brightwater does not go first for anybody unless going first is the only way to keep the rest of us from doing something worse.

The main cabin was a cathedral of silt. Deck chairs lay on the bulkhead like they'd been thrown there by a very tidy storm. There were dishes. There were dishes, forty years old, still stacked in a rack, and something about that undid me a little—somebody had washed up after dinner on the last ordinary night, and then the extraordinary night came, and nobody ever put those dishes away.

Casper's light swept across a chart table, a collapsed berth, a door with a brass plate: CAPTAIN. Then a smaller door: NAVIGATOR.

Casper stopped in front of that one the way you stop in front of a grave you're not sure you have the right to visit.

I'd known Tam since I was nineteen. He taught me to read water the way other people taught me to read, and he told stories—river stories, mostly, but once, drunk on one glass of wine because Tam never had two, he'd mentioned a woman named Ada and then changed the subject so hard I nearly got whiplash. I'd spent two weeks thinking about that name since Marisol-the-curious started asking around, and now I was floating in the navigator's cabin of the ship that name belonged to, and the whole shape of Tam's life was rearranging itself around me like furniture in the dark.

Casper's light found a desk drawer, half-open, silt fanning out of it like a frozen breath. Inside: a canvas satchel, tarred, the kind rivermen use for papers. Casper worked it free with hands I could see shaking even through the gloves.

We surfaced into the air pocket beneath the collapsed deck—a pocket of trapped air, stale and mineral, forty years old. Casper pulled back a mask and breathed it anyway, which was either very romantic or very stupid, and I followed, because when in Rome.

Casper opened the satchel right there, in the dark, in the lungs of the ship.

* * *

**CASPER**

The logbook was wrapped in oilcloth and it was dry. Dry, after forty years in salt water, because Tam had wrapped it the way Tam did everything—thoroughly, obsessively, like a man who expected the world to try to take things from him.

I opened it with hands that would not cooperate.

The early pages were ordinary. Soundings, courses, weather in a neat hand I recognized with a jolt—Tam's hand, younger, more careful, the letters not yet worn down to the shorthand of an old man's pen. *Held station off the light. Fog. The sponsor's man complains about the delay. The sponsor's man complains about everything.*

Then the entries changed.

*The sponsor's man came aboard at Port Callow with crates marked "survey instruments." They are not survey instruments. I helped carry them. I know the weight of survey instruments and I know the weight of money, and this was the weight of money.*

*Counted the crates again. The sponsor has been reporting losses to the underwriters—vessels lost, cargo lost, expeditions failed. The Meridian is to be "lost" on the home leg. I have seen the chart he keeps in his cabin. The wreck position is already marked. The ink is older than the voyage.*

I stopped. In the air pocket, my own breathing sounded enormous.

Marisol's light came up over my shoulder. She read in silence. I heard her breath catch at the same line I'd caught on.

The entries went on. Tam had copied figures—disbursements, insurance claims, the sponsor's signature on documents dated before things they certified. He'd copied the sponsor's private chart, the one with the wreck position pre-drawn. He'd written, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages: *If the Meridian sinks as planned, I will not be aboard. I will not be a dead man in a story someone else is telling. Let them lose their ship. I am keeping the proof.*

The last entry was dated the night the Meridian came into this coast in a storm that was, according to the log, real—realer than planned, the sea not cooperating with the embezzlement, the wreck happening for true, just in the wrong place, in a cave the sponsor's charts didn't know existed. Tam had been in the ship's boat when the Meridian drove in here. He'd watched her go down from the water. And then, instead of reporting home, he had walked inland, found a river, and become Old Tam of the Kestrel—because a dead navigator is a loose end, and loose ends get tidied, and the only way to testify was to disappear and keep the testimony.

He hadn't faked his death out of shame. He'd faked it out of *witness*. He'd carried this book for forty years the way you carry a coal that won't go out, and he'd never told me, never told anyone, because the man he was hiding from had money, and reach, and patience.

And the sponsor's name was in the book. Over and over. Halloran Maritime Trust. A name I knew—not from river gossip but from the consortium's paperwork, from the permits Vesper Kline carried, from the letterhead of the people currently steaming up the coast to buy this cave out from under the truth.

The Meridian's sponsor and the salvage consortium's money were the same money. Had always been the same money. They weren't hunting a wreck. They were hunting a witness, and they'd finally found him, forty years too late, and he was dead, and the only thing left of him was a tarred satchel in the hands of a riverboat captain who'd spent twenty years being nobody on purpose.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me in the trapped air of that cabin like a bubble finding the surface—harsh, wet, half grief. Marisol put a hand on my shoulder through the neoprene and didn't ask, which is why I'd have followed her into any wreck on any coast.

"He wasn't hiding from himself," I said, and my voice bounced around the dead ship. "He was hiding *for* something. He was the last witness and he knew it."

* * *

**MARISOL**

Casper cried, a little, in the air pocket, and pretended not to, and I pretended not to notice, and that is the deepest kind of intimacy I know.

We photographed everything—the log, the crates in the hold, the sponsor's chart in the captain's cabin, which was exactly where Tam said it would be, because Tam was never wrong about water or paper. The flash strobes turned the cabin into a series of frozen lightning storms. I kept thinking about the dishes. I kept thinking about a nineteen-year-old girl learning knots from a man who was quietly carrying the evidence that could have burned down an empire, and who chose, every single day, not to burn it—because he'd seen what the fire did, and he wanted to live long enough to hand it to someone who could aim it.

He'd handed it to Casper. He just hadn't lived long enough to explain the handing.

On the way out, my light caught something wedged behind the navigator's bunk: a second satchel, smaller, and inside it, wrapped in waxed cloth, a photograph—the woman from Casper's strongbox, younger, standing on the Meridian's deck, laughing, her arm around a young man with Tam's eyes. On the back, in that careful pre-Kestrel hand: *Ada. Last day of the good part. —T.*

He'd been carrying her too. All those years on the river, he'd been carrying all of them.

We came up the anchor line into grey afternoon light and Dovie's worried face, and the world was still there, and the storm was still coming, and somewhere downstream the consortium's lights would be moving. Casper held the satchel against their chest like a rescued child and said, "We need to get this above the waterline. All of it. Copies, witnesses, everything."

And that was when Dovie said the thing that turned my blood cold:

"Then you'd better see what Rill's been doing on the radio."

* * *

**CASPER**

There are moments when the river hands you everything at once—truth, storm, and betrayal in the same current—and you learn what you're made of by whether you drop any of it.

I climbed aboard holding forty years of Tam's testimony, and I looked at Dovie's face, and I understood that the forty minutes of slack water had cost us more than time.

"What did Rill do," I said. It wasn't a question. I was out of questions. I was down to weather.

Dovie handed me the radio log, and the coordinates written in Rill's hand were already in it, already sent, already gone.

The tide was turning. The cave was starting to breathe again behind us, swallowing the Meridian back into the dark—but not all of her. Not anymore. We had the logbook, and the storm was coming, and downstream, right on schedule, the lights of the consortium's barge were coming up the channel like a fever.

They were coming for the wreck.

They had no idea they were really coming for the witness.

I intended to introduce them.
