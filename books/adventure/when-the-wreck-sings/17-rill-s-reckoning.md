## 17. Rill's Reckoning

The thing about guilt is that it doesn't knock. It just moves in, unpacks its bags, and starts rearranging your furniture while you sleep.

I'd been carrying mine for six days by the time the *Kestrel Queen* — that's Kline's barge, all chrome railings and bad intentions — sent a launch for me at the Grayling dock. The message came through Marisol's cousin's bait shop, which is how everything official comes through in a river town: sideways, damp, and smelling faintly of minnows.

"Mr. Kline requests your presence aboard," said the man piloting the launch. He had the flat voice of someone paid to say sentences he didn't write.

"Requests," I repeated. "That's a funny word for it."

He didn't laugh. Nobody on that barge laughed. I'd noticed that the day we'd guided them up the dead slough — Casper's beautiful, lying detour through water barely deep enough to float a rumor — and I'd told myself their grimness was seasickness. It wasn't. It was purpose. There is nothing more frightening than people who know exactly what they want, except people who know exactly what you've done.

Here's what I'd done, in case you're keeping score, because I certainly was. Six nights ago, alone in the galley of Casper's boat with my debts breathing down my neck like a bad roommate, I photographed the charts. The cave coordinates. The tide windows. All of it. And I texted it to a number Vesper Kline had slipped me back when they were still pretending to be a friendly salvage scout buying rounds at the Anchor Line.

Then I deleted nothing. Confessed nothing. Slept badly, ate worse, and flinched every time Casper looked at me, which was often, because captains look at their crew the way doctors look at patients — constantly, professionally, and with a running diagnosis they don't share.

The launch puttered us out past the breakwater. The *Kestrel Queen* sat at anchor like a wedding cake that had learned to hate. Two decks of floodlights, a crane on the stern big enough to lift a small church, and divers' gear racked along the rail like weapons. They'd been busy since the slough humiliation. Busy and patient. That combination should have scared me more than it did.

Vesper Kline met me at the gangway personally. That was my first warning. Important people don't meet nobodies at gangways unless the nobody is about to stop being one.

"Rill." They smiled the way ice smiles. "Come in. Can I get you something? Coffee? Tea? A conscience?"

"I'm fine."

"I doubt that." They led me into a cabin that smelled of new upholstery and old money, all teak veneer over steel, and gestured me into a chair across from a table that held exactly two things: a tablet, face up, and a checkbook. "Sit. Please. This won't take long, and then we can both stop pretending."

I sat. My hands wanted to be anywhere else, so I put them under my thighs like a kid at a school assembly.

Kline turned the tablet toward me.

My texts. All of them. The chart photos, timestamped, geotagged, laid out in a neat little gallery like evidence at a trial, which is precisely what they were. My thumb hovering over send in the first frame — no, wait, that wasn't possible, they couldn't have — but there it was anyway, or something close enough, some screenshot or reconstruction that made it look like they'd watched me do it live. Maybe they had. Maybe Kline had people everywhere, or maybe the tablet was theater. With Kline, I was learning, the difference between truth and performance mattered less than the effect.

"You have questions," Kline said.

"Nope."

"They always start with nope." They leaned back, folding their hands. "Let me tell you a story instead. Once upon a time, a riverboat captain named Casper Brightwater led my survey barge up a slough that appears on no functioning chart as a navigable channel. We lost two days and a propeller blade. Do you know what that cost me?"

"A propeller blade?"

"Funny." Kline didn't smile. "It cost me credibility with my board, four days of tide window, and roughly the price of this vessel's annual insurance premium. So I did what any reasonable person does. I stopped trusting the charming ones and started trusting the frightened ones. You were the frightened one, Rill. You've been frightened since the day you signed on. Debts do that. I know. I used to have them."

"I don't know what you—"

"Please." They tapped the tablet. "You sold my coordinates to me. Which means Casper gave you false ones, or true ones, and either way you're now the only person on this river who knows which. So here's my offer, and I'll only make it once."

They slid the checkbook forward. Opened it. Wrote a number.

I'm not going to tell you the number. Not because I'm protecting anyone — God knows I'd already failed at that — but because the number isn't the point. The point is that it was enough. Enough to erase every debt I owed, every name I owed it to, every 3 a.m. calculation of how many months before somebody broke my fingers or my engine or both. It was the number I'd been dreaming about since before I ever heard the word *Meridian*, and it was sitting three feet away from me in Kline's handwriting.

"In exchange," Kline said, "you confirm the coordinates are genuine, and you give me the interior layout of the cave. Approach angles, slack-water timing, where the wreck sits relative to the entrance. Everything Casper knows and hasn't shared."

"And if I don't?"

Kline's expression didn't change, which was somehow worse than if it had. "Then you stay aboard until we conclude our business. As a guest. Guests can't warn anyone."

"That's kidnapping."

"That's maritime law, interpreted generously, by lawyers I pay to interpret generously." They clicked the pen shut. "You have until we weigh anchor. Which is soon. There's a storm coming, Rill, and storms make tides generous. I intend to be inside that cave when the water is."

* * *

Here's the part I need to be honest about, because the whole rest of this story depends on it: for about ninety seconds, I considered saying yes.

Ninety seconds. I counted them afterward, the way you count anything you're ashamed of. Ninety seconds of looking at that check and doing the arithmetic of my own cowardice. The debts would vanish. The threats would evaporate. I could walk off this barge a free man and never think about the Meridian again except as a story I'd helped steal, which — let's be real — nobody goes to prison for. Casper would lose the wreck, maybe lose the boat, definitely lose the fight, but Casper was always going to lose fights; that's what happens when you bring integrity to a gunfight. And I would be whole. Paid-off, debt-free, intact.

Ninety seconds.

What broke the spell wasn't heroism. I want to be clear about that. It wasn't some sudden rush of loyalty or a vision of Casper's face or the memory of Marisol teaching me to splice line on a cold morning while she hummed off-key. It was smaller and uglier than all of that.

It was Ada Voss.

No — wait. Let me back up. I never met Ada Voss. But Casper talked about her, or around her, in the way people talk about things that matter too much to name directly. One night on watch, half-asleep against the wheel housing, Casper said — to me, or to the river, I still don't know — *"Tam spent twenty years being nobody so his sister wouldn't spend twenty years being the sister of a liar. That's not cowardice. That's the most expensive gift I've ever seen anybody give."*

And here I was, twenty-six years old, three debts deep, about to sell out the one person who'd taken me on with no references, no history, no questions — because Casper knew what questions cost. Casper had hired me *because* I was running from something. That was the joke, right? That was the whole rotten joke. The captain who never asked about your past had given me a berth specifically so I wouldn't have to sell it.

And I'd sold it anyway. For less than Kline was offering now, even.

"Ninety seconds," I said aloud.

Kline raised an eyebrow. "Pardon?"

"Nothing." I pushed the checkbook back across the table. "No."

"No?" For the first time, something moved behind Kline's eyes — surprise, maybe, or the recalibration of a machine encountering an input it hadn't modeled. "Rill. Be reasonable. This number doesn't change. But my patience does."

"I said no." My voice cracked on the second word, which undermined the delivery considerably, but I kept going. "You want the cave layout? Ask the river. Ask the tide. Ask literally anyone except me, because I'm done. I already did the worst thing I'll ever do, and I'm not going to do the second worst thing on top of it. One betrayal per lifetime. That's my ceiling."

Kline studied me for a long moment. Then they did something I didn't expect: they laughed. Short, genuine, almost warm.

"There it is," they said. "Shame. I wondered if you had any. Most people in your position don't, you know. It makes them predictable. You, though—" They stood, buttoning their jacket. "You're going to be a problem, aren't you?"

"I'm going to be a lot of things."

"Deckhand Okafor." They raised their voice slightly, and the cabin door opened. The launch pilot stepped in — the flat-voiced man, whose name apparently was Okafor and whose job description was apparently broader than piloting. "Our guest has declined hospitality. See that they enjoy it anyway."

"That's not— hey. *Hey.*"

Okafor took my arm with the gentle efficiency of a nurse and walked me aft, past the dive racks, past the crane, down a companionway that smelled of diesel and wet rope, to a cabin with a door that locked from the outside. There was a bunk, a porthole bolted shut, and a copy of a boating magazine from four years ago. Luxury accommodations, by hostage standards.

The lock clicked.

I sat on the bunk and listened to the engines come alive beneath me, one after another, a deep mechanical heartbeat. Through the porthole — bolted, but glass is glass — I watched the gray water begin to slide past. The *Kestrel Queen* was getting underway. Toward the cave. Toward the spring tide. Toward Casper.

Toward the storm, though Kline didn't know that yet. I did. I'd heard Dovie muttering about the barometer for two days, watching the sky go the color of an old bruise, and Dovie's weather sense was the closest thing to scripture I'd ever trusted. The storm was early. The storm was mean. And Kline was steaming straight into it with a barge too heavy to turn fast and a schedule too rigid to bend.

Which meant I had information. Me. The traitor. The guy in the locked room with the outdated boating magazine.

I lay back on the bunk and stared at the overhead and made myself a promise, and I want you to understand that I am a man who has broken every promise he's ever made to himself, so when I tell you this one held, you'll know what it cost.

I got myself into this. I was going to get myself out, and take Kline's whole operation down with me on the way.

First problem: locked cabin. Second problem: no phone — Okafor had relieved me of it at the gangway, along with my knife, my multitool, and, inexplicably, my lucky bottle cap. Third problem: I had the tactical awareness of a wet paper bag and the upper body strength to match.

Fourth problem, and this was the big one: whatever I did, I had to do it before the barge reached the bar Casper had falsified on those charts. Because Kline thought those charts were gospel. Kline had cross-referenced them, planned around them, committed to them. And somewhere ahead, under a rising storm tide, waited a sandbar that existed on paper and nowhere else — a sandbar that was about to eat this barge alive.

I needed to warn Casper. Or help Kline. No — not help Kline. Warn Casper, obviously, warn Casper, that was the whole point, focus, Rill.

But here was the thing I realized, lying there in the engine-thrumming dark: Casper didn't need my warning. Casper had built the trap. Casper knew Kline was coming, because Kline was coming on Casper's own falsified charts, following a route Casper had drawn like a fisherman chumming water. The captain wasn't fleeing the consortium. The captain was *fishing* for it.

Which meant the only person on this river who didn't know what was about to happen was the person locked in this cabin.

Me.

I got up. I tried the door — locked. I tried the porthole — bolted, but the bolts were old and the frame was older, and I'd spent two years working deck on boats held together by rust and prayer. I knew rust. Rust and I were old friends. Rust, unlike Kline, could be reasoned with.

I found the first loose bolt with my thumbnail in under a minute.

Outside, the storm light was failing, and the river was starting to sing — that low, grinding note it makes when the tide turns and the wind comes up all at once, the sound Casper called *the wreck singing*, though I hadn't understood why until now. Somewhere downstream, in that growing dark, a cave mouth was opening for ninety minutes only. And somewhere upstream, a beat-up cargo boat with a forged route plan and a crew of liars was racing to reach it first.

I put my shoulder against the porthole frame and started working the second bolt.

"Hold on, Captain," I said to nobody. "I'm coming. Late, and useless, and coming."

The bolt gave a quarter turn. The engines surged. The storm hit the windows like a fist, and the *Kestrel Queen* drove on into the dark, straight down the throat of the lie I'd drawn for her — and for the first time in six days, I wasn't afraid of what I'd done.

I was afraid I wouldn't be fast enough to undo it.
