## 18. Storm Tide

### Vesper Kline

I have been called many things in my career—vulture, shark, "that woman," and once, memorably, by a weeping shipowner in Port Calloway, *the angel of death with a clipboard*. I accept all of these. What I have never been is wrong about water.

Which is why, standing on the flying bridge of the *Iron Meridian*—yes, we named the barge after the wreck; yes, I told them it was tacky; no, they didn't listen—I knew before the barometer did that the storm had jumped its schedule.

The forecast said tomorrow night. The forecast was written by people who look at weather the way accountants look at rivers: as numbers on a page. I looked south, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise going green, and where the wind had that particular flat taste it gets when it's carrying half the ocean with it.

"Captain Okafor," I said. "Turn us around."

He didn't even look up from his coffee. "We're forty minutes from the coordinates."

"The coordinates are wrong."

"They came from your own source."

"My own source," I said, "is a twenty-two-year-old deckhand with gambling debts and the moral fiber of wet tissue paper. The coordinates are a guess wearing a suit." What I did not say—what I had known for three days and told no one, because telling would mean admitting how thoroughly I'd been played—was that Casper Brightwater had fed me those coordinates like a mother bird feeding a cuckoo. The slough run upstream. The "engine trouble." The helpful little tide tables Rill had photographed, which I had cross-referenced against the survey charts until two in the morning, when the arithmetic finally stood up and introduced itself: the cave mouth Brightwater described could not exist at the depths given. Either the river had rewritten itself, or I had been steered down a beautifully drawn dead end while the real run happened behind my back.

I had chosen not to confront. Confrontation warns the quarry. Instead I'd bought Rill's silence with a promise of full payment and a meeting aboard, set a course for the cave on my own corrected figures, and waited for the moment I could arrive first and let the law do my talking.

That was this morning. This morning, the sea had been a rumor.

Now the wind hit the barge broadside like a debt collector, and Captain Okafor finally looked up from his coffee.

"That's early," he said.

"Everything about this river is early," I said. "It's a river that lies about time." Which, I would later reflect, was also an accurate description of its most famous captain.

The first gust took the awning off the aft deck and folded it into the water like laundry. The second gust made the barge lean, all four hundred tons of her groaning in her welds. And then the tide came in—not the gentle, scheduled tide of the tables, but a storm tide, wind-driven, piling water into the estuary faster than it could drain, and the channel markers began to disappear under white water one by one, like candles being pinched out.

"Hard to starboard!" Okafor roared, and the helmsman hauled the wheel, and I grabbed the rail and watched the depth sounder with the calm of a person who has already done the math and hates the answer.

The bar Casper Brightwater had drawn onto my chart—the false shoal, the fiction, the beautiful lie—was exactly where they'd put it. Because of course it was. They hadn't invented a sandbar. They'd moved one on paper, and the storm had moved one in fact, and the two of them had conspired to be in the same place at the same time, and the *Iron Meridian*, drawing three point two meters loaded, met the estuary bottom at eleven knots.

The sound was enormous. Not a crash—a grinding, a long geological complaint, as if the river had picked up the barge in its teeth and was deciding whether to swallow.

I went down the ladder on hands and feet because the ladder was at forty degrees. Water was already coming through the seams in the forward hold—not gushing, seeping, which is worse, because seeping means you have time to think.

"Rill," I said, finding the kid lashed to a stanchion, gray-faced, "you wanted to earn your money. Start earning it. Bilge pump, forward hold, now."

They looked at me with something worse than fear. Recognition. The look of a person watching their lie come true in real time.

And out through the rain-lashed windows, downstream, I saw running lights. One boat. Small, fast, coming toward us against every sane instinct, into a storm tide, straight at the bar that had just eaten us.

I knew whose lights those were. I had spent two weeks studying that boat's wake patterns from satellite imagery, and I will admit—to no one, ever—that for one unprofessional second I felt something adjacent to relief.

Then the lights went dark, and I understood: Brightwater wasn't signaling. They were hiding. Coming in blind, on memory, through a storm, to a bar they themselves had falsified.

To save us.

"Get me the radio," I said. "And someone find out how much water we're taking."

Because whatever else happened tonight, I intended to be alive to settle accounts.

### Rill

Here's what nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't feel like anything when you do it. It feels like Tuesday. You take the photo, you send the text, you tell yourself a story—*it's just coordinates, it's just business, they'll never know, and anyway Dovie's got savings and Marisol's got family and I've got nothing but a ledger with my name on it and men who don't send letters, they send reminders.*

Then Tuesday comes due, and it feels like everything.

I was on the bilge pump in the forward hold of the consortium barge, ankle-deep in cold river, working a handle that fought me like a live thing, when Kline came down the ladder and turned on the work light, and I saw my own texts glowing on her tablet screen. My words. My photos of Casper's charts. Timestamped, geotagged, organized—she'd built a folder. She'd named it. I didn't read the folder name. Some things you can't unread.

"You sent these," she said. Not a question. Kline doesn't ask questions she hasn't already answered.

"I—" My hands were shaking, and not from the pump.

"Here's what happens now," she said, in the tone of a person reading a menu. "The barge is aground and taking water. We are forty minutes' hard walk from the cave mouth at low tide, except there is no low tide tonight, there is only a storm tide, so there is no walk at all. There is only the boat coming out there in the dark, and whether or not it stops for us. So you are going to tell me everything about the interior approach to that cave. Every rock, every current, every depth. And when we're off this bar, your debts are paid, your file is deleted, and you never see me again."

"And if I don't?"

Kline looked at me the way you look at weather. "Then you'll still be here when the storm finishes deciding."

I thought about Casper. About the way they'd taught me to read the lead line, hand over hand, saying *the river tells you the truth, Rill, it's the only thing that does, so you better be worth its time.* About the strongbox I'd seen once, accidentally, and the initials on it, and how Casper's face had closed like a door.

I thought about the men I owed. About how their reminders had started including addresses. My mother's address.

"No," I said.

The word came out small. Kline raised an eyebrow, genuinely curious, the way a scientist is curious about a mold that won't grow.

"No," I said again, louder, and the storm outside seemed to cosign it, slamming the hull so hard the work light swung. "You want the cave? Ask the river. That's who owns it. I'm not giving you Casper. Not for a clean slate. Not for—" and here my voice cracked, because I am twenty-two and honest at the worst possible moments, "—not for anything. I already sold them once. I'm not doing it twice."

Something flickered across Kline's face. I want to say respect. It might have been indigestion.

"Brave," she said. "Stupid, but brave. Those often travel together." She turned to go up the ladder, then stopped. "For the record, the file exists whether you cooperate or not. But I'll note your refusal. It may matter later. It occasionally does."

Then the lights died—all of them, generator drowned—and we were in the black, in the roaring, with the river coming in through the seams, and I sat down in the freezing water next to a pump I couldn't turn and did the bravest, stupidest thing I have ever done, which was nothing. I stayed. And I prayed, to whoever handles rivers, *let them come. Let them hate me, but let them come.*

### Casper

Twenty years on the Kestrel teaches you things you can't unlearn. It teaches you that fog has layers, like an onion made of cold. It teaches you that a sandbar moves an average of nine meters a season, which is why charts are opinions. It teaches you that the sound a river makes when it's angry is different from the sound it makes when it's lying, and tonight it was doing both at once, and I had Dovie's engine hammering underneath me like a second heartbeat, and Marisol calling depths off the bow in a voice that stayed level the way a tightrope stays level.

"Twelve feet! Ten! Sandbar off the port bow—Casper, that's *your* sandbar, that's the fake one—"

"I know," I said. "Go around it."

"Go around—Casper, the real channel's flooded, the markers are under, we're navigating on *memory*—"

"We're navigating on the truth," I said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back. "The bar I drew for Kline is real tonight. Storm tide piled the actual shoal right where I lied about it. Which means the deep water is where I always knew it was. Starboard of the false line. Trust the old channel."

Dovie, at the engine, didn't look up. "You're steering us through your own forgery."

"I'm steering us through the gap next to my forgery. It's different."

"It's really not."

But Dovie gave us the power, because Dovie had rigged this boat for a real run three weeks ago and had been waiting, with the patience of a person who oils things, for the moment it would matter. And Rill wasn't aboard to ask questions, which should have felt like relief and instead felt like a missing tooth.

Here is the choice, laid out plain, the way the river lays things out plain when it's done being subtle:

Behind us, upstream, the cave. The Meridian. Tam's logbook, sealed in a wreck that had kept its secret for thirty years, proof that would burn down the expedition's sponsors and give Tam back their name. The storm window was closing. By dawn, the surge would make the cave mouth unnavigable for days. Slip away now, run for shelter, surface the logbook next week from some safe harbor, and the consortium—grounded, humiliated, drowning in bad press—would never touch it.

Ahead of us, downstream, four hundred tons of salvage barge grinding itself apart on the bar I had drawn for it. Eleven souls, give or take. Kline, who had hunted me with permits and patience. A crew of hired divers and deckhands who didn't know what they were hunting. And Rill. Twenty-two years old, scared, bought, and—according to the text Marisol had intercepted an hour ago, relayed from a barge hand's phone in a last burst of signal—locked below when they'd refused to talk.

Marisol came aft, soaked through, and put her hand over mine on the wheel.

"Casper." Just the name. She'd earned the right to use it bare, no captain attached.

"If we turn," I said, "we lose the window. Maybe the whole claim. Everything Tam—everything Ada—everything we—"

"I know what we lose." Her voice was quiet under the storm, which meant it was loud. "I'm asking what we keep."

And there it was. The fulcrum. Twenty years I'd built a life the shape of a shadow—no fixed address, no past discussed, no name spoken above a murmur—because Old Tam taught me that the river keeps you safe by keeping you unknown. And I'd learned it so well I'd forged a chart, played a consortium, and steered eleven human beings onto a sandbar without losing a minute of sleep, because they were the hunters and I was the ghost, and ghosts don't owe anybody anything.

Except ghosts don't get to choose. That's the whole problem with being a ghost. Somebody else decides what you haunt.

Tam chose erasure and it cost them everything—their sister, their name, their grave. I had a chance, right now, at the wheel, in the rain, to choose differently. To be the kind of person whose chart tells the truth. Whose name gets said out loud. Who turns the wheel around.

"Dovie," I said. "How much engine do we have left?"

"All of it. Why?"

"Because we're going to need all of it." I put the wheel over. The boat leaned into the turn like it had been waiting twenty years for me to ask. "Take us to the bar. Marisol—get the lines ready, life rings, the throw bag, everything. If anyone goes in the water, we go in after them. Nobody drowns on my river tonight. Not even them."

"*Especially* them," Marisol said, and for the first time since the storm started, she smiled.

The lightning came then, one vast white flash that lit the whole estuary at once—and I saw it all in that single frame, the way a camera sees: the barge down by the head, listing, its deck lights dead, its hull groaning against the shoal. Figures on the deck clinging to rails. A raft, half-launched, flapping. And beyond it, upstream, barely visible between squalls, the black seam in the cliff that was the cave mouth, holding its breath, keeping Tam's secret one more night.

Two destinations. One boat. One tide.

I'd spent the whole book of my life measuring in miles—miles of river, miles of distance from my own name. Tonight the only measurement that mattered was the distance between the person I'd been pretending to be and the one at this wheel, turning toward the people hunting me, throttle wide open, into the teeth of the storm.

"Hold on," I said, to the boat, to the crew, to the ghost riding shotgun in my chest who had taught me all the wrong lessons and one right one. "This is going to be loud."

The *Kestrel Queen*—my boat, my name for her, the only name I'd ever said proudly—drove into the dark.

What happened next took eleven minutes, cost me an anchor, nearly took Dovie overboard, and included a confession I did not see coming from the person I least expected to give it.

But that's the next part.
