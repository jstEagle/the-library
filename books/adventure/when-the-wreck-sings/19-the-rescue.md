## 19. The Rescue

The storm came in like a debt collector—no courtesy call, no warning letter, just the sky opening its ledger over our heads.

I'd felt it building all afternoon, that pressure behind the ears you get when the river knows something you don't. By the time Dovie and I had the *Kestrel Queen* tied off in the lee of Gull Point, the wind was coming sideways off the water and Marisol was shouting something I couldn't hear over the drumming rain on the cabin roof. She pointed downstream.

Lights. Three of them, strung out across the black water like a broken necklace. The consortium's barge, running the bar in weather no sane pilot would choose.

"They're going to hit it," Dovie said beside me, her voice flat as a sounding line. "The bar you falsified. They're going to hit your bar."

"I know."

"Casper." She grabbed my sleeve. "The tide's storm-lifted. It's four feet over what their charts say. They think they've got clearance they don't have."

I stood there in the rain and did the arithmetic every captain does when another boat's in trouble: my hull against theirs, my crew against mine, the window before the water or the wind made the decision for us. Every honest number said stay put. Stay put, radio it in, let the coast guard earn their keep.

Then I saw the launch.

It came off the barge's stern in the worst possible way—sideways, broadside to the chop, a little aluminum skiff with too many people in it trying to make shore and failing. A wave took it and rolled it like a child's toy. Heads in the water. Four, maybe five. And behind them, still aboard the barge, more lights moving frantic along the rail—and one figure at the stern, arms wrapped around a stanchion, small and stubborn and unmistakably Rill.

"Get me off this point," I said.

Dovie looked at me for three full seconds. Rain ran down her face and she didn't blink it away. "You're going back toward them."

"I'm going toward the water," I said. "The water doesn't care whose side anybody's on."

* * *

Here is the thing about a storm-lifted river: it isn't just higher, it's *wrong*. Twenty years on the Kestrel taught me its moods the way you learn a lover's—the lazy eddies, the honest channels, the lies it tells at low water. But a storm tide scrambles all of it. Currents reverse. Sandbars become reefs become nothing at all. The river stops being a road and becomes an argument.

And I steered into it anyway.

"Marisol, lead line!" I shouted through the wheelhouse door. "Sound every cast, don't trust your eyes!"

She was already rigged, standing braced in the bow well with rain hammering her hood, calling depths into the wind. "Twelve! Ten! Twelve! Bar shelf port side—hard starboard!"

I gave it hard starboard. The *Queen* heeled, groaned, answered. That old girl has never once let me down, which is why I nearly cried when I asked her to do what I asked next.

The barge was aground exactly where I'd promised Kline she'd be safe—or rather, where I'd told Rill to tell Kline she'd be safe, which amounts to the same sin with different fingerprints. She'd driven up onto the false channel I'd charted, the dead slough's sandbar now crowned by four feet less water than any honest survey would show, and she'd hit it at speed. Her bow was high and dry. Her stern was awash. She lay canted like a drunk leaning on a lamppost, and every wave shoved her a little further over.

The people in the water were between me and her.

"Bring us around their upstream side," Dovie said. She'd appeared at my shoulder with a coiled throw line and the expression of a woman who has decided to be furious later. "Current'll sweep them down to us if we hold here."

"Hold here in this?" I gestured at the maelstrom generally.

"We're not anchoring in six feet of whitewater, Casper. We hold position under power and we pluck them out as they come past. You thread us, I throw."

"And if the current takes them past us?"

"Then we do it again." She met my eyes. "As many times as it takes. Now stop talking and drive."

I stopped talking and drove.

* * *

The first one came to us almost politely—a big man in a consortium jacket, treading water and screaming, swept down the seam between the barge and the bar. Marisol called him: "Contact! Thirty yards, mid-channel!" and I nudged the *Queen* into his path like threading a needle in an earthquake. He hit our hull shoulder-first; Dovie had him by the collar before he finished gasping, hauled him over the rail, and deposited him on the deck plates with all the tenderness of a longshoreman stacking crates.

"Two more," he coughed. "Two more went under by the—by the stern—"

"Rill?" My voice cracked on it. I hated that it cracked.

"The kid? Kid stayed aboard. Said somebody had to—" He doubled over, coughing river.

Somebody had to stay with the boat. Of course Rill had said that. Rill, who sold my charts for debt money and then couldn't delete the texts, who'd been carrying that guilt around like ballast for days while I read their nervousness as ordinary fear of storms. Somebody had to stay with the boat. That was the most Rill sentence ever spoken, and it broke something loose in my chest that I didn't have time to examine.

The next two weren't polite. The river had them and meant to keep them—one went under twice before we got a line on her, and when Dovie dragged her aboard she wasn't breathing until Marisol, soaked to the bone, knelt and put her mouth on cold lips and breathed for her. Three breaths. Five. Then the woman convulsed and vomited half the Kestrel onto our deck, and Marisol sat back on her heels and laughed, a wild, wet, unhinged sound I'll remember the rest of my life.

"That's four," Dovie shouted. "Barge still has people on it!"

I looked at the barge. Storm-lifted waves were breaking clean over her low stern now. The lights along the rail had gone from frantic to sparse. And at the stern rail, still clinging, one figure raised an arm—not waving for help. Pointing. Down, toward the flooded aft deck, where a shape moved against the wash.

Rill was pulling someone toward the rail. Someone who wasn't moving.

* * *

There are maneuvers you practice and maneuvers you invent, and the difference between them is usually how much of the river you're willing to bet. What I did next belonged firmly in the second category.

I brought the *Queen* alongside the barge's leeward quarter, close enough that the two hulls kissed and ground together with a sound like teeth. Every swell slammed us apart and threw us back together, and between those collisions I had maybe two seconds of relative calm per wave.

"Marisol! Fenders gone, get the heaving line to Rill!"

Rill couldn't hear me over the storm, but Rill saw the line arc across the gap and caught it on the second throw, snubbing it around the stanchion they'd been hugging. Then they turned back for the body on the deck, and I watched them drag it upright—it was Kline. Vesper Kline, the sharpest instrument the consortium owned, limp as kelp, and Rill hauling them to the rail with arms that shook.

"You can't lift them!" I screamed. "Get them to the rail, we'll take them from there!"

What followed was thirty seconds I have replayed a thousand times since and will replay a thousand more. Dovie and I holding the *Queen* against the barge's grinding flank. Marisol reaching across the gap. Rill getting Kline's torso over the rail, both of them half in the flooding water, and the wave that came through right then lifting the barge and dropping her hard, throwing Rill forward so that they and Kline came off the rail together—

—and landed across our gunwale in a tangle of limbs and rope, all of us going down in a pile on the deck plates as the two boats crashed apart behind us.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Kline coughed, Rill coughed, Marisol laughed that laugh again, and I lay on my back in the rain looking up at a sky the color of wet slate and thought: *we're all alive, we're actually all alive.*

That was when I heard the sound from below decks that meant we were not, entirely, all fine.

* * *

The last collision had opened the *Queen*'s cheek. Not fatally—Dovie found it with a flashlight in ninety seconds, a split seam forward of the water tank, weeping but not gushing—but "not gushing" is a relative term on a sinking river, and the bilge pump started making the conversational noise pumps make when they're losing an argument.

"Dovie?"

"I can slow it. Can't stop it till we beach her." She wiped her hands on her trousers, leaving rust-colored streaks. "Gull Point shoal's a mile upstream. We ground her there, patch at dawn."

"A mile upstream in this."

"A mile upstream in this," she agreed. "Unless you've got a better idea."

I looked at my deck. Two consortium crew wrapped in blankets, one diver breathing thanks to Marisol, Vesper Kline gray-faced and conscious and staring at me with an expression I couldn't read, and Rill—soaked, shaking, kneeling on the deck plates like a sinner at a rail, looking at nothing but the boards beneath their hands.

"Take us to Gull Point," I said.

* * *

We grounded the *Queen* on the shoal as the storm reached its full argument with the world, and I will spare you the hour of bilge-pumping and canvas-patching and blanket-distributing except to say this: nobody spoke about anything that mattered until the leak was slowed and the coffee was hot and the five of us who could fit crowded into my cabin while the wind tried to peel the roof off.

Kline sat wrapped in a blanket, drinking coffee with both hands, watching me with those appraising eyes that had sized me up in a dockside café weeks ago. Whatever calculation was running behind them, it was running slower than usual. Nearly drowning does that to sharp instruments.

"Captain Brightwater," Kline said finally. "Or should I say Captain Joubert. Theo Anand's research was thorough."

"Anand," I repeated. The journalist. Of course. "How thorough?"

"Thorough enough that the consortium's lawyers are very interested in the Meridian's cargo manifest." Kline took a sip. "Thorough enough that I'm no longer certain whose interests I'm serving."

Before I could answer that—and believe me, I wanted to answer it—Rill stood up.

They were still dripping. Their hands wouldn't stay still. They looked at Kline first, then at me, and their voice came out small and wrecked and absolutely steady in the way voices go when someone has decided to stop carrying a thing.

"It was me," Rill said. "The coordinates. I photographed Casper's charts and sent them to Kline. That's how the barge knew where the cave was. That's how they beat us to the bar tonight—they trusted my numbers instead of their own survey, because I told them yours were better." A breath that shuddered going in and came out level. "I owed money. Bad people kind of money. And I told myself it was just pictures, just paper, nobody gets hurt. And then tonight people almost died, and it was my numbers that put them on that bar, and—" Their voice finally cracked. "Casper, I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Take whatever you want. Throw me off at the next town. I'll swim."

The cabin went quiet except for the wind and the pump doing its stubborn work below.

I looked at Rill for a long moment. This kid—I thought of them as a kid though they were twenty-six—who'd eaten at my table for three seasons, who'd learned knots from Marisol and engine lore from Dovie, who'd stayed on a sinking barge to pull their own betrayer out of the water. Because that's what they'd done. Kline had bought their coordinates and left them to drown on it, and Rill had gone back for Kline anyway.

Twenty years ago, a man named Tam had lied to everyone who loved him, and I had spent this whole season learning that the lie had been shelter, not malice. People are not their worst transaction. I'd have to be a hypocrite of the first order to forget that now.

"Sit down, Rill," I said. "Your coffee's going cold."

"But—"

"You'll work it off," I said. "Mile by mile. Starting tomorrow, when we patch this hole and go see what the Meridian has to say for herself. And Rill?" They flinched. "Next time somebody offers you money for my charts, you come tell me first. I might even cut you in on the deal."

A sound came out of Rill that was half sob, half laugh. Dovie put a hand on their shoulder and pushed them gently back down onto the bench.

Kline set down the coffee. "You're taking them back. After they sold you out."

"I'm taking them back because they came back for you," I said. "Loyalty isn't proven by words, Ms. Kline. It's proven by which way you turn the wheel. Tonight everybody on this boat turned the wheel toward the trouble. That's the whole test. There isn't another one."

Kline held my gaze for a long moment. Outside, the wind screamed and the rain came horizontal and somewhere downstream the Meridian waited in her cave with a truth inside her worth more than any salvage claim.

"You know what's in that wreck," Kline said quietly. "Don't you. You've known since before I ever came to this town."

"I know what Tam left in it," I said. "And tomorrow, when the tide's right, I'm going to show everyone—including you. All of it. No more false routes, no more dead sloughs. The story goes where it goes."

Kline studied me the way you study a chart of waters you once thought you knew. Then, slowly, they nodded.

"Then I suppose," they said, "I should hear it from you first."

Below decks, the pump kept working. Above us, the storm began, at last, to spend itself. And I sat in my cabin with my strange, soaked, betrayed-and-forgiven family, and understood that come morning, everything I'd spent twenty years keeping hidden was going to surface—whether the river was ready or not.
