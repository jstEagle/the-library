## 22. The Next Tide

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Rivers don't keep calendars, but river towns do, and Tuesdays are when the mail boat runs and the bakery burns the cinnamon rolls and the world pretends to be ordinary.

I was on the dock with a cup of coffee going cold, watching Rill re-tar the seam where we'd patched her during the storm—my storm, our storm, the one that nearly took everything—and Marisol was inside arguing with the radio about the weather forecast, which she considered a personal insult. Dovie had gone upstream to Ada Voss's place to deliver a crate of oranges and come back with a jar of quince preserves and, knowing Ada, at least one story I hadn't asked for.

The envelope was heavy paper, cream-colored, addressed in handwriting so careful it looked printed. *Casper Joubert, M/V Kestrel's Daughter, Town Dock, Graywater Landing.* My name. Not "the captain of," not "the person who found," not "the survivor of the Meridian affair." Just my name, sitting there in public like it belonged on an envelope.

Twenty years I'd kept that name off things. Twenty years of signing manifests as C. Joubert and letting people assume what they liked, of having no fixed address because a fixed address is a thing that can be found, and being findable was the one skill I'd never let myself learn from Tam. He'd taught me everything else: how to read water, how to read people, how to disappear into a job well done. The disappearing part I'd gotten good at. The rest of it—the claiming part—I was still learning, and I'll tell you honestly, some mornings my hand still hesitated before writing those letters down.

But not this morning. This morning I opened it standing up, in the wind, like a person with nothing left to hide.

*Dear Captain Joubert,*

*My name is Dr. Imogen Hale, and I write to you from the Institute of Maritime History in Corvane. You will likely have heard enough from academics since the story broke, so I will be brief and I will be honest.*

*I have spent eleven years searching for the Corvane Star, a coastal steamer lost in 1911 with forty-two souls aboard, whose last known position was recorded by a navigator who later turned out to have falsified his charts to cover for the shipping line's insurance fraud. The parallels to your case will not have escaped you. The sponsor's name in your documents—the name you made public—is the same family trust that underwrote the Corvane Star's final voyage.*

*I am asking, formally, whether you would consult on the search. Your knowledge of the falsified charting methods, your reading of the river and the coast, and frankly your reputation for finding what powerful people paid to lose—all of it would be invaluable. We can offer a fee. What we cannot offer is discretion, and I suspect you no longer want it.*

*Yours in the work,*
*Imogen Hale*

I read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you read a chart of water you're about to run at night.

Forty-two souls. A navigator who lied on paper because someone above him told him to, or paid him to, or made him afraid not to. And somewhere downstream of all that, a man who faked his death and became Old Tam, who taught a kid on the Kestrel River how to hold a wheel in fog and never once said why he knew so much about running from things.

Ada had told me, back in her kitchen with the quince trees out the window, that her brother hadn't been a coward. "He was a witness," she'd said, "who chose to survive long enough to testify." It had taken me most of a year and one sunken barge to understand what she meant. Tam hadn't erased himself out of shame. He'd done it the way you beach a boat in a storm—on purpose, into the softest ground available, to save what mattered. What mattered, it turned out, was a logbook and the truth inside it, and eventually, though he probably didn't plan it, me.

And now here was a stranger asking me to go looking for another wreck full of somebody else's buried testimony. Asking me to use the exact skills Tam gave me, aimed at the exact kind of lie that made him.

I stood there until the coffee went fully cold, and then I went inside and put the letter on the table where Marisol could see it.

She read it without picking it up, which is a talent. "Corvane Star," she said. "That's the one Theo mentioned. In his follow-up piece. He said if the Meridian trail led anywhere, it led there."

"Theo gets everywhere first and tells everyone second."

"He's writing a book now. Did he tell you? No, of course he didn't." She tapped the letter. "What are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking I spent twenty years learning how to say no to things."

"And?"

"And I'm out of practice saying yes."

Marisol smiled the way she does when she's already three moves ahead and letting me catch up. "Good. Because there's a woman on the dock who's been waiting twenty minutes to talk to you, and I don't think she's here about cargo."

* * *

Vesper Kline looked different on my dock than she had on hers.

On the consortium's survey barge, back when she was hunting the Meridian with permits and money and that flat professional stare, she'd dressed like the job: hard-wearing, anonymous, armor-colored. Today she wore a plain gray jacket and held her hat against the wind, and she stood on the planks of Graywater Landing like someone who had learned, recently and expensively, that the river doesn't care about your org chart.

"Captain Joubert." She said it correctly. She'd had practice by now; half the coast had said it correctly since the hearings.

"Ms. Kline."

"Casper," she corrected herself, and almost smiled. "Sorry. Habit."

"What can I do for you?"

She looked past me at the boat—at Rill on the deck with tar to the elbows, at the patched hull line, at the new name board Marisol had painted over the winter, *Kestrel's Daughter* in letters that caught the light. Everyone looks at boats differently once they've seen one sink.

"I wanted to say something in person, because a letter would have been cowardly, and I've had my fill of paperwork that says one thing and means another." She squared herself. "The consortium is dissolving the salvage division. After the grounding, after the embezzlement evidence, after—" she gestured vaguely at the whole coastline, the whole scandal, the whole year— "after everything, the investors want out, and honestly, they should. We were hunting a wreck to strip it, and the wreck turned out to be a witness stand. That's on us."

"That's on the people who signed the checks," I said, because it was true, but I said it gently, because she'd been the one holding the flashlight.

"Some of it's on me. I read the tide tables and I saw you were playing us, and instead of walking away I set a trap. You know how that ended." Her jaw tightened. "Rill told me no, you know. Down in that cabin, with the debt hanging over them, they told me no. I've thought about that more than I've thought about anything else this year. People keep surprising you when you give them the chance."

"They do."

"So." She took a folded paper from her jacket—not a contract, I noticed, not a permit, just paper. "There's a group forming. Former consortium people, some independents, a couple of archaeologists who actually care about provenance. Recovery work done right: documented, shared, families notified first, profit last if at all. We're calling it the Tidewater Cooperative, which sounds like a bakery, and I've been assured that's a feature and not a bug." She held it out. "We don't want to acquire anything of yours, Captain. We want to hire you. Or partner with you. Or, failing that, just ask you questions and buy you dinner while you answer them."

I took the paper. It was a prospectus, two pages, honest-looking numbers, and a line near the bottom that said: *Advisory position available; terms negotiable; name of advisor to appear as advisor.* As in, publicly. As in, mine.

"You know," I said, "a year ago you offered me money to hand you coordinates."

"A year ago I thought coordinates were the valuable thing." She looked out at the river, at the low-water line where the sandbar showed its teeth. "Turns out the valuable thing was the person who knew which ones were lies."

* * *

I walked her to the end of the dock, because that's what you do, and then I kept walking, alone, past the fuel shed and around the point, to the place I'd been avoiding and visiting in equal measure all winter.

The cave mouth sat above the low-tide line like a held breath. Even now, even named, even surveyed and photographed and argued about in journals, it did that thing it had done the first morning—made the air feel older, made the water sound different going in. There's a sign there now. The historical society put it up in the fall: a plaque with the Meridian's dates, the forty-one names of the crew and expedition, and at the bottom, smaller: *Recovered through the testimony of T. Voss, navigator, and C. Joubert, master of the Kestrel's Daughter.*

Tam's real name, in bronze, on my river. Mine, too.

I used to think anonymity was safety. I built twenty years on that idea, the way you build a house on pilings—solid enough, until the flood comes and shows you what the ground was made of. But standing there, reading my own name in metal while the tide crept back in over the bar I'd once falsified to save this very story, I understood that the safety had always been the other thing. The name. The people. The fixed address I was starting to want.

The cave swallowed the light and gave back echo. Somewhere in that dark, timbers I'd touched with my own hands were settling into their long argument with the salt, and the logbook that changed everything was safe behind glass in Corvane, and Ada Voss had finally, after sixty years, been able to visit her brother's grave—which was not a grave at all, but a cave, and a name, and a story told whole.

The tide was coming in. It would close the mouth by evening, the way it always did, keeping its appointment. But it wasn't a secret anymore. It was just a place. Known. Named. On the charts, in both senses.

My phone buzzed. Marisol: *Well? Kline's still on the dock drinking our coffee. Also Theo called AGAIN.*

I laughed, alone on the rocks like a person who talks to themselves, which after this year I had earned the right to be.

I typed back: *Tell Kline yes. Tell Theo he can have the exclusive on the announcement. Tell Dovie to warm up the engine—we're taking the long way home.*

Then I put the phone away and stood a minute longer, watching the water climb the rocks toward the cave, patient as a promise. Forty-two souls on the Corvane Star, waiting a hundred years for somebody to read their water right. A historian with honest handwriting. A rival turned ally. A crew that had chosen me back.

Somewhere upstream, the Kestrel ran high and cold with snowmelt, carrying nobody's secrets that I knew of—for once—and I felt the old pull in my hands, the wheel-hands' itch, the wanting-to-be-moving.

I'd spent my whole life measuring it in miles. Miles upriver, miles of cover, miles between me and every question I didn't want asked. But miles run out. Years don't. And the tide, which asks nothing and returns everything, was already on its way in.

I turned back toward the dock, toward the coffee and the crew and the yes I'd just given, and the river went on ahead of me, open all the way to the sea.
