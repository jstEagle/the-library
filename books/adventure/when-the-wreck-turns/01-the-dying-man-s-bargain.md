## 1. The Dying Man's Bargain

The trading post at Marsh Hollow smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular sweetness of apples rotting in their barrels—autumn's slow confession that winter was coming to collect. Ulyana Amberdale, who had not been Amberdale in three years, stood behind the counter weighing out salt pork for a trapper who would not look her in the eye, which suited her fine. She had gotten very good at not being looked at.

"Two pounds," the trapper said. "And a twist of the black pepper, if you've it."

She had it. She weighed it out with hands that had once read mountain faces the way priests read scripture—hands that had judged cornices from a hundred yards, that had felt the wrongness in a snowpack through a probe pole before her mind caught up to what her body knew. Now they weighed salt pork. The scales were honest, at least. That was more than she could say for the name stitched into her apron: *Mira Voss*. A name like a borrowed coat, slightly too small, smelling of someone else's smoke.

The bell above the door rang, and the cold came in with it.

She knew before she turned. There was a sound a man made when he had walked too long on feet that were dying—something between a shuffle and a drag, the body's grudging negotiation with itself. She'd heard it on the trails, in the last camps of men who'd waited too long to turn back. She turned anyway.

He was a long stick of a man, sixty if he was a day, beard crusted with ice that hadn't melted because the fire in him was nearly out. His coat was good once—mountain wool, double-stitched—and now it was a ruin, torn at the shoulder where something with teeth had tried its luck. He stood in the doorway a moment, blinking at the lamplight like a man surfacing from deep water, and his eyes found her across the room with a precision that made her spine prickle.

Not the trapper. Not the post's owner, fat Hobb, who was counting ledger pages by the stove. Her.

"You're the one," he said. His voice was gravel rolled in snow. "They said there's a woman here who knows the Wreckline."

The pepper tin nearly slipped from her fingers. "You've got the wrong post," she said. "We sell flour and lamp oil. We don't sell stories."

He came forward anyway, one dragging step at a time, and the trapper took his pork and his pepper and left with the quick, embarrassed haste of a man leaving a room where grief was about to happen. The dying man reached the counter and gripped it, and Ulyana saw his hands—black at the tips, the frostbite gone past the red stage, past the blistered stage, into the country from which fingers did not return.

"Sit down," she said, and her voice came out different. Not Mira Voss's voice. Lower. Older. The voice of a woman who had knelt beside broken men on cold stone and known exactly which ones would live.

"I'll stand." He smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing, that smile—all the stubbornness of a long life packed into it. "Corliss," he said. "Ezra Corliss. And you're no shop girl. Shop girls don't look at a man's feet first and his face second. Shop girls don't know to put the kettle on before he asks."

She had already put the kettle on. She hated that he'd noticed.

"Sit," she said again, "or I'll let you bleed standing up, and Hobb will complain about the floor."

He sat. It took him a while, joint by joint, like a folding chair collapsing. She brought the kettle, a cup, the bottle of rye Hobb kept for valued customers and she kept for emergencies, and she set them down and said, "Drink it slow. It'll warm you faster than the fire and lie to you less."

He drank. Color came back into his face, the wrong kind first—fever-bright—and then something steadier beneath it. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and stone and the iron tang of old blood. There was a cut above his ear, crusted over, and when she reached to look at it he caught her wrist with surprising strength.

"Three days," he said. "Maybe four. I know what my body's telling me; I've listened to it sixty years and it's never once lied, only exaggerated." He released her. "I don't need a healer. I need a courier."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. The answer's no. I weigh pork."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double, and she waited it out with her arms crossed, feeling like a hypocrite, because she had already pulled the chair closer. When he could breathe again, he reached into his coat with those ruined hands, fumbling, and she wanted to help him and did not, because helping was a door she'd nailed shut years ago.

What he drew out was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and even before he unwound it she knew by the shape of the bundle, by the way he held it the way a man holds a child, that it was a map. She had spent her life among maps. She had drawn them, corrected them, watched men kill for them and die for the lack of them.

"I found it," Corliss said. "Eleven years I looked. Silver, and not a seam—a *lode*. The mother vein all those scratch-miners in Hollowmere have been dreaming about since before my daughter was born." His eyes were feverish and clear at once, the way eyes got when a man had passed beyond caring what happened to him and cared only what happened to his work. "I'm the only one who knows the route. It's in my head, and my head's about to stop."

"Then write it down and post it."

"A posted map gets read by everyone." He tapped the oilcloth bundle. "This goes to one person. My daughter. Vera. She lives in Hollowmere—keeps the assay office, or did, last I heard. We haven't spoken in nine years." He said it flatly, without self-pity, a man reporting weather. "I was a hard father. I was a worse husband to her mother. She has every reason to let my name rot. But this is hers by blood, and it'll make her rich, and rich is a kind of apology I can still make from the ground."

Ulyana looked at the stove, at the flames doing their patient work. "Why me?"

"Because you flinched when I said the Wreckline." He said it gently, and it landed like a hammer. "A shop girl doesn't flinch at a mountain's name. And because the man at the livery told me there's a woman here, three years now, who never talks about where she came from, who walks the ridge above the valley every morning in all weather, reading the sky. Who fixed Hobb's roofline after the windstorm by herself, at night, in the rain, because she couldn't stand watching it leak." He leaned forward. "I don't know what you did or what was done to you. I don't care. I care that you're the only soul in a hundred miles who could get that map over the Wreckline before the snows, and the only one who might do it for nothing but a promise."

"The passes close in six weeks. Maybe less this year."

"Then you'd best not dawdle."

"I said no."

"You said no to being asked." He pushed the bundle across the counter toward her, and his blackened fingers trembled, and his eyes did not. "I'm not asking you to guide me. I'm asking you to guide *it*. To carry a dying man's last word to his daughter. There's no shame in that work. There's no ledger in the world where that work counts against you."

The words went into her like a splinter, deep, in a place she'd thought was solid. *No ledger where that work counts against you.* She thought of the other ledger, the one that existed, the one with her real name struck through in red ink in every guide's registry from here to the coast. *Amberdale, U. — abandoned client — unfit.* Three words that had unmade her. They had never once mentioned the other part: that she'd gone back for Halloran after the cornice took the ledge, that she'd spent nine hours in the white going back, that she'd been the one who found what was left of him. Ledgers were short. Mountains were honest. People were neither.

"I can't promise the passes will hold," she said, and heard, to her horror, that she was negotiating.

"I'm not asking for the passes. I'm asking for you."

She looked at him a long time. He looked back with the patience of a man who had waited eleven years for silver and could wait out one shop girl's silence. Outside, the first spits of sleet ticked against the window glass, early, too early, the sky already rehearsing its winter.

"Swear it," he said. "Swear you'll put it in her hands, or tell me no and mean it, and I'll die easy either way. A man can ask for no more than a straight answer."

Ulyana Amberdale, who had spent three years being no one, reached out and took the bundle. It was lighter than it should have been, for all it weighed.

"I swear," she said. "Before the first snow seals the high passes, it's in her hands."

Corliss closed his eyes and let out a breath that seemed to empty him by half, and when he opened them again they were wet and he was grinning like a young fool. "Knew it," he whispered. "Knew it the moment you put the kettle on."

He had two days, not four, as it turned out. She sat with him through the second night, and near the end he talked—about the route, the landmarks, a notch he called the Coffin for reasons he was too tired to explain, a rockfall in the eastern col that hadn't been there on his way in. He talked about Vera, how she'd had her mother's way of looking at people like she was weighing them, and how she'd be angry, and how Ulyana should let her be angry as long as it took.

And near dawn, his voice dropping to almost nothing, he said the thing that changed the shape of everything after:

"There's men looking for this map. My old partners. Dreele and Sannit. They didn't find the vein—I did, alone—but they'll say otherwise, and they won't ask polite." His hand found her sleeve, gripped with the last of his strength. "They'll come asking at this post. When they do, you'll want to already be gone."

* * *

She buried him on the ridge above Marsh Hollow, in the thin soil under a leaning pine, with Hobb's shovel and no preacher, because Corliss had asked for stone and sky and nothing that talked. She marked the grave with a cairn, seven stones, the old way, and stood over it while the wind came down off the Wreckline—visible from here, even in the lowlands, a white and jagged jaw on the horizon, wearing its glaciers like a grin.

She had told herself she would hire it out. Put the map in a mail rider's hands, wash her own of it, stay Mira Voss forever. But mail riders went by the low road, and the low road didn't reach Hollowmere before the snows, and a promise was a promise, and she had made it over a dying man's hand.

She packed by lamplight that night: rope, pitons, the good tent, rations for eight days. She wrapped the map in oilcloth against her chest, where a heartbeat could keep it warm. She was lacing her pack shut when the bell above the door rang, and two men stepped in out of the dark, stamping the cold from their boots—big men, road-worn, with the flat, patient way of looking around a room that she had last seen on men hunting a different fugitive, years ago, in a different life.

The taller one smiled at her across the counter.

"Evening," he said. "We're looking for a friend of ours. Old fella, name of Corliss. Comes through this way, does he?"

Behind her back, her hand closed around the map, and Ulyana Amberdale smiled the smile of Mira Voss, shop girl, and began to lie.
