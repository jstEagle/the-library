## 2. Company of Strangers

The stowaway made herself known by sneezing.

Ulyana had been re-packing the supply sled in the blue hour before dawn, counting rope coils by feel, when the tarp over the cargo shifted and a small, muffled *ah-choo* rose from among the flour sacks. She stood very still. Then she reached down, took hold of the tarp's edge, and pulled it back in one clean motion.

A girl stared up at her from the nest she'd made between the crates—sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and sharp collarbones, with wind-burned cheeks and a knit cap pulled down to her eyebrows. A stolen wedge of hard cheese was still clutched in one fist, as if surrendering it would be the last indignity.

"You're eating my provisions," Ulyana said.

"I was going to pay you back." The girl's chin came up. "Soon as I find work."

"Where?"

"Hollowmere. Eventually." She swallowed. "My name's Tamsin."

Ulyana looked at her a long moment. She knew what it was to be a name without a place, a person the road had chewed up and spat out somewhere unfamiliar. She also knew what it was to be hungry in a town that had decided against you.

"Get out of my sled," Ulyana said. "Then help me pack it properly. If you're coming north, you'll pull your weight."

Tamsin blinked. "You're not turning me in?"

"The constable charges a fee for that. I don't pay fees." She tossed the girl a coil of spare rope. "Tie those. Tight. If you tie them like a child, we'll renegotiate."

Tamsin tied them well. Ulyana pretended not to notice how her hands shook, and pretended harder not to notice that she'd already, without deciding to, begun counting the girl as cargo she was responsible for delivering.

* * *

Tamsin watched the guide the way she'd learned to watch everything—sideways, quiet, cataloguing.

The woman was wrong in a way Tamsin recognized. She moved like someone who had once been looked at a great deal and had learned to hate it. She gave her name as "Mera" to the livery man, and Tamsin, who had given three false names in two months, heard the lie the way you hear a cracked bell.

They needed four more bodies to make the crossing. Ulyana—*Mera*—said it flatly over a supper of beans: "The Wreckline in late season is not a two-person proposition. We need a cartographer who knows the old survey trails, and we need someone with mules, because I'm not carrying a sled over the scree on my back."

"Where do we find them?" Tamsin asked.

"We don't find them. We find the kind of people who are still in this town in the off-season, which means we find people with nowhere else to be." Ulyana scraped her bowl clean. "Same as us."

They found the first one in the back room of the map-seller's shop, correcting other men's errors by lamplight.

Oskar Vell was a long, folded man with ink-stained fingers and the careful, defeated posture of someone who had once been important. His maps hung on the walls of half the government offices in the lowlands—until, the shopkeeper whispered, one of them sent a survey party into a canyon that wasn't there. Two men drowned in a river Oskar had drawn where no river ran. He'd been selling corrections and secondhand atlases ever since, and drinking the profits of neither.

"I don't chart anymore," he said when Ulyana laid Corliss's map on his table.

"I'm not asking you to chart. I'm asking you to read." She unrolled the dead man's careful hand—the contour lines, the little annotations in fading brown ink: *loose shale, do not trust the eastern shelf after rain.* "Tell me if this man knew what he was doing."

Oskar bent over the map. Tamsin watched his whole face change, the way a fire changes when you feed it. His finger traced a ridge line, hesitated, traced it again.

"Whoever drew this has crossed the Wreckline in every season," he murmured. "This notation—*do not trust the eastern shelf*—that's not caution. That's grief. He lost someone there, or almost did." He looked up, and for the first time his eyes were alive. "Yes. I'll come. I want to see the ground that made this hand."

* * *

Brann they found where Ulyana had predicted: in debt.

He was a broad, weathered man with a mule-driver's bow legs and a smile that came too easily to be entirely honest, drinking in the Salt Latch with the particular stillness of a man listening for the door. Two creditors had already been through the town asking after him. When Ulyana offered him passage north and wages on delivery, he didn't even pretend to consider.

"North's the one direction nobody looks," he said. "When do we leave?"

"Soon."

"Sooner," Oskar said quietly, back at the shop. He'd spread Corliss's map beside a newer survey, and his finger rested on a mark in the lowland town of Grayfall, a day's ride south. "These men. Corliss's partners, you said. The ones asking after the map." He tapped the mark. "That's the Grayfall Mining Consortium. I've drawn their boundaries. They don't send polite men after unregistered veins. They send *finders*."

Tamsin felt the word settle in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Finders," she repeated.

"Men who find things," Oskar said. "Claims. Witnesses. Leaks." He looked at Ulyana. "How long do you think it takes a finder to trace a dying prospector's last week? Corliss talked. Dying men always talk. Someone fed him, sheltered him—" his eyes went, gently, to Ulyana, "—listened to him."

Ulyana was already pulling the map off the table.

"Then we leave tonight," she said. "Light. No wagon. The mules carry what we need and nothing we want. Oskar, I need the route in your head, not on paper—paper can be found. Brann meets us at the north ford with the animals. Tamsin—"

"I'm coming." The girl's voice cracked on it, and she hated that, so she said it again, harder. "I'm coming. I carried your sled forty miles in the dark. I'm not cargo."

Ulyana looked at her for a long, unreadable moment. Outside, the first wind of the season came down off the distant peaks and rattled the shutters, a sound like a door being opened somewhere far away.

"No," Ulyana said at last. "You're not cargo. Cargo doesn't get a vote, and you just used yours." She rolled the map and thrust it into the oilskin sleeve. "Get your coat. We walk out of this town like we're going nowhere, and we don't stop walking until the dark swallows us."

* * *

They made nine miles the first night, and Tamsin learned the shape of each of them by sound.

Brann's mules came jingling out of the blackness at the ford exactly when he'd said they would, which surprised everyone, including, she suspected, Brann. Oskar navigated by stars and muttered arithmetic, correcting their bearing twice before midnight without ever raising his voice. And Ulyana walked at the front like a woman who had made this exact walk in a dream a hundred times, setting a pace that was hard but never cruel, and stopping, without ever saying so, exactly when Tamsin's breath began to tear.

They camped in the lee of a granite shoulder, no fire, sharing oat cakes and cold tea. Above them the Wreckline stood up against the stars, white-toothed and enormous, and Tamsin felt very small under it and, strangely, not afraid.

"First snow's three weeks out," Brann said, chewing. "Maybe four."

"It'll be two," Ulyana said. "It's always sooner than the old men say."

Oskar nodded slowly. "Corliss's map puts the high pass at five days' hard travel. If the finders are a day behind us—"

"They're not behind us." Ulyana's voice was quiet in the dark. "They're ahead. They'll want to take the pass before we reach it, or take us on it, where there are no witnesses." She looked up at the mountains, and Tamsin saw something in her face she couldn't name—grief, or recognition, or the look of a woman returning to a room where something terrible had happened to her. "I've crossed this range before. It knows me."

The wind came down off the icefields then, carrying the first faint smell of snow, and far to the south, back along the black thread of the road they'd left, Tamsin saw it—a single point of light, bobbing, where no road-light should be.

Lanterns. Traveling fast.

Nobody said anything. But Ulyana was already on her feet, kicking snow over the tea fire's ashes, and Brann was already reaching for the mules' leads, and Oskar was already folding the map away, and Tamsin understood that this was what a crew was—not people who liked each other, but people who moved when the same silence moved them all.

"Break camp," Ulyana said. "We climb at first light."
