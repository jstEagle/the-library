## 3. What the Pass Remembers

The Wreckline did not rise so much as it *broke*—a spine of black granite snapped upward through the ice, its ridgelines jagged as a shattered jaw. Ulyana had spent six years telling herself she would never see it again. Now it filled half the sky.

She paused at the tree line where the pines gave out into scree and wind-scoured snow, and let the mountain look at her. It seemed to. The wind came down the pass in long, keening gusts, carrying the smell of stone and old cold, and somewhere above, half-buried in the white, were the bones of the Aldervane Expedition—the tents, the sledges, the rope still strung across the couloir like a ligament that had never healed.

"You've been here before," Tamsin said beside her. Not a question. The girl had learned to read her silences the way Oskar read contour lines.

"Once." Ulyana tightened the strap on her pack until the leather creaked. "Keep moving. We want the saddle before noon, or we don't want it at all."

Behind them, Brann's mule, Juniper, snorted and stamped, breath pluming like a forge. Oskar stood with his map case clutched to his chest, squinting up at the ridge with an expression Ulyana recognized from her own mirror: a man looking at the place that had ended him.

They climbed. The world narrowed to boot-placement and rope-length and the rasp of breath in thin air. By midmorning they reached the saddle—a saddle of wind-packed snow between two fangs of rock—and that was where the first shot cracked the morning open.

It came from the rocks above the eastern flank, and the bullet spanged off granite a hand's width from Oskar's head, spraying chips of stone like thrown gravel.

"Down! All of you—*down!*"

Ulyana was already moving, hauling Tamsin by the collar behind a shelf of rock. Brann dragged Juniper flat against the slope, one arm over the mule's neck, his other hand fumbling for the hatchet at his belt. A second shot. A third. Then voices—two, maybe three men, shouting positions to each other in the clipped cadence of men who had done this before.

"Corliss's partners," Oskar breathed. His face had gone the color of the snow. "How did they—"

"They didn't follow us. They *knew* the route." Ulyana pressed her back to the stone, heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to escape. "There are only two ways through this range for a loaded party. They chose this one."

Tamsin's eyes were wide but steady. Six days on the trail had burned the stowaway softness out of her. "What do we do?"

Ulyana looked up-slope, toward the only shelter within reach: a dark notch in the mountainside where the rock folded inward, three hundred yards of exposed traverse away. She knew what was in that notch. She had hoped never to know again.

"The ruins," she said. "There's an old camp in the lee of that wall. We run for it."

"And they'll shoot us crossing open ground," Brann said flatly.

"Not if I give them something else to shoot at." She pulled her coil of rope free, began knotting a sling for a loose boulder the size of a bread loaf. "On my mark, everyone runs. Don't stop. Don't look back. If you fall, get up. Whatever you hear behind you—" she met each of their eyes in turn, "—keep going."

"What will we hear?" Tamsin asked.

"Me."

She heaved the boulder loose and sent it bounding down the eastern gully, a thunderclap of displaced stone, and in the half-second the shooters' heads turned toward the noise, she shoved her people into the open and ran behind them, firing her own pistol wild and high to sell the deception. Bullets chased them across the traverse—one kicked snow into Tamsin's heels, another whined off the rock above Oskar's shoulder—but then the notch swallowed them, and the wind, and the dark.

* * *

The ruins smelled the way grief smells.

Old canvas gone gray and brittle. A collapsed cook-tent sagging under years of snow-load. A sledge frame sticking out of a drift like a ribcage. And against the far wall, half-frozen into the ice that seeped through every crack, a rusted carabiner still clipped to a length of frayed rope—the rope. *Her* rope. The one she had cut.

Ulyana stood in the entrance with the storm howling down behind them and could not make herself take another step.

"It's a camp," Tamsin whispered, brushing frost from a crate stencil-marked ALDERVANE. "This is—it's the Aldervane Expedition. The one where the guide..." She stopped. Ulyana felt the girl's eyes find her back like a hand.

"Where the guide abandoned her client," Oskar finished quietly. He had gone very still. "Everyone in the trade knows this story. Four clients went up. Three came down. The guide was never hired again anywhere from here to the coast."

"Oskar." Brann's voice carried a warning.

"No, let him talk." Ulyana turned at last. Outside, the pursuers' shouts were being eaten by the rising wind; the storm was closing the pass like a door. They had hours, maybe less, before it became a tomb for anyone caught in the open—including three men who wanted Corliss's map badly enough to die for it. But that truth could wait. This one couldn't. "You all trusted me with your lives for six days. You deserve to know whose lives you were trusting them *to*."

She told them.

She told them about the Aldervane climb, seven years ago, when the weather turned early and turned vicious. About the cornice collapse that put a crevasse between her and her four clients—Aldervane himself, and the Voss brothers, and a quiet young surveyor named Pell. About the rope stretched across the gap, groaning, the ice around its anchor failing, holding weight it was never meant to hold. About the arithmetic that lived in her hands before it ever reached her head: the anchor would hold two more minutes, perhaps three. Four climbers on the line. She could reach three of them if she cut the fourth loose.

"I chose," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's, scraped raw by the wind. "I chose the three I could reach fastest. Pell was furthest down the rope. I cut him loose to save the others, and I have spent seven years not saying his name out loud, because the guild called it abandonment and the mountains called it murder and I could never decide which of them was right."

Silence, except the storm. Juniper shifted uneasily against the rock wall.

"So it's true," Oskar said. Not cruel. Worse than cruel—careful. "You left a man to die."

"I made a choice no one should have to make, and I chose wrong, or I chose right, and I don't know which, and neither does anyone else, because the only witnesses were three men who lived and one who didn't." She looked at each of them. "That's why I hide in lowland trading posts under a false name. That's why I took Corliss's promise even though it led here. Because some part of me thought—if I could finish one delivery, one honest thing, all the way through—maybe the ledger would balance."

Tamsin stepped forward. Ulyana braced for the girl's disgust, and it hurt more than she'd expected, how much she'd come to dread losing it.

But Tamsin just looked at the rusted carabiner in the ice, and then at her. "Pell," she said. "Say his name again."

"...Pell."

"Good." The girl nodded once, fierce. "He gets to be a person, not a story you use to punish yourself. That's all."

Brann grunted—an old man's sound, unreadable—and began unpacking the stove. Oskar said nothing, but he unrolled his maps near hers, shoulder to shoulder, which was its own kind of verdict.

Then Ulyana heard it beneath the wind: shouting outside. Fainter now, ragged, human. The three men in the storm, lost, their tracks filling in behind them, calling to each other in voices already slurred with cold.

She closed her eyes. Every sensible instinct she owned said leave them. The mountain would do the work. No trial, no testimony, no risk.

"Pell was furthest down the rope," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "And I still went toward him first. Remember that about me, whatever else is true."

She pulled her hood up and stepped out into the white.

Behind her, after a heartbeat, she heard three sets of boots follow—and ahead, through the driving sheets of the blizzard, the faint, desperate flicker of a lantern swinging in the dark, coming toward them through the storm.
