## 4. Delivered Before Snow

Tamsin had never seen snow fall like this—not drifting, not settling, but *hunting*, flung sideways in white fists that struck the sled runners like thrown gravel. She crouched on the load between the oilcloth bundles, one mittened hand hooked through a rope loop, and watched Ulyana's back bend into the wind ahead of them like a woman leaning against a door that wanted to open.

"Half a mile!" Oskar shouted from behind the mules, his voice shredded to ribbons by the gale. Brann answered with something that might have been a word or might have been a curse; either way, the mules leaned harder into their collars.

Hollowmere showed itself all at once, the way a ship shows itself out of fog. One moment there was nothing but the grey roar of the pass and the black teeth of the ridgeline; the next, lamplight—dozens of lamps strung along a single crooked street, glowing amber through the murk like embers raked out of a fire. Tamsin felt her chest crack open with something she didn't have a name for. Not relief exactly. Something more like *arrival*.

They came down the last slope in a stumbling rush, and then they were among buildings, and the wind dropped away as if it had lost interest, and there were people—miners in wool caps, a woman hauling a barrel, children shrieking and chasing one another through the falling white. Everyone stopped to stare at the four ragged figures and two exhausted mules staggering down the middle of the street.

Ulyana pulled up at the well in the town square, and for a long moment she just stood there, snow crusting her eyelashes, looking at the houses as though she were reading something written on them.

"The Corliss house," she said at last, and her voice was strange—careful, the way you'd handle a cracked plate. "Oskar?"

"End of the lane. The one with the blue door." He hesitated. "You want me to knock?"

"No." Ulyana unwound her scarf with stiff fingers. "I made a promise. I'll keep it myself."

Tamsin fell into step beside her without being asked. Nobody told her not to.

* * *

The blue door was worse than weathered—it was abandoned, paint peeling in curls, a notice nailed to it that had faded past reading. But smoke curled from the chimney, thin and stubborn, and when Ulyana knocked, boots sounded inside almost immediately.

The woman who opened the door was perhaps thirty, with her father's same heavy jaw and eyes red-rimmed from more than cold. She looked at the four of them standing in the blizzard's spill of light, and Tamsin saw her gaze snag on Ulyana's face and stay there.

"You knew him," Vera Corliss said quietly.

"I promised him I'd come."

Vera stepped back without another word, and they crowded into a small room where a fire burned low and a half-packed trunk stood open against the wall. On the table lay a letter, sealed but unaddressed.

"He wrote me every year," Vera said. "Every year, and I never once wrote back. And then three weeks ago the letters stopped, and I—" Her voice caught. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I was packing to go find him when you arrived."

Ulyana took off her gloves, finger by finger, and drew the map from its oilskin wrapping. She laid it on the table between the letter and the trunk, smoothing it flat with both hands, and Tamsin watched her do it with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children.

"There's silver up there," Ulyana said. "A vein he found and never worked. This is how you get to it. It's yours now."

Vera stared at the map. Then at Ulyana. Then at the map again, and something moved across her face—grief and wonder and a kind of fierce, disbelieving anger all tangled together.

"Do you know what this is worth?"

"I know what it cost him," Ulyana said. "That was enough."

"You could name your price. Any price. You carried it across the Wreckline in the dying of the year—"

"I don't want a share." Ulyana said it simply, without drama, the way you'd decline a second cup of tea. "I brought what I was asked to bring. That's the whole of it."

And Tamsin, watching from the doorway, understood suddenly that this was the truest thing she'd ever heard anyone say. Ulyana Amberdale, who had crossed a mountain range with a dead man's promise strapped to her back, wanted nothing from it. Nothing except—

Except to have done it. Except to be, for one moment, the person who kept her word.

* * *

They didn't even make it back to the inn before the partners found them.

Tamsin heard them first—the crunch of too many boots moving in step—and turned to see five men coming up the lane through the snowfall, led by a broad-shouldered man with a grey beard and a pistol worn openly on his hip. Verrick. She'd only seen him once before, through the window of the trading post, but she'd know that walk anywhere: the walk of a man accustomed to rooms going quiet when he entered them.

"Amberdale." He stopped ten feet away. His men fanned out behind him, casual as anything, blocking the lane. "We've been riding hard to catch you. Thought we'd save you the trouble of the pass."

"The pass is behind us," Ulyana said.

"So it is." Verrick smiled without warmth. "Which means the map is here, and so are you, and Hollowmere's got maybe an hour before this storm seals everything in. Nice quiet place for a conversation about property that belongs to my company."

"It belongs to Vera Corliss."

"It belongs to whoever holds paper on it, and I hold paper." He took a step forward. Behind Tamsin, Brann shifted his weight, and Oskar moved—subtly, deliberately—to stand where he'd be between Verrick and Ulyana. Tamsin did the math on the men, the pistol, the distance, and did it again because she didn't like the answer.

Then doors began to open.

It happened slowly, the way weather happens. A miner stepping out onto his stoop, arms folded. The woman with the barrel, setting it down and staying beside it. Children gathered at windows. An old man on the porch of the assay office, whittling, not looking up but not leaving either. Within a minute there were thirty people in that lane, silent, watching, and every one of them between Verrick's men and the four travelers.

"Hollowmere doesn't care for your company's methods," said the old man, without pausing his knife. "We remember what happened at Kestrel Gulch."

"This is none of your concern—"

"Then you won't mind doing it somewhere else." The old man looked up at last. "After the lady tells us her side. Word travels fast in a storm, son. We've been hearing pieces of it since the sleds came down the hill. Now we'll hear the rest."

And Tamsin watched Ulyana Amberdale stand in the falling snow before thirty strangers and a man with a gun, and she watched her take a breath like a diver taking air, and begin.

She told it all. The expedition six years ago, the client who slipped on the scree, the rope team anchored over the drop, the choice made in nine seconds that took eleven years to explain. Three lives held on one side of her, one life on the other, and no way to hold both. She told them about the inquiry, the blacklist, the whispers that followed her from range to range like a smell she couldn't wash off. She told them she had run ever since, working under false names, sleeping in stables, until a dying prospector handed her a reason to stop running and she hadn't even known that was what she needed.

"I left a man to die," she finished, and her voice didn't shake. "I saved three others doing it. I've never decided if those facts forgive each other. I only know I'm done hiding from the question."

The silence afterward was enormous. Snow ticked softly against coat shoulders. Somewhere a shutter banged.

It was Vera Corliss who broke it. She came out of the blue-doored house with the map still in her hands and stood beside Ulyana, shoulder to shoulder, facing the lane.

"My father chose her," Vera said. "A dying man with everything to lose chose the disgraced guide to carry his fortune home. If that's not testimony, I don't know what is."

"She abandoned a client," Verrick said flatly.

"And saved three people," said the old whittler. "I read the inquiry report, Mr. Verrick. It came through here six years ago. Every guide in this valley knows it by heart—we teach it to apprentices. You'd know that too, if you'd ever asked anyone in this town anything except what they'd sell."

Verrick's men looked at each other. Thirty faces looked back. The pistol stayed holstered, and everyone understood why: some arithmetic even armed men decline.

"We'll be back when the passes open," Verrick said at last, but his voice had gone thin, and everyone heard it.

"Likely," said the old man, and went back to his whittling.

* * *

The blizzard arrived in earnest that night, and Tamsin stood at the inn window with a mug of cocoa going cold in her hands, watching the world outside dissolve into white roar. By morning the pass would be shut. By morning, nothing would be getting in or out of Hollowmere for months.

Behind her, the common room glowed and murmured. Brann was teaching Oskar a dice game and cheating badly enough that Oskar kept winning, which both of them pretended not to notice. Vera sat close to the fire with the map open on her knees, tracing her father's handwriting with one fingertip, crying and smiling at the same time. And Ulyana sat apart, as always, but not far—as near to the others as she'd ever let herself sit—and Tamsin saw her look around the room, slowly, like a woman memorizing something.

Their eyes met. Ulyana didn't look away this time.

"Come sit down, Tamsin," she said. "You're letting the cold in."

Tamsin set down the cocoa and went. Outside, the snow piled higher against the glass, sealing the pass, sealing the road, sealing away every place she'd ever run from. Inside, someone had saved her a seat.

In the spring, she thought, there would be new routes to map, and Vera's mine to help open, and whatever trouble Verrick brought back with the thaw. In the spring there would be work.

But tonight there was only the fire, and the family she'd stowed away into, and the long white silence of a mountain finally, mercifully, letting them rest.
