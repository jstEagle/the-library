## 1. Miles, Not Years

Beatrix Marchetti measured his life in track miles the way other men measured it in birthdays. Twelve years since the Kestrel Tunnel came down on a Tuesday morning in April — four thousand two hundred and eleven miles of rail walked, surveyed, flagged, and forgotten since. He kept the number in a leather notebook that lived in his breast pocket, and every night he added the day's distance in a hand so small and precise it looked like typesetting.

He did not count years. Years were how you counted what you'd lost. Miles were how you counted what you'd outrun.

The contract had come to him in November, folded inside a plain envelope with no return address: *Survey and condition assessment, decommissioned coastal spur, Grayhaven Junction to Cape Verity, seventeen point three miles.* No client name. A check enclosed that was generous enough to be insulting. Beatrix had stared at it for a long time over the greasy table of a diner outside Corvallis, and then he'd signed anyway, because a line nobody wanted was a line where nobody would ask him questions, and because seventeen miles of dead track along a winter coast sounded like exactly the kind of silence he'd been buying by the mile for twelve years.

He arrived at Grayhaven Junction on the last day of January, with a transit level, a chain, forty pounds of instruments, and a mule named Bess who was smarter than most of the men he'd worked for. The station house was boarded. The platform had gone soft as old bread under his boots. Out past the end of the rusted bumper rail, the land simply stopped, and beyond it the sea went on forever, gray-green and patient, working at the cliffs the way time works at a man's memory — slowly, and without mercy, and never once pausing to see if he minded.

* * *

The first week was good work, if you could call it that. Good silence, anyway.

The coastal spur had been abandoned nine years ago when the company rerouted freight inland, and the rails were still there, brown with rust, half-buried in beach grass and gorse. Beatrix walked them mile by mile, chaining distances, checking culverts, noting which ties had rotted through and which bridges had sagged past saving. He wrote his findings in the notebook in that same small precise hand. He cooked beans over a spirit stove. He listened to the wind come off the water and felt nothing in particular about anything at all, which was the closest thing to peace he knew how to find.

At night he slept in the old section shed at Mile 9, a plank building with a stove and a cot some long-gone trackwalker had left behind, and he read by lamplight from a paperback western with the cover torn off, and he did not think about tunnels. He was very good at not thinking about tunnels. He'd had twelve years of practice, and like any skill it had cost him everything he had and made him excellent at it.

On the eighth day the weather turned.

It came in off the ocean at dusk without any manners at all — a wall of black cloud dragging rain sideways, and behind the rain a wind that made the shed timbers groan like a ship. Beatrix staked Bess in the lee of the building, double-checked the stove pipe, and sat up late listening to the storm try to take the roof off. He'd worked mountain lines in avalanche country. He knew what wind could do. But this was different; this was the sea's wind, wet and enormous, a thing with weight behind it, and around two in the morning a wave came ashore somewhere north of him with a sound like a door slamming in the earth itself.

He lay awake after that and let himself do the thing he never let himself do, which was remember. Just for a minute. Daniel Voss laughing in the mouth of the Kestrel Tunnel, saying, *Bea, you worry like an old woman, this rock's been standing a million years, it'll stand a million more.* Daniel going in first because he always went in first. The sound the mountain made — not a crash, not at first, but a kind of deep clearing of its throat, a rumble like the world swallowing — and then the dust, and the dark, and Beatrix clawing at rubble with his hands until they were ruined, screaming a name into stone that did not answer.

Four thousand two hundred and eleven miles since. He fell asleep with the notebook open on his chest.

* * *

The storm blew itself out sometime before dawn, the way storms do — all that fury spent and nothing to show for it but wreckage and a strange, scrubbed-clean light.

Beatrix stepped out of the shed into air so cold it hurt his teeth. The sky was the pale gray of dishwater rinsing pink in the east. Bess regarded him with her usual expression of mild disappointment. Everything looked ordinary. Everything looked exactly as it should.

Except the sound.

The sea was wrong. He'd spent eight days learning its voice — the long sigh and drag of it against the shingle below the bluff, the rhythm of it, steady as a heartbeat. This morning the rhythm was broken. There was a new note in it, a hollow booming, rhythmic but deeper, like breath drawn through a bottle, and it was coming from the north, from the stretch of cliff at Mile 11 where the line ran closest to the edge.

He walked out to look. It was the kind of thing an engineer does without deciding to: something is wrong with your structure, you go and look. Seventeen miles of railway was his responsibility now, even a dead one. Even one nobody would ever ride again. Especially that one.

The frost had silvered the gorse. His boots crunched. The booming got louder with every hundred yards, and by the time he topped the rise at Mile 10 and a half he could feel it in his chest, a slow pulse, in and out, in and out.

Then he saw what the sea had done in the night, and he stopped walking.

The storm had taken the cliff face. Not eroded it — *taken* it, sheared away a whole slab of headland fifty feet high and eighty feet across, leaving a raw scar of fresh rock where eight days ago there had been grass and gorse and a safety fence whose posts still stood, absurdly, at the lip of nothing. Below, where the cliff had been, the fallen face lay tumbled across the shore like the bones of something enormous. And where the cliff had been solid —

There was a hole.

A cave mouth, tall as a boxcar door, opening out of the fresh-cut rock above the tideline, black as a pupil. As Beatrix watched, a swell rolled in beneath it and the cave breathed — that hollow boom, in and out — exhaling spray in a white plume that drifted and dissolved in the cold light.

He stood there a long time. Long enough for his feet to go numb. Long enough for the sun to clear the horizon and throw his shadow long and thin across the dead rails, pointing at the hole like an accusation.

Caves in coastal bluffs weren't remarkable. He knew that. Water found its way into limestone and sandstone and carved rooms where no room should be; he'd seen a dozen sea caves in his life and thought nothing of any of them. But this one had been *sealed*. He could see it even from here, even at this distance: the exposed cross-section of the rock showed strata running clean and unbroken right up to the edges of the cavity, and around the mouth itself the stone was dressed — squared, tooled, the marks of picks and chisels still visible in the fresh face. Men had closed this opening once, deliberately, and packed it, and built a cliff's worth of concealment over it, or enough of one that the sea had needed a century and one furious night to undo the work.

And wedged high in the mouth of it, jammed between two blocks of the collapsed facing where the storm had shaken it loose, hung a lantern.

Even at eighty yards Beatrix could see it was old — a brass-framed thing, green with patina, swinging gently in the cave's slow breathing. And stamped into the brass plate on its front, catching the early sun, something written. Letters. Numbers.

He told himself he wasn't going down there. He told himself it wasn't his problem; he was hired to survey track, not geology, and the sensible thing was to note the washout in his report, flag the mileage, and wire the client. He said all of this to himself quite clearly while he walked back to the shed, harnessed Bess, loaded the transit and the chain and a coil of rope, and rode north along the dead line toward the hole in the world.

The cliff base was reachable at low tide, and low tide was in ninety minutes. He used the time badly — checking the rope twice, checking it a third time, drinking coffee he didn't taste. Then he left Bess tied well back from the edge and picked his way down the talus of the fallen cliff face, testing each block before he trusted it, engineer's habit, until he stood on the shore below the cave mouth with the swell surging in the rocks around his boots and the boom of the breathing cave directly overhead.

The lantern was wedged fifteen feet up, just inside the shadow of the arch. He climbed to it on the fallen blocks, fingers burning with cold, and worked it free, and turned it over in his hands.

Brass. Heavy. Salt-eaten but whole, the glass fogged white inside with age. And on the plate, struck deep in letters meant to last:

**G.C.R.R. — SURVEY PARTY No. 4 — 1911**

Grayhaven Coast Rail Road. Survey Party Number Four. Beatrix stood there holding it while the cave breathed over his head, and the cold went through him in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

Because he knew that name. Every man who'd ever worked these lines knew it, the way children know the name of the thing under the bed. Survey Party Four had gone out along this coast in the autumn of 1911 — nine men, instruments, a full mapping commission — and had never come back. No bodies. No equipment. No trace. The company had called it a tragedy of the sea, paid out settlements to nine widows, and quietly abandoned the entire coastal extension, and for a hundred and fourteen years the people of Grayhaven had told their children about the Wreck Cave, the hole in the cliff that swallowed nine men whole.

Beatrix had heard the story as a young man and filed it where he filed all such things: under *not my problem.*

Now he stood under the cave itself with the proof of it swinging from his fist, and the tide was turning, and the water was coming back in around his boots, cold as a hand closing.

He looked up into the black of the mouth, and the black looked back, patient as the sea, and Beatrix Marchetti — who had spent twelve years and four thousand two hundred miles learning how not to feel anything at all — felt the numbness crack straight down the middle like ice under load.

He reported it that evening. One telegram, sent from the junction office at Grayhaven, addressed to the unnamed client, stating facts only: cliff failure at Mile 11, exposure of sealed cavity, artifact recovered bearing company mark dated 1911. Request instructions.

The reply came back before dawn, faster than any telegram had a right to travel, typed on company letterhead with no signature at the bottom:

*Recovery team required immediately. You will lead. Triple rate. Depart within 48 hours. Further instructions to follow.*

Beatrix read it twice, standing in the cold office with the lamp turned low, and the words *you will lead* sat on the page like a hook sitting in bait.

Nobody tripled a man's pay to look at rocks. Nobody moved that fast unless they already knew what was in the cave — and were afraid of who else might find it.

Outside, the sea boomed against the new-made cliff, in and out, in and out, breathing over its dead.
