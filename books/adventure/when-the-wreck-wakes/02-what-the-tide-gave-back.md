## 2. What the Tide Gave Back

The storm died sometime before dawn, the way storms do on this coast — not with a farewell but with a shrug, as if it had simply grown bored of them. Beatrix Marchetti woke in the survey car to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath, and lay still for a moment listening for what was missing. The wind was gone. The rain had stopped its drumming on the roof. And underneath both, faint but unmistakable, the sea sounded wrong.

He'd spent enough winters along water to know its moods the way he knew rail joints by the sound of wheels over them — the flat slap of a calm sea, the ragged roar of a working one. This was neither. This was a long, low exhalation, rhythmic and strange, like something breathing through a passage that hadn't been there before.

He dressed in the dark, boots cold from the floor, and stepped out into a world scrubbed clean and glittering. The sky over the water was the pale gray of unpolished steel, streaked with the first dishonest pink of morning. The cliffs south of camp ran down toward the old right-of-way in tiers of black basalt and clay, and last night they had been a wall against which the whole Pacific threw itself. This morning there was a hole in them.

Beatrix stood very still.

The cliff face had sheared away in the night — a slab of it, forty feet high and twice that wide, lying now in shattered blocks across the shingle below like the bones of some enormous animal. And where the wall had been, exposed raw and dripping, was an opening. A cave mouth, tall as a boxcar door, its edges still crumbling, seawater sliding out of it in long silver sheets with each retreating wave. That was the breathing he'd heard. The sea going in and out of a throat the storm had cut open.

He should have felt nothing. That was the arrangement he'd made with himself twelve years ago: look at the work, log the work, be the work. A cave in a cliff was geology. Geology was not grief.

But his heart was going anyway, hard and quick, and he didn't know why until he was halfway down the slope, boots skidding on wet clay, telling himself he only wanted a look at the structural damage because the line he'd been hired to survey ran within two hundred yards of that face and any mass wasting event was a hazard to the alignment. All true. All beside the point.

The shingle crunched under him, acres of rounded stone still streaming with runoff. Up close the cave mouth was worse than it had looked from above — not a clean fracture but a wound, the rock around it bruised in shades of rust and ocher where water had bled through the new exposure. He stood at the edge of the outflow, letting the cold spray hit his face, and looked inside.

The passage went back farther than the light would follow. Twenty feet in, the daylight gave up, and beyond that was a darkness with texture to it, a blackness that seemed less empty than withheld. The air coming out of it smelled of salt and stone and something older underneath — a mineral smell, ironish, like the inside of an old tunnel. Like home, said a voice in his head he did not listen to.

That was when he saw the lantern.

It was wedged in a crack in the rock six feet up the cave's eastern lip, half-buried in the rubble the collapse had dragged down with it, and if the storm had put it there it had done so with a cruel sense of theater. It was a hand lantern, brass once, now scaled orange with rust, its glass fogged white with age. Beatrix climbed to it, worked it loose with both hands — it came free with a grinding reluctance, as if the rock had been holding it a long time and resented giving it up — and turned it over.

Stamped into the base, beneath the corrosion, letters still legible:

**C.P.C.R. — 1911**

Coastal Pacific Coast Railway. The company whose dead line he was being paid to walk. The company whose founding charter he'd read in the county records office three weeks ago, looking for alignment surveys and finding instead a gap — a season, in late 1911, when the correspondence simply stopped, as though everyone involved had agreed at once to stop writing things down.

And the legend came back to him then, the one the stationmaster at Millbrook had told him over bad coffee his first night on the coast, laughing a little as he told it, the way people laugh at stories that frighten them. The Wreck Cave. In the winter of 1911 a survey party went into the cliffs to chart a route through them — nine men, instruments, a full complement — and never came out. No bodies. No equipment washed ashore. The company searched for eleven days and then, abruptly, stopped searching, paid off the widows, and moved the line inland. The sea kept them, people said. The sea swallowed the expedition whole.

Beatrix stood on the shingle with the lantern in his hands and felt the numbness he had maintained for twelve years shift along its seams, the way ice shifts before it goes.

He did not believe in legends. He believed in load-bearing capacity and drainage gradients and the angle of repose. But he also believed that rock did not lie, and the rock here was telling him something: that collapse had not opened this cave. Collapse had *closed* it, once, long ago — he could see it now that he knew to look, the older scar higher up the face, healed over with decades of lichen and soil, a seam in the cliff like a badly set bone. The storm hadn't created the cave mouth. It had torn the bandage off.

Someone had sealed this place. And someone had left a lantern in the door.

* * *

He reported it the way he reported everything: precisely, without adjectives. The radio crackle carried his voice north to the district office while he paced the length of the survey car, the lantern sitting on the table between the transit case and his cold coffee, dripping rust onto the schematic of the coastal alignment.

"Cliff failure at approximately mile marker forty-one point three," he said. "Estimated four hundred cubic yards of displacement. Exposure of a previously unknown sea cave. Artifact recovered bearing Coastal Pacific markings dated 1911. Recommend geological assessment before any further work on the lower alignment."

A pause on the line. Then a voice he didn't recognize — not the district engineer he'd dealt with for three weeks, but someone new, someone whose vowels were boardroom vowels. "Mr. Marchetti. Hold the site. Do not enter the cave. We'll have someone to you today."

"I haven't given you my report reference—"

"We have your file, Mr. Marchetti." A pause, and then the voice changed register entirely, warmed like a stove. "In fact, we've been impressed with your file. Your tunnel work especially."

Something went cold along Beatrix's spine, a sensation with a specific address, twelve years deep. "My tunnel work is public record."

"All the best work is." Papers shuffled somewhere far away. "Here's our situation. That cave sits directly on the corridor for the Meridian extension — you'll have seen the land parcels. Before anything else happens, we need to know exactly what's in there. Officially it's a recovery and documentation operation. Unofficially—" the warmth again, "—we want it handled quickly and quietly, and we're prepared to pay for quick and quiet. Triple your contract rate. You'd lead the team. Full authority on site."

Beatrix looked at the lantern. Rust-water had pooled on the table beneath it in a small stain shaped like nothing at all, or like everything.

"Triple rate," he said, "to go into a cave your own company sealed in 1911 after losing nine men in it."

The silence on the line lasted three seconds too long, and in those three seconds Beatrix heard more than any words would have told him. He heard a man deciding how much truth a stranger could be trusted with, and deciding on none.

"The 1911 incident predates the current organization," the voice said finally, smooth as ballast gravel. "We consider it closed history. This is about safety and schedule, Mr. Marchetti. Can you have a team assembled by Thursday?"

Every instinct he owned — the ones that had kept him alive in collapsing ground, the ones that had failed Daniel Voss — were all saying the same word. *No.* Walk away. Log the hazard, flag the anomaly, take the fee for the coastal survey and let the cave keep whatever it was keeping. There were men who dug into the past the way other men dug into hillsides, and he had watched what came down on top of them.

But there was another voice too, quieter, one he hadn't heard in years, and it sounded like Mara Voss on her porch in the rain, asking him the same question she'd asked for twelve years: *What happened in that tunnel, Beatrix? What aren't you telling me?*

His tunnel had a seal on it too. His tunnel had a body behind the seal. And he understood, standing in the rust-stained light of the survey car, that this cave and that tunnel were the same shape — that companies buried their mistakes in rock, and that rock, given enough time and enough storm, always gave them back.

"Thursday," he said.

"Wonderful. You'll have support staff assigned — corporate will send an archivist to handle the documentation, and there's a diver on the regional roster for the underwater approaches. Details to follow." The voice paused, and when it resumed there was something almost gentle in it, which was somehow the worst thing yet. "One more thing, Mr. Marchetti. Whatever you found at the site — the artifact — corporate requests that it remain in your custody. Off the books. For chain-of-custody purposes."

Meaning: tell no one. Meaning: this conversation never happened. Meaning: you work for us now, and you knew that before you said yes.

The line clicked dead.

Beatrix sat down slowly on the bench seat and picked up the lantern. Up close, in the gray window light, he could see scratches on the glass — fine, deliberate marks, not weathering. He tilted it, caught the light, and his breath stopped.

Letters. Scratched into the glass from the inside, by someone with time and a sharp point and very little hope. Four characters, crude but certain:

**HELP**

Below them, smaller, crowded against the rim:

**9 OF US**

Beatrix set the lantern down with hands that were not quite steady, and looked out the window at the cliff, at the raw wound in it, at the sea sliding in and out of the dark.

Nine men had gone in. Nine men had known they were sealed in. And for a hundred and thirteen years, the company that owned this coast had paid triple rates to make sure no one asked what happened next.

Thursday, they'd said. He had until Thursday to assemble a team.

He wondered, watching the tide begin its slow climb back into the cave's dark throat, whether he was leading a recovery —

or walking into the second burial.
