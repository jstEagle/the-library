## 3. A Crew of Strays

**Beatrix**

The company sent his crew by the morning freight, and Beatrix met it at the coast junction with a clipboard and a bad feeling.

He had asked for three men and a diver. What stepped down from the caboose was something else entirely.

First came a woman in a city coat too fine for salt air, carrying a document case chained to her wrist like a prisoner's ball. She looked at the cliffs, at the gulls, at Beatrix himself, with the flat appraisal of someone pricing a used engine.

"Ferrand," she said. "Odile Ferrand. Corporate archives, seconded." She produced papers stamped with the seal he'd come to distrust on sight — the same seal that had offered him triple pay for a recovery job that should have been a salvage crew's routine work. "I'm told you found something."

"I reported a cave."

"You reported *the* cave." Her eyes were gray and unblinking. "I've read every file this company ever burned or buried, Mr. Marchetti. I know what 1911 cost them. I was hired to make sure it costs them nothing now."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because I want you to understand I'm not one of them." A pause, the first crack in her composure. "Not anymore. They retired me eleven months ago without telling me. I found out when my keycard stopped working. So whatever they think they're sending me to do—" she lifted the chained case slightly, "—I intend to do the opposite."

Beatrix studied her a long moment. Twelve years of measuring people in track miles had taught him one thing: everyone riding a line has a reason they're not flying. He didn't trust her. But he recognized the freight she carried.

"Second car," he said. "Bunks are lumpy. Complaints go to the sea."

Behind her came a broad, quiet man with dive gear slung over one shoulder like a sleeping animal. Tomas Reyes said perhaps forty words all day, and half of them were "rope" and "no." When Beatrix asked about his certifications, Reyes handed him a laminated card and looked out at the water as if it owed him money. The card listed more qualifications than Beatrix had fingers. It did not list a reason.

He'd learn it eventually or he wouldn't. That was how crews worked.

It was the third car that broke him open.

He heard her before he saw her — a sneeze, small and catastrophic, from behind a stack of survey crates. Then a boot heel scraping, then silence so deliberate it was practically a confession.

Beatrix pulled the tarp back.

A girl. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, folded into the gap between a crate of dynamite primer and a case of emergency rations, wearing a man's coat cut down badly at the sleeves and an expression of magnificent defiance, as though *she* had caught *him* hiding.

"Juniper Hale," she said. "You're Beatrix Marchetti. Your hat's ridiculous."

"That's not your name on any manifest I signed."

"No," she agreed. "Because you didn't sign me."

"The freight terminates here and turns around."

"So does everything else, apparently." She unfolded herself and stood — tall, thin, chin up, eyes red-rimmed in a way that suggested the defiance was load-bearing. "There's nothing back where I came from except a town that sold its schoolhouse to pay a debt to your company. I read about the cave in a newspaper somebody used to wrap fish. Wreck Cave. 1911. Eleven men went in and nobody ever wrote down what happened to them." Her voice wobbled once; she killed it. "Somebody should write it down. Somebody always should."

Beatrix looked at her for a long time. Behind her, Ferrand had drifted close, listening, saying nothing. Even Reyes had turned from the water.

"There's a train back at six," Beatrix said finally. "You'll be on it."

"There's nothing back at six."

"That's not my problem."

"No," she said, and something in her face shifted — not softer, exactly, but truer. "Your problem is that you keep saying things aren't your problem until they kill somebody. My father worked for you people. Section gang, mile forty-one. There was a rockslide and the company said the schedule mattered more than the warning signs. Nobody wrote that down either. So I'm going to stay until somebody does."

The wind came off the sea and filled the silence between them. Beatrix felt twelve years of carefully measured distance shift under him like ballast.

"Bunks are lumpy," he said at last. "Complaints go to the sea."

Juniper Hale grinned like sunrise through storm damage.

**Odile**

Odile Ferrand had spent nineteen years learning that documents tell the truth only when no one is watching them, and people lie even then. She watched her new crew over the first supper of tinned stew and hard bread, and catalogued them the way she catalogued everything: by what they were hiding.

Marchetti hid grief. It was in the way he counted things — rails, cans, minutes — as if arithmetic could hold a wall up. She'd seen that habit before, in files. Men who survived things other men didn't became accountants of the physical world.

Reyes hid a name. He ate facing the door, and when Juniper asked him where he'd learned to dive, he said, "Work," and the word had a tombstone's weight. Odile had processed enough severance files to know a man who'd been blamed for something. The question was whether he'd earned the blame or been assigned it.

The girl hid nothing well and everything deeply. Juniper talked constantly — about the cave, about 1911, about the geology she'd taught herself from a library book — but never about the coat she slept in, which had a name tape inside the collar, carefully unpicked, the ghost of the stitching still legible if you knew to look. Odile knew to look. H-ALE. Her own name, then. Or her father's.

And Odile herself? She hid the file. The one she'd copied the night before her keycard died — the one that made her certain the company hadn't sent her to recover records but to confirm their destruction. She'd chained the copy to her wrist because she trusted locks less than she trusted her own stubbornness.

"Survey starts at dawn," Marchetti said, breaking her inventory. "Low tide's at seven-forty. We go in, we map, we come out. Nobody touches anything that isn't structural."

"The journals," Juniper said.

"If there are journals, Ferrand touches them. That's what she's for."

"How touching," Odile said dryly, and was rewarded with the ghost of a smile from a man who seemed to ration them like wartime sugar.

Reyes spoke without looking up. "Water table's high after the storm. Cave floods fast. We don't linger."

"We won't," Marchetti said.

But Odile saw his eyes go to the cliff in the last light — to the black seam of the cave mouth above the tideline, where a rusted lantern hung wedged in stone like a lantern hung by a hand — and she saw something else there too. Recognition. Not of the cave.

Of the pattern.

She leaned across the table. "Mr. Marchetti. In 1911, the company paid out triple indemnities to eleven families within nine days, sealed the site, and bought the headland parcel at distressed value before the bodies were cold. I know because I filed the paperwork of everything they failed to destroy." She kept her voice low, beneath the girl's chatter. "Twelve years ago, your tunnel. Same signature. Same speed. Same seal."

His jaw tightened. "You don't know anything about my tunnel."

"No," she agreed quietly. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Neither does anyone. And men who keep secrets that size don't take jobs like this one unless part of them is hoping the secret comes up with the tide."

For a long moment the only sound was the sea working at the cliffs below, patient as a filing clerk.

"Seven-forty," Marchetti said finally, standing. "Get some sleep, Ms. Ferrand."

She watched him walk out into the wind, shoulders squared against it like a man leaning into a curve he knew was coming. Around her, the strays settled: the girl already asleep mid-sentence, the diver awake and watching the door, the fire ticking down.

Four strangers, Odile thought, each hauling private cargo, bound for a hole in the rock that history had filled with lies.

Outside, far down the line, a lamp flickered at the junction — a train coming in off-schedule, unscheduled, unannounced.

Odile checked her watch. Nothing was due.

The lamp grew brighter.
