## 4. The Widow on the Platform

The last station before the coast was called Grey Harrow, and it was the kind of place that existed only because a timetable had once insisted it should. A single platform of cracked concrete. A signal box with its glass gone grey as cataracts. One lamp burning against the early dark, moths ticking against it like small desperate hands.

Beatrix stood at the edge of the platform with his survey case and watched the storm line crawl in off the sea, and he told himself — as he had told himself for twelve years — that the feeling in his chest was only weather.

They had stopped for water and coal. Forty minutes, Odile said, checking her watch with the grim precision of a woman who had been made to account for every minute of her professional life and did not intend to lose the habit now. Tomas was checking the coupling on the supply car for the third time since dawn, which meant he was worried about something and would not say what. Juniper was supposed to be locked in the crew compartment, having been formally caught, formally scolded, and informally fed, because none of them — not even Odile, who pretended otherwise — had the stomach to put a fifteen-year-old off a train in the middle of nowhere.

And Beatrix was standing where he always stood when a train stopped anywhere: facing the track, back to whatever might come up behind him.

He heard the heels first.

Not running. Not hurrying. The measured, deliberate click of a woman who had decided exactly how fast she wanted to arrive, which was slowly enough that you would hear her coming and have time to be ashamed.

He knew those footsteps the way he knew the sound of a rail about to fail — a frequency he could never again unhear.

"Mr. Marchetti."

Mara Voss stood six feet behind him, wrapped in a coat too good for this station, holding a leather satchel against her hip the way other people held weapons. Her hair was going silver at the temples in a way that seemed less like aging than like frost claiming ground inch by inch. She had Daniel's eyes. That was the cruelty of it. Twelve years, and every time he looked at her he saw his dead friend looking back out of a living face, patient, waiting for an answer.

"Mara." His voice came out level. He'd practiced that. "What are you doing here?"

"I'm told there's a cave." She said it simply, the way she said everything, no drama in it, which somehow made it worse. "A cave with 1911 written all over it. And I'm told the railway company is paying triple rates for men willing to go look at it in winter."

"That's company business."

"Yes," she said. "It usually is."

* * *

She had boarded at Fenwick Junction, two hours back, with a third-class ticket and a letter of introduction from an MP whose name she declined to give Odile — Odile had already told him this, in the flat tone of a woman reporting a security breach and privately enjoying it. Mara had sat in the empty rear carriage for two hours reading, and no one had known she was aboard until the train stopped and she stepped down onto the platform as if she owned it.

"You can't come with us," Beatrix said.

"No," Mara agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you'd prefer I didn't."

"That's not what I—"

"It's exactly what you mean." She moved past him along the platform, unhurried, looking at the storm-light on the hills, at the dead signal box, at anything but him, and that too was familiar: twelve years of conversations conducted in careful obliques, both of them circling the same collapsed tunnel like water finding its way around a stone. "Daniel's mother used to say you were the honest one. Did you know that? Out of the two of you, she always said, Bea's the one who'll tell you the truth even when it costs him. She said it at the funeral. She said it while they were lowering the box into the ground, and I remember thinking — yes. Yes, he'll tell me what happened. Any day now. He'll come to the house and sit in Daniel's chair and tell me."

The wind came off the moor and pushed at them both. Somewhere behind them, Tomas's wrench rang against steel.

"And then twelve years went by," Mara said, "and you never came. So I stopped waiting for the truth to visit me, Mr. Marchetti, and I started going to find it instead."

* * *

He walked with her to the end of the platform because he could not think of a way not to. That was the thing about guilt — it didn't make you run. It made you stay within reach.

She opened the satchel on the bench beneath the lamp, and the moths scattered, and she took out a letter.

He knew it before she touched it. Of course he knew it. He had written half of it.

"This came the day before he died," she said. "You wrote the address. Your hand."

"We shared a desk car. We addressed each other's letters sometimes. It meant nothing."

"It meant you were together when he wrote it." She smoothed the paper flat on her knee without opening it; she had read it so many times she no longer needed to. "Listen to what my husband wrote me, three days before your tunnel killed him. *We've found something on the new coastal extension. Something under the cliff. Bea says if it's what he thinks it is, it changes everything — the route, the money, maybe the whole company. He wants to do it right this time. He says we owe it to the ones who came before.*"

The words landed in Beatrix's chest like ballast dropped from height.

"He wasn't talking about minerals," Mara said. "I've spent twelve years learning to read between the lines of railway men. *The ones who came before.* Do you know how many expeditions vanished on this coast? Do you know what the old men in the pubs say about the Wreck Cave?"

"Pub stories aren't survey data."

"No," she agreed. "But letters are. And ledgers are. And I have spent twelve years and most of Daniel's insurance money buying both." She looked up at him then, finally, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "The company sealed something in 1911 and paid men to forget it. And eleven years ago they sealed something else and paid one man to survive it. I want to know if it's the same seal, Mr. Marchetti. I want to know if the same hands built both walls."

The lamp buzzed. Down the platform, Juniper's face appeared briefly in the window of the crew compartment — watching, always watching — and vanished.

"You should go home," Beatrix said quietly.

"Home." Mara laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of exhausted wonder. "Do you know what home is after twelve years? It's a house where a man's coat still hangs on the hook because I couldn't decide whose cowardice I resented more — his, for dying, or yours, for living and saying nothing." She folded the letter away with hands that did not quite tremble, and he understood suddenly that the trembling had been spent years ago, in the first months, and what was left was harder than grief. Grief bends. This had set, like concrete. "I'm not asking you to confess, Beatrix. I learned long ago that you'll die before you do that. I'm asking you to let me stand next to you when the truth comes out some other way. Because it will. It's standing at the bottom of that cave right now, waiting for the tide to introduce us."

* * *

Odile found them there ten minutes later, standing at opposite ends of the bench like two witnesses who'd been left alone in a courtroom. Beatrix saw the archivist take in the scene — the widow, the satchel, the engineer's face doing what it always did, which was nothing — and something shifted behind her eyes. Recognition, maybe. Two people who spent their lives around buried records knowing exactly what an unopened letter looked like.

"The water's taken on," Odile said. "Reyes wants to move. Also—" she hesitated, which Odile Ferrand never did, "—the girl found a copy of the recovery contract in my files. She has questions about clause nine."

"Which is?"

"The one authorizing disposal of 'materials deemed hazardous to ongoing operations.'" Odile's mouth went thin. "I wrote cleaner language than that, once. Before they taught me what the language was for."

Beatrix closed his eyes. Behind them the storm was coming down the coast like a debt collector, and somewhere ahead of them a hole had opened in a cliff, and inside that hole, if Mara was right — and God help him, she was right, he'd known she was right since the lantern came up out of the rock stamped with a year that matched the year in Daniel's letter — inside that hole lay the answer to a question he had spent twelve years making sure no one asked.

"Put her in the second car," he said. "Mara. Give her the bunk across from the girl."

Odile raised an eyebrow. "I thought she couldn't come with us."

"She can't." He picked up his survey case. It had never felt so heavy, and he had carried core samples up mountains with it. "But she'll be on that train whether I put her in a berth or she rides the roof, and I'd rather know where she is."

Odile studied him a moment longer than was comfortable. "You know her from before."

"I knew her husband."

"That wasn't my question."

"No," Beatrix said, and walked toward the engine, "it wasn't."

Behind him, Mara Voss gathered her satchel and followed, her heels striking the platform boards in that slow, deliberate rhythm, and Beatrix Marchetti understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching a signal drop to danger, that the story had slipped its leash. He had thought he was leading a survey. He was not. He was driving a trainload of grief and evidence into the teeth of a company that buried things, and every soul aboard was carrying a piece of the truth he had spent twelve years keeping underground.

The whistle blew. The storm reached the station and put out the lamp.

As the train pulled out of Grey Harrow into the rain, Juniper appeared beside him in the corridor, uninvited, sharp-eyed, holding out a scrap of paper she'd torn from Odile's contract file.

"Mister Marchetti," she said. "Clause nine. Somebody signed it yesterday. You're going to want to see the name."

He took the paper. Read it. And felt the tunnel close around him all over again.
