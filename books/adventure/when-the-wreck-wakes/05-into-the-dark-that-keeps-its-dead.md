## 5. Into the Dark That Keeps Its Dead

Tomas Reyes had dived wrecks off three coasts and gone down into flooded mines in two countries, and he had learned one rule above all the others: the water tells you when it's time to leave. Not with words. With pressure, with temperature, with a change in the taste of the air. Most people drowned because they stopped listening.

So this morning, standing on the shingle below the sheared cliff with his dive watch open on his wrist, he listened.

Low tide had come at 4:47. The cave mouth — that black seam the storm had torn out of the rock like a wound someone had been keeping bandaged for a century — stood exposed now, dripping, breathing out cold air that smelled of stone and salt and something older underneath. The sea was already turning. They had maybe four hours before it came back for its own.

"Four hours," he said, and Marchetti nodded like a man who'd been waiting for exactly that number.

The crew of strays assembled at the mouth: Marchetti with his survey lamp and his rolled charts, moving like the cave owed him money; the archivist, Ferrand, clutching a waterproof document case against her chest as if the records might already be inside it; the girl, Juniper, who was supposed to have been sent home from the last station and instead stood there in an oversized oilskin with her chin up, daring anyone to say otherwise. And the widow, Voss. Mara. She said little. She watched everything. Tomas had spent enough years around grieving people to know the difference between quiet and empty, and hers was not empty.

He checked their lamps himself. Checked the spare batteries, the line reels, the first aid kit. Nobody argued. They'd learned by now that when Reyes went quiet and started checking things twice, you let him check.

"Rules," he said. "Nobody touches the water once we're inside. If I say back, you go back — no questions, no arguments, no heroics." He looked at Juniper when he said it. She looked away first, which was new. "And nobody goes anywhere without the guide line. This cave doesn't care how brave you are."

"And if the tide comes early?" Ferrand asked.

"It won't," Tomas said. "But the water table might. Caves like this drink from below. You'll feel it under your boots before you see it." He clipped the first reel to a bolt of ironstone near the entrance. "Let's go meet the dead."

* * *

The passage swallowed them in stages.

First the daylight died behind them, then the sound of the surf changed — muffled, then strange, then gone entirely, replaced by the drip and tick of a place that hadn't heard voices since before anyone alive had been born. The floor sloped gently upward, which Tomas noted with relief; they were climbing away from the sea, buying time. The walls were wet black granite veined with quartz that caught the lamplight and threw it back in fragments, so that walking through the cave felt like wading through shattered glass.

Marchetti led with the surveyor's certainty of a man who had spent twelve years mapping lines no one else wanted. He read the rock the way other men read newspapers. Twice he stopped, put his palm flat against the wall, and frowned.

"What?" Ferrand asked the second time.

"Blast scars," Marchetti said. His voice had gone flat, careful. "Old ones. Deliberate. Someone shaped this passage."

"Shaped it how?"

"Made it bigger than it needed to be. Or made it smaller." He didn't elaborate. He moved on, and Tomas filed the frown away with all the other things about Beatrix Marchetti that didn't add up.

They walked forty minutes by Tomas's watch. Then the passage opened, and every one of them stopped at once, four lamps rising together like a congregation lifting candles.

It was a chamber the size of a chapel, domed, dry — impossibly dry, given the ocean pressing against the rock on every side. And it was furnished.

A camp. Intact. Preserved.

Canvas tents sagged but held, their guy-lines still taut between iron pitons. A folding table stood against the far wall bearing brass instruments — theodolite, levels, a compass rose etched into a slate board — all of it green with age but whole, arranged with a precision that made the hair rise on Tomas's neck. Tools hung on nails driven into the rock. A tin lantern sat on a crate, unlit, its glass clean. Boots were paired neatly beside a bedroll. A coat hung on a hook.

Everything in its place. Everything waiting.

"They didn't die here," Ferrand whispered. Her professional composure had cracked wide open; her hands shook as she lifted a leather journal from the table. "This is — look at this. This isn't abandonment. This is *order*. Someone tidied up."

"Or everyone did," Marchetti said quietly. "Before the end."

Juniper drifted toward the tents, and Tomas almost called her back, then didn't, because what she'd found deserved a witness. "There's more," she said, and her voice had lost all its stowaway bravado. "There's — oh. Oh no."

Bones.

They lay along the eastern wall, seven of them — seven skeletons, and Tomas counted twice because he needed it to be wrong and it wasn't — laid side by side on canvas ground sheets, arms folded over sternums, heads toward the chamber's center. Someone had covered each with a blanket, folded down to the waist with care. At each skull's right hand rested a personal effect: a pipe, a pocket watch, a wedding ring threaded onto a bootlace, a child's carved wooden horse.

Not scattered. Not panicked. Arranged.

"The survivors buried them," Mara Voss said. Her voice was steady in a way that cost her something visible. "Whoever lived long enough. They laid them out and covered them and put their things where their families could find them." She crouched beside the nearest skeleton, close but not touching, the way you kneel at a grave. "Hello," she said softly, absurdly, and Tomas had to look away.

Ferrand had opened the journal. Her lamp trembled over the page. "Hollis," she read. "E. Hollis, expedition lead. The handwriting's beautiful. Survey notes, mineral assays — there are seams in these walls, real ones, copper and something they're calling argentiferous galena, and Hollis writes like a man who thinks he's found El Dorado." She turned pages faster. "Dates. It's all dated. September 1911, October... they were here two months. They knew the tide tables. They had a rotation for going in and out." She stopped.

Tomas saw it happen — saw the exact moment the wonder curdled. Ferrand's face went white around the mouth.

"What," Marchetti said.

Ferrand didn't answer him. She read aloud instead, her voice thin:

"*November 3rd. The entrance has been closed to us. Not collapsed — closed. The fall is cut stone, dressed blocks, mortared joints. There is powder smoke in the air that did not come from our own stores. Rourke climbed to the chimney vent and reports timber shoring beyond, set from outside. We are not entombed by accident. We are entombed by design.*"

Silence took the chamber. Even the drip seemed to hold itself.

"Keep reading," Marchetti said. His voice sounded like it had traveled a long way to get there.

"*We have food for nine days if we are careful. The spring holds. I write this record so that whoever finds us will know we did not lose our way, did not grow careless, did not die of the sea. We were put here. Mr. Ashcombe's men sealed us in with our own discovery in our hands, and God forgive me, I believe it was for the land, only for the land.*"

Ferrand lowered the journal. Her eyes found Marchetti's across the chamber, and something passed between them that Tomas couldn't read — some recognition, some old arithmetic.

"There are more journals," she said. "Boxes of them. Ledgers. Correspondence. It's all here, preserved, dry, complete. Hollis kept records until the end. They all did. It's—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself. "It's a hundred and thirteen years of evidence, sitting in the dark, waiting for someone to come and be told."

Mara Voss stood among the bones with her hand pressed flat over her heart, and Tomas watched her lips move — counting, or praying, or both.

Juniper sat down hard on a supply crate. "They were murdered," she said. "They just — they were working, and somebody blew the door shut behind them, and they *knew*, and they wrote it down, and then they—"

"Yes," Tomas said. Gently, because she was fifteen, and because someone had to say it plainly.

He turned a slow circle with his lamp, and the engineer's part of his mind — the diver's part, the part that mapped exits before dangers — finally spoke the thing it had been saying since the blast scars in the passage. Dressed stone. Mortared joints. Timber shoring set from outside.

A century ago, men had stood where Tomas now stood and understood, with months to think about it, exactly what kind of men had done this to them. And those men had chosen, with their final strength, not to smash the records or curse the company or even try to claw free. They had folded their dead's arms. They had squared their boots. They had written everything down in beautiful handwriting and left it where the light would find it someday.

The cave wasn't a grave.

It was a filing cabinet. And someone, somewhere, had decided it should stay locked.

"We need to go," Tomas said. "Now. While the tide's still out. We take what we can carry and we come back with proper equipment and witnesses and—"

He stopped.

Because he felt it. Through his boots, faint but unmistakable, the thing he'd promised them they'd feel before they saw it: a cold pulse rising through the stone, rhythmic, patient. The water table, drinking upward. Or something else, somewhere above, doing work of its own.

From far back down the passage — the direction of the entrance, the direction of the sea — came a sound none of them could mistake.

Voices. And the scrape of timber being dragged across rock.
