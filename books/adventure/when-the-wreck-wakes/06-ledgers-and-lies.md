## 6. Ledgers and Lies

Odile Ferrand had spent eleven years reading other people's secrets, and she had learned one thing above all: the truth never announces itself. It arrives in margins. In a figure that doesn't reconcile. In ink that's darker where someone pressed too hard, trying to make a lie look like confidence.

She sat cross-legged on a camp blanket inside the record chamber with a hurricane lamp turned low, and she let the dead speak to her in their own handwriting.

The 1911 expedition had been thorough. That was the first thing that unsettled her — how *thorough*. Surveyor Aldous Wren and his six companions had documented everything: barometric readings, tide tables, stratigraphic sketches rendered in pencil so fine it looked like breath on paper. They'd catalogued their supplies down to the last tin of condensed milk. Odile had processed enough corporate archives to know what records like these meant. They meant men who expected to come home. Men who kept books because they believed someone would read them.

The journals were bound in oilcloth, stacked in a dry-stone niche like a man-made reliquary. Beside them lay the instruments — a theodolite green with age, brass calipers, a sextant wrapped in flannel — arranged with the same care as the bones at the chamber's far end. Seven skeletons, laid side by side, boots aligned, coats folded beneath their heads like pillows. Someone had tended them. Someone who loved them, or owed them.

Odile pulled on cotton gloves — her own small ritual, armor against the intimacy of it — and opened Wren's field ledger.

*Day 14. The seam is better than we dreamed.* 

Her breath went shallow. She made herself slow down, the way she'd taught junior archivists back when she'd still been allowed to have juniors: read twice, transcribe once, trust nothing until you've traced it to its source.

Wren wrote like a man drunk on good news. The coastal basalt, he reported, was shot through with veins of copper sulfide — and deeper, below the water table, something rarer. He didn't name it precisely; his assay notes did that, tucked into an envelope marked *FOR THE COMPANY'S EYES ONLY*, sealed with wax that had gone the color of dried blood. The figures inside, once Odile decoded Wren's cramped notation, were staggering. At 1911 prices, the seam under this cliff was worth more than the railway itself. More than the land it stood on. Enough money to build a dynasty.

Enough money to kill for.

She felt it before she could prove it — the cold slide of recognition, the archivist's oldest instinct. Something in the record's shape was wrong. Expeditions don't end mid-sentence. But Wren's final entry, dated the ninth of November, 1911, ended not with fear but with logistics: *Low tide at dawn. We return to camp by the north passage. Halloway to signal the tender from the cliff top.*

Halloway. The tender. The man on the cliff.

Odile set the ledger down and reached for the second document, the one she'd saved because it frightened her most: a carbon copy of an internal memorandum, typed on letterhead she knew intimately. The Coastal & Northern Railway. Her former employer. Her former *family*, before the scandal that hadn't even been hers, before they'd needed a scapegoat and she'd been the right shape for it.

The memo was dated the twelfth of November — three days after Wren's last entry. It concerned the purchase of the headland parcel from the Ellery estate. The price recorded was a fraction of the land's assessed value, justified by a single line: *Parcel compromised by geological instability; survey expedition lost; site unsuitable for development.*

*Survey expedition lost.*

Not *missing*. Not *overdue*. Lost — past tense, certified dead, three days after they'd last been seen alive, before any search could have run its course. And the parcel, "compromised," sold cheap, snapped up by the company's founders, who then sat on it for forty years while the mineral rights quietly appreciated in the dark.

Odile's hands were steady. Eleven years of practice. Inside, something was screaming.

Because she'd seen this pattern before. Not in 1911 — in 2019. A tunnel on the Meridian extension. A geotechnical report flagged unstable strata, a report that was filed and buried, a collapse that killed two men, a land settlement that followed like a shadow follows a body. She'd pulled those files herself, late one night, looking for something else entirely. She'd found the discrepancy. She'd brought it to her director. And three weeks later she'd been fired for "records mishandling" she had never committed, and the file had vanished, and the company had moved on, and so — she'd told herself — had she.

Except she hadn't. Except she was sitting in a cave full of murdered men, holding the same fraud in an older hand, and the same company's fingerprints were on both.

The lamp flickered. Somewhere deeper in the cave, water dripped with metronome patience, counting down a tide she couldn't see.

She worked until her neck ached, building the chain link by link: Wren's assay, the memo, a second document — a payment authorization, initials *E.C.H.* in the corner. Edmund Cornelius Hale. Founder. The name still sat on the company's letterhead in the form of its charitable foundation. Juniper Hale. The girl stowaway carried the founder's name like an unexploded shell, and probably didn't know it.

Odile photographed every page with the little camera Tomas had rigged, then photographed them again. Then she sat very still for a long time, listening to the sea breathe through stone, and understood exactly what she was holding.

This wasn't history. This was a live round. The company that had ruined her had done this — had done *this* — and if the records surfaced, everything would burn: the founders' names, the foundation, the settlements, the pension funds, the whole rotting edifice. And the people who lit the match would need to be very far away when it caught.

Or very quiet. Or very dead.

She thought about Beatrix Marchetti, out in the antechamber checking anchor points with that careful, closed face of his. She'd watched him for three days now. Watched him flinch when Mara Voss said her husband's name. Watched him measure distances the way guilty men do — always calculating exits. She'd spent a career learning to read the gap between what documents say and what people know, and Beatrix Marchetti had a gap in him wide enough to drive a train through.

It was time, she decided, to compare ledgers.

* * *

She found him outside the chamber mouth, in the antechamber where the lantern light gave out and the dark took over. He was crouched at the old mooring ring bolted into the rock, testing it with a wrench, though nothing here needed testing. Busy hands. Guilty hands.

"Marchetti."

He glanced up. "Find anything?"

"You know I did." She lowered herself onto a crate across from him, deliberately unhurried. "I've been wondering why you took this contract. Triple pay, winter season, a cave with a body count. You're a good engineer. Good engineers don't take bad odds."

"I take contracts that pay."

"Mm." She let the silence stretch. Silence was her sharpest tool; most people couldn't bear it. Beatrix bore it, but his jaw tightened, and that was answer enough. "Aldous Wren found a copper seam under this cliff in 1911. His employers sealed seven men inside it and bought the land for pennies. Three days later they filed paperwork calling the men *lost*." She held up the memo. "Edmund Hale signed the payment authorization. The same family name as our stowaway. And the same pattern—" she watched his eyes, "—as a tunnel collapse on the Meridian extension. Twelve years ago. Two dead. A safety report that never made it into the record."

The wrench stopped moving.

"I archived that file," Odile said quietly. "Before they erased it. Before they erased me. So I know what a buried report looks like, Marchetti. I've been looking at one all week — in you."

The lamp between them hissed. Out beyond the cave mouth, the sea boomed against the new cliff face like something demanding entry.

"You don't know what you're talking about," Beatrix said.

"No. I know what the *documents* say. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters." She leaned forward. "I'm not your enemy. I'm the person who can prove you right or bury you with the rest of it. So I'll ask once, and I'll ask as a colleague: what happened at Meridian?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he set the wrench down with exaggerated care, the way a man puts down something he might otherwise throw.

"You want to trade secrets," he said. "Fine. Here's mine for free: I wrote the report. The strata were unstable. I flagged it, I escalated it, and my supervisor told me the schedule mattered more than the geology. So I shut up. I told myself the numbers were conservative, that the factor of safety had margin, that I'd done enough by writing it down." His voice didn't waver; that was somehow worse. "Three weeks later the roof came down. Daniel Voss went back in for a man who was already dead. He didn't come out. And everyone — the inquiry, the company, his widow — believes the collapse was unforeseeable. Because my report doesn't exist anymore. Because I let it not exist."

Odile exhaled slowly. "And Voss's wife is standing in your camp."

"And Voss's wife is standing in my camp." He laughed, short and airless. "Twelve years I've been running track away from that platform. And now there's a cave full of men who got buried the same way Daniel did — on paper first, then in stone — and I'm supposed to just *survey* it."

"The company will do to these records what it did to yours," Odile said. "You know that. Whatever they sent us here to recover, it isn't for the historical society."

"They offered triple pay before I'd filed a report. Before anyone could know what was down here." His eyes came up to hers, and in the lamplight she saw the thing underneath the numbness: not guilt exactly, but its engine — fear, worn smooth by years of use. "They knew. Somehow they already knew."

"Then we're not a recovery team," Odile said. "We're evidence. And evidence gets destroyed."

He looked at her for a long moment. Two keepers of secrets, recognizing each other across the lamplight — she with her erased career, he with his erased report, both of them fluent in the grammar of institutional lies.

"What are you proposing?" he asked.

"That we finish the job properly. Every page photographed, every chain of custody documented, copies in three places before we surface. And that when we get out of here, we don't hand anything to anyone wearing a company logo." She paused. "There's a girl in the supply car carrying Edmund Hale's name. If Juniper ever learns what her great-grandfather authorized—"

"She learns it from us," Beatrix said. "Not from a headline."

"Agreed." Odile rose, knees cracking, and gathered the memo. Then, because honesty between conspirators should be total, she added: "One more thing, Marchetti. The widow. If you don't tell Mara Voss the truth before this ends, the records will. Your report may be gone, but fraud leaves sediment. I can find the Meridian file again — I found it once in the dark, I can find it twice. Do you want her hearing it from me?"

Beatrix stared at the black mouth of the passage leading down toward the sea. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough, like gravel under load.

"Not from you," he said. "From me. Just— give me until we're out of this hole."

Outside, the tide was turning. Odile heard it before she saw it — a change in the cave's breathing, the drip-rate quickening, the deep channels beginning to fill with a sound like the whole ocean clearing its throat.

And above them, faint through sixty feet of rock, came a sound neither of them liked at all: engines. Two vehicles, maybe three, grinding down the coast road toward the camp.

Beatrix was on his feet instantly, wrench forgotten. "That's early. The inspection team isn't due until Thursday."

Odile slid the memo into her coat, against her heart, like the weapon it was. "Then whoever's coming," she said, "didn't come to inspect."
