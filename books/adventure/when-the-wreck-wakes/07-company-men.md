## 7. Company Men

Juniper Hale had learned to read men the way other kids learned to read weather — out of necessity, back when reading them wrong meant going hungry. So when the black SUVs came grinding up the coast road at half past two in the afternoon, she knew before anyone spoke that these were not safety inspectors.

Safety inspectors arrived in dented pickups. They complained about the coffee and wore jackets with faded logos and asked boring questions and left by four. These men arrived in three identical vehicles, washed and gleaming, and they stepped out in unison like dancers who'd rehearsed. Four of them. The fifth stayed behind the wheel of the last SUV with the engine running.

Juniper was in the supply car, which was where she lived now, technically. Nobody had said it out loud, but after that first night — Beatrix standing in the doorway with his lantern, her heart hammering against her ribs like a bird in a box — nobody had made her leave, either. She'd made herself useful. She inventoried the rope coils. She learned which crates held the good biscuits. She kept her ears open, because ears were the one tool nobody ever took away from you.

She slid deeper behind the stacked crates and watched through the gap where the door didn't quite close.

The leader was a big man with a face like a closed ledger — gray suit, gray expression, hands folded in front of him the way undertakers fold theirs. The others flanked him: a wiry one with a camera slung around his neck, a heavy one whose jacket didn't quite hide the shape of something under his arm, and a young one who kept touching his collar like it was too tight.

"Marchetti," the big man said, when Beatrix came down from the cliff path. Juniper saw the surveyor's shoulders go stiff before he even turned around.

"That's me."

"Whitlock. Regional safety director." He offered a hand. Beatrix didn't take it, and Whitlock let it hang there a moment before folding it away, unbothered, like a man who'd been refused handshakes before and would be again. "We've had reports of structural instability at the cliff face. Company policy requires a full inspection before your team re-enters the cave."

"We're not scheduled to re-enter for two days."

"Which is why we're here now." Whitlock smiled. It was the smile of a man who had learned smiling from a manual. "Efficiency."

Juniper saw Odile Ferrand come around the corner of the equipment tent and stop dead. The archivist had a face built for hiding things, but Juniper had spent a week watching her, and she'd learned the tells: the stillness of the hands, the way Odile's eyes went flat and calculating when she smelled a lie. Right now her eyes were flat as slate.

"Regional safety," Odile said. "I don't recall your office on the notification list."

"You would be Ms. Ferrand." Whitlock's gaze slid to her and stuck there, a fly on paper. "Yes. We know your work."

Something passed between them that Juniper couldn't parse — some old corporate history, some debt — but she saw Odile's chin lift a half inch, and she saw the wiry man with the camera drift, casual as fog, toward the cave mouth. Toward the entrance camp. Toward the crates where the records were being catalogued.

* * *

Juniper had one rule, learned the hard way in three foster homes and eleven months on her own: when the grown-ups start moving in patterns, get small and listen.

She slipped out the back of the supply car, dropped into the gully behind it, and worked her way along the rock shelf toward the entrance camp. The wind was picking up — she could taste the storm coming, that iron-and-salt tang the sea got before it turned mean — and it covered her footsteps. She crouched behind a stack of timber baulks and pulled her jacket over her head to muffle the wind, the way she used to do under bridges.

The wiry man with the camera stood at the cave mouth, photographing everything. Not the cliff. Not the rockfall. The crates. He photographed the labels Odile had written. He photographed the tarp. He photographed the generator, the winch, the rope cache — and then he crouched and ran his thumb along the winch cable, and Juniper watched him turn back and give the big man a single nod.

She moved closer. Closer was stupid. Closer was what got you caught. She moved closer anyway.

"—recover everything tonight," Whitlock was saying, his voice pitched low under the wind, but the wind was her friend now, bending the words toward her. "The originals especially. Ms. Ferrand's copies go in the burn barrel. And the site—" he glanced at the cave mouth, black and patient as a throat, "—gets sealed. Permanently. The cliff is unstable. Tragic, really. These old coastal lines."

"And the people?" That was the young one, the collar-toucher. His voice cracked on *people*.

Whitlock looked at him for a long moment. "There are no people, Kessler. There's a survey team that will have concluded its work and departed. If anyone is still inside when we seal it—" he said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest, "—then the site will be marked and recovery efforts will begin in the spring. When conditions improve."

The wind screamed through the gap in the cliffs, and Juniper's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

Inside. They were going to seal the cave with people inside. With Beatrix and Tomas and Odile and—

*Mara.* Mara Voss had gone down to the entrance camp an hour ago to help catalog. Juniper's stomach dropped through the rock beneath her.

She should run. She should run to Beatrix, shout it from the clifftop, consequences be damned. But her feet wouldn't move, because the engineer in her blood — no, not engineer, she wasn't anything yet, but the part of her that had memorized Beatrix's tide tables just because they were beautiful — that part was doing arithmetic. Low tide had been at 1:40. It was past three. The water was already coming back. If the team had gone into the cave this morning for the final cataloguing run — and they had, she'd watched their lanterns disappear into the dark at dawn — then they were deep in there, deep where the journals were, and the tide was turning, and these men were going to take the winch, the ropes, the generator, everything—

She looked at the winch. The wiry man was already unclipping the cable.

* * *

Getting to the cave mouth unseen was the hardest thing Juniper had ever done, and she had once ridden a freight car through a yard with dogs.

She went the long way, down through the gully, along the shelf where the spray came up cold and stinging, moving between the wind's shouts the way she'd learned to move between a drunk's snores. The entrance camp was stripped with terrifying speed. The wiry man coiled the rope cache under his arm like it was laundry. The heavy one — the one with the shape under his jacket — dragged the generator toward the SUVs, grunting. Whitlock stood at the cave mouth with a flashlight, shining it into the dark, and Juniper pressed herself flat against the rock ten feet away, close enough to hear him murmur into a satellite phone:

"...confirmed. Records intact. We'll have everything by dark. The cave, yes — the charges are already in the truck. It'll come down tonight, look like the storm finished the job. No, nobody's coming out. The tide's on our side."

A pause. Whatever the voice on the other end said made him smile again, that manual smile.

"Understood. Nothing gets out."

Juniper's hands were shaking. She made them stop. She was very good at making her hands stop, because there had been a winter when shaking hands meant dropping the only food you had.

They hadn't seen her. Nobody ever saw her. That was the whole tragedy of her life and today, for the first time, it was going to be useful.

The entrance was still open. The fixers were loading the far SUV. She had maybe two minutes. Maybe one.

She thought about Beatrix's face the night he'd found her — not angry, worse, *tired*, like she was one more thing the world had handed him to carry — and how he'd said, *I should put you on a train back*, and how she'd said, *There's no back*, and how he'd looked at her for a long time and then just... hadn't put her on a train. He'd started teaching her tide tables instead.

Nobody had ever done that. Nobody had ever just not put her on the train.

Juniper Hale went into the dark.

The first fifty feet she knew — she'd memorized them from the maps pinned in the equipment tent, because maps were the other beautiful thing, maps and tide tables, things that told you where you were when everything else lied. Then her flashlight found the surveyor's tape markers, yellow flags fluttering in the cave's dead air, and she followed them the way she'd followed freight trains: fast, low, and trusting nothing.

She heard the water before she saw it — a deep, rhythmic breathing in the rock, the tide filling the lower galleries — and she heard voices, and then she rounded a bend of ribbed black stone and there they were, lantern-lit, alive: Beatrix kneeling over an open journal, Odile photographing a page, Tomas at the flooded passage with a depth gauge, Mara cataloguing bones with a gentleness that made Juniper's throat hurt.

"Beatrix," she said, and her voice came out cracked and small, so she said it again, louder. "Beatrix. You have to come *now*."

Every lantern turned. Beatrix was on his feet before she finished, and up close his face was all sharp planes and shadow, and she saw him register her — the stowaway, the girl who wasn't supposed to be here — standing in the deep dark, alone, breathless.

"Juniper? How did you—"

"They're here." The words tumbled out of her, fast and flat, the way she'd rehearsed them against the rock wall outside. "Five of them. They took the winch and the ropes and the generator, everything. They're not inspectors, they're fixers. I heard them. They're going to blow the entrance tonight and make it look like the storm did it, and—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through, "—and they said if anyone's still inside, recovery starts in spring. They're going to seal you in. They said *nothing gets out*."

Silence, except for the water, breathing.

Odile moved first — she always moved first — snapping the journal shut and thrusting it into the waterproof case with hands that didn't shake at all. "How long since low tide?"

"Past three. It's turning now."

"Then we have maybe three hours before the lower galleries flood," Tomas said quietly, from the water's edge. "Less, if the surge is big."

Beatrix hadn't moved. He was staring at Juniper with an expression she couldn't read, and she braced herself — for anger, for *I told you to stay on the train*, for the look people got right before they handed you back. But what he said was:

"You came down alone. Through the dark. To warn us."

"Somebody had to. You were all down here being *fascinated*."

And Beatrix Marchetti, who measured life in track miles and hadn't smiled in twelve years, huffed something that was almost a laugh, and put his hand on her shoulder — heavy, warm, real — and said, "Good work, Hale."

It shouldn't have mattered. It was two words. It mattered more than anything anyone had said to her in eleven months.

Then the cave shuddered.

Not the water. Not the tide. A deep, percussive *thump* rolled down through the rock, and dust sifted from the ceiling, and every lantern flame bent sideways.

"They're not waiting for tonight," Odile said, very calmly, in the voice of a woman reading her own death warrant. "They're testing the charges now."

Tomas was already at the flooded passage, gauge in hand, and when he turned back his face in the lantern light was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "The main entrance is gone. They've dropped it." He looked at Beatrix. "Unless you know another way out of this rock, engineer, we're on the wrong side of the tide."

Beatrix stood very still, and Juniper watched him go somewhere inside himself — back through twelve years of closed lines and buried maps — and when he came back, his eyes had changed.

"There's a drainage adit," he said slowly. "Nineteen-oh-six survey. It shouldn't exist anymore. But rock doesn't read the paperwork."

"Then lead," Odile said, hugging the record case to her chest.

The water was rising. Somewhere above them, men were packing explosives. And Juniper Hale — stowaway, stray, the girl nobody ever saw — fell into step behind Beatrix Marchetti, second from the front, exactly where family would stand.
