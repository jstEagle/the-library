## 8. Rising Water, Rising Truth

The water found them before they found the adit.

It came as a sound first — a soft, insistent hiss from the dark behind them, like the cave breathing in. Then Beatrix's boot broke a skin of water that hadn't been there ten minutes ago, and the cold climbed through leather and wool and into the bone.

"Floor's flooding," Tomas said behind him. The diver's voice was flat as always, but flat the way a spirit level is flat — precise, calibrated, telling you exactly how far off true the world had gone.

"How fast?" Odile asked.

Beatrix crouched, laid two fingers against the black water, and hated himself for knowing the answer before he measured it. "Spring tide. Storm surge pushing up through every crack in this coast. An inch every few minutes now. It'll accelerate." He straightened. "Water doesn't rush. It just refuses to stop."

Juniper's headlamp swung toward him. In its glow her face was pale and set, older than fifteen by about a decade of bad nights. "Then we don't stop either."

He almost smiled. Almost. Instead he turned back to the passage ahead — a low throat of rock sloping down and away from the record chamber, exactly where it should be if the 1906 survey had told the truth, which surveys rarely did and rock usually did. The old drainage adit. A tunnel cut not for trains but for water, to bleed the cliffs dry so the rails above wouldn't heave with frost. Abandoned on paper within a year, probably collapsed on paper too, because paper was cheaper than rock and companies knew it.

But rock didn't read the paperwork. And neither, tonight, did the sea.

They moved. Odile carried the record case strapped across her chest like a child, one arm curled around it even as she steadied herself against the wall. Tomas took the rear with the coil of rope and the calm of a man who had made peace with drowning long ago and was simply negotiating terms. Juniper stayed second from the front, close enough that Beatrix could hear her breathing — quick, controlled, the breathing of someone who had decided fear was a luxury she'd bill later.

The water followed them at their heels like a dog learning to heel.

* * *

The adit mouth appeared out of the dark the way truth appears out of a long lie: suddenly, and smaller than you'd hoped.

A rectangle of older masonry, hand-cut granite blocks furred with mineral bloom, set into the living rock like a door in a house no one remembered building. Beatrix put his palm against it. Cold. Sound. The mortar had failed along the crown but the blocks held their own weight and each other's, and beyond it the passage ran level and dry into the hill's interior.

"It's real," Juniper breathed.

"It's real," Beatrix said, and something in his chest unclenched for the first time since the fixers had seized the entrance equipment — one breath of relief, no more, because an engineer knows better than anyone that a way out is only a way out until it isn't. Nineteen-oh-six masonry. A hundred and eighteen years of tides pressing on the other end. There would be falls. There would be flooded sections. There would be a moment, somewhere in that black throat, when every one of them would have to decide whether to keep going.

He was reaching for his lamp to check the crown joints when Mara spoke.

"No."

One word. She hadn't moved from where she'd stopped three paces back, water to her ankles, hands empty at her sides. She had walked the whole route from the record chamber without complaint, without stumbling, without a single question — and Beatrix had known, with the sick certainty of a man who has dodged a debt for twelve years, that she was saving it.

"Mara—" he began.

"You don't get to say my name yet." Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it. Twelve years of grief had burned everything flammable out of her voice and left something that would hold an edge forever. "Nobody moves. Nobody takes another step toward that hole in the rock. Not until you tell me what Daniel wrote to me."

The water hissed against their boots. Somewhere far above, faint as a heartbeat through stone, came the knock of the company men at work.

Beatrix looked at the adit. Dry, level, waiting. Then at the faces around him — Odile with the records pressed to her chest, watching him with the expression of a woman who already knew; Tomas impassive, patient, a man who understood that some floods you wait out; Juniper with her chin up and her eyes enormous, seeing everything, missing nothing, the way strays learn to.

"He died covering for me."

The words came out of him like a tooth coming out of the jaw — whole, bloody, and with a horrible sense of relief underneath the pain.

Mara didn't move. "Say all of it."

"The Kestrel Cut tunnel. March 2012." His own voice sounded strange to him, stripped of twelve years of careful nothing. "I ran the stress analysis on the eastern heading. The shale seam was wrong — wetter than the borings showed, fractured, unstable. I filed a safety report recommending we halt and regrout. My supervisor shelved it. And I—" His throat closed. He made himself go on, because Mara Voss had waited twelve years and the sea was rising and he was done being a man who let things rise over him. "I let it be shelved. I told myself I'd push again after the next milestone payment. One week. I gave them one week I knew we didn't have."

The water was at their shins now. Nobody looked down.

"The collapse happened on day shift. Daniel was running the heading because I'd swapped shifts — I wanted to be topside to argue the report in person. If I'd been underground, if I'd kept my shift—" His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a kind of distant curiosity, the engineer observing a failure outside tolerances. "Daniel saw the seam let go before anyone else did. He had time to run. Instead he went back for Amado Ferreira, who was pinned. He got Amado out. He didn't get himself out."

"And the inquiry," Mara said quietly.

"Said the seam was unforeseeable. Said my report — the one copy that reached the file — recommended only 'continued monitoring.' Someone rewrote it. I never signed the rewrite. But I also never proved it wasn't mine." He lifted his eyes to hers. "They paid out your widow's settlement with a clause. You sign, you can't sue. I signed my part too. We both signed away the truth, and I've been riding closed lines ever since because every open line sounded like that tunnel coming down."

For a long moment there was only the water, climbing, patient, indifferent to the affairs of the guilty.

Then Mara crossed the flooded floor and struck him — once, open-palmed, across the cheek, a blow with no anger in it at all, only twelve years arriving all at once. And then she gripped the front of his coat with both fists and pressed her forehead hard against his chest, and Beatrix Marchetti stood in rising seawater in a cave full of murdered men's records and held the widow of the friend he had killed, and wept without sound, the way men do who learned crying in tunnels where noise carries.

"I don't forgive you," she said into his coat. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But I believe you. And believing you is worth more than twelve years of not knowing."

"That's more than I deserve."

"Yes," she said, and stepped back, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a woman closing an account. "Now get us out. That's how you start paying."

* * *

They went into the adit at 3:40 in the morning, by Beatrix's watch, with the water at mid-shin and rising faster.

He led with the lamp low, reading the old masonry joint by joint the way he read track — looking for the tells, the sag, the spall, the places where a hundred years of pressure had written its complaints in stone. The passage ran level for forty meters, then stepped down, then rose again, following the dip of the shale seam exactly as a drainage adit must, chasing water downhill so the hill itself would stay dry.

At sixty meters they hit the first fall.

A slab of roof rock, big as a dining table, had come down sometime in the last century and wedged itself against the far wall, leaving a gap maybe half a meter high at the base. Black water trickled through it.

"Tomas," Beatrix said. "You're smallest after Juniper, and Juniper isn't going first."

Tomas handed Odile the rope without a word, dropped to his belly, and went through the gap like water finding its own level. Ten seconds. Twenty. Then his voice floated back, echoing strangely: "Open beyond. Two meters of crawl, then standing height. Come."

Odile went through with the case pushed ahead of her. Then Juniper, wriggling like an eel, pausing halfway to call back, "There's air moving! Feel it? Real air!" — and the joy in her voice was so fierce and young that Beatrix felt something in his chest turn over.

Mara went through. Beatrix went last, flattening himself into the cold trickle, the rock pressing his spine, and in that squeezed black moment — cheek against stone older than any company, any crime, any grief — he understood with total clarity what honesty had bought them. Not safety. The fall could still kill them. The water could still take them. The men upstairs with the explosives could still win.

But nobody in this passage was carrying a secret anymore, and they moved like it — fast, light, unburdened — and that, he thought as he scraped through into standing air, might be the only thing that got them out.

Beyond the fall the adit climbed. They climbed with it, water falling away below, air moving fresh against their faces, and Beatrix counted paces and checked his mental map against the 1906 elevations and allowed himself, for the first time in twelve years, to plan past the next hour.

Two hundred meters to the ventilation shaft. Maybe three hundred. Daylight somewhere above it — dawn, if the storm had spent itself.

Behind them, deep under the hill, the sea finished swallowing the record chamber floor.

Ahead, faint but unmistakable, drifting down the old shaft like a promise:

Gulls.
