## 9. The Adit

**BEATRIX**

The water was coming for them, and Beatrix Marchetti had never been so glad of anything in his life.

"Open it," he said. "All of it."

Tomas Reyes stood at the record chamber's inner wall, hands braced against the old sluice gate the 1911 men had built to keep the sea out of their camp. A century of rust had welded it shut, but Tomas was a diver before he was anything else, and he understood water the way Beatrix understood rail — as a force you did not fight, only aimed. He'd spent twenty minutes with a pry bar and a headlamp and a patience that looked like fury.

The gate groaned. Gave an inch. Groaned again.

Behind them, down the passage toward the cave mouth, boots splashed. Voices. The fixers had found the entrance equipment abandoned and were coming in with the tide, flashlights cutting wild arcs off the wet stone.

"They're two minutes out," Mara said. She stood with the journals strapped to her chest in a waterproof satchel, arms crossed over them like a mother shielding a child from weather.

"Then we're on time." Beatrix put his shoulder against the gate beside Tomas. The iron bit through his jacket, cold as a confession. "On three. This is for Daniel, and for eleven men who've been lying in the dark since 1911. One—"

They threw their weight together. The gate tore loose with a shriek that echoed through the whole cavern, and the sea came in.

It didn't pour; it *detonated*. A wall of black water burst through the gap and hit the far wall of the chamber, rebounded, and began filling the low ground where the expedition's camp had lain for a hundred years — the canvas, the instruments, the careful rows of bones they'd wrapped in sailcloth and carried past with their hats off. Now the chamber would keep its dead the way it always had, but no one would ever walk among them again, and nothing in it could be stolen or burned or buried twice.

"Move," Beatrix said. "Everyone up the shelf. Juniper first. Go!"

They climbed. Behind them the water rose with terrible speed, swallowing the camp, drowning the lantern light of the fixers' approach. Beatrix heard shouting — rage, then something worse, panic, as the men realized the tide was doing what they'd been sent to do, but without them. He did not look back. Looking back was how people died in tunnels.

At the top of the shelf, where the rock narrowed to a crawl, Odile Ferrand pressed the old survey map flat against her knee and traced a line with a shaking finger.

"Here," she said. "The 1911 plans show a drainage adit. It runs up-dip, northwest, maybe four hundred meters. It should daylight above the cliff line — there's a ventilation shaft marked near the summit, here, under this symbol I can't read."

"I can read it." Beatrix leaned over her shoulder. His heart was hammering but his mind had gone suddenly, blessedly quiet, the way it did when the problem was finally one he knew how to solve. "That's a fan symbol. Air shaft. And the adit grade — look, they drew it at one in forty. That's a drainage tunnel, Odile. It was built to run dry." He met her eyes. "It'll run dry now, if the lower gates are still closed. Four hundred meters of standing water at most, then rising ground."

"And if the gates aren't closed?"

"Then we swim." He shouldered his pack. "I surveyed lines like this for twelve years. Every dead railway has bones like these — adits, culverts, air shafts. Nobody builds a tunnel they can't get out of. They just forget it, and then somebody like me comes along and remembers."

Juniper Hale, soaked to the ribs, fifteen years old and white-lipped, said: "Then remember fast."

* * *

**ODILE**

Odile Ferrand had spent nine years in the company archives learning one lesson above all others: paper is heavier than it looks.

She felt it now. The satchel of records she'd divided between herself and Mara weighed almost nothing by mass and everything else. Founders' ledgers. The 1911 correspondence. A payroll entry, dated six days after the cave was sealed, disbursing "special compensation" to five men whose names matched the company's first board of directors. She had held the truth of eleven murders in her hands for three hours, and every minute of those hours she had expected someone to come take it from her, the way the company had once taken her career for a filing error she hadn't even made.

Now she crawled through a century-old drainage adit behind a railroad engineer who was mapping from memory, and she found she wasn't afraid anymore. There was a strange liberty in having passed the point where fear still had somewhere left to go.

The water in the adit came up to their waists, then their chests, then — for eight meters of low ceiling — their chins. Beatrix went first, holding the map above the surface in a freezer bag, calling back grades and distances in a voice gone calm and professional, as though he were reading a timetable. *"Fifty meters, grade rising. Watch the joint on your left, don't trust it. Seventy meters, there should be a timber set — yes — good wood, they built this right."*

"He talks to the dead men like they're colleagues," Juniper whispered, treading water beside her.

"They are," Odile said. "He just got here late."

Ahead, a splash that wasn't part of the rhythm. Then Tomas's voice, tight: "The satchel. It's caught."

* * *

**TOMAS**

The strap had snagged on a broken spike driven into the adit floor a hundred years ago, some forgotten anchor for a timber that had long since rotted. Tomas Reyes dove.

He'd told himself he was going back for the records because the records mattered — because Odile's face when she'd read the payroll entry was the face of a woman handing him a live coal and asking him to carry it, and he had said yes. But underwater, in the black, with his fingers hooked into the strap and the ceiling pressing down like a lid, he knew the truer thing: he had spent six years since the diving accident being careful. Careful had gotten him fired from two crews and a divorce. This — this stupid, needless, absolutely necessary dive — was the first time since then he had chosen the risk instead of hiding from it.

The strap wouldn't free. His air ran short. He pulled anyway, tearing skin, and the ceiling seemed to lower, and somewhere above him the water surface turned to hammered silver very far away—

A hand closed on his collar. Small hand. Strong grip.

Juniper hauled him up by main force, screaming words he couldn't parse, and together — her pulling, him kicking — they broke the surface into a pocket of air under the low ceiling, gasping, the satchel of records floating free beside them, strap torn clean through.

"You idiot," Juniper was saying, shoving wet hair out of her face, half laughing, half furious. "You absolute — you could have — *don't do that.*"

"Got it, though," Tomas managed.

"Yeah." She grabbed the satchel and shoved it at him, and her voice cracked. "Yeah, you got it. Don't make me be the one who tells Odile you drowned."

They swam on. Beyond the low section the adit climbed, the water dropping to knees, to ankles, to wet stone and echoing dark. Beatrix counted meter marks aloud like a rosary. At three hundred and eighty meters, his boot struck something solid — a ladder, iron, bolted into the shaft wall, running up into blackness that smelled, unmistakably, of pine trees and open sky.

* * *

**BEATRIX**

The ventilation shaft was barely wider than a man's shoulders, lined with rungs that Beatrix tested each one before trusting, the way he'd tested twelve years of track. Above him he could hear wind — real wind, moving through branches — and beneath it, faintly, the long sigh of surf on rock below the cliff.

Mara climbed ahead of him. Halfway up she stopped, and in the glow of his headlamp he saw her looking down at him, hair plastered to her skull, eyes bright.

"The letter," she said. "Daniel's last letter. He wrote that he'd found something in the coastal survey, something he was going to take to the board. I thought for twelve years I'd failed him by never finding out what." Her voice didn't break; it had done all its breaking years ago. "It was this, wasn't it. He found the pattern too. Same as you."

Beatrix hung on the rungs, water dripping off him into the dark below, and could not lie to her anymore even if he'd wanted to.

"Yes," he said. "And when we're up, and the sun's up, I'm going to say his name out loud for the first time in twelve years. All of it, Mara. Everything I owe you."

She nodded once and climbed.

The last rung ended at a wooden hatch, swollen with age. Beatrix put his shoulder to it, and it burst open into cold grey-gold light, and he hauled himself out onto a hillside of gorse and wind-bent pines with the whole dawn spread out below him — the sea running silver to the horizon, the storm spent, the cliff face scarred open like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak.

One by one they came up out of the ground: Odile, gray-faced and gripping the records; Tomas, limping, alive; Juniper last, hauling herself over the lip and immediately lying flat on her back in the wet grass, laughing at the sky.

Below, at the cave mouth, tiny figures were emerging — the fixers, retreating from a tomb they'd failed to empty, watched by five witnesses they could no longer reach.

Beatrix stood at the edge of the shaft and looked east, where the sun was coming up over the water, and for the first time in twelve years he let himself think about tomorrow. Not track miles. Tomorrow.

"Mara," he said quietly. "There's a morning train from the junction station. First one in months. And I know exactly which office in the city keeps the original land-grant deeds."

Odile was already unfolding the records across a flat rock, dawnlight falling on a century of buried paper. "Then we'd better read fast," she said, "because the 7:40 won't wait — and neither will what's on page one."
