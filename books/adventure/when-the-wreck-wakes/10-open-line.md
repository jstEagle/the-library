## 10. Open Line

The hearing room in Corvane had once been a railway station.

I knew it the moment I walked in — the ceiling too high for any civic purpose, the ghost of a ticket window bricked over behind the judge's bench, the floorboards laid in the long parallel runs of a platform that had swallowed ten thousand goodbyes. They'd built the courthouse on the bones of the old Meridian & Coast terminus, and nobody had thought to mention it to me until I was standing there with my license number on a card in my breast pocket and forty years of railroading folded up inside it like a map I'd finally finished reading.

Fitting, I supposed. They were going to take the thing I loved most in a room that had been built for departures.

The records from the cave had gone public eleven days earlier. Odile had done it her way — not a leak, not a whisper, but a full archival release, every page scanned and cross-referenced and footnoted until denial became mathematically impossible. She called it *provenance*. She said it the way other people say grace. The 1911 expedition had names now: Aldous Wren, surveyor. Petra Ilves, instrument keeper. The Calloway brothers, drillmen. Nine souls in all, listed in their own handwriting in a ledger they'd kept right up until the water came, or the picks did. The last entry was in Wren's hand: *Entrance sealed from without. We are not lost. We are left.*

That sentence ran under every headline on the coast for a week.

The company's heirs — the Ashcombe trust, three generations of philanthropy banquets and scholarship funds built on nine murdered men — had lawyers the way the sea has waves. But Odile had the ledgers, the land deeds showing the mineral seams bought for a tenth of value six weeks after the "tragic disappearance," and a carbon copy of an internal memo from 1912 authorizing "permanent closure of the anomalous feature." The fixers who'd stranded us in the cave turned state's evidence before their own coffee went cold. Men like that always do. Loyalty is just fear wearing a lanyard.

So the hearing wasn't really about them. It was about me.

"Mr. Marchetti." The chair of the review board was a woman with reading glasses on a chain and the patient expression of someone who'd heard every kind of confession a railroad could produce. "You've submitted a written statement regarding the Kestrel Tunnel collapse of twelve years ago. I'd like to give you the opportunity to speak to it directly."

I stood up. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Twelve years of steadiness at exactly this price — I might as well spend it well.

* * *

Here is what I told them, and I will tell it the same way now, because Mara taught me that a secret repeated is a secret dying:

In March, twelve years ago, I filed a safety report on the Kestrel Tunnel. Water seepage in the crown, boreholes running wet, timber sets groaning under load. I rated it critical. Then I got a call from a district manager named Halvorsen, who told me the schedule was contractual, the penalties were ruinous, and that a man with my record ought to know the difference between a caution and a crisis. He didn't order me to stand down. He never had to. He just made standing down sound like the reasonable thing, the grown-up thing, the thing a company man does.

I revised the report. Downgraded it. Initialled the revision myself.

Eleven days later the crown let go, and Daniel Voss — my best friend since apprenticeship, the man who taught me to read rock the way other men read weather — went back in to pull two drillers clear because he believed the tunnel was sound, because he believed *me*, because my signature on that report was the safest word either of us knew.

He got the drillers out. He did not get himself out.

"I didn't kill him," I told the board, and my voice held, mostly. "But I signed the lie that killed him. And then I spent twelve years walking away from it, mile by mile, mapping dead lines because dead lines don't ask questions. That's not grief. Grief would have been honest. What I did was cowardice with a compass."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere behind me, I heard Juniper blow her nose, loudly and without apology.

They took my license. Of course they did. I handed over the card myself, and the strange thing — the thing I keep turning over like a coin — is how light my pockets felt afterward. Forty years of certification, gone, and I walked out of that converted station into a gray bright morning feeling like a man who'd finally put down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Integrity, it turns out, isn't a credential. It's what's left when you surrender everything else and find you can still look people in the eye.

* * *

They buried the nine on a headland above the old line, in ground the Ashcombe trust was compelled to deed to the county. It was the first warm day of spring, which felt like cheating after everything, but the sea doesn't apologize for its moods and neither, apparently, does the sky.

Mara wore black, but not mourning-black — the good black, the Sunday black. She'd spent twelve years asking questions into a silence shaped exactly like me, and now she stood at the grave of nine strangers and wept for all of them at once, Daniel included, and when it was done she took my arm on the walk back down to the cars like it was the most ordinary thing in the world.

"You could have told me at any point in twelve years," she said.

"Yes."

"And I'd have hated you for about a year, and then I'd have had you back."

"Yes," I said. "That's the part that breaks me."

She squeezed my arm. "Then don't waste the next twelve finding out if I was right."

Odile came to the burial in an archivist's dark suit and read the nine names aloud from the ledger itself, her voice flat and precise, because she believed — and I have come to believe her — that precision is its own kind of prayer. Tomas stood at the back with his cap off, which I'd never seen, and Juniper cried through all of it and then, at the reception afterward, ate an amount of funeral cake that suggested genuine recovery.

It was Juniper who asked the question that settled the shape of everything after.

"So where does everybody live now?" she said, mouth full. "Because I've been thinking about it, and the cottage at the end of the coastal line is empty, and the line itself is being decommissioned anyway, and somebody should probably maintain it, and — I mean, hypothetically — a person needs an address to get a job, and I have a job interview at the tide-gauge station, so."

She said it all in one breath, the way she did everything, and then looked around the table with the fierce defiance of a girl who had learned that homes are things you propose, not things you're given.

Mara answered first. "I'm staying," she said simply. "I'm done living in questions."

"Tomas?" Odile asked.

Tomas shrugged, which from Tomas was a sonnet. "Water's warmer here."

Everyone looked at me. I looked out the window at the coast road, at the cut where the cliff had sheared away, at the place where the sea had already taken the cave mouth back — the winter storms had finished the job, and divers confirmed in February that the passage was closed, sealed by the same patient water that had opened it, keeping its dead now in peace instead of in evidence.

"The line's decommissioned," I said. "But rails don't stop being true just because trains stop running them. Somebody could walk it. Survey it. Keep it honest." I cleared my throat. "Somebody with time on his hands, now."

Juniper grinned. Odile allowed herself the small nod that, from Odile, was applause.

* * *

We moved in over three weekends, which is to say Mara organized it and the rest of us carried things. The cottage smelled of salt and cold hearth-smoke. The first night, Juniper claimed the attic room, Tomas took the ground floor "for his gear," and Odile and Mara divided the study between them with the treaty-like solemnity of two nations who had discovered they shared a border and, unexpectedly, a language.

I slept badly the first week — old habit — and then one night I didn't sleep badly at all, and woke at dawn to the sound of Juniper hammering a shelf bracket somewhere below and Mara laughing at whatever Odile had said over coffee, and I lay there in the gray gold light and understood that the ache I'd carried for twelve years hadn't vanished. It had just finally been set down among friends, where aches can heal instead of only hiding.

I still walk the line most mornings. Four miles out, four miles back, past the cut, past the headland graves, past the tide gauges where Juniper — employed now, insufferably competent, taller every month — waves me in with two fingers like a yardmaster. In summer we're putting a memorial bench at the overlook; Tomas has promised to dive the sea cave's outer shelf one last time and bring up anything the water is willing to return, and Odile has a shelf waiting for it, labeled in advance.

People used to ask me how long I'd been on the rails, and I'd answer in miles — sixty thousand, eighty thousand, a number so large it meant nothing, which was the point. A number that couldn't be lived in.

Last week a woman at the market asked how long I'd lived on the coast, and I heard myself say, "Eight months," and then, without thinking, "and I mean to make it forty years."

Forty *years*. Not miles. Years — countable, spendable, mine.

This morning I walked out to the overlook at first light. The sea was running silver, the old line gleamed wet and true beneath the gorse, and far down the coast a freight train blew for a crossing, the loneliest and loveliest sound in the world. I stood there until the sun cleared the headland, and then I turned for home, because home was expecting me — Mara with the coffee, Odile with the paper, Juniper with some new catastrophe, Tomas with his silent, seaworthy calm.

Behind me the tide came in over the cave, as it does twice a day, faithful as a timetable, keeping what it keeps.

Ahead of me: breakfast. And after that, all the years.
