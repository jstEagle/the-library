## 1. The Silence on Channel Nine

The radio had been silent for eleven days when Odile found him.

Jonah Winterbourne heard her boots before he saw her — a deliberate, unhurried crunch of gravel outside his cabin, the kind of walk that belonged to someone who had already decided how the conversation would end. He kept his hand on the axe by the door and watched through the gap in the curtain as she stood in his yard, taking inventory the way brokers do: the sagging woodshed, the rusted stovepipe, the man inside who was trying very hard to be nobody.

"Mr. Voss," she called out, using the name he'd bought six years ago from a forger in Whitehorse who charged too much and asked nothing.

He opened the door anyway. In the bush you learned that refusing to answer a knock only made people curious, and curiosity was the one thing Jonah could not afford. "You've got the wrong cabin."

"I don't think I do." Odile Marchand was small and weathered, sixty if she was a day, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had priced everything they'd ever seen. She held up a folded paper. "Jonah Winterbourne. Licensed engineer, Northern Pacific & Cascade Railway. Discharged 1919 following the Ashford Bridge inquiry. Presumed dead or fled north." She let the name hang in the cold air like something gutted and left to bleed. "I'm told the dead make excellent employees. No past to argue with."

The river behind his cabin ran high with early melt, loud enough to cover the sound of his own pulse. He'd chosen this stretch of water precisely because it was loud, because it drowned out the nights when he woke hearing rivets popping like rifle fire, hearing the groan of steel giving way under the 4:15 express, hearing forty-one voices that had stopped mid-sentence.

"You're mistaken," he said.

"Probably." She tucked the paper away. "But I need a man who knows the closed lines upriver — the old Meridian spur, the junction at Kettle Narrows, all of it. The survey expedition stopped answering Channel Nine eleven days ago. Eleven days, Mr. Voss, and the company that outfitted them is very interested in its equipment coming home. Radios, instruments, the launch. Bodies optional." She said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you'd read a freight manifest. "I'm offering three months' wages and passage back down before the monsoon season closes the river. That's more money than your trapline will see in ten years."

"The river's not navigable above the falls this time of year."

"That's why I'm hiring an engineer and not a deckhand."

* * *

He told himself it was the money. That was the story he built while he packed — a canvas bag, a tin of tools he hadn't touched since Whitehorse, the oilskin-wrapped map he swore he'd burned years ago and somehow never had. The map lived under the floorboards like a body. He'd drawn it from memory in his first winter up here, every abandoned grade and trestle between the coast and the interior plateau, the whole skeleton of a railway the company had built and then abandoned when the copper played out — including the line that ran through the valley where the survey party had gone quiet.

He should have burned it. He knew that even as he wrapped it tighter.

The scandal had a shape he could still trace with his eyes closed: the Ashford Bridge, his design, his signature on the stress calculations. A winter crossing, a trainload of passengers, and a span that sang once — a high, thin note like a struck wire — before the trusses folded and dropped forty-one people into the gorge. The inquiry found fatigue cracks in steel that had been certified sound. They found his signature beneath the certification. What they never found — what Jonah had spent six years failing to prove — was the second set of initials on the mill order, the substitution of inferior stock signed off by a district manager whose name carried weight in rooms where Jonah's never would. The company needed a fall. He was the cheapest one available.

So he had come north until north ran out, and built a life the size of a cabin, and told himself the silence was peace.

Now he stood on the bank watching the *Kestrel* come upriver against the current — a flat-bottomed salvage boat with a patched wheelhouse and a stack that coughed black smoke, listing slightly to port in a way that told him, before anyone said a word, that her planking was rotten below the waterline. Everything about her said *temporary*. Everything about her said *one last run*.

"She leaks," Odile said, appearing beside him with his bag already over her shoulder, having decided the matter was settled. "She leaks, she's ugly, and she'll get us there. You can complain below decks."

"What's above the falls?"

"Crew of four. Myself, a pilot named Sable who's run this water since he was twelve, a deckhand called Finch who talks too much, and a young fella named Tam Osgood, company man, along to see the equipment comes home." She looked at him sidelong. "And now you."

* * *

The first three days were good days, if a man could call them that. The river narrowed and quickened; the banks rose into walls of spruce and black rock hung with old flood debris ten feet above the current — a warning written in driftwood. Jonah stood at the rail and read the water the way he used to read blueprints, noting the eddy lines, the submerged gravel bars turning the current glassy-smooth over their backs, the places where the river held its breath before dropping.

Sable piloted with his whole body, leaning into the wheel like a man dancing with something much larger than himself. "You've got river eyes," the old pilot said on the second evening, watching Jonah track a seam of current no one else had marked. "Where'd a trapper learn river eyes?"

"Books," Jonah said.

Finch laughed so hard he nearly went overboard.

It was Tam Osgood who broke the ease. On the third night, moored against the bank while rain needled the tarps, the young company man spread a folder on the crate that served as a table: photographs of the survey camp taken from the air two seasons ago, supply manifests, a radio log ending abruptly on the eleventh of the month. *Position holding. All well. Weather deteriorating.* Then nothing. Not static, not distress — nothing, as though someone had simply reached over and lifted the needle off the record.

"They were mapping the old Meridian corridor," Tam said. "Company wants to know if the grades are still sound. Reopen the line someday, maybe. Copper's coming back." He tapped a photograph showing a valley walled in by glacier, a river running through it silver-bright, and along the valley floor — faint, but unmistakable to a man who had drawn such things in his sleep — the raised bed of an old rail grade, cut clean through the wilderness like a scar that refused to heal.

Jonah's chest went tight. He knew that grade. He had walked it once, years ago, in another life, with a transit instrument and a crew of men who called him *chief* and meant it. He had stood at Kettle Narrows and decided where the rock would be cut and where the river would be bridged, and the valley had obeyed him, and he had loved it — God help him, he had loved the feeling of a landscape bending to a drawn line.

That was the part of himself he had buried deepest. Not the disgrace. The love.

"You all right?" Tam asked. "You've gone gray."

"Tired," Jonah said, and turned in early, and lay in the dark of the leaking hold listening to water drip into the bilge — patient, rhythmic, inevitable — and understood that he had been lying to himself for six years. The silence up here had never been peace. It had been a held breath. And the river was carrying him, mile by mile, back toward the life he had buried alive, toward a valley he had helped draw into being, toward whatever waited at the end of a radio channel that had gone quiet without even the courtesy of static.

* * *

On the fifth day the river bent east into country none of them had names for, and the mountains closed in, and near dusk Sable killed the engine and let the *Kestrel* drift.

Ahead, where the charts showed nothing but white space, a light was burning on the north bank. Not a campfire — steady, electric, white, impossible this far from any grid. And beneath it, half-lost in the evening shadow, the unmistakable silhouette of a trestle bridge spanning the river, timber and steel, maintained, manned, alive.

"That's not on any chart," Odile said quietly.

"No," Jonah said. His mouth was dry. He knew that bridge. He had designed it — the span, the piers, the load ratings — under a name that had died with the Ashford Bridge. Someone had built his ghost into the wilderness and lit it like a lamp. "It isn't."

From the far bank, thin across the water, a voice came over a loudspeaker, warm and welcoming as a host opening his door:

"*Kestrel*, we see you. Come alongside. We've been expecting you — all of you."

A pause, and then, gently, terribly:

"*Especially you, Mr. Winterbourne.*"
