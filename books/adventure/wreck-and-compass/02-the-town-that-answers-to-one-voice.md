## 2. The Town That Answers to One Voice

The river turned north at a place the charts called only The Elbow, and Jonah felt it in his teeth before he saw it — that faint tremor of steel under load, rails singing somewhere beyond the treeline. He had spent eleven years teaching himself not to hear it.

"Smoke," Odile said, one boot on the Kestrel's rail, her binoculars steady. "Kiln smoke, maybe. Or a forge."

Or a generator, Jonah thought, feeding a radio tower. He said nothing. The Kestrel's hull groaned beneath them, taking water in the way old boats do — patiently, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Then the trees opened, and Meridian was there.

It should not have been there. That was Jonah's first thought, professional before it was personal: this valley was a floodplain, a dead-end branch off a line abandoned twice over, worthless by every survey he'd ever read. And yet here were forty roofs laid out in clean rows, a sawmill breathing its sweet rot into the morning air, gardens terraced up the slope like green stitches. And running through the middle of it all, straight as a sermon, the rail line — cleared, ballasted, alive, with a junction house squatting at its heart like a spider in a web of switches.

A man stood on the platform waiting for them. Tall, silver-templed, wearing a surveyor's canvas coat with the cuffs worn soft from work. He raised one hand in welcome before Odile had even thrown the line.

"You'll be the salvage crew," he called. "Cassius Hale. Welcome to Meridian." His eyes found Jonah across the water and held there a half-second too long, the way a man looks at a lock he recognizes. "We've been expecting you since the expedition stopped answering. Come ashore. You're expected."

*Expected.* The word went down Jonah's spine like cold water finding a seam.

* * *

Wren had treated four hundred people in Meridian and never once been thanked properly, because gratitude implied choice, and no one in Meridian had chosen anything. They had arrived lost or desperate or simply curious, and Hale had folded them in with that voice of his — warm as a stove, precise as a transit — until leaving became unthinkable. Not forbidden. Unthinkable. That was his genius, she'd long ago admitted to herself. He never locked a door. He just made every other door invisible.

She was sorting sutures when the salvage crew came up from the dock, and she heard the injured man before she saw him — the particular wet rattle of a lung working around blood. A deckhand, they said. Crane cable snapped in the rapids below the gorge. Broken ribs, three of them at least, and the man gray-faced and trying to apologize for the trouble.

"Table," Wren said, and the room rearranged itself around her the way it always did.

She worked with her hands and half her mind; the other half was listening, because doctors in Meridian learned to listen. The crew's stories didn't fit. The expedition — Hale's own survey team, silent for two months — was supposedly scattered, lost, dead in the bush. But these strangers spoke of seeing expedition gear in use, of fresh-cut corduroy road on the old portage trail, of a cook who swore he'd recognized the expedition's botanist hauling timber *upriver*, healthy, sunburned, waving cheerfully.

And Hale had told the settlement they were dead. Told them gently, gravely, with a moment of silence at supper. Wren had led that silence herself.

She taped the deckhand's ribs and caught his eye. "Hurts to breathe?"

"Like drowning slow," he rasped.

"Then don't talk. But if you wanted to tell me what you saw on the portage trail—" she kept her voice light, folding gauze, "—I'd listen while you rested."

He looked at her a long moment, weighing something. Then, barely audible: "They weren't prisoners, miss. That's the strange part. They looked *settled*."

* * *

Jonah walked the main street of Meridian and knew every plank of it.

Not the street itself — he'd never been here — but the thinking behind it. The drainage culverts set at his own favored gradient. The buildings sited back from the rail line at exactly the clearance he'd have specified. The junction house with its switch levers arranged left-to-heavy, a layout so idiosyncratic he'd once argued about it in a conference room in another life, in another name. Someone had built his blueprint in a valley he'd never surveyed, down to habits he hadn't known he had.

Hale walked beside him, narrating proudly. "Rationed radio, I'm afraid — the tower serves the whole settlement, and power is dear. One scheduled transmission a week, community business first. You understand. Isolation is a discipline." He smiled. "Everything here works because everything here answers to one voice."

"And whose voice is that?" Jonah asked.

"Mine, at present." Hale said it without vanity, which made it worse. "Authority is just maintenance, Mr. Winterbourne. Somebody has to grease the machine."

The name hit him like a coupling under tension. Winterbourne. Not the name he'd carried upriver on Odile's manifest — the false one, the cheap one, bought with cash in a coastal town. His real name. The one attached to the inquiry, the collapsed trestle, the eleven dead, the engineering board that had stripped him bare. A name he had buried so deep even Odile didn't know it.

He stopped walking. "I think you've mistaken me for someone else."

"I make very few mistakes," Hale said pleasantly. "I surveyed this entire valley with your own field manual open on my knee. Chapter six. *Water always tells you where it wants to go; your job is only to disagree persuasively.*" He turned, and his eyes were kind, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "You designed this place, Jonah. Years ago. Before you ran. Every culvert, every grade, every switch. You just didn't know it yet. Would you like to see the plans? I keep them framed."

* * *

That night Wren climbed to the infirmary loft and sat with the deckhand's chart in her lap and did not sleep.

*Settled,* the man had said. She thought of the expedition's supposed graves — Hale had shown her the site himself, two months ago, a row of crosses on the bluff, and she had noticed even then that the ground was undisturbed, that no ground in this wet country settled that fast, that the crosses were too evenly spaced, too new, too *tidy*. She had told herself grief made her suspicious. Grief made everyone stupid, Hale said so often.

But grief wasn't making her stupid now. Something else was. Something that looked a lot like waking up.

Below her window, the stranger named Winterbourne — though she'd already heard Hale say the other name, and watched the man flinch like a struck animal — stood alone in the dark beside the junction house, staring at the rails. He looked less like a fugitive than a man reading his own handwriting on a confession he never wrote.

She should report him. Hale would want to know the newcomer had been walking the perimeter after hours, counting switches, checking the tower's guy-wires. It was what a loyal citizen of Meridian did.

Instead she found herself memorizing the way he stood, and wondering what a man had to run from to bury his own name that deep.

* * *

In the morning, Hale summoned them all to the junction house and made his offer with the whole settlement watching from the doorways, because Hale understood audiences.

"The monsoon comes early this year," he said. "The gauge readings are bad. If we seal the lower culverts and raise the levee before the flood, Meridian survives another winter. If we fail—" he spread his hands, letting the valley speak for itself, "—we drown. I need an engineer. I need *the* engineer." His gaze settled on Jonah, warm and terrible. "Stay. Help me seal the valley. Stand beside me as more than a guest. This place is yours, Jonah. It has been yours since you drew it. All you have to do is claim it."

The settlement murmured approval. Somewhere in the crowd, Wren watched Jonah's face go white and still, and understood, with a lurch, that she was watching a man being offered a kingdom he'd built in a nightmare.

And Jonah, staring past Hale at the rails gleaming north toward the glacier, heard himself say: "I'll need to see the culvert drawings first."

It was surrender shaped like caution. It was also time. Time enough, he prayed, to find a way out of the valley before the water — or the man who owned his name — closed every door but the one Hale wanted open.

Behind him, unnoticed, Wren slipped the deckhand's chart into her coat, and began, quietly, to plan.
