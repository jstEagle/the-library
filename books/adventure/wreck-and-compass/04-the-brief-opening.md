## 4. The Brief Opening

The Kestrel ran the flood like a wounded animal running from fire — low in the water, taking green over the bow, her seams weeping where Odile's betrayal had left them patched with canvas and prayer. Jonah stood at the wheel with his hands bare to the rain and drove her anyway.

Behind them, Meridian's launch came down the river with its searchlight swinging like a lighthouse gone mad. He could hear the engine through the storm's roar, a deeper note than the Kestrel's tired throb, and he knew what it meant: Hale had armed it. Hale would not stop at the rapids. Hale had never stopped at anything in his life.

"Bridge!" Wren shouted from the bow, one hand locked on the rail, the other shielding her eyes against the driving rain. Her hair had come loose and plastered across her face, and she was laughing — actually laughing, wild and terrified, the sound of a woman who had burned her oath papers an hour ago and felt the ash still warm in her chest.

"I see it!" Jonah called back.

He had seen it for twenty years. That was the trouble.

The old rail bridge stood where the valley pinched to a throat of granite, spanning the river on stone piers his own hands had surveyed, his own calculations had placed, back when he signed his drawings with a name that wasn't Jonah Winterbourne. Elias Vane. The man who built things that lasted. The man whose blueprints Cassius Hale had found in a government archive and used like a key — every culvert, every flood gauge, every weakness in the valley's bones laid out in Jonah's own careful ink.

The bridge was his work. The piers were set exactly where he'd set them, because stone does not forget, and neither did Hale.

And the bridge was the only thing that could stop the launch.

* * *

The rapids began a half mile below it, white water standing up in the dark like the river had grown teeth. Any sane pilot would run them first and deal with pursuit after. But the launch drew less water than the Kestrel. It would come through the rapids behind them, angry and intact, and then there was nothing between Meridian and the narrows but open river.

Unless the river closed.

Jonah did the arithmetic the way he'd once done it for a living, fast and cold, numbers sliding through the fear like ball bearings. The monsoon had raised the water eleven feet since they'd tied up at Meridian. Eleven feet of new current hammering against piers designed for a river half this size. The upstream span sat on a pier he had flagged as suspect in his own field notes — a seam of fractured granite he'd recommended blasting out and re-pouring, a recommendation some accountant had struck from the budget before it ever reached a foreman.

His signature was on that flag. His handwriting. If any part of him wanted proof of who he used to be, it was written in red pencil on a page forty years old, waiting under the waterline like a confession.

"Elias." Wren had come aft to stand beside him, shouting over the engine. She only used that name when she needed all of him — the buried engineer, the man who understood why bridges fell. "Tell me."

"The pier under the third span is rotten at the bedrock," he said. "It was rotten when I flagged it. The flood's doing my work for me — another hour, maybe two, and the river takes it down on its own. But we don't have an hour."

"So we give it help."

He looked at her. Rain ran off her chin, off the knuckles of the hand she'd wrapped around his forearm, holding on to him as if he were something worth keeping. Behind her, the searchlight swept the water and slid away again, and in its brief glare he saw her face clearly: no doubt in it anywhere. She had chosen. She was asking him to choose too.

"There's a maintenance gallery in the second pier," he said slowly. "A crawl space, iron ladder, access hatch on the downstream face. I put it there." A breath that was almost a laugh. "I put everything there. That's the whole disease, Wren. Everything in this valley is mine."

"Then you get to decide what happens to it." She squeezed his arm. "Not Hale. Not the past. You."

The Kestrel bucked through a standing wave and shipped a ton of green water that went ankle-deep across the deck and swirled out the scuppers. Ahead, the bridge loomed out of the rain, black iron and wet stone, and beneath it the river ran high and brown and furious, carrying whole trees in its grip like straws.

Jonah turned the wheel and headed for the pier.

* * *

They came alongside in the lee of the stonework, where the current eddied backward and the Kestrel could lie against old rubber fenders with her engine idling. The hatch was exactly where his drawings said it would be, three feet above the present waterline, crusted with rust but unsealed. Of course it was unsealed. No one had come here in decades. No one came to Jonah's forgotten places except Jonah.

"You're not going alone," Wren said, already knotting a line around her waist.

"It's a crawl space full of black powder residue and bad air—"

"And you're a man who lies about being fine." She finished the knot and met his eyes. "We've been over this, Elias. Where you go, I go. That was the terms. You agreed in the boathouse with a gun in the room, so don't pretend you've forgotten."

There was no arguing with her. There never had been, from the first night she'd stitched his crewmate's scalp by lamplight and asked questions Hale wouldn't allow. He kissed her once, hard, tasting rain, and then they went down together into the dark of his own design.

The gallery smelled of iron rot and river mold. His lamp found the walls he'd specified — brick lining, weep holes drilled at intervals, his initials chiseled by some mason into the corner stone where inspectors could find them. E.V., 1911. He put his palm flat against the letters for a moment. Cold stone. Cold name. A dead man's mark on a living structure.

Above them, faint through forty feet of rock and water, he heard the launch's engine change pitch. Slowing. Hale had seen the Kestrel tucked against the pier, and he was coming to take back what he believed he owned.

"He'll board the boat first," Wren whispered. "Buy us minutes."

"We don't need minutes. We need this." Jonah's lamp swung to the far end of the gallery, where the pier's heart opened into a flooded shaft — the old foundation caisson, and at its base, visible as a pale smear through the turbid water, the fractured seam he'd flagged in red pencil forty years ago. Beside it, rusted shut, the demolition charge housing he'd designed for exactly this contingency and never once seen used. Two sticks of salvaged dynamite from the Kestrel's flare locker would fit it perfectly. He had measured the housing himself. He knew its dimensions the way he knew his own hands.

Wren watched him crimp the fuse, her face lit orange and trembling. "You're sure."

"No," he said honestly. "But I'm certain. There's a difference. Sure is for men with options."

He touched flame to fuse, and they climbed.

* * *

The explosion came up through the pier like a heartbeat from underground — a deep concussion more felt than heard, a shudder in the stone, a gout of spray from the shaft mouth. For three seconds nothing happened. Then the river leaned on the wound.

The third span sagged. Groaned. The iron truss above it shrieked like something alive, and then the whole span folded sideways into the flood with a roar that swallowed the thunder, dragging a hundred tons of stone and steel down onto the launch's path. Jonah, hauling the Kestrel away from the eddy with the throttle wide open, saw the searchlight swing wildly once — saw, absurdly, a figure at the launch's rail, coat streaming, arm raised either in fury or command — and then the falling span hit the water and the light went out, and the river closed over the wreck of Hale's pursuit like a mouth.

Whether he lived, Jonah didn't know. He found, examining himself in the dark of the wheelhouse, that he hoped so. Not for Hale's sake. For his own. He did not want to be a man who built bridges and drowned surveyors. He wanted, very simply, to be free.

"Wrecked your own work," Wren said beside him. She was soaked through, breathing hard, alive.

"No," Jonah said. "Finally finished it."

* * *

The narrows opened at dawn, or what passed for dawn under monsoon cloud — a gray brightening, the rain thinning to a drifting veil. The glacier came down to the water on the north shore like a frozen waterfall the size of a cathedral, blue-green in its depths, groaning continuously, a sound like the earth clearing its throat. And at its toe, exactly as the old charts promised, the calving channel: a lane of open black water between the ice wall and the pack, held clear for a few hours each season by the glacier's own exhalations, already beginning to bristle at its edges with new floes.

They went through with the engine at half-throat, quiet, because noise brought the ice down. Jonah steered by eye and instinct while Wren stood watch forward, calling the leads — *clear to starboard, hard left, ice, ICE* — and the Kestrel threaded the collapsing world with her seams leaking and her fuel nearly spent and both of them grinning like lunatics whenever a chunk of glacial ice the size of a house calved off the wall and went under with a boom they felt in their teeth.

Behind them, once, far back through the rain, Meridian's lights were visible on the bluff — the junction tower, the rationed radio mast, the neat grid of a town built on stolen blueprints. Then, one by one, they failed. Wren's sabotage had done its patient work; the generator she'd crippled with burning papers and a crowbar finally gave out entirely, and the settlement went dark section by section, like a body deciding to sleep. The last light to die was the tower beacon. It blinked twice, stubbornly, and went out.

No one spoke. There was nothing to say that the darkness didn't say better.

Then the channel narrowed to its final throat, ice grinding along both sides of the hull with a sound like slow applause, and they shot through into open water — gray, wind-scoured, enormous, leading south toward country no map of theirs had ever covered.

Jonah killed the engine and let the Kestrel drift.

For a long time there was only the creak of the hull, the drip of the rain, the distant artillery of the calving glacier. Wren came aft and stood beside him at the wheel, close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them said anything, because everything that mattered was already known. He was not Elias Vane anymore; that man lay under a collapsed span with his red-pencil confessions. He was not yet anyone else. He was a man in a boat with a woman who had seen every version of him and stayed.

"Hungry?" she asked at last.

"Starving."

She smiled and went below to find the tinned food, and Jonah Winterbourne — engineer of nothing, captain of one leaky boat, visible at last to exactly one person in all the wide world — turned the wheel south and let the river carry them wherever it pleased.
