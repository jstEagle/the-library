## 1. The River Keeps Its Books

The river had been eating Kestrel Bend for three weeks before it started giving things back.

Dominic Zhou stood hip-deep in the churn at six in the morning, working the dredge hose with the particular resentment of a man who had once been told he'd amount to something. The water was the color of milk tea and moved with the confidence of a freight train. Somewhere under all that brown violence lay the old ferry landing, two sunken rowboats, a drowned orchard fence, and — if the river felt generous today — enough salvage to knock another few hundred dollars off the debt his mother had left him like an heirloom nobody wanted.

"Zhou!" bellowed Odell from the bank, cupping his hands. "You're drifting!"

"I'm aware," Dominic said, though the current took most of it. He planted the hose again and let the suction do its dumb, faithful work. Gravel rattled up the pipe. A beer can. Half of a lawn chair. The river kept its books, Odell liked to say, and sooner or later it settled every account.

Dominic had learned to stop hoping around week one. Hope was expensive. It cost attention, and attention was what you needed to keep from getting folded into a bridge piling. So he worked in the flat gray register of a man paying off a dead woman's creditors: pump, sweep, listen for the change in pitch when the hose found something denser than mud.

The change came at 6:40.

A deep, wrong *thunk*, felt more than heard, traveling up the pipe and into his teeth like a struck bell. Dominic killed the throttle and stood very still in the water, which is what you did when the river spoke. Then he waded toward the sound, feeling along the bottom with his boots until his toe hit something that was neither rock nor log nor any of the hundred ordinary objects a flood could swallow.

It was square. That was the first strange thing. Rivers don't make squares.

He went down twice for it, lungs burning, fingers numb past feeling, and on the second dive he got his arms around it and understood immediately that he had made a serious error, because whatever this was weighed as much as sin. He surfaced, spat out half the Kestrel, and hollered for Odell without much dignity.

* * *

They got it onto the bank by seven-thirty, using the winch, two come-alongs, and language that would have gotten them both excommunicated from any church in the valley. A small audience assembled the way audiences do in river towns — silently, from nowhere, holding coffee.

The thing was a chest. Bronze, or bronze-plated, green with age except where the flood had scoured it bright. It was the size of a footlocker and built like an argument: riveted corners, a lid that fit with jeweler's precision, no lock that Dominic could find. And stamped into the top, deep enough to have survived who knew how many decades underwater, was a seal.

A circle of interlocking script he didn't recognize, arranged around a mountain with three peaks and, beneath the peaks, a door.

"Huh," said Odell.

"That's my feeling exactly," said Dominic.

Odell crouched, squinted, and touched the script with one reverent finger, which was unlike him; Odell's relationship with reverence was distant and mostly epistolary. "That ain't Chinese," he said. "That ain't anything."

"It's not Latin either." Dominic turned the chest. The script ran clockwise around the rim, dense and angular, letters like scaffolding. His mother would have known. His mother had known forty dead languages the way other people knew their neighbors' dogs — by name, by temperament, by the sound they made at night. She had also known how to leave a son twenty-two thousand dollars in debts to a salvage outfit, a storage unit in a town she'd never lived in, and a phone number that disconnected the month after her funeral.

The crowd grew. Mrs. Adeyemi from the bait shop arrived and pronounced it a coffin, then revised to a treasure, then settled on "trouble" with the satisfaction of a woman who liked being right early. Two teenagers filmed it vertically. Somebody's dog barked at it on principle.

And through all of it, Dominic felt the thing he hadn't felt in fourteen months — since before the funeral, maybe since before the last unanswered letter — rising in him like water in a locked hold.

Wonder. Reluctant, unwelcome, insistent wonder.

Because here was the thing about growing up as Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou's son: you learned to read objects. You learned that a chipped potsherd could be a sentence, that a coin could be a confession, that the past didn't stay buried out of modesty but because nobody had asked it the right question. She'd tried to teach him. He'd spent his adolescence refusing the lessons with the full theatrical commitment available only to teenage boys, and now here he stood, forty pounds of riverwater heavier, reading a bronze chest like it was a telegram addressed specifically to him.

*A mountain with three peaks. A door beneath.*

He knew that image. Not from any book. From a paperweight on his mother's desk when he was nine — a lump of cast bronze she'd never explained, that vanished along with everything else of hers every time she left for a dig.

"Open it," said one of the teenagers.

"No," said Dominic, at the same moment Mrs. Adeyemi said "Yes," and the chest sat between these verdicts, patient as a judge.

It wasn't locked. That was the other thing. He ran his thumbs along the seam and found the mechanism — a recessed stud, worn smooth, that depressed with a click he felt in his back teeth. The lid didn't open so much as *consent*. Inside, packed in what looked like waxed linen gone stiff as bark:

Half a book.

Vellum, edged in corroded bronze, cut clean down the middle like something split on purpose. The visible page bore columns of that same angular script and, running down the margin, a line of numbers that made the hair on Dominic's arms stand up despite the morning heat.

Coordinates. He didn't need his mother's ghost to tell him that. Any kid raised on her knee could read coordinates the way farm kids read weather.

"Close it," said Odell quietly. Odell, who feared nothing except paperwork and his ex-wife, had gone pale. "Boy, close the box. There's folks watching, and that there is somebody's history, and history like that gets people talked about."

Dominic closed it. But not before his thumb brushed the vellum edge, and not before he noticed — pressed into the waxed linen lining, faint as a watermark — a second stamp. Smaller. A monogram.

*M.Z.*

His mother's initials.

* * *

By noon he had done three things, in order: lied to the county salvage officer (the chest was "farm equipment, probably"), lied to Mrs. Adeyemi ("brass boiler parts"), and failed, entirely, to lie to himself.

He sat in the cab of Odell's truck behind the bait shop with the chest wedged between them like a chaperone, eating a sandwich he couldn't taste. Rain ticked on the roof. Downriver, the flood was dropping, revealing mudflats scored with debris lines like tide marks on a wound.

"You gonna tell me where you really found the headspace for that thing?" Odell asked. "Because I was there, and 'farm equipment' was cowardice."

"It was efficiency."

"It was cowardice wearing efficiency's hat." Odell chewed. "Your mama ever talk about her work? Around you?"

"She talked *around* it. Like weather. Like traffic." Dominic looked at the chest. Under the tarp he'd thrown over it, it radiated presence anyway, the way a sleeping dog changes a room. "She'd call sometimes from wherever. Never said where. Once she told me she was somewhere the maps got wrong, and I thought she meant she was lost."

"Was she?"

"She was never lost. That was the problem. She always knew exactly where she was." He heard it come out sharper than intended, and Odell had the grace to look at the rain instead of at him. Fourteen months, and the grief still ambushed him sideways, dressed as irritation. "I'm sorry. I didn't sleep."

"Nobody sleeps in a flood year." Odell finished his sandwich, dusted his hands, and became, briefly, the friend instead of the boss. "Dom. Listen. I've known you since you showed up here broke and lying about your welding certs. You're a good liar, but you got a face like a window. Whatever's in that box, it already has you. Question is whether it has you cheap or dear."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means your mama owed me money too, and I forgave it, because she once pulled my boy out of this same river, and some accounts don't settle in dollars." He shrugged. "So when the men come asking — and they'll come, son, nothing travels faster than a secret with a shape — you think hard about who you let in the truck."

"The men."

"Mm." Odell started the engine. "Fella called the shop this morning while you were drowning yourself. Polite as a deacon. Wanted to know if we'd pulled anything unusual. Said he represented parties interested in 'regional heritage recovery.'" Odell snorted. "Regional heritage recovery. In Kestrel Bend. Our regional heritage is a meth lab and a haunted Denny's."

"When was this?"

"Eight-forty this morning." Odell looked at him. "Chest came out of the water at seven-thirty."

The rain filled the silence for both of them. Dominic did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the bank: someone had known what the river held before the river gave it up. Someone had been watching the flood line, or the salvage permits, or — colder thought — *him*, waiting for Dr. Zhou's son to dredge up what she'd left in the water like a message in a bottle with the address half torn off.

"There's a phone number," Odell added, gentler now. "On the shop machine. Said to call collect. Any hour."

Dominic stared through the windshield at the brown, retreating river. Every instinct he owned — the ones built in fourteen months of creditors and condolences, the older ones built in a childhood of unanswered calls — said *burn it, sink it, walk away*. Let the river keep its books. Let the past settle its own accounts.

But his hands were already reaching for the tarp.

Because underneath the dread, quiet as the stud that opened the lid, sat the other thing. The wonder. And beneath the wonder, oldest of all, a child's stubborn arithmetic: *she left this where I would find it.* Maybe. Possibly. Probably not. But the chest bore her initials, and the book inside was cut in half — *custodian, second half*, some traitor part of his memory whispered, though he couldn't have said why — and half of anything is a message that says *look for the rest.*

"Dom," Odell said.

"Yeah."

"Don't make that call."

Dominic looked at the chest. Three peaks. A door beneath.

"I'm not going to," he said, and both of them understood he was lying, and the river, indifferent, kept its books.
