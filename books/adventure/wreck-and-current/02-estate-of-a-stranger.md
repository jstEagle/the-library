## 2. Estate of a Stranger

The storage unit smelled like every storage unit on earth: cardboard, dust, and the faint chemical ghost of things people had decided not to deal with.

"Unit 114," Dominic said to himself, as if announcing it would make the number mean something. It didn't. It was just a corrugated steel box in a lot outside Kestrel Bend where the asphalt had gone soft in the sun and a cat was sleeping on the pay kiosk like it owned the place. Maybe it did. The cat looked like it had better credit than he did.

He'd been putting this off for eleven days. Eleven days of legitimate excuses — the dredging contract, the chest sitting under a tarp in his apartment like a sleeping animal, the phone calls from creditors that he let ring out because answering them required a version of himself he hadn't located yet. But the truth was simpler: his mother had rented this unit for nineteen years, paid on time every month, and never once mentioned it. And now he was going to open a door she'd kept locked her whole life, including, apparently, the part of it that overlapped with his.

He hauled the roll-up door until it clattered against its stops and daylight fell inside.

It was not what he expected. He'd braced for chaos — the archaeologist's equivalent of a hoarder's nest, trowels and potsherds and forty years of field jackets. Instead the unit was almost aggressively organized. Crates labeled by year. Boxes labeled by site. A folding table with a lamp clipped to it, still set up, as if she'd stepped out for coffee and gotten hit by a bus instead of a stroke, which was more or less what happened.

"Oh, Mom," he said quietly. "You absolute weirdo."

There was something both heartbreaking and darkly funny about it. Most people's grief came in waves; his came in filing systems. She had catalogued her own life down to the last pencil stub, and somehow that was worse than mess. Mess would have meant she'd been too busy living. This meant she'd known exactly how much time everything took, and had spent it all here instead of at home.

He started with the crates nearest the door, because starting anywhere else felt like cheating.

Mostly it was professional detritus. Notebooks in her small, slanted hand. Copies of papers she'd published in journals nobody read. A plaster cast of something that might have been a footprint or might have been an ambitious rock. A tin of biscuits that, when he opened it, contained not biscuits but thirty-seven pencils sharpened to identical lengths, which was so precisely his mother that he laughed out loud, alone, in a storage unit, and then had to stand there for a minute with his hand over his mouth.

*She chose the work.* That was the sentence he'd been carrying since the funeral, handed to him by an aunt who meant well and delivered it like a verdict anyway. *You know how she was, Dominic. The work came first.* And he'd nodded along because arguing with grief-adjacent relatives is like arm-wrestling a river.

But standing in her secret room, looking at her secret order, he found the sentence didn't fit right anymore. It wasn't that she'd chosen the work over him. It was that she'd built a whole second life and furnished it without him, and he couldn't tell if that was cruelty or cowardice or just the way she'd learned to keep two incompatible things alive at once.

He found the photographs in the fourth crate.

They were in a manila envelope, unmarked, tucked beneath a stack of site reports. He knew before he pulled them out. Some part of him — the part that had grown up watching her face go carefully blank whenever certain subjects surfaced — knew.

Black-and-white first. Then color, grainy, the kind you got developed at a pharmacy counter. And in nearly every one, the same object: a bronze fitting, circular, worked with interlocking lines around a central emblem he didn't have a word for. A seal. Stamped into metal, pressed into clay, photographed lying on a survey grid with a scale bar beside it like a criminal mugshot.

The same seal that was currently sitting under a tarp in his apartment, hauled out of the Kestrel Bend riverbed six days ago by a man with forty thousand dollars of inherited debt and a dredge barge he didn't own.

Dominic sat down on a crate labeled *SERRANO '09 — FRAGILE* and went through the photos twice, three times. Dates on the backs, in her handwriting. 1987. 1991. 2003. The most recent was dated four months before she died. In it, the seal lay on white cloth, shot straight down, and someone had written beneath it in her tight script:

*Second casting. First is accounted for. Where is the third?*

"Where's the third," he repeated. "Great. Love a mystery with homework."

His phone buzzed in his pocket and he flinched hard enough to nearly drop the photo. It was just the storage facility's automated reminder about gate hours. He put it away and noticed his hands weren't entirely steady, and decided not to have feelings about that yet. Feelings could queue. Feelings were good at queuing.

* * *

The letter was under the table. Not hidden, exactly — his mother didn't hide things, she *filed* them, which was different in spirit and identical in effect. A flat document case, and inside it, a single sheet of heavy paper, folded once, addressed in her handwriting:

*To my child, if anyone ever gives you this.*

He read it standing up, then sitting down, then standing again, because apparently his body thought pacing helped.

*Dominic —*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead, or I have finally run out of ways to avoid this conversation, and knowing me, it will be the former. I am sorry for a great many things and I have never been good at saying so out loud, so I am doing it on paper, which is where cowards do their best work.*

*You are my heir. Not of money — I'm aware of the state of the accounts, and I'm sorry for that too. You are heir to the other thing. The ledger has two halves. One travels with the work. The other stays with the family. That arrangement is older than either of us and I don't fully understand it myself, only that I promised, and promises made to dead people are the hardest kind to break.*

*Custodian, second half. That is what you are, whether you want it or not. I suspect you do not want it. I suspect you have excellent reasons.*

*If anyone comes asking questions — polite men, careful men, men who use words like "provenance" and "acquisition" — remember that they are not asking. They are measuring. Do not be measured.*

*I would explain more, but explanations travel badly. Come find me if you can. If you can't, forgive me if you can. Either way, lock the door behind you.*

*— M.*

He read it four times. On the fifth pass he noticed his jaw hurt and realized he'd been clenching it since the second paragraph.

*Custodian, second half.* The same phrase, almost word for word, that had been rattling around in his head since the chest came up out of the river — except he hadn't known it was a phrase yet. He'd felt it, the way you feel weather coming. Half a ledger. His mother's letter said it plain: the ledger had two halves, and one of them was supposed to be his, and nobody had given it to him, and now some bronze box fished out of a flooded river was sitting in his living room wearing a seal from photographs his mother had secretly collected for decades.

"Lock the door behind you," he told the empty unit. "Sure. Easy. Very normal letter, Mom. Very reassuring."

The cat from the kiosk had wandered in at some point and was sitting in the doorway regarding him with the flat judgment of all cats. He appreciated having an audience. It was either that or talk to himself, and he'd already crossed that line.

"Okay," he said to the cat. "Hypothetically. Your mom dies, leaves you debts and a storage unit. In the unit there's a letter saying congratulations, you're the custodian of half of something, identity unknown, location unknown, pursued by unspecified polite men. Meanwhile you've already pulled the other half out of a river, except you didn't know it was the other half, because why would you, because your mother communicated exclusively through riddles and biscuit tins full of pencils." The cat blinked slowly. "And the question is: do you take the letter seriously, or do you decide your mother was eccentric and go back to drowning in debt like a normal person?"

The cat left. Dominic decided that was also an answer.

* * *

He was repacking the crate of photographs when the shadow fell across the doorway.

Not dramatic, not ominous — just a man standing in the late-afternoon light, silhouetted, hands visible and relaxed at his sides in a way that registered as deliberate even before Dominic consciously noticed it. When the man stepped forward, he resolved into somebody's idea of reasonable: mid-fifties, gray at the temples, a jacket that cost more than Dominic's dredge contract, and a smile calibrated to the exact degree of warmth that puts people at ease without inviting them closer.

"Mr. Zhou? Dominic Zhou?" The voice matched the smile. Smooth, unhurried, faintly accented in a way that had been sanded down by expensive schools. "I'm sorry to intrude. Elias Voss."

"I didn't hear a car," Dominic said, which was true, and which he said mostly to buy time while his brain did the math on the distance between himself and the doorway. Three meters. The man was standing in it.

"There isn't one. I walked up from the depot." Voss glanced around the unit with an expression of mild, respectful interest — the interest of a man browsing, not searching. Though Dominic noted his eyes did a full circuit of the space before returning, unhurried, to Dominic's face. "I understand Dr. Zhou was your mother. I wanted to offer condolences. Belated ones. I was abroad when I heard."

"You knew her?"

"We moved in overlapping circles. Academia is a small pond with very sharp rocks." Voss said it lightly, but something moved behind his eyes when he said *rocks*, quick as a fish turning. "May I?" He gestured at the interior without waiting for permission, stepping just inside the threshold — enough to be present, not enough to be threatening. Everything about him was calibrated to that line. Dominic had grown up around field crews and smugglers and one memorable season of river pirates; he knew what a man looked like when he was managing his own threat profile. This man was managing it beautifully.

"How did you find me?" Dominic asked.

"The estate notice. Public record." A small shrug. "Also, frankly, the news from Kestrel Bend. A dredger pulls a bronze chest out of the flood channel, and the chest bears the seal of Kharvath." He pronounced the name carefully, the way you'd handle something with an edge. "That is a remarkable story, Mr. Zhou. Remarkable stories attract remarkers."

"Is that what you are?"

"A friend of the discipline." Voss smiled again, and this time let it fade naturally, which was somehow worse than holding it. "Look — I'll be direct, because I suspect directness is a courtesy in your family. The chest you recovered. Has anyone approached you about it? Made offers? Asked to examine it?"

"Why?"

"Because there are people who will. And not all of them will be as pleasant as I am." He paused, and the pause was a masterwork — long enough to imply danger, short enough to deny it. "Your mother understood that better than most. She was... cautious. Perhaps you've gathered that."

Dominic looked at the man's polished shoes, at the dust they were collecting from his mother's floor, and felt the letter's warning rise in him like water in a bilge. *Polite men, careful men, men who use words like provenance and acquisition.* Voss hadn't used those words yet. Dominic found himself listening for them the way you listen for a floorboard creak in a house you've just realized has someone else in it.

"She was cautious," Dominic agreed. "She was also private. So I'll return the courtesy of directness: I don't know you, Mr. Voss. My mother never mentioned you. And I don't discuss finds with strangers in storage lots."

"Entirely fair." Voss produced a card from his breast pocket — heavy stock, embossed, no title, just a name and a phone number, which told Dominic more than a résumé would have. "No pressure. No agenda. Only —" and here the smoothness thinned, just slightly, just enough to show the wire underneath, "— be careful with it. The chest. There are parties who consider such objects theirs by prior claim, and they are not academic about disagreement. If you ever need to make something disappear, or need advice on how to hold onto it, call that number. Any hour."

"That's very generous of a stranger."

"I owed your mother a debt." Something crossed Voss's face then — fast, genuine, gone. Grief, maybe. Or its well-rehearsed understudy. "More than you know. Consider this a first installment."

He inclined his head, stepped back into the light, and walked away down the row of units at an easy pace, no hurry, no glance back. Dominic watched him go and counted to sixty before moving.

Then he checked the envelope of photographs. Still there. The letter — he'd put it in his jacket an hour ago, an instinct he now understood had been the smartest thing he'd done all week. Still there. The chest was at his apartment, under the tarp, and suddenly six days of leaving it there felt less like caution and more like bait.

*They are not asking,* his mother had written. *They are measuring.*

Voss had measured the whole room in four seconds. Dominic had watched him do it.

He stood in the doorway of Unit 114, holding a card worth more than his truck, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding: a man who walks miles from a depot to ask polite questions about a bronze chest is a man who already knows the answers, and is checking them against your face. Which meant somewhere between the river and this dusty steel box, Dominic Zhou had stopped being a debtor with a strange find and become a man in possession of something.

He looked at the card. *Any hour,* it promised, in expensive ink.

"Yeah," Dominic muttered, sliding it into his pocket next to the letter, where it lay against his ribs like a splinter. "Something tells me you're going to regret offering that."

Behind him, Unit 114 stood open on his mother's ordered life, everything filed, everything labeled, nothing explained. He pulled the door down hard. The lock caught with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence — and somewhere across town, he was suddenly certain, a phone was ringing, and the man on the other end of it was being told exactly which address to visit next.

Dominic started walking toward his apartment, faster than the heat justified, telling himself it was just the exercise.
