## 3. Half a Ledger

The chest sat on the kitchen table of Dominic's apartment like an accusation with hinges.

He'd gotten it home by the simple expedient of not thinking about it. He'd wrapped it in a tarp, told the foreman at Kestrel Bend that it was "engine parts," watched the foreman's eyebrows perform a small skeptical ballet, and then driven forty minutes with the thing belted into his passenger seat like the world's most ominous toddler. Now it sat under the kitchen's one working light, water still weeping out of its seams, and Dominic stood across from it with a beer he'd opened twenty minutes ago and not touched.

"You're a chest," he told it. "You're a box. You have no power over me."

The chest said nothing, being a chest, but the seal of Kharvath caught the light and seemed to disagree.

He'd spent the evening doing what any sensible person would do: he'd Googled it. *Kharvath* returned eleven results, six of them a boutique hotel in Portugal, three of them a misspelling of a video game faction, and one a genealogy forum post from 2009 in which a user named GrumpyPete asked if anyone had heard of the country and was answered, four years later, by a single reply: *"never existed, stop asking."* That was it. That was the sum total of human knowledge about a nation whose seal was currently dripping onto his kitchen table.

His mother, on the other hand, had a lot of results. Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou, 1961–2019. Obituaries in two languages. A lecture series. A scandal he still didn't fully understand, involving a dig in the Serrano foothills, a destroyed partnership, and a fine she'd died still paying. He'd spent three years not reading about any of it, and tonight the chest had dragged all of it back into the room like a dog with a dead bird.

Dominic set down the beer. He got a screwdriver, a towel, and the good kitchen shears — the ones he'd bought himself, with his own money, the one luxury of his adult life — and he knelt at the table.

The lock was bronze, salt-fused, and beautiful. It took him an hour and cost him a slice off his left thumb, and when it finally gave with a sound like a sigh, the lid rose on a breath of air that smelled of cedar, river, and something older underneath — the smell of a room that had been closed for a very long time.

Inside, on a bed of tarred canvas, lay two things.

The first was a roll of vellum, tied with cord gone soft as yarn. The second was a notebook — his mother's. He knew it before he touched it. He knew the green cloth cover, the cracked spine, the way she'd always taped a strip of masking tape across the front and written the site designation in her small, ruthless handwriting. He'd grown up watching her fill these. He'd once drawn a dinosaur in the margins of one when he was six, and she hadn't erased it; she'd labeled it. *Juvenile rendering, inaccurate, affectionate.* He'd found that notebook after she died and thrown it across a storage unit, and then picked it up, and then put it in his jacket pocket, where it stayed for a week.

This notebook was different. The handwriting inside was hers, but the words were not words. They were symbols — dense, angular, marching across the page in lines that made his eyes slide off like rain off wax.

"Of course," he said to the empty apartment. "Of course it's in code. Why would it be in a language? Languages can be *read*."

He unrolled the vellum instead, and the breath went out of him.

It was a map. Or half of one — that was the first thing he understood, the way you understand a face is missing its other eye. The vellum had been cut, cleanly, deliberately, down the center of the drawing, and what remained showed the eastern half of a mountain range he recognized in his bones: the Serranos, drawn with a precision that made modern survey maps look like children's crayon work. Rivers he'd dredged. Ridges he'd stared at from the cab of a crane. And in the heart of it, rendered in ink so fine it looked embroidered, a structure — a descending sequence of chambers, stairways, galleries, drawn in section like an architect's dream of a mountain with a secret skeleton.

At the top of the drawing, in an alphabet he'd never seen, ran a line of text. Below the cut edge, the drawing simply stopped, mid-chamber, mid-stair, like a sentence with its verb amputated.

Dominic sat back on his heels.

His mother's debts were eleven thousand dollars, spread across four creditors and one very persistent man named Okafor who called every second Tuesday and had, over the years, become something adjacent to a friend. Dominic had a plan for those debts: work them off, one dredging season at a time, until the ledger of his life read zero and he could maybe, possibly, buy a couch that didn't have a history.

He looked at the half-map of a buried archive in a mountain range, and at his mother's notebook full of a cipher, and at the seal of a country that had been deleted from the world, and he thought, with the weary clarity of a man who has been broke a long time:

*This is going to cost me.*

* * *

He did not sleep. He told himself he was being careful, and he was, but mostly he was reading. He photographed every page of the notebook with his phone — the battery died twice, and he charged it with the charger he kept in his jacket because his life had a certain rhythm to it — and he laid the half-map flat under the good light and memorized it, ridge by river, the way his mother had taught him to memorize terrain when he was small and she was still, sometimes, taking him along.

*Name the landmarks, Dominic. A map is just a promise the land made to someone who looked carefully.*

She'd said that on a trail in the coastal hills, when he was nine. He'd forgotten he remembered it. That was the thing about her — she kept turning up in his head at odd hours, like a tenant who'd moved out but still had keys.

Around two in the morning he noticed the detail that changed the temperature of the room.

The cut edge of the vellum wasn't random. He'd assumed whoever split the ledger had just sliced it in half, but the cut was precise — it ran exactly along a contour line, a deliberate border, and at three points along that edge, tiny holes had been punched through the vellum. Registration marks. Whatever the other half was, it was meant to be laid against this one, aligned by those holes, and the full drawing would only resolve when both halves were joined.

Two keys. One lock. His family had one key.

The phone call he didn't make was to Okafor, and then to a number he found on a Kestrel Bend dock notice for "artifact appraisal services," and then, at 2:40 a.m., with his thumb actually hovering over the screen, to the university department where his mother's name was still spoken in the tone people use for weather events. He didn't call any of them. He had a feeling — not mystical, just the animal instinct of a man who'd grown up around his mother's world, where a find announced too loudly changed owners — that the moment he told anyone, the chest would stop being his problem and start being his *situation*.

So he did the unglamorous thing. He put the vellum in a ziplock bag, the notebook in a second one, and he hid them in the one place he was confident no one would look: the box of his mother's unpaid bills, in the closet, under a layer of final notices so psychologically impenetrable that even he avoided it.

Then he slept four hours, went to work, dredged mud for ten hours, and came home to a door that had been opened with a skill he didn't have and closed again with a care he didn't either.

* * *

He knew before he touched the knob. There's a specific silence to a room that's been searched — not the silence of an empty place, but the silence of a place that's been *handled*. His mail on the table had been fanned. His dishes in the rack had been moved a half-inch. The couch cushions sat with a symmetry he had never achieved in his life.

Dominic stood in the hallway of his own building, keys in hand, and made the smart choice, which was to not go in.

He went in anyway, because the chest was in there.

It was still on the table. Open. Empty of everything except the tarred canvas lining, which had been lifted out and replaced — replaced *carefully*, which frightened him more than wreckage would have. His kitchen drawers had been opened and re-closed. His mother's storage receipts, which he kept in a jar by the window for reasons he couldn't defend, were gone. The jar was still there. The receipts weren't.

They'd been looking for what came out of the chest. They'd found the chest. They hadn't found the contents, because the contents were under a landslide of final notices, and for the first time in three years, Dominic felt a flicker of gratitude toward his own avoidance.

He took the bag with the vellum and the notebook out of the closet, put his jacket on over it — the jacket was heavy now, and warm against his back, and it felt less like carrying documents and more like carrying a passenger — and he was turning to leave when the stairwell door at the end of the hall opened without a sound.

Two men. Not police. Police announce themselves with their posture. These two had the posture of men who had never once in their lives needed permission to enter a room. The taller one wore a rain jacket too clean for the weather. The other one had the flat, patient face of a man who does the lifting.

"Mr. Zhou," said the tall one, pleasantly. "We've been looking forward to meeting you. Mr. Voss sends his regards. He tried to reach you at the depot — you'd left."

"I do that," Dominic said. His voice came out steadier than his hands felt. "People leave. It's a whole thing."

"It is." The tall man smiled like a form being filled out. "Mr. Voss is an admirer of your mother's work. He has questions about the chest you recovered from the river. He's prepared to be *extremely* generous about your mother's outstanding obligations — all of them — in exchange for a conversation. A short one."

Dominic did the math he'd been doing all day, the math that never came out in his favor. Eleven thousand dollars of debt against a half-map of something that made two professionals ransack his kitchen with the tenderness of museum conservators. Whatever the archive was, it was worth more than his entire life's arithmetic. Which meant the generous offer was a down payment, and the rest of the price would be collected later, in a currency he didn't want to think about.

"Tell Voss," Dominic said, backing toward the fire escape window he was already calculating the distance to, "that my mother's debts die with my mother."

The tall man sighed, genuinely regretful, the way a dentist is regretful. "They said you'd say that."

*They.* Dominic filed the word where he kept all the words that scared him, which was a rapidly expanding file.

The patient-faced man started forward, unhurried, and Dominic did the thing he'd never done in his life, the thing his mother would have had opinions about: he broke his own window. Glass went everywhere. He went through after it, onto the fire escape, taking a stripe of pain down his forearm that he'd have time to resent later, and he took the stairs three at a time into the dark and the drizzle, the ledger warm against his spine like a second heartbeat.

Behind him, above, he heard the window he'd climbed out of — heard a voice, calm and carrying, not shouting at all:

"We'll find you, Mr. Zhou. We're very good at finding things. Ask your mother."

Dominic ran.

He ran six blocks before his lungs filed a complaint, and then he walked, and then he sat in the plastic shelter of a bus depot he'd sworn off two days ago, under the fluorescent light, with rain coming off his hair and blood drying on his arm and his mother's cipher pressed flat between his shoulder blades.

The past had always been a landlord to him — monthly, predictable, collectible in installments. Somewhere between the kitchen table and the broken window, it had changed professions. It was a debt collector now, and it had found out where he lived, and it did not accept payment plans.

Dominic pulled out his phone with a numb hand and looked at the only contact in it he hadn't dialed yet, the name he'd been avoiding since the river: *I. Marchetti*, saved three years ago and never once called, a number his mother's lawyer had pressed on him with the words *"if anything ever — just, if anything."*

The depot clock said 11:52. The next eastbound left at midnight.

Dominic pressed call.

It rang twice. A voice answered — low, dry, awake at midnight in the way of people who are always awake — and said, before he could speak:

"You found the chest, didn't you. Get somewhere with more than one exit, and tell me where."
