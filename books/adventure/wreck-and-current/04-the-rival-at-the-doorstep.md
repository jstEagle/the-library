## 4. The Rival at the Doorstep

I had been waiting at the Kestrel Bend bus depot for six hours when I finally admitted to myself that I was not there by accident.

I had told the taxi driver I was passing through. I had told the woman at the ticket counter I was meeting a colleague. I had told myself, somewhere around hour three, that I simply enjoyed sitting on a plastic bench bolted under a ceiling fan that turned with the enthusiasm of a tired dog. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: I had read Mei-Lin Zhou's obituary eleven months ago, filed it away in the drawer of my mind marked *unfinished business*, and then — when a bronze chest bearing the seal of Kharvath surfaced in a dredging barge's net two weeks ago and made the regional news — I had gotten on a train.

Because I knew what that seal meant. And I knew who would come for it.

The depot smelled of diesel and fried dough and the particular staleness of buildings that have never been loved by their owners. A departures board flickered. A boy sold roasted corn from a bucket. I had bought three cobs out of politeness and eaten all of them out of boredom, and now my fingers were greasy, which felt appropriate. Archaeology is mostly grease.

He came in at 4:40 in the afternoon, and I recognized him before I could have explained how.

Dominic Zhou was taller than his mother, which surprised me; I had built him small in my head over the years, the way you build the children of people you resent — smaller, easier to dismiss. He had her jaw, though. That stubborn, slightly asymmetrical jaw that Mei-Lin used like a crowbar against the world. He carried a canvas duffel bag that hung wrong, too heavy at one end, and he walked the way people walk when they haven't slept in a place they trust for several nights. His eyes did a slow circuit of the depot — exits first, faces second — and something in my chest went cold and warm at once.

He'd been ransacked. Of course he had. Voss worked fast.

I stood up, brushed corn salt off my trousers, and crossed the floor before my better judgment could catch me at the door and lock it behind me.

"Dr. Zhou," I said. "You don't know me. You're going to want to."

He stopped. Looked at me the way his mother used to look at a suspect stratigraphy layer — politely, thoroughly, and with total willingness to find me worthless.

"I really don't," he said.

"Ines Marchetti." I offered my hand. He looked at it. "Your mother and I dug together in the Tarim Basin in '09. We hated each other professionally for fifteen years after that, which is its own kind of intimacy. I can read the cipher in your mother's field notes."

That got me something. Not trust — nothing so generous — but a flicker, quick as a fish turning under water.

"You've seen her field notes?" he said.

"No," I admitted. "But I know she kept them in cipher, because she kept *everything* in cipher after 2011, including — I'm fairly sure — her grocery lists. And I know the cipher, because half of it is built on a joke she played on me in the Tarim Basin, and she never let a good joke die. She recycled material the way bad comedians do. It was one of her most infuriating qualities." I paused. "She had many."

His eyes narrowed. "You're either telling the truth or you're very well briefed."

"Both things are possible," I said. "But only one of us has dirt under our fingernails from actual digging, and it isn't whoever briefed the other option."

* * *

We sat in the corner of the depot café, if a machine that dispensed coffee-adjacent liquid and a counter sticky with decades could be called a café. He took the seat facing the door. I noted it and said nothing, but I revised my estimate of him upward. Children of hunted parents learn to sit facing doors even when no one has told them why.

"Start from the beginning," he said. "Not your beginning. The relevant one."

So I told him about the Halvard Collection.

"Not a museum," I said. "Museums are public, accountable, full of grad students eating sad sandwiches. The Collection is private. It acquires — or rather, it *concludes*. When the Collection concludes an object exists, the object enters its catalog, and the catalog does not circulate. No exhibitions. No peer review. No country of origin getting sentimental about repatriation. Things go into vaults in Switzerland and Liechtenstein and, I believe, a decommissioned church in Malta, and they are never seen by human eyes again except the eyes of people paid not to see them."

"And this Voss works for them."

"Voss doesn't work *for* them the way a clerk works for a firm. Voss is their acquisition specialist. Their provenance man. If an artifact is a painting, Voss is the person who arrives before the frame is even dry." I wrapped my hands around the terrible coffee. "I've crossed paths with him twice. Once in Tbilisi, once in Palermo. Both times, afterward, someone who had been planning to publish vanished from academia. Not dead — just gone. Teaching high school in another country. Deciding they'd rather grow tomatoes. People who meet Voss tend to develop sudden, comprehensive disinterest in their life's work."

Dominic was quiet for a moment. Outside, the fan turned its tired circles.

"They went through my apartment last night," he said. Flatly, the way you report weather. "While I was out. They didn't take the ledger — I had it with me — but they took everything else. My laptop. My mother's letters. Photographs. They left the furniture, which frankly was an insult to the furniture."

"They weren't looking for the ledger alone," I said. "They were looking for whether you'd *read* it. Whether you understood what you had. There's a difference between stealing a thing and knowing a thing, and the Collection knows the difference. Stealing comes later. First they establish what you know, and then they decide what you're worth alive."

"That's a horrifying sentence."

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to archaeology. The digs are the fun part."

He almost smiled. It was brief and crooked and it vanished immediately, but I caught it, and I confess it did something to me — some old muscle in my face remembered that Mei-Lin smiled exactly like that, right before she demolished your entire interpretive framework at a conference panel with the gentleness of a landslide.

"Why do you care?" he asked suddenly. He leaned forward. "You said you hated her. Fifteen years. So why are you sitting in a bus depot drinking coffee that tastes like a radiator, waiting to hand yourself to her son?"

And there it was. The question I had spent six hours on a plastic bench failing to answer honestly.

"Because hating her was the closest I ever came to collaborating with her," I said. "We spent fifteen years trying to prove each other wrong, which means we spent fifteen years reading each other's work closer than anyone else on earth ever has. I knew her arguments better than she did. She cited my errors from memory. At conferences we couldn't be seated near each other." I looked down at my coffee. "When she died, I found out from an obituary. Eleven months ago. And I realized I was the person on earth best positioned to understand whatever she'd been working on in secret, and I had found out about it from an obituary, and that — that was unbearable. Not grief exactly. Something adjacent to grief. The feeling of a book ending mid-sentence."

He stared at me.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in weeks," he said finally, "and it's also completely insane."

"I'm told those often travel together."

"My mother never mentioned you."

"She wouldn't have." I shrugged. "Mentioning me would have required thinking about me, and thinking about me was a cost she avoided wherever possible. Don't take it personally. She avoided thinking about lots of costs. I hear you were one of them."

The words were out before I could weigh them, and I watched them land — watched his face close like a door in winter — and I thought, *Ines, you absolute disaster, thirty years in the field and you still dig where you shouldn't.*

"I'm sorry," I said. "That was cruel, and it wasn't mine to say. I only knew her as a colleague. Whatever she was to you is none of my business, and I apologize."

He was quiet for a long time. The departures board flickered through its cycle: KELOWNA, DELAYED. SPOKANE, ON TIME. A pigeon walked through the café with the confidence of management.

"She chose the work," he said at last, to the table. "Everyone keeps dancing around it. You, the lawyers handling her estate, the one nice journalist who wrote the obituary. She chose the work. I got postcards. Coordinates, sometimes. Never a phone call that lasted more than four minutes." He looked up. "So yes. Fine. It's true. Say it plainly, everyone else does."

"It is true," I said, "and it's also incomplete, and I say that as her least qualified biographer. Mei-Lin Zhou was the most complete person I ever met and the worst at being known. Those aren't contradictions until you love her. Then they're just a wound." I drained the coffee and grimaced. "Anyway. I'm not here to heal you. I'm here because you have half a ledger written in a language that officially never existed, and a cipher key that lives partly in my memory, and a man named Voss who will follow you to the ends of the earth and then, upon arriving, bill the earth for expenses."

* * *

He didn't say yes at the café. He said yes forty minutes later, on the bench by the departures board, after walking away from me twice and coming back both times, which I respected enormously. A man who walks away and comes back is a man who checks his own leash.

"The cipher," he said. "Prove it. Right now. One line."

I almost laughed. "You don't have the notes on you."

"I have one page memorized." He closed his eyes, and recited — haltingly, in transliteration, a string of characters that meant nothing to a stranger and everything to me. It was from the chest, he said. A marginal note beside the ledger's opening entry.

I listened, and my skin prickled, because I heard it. Under the surface grammar, the false script, the beautiful junk characters Mei-Lin had invented to confuse casual readers — underneath all of it ran the real structure, and the real structure was keyed to a joke.

"Tarim, 2009," I said. "Tell me I'm right. Tell me the substitution grid is keyed to the incident."

"What incident?"

"The night she convinced the entire dig crew that the pottery sherds we were finding were Byzantine, and let them catalogue two hundred fragments wrong for nine days, purely to demonstrate that confirmation bias is a site formation process. The Great Sherd Hoax. She numbered the fake sequence herself. Grid coordinates, dates, sherd counts — the whole fictional stratigraphy. If she built a cipher, she'd key it to that, because it was the funniest thing she ever did and she never let anyone forget it, especially me, since I was the one who published the correction and looked like an idiot doing it."

Dominic opened his eyes. For the first time since I'd approached him, he looked at me without weighing me.

"The numbers in the margin," he said slowly. "I thought they were measurements."

"They're sherd counts. From a hoax. Your mother hid her life's work inside the punchline of a practical joke she played on me fifteen years ago." I laughed — actually laughed, there in the depot, and a pigeon startled off the counter. "Oh, that is *exactly* like her. That is so exactly like her that I need to sit down, and I am already sitting down."

"So you can read it."

"I can read it," I said. "Given time, paper, and a strong opinion about tea. Which brings us to the unpleasant part of the conversation, Dominic, so let's have it while the coffee's still bad enough to keep us angry."

I laid it out plainly, because he struck me as a man who had recently had enough things hidden from him to last a lifetime.

"Voss will not stop. You've seen the seal; he's seen the seal. The Halvard Collection has spent seventy years buying up every trace of Kharvath — documents, maps, photographs, survivors' letters — because a nation that erased itself left behind the largest unclaimed archive of the twentieth century, and the Collection intends to own it exclusively. Your mother found it first. Or found the road to it. And now you're holding her map." I held up a finger. "Here's what you need to understand about the Collection: they don't send lawyers first. Lawyers are expensive and slow. They send Voss, and Voss sends men, and the men have already demonstrated their approach to property law in your apartment. The next demonstration will be less tidy."

"So I run."

"You run *smart*. Alone, you're a courier carrying a package — easy to intercept, easy to make disappear. With me, you're a colleague of record with institutional ties, publications, a university that notices if I stop answering email. Annoying, isn't it? Respectability as armor. I spent thirty years sneering at tenure and now it may save our lives."

"And Tam?" he said, and I blinked.

"Who is Tam?"

"A ferryman. Maybe. I haven't met him yet. I've been reading about river crossings east of here and his name keeps coming up." He rubbed his face. "I've been planning since about three this morning. It's not a plan. It's more of a direction with anxiety attached."

"Then we'll build it into a plan on the bus," I said, standing, "because the 5:15 to Kelowna is delayed, which means it leaves in twelve minutes out of pure spite, and I would very much like to be on it when it does."

He looked at me for a long moment. Weighing me, one last time — the rival, the stranger, the woman who'd appeared out of nowhere claiming to hold a piece of his mother that he'd never been given.

"Why should I trust you?" he asked. It wasn't hostile. It was genuinely curious, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse.

"You shouldn't," I said. "Not yet. Trust is earned in the field, same as everything else. All I can offer today is this: I am the only person in the world who wants what you want for the reason you want it. Everyone else wants the archive. I want to know what she knew. That makes me useful and it makes me safe, because you can't bribe someone whose price is a dead woman's handwriting." I picked up my bag. "Also, I know where the good food stops are on the eastern line, and you look like a man who's been eating gas station sandwiches out of grief."

"That's — disturbingly accurate."

"I'm an archaeologist. We read strata."

He almost smiled again. Then the board flickered — KELOWNA, BOARDING — and the moment passed, and we moved toward the doors together, two strangers bound by a woman who had never introduced us and had, in her way, been introducing us for years.

At the door he stopped. "Marchetti. The cipher joke. The sherds. Did she ever admit it was funny?"

I pushed open the door into the hot late light.

"She sent me a birthday card, three years ago," I said. "No message. Just a drawing of a sherd. Numbered forty-one." I stepped out into the sun. "It took me a year to work out that forty-one was the fragment that broke the hoax. It was her way of saying she'd trusted me with the punchline." I glanced back at him. "Your mother communicated exclusively in archaeological in-jokes, Dominic. Learning to read her is going to be the hardest excavation of your life. Fortunately for you—" I nodded at the bus, at the road east, at the mountains waiting beyond it like a locked cabinet, "—I've done the survey work."

Behind us, across the parking lot, a grey sedan that had been parked since noon started its engine, unhurried, and pulled out to follow.

Neither of us saw it. But we would.
