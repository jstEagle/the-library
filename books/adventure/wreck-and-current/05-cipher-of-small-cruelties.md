## 5. Cipher of Small Cruelties

The train east was the kind of train that had been declared obsolete by three separate governments and had outlived all of them. It clattered through the dark with the confidence of something that had nothing left to prove, its carriage smelling of coal smoke, fried onions, and the particular despair of upholstery that had been sat on by forty years of tired people.

Ines Marchetti sat opposite Dominic Zhou with the ledger open between them like a patient on an operating table, and she was, despite everything, despite the ransacked apartment they'd fled twelve hours ago, despite the man named Voss who was almost certainly already making phone calls — she was happy.

She would never have admitted it out loud. But her hands knew. Her hands were doing the thing they did when she was close to a solution: moving without her, turning pages, tapping rhythms, alive.

"You're smiling," Dominic said. He had the window seat, which he'd claimed with the silent desperation of a man who had lost his home and intended to keep at least the view.

"I am not."

"You're smiling at a piece of vellum."

"It's a very well-preserved piece of vellum." She turned it toward the reading lamp. "Thirteenth-century Kharvathi binding technique. Look at the stitching — double thread, offset. Whoever made this expected it to be handled for centuries. Expected it to *matter*."

Dominic looked at the stitching. He looked at it for a while. "I see thread," he said finally.

"Yes, well." Ines allowed herself the smile now, briefly, like a cat letting a bird go to prove it could. "That's the difference between us, Zhou. Your mother used to say the same thing. *I see thread.*"

The name landed between them the way it always did — a small dropped stone in still water. Dominic turned back to the window. Outside, the dark country unspooled: a lit farmhouse, a river catching moonlight, a town that was one street of sodium lamps and then nothing.

Ines watched him not-look at her and decided, in the way of people who had spent thirty years in trenches and libraries where patience was the only tool that worked, to wait him out.

It took eleven minutes.

"She documented everything," he said, to the window. "That's the thing I keep — everything. There are forty-one field notebooks in that storage unit. Forty-one. She recorded soil strata like other people record birthdays. She has a note about the migratory patterns of a specific goat. But there's not one — " his jaw worked — "there's not one entry that says *my son lost his first tooth today* or *my son is afraid of the dark* or *my son exists.*"

"No," Ines said.

"You knew her. You must have — "

"I knew her in the field. Which is to say, I knew her at her most fluent." Ines pulled the ledger back toward her. "But I'll tell you what I know, since we're apparently going to spend the whole night in this carriage and you've already asked the conductor twice if the café car is open. It is not. I asked first."

Dominic's ears went slightly pink. "Go on, then."

"Your mother kept two sets of books. Every archaeologist does — one for the record, one for the truth. The record was immaculate. The truth she hid, and she hid it well, and she hid it in places like this." Ines tapped the cipher column that marched down the ledger's margin: dense rows of characters that looked like a language having a nervous breakdown. "Kharvathi script, but wrong. Deliberately wrong. She's using it as a substitution system, but the substitutions aren't arbitrary. They're keyed to something."

"To what?"

"To a memory." Ines leaned back. "That's how she always built her ciphers. Anyone can memorize a key table. But a memory — a *shared* memory — that's a key that walks around with you. No one can confiscate it."

"And you shared one."

"We shared several." Ines said it lightly, but her finger had gone still on the page. "We were rivals, Zhou, not friends. For fifteen years we competed for the same permits, the same funding, the same chairs at the same insufferable conferences. She beat me to the Vellor strata in '09 and I have never fully forgiven her. But you spend enough years fighting someone, you end up knowing them better than their friends do. Friends are polite. Rivals pay attention."

The train rocked through a curve. Somewhere down the carriage, a child was asleep against a mother's shoulder, and Dominic looked at them for exactly as long as it took to make himself look away.

"Tell me about the memories," he said.

* * *

The first one failed.

Ines tried the field school at Karst Hollow — the summer of 2003, when a flash flood had stranded the whole excavation team in a barn for four days and Mei-Lin had organized the rations with terrifying precision, and someone had hidden the last of the coffee and Mei-Lin had conducted, in Ines's words, "a tribunal that would have shamed the Hague" until the culprit confessed. They used the number of people in the barn. They used the number of days. They used the number of confiscated coffee tins.

The cipher column refused to resolve. The characters sat on the page, smug.

"Maybe she changed the system," Dominic offered.

"Mei-Lin Zhou did not change systems. She *deepened* them." Ines rubbed her eyes. "It's later. It has to be later. Something after we stopped speaking."

"When did you stop speaking?"

"2016. The Serrano preliminary survey. She filed a report that contradicted mine, and she was right, and I took it badly, and I said something at the conference dinner that I have regretted with a professionalism I can only describe as total." Ines's mouth twisted. "We didn't speak again. She sent me a card when I won the Aldringham Prize. I still have it. I never answered it."

"What did it say?"

"*Ines — you earned it. M.*" Ines looked out the window. "Three words. That was Mei-Lin. She could write ten thousand words on ceramic fragment typology and three words to a person."

Dominic was quiet for a moment. "She sent me a postcard from every dig," he said. "Every single one. Never a letter. A postcard. Usually of a landscape. Usually with one line." He pulled out his phone, hesitated over it — the screen was a liability now, Voss's people would be monitoring — but there were photos saved offline, and he thumbed to one. A postcard of a salt flat, blinding white. His mother's handwriting: *The ground here remembers water. — M.*

"That's it," Ines said. "That's the whole message?"

"Sometimes two lines. Once she sent one that just said *The goats here are liars.* I still don't know what that meant."

Ines laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly strangled, but real. "Oh, I know what that meant. The Serrano foothill goats. They'd get into the survey trenches and move the marker flags. She became convinced they were doing it deliberately. She wrote a formal incident report about it. Two pages. She cited precedent."

Dominic stared at her. And then, despite everything — the ransacked apartment, the dead mother, the bronze chest sitting wrapped in a tarp in the luggage rack above them like a sleeping animal — he laughed too. It came out of him rusty and surprised, and it went on a beat longer than he expected, and when it stopped the carriage felt less like a fleeing vehicle and more like a room.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So what memory are we using?"

Ines had gone still again. Her finger was resting on the cipher column, and her eyes had that far-away, listening look that Dominic was beginning to understand meant she was three moves ahead.

"The card," she said slowly. "She sent me a card in 2016, after the fight. Not the prize card. Before. A week after the conference. I threw it in a drawer and told myself I'd burn it." She was speaking faster now. "It was a postcard. Of a salt flat — no. No, of a *river.* A flooded river. And it said — "

She stopped. Dominic watched something move across her face that he recognized, horribly, from his own mirror: the look of a person finding a letter they'd never opened.

"It said *Some currents only look like they're against you,*" Ines recited. "And then her initials. And I thought it was an apology, and I was too proud to take it, so I filed it under *later.*" She looked up. "Zhou. What postcard did she send you? The last one. The one from the final dig."

Dominic's throat had gone tight. He scrolled. There it was. The last postcard, arrived eleven days before the news. A photograph of a mountain valley, gray-green under cloud. One line in his mother's small, slanted, infuriatingly steady hand:

*The river keeps its books. So do I. — M.*

"Same phrase," Ines breathed. "She sent us both the same phrase. She was keying us. She was — " and now her hands were flying, pulling the ledger close, flipping to the cipher column, pulling a pencil from behind her ear — "she built the key *into the correspondence.* The phrase is the seed. *The river keeps its books.* Count the letters. Assign the values. Zhou, give me the exact postcard, word for word, punctuation and all, she'd have used the punctuation, she was a *maniac* about punctuation — "

Dominic read it to her twice. Ines wrote it out in block letters across the back of a train timetable, numbered the letters, crossed out the repeats, and began building the substitution table in a hand so fast it barely qualified as writing.

And Dominic sat back and watched his mother's rival — a woman who had spent fifteen years competing with her and one decade not speaking to her — read her handwriting more fluently than her own son could.

"She hid the key in plain sight," he said. "In the one thing she actually — " he almost said *sent me* — "in the mail."

"She hid it in the only channel the Collection would never watch." Ines didn't look up. "A mother's postcard. Boring. Sentimental. beneath suspicion. God, she was good." There was real admiration in it, and under the admiration, something rawer that she didn't name. "First character. The substitution should resolve as — "

The pencil stopped.

"What?" Dominic leaned over. "What is it?"

Ines turned the ledger toward him. The first line of the cipher column had resolved. It was no longer a nervous breakdown of Kharvathi script. It was words. His mother's words, in her own transliteration system, in a language he could read.

It said: *If you are reading this, one of you finally answered the postcards.*

* * *

They worked until three in the morning, and the train carried them east through sleeping country, and the ledger gave itself up line by line.

It was slow, intimate work. Ines read the resolved text aloud in a low voice, translating Mei-Lin's shorthand as she went — the field abbreviations, the private symbols, the little arrows that meant *see my other notebook, the one I burned.* Dominic held the postcards. All of them. He'd carried them out of the apartment in a plastic sleeve inside his jacket, along with the ledger and nothing else, because when the choice came down to what to save in ninety seconds, he had discovered what his hands already knew.

The ledger's first section was a map key — landmarks, distances, a route into the Serrano Range rendered in the careful, paranoid language of someone who expected the reader to be pursued. But between the coordinates, Mei-Lin had left notes. Working notes. The kind that were never meant to be read, which is to say, the kind that told the truth.

"*The pass at Sorrel Gap is passable until first snow. After that, do not attempt it, no matter what the second half of the ledger suggests. I have watched men argue with that mountain. The mountain has a perfect record.*"

"*Note on local dialect: the word translated as 'archive' also means 'seed bank' and 'apology.' This is not a coincidence and I do not have time to explain why.*"

"*Day 40. The porter's dog has adopted me. I have named him Colleague. He is the only member of this expedition who does not want anything from me.*"

Dominic read that one twice. "She had a dog."

"For one dig, apparently." Ines's voice was gentle in a way that cost her something; he could hear the cost. "She named it Colleague."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"It's the saddest thing I've ever translated, and I once worked on a text that was mostly a man apologizing to his ox." Ines turned the page. Then she stopped.

The next note was longer. Her pencil hovered over it.

"What?" Dominic said.

"Nothing. It's — it's a supply note. It's fine." But her voice had changed registers, the way it did when she was deciding whether to show him something, and he was learning to hear that too.

"Read it," he said. "Whatever it is. I've spent thirty years not reading things she wrote. I'm done with that."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she read.

"*Supply note: the cache at the col must hold for five years minimum. If no one comes in five years, it means I failed, and the contents should rot with my reputation, which will by then be the only part of me anyone is willing to dig up.*"

She paused.

"*Addition, later, same day: I have packed a second item in the cache that is not on any inventory. If the person who finds this is who I hope it is, they will know why it's there. If it is anyone else — burn it.*"

The train rocked. The child down the carriage murmured in its sleep.

"What's the second item?" Dominic asked.

"The cache is in the mountains," Ines said. "We won't know until we're standing in front of it." She said it evenly, but her pencil had stopped moving, and Dominic understood — with the strange new fluency this night was giving him in the language of Ines Marchetti — that she had a guess, and that the guess had frightened her.

He looked down at the ledger, at his mother's resolved handwriting, and felt the old ache shift into a new shape. She had documented everything. Soil strata. Goats. Ceramic typology. The migratory habits of a dog named Colleague. She had documented everything except the one subject that mattered, and now he was reading her in translation, through a rival, on a night train, and the closest thing to a letter she had ever written him was buried in a supply cache at the top of a mountain that had a perfect record.

"She knew someone would come," he said. "She built the whole thing so someone would come."

"She built it so *you* would come," Ines said. "She just couldn't say so. That was the cruelty of her. Not the big cruelties — the small ones. The unsent card. The unanswered prize. The postcards that were one line long." Ines closed her eyes briefly. "She loved in cipher, Zhou. And the worst part — the part I understand now, sitting here — is that she encrypted it because she thought that was the safest place for it. She thought if she hid it well enough, no one could take it from you."

"Did anyone ever crack it?"

Ines looked at him across the ledger, across the lamp light and the coal smoke and the dark rushing country.

"You're reading it, aren't you?" she said.

Dominic didn't answer. He couldn't. He turned back to the window, and it was a long time before either of them spoke again.

* * *

He dozed near dawn, and woke to Ines swearing at the ledger in three languages.

"What is it?" He was on his feet before he was fully awake, some new instinct firing. "Is it Voss? Did you — "

"It's the route." Ines's face in the gray light was grim, and there was something else in it, something he'd never seen on her: reluctance. "The first section maps the approach. Sorrel Gap, the dry valley, the cairn — it's all here, it's all legible now. But Zhou, the ledger is in two halves. We have the *geography.*" She turned the page and showed him the last resolved line, and the pencil-marks where the cipher simply stopped, mid-sentence, the characters dissolving back into noise.

The line read: *The door requires both halves. The second half is not with me. I have sent it —*

And then nothing.

"Sent it where?" Dominic said.

"That's what I've been swearing about for the last twenty minutes." Ines dropped the pencil and pressed both hands flat on the table, and when she looked up at him, her expression had gone from reluctant to afraid. "Zhou — the sentence doesn't end. It's not damaged. It's not smudged. She stopped writing. Which means she finished the thought somewhere else." Ines tapped the last line twice. "Your mother didn't lose the second half of this ledger. She *mailed* it."

Outside, the sky was going from black to iron, and somewhere behind them — two days back, maybe less now — Dominic understood that a man named Voss was also awake, and also moving, and also reading the same silence at the end of the same sentence.

"She mailed it," Dominic repeated. "Before she died."

"Yes."

"Then there's a record. A postal record. A *trail.*"

Ines was already pulling out the timetable, already flipping to the map printed on its back, her finger moving west — back the way they'd come, back toward the coast, back toward everything they had just spent two days fleeing.

"Her last dig was on the coast," she said quietly. "Zhou, I think we just got on the wrong train."
