## 6. Kharvath, Erased

The border town was called Vetchka, which Ines Marchetti considered a name that had given up on itself halfway through. It sat in a fold of hills like something the mountains had coughed up and then pretended not to notice: one paved street, three cafés of identical despair, and a regional archive housed in what had clearly once been a granary, because history in these parts was stored wherever the rats had finished first.

"Charming," Tam said, looking up at it.

"It has a roof," Ines said. "That puts it ahead of most archives I've loved."

Dominic said nothing. He'd said very little since the night train, since Ines had cracked the first layer of his mother's cipher using nothing but an old grudge and a memory of Mei-Lin Zhou laughing at her over a broken total station in 2009. He carried the ledger half against his chest now, inside his jacket, like a man carrying either a child or a bomb. Possibly both.

Ines pushed open the archive door and was greeted by the smell she associated with all her happiest decades: dust, mildew, and the faint vinegar ghost of degrading microfilm. A bell above the door gave a single apologetic clank.

Behind a counter stacked with unopened crates sat a man of about seventy, reading a newspaper from 2011. He lowered it with the unhurried gravity of someone who had learned that urgency was for other people's emergencies.

"We're closed," he said.

"It's two in the afternoon," Ines said.

"Yes," he agreed. "We're closed."

She set her credentials on the counter — the real ones, the ones with the university letterhead she'd stopped being allowed to use after the Tashkent affair, but which still opened certain doors if you held them at the correct angle — and said, "Doctor Ines Marchetti. I'm conducting provenance research on behalf of the Kestrel Bend Historical Society."

It was a lie so small and so boring that it slid under the door of his suspicion without disturbing the dust.

He looked at the credentials for a long time. Then at her. Then, with visible reluctance, at Dominic and Tam, who were doing their best to look like research assistants, which is to say Dominic looked like a man being slowly digested by grief and Tam looked like a smuggler who had wandered into the wrong building and couldn't find the exit.

"They're with me," Ines said.

"Everyone is with someone," the archivist said, which Ines would later decide was the most profound thing anyone said to her on the entire expedition. He stood, joints announcing themselves like a slow drumroll, and led them back into the stacks.

* * *

The map room was at the rear of the building, a long low chamber where the light came through high slit windows and fell in dusty bars across steel cabinets. The archivist — his name, extracted eventually, was Pavel, delivered as though it cost him money — pulled drawer after drawer with the tenderness of a man handling sleeping animals.

"You want maps," he said. "Everyone wants maps. Nobody wants the index cards. The index cards are where the truth lives. Maps are just the truth wearing its good clothes."

Ines liked him immediately and completely.

"What we're looking for," she said carefully, "is a country. Or rather, the absence of one."

Pavel stopped mid-drawer. He turned around slowly, and Ines watched something move behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been waiting forty years for someone to ask the question and is annoyed that it took this long.

"Ah," he said. "You're one of those."

"One of what?"

"People who ask about Kharvath." He said the name quietly, the way you say the name of a dead person in a house where the family can still hear you. He wiped his hands on his cardigan, though they were clean. "Sit down. This takes longer standing up."

They sat on wooden chairs that had been designed by someone who hated scholars. Pavel lowered himself onto a stool with the groan of a ship changing course.

"In 1953," he began, "there was a purge. Not here — further east, but the paperwork came through this region because the borders were being redrawn. There was a nation called Kharvath. Small. Mountainous. Annoyingly well-educated. They had universities when our valleys had goats, and I say that with love, because my grandmother could read in three scripts and she was Kharvathi on her mother's side."

Tam shifted in his chair. Ines noticed but didn't look at him directly. She was learning to read Tam the way you read weather — peripherally, or you got nothing.

"The purge wasn't about politics," Pavel continued. "Or it was, officially, but really it was about pride. Kharvath refused to hand over its scholars. Refused to let its archives be moved to the capital. So the state did what states do when embarrassed by a small clever neighbor." He drew a line in the dust on the table with one finger. "It erased them. Not the people — well. Also the people. But administratively. Borders redrawn, names reassigned, every map struck. Within five years, official cartography showed nothing there but 'unincorporated highland territory.' As if a country could be un-invented by a clerk with a good eraser."

"And the records?" Dominic asked. His voice was rough. He hadn't spoken since Vetchka's single café, where he'd drunk tea he clearly hadn't tasted.

"That," Pavel said, "is the interesting part. Come."

* * *

He led them deeper into the stacks, past cabinets labeled in fading ink, to a section that seemed almost forgotten — drawers marked with a symbol Ines didn't recognize at first, and then did, and felt the floor tilt slightly under her professional feet.

The seal. The same seal stamped into the bronze chest Dominic had hauled out of the Kestrel Bend river. A mountain split by a river, crowned with seven stars.

"This drawer," Pavel said, "was transferred to us in 1961 by a ministry clerk who was supposed to destroy it. Instead he drove all night and left it on our step with a note saying 'for whoever asks.' Nobody asked for fifty years." He smiled without warmth. "Bureaucrats make excellent cowards. Cowards make excellent preservers. The two professions overlap more than people think."

He drew out folders. Ines leaned in, and there it was — the paper corpse of a nation. Survey maps of the Serrano Range with place-names in a script she'd only ever seen in fragments. Census abstracts. University charters. And photographs — grainy, silver-fading photographs of buildings carved into mountain faces, of scholars posed stiffly before shelves of sealed cylinders, of a library entrance built into living rock behind a cairn of black stones.

Dominic made a sound. Small. Ines glanced over and saw him holding one photograph at arm's length, as though it might bite, and she understood: he was looking at his mother's seal. His mother's obsession. The thing that had eaten the years that should have belonged to him, laid out now in gray-and-silver evidence.

"She knew," he said quietly. "All of this. She knew."

"Dr. Zhou," Pavel said, and both Ines and Dominic went still. "Yes. She was here. Four years ago. Maybe five."

Ines's stomach dropped. "Mei-Lin Zhou came to this archive?"

"Oh, yes." Pavel's face softened into something almost fond. "Difficult woman. Brilliant. She sat where you're sitting and read every folder in this drawer for eleven days. Brought her own gloves. Corrected my cataloguing twice, and she was right both times, which I found unforgivable and delightful." He paused. "She asked me the same question you just asked. Whether anyone else had come asking about Kharvath."

"And what did you tell her?"

"I told her no." Pavel met Ines's eyes. "And then, after she left, a man came asking. Very polite. Very expensive coat. He also wanted to know who had been asking about Kharvath. I told him nobody." He shrugged, a small dry movement. "I am also an excellent coward."

Ines felt the cold arrive in her hands before it arrived anywhere else. "Describe him."

"Tall. Gray at the temples. Speaks softly, like the volume costs him money too. Had assistants who stood like furniture." Pavel studied her. "Why? Do you know him?"

"We've met," Ines said. "Briefly. At a bus depot."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the archive became what archives always became for Ines: a crime scene. That was the phrase she used in her own head, and she used it deliberately, because it was true. Every folder was testimony. Every struck-through place-name was a wound dressed in administrative gauze. You could read the erasure like forensics — where the ink had been scraped, where new borders had been drawn over old ones with the confidence of a forger, where whole districts had been renamed with names so bland they were practically confessions. *New Hope Valley.* *Prosperity Ridge.* Nobody names a stolen valley Prosperity Ridge unless they need to believe their own story.

"Look at this," Tam said suddenly. He'd gone quiet hours ago, drifting along the edges of the work the way he drifted along everything, but now he was holding a census page, and his voice had changed. Ines had heard that change once before, on the ferry, when he talked about his grandmother. "This district. Sereth Hollow. My grandmother's stories — she called it *Seret'kha*. The singing valley. She said the women there sang the harvest counts so the numbers couldn't be falsified." His thumb moved over the column of names. "Here they are. Written down. Somebody counted them anyway."

For a moment nobody spoke. The dust turned in the window light.

"They hid everything," Dominic said. He was standing over the survey maps, both hands flat on the table, and when he looked up his exhaustion had burned off into something harder. "The purge comes, the maps get erased, and instead of running — some of them ran, sure, Tam's grandmother — but some of them stayed and packed their entire civilization into the mountain. Seeds. Charts. Recordings. Insurance against being deleted." He tapped the photograph of the cairn. "They didn't build a treasure vault. They built a witness."

"Your mother figured that out," Ines said.

"My mother figured that out and told no one. Not even—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Not even me."

Ines let that sit. There was nothing useful to add, and she had learned — mostly late, mostly expensively — that grief is not a door you help people through. It's a room they walk out of on their own schedule.

What she said instead was: "Pavel. The cipher. Mei-Lin spent eleven days here. Did she copy anything? Leave notes? Work with anything beyond the official record?"

Pavel's eyebrows rose. "There was one item not in the official record. A private deposit. A Kharvathi merchant family, the Vessarin, kept papers for generations — letters, a partial key to the old scholarly cipher. The last descendant deposited them here in the seventies and died without children. Dr. Zhou read them. She asked to photograph several pages." He hesitated, and Ines watched a lifetime of archival discretion war briefly with something lonelier — the desire, after fifty years, to finally tell somebody. "She also left something. A condition of her access. She wrote out a translation summary and deposited it, sealed, with instructions that it be shown only to someone who could answer a question."

"What question?" Dominic asked.

Pavel produced a small envelope, yellowed at the edges, and laid it on the table between them. On the front, in Mei-Lin Zhou's handwriting — and Ines felt that handwriting like a slap, because she'd spent thirty years competing with it, reviewing it, envying it — was written:

*To whoever comes next. Answer first.*

Inside was a card. One line, in English, in the same hand:

*What did I break, and why did I never fix it?*

Dominic stared at it. Ines watched him and understood that he did know the answer — that the answer lived in him like shrapnel — and that saying it aloud would cost him something he'd already paid too much of.

"She broke it with me," he said finally, flatly, to the card rather than to any of them. "She chose the digs. The field seasons. The cipher and the seals and the range. She missed birthdays, graduations, funerals — my father's funeral, she sent a telegram, a *telegram*, like it was 1940. And she never fixed it. Never came home and stayed. Never even tried, near the end, when there was time." His jaw worked. "Because fixing it would have meant admitting the choice was a choice, and not a calling. And she needed it to be a calling."

The room was silent. Pavel had the grace to study his cataloguing.

Ines thought about saying something kind and decided, in the end, on something true. "I reviewed her grant applications for fifteen years, Dominic. She listed next of kin on every single one. Do you know what she wrote?"

He looked at her.

"'Son. Custodian.' Nothing else. No address, no details. Just — custodian." Ines shrugged. "I thought it was bureaucratic laziness. Now I think she was filing paperwork for a job she intended you to have, and couldn't explain."

Dominic picked up the card, folded it, and put it inside his jacket with the ledger. Two documents now against his chest. Child and bomb.

"Show us the Vessarin pages," he said.

* * *

They worked until the light died. The Vessarin key unlocked the second layer of Mei-Lin's cipher the way a specific tooth unlocks a specific lock — Ines felt the click all the way down her arms. Coordinates refined. Landmarks confirmed: the split rock, the dry riverbed, the cairn of black stones, all matching the 1950s surveys. And beneath the coordinates, in Mei-Lin's compressed notation, a warning that made Ines's blood run cool:

*Approach from the south only. Northern channel is engineered. Do not trust the mountain to hold its shape.*

Engineered. Meaning the avalanche channels weren't geology. They were architecture.

"She knew about the collapse protocol," Ines murmured.

"Of course she did," Dominic said. "She knew everything except how to say goodbye."

It was full dark when Pavel locked up behind them, pressing a spare key into Ines's palm with the conspiratorial air of a man committing his first crime at seventy. "Come back tomorrow. There's a second drawer. The clerk hid it better." Then, as they turned to go: "The man in the expensive coat. He came back last week. With four others. They asked about you specifically this time — described a woman, a young man, a boatman." He smiled thinly. "I told them I'd seen no one. But I am old, and my lies are slower than they used to be. Whatever you intend to do in the mountains, Doctor Marchetti — do it soon."

Ines stood in the cold street of Vetchka, under stars that had watched a country get erased and hadn't filed a complaint, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the bus depot.

Voss had reached the drowned mercenary's contacts. That meant roads, radios, fuel, money. Two days, maybe less, and the passes were already breathing snow.

"Dominic," she said. "How do you feel about leaving tonight?"

He looked up at the dark shoulder of the Serranos, where his mother's whole secret life was buried in the ice, and Ines saw the answer arrive before he spoke it.

"I'll get the rope," he said.
