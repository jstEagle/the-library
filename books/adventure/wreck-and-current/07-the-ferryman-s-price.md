## 7. The Ferryman's Price

The frontier river had no name on any map we could buy, which Ines said was either a bureaucratic oversight or a very old act of spite. It ran wide and brown and muscular through a valley of scrub pine, and on its far bank, forty kilometers of no-man's-land stood between us and the Serrano foothills.

"You can't swim that," Ines said, standing at the top of the muddy cut where the road gave up and became a goat track to the water. She said it the way she said most things — as though she were grading my homework.

"I wasn't going to swim it."

"You were thinking about it. You have the face of a man doing arithmetic about rivers."

I did have the face of a man doing arithmetic about rivers. The current was moving at what looked like the speed of a delivery truck, carrying whole trees sideways like they were toothpicks, and every few minutes something large and dead would surface, roll, and vanish again, just to keep morale low.

"So we find a boat," I said.

"Boats cost money."

"Everything costs money. That's the one lesson my mother's estate taught me thoroughly."

We walked downriver along the bank for an hour before we found the boat — or rather, before the boat found us, in the sense that a man sitting inside it watched us approach with the expression of someone who had been expecting worse company.

He was maybe fifty, built like a barrel that had been rolled downhill a few thousand times, with a gray beard trimmed short and hands the size of dinner plates resting on an oar that was more suggestion than oar. His boat was flat-bottomed, patched in six places, and sat in the eddy behind a rock shelf like it had grown there.

"Crossing?" he asked, in accented but functional trade-tongue.

"That depends," Ines said, sliding instantly into her negotiating voice, which was her normal voice with the warmth surgically removed. "What does it cost?"

"It depends," he echoed, "on what you're carrying."

"Two people, two packs."

"Nobody crosses this river with two packs anymore." He spat thoughtfully over the side. "Last month a man crossed with four packs and three of them were other men's boots. Before that, soldiers. Before that, nobody, because everyone with sense moved away from the border when the shooting started in the spring." He looked us over with eyes the color of river water. "You don't have soldier faces. You have *student* faces. Student faces are worse. Student faces mean somebody is paying you, or chasing you, or both."

"We're archaeologists," Ines said.

"Ah." He nodded slowly, the way you'd nod at someone who'd just told you their dog was a wolf. "Archaeologists. And what are you digging for, out here, where there is nothing but rocks and my cousin's goats?"

"Old things," I said.

That got his attention. Not much of it — a flicker, like a fish turning under the surface — but I'd spent enough weeks now watching for exactly that flicker to know I'd seen it.

"Old things," he repeated. "Get in. Price is whatever's in your left pocket, plus a question answered honestly."

Ines opened her mouth to negotiate. I put a hand on her arm, because I'd learned by then that when a smuggler offers you a deal with a strange shape to it, the strange shape is usually the part that matters. I emptied my left pocket onto the boat's boards: some crumpled notes, a bus token from a city two countries back, and — because I kept forgetting to move it — the rubbing I'd taken of the bronze chest's seal, folded into eighths until it was soft as cloth.

The ferryman went still.

Not dramatically. He didn't drop the oar or make a sound. He simply stopped being a bored old man in a leaky boat and became something else, something coiled and attentive, the way a dog goes still before deciding whether the stranger at the gate is family or meat.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get that?"

"A chest came out of a flooded river in Kestrel Bend," I said. "Bronze. That seal was stamped on the lid. Three times."

"How long ago?"

"Six weeks."

"And who else has seen it?"

"Half the dredging crew. A coroner's clerk. A man named Voss sent people to ask polite questions, and then less polite ones."

The ferryman closed his eyes for a moment, and I swear under his breath he said something that wasn't a curse so much as a lament — the tone you'd use for news of a death. Then he opened his eyes and pushed off from the rock shelf with the oar, smooth as if we'd already settled everything.

"Hey," Ines said sharply. "We haven't agreed on—"

"You agreed when you showed me the seal," he said. "Sit down, both of you, low, and don't stand up in my boat unless you want to learn to swim in a river that eats trees."

* * *

His name, he told us once we were mid-river and committed, was Tam. He steered with the oar and occasional nudges of a pole, reading the current the way Ines read strata, and he talked the way lonely men talk when they've decided you might be worth talking to — in bursts, with long silences between, each burst containing more than it appeared to.

"My grandmother crossed this river," he said, apropos of nothing, twenty minutes in. "Nineteen fifty-four. She was seventeen. She carried her mother's recipe book and nothing else, because everything else belonged to a country that had stopped being allowed to exist."

"Kharvath," I said.

Tam didn't look at me. He watched the water. "You say that word like it's safe to say. It isn't, everywhere. On this bank it's fine. On the far bank there are villages where men still get visited for saying it." He swung the pole, corrected our line past a submerged rock that I never saw at all. "My grandmother used to sing songs about it. Lullabies. Half of them were lullabies and half of them were maps — she swore later that she hadn't known that when she was small, that the old women had hidden directions inside children's songs so the children would carry them out of the country without knowing they were carrying anything at all." He snorted. "She found out when she was sixty and tried to follow one and ended up walking three days in circles above the tree line. So. Songs as maps. Maps as songs. And now two student-faced archaeologists show up at my river with the seal of Kharvath folded in a pocket like a bus ticket."

"What do you know about the seal?" Ines asked.

"I know my grandmother kissed a drawing of it once, on the anniversary of the crossing, every year, until she died. I know she said it was the mark of the *keepers* — not kings, not priests. Keepers. People whose whole job was remembering." He finally turned and looked at me, and his eyes weren't river-colored anymore; they were flint. "And I know that when a thing with that seal surfaces after seventy years, men die. My grandmother told me that too. She said: *when the country comes back for its memory, get out of the water.*"

"Then why are you taking us across?" I asked.

"Because you're already in the water." He said it simply, like reporting the weather. "Both of you. You showed me the seal, which means someone showed you, which means the current has you. I could put you back on that bank and the current would still have you. At least this way you drown on my schedule."

"Genuinely comforting," Ines muttered.

"I'm not paid to comfort. I'm paid—" He paused. "Actually, I haven't been paid yet. Left pocket, you said. That was a sad little pile of money, friend."

"That was the whole left pocket."

"Then we renegotiate on the far bank." He grinned for the first time, and the grin transformed him from a boulder into a rogue uncle. "I want three things. One: fair passage fee, doubled, because I'm about to become a man who ferried Kharvathi business, and that changes my insurance situation considerably. Two: honest answers, whenever I ask, for as long as we travel together — because I'm coming with you."

Ines and I spoke at the same time.

"*Coming with us?*"

"You have a boat," I pointed out. "Why do you need ours?"

"I don't need your boat. I need your reason." He planted the pole and held us steady against the current, muscles moving under his shirt like rope under canvas. "Seventy years my family has lived on this river, ferrying smugglers and soldiers and once, God help me, a circus bear, and never once has anything come out of that water connected to my grandmother's country. Now it has. And it surfaced in *your* hands, of all the hands in the world. My grandmother believed in signs. I believe in signs too — I just charge for mine." He ticked the pole against the hull. "Three: when this is finished, whatever you found, whoever you were running from — you tell me all of it. Every piece. Because if my grandmother's country left something behind, my family has a claim on knowing, even if we have no claim on keeping. Do we have terms?"

I looked at Ines. Ines looked at me, and her expression said clearly: *this is a terrible idea, we know nothing about this man, he could be working for Voss.* But underneath that, in smaller print, her expression also said: *he knew the word keepers, and he knew the songs-were-maps trick, and Voss's men wouldn't know those things.*

"Who else have you told?" she asked Tam. "About us. About the seal."

"No one. Yet."

"Why not yet?"

"Because 'yet' hasn't happened. If you refuse my terms, 'yet' becomes 'immediately,' and I sell the story to the garrison commander, who pays poorly but pays in things that keep a man's grandchildren alive." He shrugged, enormous and unbothered. "I'm not a good man. I'm a ferryman. Good men sink; ferryman float. Which is it to be?"

* * *

We landed on the far bank in a narrow stony cove that Tam clearly knew down to the individual rocks, and while Ines rewrapped her boots with fresh cloth strips — the altitude was already starting, she claimed, though we were barely out of the river valley — Tam beached the boat and began unloading supplies I hadn't seen him load: a coil of rope, a canvas bundle that clinked, a rifle wrapped in oilcloth older than I was.

"You planned this," I said.

"I planned *a* crossing. Then you showed me the seal and the plan changed." He cinched the bundle to his back with the ease of a man dressing in his own clothes. "There's a trail up-valley that avoids the checkpoint at the bridge. Two days' walk to the foothills, three if the weather turns. I know where the dry camps are. I know which villages will feed strangers and which will feed them to the garrison instead." He looked at us both. "You have a map that says where to go. I have a country that says how. These are different documents."

"The ledger doesn't mention a country," Ines said carefully. "It mentions landmarks."

"Ledgers never do. Grandmothers do." Tam shouldered his pack. "Come. We walk while the light's good, and I'll tell you the song my grandmother sang, the one with the river in it, and you can tell me whether your landmarks match. And tonight, over the fire, you answer questions. Honest ones. Starting with: what exactly did the drowned river give you, and why does a man named Voss want it badly enough to send soldiers?"

I hesitated. Every instinct honed over six weeks of being hunted said: give him nothing, trust no one, sleep in shifts. But I was tired — tired in the marrow, tired of counting allies on one hand with fingers left over — and there was something in the way Tam had said *my grandmother kissed a drawing of it*, something that sounded less like greed and more like homesickness for a place that had never existed in his lifetime.

"Fair warning," I said. "It's a long story, and it doesn't have a happy middle."

"Ferryman stories never do," Tam said. "That's how you know they're true. Happy middles are for people who stayed home." He set off up the stony bank, sure-footed as a goat, and called back over his shoulder: "Walk faster, students. The weather's turning early this year, and the mountain doesn't care whose inheritance you're carrying."

Ines fell into step beside me, and lowered her voice. "You realize," she murmured, "that we've just hired ourselves a guide who admits upfront he'll sell us out if the price is right."

"I realize," I said.

"And you're comfortable with that?"

I watched Tam ahead of us, humming something under his breath — low, minor-keyed, shaped like a lullaby and dense as a freight manifest — and I found, to my own surprise, that I meant it when I answered.

"He's the first person since Kestrel Bend who wanted something from this besides money," I said. "That makes him the most trustworthy man we've met."

Behind us, far downstream, faint on the wind, I heard it — or thought I heard it: an engine, rough and mechanical, where no road ran. Tam heard it too. He stopped humming.
