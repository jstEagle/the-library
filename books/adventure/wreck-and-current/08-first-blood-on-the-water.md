## 8. First Blood on the Water

Tam had a rule about rivers: they tell you everything if you shut up long enough to listen. The Kestrel frontier water talked all night — a low mutter under the hull, the slap of debris riding the current down from the high country, and once, near three in the morning, the particular silence that happens when something big moves upstream against the flow.

He'd been running this stretch since he was fourteen, and he knew what that silence meant. It meant engines idling somewhere in the dark with their exhaust baffled, which meant people who didn't want to be heard, which meant trouble with money behind it.

So when he woke before dawn and found Dominic Zhou already awake at the stern, wrapped in a tarp like an unhappy burrito, Tam didn't waste time on pleasantries.

"Get your things together," he said. "The quiet kind of together."

Dominic blinked at him. The man had the sleep habits of a scholar and the survival instincts of one too, which was going to get them both killed if Tam didn't manage it for them. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. That's what's wrong." Tam nodded upstream, where the mist was doing something unnatural — holding still, pooling thick around a bend where it should have been shredding itself on the current. "River's gone shy. Rivers don't go shy unless somebody's standing over them."

Ines Marchetti emerged from under the canopy with her hair doing its usual impression of a startled bird and her eyes already sharp as awls. She'd slept maybe two hours and looked like she resented every minute of it. "Voss?"

"Who else pays men to sit in cold water at four in the morning?" Tam was already moving, kicking the engine cover open, checking fuel, checking the bilge, checking the little tin box under his seat where he kept the things that mattered: his grandmother's papers, a photograph soft with handling, and forty years of bribes folded into oilcloth.

"Can we outrun them?" Dominic asked.

"Outrun, yes. Outrun *and* outlive, that depends on the rapids." Tam spat over the side, purely ceremonial, a captain's benediction. "And the rapids depend on me, so everybody do exactly what I say and nothing else. Especially you, professor. You have the face of a man who improvises."

"I don't improvise," Ines said.

"You argue with rivers. Same thing."

* * *

They cast off in the last grey hour, when the world is made of charcoal sketches and nothing has decided to be real yet. Tam ran them close to the eastern bank, where the current ran slow and the willows hung low enough to comb their hair. Behind them, the mist finally gave up its secret: a second boat, long and low, sliding out of the bend with its engine barely breathing.

Three figures in it. Maybe four. One stood at the bow with something long and angular cradled in his arms, and even at that distance Tam could read the posture — not aiming, just *presenting*. A message written in hardware.

"They're signaling us to stop," Ines said.

"They're suggesting it. Politely. With a rifle." Tam fed the engine more fuel and watched the willows start to blur. "Here's the geography lesson. Two kilometers ahead, the river drops through the Serrano gates — white water, big ones, the kind my grandmother called 'the place where God washes His dishes.' After that, the river forks. Left fork goes to the checkpoint at Marrow Ford, which Voss owns by lunchtime. Right fork goes nowhere anyone wants to go, which is exactly where we want to go."

"So we run the gates," Dominic said. His voice was steady. Tam revised his opinion of the man upward by a small but real amount.

"We run the gates," Tam agreed. "You two get down flat in the bottom of the boat. Hold onto the ring bolts. If I yell 'water,' you breathe *before* the wave, not during. If I yell 'swim,' you swim toward the sound of my voice and away from anything wearing a uniform."

"And if you yell nothing?" Ines asked.

"Then I'm dead and you should feel free to improvise."

* * *

The second boat gained on them the way bad news gains — slowly, then all at once. Tam heard the change in its engine note as the driver stopped being careful, and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding: loaded, his old ferry did eleven knots on a good day with a tailwind and favorable gossip. The pursuit boat would do twenty without breaking a sweat.

Which meant the race wasn't a race. It was a question of whether they could reach the gates before the other boat reached *them*, and the answer arrived as a crack of sound off the water — a rifle shot, high, deliberately wide, the shooter's patience wearing thin.

"Down!" Tam bellowed, and this time both of them obeyed without argument, folding themselves into the hull like letters into envelopes. He hauled the wheel hard left, cutting inside a gravel bar, trading distance for seconds, feeling the boat heel and come back with a shudder.

Another shot. This one closer. He heard it hit — a flat *tunk* somewhere aft, and then the small, wrong sound of water finding a hole it hadn't been invited to.

"We're taking on water!" Ines shouted up from the deck.

"How much?"

"Enough to matter!"

"Then we stop mattering slowly and start mattering fast." Tam grinned, because there was no other expression for what came next, and shoved the throttle to its stop. "Everybody hold on. We're going to visit God's kitchen."

* * *

The Serrano gates announced themselves the way all serious rapids do: first by sound, a deep rolling thunder that you feel in your back teeth, and then by smell — cold stone and pulverized spray — and only last by sight, because by the time you can see them properly it's too late to have opinions.

Tam had run these gates eleven times. He'd been young for six of them, lucky for four, and sober for three. He knew the line: enter left of the black fang, thread the gap between the first two holes, then commit hard right around the shoulder of the mid-channel rock, because the river wanted to eat boats on the left side and it always got its wish eventually.

Behind them, the pursuit boat was closing fast — close enough now that Tam could see faces, see the driver's set jaw, see the man at the bow raising the rifle again. And here was the thing Tam understood about that man, the thing his grandmother had taught him about all men who point guns from moving boats: they were thinking about their target instead of their river.

That was a fatal way to think. Not for Tam. For them.

"Hold on!" he roared, and drove the ferry straight at the black fang.

He heard Ines make a sound that in a less dignified woman would have been a scream. At the last possible moment he threw the wheel right, and the hull swung wide, kissing past the fang with a hand's width to spare, and the river took them.

It was like being shaken by a giant who loved you but had poor boundaries. The first hole swallowed the bow and gave it back. The second tried to keep the stern. Water came aboard in tons, green and white and personal, and Tam steered by faith and fury, reading the boils and the seams, choosing the channel that chose him back. Somewhere behind — muffled, distant, almost polite — came a different sound: the pursuit boat hitting the fang it hadn't seen, or the hole it hadn't respected. A crunch of fiberglass on granite, a shout, engines screaming free-revving as a propeller left the water forever.

Tam allowed himself half a second of professional satisfaction. Then the mid-channel shoulder rose up on his left, bigger than he remembered, meaner than the maps said, and he spent the rest of the gates paying full attention to his own funeral arrangements.

They came out the bottom in a chaos of foam, the ferry sitting low and listing, half drowned but alive, and Tam whooped — a raw, unlovely sound, the sound of a man who has just argued with a river and gotten away with a warning.

Then he saw the swimmer.

* * *

The man came out of the tailrace below the third drop, tumbling in the foam like a doll in a washing machine. One of Voss's mercenaries, thrown clear when the pursuit boat met the rock. No life vest — or a vest that had opinions of its own. He surfaced twice, thrashing, and Tam's hands were already on the wheel, already turning toward him, because that was the oldest law of the water and it lived in Tam deeper than any grudge: you go for the swimmer. You go for the swimmer even when the swimmer was shooting at you ten minutes ago. Especially then. That's what makes it a law.

"He's one of them!" Ines shouted.

"He's a man in a river!" Tam shouted back. "The river doesn't check paperwork!"

But the current had the man now, and the current below the gates was no negotiator. It took him sideways toward the sweeper — a fallen tree leaning off the bank, its branches combing the surface like fingers waiting to close. The mercenary saw it coming. Tam saw him see it, saw the animal clarity of a drowning man's last understanding.

And he saw the man's hand, above the water, held high — not reaching for help, not anymore, past all that. Clutching something flat and plastic-sleeved, holding it up out of the spray like an offering, like a man trying to save one true thing from the flood.

The sweeper took him under. The branches closed. The river kept its books.

They pulled alongside the tree, Tam hooking the boathook into the tangle while Ines and Dominic hauled, and for one bad minute it seemed they might actually get him — Tam had hold of a sleeve, Ines had the man's belt, and the dead weight of him was enormous, the whole river pulling the other way like a creditor. Then the sleeve tore, and the belt buckle gave, and the man went down and did not come up again.

What stayed with Tam, afterward, was not the man. It was the object. Because in those last seconds, as the sleeve ripped free, the plastic sleeve had come loose too, and it turned over in the foam right beside the hull, face-up, and Tam snatched it out of the water without thinking, the way you snatch anything floating, and then he wished he hadn't.

A photograph. Laminated against the water. A younger Dominic Zhou, maybe five years old, gap-toothed, sitting on a dig site crate, squinting into sun he clearly didn't trust. On the back, in a neat bureaucratic hand, a name and a number, the way you label inventory.

Dominic took it from him with wet hands. He looked at it for a long time. The rain of spray fell around them and nobody said anything, because there are moments when even Ines Marchetti keeps her mouth shut, and this was evidently one of them.

"They had a picture of me," Dominic said finally. "Of *this* me. From before."

"That means they've had your file a long time," Ines said quietly. "Longer than the chest. Longer than the ledger. They weren't following the find, Dominic. They were following *you*."

Tam looked from one to the other, and then at the wreck of his beautiful boat — holed, swamped, settling against the bank with the dignity of a retired admiral — and made the decision that had been making itself since the rifle shot.

"The boat's done," he announced. "She'll float as a planter, maybe. So here's what happens now. There's a trail along the right fork, two days' walk to a cousin of mine who owes me a favor and a truck. We walk. And along the way, one of you is going to explain to me why a rich man's soldiers carry baby pictures of my passenger."

Dominic was still looking at the photograph. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, harder, a keel laid down under the grief.

"Because she hid me from them," he said. "And it worked for thirty years. Until the river gave me up."

Tam nodded slowly, and put the ferry's little brass bell — salvaged, like everything else worth saving — into his pocket, where it clinked against his grandmother's papers.

"Well then," he said. "Best start walking. Rivers talk, friend. But mountains? Mountains remember."

* * *

They buried the mercenary at noon, in the quick and shallow way the ground allowed, marking the grave with stones because Tam's grandmother had taught him that the dead of any country deserved at least that much. Dominic insisted on it, and Tam found he couldn't argue — though he noticed the boy placed the laminated photograph, after a long hesitation, face-down on the man's chest before they covered him.

"Not because of him," Dominic said, catching Tam's look. "Because of her. She'd have wanted someone to be kind about it."

Tam picked up his pack and started up the trail, and privately thought that Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou, whoever she had been, must have been either a saint or a storm — and suspected, from the company her son kept, that she had been both.
