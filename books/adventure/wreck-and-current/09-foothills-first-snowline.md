## 9. Foothills, First Snowline

The radio in the tea house was older than the tea house, which was older than the road, and it crackled like a fire that had opinions.

"—unseasonable cold front descending on the Serrano massif," said a voice with the flat cheerfulness of someone reading someone else's bad news. "Snow levels expected to drop to two thousand meters by week's end. Travelers are advised—"

Tam reached over and switched it off with the tenderness of a man turning away from a mirror.

"Travelers are advised," he repeated, "to stay home and drink tea." He raised his glass of tea as evidence that at least one traveler had taken the advice seriously. "We are not serious travelers."

"We're the opposite of serious travelers," Dominic said. "We're a joke with a ledger."

They had come down out of the last green hills that morning and the world had changed its mind about color. The Serrano Range stood up ahead of them the way a wall stands up in a dream — not gradually, not politely, but all at once, white-shouldered and blue-shadowed, so tall that clouds snagged on it like laundry on a fence. The foothills rolled toward it in brown waves, scrub oak and scree and the occasional stubborn pine leaning uphill as if eavesdropping on the summit.

Dominic stood in the doorway of the tea house and looked at it for a long time.

His mother had walked into that. Twice, according to her field notes — three times if you counted the trip she'd never written down, the one whose existence you could only infer from gaps, from pages where the handwriting got careful in the way handwriting gets when someone is deciding what not to say.

"You're doing the face," Tam said behind him.

"What face?"

"The face where you look at a mountain like it owes you money."

"It might," Dominic said. "Everything else does."

* * *

The tea house keeper, a round woman named Sefa who had taken one look at their mud-caked boots and tripled the price of everything without apology, fed them rice and lamb and a yogurt so sour it felt like a personal remark. They ate on a low bench by the window while the light went long and gold across the valley floor.

Ines sat with her map spread out under her plate, eating around it, which told Dominic everything he needed to know about how she'd eaten on digs for forty years. She was fifty-something and built like a question mark that had decided to stand up straight anyway, silver hair cropped short, hands that were always moving — tapping, folding, pointing at things that weren't there yet.

"Two days to the high village," she said, tapping a smudge that was allegedly a village. "Then the goat path. Then the col. If the snow holds off."

"And if it doesn't hold off?"

"Then we learn what Mei-Lin's rope work was worth." She sipped her tea. "It was worth quite a lot. She once lowered me down a cliff face in a storm using a harness made from pack straps and optimism."

"That sounds like her," Dominic said, and then heard himself say it, and put his spoon down.

Because here was the thing he kept tripping over: he didn't know what sounded like her. He knew her handwriting. He knew her cipher, now, a little — Ines had cracked the first layer on the night train with nothing but a memory of a stolen afternoon in 1987 and a bottle of plum brandy, and Dominic had watched the woman he'd spent thirty years resenting resolve slowly into sentences. But the sound of her? The jokes? He had nothing. He was reconstructing his mother out of other people's leftovers, and every time one of those leftovers was warm — *that sounds like her* — it hurt in a way he hadn't budgeted for.

Ines watched him do all of this in the space of four seconds, because she missed nothing, and she let him finish before she spoke.

"You'll get tired of learning her," she said. "That's the honest warning. Everyone who loved her got tired of learning her eventually. It was like loving a locked room that occasionally handed you a key and then pretended it hadn't."

"Were you two friends?"

"No." Ines said it fast and clean, no varnish. "God, no. We were rivals for twenty years. We competed for grants, for sites, for chairs, for the attention of men who didn't deserve the attention of either of us. I once wrote a review of her book that made her editor cry." A pause. "She deserved better than that review, incidentally. The book was good. That's not the point."

"What's the point?"

"The point is that I knew her better than anyone alive, and we were never once friends, and I have thought about why for a long time, and I think—" Ines stopped. Her hands, which never stopped moving, stopped.

Tam, wisely, became very interested in the bottom of his tea.

"I think you should hear this from me and not from him," Ines said. "Because he will tell it differently. He'll tell it like a tragedy he got caught in. And it wasn't. It was a choice."

"Him," Dominic said. "Voss."

"Voss." She said the name like she was spitting out a seed. "1994. The Karagöl excavation. You've seen the name in her notes — the site she never finished, the one that ended her career at the Institute. Officially, an unpermitted trench collapsed. Unofficially—" Ines turned her teacup a quarter turn, aligning the handle with something only she could see. "Unofficially, there was no collapse. There was a decision. Voss found the main burial chamber three weeks before anyone else knew it existed. And instead of reporting it — proper survey, proper documentation, the whole slow boring machinery of doing things right — he opened it at night with two local laborers and a contract from a private buyer. The Halvard Collection. Same people, same checkbook, thirty years running."

Dominic's hands had gone still on the table. He noticed them the way you notice your own heartbeat when it changes.

"Mei-Lin found out," Ines went on. "Of course she did; she found out everything, that was her whole insufferable gift. And she confronted him. Privately, first — she always gave people the door before she gave them the wall. And Voss looked her in the eye and told her that if she reported it, the permits would show *her* signature on the night trench. Because he'd forged them. Months earlier. Carefully. While they were sharing a mess tent and a research budget and, I believe, a bottle of very good raki on her birthday."

"He framed her."

"He framed her so precisely that the tribunal took eleven months to sort it out, and by the end she'd lost the site, the grant, the chair, and most of the people who called themselves her colleagues. I didn't help." Ines's jaw tightened. "I saw the opening and I took it. Her fall was my promotion. I've had twenty years to decide whether that makes me a rival or just a vulture with better posture, and I still don't have an answer I like."

The tea house was quiet except for the wind coming off the foothills and Sefa clattering dishes somewhere in the back with the deliberate volume of a woman pretending not to listen.

"So he didn't just take her career," Dominic said slowly. "He took it wearing her name."

"He took it wearing her name, and then he spent thirty years working for the people who paid him to do it, and she spent thirty years becoming someone who couldn't be traced. Who couldn't be *used*. Do you understand now?" Ines leaned forward. "The erased life, the estranged son, the cipher, the whole architecture of silence — it wasn't paranoia. It was a woman who had already been buried once, digging herself out in advance."

Dominic looked at the mountain again through the window. It hadn't moved. Mountains have the luxury of not moving.

He had spent years being angry at his mother for the distance, for the postcards that stopped, for the funeral he'd found out about a week late because the notification letter had gotten lost between two bureaucracies that had also, apparently, stopped writing to each other. And the anger had been easy. Anger is easy. It comes pre-assembled; you just pick it up.

But this was a different thing forming in his chest, and it took him a moment to recognize it because it used some of the same muscles.

It was anger with a direction.

"She chose me," he said. "Out of all of it. The archive, the work, the country that doesn't exist — she chose me to carry it, knowing what carrying it cost her."

"She chose you," Ines agreed, "and she did it badly, and she did it completely. Both things are true. Welcome to mothers."

* * *

Sefa brought more tea nobody had asked for and stood over them until they drank it, then asked, in the tone of someone asking about the weather, whether they were going up-valley tomorrow.

"Past the shrine?" she said. "To Karsat?"

"You know it?" Ines asked.

"Everyone knows Karsat." Sefa wiped her hands on her apron. "The songs go there. The old songs. My grandmother sang them; I sing them wrong to my granddaughter, who corrects me, which is how songs survive." She shrugged, a whole philosophy in one motion. "You'll want a guide for the first day. Or you'll want to not go. Those are the options."

"We'll take the guide," Tam said, "and consider the second option on the way."

After she'd gone back inside, Tam looked at the two of them across the table — the estranged son with his mother's enemies' enemy, the rival with her twenty-year confession finally out of her mouth — and said, "So. Are we angry now? Is that the plan? We climb a mountain angry?"

"We climb a mountain prepared," Dominic said.

"Prepared and angry."

"Angry is fuel," Ines said. "The trick is not letting it drive. You steer with it or it steers you. Mei-Lin steered with it. Mostly." A pause. "There was the incident with the Land Rover in 1991, but we agreed never to discuss the Land Rover."

"There was an incident with a Land Rover," Tam repeated, delighted, "and it has been legally sealed."

"By treaty."

Dominic almost smiled. It surprised him, the almost. Grief does that — leaves these small doors open in walls you thought were solid, and you walk past them and feel the draft.

* * *

They slept in the tea house's back room, three bedrolls on a floor that smelled of sheep and cedar smoke, and Dominic lay awake listening to the wind come down off the range and thinking about a night trench in 1994. About his mother standing in the dark in front of a man she'd trusted, holding the truth, and watching him hand her a lie with her own signature on it.

And then — this was the part he turned over and over, the part that wouldn't smooth out — then she had gone on. Not cleanly, not happily, but she had gone on, alone, for thirty years, keeping the ledger, keeping the cipher, keeping the door in the mountain safe from exactly the kind of man who had taught her why it needed keeping safe from.

He got up once, quietly, and took out the ledger half wrapped in oilcloth, and didn't open it. Just held it. It weighed less than a brick and more than a brick, depending on how you measured.

Ines's voice came out of the dark, low: "Sleep. Angry is fuel, remember. Fuel needs a rested engine."

"You're awake too."

"I'm always awake. It's a professional condition." A rustle of bedding. "For what it's worth — and I know exactly what it's worth, which is very little — she talked about you. At conferences. To strangers. Terribly indiscreet. 'My son,' she'd say, and then describe some paper you'd written like you'd discovered penicillin. I used to think she was lying. Making a normal life to wave at people." Another rustle. "I don't think that anymore."

Dominic stared at the ceiling beams for a long time.

"Go to sleep, Marchetti."

"Custodian," she replied, which was either an insult or a title, and rolled over.

* * *

They left at dawn with a guide named Bekir, seventeen years old, who charged them full price and then spent the entire first hour trying to give it back in unsolicited advice. The foothills took them up through scrub and thorn and then into pine, and the air changed — thinner, cleaner, with a mineral cold underneath it that hadn't been there yesterday.

By midday they could see their breath.

By midafternoon, on a ridge above the tree line, Bekir pointed silently at the next ridge over, where the shadows had gone from blue to white overnight. Snowline. A week early, maybe two. The mountain had read the same weather report they had and decided to act on it personally.

"Two days to Karsat," Bekir said. "Maybe one and a half, if the old woman's grandson still keeps the path clear. Maybe three, if we stop arguing about the Land Rover."

"We weren't arguing about the Land Rover," Ines said.

"You were about to. I could hear it loading."

Dominic stood on the ridge with the wind pushing at his back like a hand between his shoulder blades, and looked up at the white shoulders of the range, and somewhere behind them — two days, maybe less now — he could feel Voss's team moving through the same landscape with the same weather and better equipment and worse reasons.

Let them come, he thought, and was surprised to find he meant it. Not bravado. Something colder and steadier. His mother had run from this man for thirty years, and running had cost her everything except the thing she was protecting.

He was done paying interest on other people's choices.

"Move," he said, and led off up the ridge, and behind him he heard Ines say quietly to Tam:

"Well. Now he sounds like her."

And below them, far down the valley they'd climbed out of, a thread of dust rose off the frontier road — engines, moving fast, closing the distance the snow hadn't closed yet.
