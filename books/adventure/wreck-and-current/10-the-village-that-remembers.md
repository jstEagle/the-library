## 10. The Village That Remembers

The village did not appear on any map Ines had ever trusted, which was how she knew it would be there.

Maps, in her long and occasionally humiliating experience, were documents of negotiation. A place omitted was a place someone wanted kept. A place drawn twice was a place someone was arguing about. And the highland valleys above the Serrano foothills — folded into cloud like letters never sent — were full of places that had been negotiated out of existence by cartographers who preferred their mountains empty.

So when the trail crested a shoulder of grey rock on the third morning out of Tam's cousin's waystation, and Ines saw smoke rising from a fold in the valley below — forty roofs, terraced barley, a stupa whitewashed so recently it glowed like a held breath — she felt not surprise but a kind of professional satisfaction, the same feeling she got when a translation finally agreed with itself.

"There," she said.

"I see it," Dominic said. He said everything now in that flattened voice he'd developed somewhere around the second day of the goat path, as though enthusiasm were a debt he'd stopped being able to service. His hands were cracked from rope and cold. He'd carried Ines's pack for two days without being asked, which she had noted and filed away and refused to comment on, because commenting would have required admitting her lungs had betrayed her at four thousand meters.

Tam looked at the village the way he looked at all settlements: calculating exits. "They'll want to know why we're here before they want to know our names."

"Then we let them ask," Ines said. "And we tell the truth."

Dominic made a sound that might have been a laugh. "That's your strategy?"

"It's my only one. Lies require a better memory than mine."

* * *

They came down into the village in the late afternoon, when the light went amber and the dogs announced them from three terraces away. By the time they reached the first wall, half the village had found reasons to be outdoors — women shaking grain, men mending a roof that needed no mending, children arranged in a viewing gallery on a woodpile with the studied casualness of professionals.

An old woman sat spinning outside the largest house, and she did not look up at all, which told Ines more than any scrutiny could have. You ignored strangers only when you were certain of them.

A man came out to meet them — broad, weathered, perhaps sixty, wearing a coat of homespun wool over a jacket that had once been military and had been demoted decades ago. He looked at Tam first, longest, and something passed between them in the local tongue, brief and unreadable.

"Your friend says you come from below," the man said then, in accented but precise English. "From the rivers."

"From Kestrel Bend originally," Ines said. "I'm Ines Marchetti. This is Dominic Zhou. Our guide is Tam."

"Zhou." The man turned the name over like a stone he suspected of being hollow. "There is a song about a Zhou."

Ines's heart performed a small, undignified maneuver. "Is there?"

"Everything is a song here," the man said. "Whether or not it is true is a different question." He stepped aside from his own doorway, an invitation that was also a test. "I am Dorje. Come in. Tea first. Questions after tea. This is the law."

"What's the penalty for breaking it?" Tam asked.

"Bad tea," Dorje said gravely. "Forever."

* * *

The house was dark and warm and smelled of juniper smoke and butter tea, which Ines had learned to drink without flinching only through years of fieldwork and years of pretending. They sat on low benches along the walls while Dorje's daughter-in-law poured, and the children migrated to the doorway in ones and twos until there were six of them stacked there like cormorants, staring at Dominic with open delight, as if he were a rare bird blown off course.

He was, Ines thought. Blown off course. She watched him hold his tea bowl wrong and get gently corrected by a grandmother who spoke no English and needed none, and she thought: Mei-Lin Zhou would have held it right on the first try. Mei-Lin Zhou had walked into a hundred houses like this one and charmed every elder in the Himalaya and the Andes and probably several ranges that hadn't been named yet. It had been, Ines reflected with the old familiar sting, her single greatest talent, and it had never once been spent on her son.

Dorje waited until the bowls were refilled. Then he asked, without preamble:

"You are digging for the library."

It was not a question, and Ines did not insult him by treating it as one. "We're looking for what your songs remember."

"Ah." He nodded slowly. "You say it carefully. Good. Most who come say 'treasure.'" He set down his bowl. "Two years ago, men came asking about treasure. Before that, twenty years ago, a woman came asking about memory."

Ines felt Dominic go still beside her, the whole architecture of him locking into place.

"She was tall," Dorje continued, looking into the middle distance where memory keeps its files. "She laughed too easily for a serious person. She paid for her lodging by fixing our school roof, badly, and then coming back the next year to fix it properly. She learned eleven words of our language and used all eleven in every sentence, even when she only needed two." He smiled at some private archive. "Her name was Mei-Lin."

"That's my mother," Dominic said.

The room changed. Not dramatically — nobody gasped, nobody stood — but the quality of attention shifted, the way water shifts when a stone enters it. The grandmother at the hearth looked up. The children at the doorway leaned in as one organism.

Dorje studied Dominic for a long time. "You have her chin," he said at last. "And nothing else. She talked with her whole body. You talk like a man trying to take up less space." He said it without cruelty, the way you'd note the weather. "So. You will answer questions. Not for us — for the song. The song must know if you are worth adding to."

"Adding to it how?" Tam asked.

"The song grows," Dorje said simply. "Or it stops."

* * *

What followed was the strangest examination Ines had ever witnessed, and she had once been orally examined for her doctorate by a professor who fell asleep twice.

Dorje did not ask about the ledger, or the seal, or the range, or the door. Instead he asked — and an old woman by the fire, whom he addressed as Aunt Pema and who was clearly the actual authority in the room, supplemented with questions of her own — about Mei-Lin Zhou. Small things. Absurd things.

What did she eat first, on arriving anywhere? (Ines didn't know; Dominic, after a pause, said, "Whatever the local kids were eating. She'd buy it off them.") Correct, said Aunt Pema, satisfied.

Did she sing? ("Badly," Dominic said. "Constantly. Mostly made-up words.") Aunt Pema laughed until she had to wipe her eyes, and pronounced this correct also.

When she was stuck on a problem, what did she do? Here Dominic hesitated, and Ines watched him reach for something and find the shelf empty. "I don't know," he said finally. "I wasn't there. I don't know anything about how she worked. I know she was gone. I know she missed birthdays. I know the phone calls got shorter every year until they stopped." He looked at his tea. "That's what I have. That's the whole inheritance, as far as I ever knew."

Silence. The fire ticked. One of the children whispered something and was shushed.

Then Aunt Pema said something rapid to Dorje, and Dorje translated, though Ines suspected the translation was a formality — everyone in the room had understood.

"She says: good. You failed the easy test correctly."

"I'm sorry," Dominic said. "Failed?"

"A liar would know how she worked," Dorje said. "The Collection trains its people well. They can recite her publications, her methods, her famous temper. We have hosted them. Very impressive. Very hollow." He tapped his own chest. "But only her child would know that she was gone. Only her child carries the shape of the absence." He spread his hands. "The absence is the proof. You cannot forge an absence."

Ines looked away, because her eyes had gone hot and unprofessional, and because she understood suddenly — with the clarity that only arrives at altitude and exhaustion — that this was the truest thing anyone had ever said about Mei-Lin Zhou in her hearing. Everyone who had loved the work knew the woman complete. Only the son knew the hole.

"And the honest answer," Dorje said, gentler now. "If we ask you the last question. The one the song requires."

"Ask it."

"Why did she come here? Why does anyone climb toward the buried library?"

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he didn't perform grief or filial loyalty or any of the things Ines half-expected him to reach for. He just told the truth, flat and unarmored.

"Because she chose the work. Over me, over everything, over her own name in the end. Whatever's down there mattered more to her than being my mother, and I've spent thirty-one years being angry about it, and I'm still angry, and I came anyway, because it's hers and now it's mine and I want to understand the thing that beat me." He shrugged, helpless. "That's all I've got."

Aunt Pema listened to this — she clearly understood more English than she admitted — and then she rose, joints cracking like green wood, and did something that made the whole room inhale.

She touched Dominic's head. Briefly, awkwardly, the blessing of a woman who had outlived ceremony's patience. Then she spoke, and Dorje translated with visible care, choosing each word like a footing on scree.

"She says: the library was built by people who chose memory over safety. Your mother chose the same. She says the anger is also inheritance. She says bring it. It will keep you warm above the snowline."

* * *

They were given the granary loft for the night, which was warmer than it sounded and smelled gloriously of barley. Tam went off immediately to sit with the men who had once been soldiers, trading news of passes and prices in the low shorthand of people whose geography is danger. Ines stayed at the fire longer than she meant to, drinking tea she didn't want, because Dorje was telling stories.

Not about the library. About Mei-Lin. The roof she'd fixed twice. The argument she'd had with the lama about whether a story could be owned — "she said no, he said yes, they argued for three days and became friends for life." The night she'd gotten drunk on arak and tried to teach the children a sea shanty from a country she'd never seen.

"She wrote to us," Dorje said. "Twice a year, sometimes three times. Always the same question at the end: *is the song still sung?* And we would write back: *it is sung.*" He looked into the fire. "Last year, no letter. This year, you arrive instead. Carrying her seal, if Tam's cousins are to be believed, and Tam's cousins are usually to be believed."

"We're carrying more than the seal," Ines said carefully.

"I know." He said it mildly, terribly. "The mountain has been patient for seventy years. It can be patient while you drink tea." He refilled her bowl. "But I will tell you what I told her, the last time she sat where you are sitting. The library is not a treasure. It is a promise. The people who built it were being erased — names struck from registers, books burned, maps redrawn. So they put everything they were into the ground and under ice, and they sang the way here into songs, so that no map could betray them and no burning could finish them." He smiled, and for a moment he looked very old and very young at once. "A treasure can be stolen. A promise can only be kept or broken. Decide, before you go up, which one you are carrying."

* * *

Ines climbed to the granary loft near midnight and found Dominic awake, sitting against the wall with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth across his knees, not reading it — just resting his hand on it, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you weren't sure had finished being dangerous.

"Heard you downstairs," he said. "The shanty story."

"She really taught village children a sea shanty."

"Of course she did." He almost smiled. "She used to sing to me. Same way. All wrong, all volume." He was quiet a moment. "Pema was right. I do carry the absence. It turns out it weighs exactly as much as she did."

Ines lowered herself onto the boards beside him, joints complaining. For a while neither of them spoke, and the wind worked at the eaves, and somewhere below a dog decided, on reflection, to trust them.

"Dominic," she said finally. "Tomorrow, the elder will show us the song-path. The verses are landmarks — split rock, dry riverbed, cairn of black stones. But there's a verse the villagers won't sing to outsiders. Pema sang a fragment of it tonight after you went up. I caught maybe a third of it." She turned to look at him. "It's a warning verse. About the door. And about what the builders left behind to guard it — not beasts, not traps. A choice. The song says the door opens differently depending on what the opener intends."

Dominic looked at her. "How can a door know what you intend?"

"How can a song survive seventy years without a map?" Ines said. "Same answer. Someone made it that way on purpose, and we're only beginning to guess how much trouble they went to." She pulled her blanket up. "Sleep. Tomorrow we learn the rest of the verse — and Tam says the weather radio picked up something this evening he didn't like. Snow, early, from the north. And a party moving up the southern valley with pack animals and no permits."

Down in the dark of the village, faint and steady, someone was singing — an old voice working through a melody older than the borders around it, keeping a promise one note at a time.

Dominic lay back and listened to it until he slept, and Ines, lying awake beside the son of her oldest rival, found herself doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Kestrel Bend: two days' lead, one warning verse, one storm, and one door that could tell what they intended.

She hoped, for all their sakes, that they intended well enough.
