## 11. Ledger, Second Reading

The village had one lamp that worked, and it hung in the elder's kitchen like a small yellow moon nobody could be bothered to take down. Dominic sat beneath it with the ledger open on a plank table scarred by forty years of bread knives, while outside the wind walked up and down the valley checking doors.

Ines sat across from him with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, which was a look he'd come to understand meant she was pretending not to watch him work. Tam occupied the third chair sideways, boots on the rung, peeling an apple in one unbroken spiral, because apparently that was a thing some people could just do.

"You've been staring at the same page for an hour," Ines said.

"I'm not staring. I'm waiting for it to make sense."

"Those are the same thing. One of them is just slower."

He turned the page anyway. The vellum had gone soft as cloth at the edges, and his mother's cipher crawled across it in columns of tiny, patient marks — the same cipher she'd used in her field notebooks, the one Ines had cracked on the night train using nothing but two shared memories and a grudge twenty years old. That first layer had given them the map: the split rock, the dry riverbed, the cairn of black stones, the valley that rain forgot. The second layer — the dense blocks of text between the landmarks — was still mostly shut.

But tonight something had shifted. He'd felt it half an hour ago, the way you feel a lock begin to turn before it actually opens.

"The numbers," he said. "Look at the margins."

Tam leaned over, apple spiral dangling. "Numbers are numbers."

"These aren't. They're dates." He ran his finger down the column. "Every block of text has a date beside it. And they're not random. They go backward." He flipped pages. "Here — this one's dated eleven years ago. This one, nine. This one—" he stopped. His finger stayed where it was.

"What?" Ines said.

"This one's dated four months before she died."

The kitchen got very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in at the window.

"She kept writing," Dominic said. "After the archive. After she sealed it. These aren't survey notes — they're letters. Or entries. She came back to this ledger over and over for years." He looked up. "Why would you keep writing to a locked room?"

Ines took her glasses out of her hair and put them on, which meant she'd stopped pretending. "Read me the last entry. The whole thing, not just the landmarks."

"It's still cipher. I can only get fragments. Words here and there where the pattern repeats." He squinted. "'Sent.' That word keeps recurring — *sent*, *sent*, *sent*. And 'half.' And something that might be 'safe' or might be 'salt,' which would be a very different letter."

"*Sent*," Ines repeated. Her pen was already moving on the scrap of paper she used for everything, the one that had survived three countries and a ferry ambush and currently held a grocery list on one side and a partial conjugation of dead Kharvathi verbs on the other. "Half. Sent. Safe." She wrote the three words in a vertical line, stared at them, then drew a box around all of them and stabbed the pen through the box. "It's a postal record."

"A what?"

"A postal record. Mei-Lin documented everything — you know this about her, you must know this about her. She catalogued her socks. If she mailed something — if she sent the second half of this ledger somewhere — there will be an entry. Date, method, destination code." She was flipping back through the ledger now, fast, past the map, past the early entries, to the last quarter of the vellum where the handwriting changed — smaller, tighter, the hand of someone writing by lamplight in rooms she didn't trust. "Here. Here, look. The dates cluster. Three entries in one week, six years ago. Then nothing for two years. Then a final entry four months before she died."

Dominic felt something cold walk up his spine and sit down. "Six years ago. That's when she stopped calling me."

Ines didn't look up. "Is it?"

"Almost exactly. There was a — there was a voicemail I never answered. I was twenty-three and furious about money she'd promised for my— it doesn't matter. I never called back. After that it was birthday texts. Then not even those." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the voice he used for talking about her, which was most of the time the only voice he had for her at all. "You're saying whatever she did that week, she did instead of coming home."

"I'm saying she was busy," Ines said, gentler than he'd ever heard her, which lasted approximately four seconds before she added, "and also possibly mailing state secrets through civilian post, which frankly explains a great deal about her scheduling."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and the elder's wife brought them tea that tasted faintly of smoke and pine and absolutely refused to let them refuse it. Tam fell asleep sitting up around midnight, the apple peel coiled on the table beside him like a small bronze artifact, and neither of them woke him, because waking Tam required a formal negotiation.

It was Ines who broke the second layer, somewhere past two in the morning, and she did it the same way she'd broken the first: not with mathematics but with memory.

"The destination codes," she said suddenly, so sharply that Tam snorted awake and reached for a knife he kept, for reasons known only to him, inside his boot. "Mei-Lin and I shared a hotel room in Vellacorte for six weeks in our twenties. Worst six weeks of my life. She had a system for remembering addresses — she hated writing real addresses down, even then, even before any of this. She'd convert them. Street name into a number using some private alphabet she thought I never figured out." She tapped the margin of the ledger. "I figured it out. It took me three days and a bottle of terrible wine, and I have never once mentioned it, because knowing how another scholar hides things is worth more than catching her at it."

"And?" Dominic said.

"And the code in this final entry decodes to a postal office." She wrote it out letter by letter, her pen scratching, her lips moving. "Harbor district. Saltmere." She looked up. "That's on the coast, Dominic. That's the coast we just spent nine days and one drowned boat getting away from."

The kitchen tilted slightly, or perhaps that was the tea. Dominic gripped the edge of the table.

Saltmere. The port town where they'd landed, where Voss's men had nearly boxed them in against the water, where Tam had bought their escape with a bribe and a lie and a route inland that no sane person would take. Nine days behind them. Two hundred miles of mountains behind them. And his mother, four months before she died, had walked into a harbor-district post office and mailed the other half of the inheritance — the half that made the archive reachable, the half a man named Voss would burn villages for — to a locker or a poste restante or a stranger's address in the one place they could least afford to go back to.

"She sent it away from herself," he said slowly. "She hid the vault in the mountains and she hid the key on the coast. Split them. So no one person could ever—" He stopped.

"No one *living* person," Ines said quietly. "Because she intended to be the bridge between them. And then she died, and the bridge went down, and both halves have been sitting there waiting for someone stubborn enough to cross."

Tam, fully awake now despite himself, turned his apple over in his fingers. "So we go back."

"We don't go back," Dominic said. "Voss is behind us. The whole point of the mountains was—"

"Voss is behind us," Ines agreed, "which means Voss is between us and nowhere. Think. He knows we came east. He doesn't know why yet — the village bought us time, and the elder paid more for it than any of us wanted to owe. But the moment he reaches the dry valley and finds the cairn disturbed, he stops chasing us and starts racing us. And if he ever learns there's a second half—" she tapped the ledger, "—he won't need us alive at all. Right now we're useful. Useful people get followed. Informed people get buried."

"So the plan is to sneak two hundred miles back through territory crawling with his men, to a post office, on the off chance that a parcel my mother mailed six years ago is still sitting in a drawer."

"Not a parcel." Ines's eyes had gone bright in the lamp light, the brightness he'd learned to distrust and then, reluctantly, to rely on. "A registered item. Look at the entry again — the fee notation. Mei-Lin paid for indefinite retention. Some postal services will hold a registered item for decades if the fees are maintained." She paused. "And there's a standing payment instruction. A bank draft, renewable annually. She set it up so the item would be held *in perpetuity*."

Dominic stared at her. "She's been paying rent on it. All these years. From wherever she was. While she wasn't calling me."

"Yes."

"While I was eating instant noodles and dodging her creditors, she was paying storage fees on my inheritance in a harbor town."

There was a silence with a lot in it. Tam, delicately, began reassembling his apple.

"That's not the hook," Ines said.

"There's a hook?"

"There's always a hook. Read the last line of the final entry. You told me you had fragments."

He looked down at the vellum. The last line, decoded in Ines's marginal alphabet, read:

*If you are reading this, child, then the holding office will release to your hand what I could not put in it. Ask for the keeper by the name I taught you. You will know the season by the gulls.*

"'The name I taught you,'" he repeated. "What name? She didn't teach me names. She taught me river systems and how to swear in four languages."

Ines and Tam exchanged a glance over his head, the kind of glance that made him feel like the youngest person in every room, which at thirty-one he resented on principle.

"When you were small," Ines said, "did she call you anything? Not Dominic."

He thought about it. The lamp hummed. Outside, the wind gave up on the doors and started on the roof tiles instead.

"She used to say—" and it came up out of him unexpectedly, whole, smelling of a kitchen twenty-five years gone, "she used to say I was her *little dredger*. Because I liked digging holes. In the garden, at the beach, anywhere. She said I'd find things everyone else had thrown away." His throat tightened around the absurdity of it. He had grown up to dig holes professionally, hauling silt out of a flood-swollen river to pay her debts, and the first thing he'd pulled out of the current was her secret. Somewhere his mother was laughing, or weeping, or both, in whatever place the dead kept their ledgers balanced.

"The keeper at the Saltmere holding office," Ines said, "will be expecting a little dredger."

* * *

They argued about it until dawn, which in that village meant they argued quietly, because sound carried down the valley and the valley had already lost enough.

Tam was for going. Not out of bravery — he was explicit about this — but out of arithmetic. "Voss comes up this valley in five days, maybe six. We can't outrun him east; the passes close in three weeks and he has helicopters. We can't hide forever in a village that's already bled for us. But if the second half is on the coast, and we get it first, then *we* hold the door and *he* holds nothing but guns. Guns open a lot of doors," he admitted, rolling the apple core between his fingers, "but not a door that needs two keys. My grandmother crossed a border with nothing once. She always said: when they own the road, own the thing the road is for."

Ines was against it, loudly, in whispers. "It's a trap. Not deliberately — worse than that, it's a *convenience*. Everything we need is in the one direction we can't safely go. That's not luck, that's design, and Mei-Lin designed it, and Mei-Lin was the most brilliant tactician I ever met and also the worst judge of collateral damage I ever stood next to. She will have accounted for the Collection. She may even have accounted for Voss. What she will not have accounted for," and here Ines's voice did something complicated, "is the fact that her son is not her. He doesn't think in contingencies. He thinks in debts. And a man thinking in debts walks straight into harbors."

"Thank you," Dominic said.

"You're welcome. It was a compliment."

"It was not."

"It was entirely. Debts get paid. Contingencies just multiply." She took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet. "I knew her for thirty years, Dominic. I fought with her on four continents. And I am telling you, as the person alive who understands her hand better than anyone breathing: this entry isn't a map. It's a test. *Ask for the keeper by the name I taught you.* She's checking whether whoever comes is really hers. Which means she expected someone might come wearing your face who wasn't you."

The cold thing on his spine shifted its weight.

"Voss has a photograph of me," he said. "The mercenary who drowned was carrying one."

"Yes," Ines said simply. "Which means the Collection has known your face for some time. Which means somewhere in Saltmere there may already be a young man asking for a package, with your face and the right name and none of the right memories." She let that sit. "Or it means nothing, and we walk in, collect, and walk out. I've done this work long enough to know you don't get certainty. You get odds and you pick the ones you can live with."

Dawn came up gray and clean over the eastern ridge, and with it the smell of woodsmoke and flatbread, and the elder's wife appeared in the doorway with the expression of a woman who had fed three strangers for two nights and intended to be compensated in gossip if nothing else.

Dominic looked at the ledger. His mother's smallest, tightest handwriting. Four months before the end, she had sat in some rented room with this vellum and a pen and she had written instructions for him — coded, careful, wrapped in an alphabet only a rival could crack — and the instructions amounted to: *go back the way you came, little dredger. The hard thing is the true thing.*

She had never once in her life made anything easy. Why start posthumously?

"We go back," he said.

Ines closed her eyes briefly, the way people do when a decision goes against them and they've already started planning around it. "Through the high pass," she said. "Not the road. The smuggler routes. Tam?"

Tam was already lacing his boots, which in Tam's language was a signature on a contract. "My grandmother's routes run downhill to the coast," he said. "We'll just be walking them the wrong way." He grinned, quick and crooked. "Everything we've done so far is the wrong way. It's working out fine."

It took them until midmorning to settle affairs with the village — food, directions, a debt to the dead elder that Dominic wrote into the back of his notebook in plain figures, because some ledgers you kept yourself. And it was as they were leaving, at the edge of the terraced fields where the path dropped toward the river gorge, that Ines caught his sleeve.

"One more thing about the entry," she said. "The date of the final posting. I checked it twice against the calendar."

"And?"

"It's four months before she died. But it's also — Dominic, it's three days after your birthday. The birthday you told me she missed. The year of the voicemail." Her grip on his sleeve was light and her eyes were not. "She didn't miss it. She spent it in a harbor post office, paying in perpetuity, so that someday her son could walk in off the street and be handed the one thing she couldn't hand him herself."

Dominic stood on the path with the wind pulling at his jacket and the whole gray gorge opening below them, and found that he had absolutely nothing to say. Hope, it turned out, had a hook in it after all — and the hook was shaped exactly like a woman who had loved him in the only language she'd ever been fluent in, which was logistics.

"Saltmere," he said finally. "Two hundred miles. The wrong way."

"Try to contain your joy," Ines said, and shouldered her pack, and started down.
