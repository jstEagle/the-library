## 12. Voss Ascending

The drowned man's name had been Ferro, and Ferro had been a talker, which was the only reason Voss learned anything at all from him now that he was dead.

Not from Ferro himself. Ferro was currently a photograph on Voss's phone — bloated, gray-lipped, laid out on a mortuary slab in the district capital with the river still leaking out of him in the form of a puddle the coroner kept mopping away. The coroner had been paid to be thorough and discreet, which in this country meant he'd been paid twice, and Voss had watched him work with the mild interest of a man reading someone else's mail.

"Water in the lungs," the coroner said. "But also—" he tapped Ferro's temple, where a knot the size of a walnut sat under the skin, "—this. Post-mortem. Someone hit him after he was already down. Or during."

Or after the rapids spat him out, Voss thought, and one of Zhou's people made sure. He filed it. The file in his head was getting thick.

"His effects?"

"A photograph." The coroner slid an envelope across the steel table. "And his phone, which is useless, waterlogged. The photograph isn't."

Voss opened the envelope with a thumbnail. Inside: a print, creased once, of a man coming down the steps of a bus depot with a duffel over one shoulder and the particular hunched walk of someone who has recently learned the world wants him dead. Dominic Zhou. Taken from across the street, amateurish framing, thumb over one corner. A surveillance photo taken by a man who did not know how to take surveillance photos, which meant Ferro had been given the job late and cheaply, which meant the local hires were getting sloppy.

Which meant Voss needed better people.

He thanked the coroner, tipped him enough to buy silence rather than gratitude — gratitude could be traced; silence was anonymous — and walked out into the heat of the border town, where the air smelled of diesel and wet stone and the particular sweetness of fruit going bad in the market stalls. Somewhere behind him, Ferro's body would go into a drawer, then a box, then a hole, and no one would come looking for it, because Ferro had been the kind of man who arranged his life so that no one would come looking. Voss respected that. It was practically a professional credential.

* * *

The Collection had a philosophy. Most people assumed it didn't — most people assumed the Halvard Collection was simply rich men buying pretty things, the way rich men bought racehorses and politicians. But there was a philosophy, written down by old Halvard himself in a memorandum that fewer than a dozen living people had read, and Voss was one of them, because reading it was part of the induction, and the induction was part of the debt.

*Provenance belongs to whoever pays.*

That was the whole of it. Four words. Halvard had built an empire on the observation that history has no owner, only custodians, and custodians can be outbid. A statue in a field belongs to the rain. A statue in a museum belongs to the state. A statue in the Collection's vaults belongs to the Collection — not legally, perhaps, not morally, certainly not in any sense the word *belong* was invented to describe, but functionally, permanently, absolutely. Whoever pays takes custody. Whoever takes custody writes the label. And the label, in the end, is all anyone remembers.

Voss had recited the memorandum aloud once, at his induction, standing in a marble room in Geneva while a woman with a voice like a filing cabinet asked him questions. He had thought, even then, that the philosophy was airtight the way a tomb is airtight. Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out. You can live in it, if you don't mind the dark.

He had been twenty-nine years old. He was forty-six now, and he had stopped minding the dark, which was either progress or surrender, and he had long ago stopped being able to tell the difference.

* * *

Ferro's contacts were three, and Voss interviewed all of them in a single afternoon, working through the town like a man checking items off a list, because that was exactly what he was doing.

The first was a bartender at the Hotel Aurora, a heavy woman named Sefa who had sold Ferro information about bus schedules for years. She talked freely once Voss set a folded bill on the bar and left his hand on it without unfolding it. Zhou had come through two days ago. With him, a woman — older, sharp-eyed, European accent, "the kind of face that looks at you like you're a specimen." Sefa had liked her immediately and said so with the frankness of a woman who liked very few people.

"They took the east road," she said. "Toward the range. Your friend Ferro followed them in a hired car and came back alone, soaked, furious, saying something about a boat."

"A boat."

"There's a smuggler works the frontier river. Tam. Half-wild, half-clever, all mouth. If they crossed, he took them."

Voss unfolded the bill. "Did Ferro say what happened on the water?"

"He said the river happened." Sefa shrugged. "He wasn't a river man."

No, Voss thought. Ferro had been a road man, a door man, a man for dark hallways and quiet rooms, and the river had eaten him for it. There was a lesson there. Voss collected lessons the way other men collected regrets — compulsively, and always too late to use them.

The second contact was a police clerk who sold access to the ferry registry, and the third was a boy of maybe nineteen who ran errands for everyone and owed Ferro money, which made him cooperative in the way only debtors are. From the clerk: a crossing logged at dawn two days ago, three passengers, destination the north bank. From the boy: that Tam had come back the next day without his boat, walking, soaked, and telling anyone who'd listen that the river gods had opinions about passengers who brought guns aboard.

So. They had crossed. They had lost their pursuers in the rapids — permanently, in Ferro's case — and they had gone north, into the foothills, toward the Serrano Range, moving fast enough to abandon equipment. Which meant they knew what they were looking for and how little time the season would give them.

Voss stood in the alley behind the police station and looked at the mountains. They were visible today, which was rare — a hard blue line above the haze, white-capped already, weeks early. The weather reports agreed with his eyes. The snowline was descending like a lid closing on a box.

He did some arithmetic. Two days behind, terrain worsening, his own team six strong but carrying too much gear and too little local knowledge. Ahead of him: a woman in her fifties who had spent thirty years in ranges like these, a smuggler who knew every goat path on the north slope, and a grieving man with nothing left to lose, which was the most dangerous supply load a person could carry.

By any rational assessment, he should lose them. By any rational assessment, he had already lost them.

Voss smiled at the mountains, briefly, without warmth. Rational assessments assumed the other side was rational too. But the Zhou woman had been irrational in ways Voss had studied for twenty years, and her son was carrying her ledger, and grief made people climb mountains they should have turned back from. He was counting on it.

* * *

He found his guide where the fixer said he would be: a tea house near the north gate, run by a man named Oskan who had once been a soldier and now sold tea, rumors, and deniability in roughly equal measure.

"I need someone who knows the high valleys," Voss said. "North slope. Off-trail. Someone who won't ask what we're carrying or why."

Oskan poured tea without hurrying. "Everyone asks what you're carrying. That's how they know what to charge."

"Then someone who asks and forgets."

"Forgetting costs more than knowing."

"Everything costs more than knowing. That's the whole economy." Voss drank his tea. It was terrible, over-steeped, bitter as a bad year. He drank it anyway. "I need six porters, cold-weather kit, and a man who can find a village that doesn't appear on maps."

Oskan set down the pot. "There's a village in the high valley that keeps songs instead of records. My grandmother sang one. About a library buried in the stone, before the war, before the erasure." He looked at Voss with the flat professional gaze of a man pricing a thing. "You want the library."

"I want the people who are walking toward it."

"That's a different job."

"It's the same job done in reverse."

Oskan almost smiled. "You're not a soldier."

"No."

"You're not a scholar, either, though you have the hands for it." He nodded at Voss's fingers, long and scarred at the knuckles — trowel scars, brush-handle calluses, the marks of twenty seasons of careful excavation that Voss had stopped doing and never stopped carrying. "What are you, then?"

Voss considered the question with more attention than he let show. What was he? An employee. An asset. A man with a debt structured across decades and denominated in obedience. In the Collection's taxonomy he was *recovery personnel*, which was a label, and labels, in the end, were all anyone remembered.

"I'm the person sent when the first person fails," he said.

Oskan nodded slowly, as if this were an answer he heard often, which it probably was. "Three days," he said. "Six porters. And my cousin Aldin guides. He knows the songs."

"Good."

"One condition." Oskan refilled Voss's cup unasked. "Whatever's up there — the library, the dead country, whatever your employers think they're buying — my cousin comes back down. Whatever happens to you happens to you. But Aldin comes back."

"Agreed."

"You agree quickly."

"I've learned that most conditions are cheaper met than argued."

Oskan studied him for a moment, and Voss recognized the look — it was the look Mei-Lin Zhou used to give him across a dig table, the look of a person trying to see the mechanism inside the machine. She had never managed it. He wondered, with a detachment that felt almost like peace, whether anyone ever would.

* * *

They moved out the next morning before light, six porters, a guide, and Voss, climbing out of the town's diesel haze into air that tasted of pine and altitude. Voss walked well. He had always walked well — it was one of the few inheritances from his father worth keeping, the flat economical stride of a man who covered ground without spending himself. Behind him the porters grumbled and shifted loads and sang snatches of songs to keep rhythm, and ahead of him the mountains rose white and silent, holding whatever they held.

On the second day, Aldin began singing one of the village songs as they climbed, absently, the way people sing when their feet have found a rhythm. It was in an old dialect, half-lost, and Voss listened with his head tilted like a man hearing a frequency others can't.

"What does it say?" he asked.

Aldin glanced back. "It says the books went into the mountain so the fire couldn't eat them. It says the door remembers both its keys." He shrugged. "It says a lot of things. Songs aren't instructions."

"No," Voss said. "But they're coordinates. People sing what they can't afford to write down."

Aldin gave him a strange look — the look of a man reassessing a customer — and said nothing more, but he sang the song again, slower, and Voss memorized it. Not the words. The shape. Where the melody bent, where it repeated, where it stopped short as if avoiding something. Mei-Lin would have understood instantly. Mei-Lin had understood everything instantly except the things that mattered off the dig site, and the unfairness of that thought arrived uninvited, as it always did, and Voss put it away, as he always did, in the room in his head where he kept everything that had a name he refused to speak.

* * *

On the third evening they reached the shoulder below the high valley, and Aldin pointed with his chin at a thread of smoke rising from behind a ridge.

"The village," he said. "They'll have seen us since noon. They'll decide whether to let us up before we decide to go up."

"Then we arrive as buyers, not soldiers."

"And if they don't sell?"

Voss looked at the smoke, thin and gray against the darkening rock, marking human life the way a flag marks a claim. Somewhere beyond that ridge, three people were warming themselves by a fire like that one, eating, resting, believing they had hours or days of lead. One of them had his mother's stubbornness and her ledger. One of them had spent a career competing with a dead woman and hadn't noticed she'd won. One of them had lost a boat and gained a cause.

Provenance belongs to whoever pays, Voss thought. But first, someone has to carry it out of the mountain. And carriers can be intercepted anywhere between the door and the road.

"They'll sell," he said. "Everyone sells. The price is just a matter of finding the currency."

Aldin looked at him for a long moment, and then, quietly, in the dialect of the songs, he said something under his breath that Voss did not ask him to translate.

They camped that night on the shoulder, and Voss lay awake listening to the wind move through the pass — a sound like breathing, like a vault exhaling — and did his final arithmetic. Tomorrow, the village. Tomorrow night, the trail above it, and the three sets of footprints in the fresh snow that his scouts had already found and photographed, heading up-valley, toward the dry shadow where the maps went blank.

Two days behind had become one. By tomorrow it would be none.

He closed his eyes and slept without dreaming, which was the closest thing to peace the Collection had ever taught him how to buy.
