## 13. Burning the Songs

I had told them the village would be safe.

I want that written down somewhere, in some ledger of my own making, because I have spent my whole life keeping accounts — fares paid, favors owed, grudges banked like dried fish against a hard winter — and I had entered *safe* into the book for Old Sarno's people the way I'd enter a fare: confidently, and on behalf of someone else's risk.

"They're herders," I said, the night before, spitting into the fire the way my grandmother taught me, which is to say dramatically and without any actual purpose. "Nobody burns a herding village. There's nothing here but wool and cheese and old men who argue about the proper way to salt mutton."

"Voss isn't after wool," Ines said.

"Then he's a fool, because the wool is excellent."

Dominic didn't say anything. He was sitting with his back against the granary wall, turning his mother's cipher pages over in his hands like a man shuffling cards he already knew were losing ones. He looked up at me then, and there was something in his face — not fear exactly, more like the expression of a man who has done arithmetic he doesn't like and is waiting for someone to check it.

"Tam," he said. "How long would it take Voss's men to get here from the ferry crossing?"

"Three days. Four, if they're careful."

"And how long since we ran the rapids?"

I did the arithmetic myself then, and I did not like the answer either. Two days. Two days and one very fast morning.

* * *

Old Sarno's village sat in a fold of the high valley like a coin dropped in a pocket — forty stone houses, a shrine with a rusted bell, terraces of barley climbing the slopes in green steps, and the granary at the center of everything, raised on pillars the way granaries are raised everywhere in these mountains, so the mice must work for their supper. It was the largest building in the village and the oldest, its beams black with a century of smoke, and it held not just grain but the village's memory: the song-boards, the woven records, the knotted cords that remembered marriages and debts and the names of the dead.

The elder — Sarno himself, ninety if he was a day, with a face like a riverbed in drought — had tested Dominic two nights ago with questions about his mother, and Dominic had answered honestly, which is the only currency the old man accepted. *She chose the work.* The words had hung in the cold air like breath, and Sarno had nodded once, and that nod had opened every door in the village.

That was why it hurt the way it hurt. Hospitality is a debt too. I keep telling you, everything is.

The morning it happened, I was down at the byres arguing with a boy named Kesh about whether his family's mule had eaten my boot. This is true. The mule had eaten my boot. I was holding up the ruined leather as evidence and demanding compensation in cheese, and Kesh was insisting the mule had merely *tasted* it, when the dogs started.

You learn to read dog-speech growing up on rivers. There's the bark for strangers-on-the-path, which is businesslike. There's the bark for wolves, which has panic in it but also a kind of professional interest. And then there's the bark they gave that morning, high and continuous and wrong, the sound of animals who have smelled something that does not belong to the world they understand.

I looked up the valley and saw six men coming down the goat path in a line, moving fast, and the sun caught metal on two of them, and my stomach performed a maneuver I usually associate with bad ferry water.

"Kesh," I said, very calmly, because calm is contagious and so is screaming and I have always preferred to be the contagion rather than the symptom. "Go get the elder. Go now. Run like your mule owes me money."

* * *

They came into the village square the way water comes into a boat — no drama, just arrival, and suddenly everything is different and sinking. Their leader was a broad man with a burn scar on his jaw and the patient eyes of someone who has been ordered to do unpleasant things often enough that unpleasantness no longer surprises him. He did not draw a weapon. He didn't need to. Six armed men in a square of forty shepherds is an argument already won; the weapons are just punctuation.

He spoke accented border-tongue, loud enough for everyone. "We're looking for three travelers. A woman, gray-streaked hair. A young man, carries himself like a student. And a riverman." His eyes found me with what I can only describe as professional appreciation. "Who is probably standing right there."

Every face in the square turned toward me. Forty pairs of eyes, and every single one of them a locked door. I have been lied about by smugglers, cheated by customs men, and betrayed by a woman in Porto Verde whose name I still drink to angrily, but I have never been protected by forty strangers at once, and I will tell you honestly: it went through me like warm liquor.

"We see many travelers," Sarno said.

The scarred man smiled. It was not a smile that reached anywhere near his eyes; it was a smile used for carrying heavy things. "I'm sure you do. Here's what happens next. You tell me where they are, or—" and he gestured, almost gently, at the granary, "—we make a point about hospitality."

Now. Let me tell you what the granary meant, because I'm not sure even Dominic and Ines fully understood it yet, though they learned fast enough afterward. My grandmother fled Kharvath with nothing but a boat and a lullaby, and she taught me that a people who lose their books become a rumor. These mountain villages kept their songs the way we keep our boats — not because they're worth money, but because without them you drift. The song-boards in that granary held the valley's whole memory. Weddings. Floods. The names of everyone buried on the hillside, going back past anyone's grandmother. When a child was born, they wove a knot. When someone died, they sang him onto the boards in thread and wood.

It was not a building. It was the village, wearing a roof.

And this man was going to burn it over cheese and mutton and old men arguing about salt.

"You won't find them here," Sarno said, and his voice was dry as his face, steady as a post driven deep. "They passed through. They went south."

"They went south," the scarred man repeated pleasantly. "Through the pass that closes tomorrow? Interesting choice." He turned to his men and said something short in their own tongue, and two of them walked toward the granary carrying jerricans, and I understood with a horrible slow clarity that the decision had been made before they ever came down the path, that the question was never really a question, that men like this bring the fire first and ask questions by its light.

What happened next happened quickly, and I have replayed it so many times that it has worn grooves in me.

Sarno moved. Ninety years old, a stick for a leg half the time, and he moved between the granary and the men with the jerricans like a gate swinging shut, and he began to sing.

I don't know the song. It was in the old tongue, the Kharvathi that survives out here in fragments, in place-names and curses and grandmothers' humming, and his voice was cracked and thin and absolutely fearless. He sang at them the way you'd throw stones. And the village took it up — not all at once, but in pieces, a woman by the well, then the boy Kesh, then a dozen voices, ragged and off-key and getting louder, until the square was full of a sound that made the armed men stop walking, because you can shoot a crowd but you cannot shoot a song, or rather you can, but it stops being a job and starts being something else, and even mercenaries know the difference in their spines if not their heads.

The scarred man's jaw tightened. "Burn it," he said quietly.

* * *

I will not describe the fire in loving detail. Some things you should look at once and then look away from forever. But I will tell you what it sounded like, because that's the part that stays: the beams going off like guns, one after another, each report a century of smoke leaving the wood at once. And the singing. They kept singing while it burned. Voices breaking, coughing on the smoke, and still singing, because that was the only weapon left and they were going to empty it completely.

We got the song-boards out — some of them. Dominic and I and Kesh and three others went through the smoke on the windward side while the soldiers watched, and I want you to sit with that for a second: they watched us do it, because letting us save a few boards made the punishment feel generous. That's the craft of cruelty. It wants witnesses to its mercy.

Dominic came out with his eyebrows singed and an armload of knotted cords, coughing, and set them down in the dirt with hands that were shaking, and Ines put hers beside his, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a debt, and we all knew who owed it.

Me. We knew who owed it was me.

* * *

Sarno found us at dusk, behind the shrine, where the three of us were doing the thing guilty people do, which is making plans loudly enough to feel useful. The pass closed tomorrow. Voss's team was hours behind us, not days — the ferry ambush had cost us our lead, and now the fire had cost us our cover, because a burning granary is a message written in a language every valley reads.

"You need the high path," Sarno said, sitting down with the care of a man whose body has stopped being a given. "Not the valley road. The road has their other team on it — four men, camped at the split rock since yesterday. But the high path goes over the shoulder of the mountain, three days, cold, and it puts you above them, looking down." He tapped the ground with his stick. "There's a cairn where the paths meet. Black stones. From there you'll see them before they see you."

"Why are you helping us?" Dominic asked. His voice was rough. "After what they—"

"After what *you* brought," Sarno said, and let that land, because honesty was the currency, we had established this. Then his old eyes softened by a degree, which on that face was practically a smile. "And after what you carried out of the fire. A people is not its granary. A people is whoever picks up the boards." He looked at me. "Your grandmother's people built vaults, riverman. Did you know that? Whole libraries under stone. Because they understood that fire comes. Fire always comes. The only question is what you've hidden where it can't reach."

My throat did something embarrassing. "She never mentioned vaults," I managed. "She mostly mentioned the boat."

"The boat was probably the vault," Sarno said, and this time it was definitely a smile.

Then he told us the rest, and his voice changed, became the dry administrative tone of a man settling accounts. He described the high path landmark by landmark — the scree chute, the frozen spring, the col with the leaning stone — and he made Dominic repeat it back twice, correcting him once, and I understood that he was not giving directions. He was transferring cargo. He was putting the village's knowledge into our pockets because his own pockets were about to be searched.

When the soldiers came for him — and we heard them coming, boots on stone, the scarred man's voice asking again, patiently, where the travelers had gone — Sarno stood up, and straightened his coat, and said to us, quietly: "Point up-valley. Loudly. Buy your hours. That's the price of the directions, and I set it myself, so none of you get to argue."

"Sarno—" Ines started.

"I am ninety," he said. "Everything from here is profit."

* * *

I will tell you what I saw, because I was watching from the shadow of the byre and I could not look away, and looking away would have been its own kind of theft.

They marched him to the square, to the smoking ruin of the granary, and the scarred man asked his question again, and Sarno pointed — a great dramatic gesture, arm extended, finger steady — up the valley, north, the wrong way entirely, toward the dead end of the glacier country where nobody could go and nobody could follow and any search party would waste a full day learning it.

"There," he said, loud enough for the whole village. "They went up. Toward the ice."

And the scarred man looked at the pointing finger, and looked at the old man's face, and I swear to you he knew — you could see it in the set of his shoulders, the small tired pause — he knew it was a lie, and he knew it was a purchase, and he let it stand anyway, because a lie costs him a day and a corpse costs him a war with every village in the range. He nodded to his men. They left before full dark, moving fast, up the wrong valley.

Sarno died that night. Not by violence — the violence had already spent itself on the granary — but simply, in his sleep, in his own house, with the smell of smoke still in the air and the song-boards we'd saved stacked safely under the shrine floor. His granddaughter said he ate a full dinner first and complained about the salt, which, knowing him, was his idea of a perfect last day. The village sang him onto a new board by candlelight, weaving his name into fresh cord, and Kesh's mule watched solemnly from the byre door, and I stood at the edge of the light with my one boot and both my debts and did not cry until everyone had gone, and then only briefly, and I deny it completely.

* * *

We left before dawn, the three of us, climbing out of the village fold while the stars were still hard and close. Behind us, the granary stood gutted against the early light, its pillars like burnt ribs, and the villagers were already at work — I could hear hammering, see figures on the roofline — rebuilding, because that is the whole argument of mountain people, made daily, in timber.

Nobody talked for the first hour. The path climbed through barley terraces gone gold, then through scrub, then into rock, and the village shrank below us to a scatter of stones and smoke, and finally Ines stopped, leaned on her knees, and said what we were all carrying.

"He bought us a day with his life's last afternoon."

"No," Dominic said. I was surprised by his voice — it was quiet but it had edges on it now, new edges, cut recently. "He bought us a day with his life. All of it. Same as the granary. Same as the songs." He adjusted his pack and looked up the mountain, where the high path switchbacked into cloud. "The Collection thinks history is something you can own. He just showed them it's something you spend. On each other." He started walking. "So we don't waste it."

I fell in behind him, and Ines behind me, and I thought about my grandmother's boat, and about vaults under stone, and about how everything I've ever loved fit inside one hull crossing one river at night, and how maybe that's all any of us are — somebody's archive, moving fast, trying to reach higher ground.

Above us, the clouds had begun, very quietly, to stack.

And below, far down the wrong valley, I would have sworn I saw a glint of metal that was moving the right way after all.

But that might have been the light.

It was not the light.
