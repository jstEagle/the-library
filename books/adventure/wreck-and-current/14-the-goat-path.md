## 14. The Goat Path

The path, when they finally found it, was less a path than an argument the mountain was having with itself.

It began behind the village, past the last terraced field where a boy watched them go with the solemnity of someone who had been told to watch and intended to do it thoroughly. Then it turned up through scree and old pine, narrowed to the width of a coffin lid, and simply kept going — switchbacking up the flank of the mountain like a stitch in some enormous, careless seam.

"Goats," Tam said, with something like reverence. He went first, because he'd grown up on slopes like this and because, as he put it, "if I fall, you'll at least have time to feel bad about me before you follow."

"And if I fall?" Dominic asked.

"You're bigger. You'll take longer. It's considerate."

Ines said nothing. She had been quiet since the village — since the elder's questions and Dominic's answer, since *she chose the work* hung in the cold air between them like breath that wouldn't disperse. She climbed well, though. That surprised him. He'd expected a woman of archives and lecture halls to move like furniture being rearranged. Instead she placed her boots the way she read a page: carefully, completely, missing nothing.

By midday the trees gave up. Above the treeline the world simplified into rock, sky, and the thin brown ribbon of the trail, and the air began to develop opinions about their presence. Dominic felt it first as a small tightness behind his eyes, then as the sense that his lungs were negotiating a new contract without his consent.

They camped that night in a hollow below a ridge, three tents' worth of shelter improvised from two tents and a tarp Tam produced from his pack with the smugness of a magician who has been waiting all day for his moment.

"Smuggler's rule," Tam said, driving a stake with a stone. "Never carry what you can cache, never cache what you can't replace, and never trust a flat stretch of ground. Flat means water wants it."

"That's two rules," Ines said.

"The third one is don't correct the smuggler."

Dominic laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised out of him — and realized it was the first time in weeks. Maybe months. The sound seemed to startle Ines too; she looked over at him with an expression he couldn't read, somewhere between assessment and something softer, and then she went back to checking her bootlaces for the fourth time.

* * *

The second day, the mountain stopped pretending to be reasonable.

The path clung to the cliffside above a valley whose river looked, from this height, like a dropped thread. There were places where the footing was a hand-width of worn stone with nothing but air and a very long education waiting below. Tam moved through it chattering. Dominic moved through it by deciding not to think, which was a skill his mother had apparently never taught him either.

Ines began to fail around noon.

He noticed it in stages, the way you notice weather. First she stopped talking. Then her pauses between steps lengthened until each footfall became its own small deliberation. Then, at a rest stop against a wind-scoured boulder, he watched her take out a piece of flatbread, look at it with genuine confusion, and put it away again untouched.

"Ines."

"I'm fine." The word came out flattened, stripped of everything that usually lived inside it.

"You're not eating."

"I'm not hungry." A pause. "That's a lie. I'm so hungry I could eat this boulder, and my body has apparently decided to go on strike rather than negotiate. My head feels like someone is ringing a bell inside it. Slowly. With great patience." She pressed the heels of her hands against her temples. "Altitude sickness. I've read about it extensively. Reading turns out to be poor preparation."

Tam crouched beside her, studied her lips, her fingernails, made her count backward from twenty while walking a straight line along the ledge. She managed eleven before her balance filed a complaint.

"Not the bad kind yet," Tam pronounced. "But the bad kind is rude and doesn't announce itself. We go slower. She drinks more than she wants to. And somebody carries her pack."

"I can carry my own—"

"You can carry your own pack down the mountain," Tam said pleasantly, "or I can carry it up the mountain, or he can carry it up the mountain, but you are not carrying it up the mountain, because pride weighs more than gear and you cannot afford both."

There was a silence. Ines looked at the pack, at the trail ahead, at the drop beyond the trail, and Dominic watched a woman who had spent her whole life being the most competent person in every room confront the arithmetic of her own blood chemistry.

"He carries it," she said finally, to Tam, as if Dominic weren't there. "And I will hate every minute of it, and I want that noted."

"Noted," Dominic said. "Loudly noted."

* * *

Her pack was heavier than it should have been. Not just gear — though there was plenty of that, including a number of items Dominic suspected no legitimate expedition required, such as a jeweler's loupe and what appeared to be a very old, very illegal sextant — but also books. Actual books. Two of them, in waterproof wrapping: a battered field guide to Central Range flora, and a slim volume of poetry in Italian with a cracked spine.

"You climb with poetry?" he asked that evening, when they'd made camp early, tucked into a fold of rock that cut the wind.

"It's Marvell," she said, from inside her sleeping bag, where she lay looking gray and furious at her own body. "'Had we but world enough, and time.' One likes to know what one is refusing." She closed her eyes. "My mother's translation. She did Marvell badly and beautifully. I keep meaning to throw it out."

"Why don't you?"

"Because then I would be the sort of person who throws things out." Her voice was going slurred with exhaustion. "Like your mother. Who threw out an entire son."

Dominic sat with that. The fire was small — Tam's rules about fires above the treeline ran to a single page, and the page said *no* — and the cold came up through the ground pad like slow gossip.

"She didn't throw me out," he said, after a while. "She just... never picked me up."

"Mm." Ines's eyes stayed closed. "I knew her thirty years, Dominic. I competed with her for grants, for digs, for a chair we both wanted and neither got. I hated her efficiently and at scale. And in thirty years she never once told me about you. I learned your name from a funding application — dependents, line four. Four words. *Son, Dominic Zhou, age nine.*" She opened her eyes then, and even sick and shrunken against the rock, her gaze had its old scalpel quality. "A woman who hides a child in line four of a grant application is not a woman who trusts anyone. I used to think it meant she trusted no one. Now I wonder if it meant she trusted everyone to hurt you."

The wind moved over the rocks above them, making its long dry sound.

"Go to sleep," Dominic said, because he had nothing else, and because her breathing had gone ragged again.

"I intend to. Wake me if I stop being clever. It would mean the edema's worsening." A pause. "That was a joke, Dominic. Mostly."

* * *

The third day was the worst day.

The path left the cliffside and committed itself to the sky, a series of switchbacks up a shoulder of bare stone where the wind came off the icefields with the personal animosity of a creditor. Ines made it to the second switchback before her legs simply declined further service. She sat down on the trail with the terrible calm of someone who has stopped arguing with reality, and when Dominic knelt in front of her, her lips had gone a faint, wrong blue at the edges.

"Descent," Tam said quietly. "Or I carry her down and we lose the season."

"We don't have a season to lose," Ines said. Each word cost her. "Voss is two days behind us at best. If we go down, we come back up, and he comes up once." She looked at Dominic. "Leave me here with the tent and most of the food. Take the ledger. Come back for me. It's the rational allocation of resources."

"It's the allocation of resources of someone whose brain is starving," Dominic said. "No."

"Don't be sentimental. Your mother wasn't sentimental, and look what she—"

"My mother," Dominic said, "left a supply cache somewhere on this mountain dated years ago, addressed to a child she hadn't spoken to in a decade. Sentimental isn't the word I'm worried about."

He stood up, took off his own pack, and started transferring things. Water. Rope. The food bag. Her books — he hesitated over the Marvell, then stuffed it in his jacket, against his back, where it rode like a small warm debt.

"What are you doing?"

"Carrying you."

"You absolutely are not—"

"Tam," Dominic said, "how do we do this?"

"Piggyback for the steep parts, fireman's carry for the flats, and she doesn't get a vote." Tam was already rigging a loop of rope into a makeshift seat harness with the brisk cheer of a man enjoying himself far too much. "Smuggler's rule: the mountain decides who walks. Today it decided you."

"This is undignified," Ines announced, as Dominic crouched and she, swaying, arranged herself against his back with the rigid horror of a duchess boarding a hay wagon. "I am a tenured professor. I have a named lecture series. There are people in three countries who call me *Professoressa* in tones of fear."

"Hold on," Dominic said.

"I want it minuted that I objected."

"Minuted."

Then he stood up, and the mountain came with them.

* * *

He would remember the next six hours in fragments, the way you remember any ordeal — as sensation without narrative. The weight of her, lighter than he'd feared and still enormous, her breath hot and too fast against the back of his neck. The rhythm Tam set from ahead, a smuggler's counting song in a language Dominic didn't know, four beats to a step, step-step-step-rest. The way the trail narrowed until there was only his own two boots and forty fathoms of nothing to the left, and how he stopped looking left. The sun crossing the sky like something indifferent. His thighs turning to wet sand. His lungs billing him hourly.

Somewhere in the middle of it, half-delirious, he started talking. He didn't decide to. It just came out, words falling into the rhythm of the climb.

"She taught me constellations," he said. "When I was little. Before. She had this terrible habit of answering questions exactly right, so you learned not to ask unless you wanted the full lecture. I asked why stars move and got forty minutes on celestial mechanics delivered to a seven-year-old in a blanket fort." His boots found the next hold. Step. Step. Rest. "But she never once said *I love you* in a normal voice. It was always sideways. Always in the work. 'Check your knots' meant it. 'Eat something' meant it. I spent twenty years reading her field notes trying to find a sentence that just said it plainly."

"And did you?" Ines murmured against his shoulder. She was more lucid now — the descent of a few hundred meters had given her brain back some of its oxygen, enough to be present for his humiliation.

"No. That's the thing. I haven't found it yet. Maybe it's in the vault. Maybe it's nowhere." Step. Step. Rest. "Maybe she wrote it in cipher."

"Mei-Lin encrypted everything," Ines said softly. "Including, I suspect, her affections. It wasn't cruelty. It was—" She searched. "It was a woman who believed anything stated plainly could be taken from you. So she hid the true things where only someone willing to work for them would ever find them." A pause. "She hid you in line four, Dominic. But she *listed* you. On a document that would outlive her. In her own hand. That may be the plainest sentence she ever wrote."

Dominic climbed. The wind pushed. His legs burned and held, burned and held, and the strange thought came to him, unbidden, that this was the closest he had ever been to his mother — not in memory, not in her notes, but here, doing the thing she had done her whole life: putting one foot above the other toward something buried, carrying someone who couldn't ask to be carried.

Endurance as devotion. He didn't know where the phrase came from. It arrived complete, like a gift from a stranger, and he carried it up the mountain along with everything else.

* * *

They made the col an hour before dark.

It opened suddenly, the way high places do — the trail crested a spine of rock and the world fell away on both sides, a saddle of frozen grass and shattered stone between two black shoulders of mountain, wind screaming across it from the icefields beyond. And there, wedged under an overhang that some earlier traveler had clearly used before, they found the cairn.

Not the black-stone cairn of the ledger — that was days and a valley away. This was smaller, older, a marker of stacked slabs with a rusted tin box wired beneath it, and Tam went to it immediately, because smugglers can smell a cache the way sharks smell blood.

"Someone's been here," Tam said. He turned the tin over. Scratched into the bottom, in a script Dominic knew better than his own heartbeat by now — the neat, compressed hand from the ledger margins, from the cipher keys, from thirty years of field notebooks —

*For the route. Take only what the winter takes from you.*

His mother's handwriting.

Ines, wrapped in both sleeping bags and propped against the overhang, color returning to her face in ugly blotches, stared at it for a long moment. Then she started to laugh — a thin, disbelieving, altitude-cracked laugh that ended in something that was not laughing at all.

"Years ago," she said. "She cached supplies on this route years ago. Which means she planned this. Which means she walked this path. Which means—" She stopped. Looked at Dominic, kneeling in the frozen grass with his hands still shaking from the weight of her, and finished quietly: "Which means she knew exactly what this climb would cost. And she left water here anyway."

Inside the tin: two dented canisters of water, a brick of salt tablets, a bar of climbing chocolate gone white with age, and a folded scrap of paper with a single line in the same hand:

*If you've come this far, you're stubborn. Good. Stubbornness is inheritable.*

Dominic read it three times. The wind tore at the paper. Somewhere below and behind them, days back down the trail, Voss was climbing.

He put the note in his jacket, against his back, where the Marvell rode, and looked west — where the clouds stacking on the horizon had begun, very faintly, to organize themselves into something with intent.

"Told you the weather was early," Tam said, squinting at the same horizon. "We want to be under stone before that arrives. There's a snow cave formation two hours on, if the ledger's landmarks hold."

"Then we walk," Dominic said.

Ines, swaying, gripped his arm — not for support, he realized, but to stop him, and her eyes had gone wide and fixed on something past his shoulder, up-slope, where the light was failing.

"Dominic," she said. "Look at the cairn again. The scratches on the tin. They're fresh."
