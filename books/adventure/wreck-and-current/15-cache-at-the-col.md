## 15. Cache at the Col

The col looked, from below, like the gap between two teeth in the jaw of the mountain — a saddle of grey scree and stubborn grass, wind-scoured, unimpressive, and utterly essential. Everything on the Serrano Range was like that, Ines had decided somewhere around the second day of the goat path: essential and unimpressive, and trying to kill you politely.

Her boots had opinions about the climb. So did her lungs. She kept both to herself, because Dominic was carrying her pack in addition to his own, and Tam was humming something that was either a Kharvathi lullaby or a smuggler's drinking song, and neither of those men needed to hear a celebrated archaeologist complain about her feet.

"You're doing the thing," Dominic said, without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and start narrating your own suffering internally. My mother did it. You do it. It's a very academic way of being miserable."

"I am not miserable," Ines said. "I am *acclimatizing*. There is a difference, and it is measured in oxygen."

Tam snorted. "In Kharvathi, the word for altitude sickness and the word for 'the mountain arguing with you' are the same word. My grandmother said you don't beat the mountain arguing. You just stop answering."

"And what do you do when the mountain is right?" Ines asked, and immediately regretted it, because it was the kind of question that invited sincerity, and sincerity at four thousand meters was dangerous. It thinned out along with the air.

But Tam just shrugged and kept humming.

They reached the col in the last hour of usable light. The wind up there had a personal quality to it, the way it found the gap between collar and neck, the seam of glove and wrist, as though it had studied them. Dominic dropped the packs behind a fin of rock that broke the worst of it, and the three of them stood in the sudden relative calm and looked at what the col actually was.

Not just a saddle. A *place*. Someone had been here before them, a long time ago, and meant to come back.

The cairn stood three meters off the path, half-swallowed by scree, but unmistakably a cairn and not an accident of geology: stones stacked with a mason's patience, the topmost one flat and deliberate, and — Ines felt her heart do something unprofessional — carved with a single line of Kharvathi script.

"Mei-Lin's hand," Dominic said. His voice had gone strange. Flat, careful, the voice of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"You can tell that from a single carved line?"

"She had a way of cutting the *kh* that looked like she was angry at it." He crouched. "She was always angry at the alphabet. Said it was designed by people who never had to write in the wind."

Ines crouched beside him. The carving matched the cipher in the first half of the ledger — a positional notation, a key. She'd been translating the ledger's marginalia for two weeks now, and the cipher had a grammar, and the grammar had a temperament, and the temperament was unmistakably Mei-Lin Zhou's: precise, unsentimental, and secretly, embarrassingly fond of jokes.

"This is a cache marker," she said. "The line reads 'bread and rope and the rest of it.' It's a supply cache. Standard practice on long surveys — you stage provisions at passes so you're never more than a day from shelter or food. She would have—" Ines stopped.

"She would have planted this years ago," Dominic finished. "Before she stopped writing to me."

The wind filled the silence the way it filled everything up here: thoroughly, without malice, without mercy either.

Tam, with the practicality that Ines was beginning to suspect was the actual foundation of all smuggler philosophy, said, "Are we going to open it, or are we going to stand here being sad at a pile of rocks? Because I vote opening it. I'm hungry. Sad is fine but hungry is worse."

Dominic laughed — a short, startled sound, like the laugh had escaped before security could stop it — and Ines thought, not for the first time, that Tam was worth his weight in whatever the Halvard Collection would have paid for this mountain.

* * *

The cache was under the cairn's foundation stone, and the foundation stone was a liar: it looked like it weighed as much as a car, but it had been set on a pivot, and when Dominic found the lip and levered it, it swung up with a grinding courtesy that spoke of careful engineering and regular maintenance.

Inside, a zinc-lined box, and inside that, the past, packed by a woman who had never in her life packed anything carelessly.

Rope — two coils, waxed against rot, still supple when Ines tested one. A spirit stove and fuel, the canisters sealed and sound. Dried meat and hardtack in tins, and — Ines actually gasped — a tin of proper tea, the good kind, the kind you could not buy within four hundred kilometers of this mountain. A down sleeping bag compressed into a stuff sack. A first aid kit with a note in English in Mei-Lin's handwriting: *The penicillin expires before you do. Use it anyway if you must. — M.Z.*

And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the kind of knot that takes a decade to forget, a second package. Flat. Book-shaped.

Ines knew what it was before Dominic's fingers finished untying the knot. She knew it the way you know a face across a train platform — the shape of it, the weight of it, the way the light refused to leave it alone.

The second half of the ledger.

Vellum. The same hand as the first half, the same cramped, furious *kh*s. The two halves would marry along a torn edge, she could see it already, the fibers reaching for each other across four hundred kilometers and two years and one grave.

And on the oilcloth, where it had been folded over the ledger's face, written in waterproof ink in English, in handwriting that was not the ledger's handwriting — looser, warmer, written by someone who was not being an archaeologist for one moment:

*My child, if you're reading this.*

Ines looked away. It was not her letter. It was not her anything. But she had spent thirty years competing with Mei-Lin Zhou, and you could not compete with a woman for thirty years without learning to read her the way you read a difficult text — every marginal note, every silence, every word she chose not to use — and what Ines read in those six words, in the *if*, in the way the ink had pooled slightly at the *y* as if the pen had hesitated there, was more than she wanted to know and less than Dominic needed to.

Dominic read it. She watched him read it. His face did the thing his mother's face had done in the field photographs Ines had studied for decades: nothing, absolutely nothing, which is what a face does when it is holding up something very heavy.

"She wrote this," he said. His thumb moved over the ink without touching it. "When? The cache is dated—" He turned the ledger over. A survey tag was stapled inside the back cover, the kind every expedition used, with a date and a grid reference in Mei-Lin's blocky print. "Four years ago. Four years. She stopped writing to me five years ago. She was *here*, Ines. She was standing on this col, a year after she cut me off, and she was packing a box with tea and rope and a letter that says *my child*, and she still didn't—" His voice did not break. It did something worse; it went very controlled and very quiet. "She still didn't send it. She still didn't call. She packed it in a box on a mountain and left it for a version of me that might someday climb high enough to find it."

"Maybe she couldn't," Ines said.

"Maybe she *wouldn't*."

"Those are not always different things, with her." Ines heard her own voice and was surprised by it — gentle, unarmored. She had not planned on gentle. "You have to understand what we were, Dominic. What the profession made us. Mei-Lin and I spent twenty years trying to destroy each other's careers, and I want you to know that I mean that with real affection. But the reason we could do it, the reason it worked, is that we had both agreed, without ever saying so, that the work was the only thing that couldn't be taken away. People could be. People *were*." She looked at the ledger. "She didn't stop writing to you because she stopped loving you. She stopped writing because every letter was a thread, and threads can be pulled, and there were people — there are people — who pull threads."

"Voss," Dominic said.

"Voss. The Collection. Whoever they had before Voss." She shook her head. "She told me once, drunk, at a conference in Lisbon, that the only way to keep something safe was to make it look abandoned. She was talking about a dig site. She was not talking about a dig site."

Dominic sat down on the scree with the ledger in his lap, and for a while none of them said anything, and the wind said plenty.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the fin, out of the worst wind. Tam got the stove going and produced the tea, and Ines watched him measure it out with the gravity of a man performing a sacrament, and thought that Mei-Lin would have liked him enormously and never once told him so.

"Half a ledger," Tam said, turning his tin in his hands. "My grandmother used to say the Kharvathi never trusted anything whole. A whole map could be stolen. Half a map, you needed two people, and two people have to trust each other, and trust was the one thing the purge couldn't burn." He shrugged. "She said it about recipes, mostly. But she meant everything."

"Your grandmother," Ines said, "sounds like she and Mei-Lin would have gotten on terribly."

"The best people do."

Dominic had the two halves of the ledger side by side on his knee, not reading them — Ines could tell he wasn't reading them, because reading them would require opening the oilcloth all the way, and the oilcloth was the last skin of the letter, and he wasn't ready to take the letter off. He was just looking at the torn edge, the place where the fibers reached for each other.

"It matches," he said. "The cipher continues. Coordinates. And there's more — marginalia, a whole second layer of it, denser than the first half." He looked up at Ines, and in the stove light his eyes had his mother's exact exhaustion, the exhaustion of a person who has just realized the work is not done. "You cracked the first layer. Can you crack this?"

Ines took the ledger. The vellum was cool and slightly greasy with age, and the handwriting was Mei-Lin's late period, the compressed hand she'd used after Lisbon, after whatever had happened with Voss on that dig in the northern valleys, after the fall Ines had spent years pretending she hadn't helped cause. The marginalia was a different cipher — or rather, the same cipher grown teeth, personal references layered on personal references, the key buried in shared memory the way the cache was buried in scree.

"I can try," Ines said. "It'll take time. It'll take—" She stopped. Because she had seen it. Three lines down, in the margin, in cipher, a phrase she could already half-read, because the cipher's first layer was memory and the memory was hers: a reference to a night in a hotel bar in Lisbon, two archaeologists and a bottle of vinho verde and an argument about whether provenance was a moral concept or an alibi.

Mei-Lin had written about her. In the ledger. In the margin of the map to the archive of a dead nation, Mei-Lin Zhou had written about the night she and Ines Marchetti had gotten drunk and honest, and Ines felt her face go hot at an altitude where faces had no business wasting heat.

"What?" Dominic said. "What is it?"

"Nothing. A marginal reference I need to work through." She handed the ledger back before her expression could testify against her. "Tomorrow. I need sleep and I need my hands to stop shaking from the altitude, and I need you to not watch me work, because your mother never let anyone watch her work and apparently it's genetic."

Tam grinned into his tea.

* * *

Ines took first watch, because she slept badly at altitude anyway and because she wanted, with a hunger that embarrassed her, to sit alone with the ledger for one hour without anyone's grief or hope standing behind her shoulder.

She didn't read the cipher. She read the letter. Just the six words on the oilcloth, over and over, by the light of a headlamp turned low, while the wind argued with the mountain and the mountain declined to answer.

*My child, if you're reading this.*

She had known Mei-Lin for thirty years. She had read her papers, her grant applications, her vicious peer reviews, her one and only attempt at a memoir, abandoned at chapter two and, Ines happened to know, still sitting in a drawer in a university office because neither of them could bear to be the one to throw it out. Mei-Lin Zhou wrote like a surgeon. She wrote like a woman defusing a bomb. She did not write *my child*. She did not write *if you're reading this* with a pen that hesitated.

Except she had. Here. At four thousand meters, a year after she had erased herself from her son's life, she had climbed a goat path with a box on her back and written the truest sentence of her career on a piece of oilcloth, and then she had buried it where no one would ever find it unless they were already following her, already halfway to her archive, already — this was the part Ines kept circling, the part that sat in her chest like swallowed stone — already more her heir than her child, in the sense that mattered to Mei-Lin, which was the only sense Mei-Lin had ever trusted.

*Love delivered too late to feel like love,* Ines thought. Or maybe not too late. Maybe just delivered the only way the sender could survive sending it.

She looked over at Dominic's sleeping bag. He was asleep on his side, one hand out of the bag and resting on the ledger's oilcloth, the way a child sleeps holding a thing they've been told not to lose. He had his mother's stubbornness and none of her armor, and Ines had spent two weeks telling herself she was traveling with him for the cipher, for the archive, for the science, and the lie had worn through somewhere on the goat path and she had stopped replacing it.

The wind shifted. Below them, far down the valley they'd climbed out of, she saw it — or thought she saw it, and then saw it again, and stopped doubting: a light. Small, steady, moving along the lower path with the unhurried discipline of people who did not need to hurry, because they knew the people ahead of them had nowhere to go but up.

She counted. Four lights. Maybe five.

"Ines," she said to herself, "you are getting old. Twenty years ago you'd have spotted them an hour ago."

She woke Dominic with a hand on his shoulder, gently, the way you wake someone when the news is bad and the night is cold and there is no version of this that isn't going to hurt.

He came up out of sleep the way his mother used to — instantly, completely, no fog, eyes finding hers in the dark.

"Lights," she said, and pointed.

He looked for a long time. Then he looked down at the ledger under his hand, and then back at the lights, and Ines watched him do the arithmetic every hunted person eventually has to do: what he was carrying, what it was worth, and what the people below it were willing to spend.

"Tomorrow," he said quietly, "I need to know what she wrote."

And Ines, who had spent thirty years reading Mei-Lin Zhou, heard in his voice not the son and not the heir but something new, something she suspected the ledger itself was about to finish making: a custodian. She nodded, and settled back against the rock to watch the lights climb, and the six words on the oilcloth sat in her chest all night like a coal that would not cool.
