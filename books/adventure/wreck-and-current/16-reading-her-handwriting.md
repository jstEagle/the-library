## 16. Reading Her Handwriting

The watch rotation had been Tam's idea, and like most of Tam's ideas it came wrapped in a proverb that might or might not have been real.

"Sleep when the mountain lets you," he'd said, dividing the night into thirds with a stick in the dirt. "And when it doesn't, you'll know. The mountain is rude that way."

So it fell to Dominic to take the middle watch, from roughly two in the morning until the sky at the col began to consider the color of things. Ines was asleep in the tent they'd pitched in the lee of the cache — the cache, *her* cache, *Mei-Lin's* cache, a steel box buried under twelve years of frost that had produced rope, fuel tablets, dried fruit gone dense as theology, and the second half of a ledger addressed to *my child, if you're reading this*.

Dominic had not yet read the second half.

He'd told himself it was strategy. Read it in daylight, with a clear head, with Ines there to help with the cipher. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: he'd been carrying the thing in his pack for two days like a stone he was afraid to set down, because once he set it down he'd have to look at it, and once he looked at it he'd have to know what his mother had thought worth carrying across a mountain range and burying under frost for a son she hadn't spoken to in six years.

Now it was two in the morning, the wind had dropped to a mutter, and the stone was in his hands.

He sat on the flat rock they'd been using as a watch post, a slab of granite the size of a dinner table, and unwrapped the ledger from its oilcloth. The two halves met along a torn edge like a healed fracture. His mother had kept the second half — the lower half, the one with the final coordinates and the closing pages — and mailed it, or carried it, or hidden it, years ago, before she died, before the debts, before Kestrel Bend and the dredging barge and the bronze chest rising out of the river like a held breath.

The first half he knew by heart now. Ines had cracked the cipher layer by layer on the night train east, using memories as keys — the nickname Mei-Lin had given her first field school professor, the year they'd both been humiliated at a conference in Turin, the name of a dog. *Cipher of small cruelties*, Ines had called it, not unkindly. Mei-Lin had built her lock out of the moments that had actually mattered to her, and only someone who'd been *there*, in the rooms where her life happened, could turn them.

Dominic had not been in those rooms. That had been the point, apparently.

He turned to the first page of the second half. His mother's handwriting — small, upright, infuriatingly even, like a seismograph having a good day — marched down the page in the same cipher, but the final pages, he discovered within minutes, were not cipher at all.

They were just words. Written to him.

He looked up at the valley. The dry valley below the col was a river of moonlight, the split rock a black tooth against the snow. Somewhere down there, boot prints under fresh scree. Somewhere behind them, Voss, closing at the speed of money. The sensible thing would be to wake Ines, to decode together, to be strategic.

He read instead.

*If you're reading this, then you found the first half, which means the river gave it up, which means the Collection is already moving, which means I'm sorry — that word will come up a lot, I'm told I use it like punctuation.*

Dominic laughed. It came out of him sideways, startled, a single bark into the cold, and he clapped a hand over his mouth and looked guiltily at the tent. Ines didn't stir. Tam, who claimed he could sleep through an avalanche but had once woken at the sound of someone opening a biscuit tin forty meters away, muttered something in his grandmother's language and rolled over.

*Sorry. There it is again. You'll want to know why I did it. Why I sent you to your aunt's in Kestrel Bend and stopped writing and let you grow up thinking your mother preferred dirt to you. The short answer is that I did prefer dirt, some days, because dirt doesn't ask you why you missed its birthday. But that's not the real answer, and you deserve the real one, even late. Especially late.*

He read the next part twice, because the first time his eyes slid off it like water off wax.

*In 2009 I found the outer marker of the Kharvathi archive. Within a month, the Halvard Collection knew. I never learned how — a satellite survey, a bribed porter, a man in a café who asked too many friendly questions. What I learned was what they do to people who find things. My dig partner — I won't write his name here, he has family who believe a kind version of the story — was in the trench when the "accident" happened. The Collection's version is that the slope failed. The slope did not fail. I took the blame for the shoring because the alternative was asking questions that got people killed, and I carried that fall, and then I understood something: they don't only bury people. They bury people's *people*. Anyone who loves you is a handle. Anyone who loves you is a door they can walk through.*

*You were my door, Dominic. You were the one door I had left, and I had watched what they do through doors.*

*So I made myself a bad mother on purpose. I made you angry. Anger is a kind of armor — it keeps a boy from looking for a woman who can't be found. I let the debts pile up because debts make a person small and legible and uninteresting; nobody hunts the son of a disgraced archaeologist who dredges rivers for other people's money. I wrote you out of my life the way you'd cut a trail marker out of a map, so that anyone following me would arrive at nothing.*

*It worked. You're alive, and you're angry, and I'm sorry. There's the punctuation again.*

The wind moved through the pass and the tent fabric breathed. Dominic realized his hands were cold and that he hadn't noticed, and that his face was wet, and that he had noticed that one considerably less.

*Here is what I couldn't make myself do,* she wrote. *Here is the coward's arithmetic, and I want you to see it plainly, because you should know exactly what kind of woman your mother was. I could not make myself disappear *completely*. I could cut you off, but I couldn't cut you off *all the way*. So I kept a thread. The ledger. The cache. This note. A trail marker cut from the map and hidden under my own tongue, in case — in case the armor held, in case you grew up and got angry and then, one day, got curious. Curious was the thing I bet on. You were a child who took apart the toaster. I bet on the toaster.*

Dominic looked at the sky. It was enormous and indifferent and very slightly pink at one edge, the way it got before dawn up here, and he thought: *she bet on the toaster.* He thought about the summer he was eleven and had dismantled the toaster on her kitchen table and she had come home from a dig and stood in the doorway with her pack still on and said, "Do you know why it stopped working?" and he'd said, "Because it's cheap," and she'd laughed until she had to sit down, and he'd been so pleased with himself that he hadn't noticed, then, that she'd been crying a little, or that she'd looked at him like a person looks at the last light of a country that's about to be erased.

He'd thought she was laughing at the toaster. She'd been laughing at the map, maybe. At the trail marker she'd just decided to hide under her tongue.

*I don't ask you to forgive me,* the ledger said. *Forgiveness is a door too, and I've asked enough doors of you. I ask you to understand, which is colder and more useful. Understand that I chose the work because the work was the only thing the Collection couldn't take from me without killing me, and they weren't ready to kill me yet, and I had one child and I intended to keep him.*

*The coordinates are on the next page. The archive is real. It's not gold — I need you to know that before you go, because if you go expecting gold you'll be disappointed, and if you go expecting what I expect you to find, you'll be the richest man alive and no one will ever be able to pay you. A nation put its whole memory in the ground so that no one could take it. That's the treasure. That's all of it. That's everything.*

*I leave you the choice I never had.*

The last line was written larger, the seismograph finally jumping.

Dominic sat with the ledger open on his knees for a long time. The pink at the edge of the sky widened into a bruised gold. Below the col, the dry valley lay in shadow still, holding its split rock and its black cairn and whatever the Kharvathi had built behind it, and somewhere in that shadow, presumably, men with rifles and expense accounts were also awake.

He noticed that he was waiting for something. Grief, maybe, to arrive in its proper costume. Or forgiveness, which Ines seemed to assume was the destination of all this — *you'll forgive her, you'll see, it's what people do* — and which he had been quietly declining to promise anyone, including himself.

Neither came. What came instead was smaller and stranger: he understood her.

Not the way you understand a person you love, which is mostly invention and generosity. He understood her the way he understood the toaster — mechanism, cause, the cheap part that failed. She had been given a map with one door on it and she had welded the door shut and thrown away the key and then, because she was who she was — a woman who catalogued everything, who documented everything, who kept field notes on her own heart in cipher — she had documented the welding. She had left the record of the door she'd shut, addressed to the person she'd shut it against, and mailed it into the future like a letter to a stranger she happened to be raising.

*She chose the work,* he'd told the village elder, and the elder had nodded as if that were an answer, and it had been, but it was only half the sentence. *She chose the work so the work wouldn't choose him.* She'd spent herself like ballast to keep the boat from capsizing with him aboard, and she'd never once said so, because saying so would have been a door, and doors were the one thing she'd sworn off.

He didn't forgive her. He tested the thought, turned it over, held it up to the coming light, and found it solid: he did not forgive her. Forgiveness would mean the anger was over, and the anger was the truest thing he had left of her — she'd built it herself, by hand, with the same care she'd built the cipher. But the anger had changed shape in the night. It was no longer a wall he was standing in front of. It was more like the mountain itself now: something he could be angry at forever and still walk up.

"You're supposed to be watching the valley," said Ines's voice behind him, rough with sleep, "not watching the sunrise. Although the sunrise is also very nice."

He hadn't heard the tent flap. He closed the ledger, too late, and she was already looking at it, and her face did something complicated — the rival's face, the woman who had spent thirty years measuring herself against Mei-Lin Zhou and losing on points.

"You read it," she said.

"The last pages aren't cipher."

"No," Ines said quietly. "They wouldn't be. She'd have wanted those legible even if the Collection got the first half. Legible to anyone." She came and sat on the edge of the rock, folding her long frame down with the economy of someone who'd slept in worse places. "Well? Was it worth the frostbite risk?"

"She sent me away to keep them from using me. The debts, the silence, all of it. It was a strategy." He heard his own voice and disliked its flatness. "Eighteen years of thinking I wasn't worth a phone call, and it was a strategy."

Ines was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, she spoke the way she decoded ciphers — carefully, testing each word before committing to it.

"I knew her for thirty years, Dominic. She was the most honest dishonest person I ever met. She would lie to your face about the route and be right about the route. She would lie to a funding committee and be right about the funding. I hated her for it and I trusted her completely, and I never once managed to do both at the same time, which I believe was also a strategy." She looked out at the valley. "She was right about the Collection, you know. About what they do through doors. The man in the trench — I've heard the true version. She's not lying to you in that book. It's the first time in thirty years she isn't lying to anyone, and she saved it for you."

"She saved it for a stranger," Dominic said. "She raised a stranger and wrote to a son."

"Yes," Ines said. "That's exactly what she did. And now you get to decide what to be about it. That's the inheritance. Not the archive." She stood, knees cracking like small gunfire, and nodded at the ledger. "The next page. The coordinates. Read them to me."

He turned the page. His mother's even hand gave the final bearings: the split rock, the dry riverbed, the cairn of black stones, and then a distance and a bearing from the cairn that put the door — the sealed door Ines had been dreaming about out loud for two hundred kilometers — under the north wall of the valley, behind stone that had been pretending to be ordinary cliff for seventy years.

He read them aloud once, then again, then made Ines repeat them back, and then Tam emerged from the tent with the terrible timing of a man who slept through avalanches and woke for coordinates, and made them both say them a third time while he chewed a strip of dried fruit with the expression of a man memorizing a prayer.

And it was Tam, folding the tarp, squinting down-valley into the strengthening light, who stopped mid-chew and said, in the mild tone he reserved for genuinely bad news:

"The mountain is being rude. Look at the south sky."

They looked. Beyond the dry valley, beyond the frontier ranges, the horizon had gone a color that had no business existing at six in the morning — a stacked, bruised, architectural purple, clouds building in towers like something being constructed rather than weathered.

"The monsoon," Ines said. "Early. It's coming early."

"Everything this year comes early," Tam said. "The snows, the storm, the men with guns." He spat over the edge of the rock, a habit Dominic had stopped finding charming around day four. "We go down this morning. We reach the door before the sky closes or we don't reach it at all."

Dominic wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth and, for the first time, put it away without it feeling like a stone. It felt like a compass now. It pointed nowhere useful, and it was his.

He took the first step down toward the valley, toward the split rock and the black cairn and the door, and behind him the south sky stacked its purple towers higher, like a nation getting ready to erase something.
