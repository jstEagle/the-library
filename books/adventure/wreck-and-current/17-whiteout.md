## 17. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive. It *arrived at them*, the way a debt collector arrives — first a polite knock of wind off the eastern ridge, then the whole mountain leaning on the door.

Tam had seen weather like this twice in his life. Once on the frontier river, when it took his uncle's boat and half his uncle's temper. And once in his grandmother's kitchen, when she described Kharvath's last winter and stopped mid-sentence, and never picked the sentence back up.

"Snow cave," he said. "Now. Not in ten minutes. Now."

Dominic looked up at a sky that had gone the color of dishwater, then down at Ines, who was walking like a woman arguing with her own knees. He didn't argue. That was the thing Tam had learned about Dominic Zhou in three weeks of mountains: he argued with everything except the facts.

They dug into the drift below a granite overhang with their ice axes and a cooking pot, which was undignified and effective, and by the time the whiteout closed like a fist they had a chamber barely big enough for three people, one stove, and the accumulated grievances of a very long climb.

* * *

Two days is a long time to be inside your own breath.

Tam took first watch because he always took first watch; sleeping in caves required a trust in other people's vigilance that he had never managed to acquire. The storm made its opinions known against the entrance — a long white roar, occasionally punctuated by the deep cracking reports of snow settling on itself, like the mountain clearing its throat before saying something unpleasant.

Rations were the arithmetic nobody wanted to do out loud. They had planned for five more days of food and the storm had just eaten two of them without chewing. Ines counted the bars twice, as if the second count might be more generous than the first. It wasn't. She put them back in the pack with the careful gentleness of someone handling something wounded.

"We cut to half rations," she said finally. "And we melt snow for water, which costs fuel, so we run the stove short."

"I've been hungry before," Dominic said.

"Everyone says that until day three," Tam said from the entrance. "Day three, hunger stops being a feeling and becomes a personality."

Ines snorted despite herself. It was, Tam thought, the first honest sound she'd made in a day and a half. Since the col, since the cache, since the second half of the ledger had appeared addressed to *my child, if you're reading this* — since then, Ines Marchetti had been moving through the world like a woman carrying a full cup across an icy floor. Careful. Watchful. Afraid of spilling something she couldn't name.

Dominic hadn't talked much either. He'd read the completed ledger by headlamp for most of one evening, alone at the edge of the firelight, turning pages with hands that were steady in the way hands are steady when steadiness is being performed. Then he'd folded it away and said only: "We go up." And they had gone up, into this.

Now the mountain had voted otherwise.

* * *

By the second morning — if you could call the gray nothing that leaked through the snow wall a morning — the stillness had gotten to all of them. Ines was re-reading her own field notes as though they might contain a plot twist. Dominic was sharpening an axe that did not need sharpening. Tam watched them both and understood that he was going to have to do the thing he hated most, which was talk.

Not about routes or rations or the mercenary army presumably digging itself out of some valley two days behind them. About the other thing. The thing his grandmother had left him the way Mei-Lin Zhou had left Dominic a bronze chest: heavy, wrapped, no receipt.

"You know why I came on this trip," Tam said. "Really?"

"Money," said Ines.

"Grudges," said Dominic, at the same moment.

They looked at each other. Tam raised a finger. "Both correct. But underneath the money and the grudges there's a third item, and my grandmother put it there, and I've been carrying it up this mountain without telling either of you what it weighs."

Ines set down her notes. Dominic set down the axe. Good. Audience acquired.

"My grandmother's name was Sefa," Tam said. "Before she was my grandmother she was a girl in a city called Vellis-Karu, which was the capital of Kharvath, which was a country, once, the way a tree is a tree right up until someone cuts it down and denies it ever shaded anything."

He fed the stove a measured pinch of fuel and let the flame settle.

"In '53 the purge came. You know the shape of it — every country that's ever been erased has the same shape. First they come for the ministers. Then the professors. Then anyone who can spell the old alphabet. My grandmother was sixteen. Her father was a mapmaker — a real one, the kind who surveyed, not the kind who flatters borders. Which meant he was on a list, and lists in those years were not things you wanted to be on."

The wind screamed against the entrance. Nobody moved.

"There was a network. Scholars, mostly. People who understood that a country doesn't die when its government falls — it dies when its memory does. So they hid things. Books. Seeds. Recordings. Maps of places that were about to be renamed into oblivion. And they moved them, piece by piece, into the high country, into the range we are currently sitting inside of, waiting out a storm like a couple of tourists."

"The archive," Dominic said quietly.

"The archive," Tam agreed. "My grandmother didn't know what she was carrying. She knew she was sixteen years old, and her father put a satchel on her back and told her: *You are the courier now. Whatever happens to us, walk west, follow the river that runs backward, and don't open the bag.*"

He paused. The stove hissed.

"She used to tell it funny, you know. That's the part people don't understand about escape stories. She'd do voices. The border guard who waved her through because he thought she was a farm girl bringing cheese to market — she'd puff up her cheeks and do this voice—" Tam puffed his own cheeks, dropped his register into a gravelly bass, "'*Cheese, is it? Fine, fine, move along, my own wife sends me for cheese and comes back with nothing but complaints.*' Sixteen years old, a nation's whole memory in a satchel on her back, and the guard worried about cheese."

Ines laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and then immediately guilty about it, and Tam held up a hand.

"No. Laugh. She insisted on it. She said, *if you tell it grim, they'll remember the fear. If you tell it true, they'll remember the girl.* The fear was real — she crossed the frontier river at night in a leaky boat with a man named Odess who charged her family everything they had left and then, halfway across, demanded her boots as well. She gave him the boots. She walked the last miles barefoot in November snow. But she also — and this is the part, this is the part she made me promise — she also *kept going*. Barefoot, bootless, orphaned by the time spring came, and she kept going. Because the bag was heavier than the grief, and somebody had to carry it."

Silence, except the storm.

"So when I saw that seal on the chest Dominic hauled out of the river," Tam said, "I recognized it the way you recognize a face in a crowd that you haven't seen since a funeral. And when I found out where the trail led — up here, into the range — I told myself it was about money. Money is easier to say out loud than *my grandmother walked barefoot so that this would still exist, and I would like to see it exist*."

He looked at the snow wall, at the white roar beyond it.

"That's my country," he said simply. "I've never stood in it. It doesn't appear on maps. It lives in my grandmother's kitchen, in a story with a cheese guard and a boot thief, and in a satchel I've never seen. But it's mine. I carry it. Everybody in this cave is carrying somebody's country — that's what I realized somewhere around kilometer forty of the goat path, watching the two of you suffer. Dominic's hauling Kestrel Bend debts and a mother's silence. Ines is hauling—" he gestured politely— "whatever it is you're hauling, which appears to be heavy."

"Forty years of competing with a dead woman," Ines said, after a moment. "That's what I'm hauling. And finding out, at the col, that she trusted me enough to plant a cache I might someday need." Her mouth twisted. "It's a terrible weight, being forgiven posthumously. There's no way to say thank you."

"No," Tam agreed. "But there's a way to carry it forward. That's the whole technology of it. That's what the scholars of Kharvath figured out. You can't save a country. You can only keep handing it to the next pair of feet."

Dominic had been quiet the entire time. Tam had learned to read his silences — there was the suspicious silence, the stubborn silence, the I'm-doing-arithmetic-about-our-odds-of-survival silence. This one was none of those. This one was the silence of a man standing in front of a shelf, looking for the book that explains him.

"They're all couriers," Dominic said finally. "My mother. Your grandmother. Me, apparently. Half the ledger, second half, cache, confession — it's all just one delivery that keeps getting handed off." He rubbed his face. "She sent me away, Tam. Twenty years of birthdays I spent deciding she didn't want me. And the ledger says she was keeping the Collection's eyes on herself instead of me. She made herself the decoy and let her own son believe he was abandoned, because a boy with a grudge asks fewer questions than a boy with a secret."

"That's not love done right," Ines said carefully. "But it might be love done at cost."

"It's love done by an archaeologist," Tam said. "Everything buried, labeled, and dated. My grandmother was the same — she never once said *I love you* straight out. She said, *eat, you're thin*. She said, *wear the good coat, the river takes the careless*. Forty years I thought she was a cold woman. Then she died, and I found a box with my baby teeth in it. Every single one. Labeled. Dated. Kept." He shrugged. "Some people bury the treasure where nobody thinks to dig. Doesn't mean it isn't treasure."

Dominic laughed — a short, cracked sound, half grief, and Tam decided to count it as a win.

* * *

The storm owned the second day entirely. They ran the stove in short, rationed breaths, melted just enough snow, ate half portions and lied cheerfully about how full they felt. Ines taught them a counting game from her childhood in Trieste that was clearly designed to distract children during air raids, which explained its grim efficiency. Dominic lost at it consistently, which improved everyone's mood considerably.

Somewhere in the gray afternoon — if it was afternoon; the cave had abolished time along with everything else — Dominic asked, "Your grandmother. Did she ever want to go back? To see it again?"

"Back where?" Tam said. "There's no *there*. That's the trick of an erased country. You can't go home to a place that's been renamed, repopulated, and re-explained. She went back once, in the seventies, stood in what used to be Vellis-Karu, and said the streets smelled wrong. Same cobblestones. Wrong smell. A country isn't stone, she said. It's a smell, a joke, a way of arguing. All of it lived in her now. She *was* the address."

"And when she died?"

"The story came to me. Boots, cheese guard, satchel, all of it." Tam poked the coals. "Which is why I'm here, in case either of you was still wondering. Not for the money. Well — some for the money. But mostly because somewhere under all this ice there's a room my grandmother's friends filled so that a country could refuse to die. And I'd like to stand in it once before the mountain or the monsoon or Mr. Voss decides otherwise. I'd like to be, for five minutes, a citizen."

Ines was quiet for a while. Then she said, "When we get there — if we get there — I'll show you the cartography cylinders first. If the vault is what the ledger promises, your grandmother's colleagues' work will be in it. Survey maps. Real ones. The kind that don't flatter borders."

Tam found, to his considerable annoyance, that his eyes had gone hot. He blamed the altitude. He blamed the smoke. He blamed the storm, loudly and internally, and said only, "See that you do."

* * *

On the second night the wind changed key.

Tam heard it first — he always heard it first; a lifetime on rivers gives you ears for when water and weather switch intentions. The scream against the entrance dropped a register, went ragged, began coming in gusts instead of walls.

"Breaking up," he said, and crawled to the gap, and scraped a peephole through the ice with his knife.

Stars. Actual stars, hard and close, the kind you only get after a storm has scrubbed the sky raw. The ridge opposite stood black and clean under new snow, waist-deep in the gullies, hip-deep in the drifts. Beautiful. Lethal. Open — for maybe a day, maybe less, before the next system stacked up behind this one.

"We move at first light," Tam said. "While the window holds."

Ines joined him at the gap. He felt her go stiff beside him, and followed her gaze down-valley — south, the direction they'd come from, the direction pursuit would come from.

Far below, tiny as grains of rice, lights. Moving. Not campfire light. Headlamps, strung out in a line, climbing.

Counting them was easy. Counting them was the problem.

"Seven," Ines breathed. "Maybe eight."

Tam scraped the hole wider and looked longer. The line of lights was still hours behind, still down where the storm had hit hardest — but they were moving, and they were moving well, spaced like professionals, unhurried as a debt.

Behind him, Dominic had come awake without a word, the way he did, and was already packing.

"Well," Tam said, and found his grandmother's voice rising in his throat, her escape-story voice, the one that made the unbearable tellable. "Barefoot in November, my grandmother would have said. At least we have boots."

Nobody laughed. But nobody needed to. Outside, the stars burned over a mountain full of buried countries, and seven points of light climbed patiently through the snow toward everything they loved.
