## 18. The Turn Back Offered

Ines Marchetti had spent thirty-one years in the field, and she had a private taxonomy of cold. There was working cold, which sharpened you. There was stupid cold, which came from vanity — refusing the extra layer because someone younger was watching. And then there was this: the deep, patient cold of the high Serrano passes at the tail end of the season, which did not sharpen or flatter anyone, and which had gotten into her left hand sometime during the second day of the col crossing and simply decided to stay.

She looked at it now — the fingers swollen, the nails gone a bruised violet, the skin waxy as candle drippings — and she thought, with the detached professionalism that had carried her through four continents and two divorces, *that is not a hand anymore. That is a liability wearing a glove.*

They had made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, if "camp" was the word for three people huddled against a rock with a stove that Tam had coaxed into life through what he called "negotiation" and what everyone else would have called "threats." The wind came off the icefield above them in long, flat shoves, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Your hand," Dominic said.

He was crouched across the stove from her, feeding it slivers of fuel tablet with the careful economy of a man who had learned that everything up here was finite — fuel, food, patience, time. He looked terrible. They all looked terrible. But there was a particular quality to the way Dominic Zhou looked terrible these days, a kind of forward lean, as though his body were already walking toward something his mind hadn't announced yet.

"My hand," Ines agreed, "is writing its resignation letter."

"Let me see it."

"Don't be maternal. It doesn't suit you. You have your mother's jaw and none of her bedside manner."

"That's fair," he said, and held out his own hands anyway, palms up, an offer rather than an order, and Ines — who had built a career on never accepting help she could avoid by suffering — found herself peeling off the ruined glove and putting her hand into his son-of-Mei-Lin warmth because the alternative was admitting she couldn't feel the difference.

The fingers were worse out of the glove. He went still when he saw them, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, and arrive at the same sum.

"How bad?" Tam asked from behind them, not looking up from the stove. Tam's gift was asking the important questions in the tone of a man asking about dinner.

"Frostbite," Ines said. "Proper. Second degree, possibly flirting with third. I've seen it on porters in the Karakoram. It wants warmth, elevation loss, and about a week of doing absolutely nothing with it."

"And we have," Tam said, stirring the pot, "none of those things."

"We have one of them," Ines said. "Elevation loss. If we go down."

*There it is,* she thought. She'd been building to it since the col, since the cache, since she'd stood in the snow reading the address on the second half of the ledger — *my child, if you're reading this* — and felt something twist in her chest that she refused to examine until she'd had a hot drink and several thousand vertical meters between herself and it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rivalry: it is a form of intimacy. You cannot hate a stranger properly. Hatred at that pitch requires years of shared conferences, stolen grants, dueling footnotes, the slow accumulation of every way another person has of being wrong in public. Ines had known Mei-Lin Zhou for twenty-two years, and for nineteen of them she had kept a mental ledger of her own — debts, insults, the 2009 Val d'Orcia paper, the grant committee of 2014, the time Mei-Lin had said, at a reception in Lisbon, with a small smile Ines could still summon on demand, *"Ines is very good at finishing things other people have started."*

And then Mei-Lin had died, and the ledger had turned out to be the only thing Ines had left of her, and it weighed exactly nothing, and she had carried it across half a mountain range anyway.

So she knew things about Mei-Lin that Dominic didn't. She knew how Mei-Lin planned retreats — meticulously, early, and then ignored them. She knew that Mei-Lin's answer to a closing window was always to look for a door, and that her answer to a locked door was to become extremely interested in the wall beside it. And she knew — because she had watched it happen to better-funded expeditions than theirs — exactly what the southern approach to the range looked like after the monsoon arrived, which was to say: not an approach at all. A wall of water and landslide, six weeks minimum, sometimes the whole season.

"The clouds are stacking," she said, nodding south, where the horizon had gone the color of wet slate. "You can see the front building over the low country. That's not weather, Dominic. That's a deadline. Three days, maybe four, and the southern valleys close. Not get-difficult close. Get-unreachable close. Every year somebody tries to beat it, and every year the helicopters go looking for them in spring."

"So we don't take the southern approach back."

"There is no other approach back. That's what *approach* means." She pulled her hand free of his — gently; it cost her something — and wrapped it again in the spare sock she'd repurposed as a bandage. "Listen to me. I am telling you this as the person on this mountain with the most experience being wrong in expensive ways. We have the ledger. Both halves. We have coordinates. We have your mother's notes. The archive isn't going anywhere — it's waited seventy years under the ice, it will wait until spring. We go down now, while my hand can still hold a rope and Tam's boat — " she gestured at the man, who raised his pot in acknowledgment — "and we come back in the first clear window with a proper team, permits, satellite phones, and a doctor who isn't me pretending to be one."

"You'd come back?" Dominic said. "With us?"

"With *me*, obviously. Whether you come is your affair." But her voice caught on the last word, betraying her, and she covered it by drinking tea that was mostly hot water with ambitions.

Tam spoke without looking up. "For the record, the smuggler's routes south close with the rain too. My grandmother's maps are good but they are not waterproof. After the monsoon starts, the rivers down there stop being rivers and start being opinions."

"Thank you, Tam."

"I'm not taking a side. I'm saying the mountain is going to make this decision for us in about seventy-two hours, and mountains are bad negotiators."

Ines leaned forward. The stove light moved on Dominic's face, and she saw — really saw, for the first time since the cache — how young he was underneath all that exhaustion. Twenty-eight, maybe. She'd been twenty-eight once, on her first dig, so certain that the ground owed her something.

"Dominic," she said, and made her voice as gentle as she knew how, which was not very, but she tried. "I knew your mother for twenty-two years. I competed with her for twenty of them. And I am telling you that Mei-Lin Zhou, if she were sitting where I'm sitting, with my hand and my frostbite and my very reasonable argument, would say exactly what I'm saying. She calculated retreats better than anyone I ever worked with. She just hated using them. Don't confuse loving her work with having to die of it."

Something crossed his face then — fast, quickly filed away, but she caught it, because she had spent two decades learning to read Mei-Lin's face and the son wore the same equipment.

"She sent the second half to a cache," he said quietly. "Dated it. She climbed to that col, alone, years ago, and buried supplies and half a ledger addressed to a child she hadn't spoken to in four years. She wasn't planning a retreat, Ines. She was planning a delivery." He looked up. "I'm not going to stand at the top of her delivery route and decide the postage was too expensive."

"The postage," Ines said, "is going to be paid in fingers. Possibly in more than fingers. Look at my hand, Dominic. Really look at it. Now imagine it's yours, on the descent, holding the rope that keeps you off the glacier below the moraine. I'm not being dramatic. I'm being arithmetic."

"You're being scared."

"Yes!" It came out louder than she intended, flattened immediately by the wind, but it was out, hanging in the cold between them. "Of course I'm scared. I'm sixty-one years old, I'm half a mountain from anywhere, my hand is dying by degrees, and the man I'm following is chasing a ghost into a valley his mother may have marked on a map she never finished. Fear is the appropriate response. Fear is the *professional* response. The people who aren't afraid up here are the ones the helicopters look for."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into a register she hadn't heard from him before — not angry, not stubborn. Something older and sadder.

"You want to know what I had, before the river gave me that chest?" He didn't wait for an answer. "A storage unit full of my mother's life, which I was selling by the box to pay her debts. An apartment I've now lost. A job dredging mud for a man who calls me 'the professor's boy.' That's the inventory, Ines. That's everything. And then a river hands me a bronze box with a dead country's seal on it, and it turns out my mother — the woman who couldn't return a phone call — trusted me with the only thing she ever thought was worth protecting." He shook his head slowly. "The archive is the first thing that has ever been mine. Not hers. Mine. Given to me. On purpose. And you're asking me to walk away from it and come back in the spring, and maybe I believe you about the spring, I *want* to believe you, but I have spent four years believing that things postponed were things lost. So no. I'm sorry. I'm going down into that valley tomorrow, and you can come, or you can go south, and either way I'll understand."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above them, the icefield boomed — a serac calving, the mountain clearing its throat.

* * *

Tam, diplomatically, went to check the rope anchors, which did not need checking, and which he checked anyway for a long time.

Ines sat with her ruined hand in her lap and let herself feel the full, humiliating weight of what she was actually feeling, which was not fear for Dominic. Or not only. It was this: that she recognized him. That the boy crouched over the stove with his jaw set like a door slamming was doing, beat for beat, the thing Mei-Lin used to do — the thing that had ended their friendship twice and should have ended it permanently, the absolute refusal to be rescued from her own decisions. Ines had spent twenty years telling anyone who'd listen that Mei-Lin Zhou was reckless, arrogant, constitutionally incapable of retreat. She had told herself it was criticism.

Sitting here, watching the son refuse her rescue in the exact cadence of the mother, she finally understood it had been grief the whole time. She had wanted, with a ferocity that embarrassed her even now, to be the person Mei-Lin listened to. Just once. Just once, to win an argument that mattered.

And here was the universe, offering her the rematch with the wrong contestant.

"Dominic." Her voice came out rougher than she meant. She started again. "I need to say something, and then I'll shut up, and you can make your decision with all the information, which is more courtesy than your mother ever showed me."

He looked up warily.

"In 2011, in the Tavşan Valley, Mei-Lin took a route I told her not to take. A river crossing, after rain. She lost most of our finds and nearly drowned, and when she crawled out on the bank she laughed. Actually laughed. And I stood there screaming at her about responsibility and protocol, and she said — " Ines closed her eyes; the words were still perfectly preserved, the way wounds preserve what cut them — "she said, '*Ines, some things are worth losing everything else for, and the tragedy is that you never get to know which ones until afterward.*' And I have hated that sentence for thirteen years, because it's true, and because she was usually right about which ones, and I was usually right about the cost, and neither of us ever once changed the other's mind." She opened her eyes. "I'm not going to change yours. I know that now. I knew it at the bus depot, honestly, and I came along anyway, and I'd like the record to show that this was my choice and I am not blaming you for my hand."

"Ines — "

"But hear the cost before you pay it." She held up the purple fingers, one by one, like a teacher counting a class. "Tomorrow I descend slower than you'll want to go. My grip is compromised, which means on any roped section, Tam carries double. If the weather turns early — not at seventy-two hours, but tomorrow, because weather lies — we will not outrun it, and we will dig in, and we will hope, and hoping is not a plan, it's a prayer with rations. Those are the terms. I'm not withdrawing them to make you comfortable. Your mother never withdrew terms to make anyone comfortable, and apparently it's hereditary."

Dominic looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — tired, crooked, and so much like Mei-Lin in that instant that Ines's chest hurt.

"Those are acceptable terms," he said. "And Ines? For what it's worth — she talked about you. At the end. In the ledger margin, in cipher. She wrote, '*M. will argue with every step and be right about half of them. Bring her anyway.*'"

Ines stared at him. The wind shoved at the tent-less camp. Somewhere in her chest, a ledger twenty-two years in the keeping quietly balanced itself, and she discovered she had no idea what to do with the change.

"*Bring her anyway,*" she repeated. Her voice betrayed her completely this time, and she let it. "That insufferable woman. That absolutely insufferable — she couldn't have written *I trust her*? One plain sentence? She had to encrypt her affection like state secrets?"

"She encrypted everything," Dominic said gently. "It was the only language she felt safe in."

Tam returned from the ropes he had not needed to check and squatted by the stove, reading the air between them with the skill of a man who had grown up around negotiations that could turn fatal. "So," he said. "We're going down into the dry valley. Tomorrow. With one and a half hands, dwindling food, and a storm on our heels."

"Yes," said Dominic.

"Obviously," said Ines, wiping her face with her sleeve in a manner that fooled no one.

"Good," said Tam, and ladled out three portions of soup that were mostly hot water with ambitions, and grinned at Ines's expression. "Great minds."

* * *

They broke camp in the grey hour before dawn, when the cold is honest and the light makes no promises. Ines flexed her bandaged hand inside its mitt — the fingers answered, sluggishly, like reluctant employees — and looked south one last time.

The clouds had stacked higher overnight. Over the low country the sky had gone the color of a bruise coming up, vast and organized, the monsoon assembling itself with the patience of something that had never once lost a negotiation. By tonight it would reach the foothills. By tomorrow, the southern valleys would begin to drown.

Which meant, she realized, watching Dominic fold the ledger into the inner pocket against his chest, that no one was behind them anymore. Voss and his men — two days back, last they could tell — were about to be sealed on the wrong side of the season, same as everyone. Whatever happened next would happen without witnesses, without rescue, without retreat.

The mountain had made its decision after all. It had simply waited for them to make it first.

Dominic turned east, toward the split rock and the dry riverbed and whatever his mother had hidden in the rain's shadow, and Ines pulled her collar up, put her half a hand on the rope, and followed him down into the valley — arguing with every step, exactly as instructed, and right about half of it.
