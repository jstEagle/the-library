## 19. Descent Into the Dry Valley

The rain began an hour before they reached the ridge, and stopped the moment they crossed it.

Dominic stood at the top of a long scree slope and watched the weather simply give up. Behind them, to the south, the monsoon had built itself a gray cathedral of cloud, thunder muttering somewhere in its nave. Ahead, the valley lay in hard sunlight and dust, so dry that the air itself seemed to have been wrung out and hung up to dry. It was like stepping through a curtain from one country into another — except the other country was a held breath.

"That's it," Ines said quietly. "The rain shadow."

She said it the way other people said *hallelujah*, Dominic thought. He'd spent three days carrying her pack down off the col while she recovered from altitude sickness, and in that time he'd learned that Ines Marchetti experienced landscape as argument. Every valley was a claim she intended to test. Every mountain range was a rival scholar she intended to beat in print.

But even she went quiet looking down at this one.

It ran north to south for as far as they could see, a long pale scar between two walls of dark rock, and nothing grew in it. Not sparse, not struggling — nothing. The riverbed that should have fed it had been dry so long it had forgotten how to be a river. From this height it looked like a vein of silver someone had scraped clean.

"The ledger says the water left on purpose," Tam said behind them. He'd come up last, as always, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had grown up reading rivers and had never quite forgiven mountains for being worse conversationalists. "My grandmother's songs talk about valleys like this. Places the old country kept things by keeping everything else away."

"Your grandmother's songs," Ines said, "are turning out to be better field documentation than half the journals I've reviewed."

"I'll tell her you said so. She'll be insufferable." Tam shifted his pack. "She's been dead eleven years."

"Then she can be insufferable from wherever dead grandmothers go, which I suspect is directly behind me, editing."

Despite everything — the burned granary still sharp behind his eyes, the elder's hand pointing up-valley at a cost none of them had asked her to pay — Dominic felt something loosen in his chest. Three days of thin air and thin rations had worn them down to their essential shapes: Ines's ferocious competence, Tam's dry patience, and whatever he himself was now, which he suspected was a man who had stopped asking whether he belonged on this mountain and started simply walking.

He took out the ledger.

It had survived the whiteout, the snow cave, the climb, and his own hands, which had developed a protective relationship with it that bordered on religious. He'd wrapped it in oilcloth inside his jacket, against his chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. He unwrapped it now and turned to the page they'd read a dozen times on the goat path, the one with the valley sketched in his mother's small, precise hand.

*Descend the western wall where the rock splits,* she had written. *Follow the dry bed south until the black stones. The door keeps itself.*

"Split rock first," he said. "Then the riverbed. Then the cairn."

"And then a door that 'keeps itself,'" Ines said. "Which is either a translation artifact or your mother being dramatic."

"She was never dramatic about field notes." He looked at the sketch again. "She was dramatic about everything else. But not that."

* * *

The descent took most of the afternoon.

The western wall did split — a vertical fissure wide enough to walk through, exactly where the ledger placed it, as if the mountain had read the same book. Inside the split the air cooled by ten degrees and the light went the color of weak tea. Their boots rang on stone that hadn't heard footsteps in a long time, and Dominic found himself listening past the echo, listening for anything underneath it.

Nothing. Just rock, and the three of them breathing.

At the bottom, the dry riverbed opened up like a road. And it had been a road, once — Tam spotted it first, crouching over a stretch of bank where the stones were laid in courses too regular to be accident.

"Cobbles," he said. "Under the silt. Someone paved this river when it still had water in it."

"A procession route?" Ines guessed. She'd gone into what Dominic privately called her archive voice — clipped, fast, hungry. "Or a supply line. If you're building a vault under a rain-shadow valley, you need to move material in somehow, and the dry season gives you—"

"Ines." Tam was still crouched. "Come look at this."

She went. Dominic went. And there, half-buried in the silt at the edge of the paving, was a fragment of bronze no bigger than a coin, green with age, stamped with a seal Dominic now knew better than his own signature.

Kharvath. Right where his mother's map said it would be.

He crouched and didn't touch it. That was the discipline she'd taught him without ever teaching him anything — the only lesson that had survived the silence between them. *You don't pick it up. You photograph it, you log it, you leave it for the people who come after.* Except there were no people coming after. There was just him, and the ledger, and the strange grief of kneeling in a dead nation's riverbed holding his mother's handwriting.

"It matches," he said finally. His voice came out rougher than he meant it to. "Everything matches. Split rock, cobblestones, the seal. She wasn't guessing. She'd been here."

"Of course she'd been here." Ines said it gently, which from Ines was a kind of alarm bell. "Dominic. Look at the sketch again. Look at the shading. That's not drawn from a survey. That's drawn from standing here."

He had known it since the ridge, if he was honest. His mother hadn't sent him coordinates. She'd sent him directions home from a place she'd already stood, alone, years ago, mailing the second half of the ledger to a cache because she couldn't bring herself to mail it to him.

"Then let's not keep her waiting," Tam said, and stood, and brushed off his knees, and pointed south. "Black stones next. Before the light goes."

* * *

They found the cairn two hours later, in the last flat gold of evening.

It stood where the riverbed bent east, a heap of black stones taller than a man, and it was wrong in a way Dominic couldn't immediately name. Then he could. Cairns slump. Time pulls them apart the way it pulls everything apart; every cairn he'd ever seen on a trail had a tired, slumped look, like an old man on a bench.

This one stood straight.

"Someone's rebuilt it," Ines breathed. "Recently. Look at the bases — those lower stones are seated in fresh scree. There's no lichen on the upper courses. That's months old, maybe weeks."

Tam had gone very still, the way he did when a river changed pitch under a boat. "And look at the ground."

Dominic looked.

The valley floor around the cairn was a fine pale silt, the kind that held a print the way snow did. And printed across it, coming from the north and circling the cairn twice before continuing south toward the bend, were boot prints. Dozens of them. Military spacing. A patrol pattern, neat as a diagram.

One set broke from the others and stopped directly in front of the cairn, toes almost touching the lowest stone, as if someone had stood there a long time, considering.

"They've been here," Ines whispered. "Voss has been here. They found it and they—" She stopped. Her eyes went to the cairn, then back to the prints, and Dominic watched the arithmetic finish behind them. "No. Wait. If they'd opened it, why rebuild it? Why circle it? They don't know how to open it. They've been here, they've searched, and they left because the door wouldn't speak to them."

"Because the door needs both halves of the ledger," Dominic said slowly. "As keys. And Voss doesn't have either half. He's been chasing us across an entire mountain range for paper."

"He's not chasing paper," Tam said quietly. "He's chasing the person who reads it. Which is worse."

The wind moved down the valley, carrying dust along the old paved riverbed, and Dominic knelt again beside the single set of prints — the ones that had stopped and considered. He measured them against his own boot without meaning to. Longer. Heavier heel. A man who carried weight deliberately.

He thought about Voss standing exactly here, in front of a door he couldn't open, in a valley that erased sound and color and mercy, and felt something he refused to call pity.

"He'll know we're coming," Ines said. "If he left watchers—"

"He didn't." Tam had climbed the scree at the valley's edge and was scanning the heights with a pair of battered binoculars. "I'd see the glint. This valley's too exposed to hide in — whoever waited here would cook. No, they came, they failed, they reported back, and Voss is doing what he does. Calculating. He knows we have the keys. He knows we have to come here eventually. So he's not going to wait at the door." Tam lowered the binoculars. "He's going to wait for us to open it. Let us do the work. Take it from us after."

"So we go in fast and come out slow," Ines said. "Or we don't come out at all."

"Cheerful," Tam said.

"I'm an archaeologist. We're famous for it."

Dominic barely heard them. He'd walked around the cairn, following the patrol prints, and found where they ended — a churned patch at the base of the eastern scree where a helicopter had landed, its skids pressed deep into the silt, now half-filled with windblown dust. Days old. Maybe a week.

Beside the landing patch, half-buried, something glinted.

He dug it out with two fingers. A ration tin, empty, military issue, the label in a language he didn't read but the manufacturer's stamp he recognized from the mercenary's gear on the ferry — the drowned man with the photograph of Dominic in his pocket. Voss's men ate well even at the end of the world.

They had stood in this valley. They had touched these stones. Somewhere ahead of him, in the warm dark under the cairn, his mother's work was waiting, and above it the man who had betrayed her had already walked, patient as debt.

"Dominic." Ines's hand on his shoulder. "Whatever you're deciding — decide it walking. We've got maybe an hour of light."

He pocketed the ration tin — evidence, or a talisman, he wasn't sure which — and looked at the cairn of black stones standing straight against the dying light, keeping itself, keeping everything.

His mother had written *the door keeps itself*. She hadn't written anything about what kept the door company.

"We open it tonight," he said. "Before Voss finishes his arithmetic."

Tam looked at the sky, then at the cairn, then at Dominic, and grinned the grin of a man whose grandmother had fled one erased country and whose grandson apparently intended to dig another one back up.

"Then somebody hand me a crowbar," he said, "and somebody else say a prayer. Preferably in Kharvathi."

Ines was already pulling tools from her pack. "Nobody prays in Kharvathi anymore, Tam. That's rather the point."

"Then improvise," Tam said. "You're good at that."

And that was when the light died all at once — not gradually, the way mountain evenings usually went, but suddenly, like a lamp switched off — and in the new dark, from somewhere south down the valley, faint and impossible and unmistakable, came the sound of engines.
