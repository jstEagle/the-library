## 20. The Sealed Door

Ines Marchetti had spent thirty-one years opening doors that other people had sealed shut — tomb portals, monastery crypts, a particularly vindictive strongroom in Tash-Kurgan that had required three days and a broken wrist. She liked to think she knew the grammar of thresholds. Every door was an argument. Every seal was a sentence written by someone who very much did not want to be interrupted.

The cairn of black stones argued loudest of anything she'd ever met.

It stood at the mouth of the dry riverbed where the ledger said it would stand, a heap of basalt the size of a hay wagon, fitted so precisely that she couldn't have slid a knife blade between two stones. No mortar. No chisel marks. The rocks looked less stacked than *grown*, as if the mountain had coughed once, long ago, and arranged itself on purpose.

"Tell me that's not it," Tam said behind her. He had his grandmother's brass compass out, though there was nothing left in this valley for it to point at but rock. "Tell me we climbed three days up a goat's nightmare for a pile of rocks."

"It's it," Dominic said.

He sounded strange. Ines turned. He was standing with both halves of the ledger open across his forearms — the vellum half from the river chest, the paper half from his mother's cache at the col — and he was reading them not side by side but *over* each other, holding them up against the low sun so the light came through both at once. The landmarks matched. The split rock above them matched. The dry riverbed matched. And the cairn —

"The cairn is drawn here," Ines said, coming to stand at his shoulder. "Look. The surveyor drew it with a little crown on top. Eleven points."

Dominic counted the stones along the top edge of the heap. His lips moved. "Eleven," he said.

"Of course it's eleven," Tam muttered. "Everything about this country is eleven. My grandmother counted everything in elevens. Cups, spoons, steps. I thought she was just peculiar." He paused. "She was peculiar. But also, apparently, correct."

Ines let herself smile, because if she didn't smile she was going to do something undignified like sit down in the dust and put her head between her knees. Three days of climbing. Altitude headaches like someone driving nails through her sinuses. Frostbite nipping at two fingers on her left hand despite the mittens. And now, at the end of it: a pile of rocks with a crown.

But it wasn't a pile of rocks. That was the thing making her heart hammer. She circled it slowly, running her mitten along the joints, and found what she was looking for on the western face — a seam. Not a crack. A seam, straighter than any fault line, running floor to crown, and set into the seam at chest height, a recess the size of a large book.

Two recesses, actually. Side by side. One slightly larger than the other.

"Keys," she breathed. "It wants keys."

* * *

They cleared the scree from the base of the cairn with their hands, working in the particular silence of people who have stopped talking because talking costs breath they might need later. Ines directed; Dominic lifted; Tam stood watch down-valley with binoculars and a face like a closed ledger himself.

The engines had started an hour ago. Faint, down at the valley mouth — a sound like a wasp trapped in a jar, rising and falling with the wind. Voss. It had to be Voss. Nobody else drove engines into a rain-shadow valley where there was no road worth the name.

"They'll reach the foot of the scree slope by dark," Tam said. "Maybe sooner. Their machines climb better than goats."

"Then we'd best be quick," Ines said, and pulled free the last slab covering the lower recess.

What lay beneath made her forget, briefly, to breathe.

A bronze plate, green with age, worked with the same seal that had been stamped into the river chest — the crowned tower, the eleven-pointed sun. And below the seal, cut deep and filled with some dark inlay that hadn't faded in seventy years, a line of Kharvathi script. Ines read it twice before she trusted herself.

"'We were never lost,'" she translated. "'We were put away.'"

"Cheerful," said Tam.

"No," Ines said slowly. "It's not cheerful. It's *confident*. Whoever built this believed, absolutely, that someday someone would come who deserved to open it. This isn't a warning. It's a welcome mat." She looked up at Dominic. "Your mother didn't find this door, Dominic. Your mother was *expected*."

He didn't answer right away. He knelt and laid his palm flat on the bronze, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and Ines watched him and thought — not for the first time since the col — that Mei-Lin Zhou had built her son out of exactly the material this place required: stubbornness, patience, and a grief deep enough to fuel a small engine.

"The recesses," he said finally. "One for each half of the ledger?"

"That's my guess. The larger one for the vellum, the smaller for the paper. But I don't want to guess with Voss's engines getting louder." She stripped off her mitten with her teeth — her frostbitten fingers screamed, and she ignored them with the practiced contempt of long acquaintance — and traced the mechanism around the recesses. Gears, or something like gears, visible through slots worn smooth. Counterweights somewhere inside the stone; she could feel the faint give when she pressed. "It's mechanical. Beautifully mechanical. No explosives anywhere near this, do you hear me? One charge and whatever counterweights are holding the door shut come down on our heads."

"So we post the letters," Tam said. "Like paying a toll."

"Like posting a letter," Ines agreed, "to a country that doesn't exist anymore, asking it to please let us in."

* * *

The vellum half went in first. It slid into the larger recess with a sound like a held breath being released — snug, exact, as though the slot had been cut to its millimeter seven hundred years ago by someone who had somehow known the dimensions of a document that wouldn't exist for six more centuries. Ines decided not to think about that too hard. Some questions were doors too, and she only had the strength for one threshold today.

The paper half — Mei-Lin's half, mailed from a border town, cached at a col, carried up a goat path on the back of a woman who had hated its author for twenty years — went into the smaller recess.

Nothing happened.

For ten seconds, nothing happened, and Ines felt the beginning of real fear, the professional kind, the kind that says *you have misread the text and misread texts get people killed.* Then Dominic said, quietly, "Listen."

Under the wind. Under the distant wasp-song of engines. A sound from inside the mountain: a slow, deep shifting, like a house settling, like a giant turning over in bed. Dust sifted from the seams of the cairn. Somewhere within the black stones, weights the size of coffins began to move.

The crown of the cairn split.

Not violently — that was the unnerving part. The eleven top stones parted along invisible lines and folded back and *down*, each one hinging on some interior pivot, until the whole upper third of the cairn had opened like a stone flower, and where the heart of the heap had been there was now a round hole, and descending from the hole, stairs.

Warm air rose out of them.

That was the detail that undid Ines. Warm air, in a valley where her water bottle had frozen solid overnight. Warm air smelling of dry stone and something older — lamp oil? cedar? — rising past her face like an exhalation, like the mountain breathing out after holding its breath since 1953.

"Well," said Tam. "That's either the most wonderful thing I've ever seen or the entrance to a very elaborate stomach."

"Both can be true," Ines said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Kharvathi engineering. If the door is this elegant, the vault will be —" She stopped. She had been about to say *magnificent*, and the word snagged on something in her chest, because magnificent was the word she'd used about Mei-Lin's work for twenty years, always through her teeth, always with the asterisk of rivalry attached, and standing here at the actual threshold of the actual thing they had both chased across two decades and three countries, the asterisk dissolved and left only the naked awe underneath.

"The stair," Dominic said. He had taken out his headlamp but hadn't turned it on yet. He was looking down into the warm dark, and Ines saw him swallow. "How far down does it go?"

"I can't tell. The light dies about forty steps in." She checked her watch. Four-forty in the afternoon. Outside light would last another ninety minutes; after that the valley would go from gold to iron in about ten minutes flat, the way rain-shadow valleys do. "If we go down, we're committed. We won't be climbing back out tonight unless we start immediately, and if the archive is anything like the size the ledger implies —"

Engines. Louder. Distinctly louder, no longer a wasp in a jar but a wasp loose in the room. Tam had the binoculars up before the sound finished reaching them.

"Dust plume," he reported. "Two vehicles, maybe three, at the bend where the riverbed narrows. An hour. Maybe fifty minutes." He lowered the glasses. "I've run from men in trucks before. On water, I win. On foot, uphill, with a frostbitten archaeologist —" he tipped his head apologetically at Ines — "no offense —"

"Some taken."

"— we don't outrun them. We hide, or we go down those stairs and hope the door locks behind us."

"Does it lock?" Dominic asked Ines.

She hesitated. Honesty was a luxury she'd learned to ration, but this wasn't the moment for rationing. "I don't know. The mechanism might re-seal. It might not. There may be a bar on the inside. There may be nothing. Seventy years is a long time for a wooden bar."

"So it's possible," Dominic said, "that we go down there to escape Voss, and then can't get back out, and starve in a dead nation's basement surrounded by ten thousand years of other people's homework."

"Yes," Ines said. "That's possible."

"And the alternative is meeting Voss on open ground with three of us and however many of him."

"Also yes."

Tam sat down on a boulder, produced a strip of dried meat, and bit off a corner with the calm of a man ordering coffee. "My grandmother crossed a mountain range in winter with a baby and a stolen horse," he said, "because staying meant the purge, and going meant maybe dying comfortable. She used to say —" he chewed, swallowed — "she used to say the door you're afraid of is usually just a door. Fear builds the rest of the house around it." He pointed the meat at the open cairn. "That's a door. Everything else is weather."

Ines looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at the stair.

"We go down," he said. "All three. And Tam — if there's a bar on the inside, we find it fast."

* * *

They prepared the way climbers prepare, which is to say obsessively, unglamorously, and with gentle humor deployed strictly as ballast against terror. Water redistributed. Rope coiled. Headlamps tested, spare batteries counted twice. Ines photographed the open door from four angles with the little camera she'd carried since Tash-Kurgan, because whatever happened next, documentation was prayer, and she had never in her life skipped a prayer.

Dominic went first. Of course he went first; he was his mother's son, and custodians lead.

The first steps were cut stone, wide and shallow, sized for people carrying loads — scholars moving their life's work, Ines thought, hauling cylinders and crates down into the dark while the soldiers came over the passes. Forty steps, and the daylight died behind them exactly as she'd predicted, and they switched on their lamps, and the beams showed walls dressed in fitted basalt, dry as bone, unmarked by water in all these years.

Fifty steps. Eighty. The air stayed warm. Somewhere below, Ines realized, there must be a source of it — geothermal, perhaps, a thread of the mountain's own heat, which meant whoever chose this site had chosen it the way you choose a house: not for hiding but for *living*. They had meant the archive to survive. Not to be found. To survive. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole tragedy of Kharvath, and she felt it settle onto her shoulders like a coat.

At a hundred and twelve steps — Tam counted aloud, because counting kept panic at a manageable distance — the stair ended.

They stood in a short passage, and at the end of the passage, a second door. Smaller. Bronze-banded wood, dark with age, and beside it, set into the wall, a lever of the same green bronze, shaped like a shepherd's crook.

And above the door, in that deep confident inlay, words. Ines raised her lamp. Her frostbitten fingers trembled, and she told them to stop, and they didn't, and she read anyway.

"'Let in those who carry the whole,'" she translated. Her voice caught on the last word and she had to clear her throat. "'Turn away those who carry only part.'"

Behind them, far above, muffled by a hundred meters of stone, came a new sound. Not engines. Voices. Shouts, thin as insects, echoing down the stairwell. Then — unmistakable, horrible — the crack of something struck against stone. Metal on basalt. Once. Twice.

They were at the cairn. They were at the door. And they had only half the ledger, because the other half was in the slot upstairs, doing its work.

"They can't open it," Dominic whispered. "Can they?"

"Not without both halves." But even as she said it, Ines heard the doubt in her own voice, and she knew he heard it too. Voss had resources. Voss had time. Voss had, if the rumors were true, a patience that outlasted governments.

Tam already had his hands on the lever. "Decide fast," he said. "Because I'm not standing in this hallway arguing philosophy while they bring tools."

Ines looked at Dominic. Dominic put his palm flat against the wooden door, the way he'd touched the bronze outside — gently, the way you knock when you're not sure anyone's home but you hope.

"They waited seventy years for someone to carry the whole," he said. "We're carrying the whole. Open it."

Tam pulled the lever.

The door swung inward without a sound — oiled, balanced, maintained by some mechanism still faithfully at its post — and warm lamplit air rolled out over them, smelling of cedar and dust and time, and beyond the threshold the dark opened wide enough that their headlamps couldn't find the far wall.

From above: a shout, sharp and triumphant. Then the grinding of stone, and a voice — even distorted by distance and echo, Ines knew it instantly, would have known it in her grave — calling down the stairwell with terrible courtesy:

"Dr. Marchetti. Dr. Zhou. Do wait for me."

Voss had found the cairn. And somewhere in the machinery above, Ines heard the first half of the ledger slide free of its slot — which meant the door behind them could close, and lock, and leave them exactly where a dead nation wanted its guests.

Inside. With the whole. With no way back.

"Down," said Tam, and pulled them both through the doorway into the warm dark, as the light from the stairwell above began, stone by patient stone, to die.
