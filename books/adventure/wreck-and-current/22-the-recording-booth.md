## 22. The Recording Booth

The vault had a smell, and Ines Marchetti was cataloguing it before she'd finished her first breath inside: cold stone, machine oil gone to resin over decades, wax, and underneath it all something faintly sweet, like cedar shavings in an old pencil case. A civilization's attic, she thought. Every attic smells like the people who packed it away.

She'd been in great archives before. The Vatican stacks, the Topkapi vaults, a private collection in Geneva she'd been shown through as a favor and had never quite forgiven herself for enjoying. But those places were curated. Things were arranged to be seen. This was arranged to be *kept* — a different grammar entirely. The cylinders stood in niches cut into the living rock, ten rows high, thousands of them, each sealed in a lead sleeve, each sleeve stamped with the looping angular script of Kharvath. No dust anywhere. Someone had built this room to outlast weather, war, and forgetting, and had then, apparently, dusted it.

"Seeds," Tam said, holding a cylinder up to the light of his headlamp, turning it like a man appraising fish at market. "Wheat. Barley. Something I don't know." He'd found the seed vault section by following the pictograms — a sprout, a sun, a mountain — and his voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when his grandmother's stories got too close to his skin. "They packed a whole country's farms into jars."

"They packed a whole country, period," Dominic said. He stood three niches down, his lamp on a row of cylinders marked with a pictogram of an eye. Maps, Ines guessed. Or astronomy. Or both — the Kharvathi, from everything they'd read in the border-town archive, had never seen much difference between looking at the land and looking at the sky.

Ines kept to herself, which was her habit and, she'd been told on more than one occasion, her flaw. She moved along the rows reading stamps, building the inventory in her head the way she built every argument: structure first, sentiment later. Language cylinders — she could tell by the pictogram of an open mouth, which was either charmingly literal or deeply optimistic about whoever came digging in ten thousand years. Music. A whole niche of music, and she found herself absurdly moved by that, more than by the seeds, more than by the maps. Anyone sensible packs grain for the end of the world. It took a particular kind of stubbornness to pack the *songs*.

"Here." Dominic's voice came from the far wall, and it had changed. Flattened. The voice of a man reading a label he didn't like.

She crossed the floor — the stone was worn in a path down the center, she noticed, worn by the feet of the people who had sealed this place, a detail she filed away for the report she would never be allowed to write — and came to stand beside him.

The niche here was different. Smaller. And set into the rock beside it, hinged and counterweighted, was a mechanism: a brass horn folded into a housing like a sleeping insect, a hand crank, a playback arm, all of it engineered with the same patient overkill as everything else in this room. A listening booth. The Kharvathi had not only recorded themselves; they'd built the means of being heard, and built it to survive.

And in the niche, alone, apart from the ordered thousands, sat one cylinder in a sleeve of plain lead with no pictogram at all. Just a name, stamped deep, in Latin letters rather than Kharvathi script.

*Zhou, M.-L.*

Ines did not move for what felt like a long time. The lamp light trembled very slightly, and it took her a moment to understand that the trembling was hers.

"She recorded in here," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "In the booth. She found the room, she understood the mechanism — of course she did, she was better with mechanisms than I ever was — and she sat down in a dead nation's recording booth and made a cylinder."

"Of what?"

"Don't be dense, Dominic. Of what do people record in vaults? Testimony. Confession. Last words." She heard herself and winced inwardly. *Last words.* She'd spent thirty years competing with Mei-Lin Zhou and had learned, somewhere in the middle decade, that the competition was also a kind of love, and she still could not say such things out loud in front of the woman's son.

Dominic reached for the cylinder.

Ines caught his wrist. She did it without deciding to; her hand simply went, the way it goes to a hot pan, a falling glass, a student about to sign a bad contract. His wrist was thinner than she'd expected. He'd lost weight on the goat path, they all had, and his pulse under her fingers was quick and hard, a man's pulse running on altitude and purpose.

"Wait," she said.

"For what?"

"For a moment's thought, which is the most underrated tool in archaeology." She let go of him and turned to face him fully, and made herself say the thing she'd been assembling since she read the name. "We should melt it down."

Tam, who had drifted over, made a sound that was half cough, half objection. "Melt — you want to *melt* it? It's the only — it's a *primary source*, Marchetti, it's—"

"I know exactly what it is. That's why I want it gone." She kept her eyes on Dominic. "Listen to me. Your mother put her name on that cylinder because she intended it to be heard. But she didn't know who would find it. She couldn't have known. It could have been Voss. It could have been the Collection's lawyers. It could have been anyone, and she took that risk because she judged the testimony worth more than her privacy. Fine. That was her arithmetic." Ines touched the sleeve, lightly, one finger, the way she'd touch a page she suspected was vellum. "But the arithmetic has changed. The Collection is behind us. If we get out of here — *when* we get out of here — everything we carry is evidence, and evidence can be seized, subpoenaed, stolen. Voss's people will tear this valley apart looking for what we took out. Do you want your mother's voice in a Halvard warehouse? Do you want her confession played back in some deposition room, edited, excerpted, used to discredit the tribunal case? Because that is what happens to testimony, Dominic. It doesn't get heard. It gets *used*."

"She's dead," Dominic said. "They can't hurt her."

"No. They can hurt *you*. And Tam. And me. And every Kharvathi descendant who joins the excavation in the spring, if there is a spring excavation, which there will only be if we come out of this valley holding something the Collection can't twist." She heard her own voice rising and made herself lower it. This was not a thing to shout. This was a thing to say the way you'd say it over a grave. "I loved her. You know that, don't you? You must know it, even if we spent twenty years pretending otherwise. I competed with her because she was the only one worth competing with. And I am telling you, as the person alive who knew her professional mind best — she would *not* want that cylinder to survive if its surviving put you in a room with Halvard's people. She spent her whole life keeping you out of those rooms. She erased herself from your life to do it. She would not thank us for handing them her voice."

The lamp light held steady on Dominic's face. He looked tired in the way that altitude makes people look tired, hollowed under the eyes, but his jaw was set in a line Ines recognized with a small internal jolt, because it was Mei-Lin's line, the exact set of it, the one Mei-Lin would deploy in a trench argument at two in the morning and win with by three.

"She also spent her whole life deciding things for me," he said.

"Dominic—"

"Not listening to me. Not telling me things. Sending me away. Choosing, on my behalf, always, what I could bear and what I couldn't." He picked up the cylinder — Ines didn't stop him this time; there was no stopping it, and she knew it — and turned it in his hands. "You're right. She'd have done the arithmetic exactly the way you said. She'd have melted it herself if she could have. But she's not here, and I am, and this is the first thing in my entire life that my mother left *to me* instead of *around* me. The ledger was instructions. The cache was supplies. This—" he lifted it slightly, "—this is her, actually her. Not her handwriting, not her cipher. Her voice. And I have spent thirty years not hearing it."

"So you'd risk it. For a voice."

"I'd risk it for the truth of it," he said. "There's a difference."

"Is there? In a courtroom there is. In a Halvard boardroom there isn't."

"In this room there is," Dominic said, and spread his arm at the vault, at the ten thousand cylinders, at the whole patient machinery of a people who had decided that memory was the only property worth dying inside of. "Look where we're standing, Ines. This is what they built. Not a treasure house — a *witness*. They sealed up everything they were so that somebody, someday, would have to decide what to do with it. That's the whole design. The door needed two keys. The archive needs a custodian. Nothing in here works unless someone chooses. So I'm choosing."

Tam shifted his weight. "For the record," he said, "I think we should listen to it. Not because of the truth. Because if we carry a dead woman's voice out of these mountains and never play it, that's worse than melting it. That's just luggage."

Ines looked at him. Tam had a gift, which she'd noticed on the ferry and had resented ever since, for saying the true thing in the plainest possible words and thereby ending arguments that she'd been prepared to conduct for an hour. "That," she said, "is an unhelpfully good point."

"I have a few."

"You have exactly that one."

She turned back to Dominic, and made herself do the last hard thing, which was not to concede but to *condition*, because she was who she was. "Then we do it properly. Not here in the open — in the booth, door shut, lamps low. And we don't leave until I've heard all of it, every second, because if any part of that recording can hurt us I need to know it before Voss does. And Dominic—" she waited until he looked at her, really looked, "—if there's anything on it that names people still living, people the Collection can still reach, we decide together what happens to it. Together. Not you alone, and not her posthumously through you. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said.

"And if it's nothing but sentiment — if it's her telling you she loved you, which, for the record, I am certain it partly is, because that woman could not leave a site without writing a site report and she could not leave a person without leaving instructions—"

"Ines."

"—then you will let yourself hear that part too. That's not a negotiation term. That's just me being right, which you'll get used to."

The corner of his mouth moved. It was the first almost-smile she'd seen on him since the col, and she counted it, quietly, as a small victory in a campaign with very few of them.

* * *

The booth was smaller than Ines had expected, which was its own kind of eloquence. The Kharvathi engineers had built the vault on a heroic scale and then built the listening booth like a confessional — a stone cell barely large enough for three people, bench against the wall, the playback mechanism mounted at chest height, the counterweighted horn folded above it like a hand waiting to be held. When Dominic cranked the housing open, the brass came out smooth, oiled, ready. Forty years in the dark and it moved like it had been wound yesterday.

"They expected listeners," Ines said softly. "Not someday. Soon. They built this to be used within a generation." She ran her thumb along the bench's edge and found it worn, polished by sitting. "People sat here. More than once. They came down and listened to each other." The thought settled over her with real weight: a nation that had known it was being erased, and had responded by building a room where the erased could speak and the not-yet-born could listen. She had spent her career among people who dug things up to own them. These people had dug in to *answer*.

Dominic set the cylinder on the spindle. His hands were steady, which Ines suspected was costing him something. Tam stood at the door, on watch and not pretending otherwise, his bulk filling the frame, his headlamp turned down to a coal-red glow.

"Whenever you're ready," Ines said. "Or never. Both are still on the table."

Dominic looked at the cylinder one more time. Then he said, quietly, to the mechanism or to the room or possibly to his mother, "You could have just written a letter," and turned the crank.

The horn woke with a hum, then a hiss, then a breath — an actual human breath, drawn in the dark of this same room decades ago, preserved in wax and lead the way the Kharvathi had preserved their wheat and their songs. Ines felt the hair rise on her forearms. Thirty years of fieldwork and she had never once been this afraid of a sound.

Then Mei-Lin Zhou's voice filled the booth, warm and dry and unmistakable, and said the last thing Ines expected, the one opening she would never have predicted in a thousand rehearsals of this moment:

"If you're playing this, you found the second half of the ledger, which means you're either my child or someone clever enough to frighten me. Let's find out which."

And Ines, who had come down into this vault prepared to defend the dead woman's silence, stood in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the cold stone wall, and listened.
