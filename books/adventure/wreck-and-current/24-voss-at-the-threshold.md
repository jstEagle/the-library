## 24. Voss at the Threshold

The stair went down farther than it had any right to.

Voss counted the steps the way he counted everything — because counting was a form of control, and control was the only currency that never devalued. Forty-one steps. The stone under his boots was worn smooth in the center of each tread, which meant feet, which meant centuries of feet, which meant the Kharvathi had built this not as a tomb but as a thoroughfare. A road turned vertical and buried.

Behind him, six men moved with the particular quiet of professionals who had stopped asking questions around day nine of the climb. Brandt carried the floodlights. Okafor carried the spare batteries and the only sense of humor among them, which he kept holstered like a sidearm. The other four were names Voss made a point of knowing and a further point of forgetting, because names were attachments and attachments were liabilities with good posture.

"Air's warm," Brandt said at the bottom. "Warm air means ventilation. Ventilation means somebody engineered this."

"Somebody engineered everything," Voss said. "That's rather the point of people."

The corridor opened, and then the vault opened, and Voss did what he always did when confronted with something genuinely large: he stood very still and let it enter him through the eyes first.

Ten thousand cylinders. Perhaps more. They rose in racks of black basalt from floor to a ceiling lost in shadow, row after row after row, each cylinder sealed with wax the color of dried blood and stamped with the seal he had spent eleven years learning to hate — the sunburst-and-key of Kharvath, a country that had been so thoroughly erased that even its erasure had been erased. The floodlights found the nearest rows and gave up on the rest. The dark between the racks wasn't empty dark. It was *archived* dark. Curated. Intentional.

"Well," said Okafor softly. "It's not gold."

"No," Voss agreed. "It isn't."

He felt the disappointment move through his men like weather — quick, gray, gone. Mercenaries wanted gold because gold converted. This would not convert easily. This would have to be *explained*, and the Halvard Collection had built its entire fortune on never having to explain anything to anyone.

But Voss had read the ledger's recovered pages. He knew what the cylinders held. Seed stocks for crops that no longer grew anywhere. Maps of rivers that had since changed their minds. Recordings — voices, songs, arguments, jokes, the entire tedious magnificent noise of two million people who had once believed they would be remembered by default. An insurance policy written by a dead nation against the exact crime that had been committed against it.

And somewhere in these racks, one cylinder bore the name Mei-Lin Zhou.

He had known her name before Dominic Zhou had ever been a problem. That was the part nobody understood about his work — that you could not hunt a thing for a decade without it getting inside you, the way sand gets inside a watch. He knew her handwriting. He knew she underlined twice when she meant it and crossed out whole paragraphs rather than erase them, as if even her mistakes deserved to remain legible. He knew things about Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou that her own son did not know, and he intended to keep it that way, because knowledge was leverage and leverage was the only inheritance worth having.

Ahead, down the central aisle where the floodlights pooled, three figures stood among the shelves like intruders in a cathedral, which was precisely what they were.

Zhou. Marchetti. And the smuggler, Tam, whose file described him as "local asset, unreliable, useful" — a description that fit most of humanity.

Voss straightened his jacket. It was a small vanity and he permitted it. If you were going to offer a man salvation, you ought to look like someone qualified to administer it.

* * *

"Gentlemen," Voss said, and then, correcting himself with a small bow toward Marchetti, "and Doctor. Forgive the intrusion. We knocked, but the door was, I'm told, several tons of basalt."

Dominic Zhou turned. He looked worse than the photographs — thinner, sunburned across the cheekbones, one hand wrapped in filthy cloth — and he looked, Voss noted with professional interest, *steadier*. Eleven days ago in Kestrel Bend the man had been a drowning animal. Something in the mountains had wrung him out and hung him up to dry. That was inconvenient. Desperate men took bargains; steady ones read the fine print.

"You're Voss," Zhou said.

"I'm Voss."

"My mother wrote about you." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *my mother wrote about the weather*. "In the cipher. She called you 'the man who counts.'"

Something moved behind Voss's ribs, brief and unwelcome, like a fish turning in shallow water. *The man who counts.* She had noticed. Of course she had noticed. She had noticed everything except how to be a parent, according to the boy's own bitter arithmetic, and Voss thought — not for the first time, and with less comfort than usual — that the two failures might be the same failure wearing different coats.

"She flattered me," Voss said. "I count because counting is honest. Numbers don't take sides. They simply tell you what is, and then it's up to people to decide whether they can live with it." He gestured, an open-handed sweep that took in the racks, the dark, the ten thousand sealed voices. "What is, Mr. Zhou, is the single most significant archaeological discovery of the last century. Possibly five centuries. And it currently has a security detail of one archaeologist, one boat thief, and yourself."

"One boat *captain*," said Tam.

"Noted. My apologies to the profession."

Marchetti stepped forward, frostbite scars livid on two fingers of her left hand. "How did you find us? The valley mouth was—"

"Cairn of black stones, split rock, dry riverbed. Your boot prints under the fresh scree, Doctor, which were admirably careful but not admirably *invisible*." Voss let a beat pass. "Also, Mr. Zhou's mother mailed half a ledger through a postal system that keeps records. You discovered that on the night train. I discovered it four days later. In my defense, I was interrogating a drowned man's contacts at the time, which is slower reading."

He watched Zhou absorb that — the drowned mercenary, the photograph, the dawn on the river. The boy's jaw tightened. Good. Anger was legible. Legible men were predictable men, and predictable men could be *paid*.

"But none of that matters now," Voss continued, warming to it, because this was the part of his work he was actually good at — the part that was almost honest. "What matters is that we are standing in the last library of Kharvath, and within seventy-two hours the monsoon will close every pass behind us, and within — what, Mr. Zhou? How much food did you bring?"

Silence. Which was an answer.

"Precisely," Voss said. "So let me make you an offer. Not a threat. An offer. There's a difference, and I'd like you to notice the difference, because everyone else in your life apparently offered you threats dressed as love, and I am offering you love dressed as arithmetic."

Okafor made a sound that might have been a suppressed laugh. Voss ignored him with long practice.

"Here are the terms." He raised one finger. "First: your debts. All of them. The loans against your mother's estate, the dredging contracts you signed in Kestrel Bend, the interest that's been breeding in the dark like something in a cellar — gone. Paid. This morning, in fact, while you were climbing a goat path. As of nine o'clock yesterday you were the cleanest man in three provinces, and nobody bothered to tell you." A second finger. "Second: your mother's reputation. The 2019 incident — the failed dig, the charges, the disgrace that followed her to her grave. The Halvard Collection commissioned certain... narratives, during that unpleasantness. We can uncommission them. Documentation exists. It will find its way to the journals, the universities, the tribunal if necessary. Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou will die posthumously innocent, which is more than most of us manage while alive."

He paused there, deliberately, because he could see the words landing. *Posthumously innocent.* He had chosen the phrase in the helicopter and he was not above being proud of it.

"And third," he said, "you walk out of here rich, clean, and free, ahead of the snows, with your friends intact. In exchange—" and here he spread both hands, palms up, the universal posture of the reasonable man, "—the location index. The master catalog. Whatever tells the Collection where each cylinder sits and what it holds. The archive itself stays buried. I give you my word."

"You give him your word," Marchetti said. Her voice was dry as the riverbed outside. "The word of the man who left Mei-Lin Zhou on a collapsing terrace in 2019 and testified that she'd ignored protocol."

Ah. There it was. Voss had wondered how much the boy knew, and now he knew that too — Marchetti had told him, probably on the mountain, probably at altitude, where all truths come out slightly bent.

"That account is incomplete," Voss said quietly.

"It's *accurate*."

"It's accurate the way a photograph is accurate. One angle. No depth." He turned back to Zhou, and here he did something he rarely permitted himself: he dropped the performance by perhaps ten percent. "Mr. Zhou. I'm going to tell you something true, and then I'm going to return to being a man you have excellent reasons to distrust, and we'll proceed from there. Here it is: I do not want this archive destroyed, looted piecemeal, or sold in fragments to men who'll use a nation's seed stocks as cocktail-party conversation. I want it *kept*. Catalogued. Preserved under conditions that will outlast everyone in this room. The Collection is many things — some of them indefensible, I've read the internal memos — but it is the only institution on earth with the climate control, the funding, and the sheer stubborn permanence to keep ten thousand cylinders safe for the next thousand years. Your mother spent her life trying to get this material *out* of private hands. I am offering to put it into the most private hands there are, permanently, safely, and — this is the part I'd underline twice, if I underlined — *irretrievably*."

"Buried again," Zhou said. "Just in a nicer grave. With a nicer gravestone. Yours."

"Every archive is a grave, Mr. Zhou. The question is only whether the dead are comfortable."

Tam snorted. "You rehearse that on the way up the mountain?"

"On the helicopter, actually. The mountain I improvised."

And there — briefly, horribly — Dominic Zhou almost smiled. Voss saw it happen and saw the boy kill it, and understood that he had just made his single most dangerous miscalculation of the expedition: he had made the target *like* him for one quarter of one second. People forgave you anything except the moments they'd enjoyed your company.

* * *

Zhou walked away from him then. Not fleeing — walking, down the central aisle, into the dark between the racks, and Voss watched him go with the patience of a man who had learned that pursuit is often just impatience wearing boots.

"He's reading the spines," Marchetti said. "Looking for her name."

"I know."

"You knew about the cylinder. With her recording on it."

"I suspected. Now I know." Voss clasped his hands behind his back. "Doctor, you may as well save your breath telling him I'm a monster. He's already decided. What he hasn't decided is whether monsters can be *useful*, and that decision is the only one that matters in the next hour."

"You think he'll take the deal."

"I think," Voss said, "that he came into this world owing money for a woman who chose rocks over him, and I am the first person in his entire life to offer to cancel the debt without asking for his soul as collateral. Men don't refuse that because it's evil. They refuse it because accepting it would mean admitting how badly they wanted it." He glanced at her sideways. "You of all people should understand. You've spent thirty years wanting to be Mei-Lin Zhou's equal, and the moment her son offers you a place at his table, you'll take it, and you'll hate yourself in exactly the proportion that you're grateful."

Marchetti's face did something complicated and then settled into contempt, which Voss recognized as the load-bearing emotion of intelligent women dealing with men who talked too much.

Down the aisle, a floodlight flickered. Zhou had stopped at a rack, one hand raised toward a cylinder, not touching it. Even from forty meters, Voss could see the stamp on the wax. He didn't need to read the name. There was only one name it could be.

*There you are,* Voss thought, and felt the fish turn again behind his ribs, and decided, as always, to ignore it.

"Mr. Zhou," he called, not loudly — the vault carried sound the way a church does, respectfully. "Before you answer, consider one more item of arithmetic. My men are tired, the weather is closing, and I have orders. If you refuse, those orders become *other* orders, and I would very much prefer — professionally, personally, sincerely — not to receive them. Take the index. Take the clean ledger. Take your mother's name back. Let the dead keep their library. Everyone walks out of the rain."

Zhou stood a moment longer, hand still raised toward the cylinder that held his mother's voice.

Then he turned around, and Voss saw the answer in his face before he heard it — saw it the way you see weather coming over a ridge, too far off to stop and far too large.

"No," Dominic Zhou said.

And behind him, in the dark of the last library of Kharvath, ten thousand sealed voices waited to hear what happened next.
