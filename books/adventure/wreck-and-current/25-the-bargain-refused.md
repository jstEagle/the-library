## 25. The Bargain Refused

The vault had a sound to it. Not silence — silence is what you get in an empty room. This was the opposite of empty. Ten thousand sealed cylinders stood in their niches like a congregation holding its breath, and if you listened long enough — and Dominic had been listening for what felt like hours, though it could not have been more than twenty minutes since Voss's boots came down the stair — you could almost hear them humming. Or maybe that was his own blood in his ears. Hard to say. Grief and awe make similar noises.

Voss stood at the center of the main chamber with six men fanned out behind him, and he was doing something Dominic hadn't expected: waiting. He'd made his offer — debts paid, Mei-Lin's name scrubbed clean of the scandal that had followed her to her grave, the archive's index handed over — and now he stood with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring architecture, letting the offer do its work.

Dominic became aware that Ines had drifted to his left and Tam to his right, which was either tactical positioning or the instinct of people who'd spent three weeks learning exactly how much trouble they were in together.

"You're doing the math," Voss said. His voice carried strangely in the vault, softened by all that clay and stone, so that even menace arrived muffled, polite. "I can see it. It's not a small number, Mr. Zhou. Your mother's creditors were not patient people."

"They weren't," Dominic agreed. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Mostly they weren't people at all. Mostly they were banks."

"Then you understand the arithmetic." Voss turned, slowly, taking in the niches. Up close, in the light of the vault's strange phosphorescent strips — some Kharvathi mineral trick that Ines had already begun lecturing about before the engines started echoing up the valley — he looked older than he had at the bus depot. Tired around the eyes in a way that didn't match the rest of him. "A civilization's entire memory, sitting under a mountain where nobody will ever see it. And on the other side of the scale: one man's freedom. One family's name restored. It's hardly a bargain at all. It's charity."

"That's an interesting word for it," Dominic said, "coming from someone whose men burned a granary two days ago."

Something moved behind Voss's face — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "That wasn't my order."

"It was your team."

"My team follows the Collection's protocols. I follow—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. Dominic watched him do it, watched the man fold whatever that flicker had been into a neat square and put it away somewhere deep. "I'm offering you a clean exit. There aren't many of those in this business. Most people never get offered one at all."

* * *

Here is what Dominic was thinking about, while Voss talked: his mother's hands.

He hadn't thought about her hands in years. He'd thought about her absence, her silences, the postcards that stopped coming, the birthday she missed when he turned eleven and the excuse that arrived three months late. But standing in the vault she had found and mapped and kept secret from everyone who would have eaten it alive, he found himself remembering her hands — ink-stained at the knuckle, always cold, always moving, sorting sherds or turning pages or, once, memorably, fixing the toaster with a butter knife and a look of pure archaeological contempt for whoever had designed it.

Those hands had written the coordinates in his head. Those hands had chosen him — not the Collection, not the work, not the dead nation with its beautiful terrible archive. Him. *Custodian, second half.*

And here was a man offering to buy the second half along with the first.

"Mr. Zhou," Voss said, when the silence had stretched long enough to become its own argument. "You're a dredger. I've read your file. You haul mud out of rivers for money you don't have. I am offering to make the mud stop. All of it. Forever."

"I know what you're offering."

"Then name your price. Anything within reason. The Collection has deep pockets and longer patience."

And that — that was the moment the line drew itself. Dominic felt it happen, felt something settle in his chest with the finality of a stone dropping into a well. Because Voss had just made the mistake. A small one, a polite one, but fatal: he'd assumed everything had a price. He'd assumed Dominic was a ledger with a balance column, and that the balance was simply high enough that no one had ever been able to pay it off.

"Some things aren't currency," Dominic said.

Voss's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Everything is currency. That's rather the Collection's founding insight. Land, loyalty, history, grief — it all trades. The only question is whether you set the exchange rate or someone sets it for you."

"See, that's the thing." Dominic took a step forward, and heard Ines shift behind him — not stopping him, just readying herself, the way she did. "You keep talking about my mother's debts like they were mine. They weren't. She owed the banks, and I paid the banks, because that's what family does even when family is a storage unit and a stack of unopened mail. But this—" he gestured at the vault, at the ten thousand cylinders, at the whole buried patience of a murdered country— "this isn't hers to sell and it isn't mine. It belongs to the people who built it. To their grandchildren. To a village up-valley whose elder died pointing us here so it wouldn't end up in a glass case in Zurich with a little card that says *acquired through private channels.*"

"Sentiment," Voss said softly.

"Principle," Dominic said. "There's a difference. Sentiment is what you feel. Principle is what you do when you'd rather not."

He heard Tam exhale through his nose — the smuggler's equivalent of applause — and had to fight down a laugh that would have been entirely inappropriate and possibly hysterical. Three weeks ago he'd have traded this whole mountain for the phone number of a decent bankruptcy lawyer. Three weeks of cipher and goat paths and a dying elder's finger tracing the way up-valley had rearranged something in him, quietly, without asking permission.

"The debts," Dominic said. "Pay them yourself. You clearly have the budget — you brought paramilitaries to a place with no roads. Consider it reparations for the granary."

"That's not how—"

"No," Dominic agreed. "It's really not. That's sort of my point."

* * *

Voss was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, the warmth had drained out of his voice entirely, leaving something flat and administrative, and somehow that was worse than any threat could have been.

"Then we proceed under standard acquisition protocol," he said. "The archive will be catalogued and removed. The cylinders go out by mule train starting tomorrow; my surveyors are already marking the route. As for witnesses—" he glanced at Ines, then Tam, then back to Dominic, with the mild attention of a man checking items off a list— "the Collection's insurance requirements are quite specific about loose ends. I'm sorry. I genuinely am. You seem like reasonable people, and this could have been simple."

"Simple," Ines repeated. Her accent thickened when she was angry; Dominic had learned to hear the difference between her ordinary precision and the blade underneath it. "You know what your protocol was last time, Voss? On the Karst dig? Simple?"

"Ines." Just her name, but it landed oddly — not dismissive. Wary.

"Mei-Lin told me about it, near the end. The shaft collapse. How you walked out and let her take the blame for the permits, the shortcuts, all of it. How she spent four years rebuilding a reputation you spent four years spending." Ines stepped forward until she and Voss were an arm's length apart, and Dominic realized with distant surprise that she was shaking — not with fear. With something that had been waiting decades for a room like this one. "She never hated you. Do you know that? She said you were the best field mind she ever worked with, and that something in the profession had rotted you, and she mourned it. *Mourned*, Voss. Like a death."

For one unguarded instant, Voss looked like a man who had been struck in a place he'd armored carefully and forgotten about. Then the armor came back down, plate by plate, and he was only tired again.

"Dr. Marchetti," he said formally, "you should sit down. This next part goes better for everyone if nobody does anything heroic."

"Nobody here is heroic," Tam said cheerfully. "We're just very hard to kill. Ask the river."

One of the mercenaries laughed — a short, startled bark — and immediately looked mortified. Voss didn't turn around, but Dominic saw his jaw tighten. Even private armies, apparently, contained human beings, though they tried to keep it quiet.

"Six men," Ines said to Dominic, low, in English. "Two at the door. If we can reach the stair—"

"And go where?" Dominic murmured back. "He controls the valley mouth. We'd be running into the monsoon with no supplies and frostbite casualties." He glanced at Ines's bandaged hand, and she had the grace to look briefly sheepish. "No. We hold here. This room is ours. Let him come take it."

"That is a romantic statement with terrible logistics."

"I've been told that's my mother's whole legacy."

Despite everything, Ines snorted. Somewhere in the dark of the niches, the vault seemed to approve.

* * *

What happened next happened slowly, which Dominic hadn't expected. He'd expected violence — the sharp kind, muzzle flashes and shouting. Instead Voss simply began to work. He pulled a tablet from his pack, nodded to two of his men, and they started at the nearest niche with crowbars and padded crates, prying the first cylinder free of its cradle with a care that made Dominic's teeth ache. They wrapped it like porcelain. They labeled it. Somewhere in a warehouse in another hemisphere, a shelf was already waiting.

"That one's a seed stock," Ines said loudly, unable to help herself. "Wheat strains from before the purge. You're carrying four thousand years of agricultural memory in a crate with a shipping label."

"Noted for the catalogue," Voss said, without looking up. "Provenance: recovered material, Serrano Range. The Collection prefers *recovered*. It sounds so much kinder than *taken*."

"You could still stop this," Dominic said. "You could walk out of here and tell them the vault collapsed. Nobody would check. Nobody checks anything anymore — that's the whole problem, isn't it? Provenance belongs to whoever pays. Well, nobody's paying you to be honest, so be dishonest in our favor for once."

Voss paused, crowbar in hand, and for a moment — just a moment — he looked tempted. Dominic filed that away, the way his mother would have filed away a promising sherd: *interesting, return later, context unclear.*

"You don't understand what they'd do," Voss said finally. "To me. To my—" He stopped. Started over. "I have obligations too, Mr. Zhou. Everyone in this valley does. That's the entire shape of the world. You think you've refused a bargain. You've just refused *my* bargain. Yours is coming. Everyone's does."

"Maybe," Dominic said. "But I get to set the exchange rate."

The second cylinder came out of its cradle. The third. The sound of it — that soft ceramic scrape, that careful wrapping — was somehow more obscene than gunfire would have been. Ten thousand voices, and they were being lifted out one at a time and silenced with packing foam.

Tam had gone very still beside him. Dominic knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic with different numbers than everyone else in the room — boat numbers, river numbers, *how many seconds until the rapids* numbers. He caught Tam's eye. Tam gave the smallest possible nod toward the far wall, where the avalanche channel gaped behind its ancient counterweight mechanism — the thing they'd found on the way in, the vault's own last resort, designed by people who understood that some inheritances must be buried rather than surrendered.

*Not yet,* Tam's expression said. *Not yet. But soon.*

Dominic breathed. Ines flexed her bandaged hand. Behind them, the phosphorescent strips hummed their mineral hymn, and the dead nation waited to see what its custodians were made of.

"Last chance," Voss said, not turning from his work. "Index, or graves. I'd prefer the index. Graves are paperwork."

"You know," Dominic said, "for a man who used to be an archaeologist, you have a remarkable talent for digging your own hole."

Voss's shoulders went rigid.

And in the dark above the entrance stair, old stone shifted on old stone — a sound like a held breath finally being released — as Tam's hand found the counterweight lever, and the vault decided, on behalf of everyone inside it, that the negotiation was over.
