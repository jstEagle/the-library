## 26. Collapse Protocol

I want it on the record — my grandmother's record, the one she kept in her head for sixty years because paper could be burned — that I never wanted to go into the vault at all.

I said so. Twice. Once at the cairn, when the door ground open like a coffin clearing its throat, and once on the stair, when the warm dark came up to meet us and Ines Marchetti's headlamp caught carvings on the walls that made her go quiet in a way I did not like. Quiet people are thinking people, and thinking people in my experience are about to do something everyone regrets.

"Tam," Dominic had said, which was all he needed to say, because that is the kind of man he has become in the six weeks I have known him: the kind who says your name like it's a whole sentence.

So we went in. And now the Collection's men are coming down the stair behind us, six boots and six guns and one voice — Voss, calm as a customs officer — and the vault is full of ten thousand dead voices in sealed cylinders, a whole country's memory stacked to the ceiling, and the only person in this room who has ever been on the losing side of a siege before is me.

My grandmother fled Kharvath in the winter of '53 with a boat, a baby, and a song. She used to say: *When they come for what the dead have kept, the dead will not hand it over. The dead have had longer to practice.*

I did not understand her until tonight.

* * *

Here is what the room looks like when your life is ending in it, in case anyone asks later, which no one will, because the only witnesses will be either dead or rich:

Cylinders. Thousands of them. Bronze and clay and some kind of black glass, each one sealed with wax and stamped with the seal I have known since I was small enough to sit on my grandmother's knee — the mountain over the river, the mark of a country that the maps were ordered to forget. They stand in racks cut into the living rock, row after row after row, and the racks are not arranged the way you would arrange treasure. Treasure goes in the middle, where you can guard it. These go everywhere, evenly, like a man distributing seed to every field because he does not know which field will survive the winter.

Seed. That is what they are. I understood that before Ines said it out loud. Seed stocks. River charts. Songs, probably. The whole insurance policy of a nation that saw the axe coming and spent its last good years not running, but *copying*.

"Tam." Ines's voice, low, at my elbow. "The racks. Look at how they're anchored."

I had already looked. That was the problem. I had been looking at the racks since the moment my headlamp came up, because my grandmother did not raise a fool, and because I have spent my whole life on water, and water teaches you one lesson better than any teacher: *where the weight is, and where the weakness is.*

The racks were not built into the mountain. They were built *under* it. Every row sat in a channel cut into the floor, and the channels all ran the same direction — back and up, toward the rear wall, where the rock above the vault showed a long, deliberate seam. Not a crack. A seam. Cut stone, dressed and fitted, with a plug of softer rock set into it like a cork in a bottle.

And above the cork, if you followed the seam up with your light, the ceiling of the passage behind us — the long entry corridor we had walked down, the one with the beautiful carvings — was hung with a shelf of stone the size of a chapel roof.

"Tam," Ines said again, and her voice had changed. She had seen it too. She was an archaeologist; she had spent her career reading dead people's intentions out of stone. "Oh," she said. "Oh, you clever, terrible people."

"They built the door to be opened," I said. "And they built everything behind the door to be closed."

* * *

Let me tell you what my grandmother told me about the purge, because it matters, and because I need a moment before I do the thing I am about to do, and a man should fill his last quiet minutes with his grandmother's voice.

They came in the spring. Not soldiers — clerks. Men with lists. The lists said which scholars, which teachers, which mapmakers. Kharvath had been on the losing side of a war nobody in the villages had even been told they were fighting, and the new government had decided that the cleanest way to kill a country was to kill its memory. Burn the archives. Strike the name. Redraw the rivers.

My grandmother was seventeen. Her father was a keeper — that was the actual word, *keeper*, a job like priest or lighthouse-keeper, except what he kept was not a flame but a room. He put her in a fishing boat with her baby brother and told her the one thing he was allowed to tell her, which was not where the archive was, because he was a good keeper, but *that it would keep*. That the dead had made arrangements. That a country is not its maps.

Then he went back inside, and she rowed downstream, and she never saw him again, and she sang the leaving-song of Kharvath every year on the anniversary until the year she died, and she sang it badly, on purpose, she said, because *a song sung perfectly can be confiscated; a song sung badly belongs to whoever is singing it.*

I am telling you this so you understand that when the shooting started, I was not thinking like a smuggler. I was thinking like a grandson.

* * *

It went like this.

Voss came down the stair with his six men, and his voice came before him, reasonable as ever, the voice of a man who has never once in his life raised it because he has never once needed to. "Dr. Zhou. Dr. Marchetti. Mr. Tam." He said my name like he had practiced it, which frightened me more than the guns. "You've done remarkable work getting here. Truly. And now the hard part is over. There's no need for anyone to be a hero."

Dominic stood in front of the racks with his mother's ledger under his arm and said, "No."

Just that. I have heard Dominic Zhou say many words in six weeks, most of them apologies, some of them jokes so bad that Ines once threw a boot at him, but I had never heard him say *no* like that — flat, finished, a door closing. His mother's son after all.

"There's an index," Voss said. "A catalog. Somewhere in this room there is a master list of what's here. Give me the index, and the Collection's interest ends. You can have the rest. Publish it, donate it, bury it again — I genuinely don't care. I want the index."

"You want to know what's here," Ines said, "so you can decide what never existed."

"I want," Voss said, and for one moment the customs-officer calm slipped and something older and more tired showed through, "to go home. Give me the index."

And that is when I stopped listening, because Ines had drifted to my side, and her hand found my sleeve, and she said, very quietly, in the tone of a woman asking a favor she already knows will be granted: "The seam. The cork. Tell me you see it."

"I see it."

"Can you reach it?"

The cork was set into the rear wall, forty feet back, behind the last row of racks. Between us and it: open floor, six men with rifles, and a man named Voss who had already killed at least twice that I knew of on this journey and had apologized for neither.

"Slowly," Ines breathed. "They're watching Dominic. They're watching the door. Nobody watches the smuggler. Nobody has ever once watched the smuggler."

She was right. It is one of the great constants of my profession. Guards watch the gold, the scholar, the gun. The boatman is furniture. My grandmother crossed a militarized frontier in a fishing boat because the sentries were watching the bridge. There is a whole education in being overlooked, and I had a first-class one.

I moved. Not fast — fast is what gets furniture noticed. I moved the way you move on a pitching deck, loose and slightly wrong, a man drifting toward the back of the room the way men drift, aimlessly, and I put one rack between me and the nearest rifle, and then another, and my hand was on cold bronze, and my heart was going like a drum in a parade, and I thought: *Grandmother, if you have any pull with the dead, now is the hour.*

The cork was the size of a man's chest. It had been fitted to be removable — there were handholds cut into it, worn smooth, which means hands had been there before, which means keepers had stood exactly where I was standing and asked themselves the same question I was asking.

The question is: does it come out easy, or does it come out loud?

* * *

I got both hands into the handholds and I pulled, and it came out easy, and that was the loudest silence I have ever heard.

Because the moment the cork slid free, the mountain *noticed*. I don't know how else to say it. There was a sound under the sound — a deep, mineral shifting, like a ship settling at low tide — and every head in the vault turned toward me, and Voss said, in a voice that had finally, finally stopped being calm:

"Stop him."

And the dead defended themselves.

I have thought about how to describe the next ninety seconds, and I have decided that the honest way is the old way: the mountain fell. Not all at once. The seam above the corridor let go first — that chapel-roof shelf of stone that the Kharvathi keepers had cut free of the living rock and left balanced on a cork and a prayer — and it came down the entry passage like a river deciding to be a waterfall, and the passage was where four of Voss's six men were standing.

The gunfire was nothing. I want to be clear about that, because in the stories the gunfire is the big sound. The gunfire was *small*. Cracks, like green wood in a hot stove. What was big was the stone. The whole corridor exhaled, dust came out of the doorway like smoke from a struck bell, and the floor of the vault jumped under my feet hard enough to put me on one knee, and the racks sang — ten thousand cylinders humming in their channels like the strings of one enormous instrument — and somewhere in that hum I swear I heard the leaving-song, though Ines tells me that's impossible and Dominic tells me nothing, because Dominic was not there to tell me anything.

Because the corridor was not the only thing that fell.

The channels in the floor — the ones all running back and up — they were not drainage. They were *rails*. The whole vault floor was built to shed its weight backward, into the space behind the rear wall, and the rear wall was the next thing to go. The racks began to slide. Slowly at first, ponderous, a library deciding to lie down, and I was scrambling the other way through falling dust with Ines hauling me by the collar like a kitten, shouting words in Italian that I will not repeat but will treasure.

"The stair!" she was screaming. "The stair, the stair —"

The stair was half gone. The top of it had taken the corridor's stone on the shoulders and folded, and beyond the fold I could see, in the strobing of gunfire and headlamps, Voss on his feet with his coat white with dust, dragging one of his men out of the rubble, and I could see the other two of his six — no, one other, one had not come out — and I could see Dominic, at the mouth of a side passage I had never noticed, a low gallery behind the last rack, and I saw the rockfall come down between us like a curtain coming down between acts.

He did not scream. That is the thing I will carry. The ceiling came down between us and Dominic Zhou did not scream, and the last thing I saw of him was his headlamp beam, swinging once, and then the dust took even the light.

* * *

We came out of the dust into the upper passage on our hands and knees, Ines and I, coughing like the two oldest smokers in the Serrano Range, and behind us the mountain was still settling — long, grinding exhalations, the sound of a giant turning over in bed — and the entrance we had come in by three hours ago was no longer an entrance. It was a suggestion. A memory of a door.

Ines got her light up. Played it along the collapse. The beam found stone, and more stone, and a single bronze cylinder lying in the rubble like a dropped coin, its wax seal unbroken, its stamp facing up: the mountain over the river.

She picked it up. Held it. For a moment I thought she was going to weep, and I braced myself, because I had never seen Ines Marchetti weep and I suspected it would be like watching a glacier calve.

Instead she said, in a voice like a filed report: "The inner gallery. The one he was standing at. It's behind the fall line — the main collapse shed *past* it. If the gallery holds its roof, he could be alive. Trapped, but alive."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then he's with his mother," Ines said, "and she has some explaining to do."

We dug. I will not make a saga of it — we dug, with hands and a pry bar and a climbing hammer, and the dust was so thick we breathed it more than air, and every time the mountain groaned we stopped and looked at each other like two people listening for a knock inside a coffin, and then we dug again. My grandmother used to say the dead defend what they keep. She never said anything about the dead *helping*. But the fall had left a gap — a fissure, a breathing hole, air moving faintly through it from the far side — and where there is moving air there is a living man, or there was, or there will be if two fools with a pry bar have anything to say about it.

It was Ines who heard him first. She went still, hand raised, the way she went still over a promising trench, and I shut up — a miracle — and we both listened.

From behind the stone, faint, muffled, half-buried, came the sound of a man tapping rock with a piece of metal. Slow. Deliberate. Three taps, pause. Three taps, pause.

And then — I would swear this on my grandmother's boat — a voice, thin as paper through the stone, dry as dust, absolutely unmistakable:

"Ines? Tam? I'm fine. Mostly. I have a candle, half a water bottle, and — " a cough, a long one — "and I'm not alone."

Ines and I looked at each other through the dust.

"Who's with you?" she called, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, which I will also treasure.

A pause. Then, in a tone of a man reporting a shipwreck he had personally steered into:

"Voss."

* * *

We are digging. That is the whole of the situation, and it is nearly dawn by my reckoning, and the mountain groans, and we dig. Ines has the pry bar. I have my hands and my grandmother's leaving-song, which I am singing under my breath, badly, on purpose, because a song sung badly belongs to whoever is singing it, and tonight I am singing it for a man behind a wall of stone who once said my name like it was a whole sentence, and for the country that built this room, and for the dead who have kept their word so far.

The dead defend themselves. It seems they also, if you dig, let you in on the defense.

The mountain groans again. Ines says, "Faster."

I dig faster.
