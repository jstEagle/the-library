## 27. Buried Alive

The last thing Dominic heard before the world went white was Tam shouting his name — and then the mountain answered instead.

Stone came down like a verdict. Not all at once; that was the terrible part. It came in stages, a groan, a shudder, a rain of gravel the size of fists, and then the main event: a slab of the vault's outer gallery shearing loose and slamming across the corridor behind him like God closing a door. The concussion picked him up and set him down again three meters away, on his side, in the dark, with a sound in his ears like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

He lay there for what might have been thirty seconds or thirty years, taking inventory. Ribs: bruised, probably not broken — he could breathe, which was encouraging, though each breath came with a wet click on the left side. Left forearm: scraped raw, bleeding freely enough to feel dramatic but not arterial. Head: he probed carefully and found a lump above his ear already swelling to the size of an egg. All things considered, he'd had worse Tuesdays.

"Tam?" His voice came out as a croak. "Ines?"

Nothing. The dark swallowed it whole.

He fumbled for the headlamp strapped to his jacket pocket and found it cracked, one lens spiderwebbed, but when he thumbed the switch a weak yellow cone sputtered to life. He swept it around.

The corridor was gone. In its place: a wall of rubble from floor to ceiling, boulders wedged against each other in the casual, permanent way of geology. Fine dust hung in the air, turning his little cone of light into something solid, almost architectural. Somewhere beyond the wall — muffled, distant, possibly imagined — he thought he heard shouting. Then nothing. Then nothing kept being the answer.

"Ines! Tam!" He hammered on the rockfall with the heel of his good hand until his palm stung. The rocks did not care. Rocks never cared; his mother used to say that, kneeling at some trench wall, brushing dirt off a pot shard older than language. *The earth keeps what it keeps, Dominic. That's not cruelty. That's just bookkeeping.*

"Well," he said aloud, to no one, "the books are balanced now."

A cough answered him.

It came from his left, from deeper in the surviving stretch of corridor, and it was followed by a voice he knew better than he wanted to. Thin. Dusty. Infuriatingly composed.

"If you're quite finished performing for the dead, Mr. Zhou. There's a candle over here, and I appear to be pinned."

* * *

Voss was lying on his back beneath a shelf of fallen ceiling stone that had come down across his right leg from mid-thigh to knee. Not crushing it flat — the slab had caught on a fallen cylinder rack first, leaving a gap of perhaps ten centimeters — but pinning it thoroughly, the way a paperweight pins a document you very much want to read. His face was gray under the dust, and there was blood in his hairline, but his eyes were clear and cold and utterly himself.

Dominic stood at the edge of the headlamp's reach and considered, seriously, walking the other way. There wasn't much other way. The corridor ran maybe forty meters before ending in another collapse, but still. He could go sit in the dark alone, which had a certain dignity to it.

"You're bleeding," Voss observed.

"So are you."

"Yes, but I'm bleeding *usefully*. It makes me look sympathetic." He gestured, wincing, toward a canvas satchel near his hip. "Candle. Matches. There may be water. I haven't inventoried."

Dominic crouched, retrieved the satchel, and found inside it exactly what Voss said: a stub of candle in a tin holder, a matchbook gone soft with damp, two bottles of water, a foil blanket, and — because the universe has a sense of humor — a protein bar, almond flavor, the kind nobody buys voluntarily.

He lit the candle. The flame stood up small and steady, and the dark pulled back just far enough to make the situation legible: two men, one trapped, one free, roughly four hundred tons of Serrano granite between them and everyone they'd ever known.

Dominic sat down against the opposite wall, out of arm's reach, and opened one bottle of water. He drank. Then, after a moment he wasn't proud of, he passed the second bottle down to Voss.

Voss took it without comment. They drank in silence, which was somehow louder than arguing.

* * *

The air was the thing that started bothering him first. Not the cold — the vault held a strange cellar warmth, the deep-earth temperature his mother had once explained to him over a hotel breakfast when he was eleven and she was leaving again — but the quality of it. Stale. Pressed. Each breath felt slightly secondhand.

He did the arithmetic without wanting to. A corridor this size, sealed this well, two men. Days? He'd read somewhere it was more about panic than oxygen, that people suffocated themselves by breathing fast. He made a conscious effort to breathe slowly, which lasted about ninety seconds before the lump on his head began to throb in time with his pulse and he forgot all about it.

"We should conserve the candle," Voss said.

"It's my candle now."

"It was my candle twenty minutes ago."

"Finders keepers is a looter's ethic," Dominic said. "I'd have thought you'd approve."

Something moved through Voss's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile would be if the building were still standing. He settled back against the slab pinning his leg and closed his eyes. "Your mother would have liked that line."

"Don't." The word came out harder than Dominic intended, and the candle flame bent with the force of it. "You don't get to talk about her."

"She talked about you."

"I said don't."

Voss opened his eyes. In the candlelight they were pale, almost colorless, and very tired. "Mr. Zhou. We are buried under several hundred meters of mountain with a finite supply of air. You can spend it hating me, or you can spend it listening. I'm going to talk either way. I've been paid to be quiet for eleven years and I find I've lost the knack."

Dominic turned away, put his back to the man, and stared at the rockfall. The rockfall stared back. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped with the patience of something that had been dripping since before either of them was born and intended to continue long after.

He hated him. That was the simple version. Voss had come up the valley with six men and a mandate to strip a nation's grave. Voss had ordered witnesses buried. Voss had — whatever else lived in the ledger of it, whatever his mother had never written down — been there, in the years she wouldn't discuss, in the photographs where her smile had gone thin and professional.

But hatred, Dominic was discovering, required energy, and energy required air, and the air was running a tab.

* * *

An hour passed, or what he estimated was an hour, measured in drips and the slow shortening of the candle. Dominic tried digging twice — working at the base of the rockfall with a flattened piece of cylinder rack, moving stones the size of grapefruits and revealing only larger stones behind them, the geological equivalent of peeling an onion that fights back. Sweat ran cold down his spine. His ribs filed formal complaints.

"The collapse channel," Voss said from behind him, during the second attempt. "The Kharvathi built it deliberately. Your friend with the boat understood that faster than my engineers did. The archive was designed to bury itself rather than be taken. We're inside the mechanism."

"Wonderful. So we're not trapped by accident."

"We're trapped by architecture. It's more dignified." A pause. "You're wasting effort. The face you're digging won't move. It's keyed. Look at the joints — fitted, not fractured. The old builders cut those stones to lock. You'd need a week and machinery."

Dominic stopped. He looked. The stones did look fitted — too fitted, edges meeting edges with a precision no avalanche produces. He dropped the metal bar and sat back, breathing hard, dust coating his tongue.

"Why are you helping me?"

"I'm helping myself. If you dig us into a smaller hole, we die faster."

"That's not what I asked."

Voss was quiet long enough that Dominic turned around. The candle had burned down to half its height, and the light had changed character, softer, more forgiving, gilding the dust on Voss's gray face until he looked almost like a statue of himself.

"No," Voss said finally. "It isn't."

* * *

They rationed the second candle — Voss's satchel, it turned out, held a spare — lighting it only in shifts, talking in the dark between. Talking, because the alternative was listening to the drip and the blood in their own ears, and both of them had apparently decided that madness could wait.

It went in pieces. Voss asked questions in the flat tone of a man conducting an inventory: How badly was your ribs? Where did the boy with the boat learn rapids like that? Does Marchetti still annotate everything in pencil? And Dominic, to his own surprise, answered most of them, because answering was easier than silence, and because every answer seemed to land somewhere in Voss that echoed.

Then Voss said, apropos of nothing, "She kept a photograph of you in her field kit. You were maybe nine. Standing on a dock."

Dominic's breath stopped doing its job for a moment. "You're lying."

"I'm many things, Mr. Zhou. About her, I have never needed to lie. She showed it to me the last season we worked together. Said—" his voice caught on something, cleared it with the efficiency of a man clearing debris, "—said you were the only excavation she ever regretted finishing."

"What does that even mean?"

"It means she was a woman who expressed love the way Kharvathi architects expressed safety. Structurally. Load-bearing. Invisible until the day you need it to hold."

Dominic laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, though some arrived later, uninvited. "That's the most pretentious thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once dated a poet."

"Marchetti mentioned the poet."

"Marchetti needs to learn what a confidence is."

The dark held them. The drip counted. And Dominic found, sitting there in the black with his enemy four meters away, that the hatred hadn't vanished — it had simply gotten tired, the way a muscle gets tired, and in its loosened grip other things had begun to surface. Things like: *this man knew her seasons.* Things like: *he saw the photograph before I did.*

"My leg is worse," Voss said eventually, in the tone of a weather report. "I want to say that plainly, so you can plan around it. The foot is numb. That's not good. When we're out — if we're out — I'll need extraction within hours or the surgeons will take it below the knee."

"Noted."

"You'll leave me anyway. When the chance comes. I would."

"Probably," Dominic admitted. It felt important, suddenly, to be honest in here, where lies would just be extra weight. "But probably not before I know why a man who works for the Halvard Collection carries candles and a foil blanket like he's *planning* to get buried."

Silence. Long enough that Dominic thought Voss had gone to sleep, or further.

Then, in the dark, almost gently: "Because I helped design the collapse protocol. Eleven years ago. With your mother. And I've spent every year since waiting for it to close over me."

The drip counted on. Dominic sat with that, turning it over like a stone too heavy to lift, feeling its true weight arrive.

"Start from the beginning," he said.

And in the dark, with half a candle left and the air growing thin, Voss did.
