## 28. What Voss Wants

The candle had maybe an hour left in it, and Voss had done the arithmetic twice because he didn't like the answer either time.

They were in a pocket the size of a generous coffin — two men, one rockfall behind them, one rockfall ahead, and between those bookends a wedge of mountain that had decided, sometime in the last six hours, to stop being architecture and start being geology again. The air tasted of stone dust and old cold. Somewhere above them, muffled through forty feet of collapsed corridor, the vault's countermeasure was still settling in slow, grinding increments, like a giant turning over in its sleep.

Dominic Zhou sat across from him with his back against the wall and his broken arm cradled against his chest, watching Voss the way you'd watch a snake you'd found in your boot.

"You should sleep," Voss said.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep much anymore." He turned the candle a quarter-turn on its flat stone, mostly for something to do with his hands. "Occupational habit. You spend enough years waiting for someone to come through a door at three in the morning, you stop trusting the dark to hold."

"That's not an occupational habit," Dominic said. "That's a guilty conscience."

Voss laughed. It surprised him more than it seemed to surprise the boy — a dry, cracked sound, like a door hinge in a house nobody lived in. "Yes," he said. "It is. You're your mother's son after all."

He regretted it the moment it was out. Not because it was cruel — cruelty was easy, cruelty was a tool he kept oiled — but because it was true, and true things had a way of expanding in small spaces until there was no room left for anything else.

* * *

They had been silent for perhaps twenty minutes when Dominic said, "You knew her."

It wasn't a question. Voss appreciated that. Questions could be dodged; statements just sat there, waiting.

"I knew her," he said.

"At the dig. The one where—" Dominic stopped. Recalibrated. Voss watched him do it, watched the boy assemble the story he'd been told — fragments, rumors, the shape of a scandal that had ended a career before it properly began — and then watch him arrive, visibly, at the wrong end of it. "They said she falsified the survey data. That's what the tribunal record says. That Mei-Lin Zhou fabricated stratigraphy to inflate the significance of the Halvard-sponsored excavation, and that her partner's negligence contributed to the tunnel collapse that killed—"

"Say it," Voss said quietly. "You've carried the name this long. Say it here, where it can't hurt anyone new."

"Killed her partner." Dominic's voice was flat. "Aldous Voss."

"Aldous Voss," Voss agreed. "Dead eleven years. Very tidy death. Nice little plaque at the Institute, I'm told. I've never seen it. I try not to visit my own grave — bad luck."

The candle flame leaned in a draft that came from nowhere and went nowhere. Dominic had gone very still, the stillness of a man reordering every fact he owned.

"You faked your death."

"The Collection faked it. I signed the paperwork." Voss shrugged, and immediately regretted it — his ribs had opinions about shrugging now, strong opinions, delivered in the language of white-hot pain. "There were forms. There are always forms. That's the thing nobody tells you about evil, Zhou. It doesn't cackle. It has a notary."

"Why?"

"Because a dead man is cheaper than a lawsuit." He heard his own voice go flat and procedural, the voice he used for inventory, and hated it, and kept going anyway, because if he was going to do this — and it appeared he was going to do this, the words were already climbing out of him like survivors from rubble — then he would do it completely. "The tunnel collapse killed four people. Local laborers. Men whose names never made any tribunal record, because the tribunal record was written by Halvard's lawyers before the dust settled. The survey data wasn't falsified by Mei-Lin. It was falsified *for* her — altered upstream, in the report chain, so that when the shaft failed, the failure would land on her. She was getting too loud. She'd found something in the range that the Collection wanted very badly and wanted very quietly, and she'd started asking questions about why a private collector needed a buried nation's archive so much more than any museum did."

"And they buried her reputation instead."

"They buried her reputation, and they buried me — literally, briefly, and then bureaucratically — and they paid off four widows with settlements that required silence, and the world moved on. That's how it works. That's all it is. A machine for moving on." Voss looked at his hands. In the candlelight they looked like someone else's hands, older, more compromised. "I survived the collapse. Four hours under the rock. When they dug me out, Herr Halvard's man was standing over me with a blanket and a contract, and he said — I remember this exactly, I have always remembered this exactly — he said, 'Congratulations, Dr. Voss. You're the only witness, which makes you either our biggest problem or our best employee. The salary is better.'"

"What did you say?"

"I said yes." He met Dominic's eyes. "Eleven years ago, half-dead, concussed, with four dead men under the mountain and my name already being carved onto a memorial plaque, I said yes. And every year since, the yes has gotten easier to say and harder to survive. That's the trick of it. Debt compounds. Not money debt — I mean the other kind. Every year you work for them, you know more things that require you to keep working for them. Eventually the knowledge and the employment become the same thing. You don't have a job at the Collection. You *are* the Collection's, the way a field is the farmer's."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. Then: "My mother's debts. After she died. The ones I've been drowning in for two years." His jaw worked. "Those were Collection paper, weren't they. They didn't even wait for her body to cool."

"They waited for the funeral," Voss said. "I'm told someone thought that showed restraint."

* * *

The candle was down to a thumb's width. Voss did the arithmetic again, out of habit, and got the same answer, and put the arithmetic away.

"You want to know the stupid part?" he said.

"There's a stupid part?"

"There's always a stupid part. The stupid part is that I told myself, for the first five years, that I was playing a long game. Gathering evidence. Waiting for my moment. Every informant network has a man like me somewhere in its guts telling himself the same story — *I'm not really one of them, I'm a spy among pirates.* It's how they keep you. They don't need chains. They let you supply your own." He smiled, and it felt like borrowing someone else's face. "Then around year six I went looking for my evidence file — the real one, the one I'd been building — and discovered I'd stopped updating it three years earlier. Do you understand? I hadn't decided to stop. I'd just... stopped. The way you stop writing letters home. The long game requires believing there's a long, and somewhere in there I'd quietly cancelled the future and kept collecting the paycheck."

"So why come here?" Dominic shifted against the wall, wincing. "If you're theirs body and debt — why lead a team up a mountain to strip a dead nation's insurance policy? What does Aldous Voss get out of it? Not glory. You just told me you don't believe in the future."

"No," Voss said. "But I believe in exits."

He let that sit. The boy was quick — Mei-Lin's quickness, the same impatient intelligence that had made her unbearable at conferences and irreplaceable in the field — and Voss watched him get there in stages.

"The contract," Dominic said slowly. "Your employment. It's tied to the archive somehow."

"My contract has a completion clause. Deliver the Kharvath index to the Collection, and my obligations terminate. All of them. Including the financial ones." Voss turned the candle again. A quarter inch left, maybe less. "Halvard wrote it himself, years ago, as a joke — or what passes for a joke among people who own other people. *Bring me the library, Doctor, and you may retire.* He never expected anyone to find it. Mei-Lin spent nine years looking and only ever found the door's address, and they killed her partner and burned her life over an *address*. But then her son dredges a bronze chest out of a river in Kestrel Bend, and suddenly the joke clause is the only door out of the building, and Herr Halvard remembers he wrote it, and sends me."

"And if you'd delivered it?"

"Then I'd be free. Free to do what, I genuinely no longer know. That's the part that kept me awake on the climb, Zhou, I'll tell you honestly, since we're apparently doing honesty now — I couldn't picture it. Retirement. Freedom. I stood at the col looking down into this valley and tried to imagine the man who walks away from all this, and I couldn't see his face. Eleven years is a long time to practice being a tool. Tools don't have faces."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to shield it.

"But you hesitated," Dominic said. "In the vault. Before Tam triggered the collapse. Voss ordered the stripping and then — you gave the order twice. I heard you. Nobody gives an order twice unless part of them is hoping it won't be obeyed."

"Did I?" Voss said. "How observant of you. Your mother used to do that — catch the second version of a thing, the revision, the tell. Drove me mad. She'd be standing in a trench holding a potsherd and she'd say, 'You said Bronze Age, but you said it twice, so you think it's Iron Age,' and she'd be right, and I'd hate her for a full day." He paused. "Yes. I hesitated. Ten thousand sealed cylinders, each one a voice that refused to be erased, and my orders were to crate them like dishware. I hesitated. Write it on my plaque."

"It won't absolve you."

"No," Voss agreed. "Nothing will. That's rather the point of what I am. Understand the difference, Zhou, because your mother understood it and I need you to hear it from someone who isn't her: there is a difference between a man who is sorry and a man who is *owed*, and I am the second kind. I'm not asking forgiveness. Forgiveness is a currency and I've spent my whole life learning that currencies are the problem. I'm telling you what I want because we may both die in this hole in the next few hours, and a dying man's wants should be on the record. I want out. Not of this pocket of rock — of the whole arrangement. The contract, the debt, the machine. I wanted the exit clause. That's all I ever wanted. And I told myself it was different from greed because what I was trading for it was somebody else's heritage, and I believed that right up until the moment a smuggler pulled a lever and the mountain said no."

* * *

The candle died without ceremony. Just a lean, a stutter, and then the dark closed in like water.

For a while neither spoke. Voss listened to the mountain settle, and to Dominic breathing — carefully, rhythmically, a man managing pain through discipline, another inheritance from his mother — and to his own heart doing its stubborn, unglamorous work.

"She knew," Dominic said at last, in the black. "Didn't she. About you. About the fake death, the contract. That's why she sent me away. That's why she erased herself from my life. She wasn't hiding the archive from them. She was hiding *me* from becoming you."

The observation landed in Voss's chest like a dropped tool.

"Yes," he said finally. "I think she did. I found traces, over the years — she was careful, but she was careful the way honest people are careful, which is to say not nearly enough. She knew what I'd become. I don't know how. Maybe she saw it before I did. She always did see the second version of a thing." He exhaled. "She could have exposed me. One letter, one leak, and the Collection loses its best retriever. Instead she stayed quiet. For eleven years she let me hunt her shadow across half the world's archives and never once burned me back. I used to think it was mercy. Now I think it was worse than mercy. I think she was leaving the door open. I think she believed there was still a man inside the tool, and she was willing to wait decades for him to walk out on his own legs."

"She waited too long."

"We all waited too long. That's the family disease, apparently. You Zhou children of circumstance — you inherit patience like a curse." Voss laughed softly in the dark. "God, she'd have loved this. Trapped in a hole with her son and her ghost of a partner, air running out, and the two of us discovering we're the same kind of damned. She'd have made us dig. She never met a wall she didn't take personally."

Silence again. But it was a different silence now — thinner, more porous. Voss noticed that Dominic's breathing had changed, gone quicker, shallower. Pain, or thought, or both.

"The air," Dominic said. "It's thinner than it was an hour ago."

"I know."

"How long do we have?"

"If we sit here and philosophize? Six hours. Perhaps eight. Long enough to die comfortably and well-documented." Voss pressed his palm flat against the rockfall ahead of them. Cold stone, packed tight — but above it, near the ceiling of their pocket, he'd felt it earlier and felt it again now: a thread of moving air. Faint. Cold. Alive. "But there's a fissure. Up high, two meters, maybe three. Air's coming through it, which means daylight's probably on the other side of it, which means the rockfall thins out somewhere above us. Which means—"

"Which means we dig," Dominic said.

"Which means we dig." Voss found, to his considerable annoyance, that he was smiling in the dark. "One arm between us, Zhou, and a shared talent for surviving things that should have killed us. Your mother dug me out of a collapse once with a trowel and pure fury. It seems fitting that her son finishes the job."

Above them, faint as a promise, the fissure breathed its thin cold thread of daylight-smelling air — and somewhere beyond it, fainter still, Voss thought he could hear water. The monsoon, arriving early, hammering the valley mouth where two people were almost certainly standing in the rain believing him dead.

"Hold on," he murmured, to no one, to everyone. "I'm coming out of my grave a second time. This one's going on the plaque."
