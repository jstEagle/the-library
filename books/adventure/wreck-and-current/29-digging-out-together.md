## 29. Digging Out Together

The dark after an avalanche has a texture. That was the first thing Dominic learned in the minutes after the rockfall sealed the passage behind them — that darkness isn't one thing. There's the darkness of a closed room, which has edges. And there's the darkness of a mountain that has decided you are part of its geology now, which has no edges at all, only weight.

He was on his side. He knew this because his cheek was against stone and his left arm was pinned under something that groaned when he breathed, which meant it was either rubble or his own rib doing something ribs shouldn't do. Dust filled his mouth. Somewhere very close, another person was coughing.

"Zhou." The voice came out of the black like gravel poured through a funnel. "Are you dead?"

"Not yet."

"Pity. I had money on you."

Dominic laughed, and immediately regretted it, because laughing cost him a white flare of pain from the pinned arm and a long, wheezing negotiation with his lungs afterward. When he could breathe again he said, "Voss. Where are you?"

"Two meters, perhaps. Follow my voice. Mind the—" A scraping sound. A curse in three languages, two of which Dominic didn't recognize. "Mind everything. Everything is trying to kill us."

He crawled. It wasn't dignified. His right hand found broken stone, then finer dust, then — abruptly — a boot. The boot was attached to a leg, the leg to a man lying on his back somewhere in the absolute dark, and the man made a sound when Dominic's hand closed around his ankle that was halfway between pain and outrage.

"Your other ankle," Voss said tightly. "I appear to have misplaced the use of the first one."

* * *

They found each other the way people find each other in disasters: clumsily, painfully, with more elbows than necessary. Dominic got a hand under Voss's shoulder and hauled him upright against a slab of fallen ceiling, and Voss hissed through his teeth and then went quiet in a way that worried Dominic more than any noise would have.

"I need light," Dominic said.

"There is no light."

"There's always light. There's a fissure somewhere. Rockfalls don't seal perfectly. My mother taught me that." He said it before he could stop himself, and felt the words land between them like a dropped tool.

Voss was silent for a moment. Then: "She taught me that too. Same lesson. Different decade." A rustle. A click. And then a small, unsteady circle of candlelight bloomed in the dark — a stub of candle in a tin cup, the kind of thing field archaeologists carried the way priests carried last rites.

In the light, Voss looked terrible. Dominic supposed he looked terrible too, but it was easier to catalog someone else's damage. Voss's face was gray with dust, cut along the jaw, and his left leg was bent wrong below the knee, the trouser already darkening. He caught Dominic looking and said, with terrible mildness, "It's broken. You may say it out loud. I've said it out loud twice already and the mountain hasn't changed its mind."

"We need to splint it."

"We need to get out. Air first, legs later." Voss lifted the candle and turned slowly, and the flame leaned — just barely, just once — toward the wall on their left. Toward a seam in the rock where the dust was moving. Faintly. Inward.

"Draft," Dominic breathed.

"Your mother's lesson number two," Voss said. "Rock breathes if you listen."

* * *

The seam was barely a hand's width wide, choked with rubble, and it took them an hour of digging to confirm what they both already knew: it went somewhere. Cold air moved through it in slow pulses, and each pulse smelled faintly of pine and snowmelt and the outside world, and Dominic had never been so moved by the smell of trees in his life.

The problem was arithmetic. One of them could dig. The other had to hold the candle, because there was exactly one candle and it was Voss's, and because a man with a shattered leg cannot dig anyway. So Dominic dug, and Voss held the light, and for the first hour they did not talk, because some arrangements are too fragile for conversation.

It was Voss who broke the silence, eventually, in the tone of a man commenting on weather.

"You dig like her."

Dominic's hands stopped in the rubble. "Don't."

"I'm not flattering you. She dug like someone apologizing to the ground. Fast, furious, sorry about every stone she moved. You do the same thing. It's inefficient. It's also—" He paused, and Dominic heard him choose the word carefully, which was unlike him. "—honest."

"She told you about me?" Dominic kept digging. Faster now. Apologetically.

"She never talked about you. That was the point of her, as far as I knew her. She talked around you. The way water talks around a stone in a riverbed — you could see the shape of it in how she moved around certain subjects. Birthdays. Photographs. The word 'son.'" The candle flickered as Voss shifted. "I used to think she was cold. Later I understood she was holding a door shut with her whole body, and none of us could see the door."

"And now?"

"Now I know what was behind it," Voss said quietly. "I'm lying behind it. With you."

Dominic threw a rock aside harder than necessary. "You don't get to be poetic. You sent six men into that vault to strip it and bury witnesses. I watched you offer to buy my mother's life work while standing in the room where she recorded her own confession."

"Yes," Voss said. No defense in it. Just acknowledgment, flat as a ledger line. "And then Tam pulled a lever built by a civilization that decided its memory was worth more than its survival, and now we're both going to die in here unless you keep digging, so perhaps we can table the moral accounting until one of us can walk."

It was such an absurdly reasonable thing to say that Dominic almost laughed again. He settled for digging.

* * *

Hours passed. He had no way to count them except the candle, which shortened like a fuse. They ate half a protein bar Voss produced from an inner pocket — "Field rations. Old habit. Mei-Lin used to carry dates; I never acquired the taste" — and split the water from a dented flask, Voss insisting Dominic take the larger share and dressing it up as strategy. "You're digging. A tired digger digs slower. This isn't kindness, Zhou, it's logistics."

"Everything with you is logistics."

"Everything with everyone is logistics. Kindness is just logistics you're embarrassed about."

Dominic worked. The seam widened by centimeters. Rubble came away in pieces the size of fists, then loaves, then small boulders he had to roll backward down onto the chamber floor, each one landing with a boom that made them both flinch and wait, listening for the mountain's opinion. Twice the mountain grumbled. Twice it let them live.

Somewhere in the middle hours — the candle told him nothing useful anymore — Voss started talking. Not confessing, exactly. Narrating. The way men narrate when they're in pain and trying to stay ahead of it.

"The first site I ever worked was in the Karst country. Nineteen years old, volunteer, thought archaeology was about treasure because I'd read too many novels." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "My supervisor caught me prying at a burial with a trowel like it was a paint can, and instead of firing me she sat me down in the dirt and said: *the ground is not a safe. The ground is a library, and everything in it is checked out to somebody.*"

Dominic's hands slowed.

"That's why the Collection hired me, in the end," Voss went on. "Not despite that. Because of it. They wanted people who understood provenance — who understood that objects mean things — so they could buy the meaning and sell the object separately. I told myself for twenty years that I was protecting sites by getting to them first. Controlled destruction. Better us than dynamite fishermen." The candle guttered; he steadied it with a careful hand. "Mei-Lin never believed a word of it. She stood in a tent in the Serrano foothills and told me I'd sold the library card and kept calling myself a librarian. I reported her excavation permit violations within the week. You know the rest."

"I know the rest," Dominic said. His voice came out low and even, which surprised him. He'd expected rage. What he felt instead was older and stranger — the feeling of finally seeing the whole shape of a thing that had wrecked his childhood from the edges inward. "She lost everything because of you. Ten years of permits. Her name. Me — effectively. She sent me away because people like you were circling, and I spent fifteen years thinking she just... preferred the work."

"She did prefer the work," Voss said. "Both things are true. People are rarely one crime or one virtue. We're ledgers." He exhaled. "Bad metaphor. Forget it."

"No," Dominic said, surprising himself. "Keep it. I grew up on ledgers."

* * *

The candle died with maybe a finger of wax left, and they dug the last stretch blind.

This should have been terror. Instead it was almost restful — the draft stronger now, cold and clean on his sweat-soaked face, the rubble coming away softer, mixed with frozen soil and root fibers, which meant near-surface, which meant *close*. Voss talked him through it from behind, voice hoarse, calling out angles he couldn't possibly see: "Left. Your left. No, your other left — God, no wonder your mother despaired of you." Gentle humor worn thin over fear, and Dominic recognized it because it was exactly how he talked when he was scared, and he hated how much that told him about the two of them.

Then his fingers broke through into moving air, and beyond the air, a gray thread of light.

"Dawn," he whispered. Or dusk. He didn't care. He put his shoulder into the gap and stones gave way around him in a rush of cold, and suddenly he was looking at a sky the color of pewter, streaked with the first bruised pink of morning, framed by a fissure barely wider than his chest. Snowfields above. Pines below. The world, enormous and indifferent and *there*.

"Voss." His voice cracked. "There's a way out. It's tight, but—"

"Go through," Voss said. "Assess. Come back if it opens onto anything."

"It opens onto the mountainside. It's a hundred-meter scramble down scree to the tree line. I can go for help—"

"Zhou." Voss's voice had gone flat and precise, the voice of a man spending his last reliable coin. "A broken leg does not fit through a fissure. You know it. Stop performing."

Dominic knelt there in the cold draft, half in the mountain and half out of it, and hated the arithmetic. Because Voss was right, and because leaving him meant going alone down a scree slope on a bad arm to find help that might be days away, and because staying meant two corpses instead of one.

He was still calculating when the mountain made its decision for him.

It began as a tick. A single stone shifting somewhere above the fissure, dislodged by their digging, by the thaw, by nothing. Dominic heard it and looked up and had time to think *no* — and then the secondary collapse came down the seam like a throat clearing, a rush of stone and ice, and he did the only thing his body agreed to do, which was throw himself sideways out of the gap.

Voss did the other thing. The thing with the candle and the bad leg and the twenty years of logistics.

He shoved Dominic clear with both hands — full force, no hesitation, the hardest physical act of mercy Dominic had ever received — and the collapse took the fissure, and the ledge, and most of the space where Voss had been sitting.

* * *

When the thunder stopped, Dominic was lying in the open air on a shelf of snow-dusted rock, gasping, one arm bleeding, staring at a wall of fresh rubble where the fissure had been.

Silence. Long enough to be unbearable.

Then, from deep inside, muffled but unmistakable, furious and alive:

"*Zhou.* If you are standing out there feeling grateful, stop it. Gratitude is useless. Get me out."

Dominic laughed — really laughed, wet-eyed, wheezing, doubled over with cracked ribs — because of course. Of course the man was fine. Wedged in a pocket behind the new fall, bruised and buried to the waist and swearing in four languages, but fine, because the universe apparently intended to make Dominic earn this rescue honestly rather than receive it as a gift.

He wiped his face. He took inventory: dawn light, a slope, a body that mostly worked, and behind a wall of stone, a man who had once destroyed his mother's life and had just spent his hands saving her son without a second's calculation.

"Hey, Voss," he called, already turning to assess the scree run below, already planning the descent, the tree line, the village, the rope, the return. "That library your supervisor mentioned? The one where everything's checked out to somebody?"

A pause from inside the rock. "What about it?"

"I'm checking you out." He spat dust, tested the slope with his boot, and began to climb down. "One day. Maybe two. Don't spend the time philosophizing — I'll hear it through the wall and I'm not strong enough for it yet."

From the depths came a sound that was either a groan or the closest thing to laughter Voss had left, and Dominic went down the mountain fast, apologetic to every stone he moved, honest all the way.
