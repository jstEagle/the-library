## 30. The Long Way Down

Ines Marchetti had attended eleven funerals in her life and had never once believed the deceased was actually dead until now.

She stood at the mouth of the dry valley with her hands jammed in her armpits against the cold, watching the dust settle over the rockfall, and she thought: *this is the one.* Eleven funerals of colleagues, aunts, a husband she had outlived by twenty-two years and never quite forgiven for dying mid-argument — all of them had left some residue of doubt, some small stubborn voice saying *perhaps, perhaps*. But the mountain had just dropped four hundred tons of stone on a man she'd known for three weeks, and there was no voice at all. Only arithmetic.

Tam stood beside her, saying nothing, which was unlike him. Tam's silences were usually load-bearing; you could hang things on them. This one was just empty.

"He was under the second collapse," Tam said finally. "The one that came after I pulled the countermeasure lever. The channel was designed to fail inward. I did the math while we were running." He spat, a small bitter gesture he'd learned from his grandmother, who had apparently spat at a great many things in her flight from Kharvath. "The math says nobody walks out of that."

"The math also said we'd beat the snows," Ines said. "And here we are, losing to them."

"That's not the same kind of math."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Above them the sky was doing something obscene. All morning the clouds had been stacking on the southern horizon like plates in a dishonest restaurant — layer on layer on layer, grey going violet going black, the monsoon arriving early and without apology. By afternoon the light had gone the color of old pewter, and the air had that thick, expectant quality of a room before bad news. Somewhere up-valley, thunder spoke in a language Ines didn't care to translate.

"We should go," Tam said. "Now, while the trail's still a trail."

"I know."

"Marchetti."

"I *know*, Tam." She heard her own voice crack on his name and hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it, hated the mountain most of all. She turned her back on the valley mouth. It was the hardest physical act of her life, harder than the goat path, harder than the col, and she had once spent nine days in a cave in Tashkurgan eating boiled boot leather, so she knew from hard acts.

They went south.

* * *

Ines grieved the way she worked: systematically, with notes.

This was not a choice so much as a congenital condition. Mei-Lin Zhou had once told her, over a bottle of terrible Georgian wine in Tbilisi, "You catalogue everything, Ines. You'd catalogue your own funeral." And Ines had replied, "At least someone would get the details right," and they had laughed, because it was true, and because laughing was cheaper than admitting that the cataloguing was how she kept the world from dissolving.

So she catalogued.

Item: he complained about his feet every single morning, loudly, as if the feet were a separate entity he was forced to travel with against his will, and by the third day Ines had started complaining about hers too, competitively, and it had become the only ritual any of them observed.

Item: he read maps upside down. Not incompetently — deliberately. He claimed he remembered terrain better when he'd first seen it inverted, which was either nonsense or genius, and Ines had spent an entire evening at the col trying to disprove it and failed, which had annoyed her more than anything else on the expedition.

Item: he carried his mother's compass even though it pointed nowhere useful, and when Tam asked him why, he'd said, "Because it's honest about it," and Tam had looked at him the way Tam looked at very few things, and Ines had pretended not to notice.

Item: he listened. Actually listened — waited through the whole sentence, asked the follow-up question, remembered the answer two days later. In forty years of archaeology, Ines could count on one hand the number of men who'd done that, and she'd have fingers left over.

Item: he was afraid of heights and admitted it, out loud, to two near-strangers, on a mountain. Ines had spent her whole career around people who would rather fall than confess fear, and half of them had fallen.

She walked and catalogued, and the catalogue grew, and somewhere past the second river crossing she understood what she was actually doing: she was reconstructing, item by item, the case Mei-Lin Zhou must have made for her son across thirty years of silence. Because Mei-Lin had seen all of this. That was the thing Ines kept circling back to, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Mei-Lin had sent the boy away, had erased herself from his life, had let him grow up believing himself unloved and unremarkable — and she had done it knowing exactly what he was. You don't build a supply cache at a mountain col and address it to *my child, if you're reading this* unless you know your child can read a situation. You don't leave him custodian of a dead nation's memory unless you know he'll carry it instead of selling it.

Mei-Lin had seen him. Ines was only now catching up, and she was doing it alone, on a mountainside, too late to tell anyone.

"You're making lists," Tam said. They had stopped to rest under an overhang while the first fat drops began spotting the dust around them. "I can see it. Your lips move."

"My lips do not move."

"They move. When you're working something out, they move. Like a woman arguing with a ledger."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. "I'm cataloguing."

"The dead?"

"The living, mostly. The dead are easy. The dead are finished." She watched the rain thicken, sheeting off the overhang's lip. "I'm cataloguing what she saw in him. His mother. Because somebody should, and she's not here to do it, and he's not here to be seen."

Tam was quiet for a moment. Then: "His grandmother used to say my family kept our country alive by remembering it wrong. Every telling changed a little. She said that wasn't corruption. She said that was digestion." He shrugged. "Maybe that's what you're doing. Digesting him."

"That is the least comforting thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once shared a tent with Voss."

Tam grinned despite everything, despite the rain and the rockfall and the boy-shaped absence between them. "Good. Comfort's for people with time."

* * *

They lost the trail twice that day.

The first time it simply ceased, dissolved into a scree slope that the early snows had already begun to salt white, and they spent an hour traversing on faith and Tam's grandmother's stories — *the smugglers' route always keeps the water sound on its left, always, even when you can't see the water, because the smugglers built it blind in fog and trusted their ears* — until the sound of meltwater obligingly reappeared on their left and the trail reassembled itself out of loose stone like a shy animal.

The second time, the trail didn't come back, and they made their own way down through scrub pine, Ines leading because Tam's knee had begun to speak to him in the imperative mood, and it was during this stretch, hands on wet bark, boots skidding on needles, that the grief ambushed her properly.

It happened between one tree and the next. One moment she was navigating; the next she was standing still with both palms flat against a pine trunk, breathing like a bellows, and the catalogue had turned on her. Because the items weren't neutral anymore. Item: he complained about his feet. Item: he read maps upside down. Each one was now a closed account, a fact that would never accrete another fact, and the finality of it — the sheer archival completeness of a dead man — hit her with a force that no funeral had ever managed, because funerals came with casseroles and eulogies and other people's shoulders, and this came with pine sap and rain.

She had known him three weeks. Three weeks. She had known Mei-Lin Zhou for thirty years, had fought with her in print and in person, had spent a decade convinced the woman was the most insufferably rigorous mind in the field and another decade grudgingly revising that into *the most insufferably rigorous mind in the field, and possibly the bravest, though I'd die before telling her.* Thirty years, and Ines had never once been inside Mei-Lin's actual life — only adjacent to it, orbiting it, rivaling it. And then Mei-Lin died, and left behind a son who was a locked room, and Ines had followed him up a mountain partly for the archive and partly — she could admit it now, to the pine tree, if to no one else — because the locked room was the last door into Mei-Lin that existed anywhere on earth.

And the mountain had closed it.

"Ines." Tam's hand on her shoulder. Gentle, for a man who ran rapids for criminals. "Tree's not going anywhere."

"I'm aware."

"You're hugging it."

"I am *bracing* against it, there's a difference—"

"There really isn't." But he waited, and after a while she pushed off the trunk and wiped her face with a sleeve and pretended the rain explained everything, and Tam had the grace to pretend to believe her, and they went down.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a boulder field, and Ines could not sleep, and so she did what she always did with sleepless nights: she worked.

Her notebook was nearly full. She sat close to the tiny flame of their sheltered stove — Tam insisted fires were suicide, but a stove cupped in stone was, in his professional opinion, merely reckless — and she went back through the pages, and found that without meaning to she had written a portrait. Not of the archive. Of the boy.

*Zhou, Dominic,* she had written at the top of one page, in the formal register she used for site reports. *Custodian, second half. Observations.*

Below it, entries in her cramped hand:

*Doubts his own competence hourly. Acts anyway. This is rarer than competence.*

*Defers to expertise without ego — asked me to explain the cipher twice rather than pretend understanding. Mei-Lin never once asked me anything. Pride was her armor and her cage. He has neither, or has outgrown both.*

*Angry at his mother. Rightly. But the anger is load-bearing, not corrosive — he builds with it. Carried my pack at altitude without being asked and without mentioning it after. I checked. The straps were adjusted for my height.*

*Conclusion (provisional): she saw all of this from a distance of fifteen years and chose to protect it rather than possess it. I do not know if I would have had the strength. I am glad, professionally speaking, that I will never be tested.*

She read the last line three times. Then she took out her pen and, beneath it, added:

*Amendment: he is not dead. I refuse the premise. A man who reads maps upside down does not die under a rockfall; he reads the rockfall upside down and finds the way through.*

Which was sentiment, not evidence, and Ines Marchetti had built a career on never confusing the two. She stared at the amendment for a long time. Then she did not cross it out.

That, more than anything, frightened her.

* * *

Dawn came grey and drowning. The monsoon had arrived in earnest overnight — not rain so much as a vertical ocean, hammering the tarps, turning the trail below them into a brown ribbon of moving water. The southern approach, Ines knew, was closing hour by hour. Rivers that had been knee-deep on arrival would be chest-deep by tomorrow and impassable by the day after. The passes behind them were already sealed under snow. They were inside the closing fist of the season, and the only direction was down and out, fast, along routes Tam seemed to know by inheritance.

They packed in silence. Or mostly silence.

"Question," Tam said, cinching a strap. "When we get out. What do you do? With all of it. The archive, the ledger, the—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the whole drowned valley, the whole buried nation. "He had a plan. Refuse Voss, bury it, walk away. Very dramatic. Very him." His voice caught slightly on *him*, and he covered it with a cough. "You? You photograph everything. You keep records. What happens to Kharvath now?"

Ines looked at him for a long moment.

"Now," she said slowly, "Kharvath lives in a filing cabinet in my head, next to thirty years of Mei-Lin, next to three weeks of her son, next to a village elder who died pointing up-valley so strangers could live. Which is, historically speaking, how nations survive. Badly. In the wrong places. In people who were never supposed to carry them." She shouldered her pack. "It's not much of a plan. It's the only kind there is."

Tam nodded, satisfied, or pretending to be, and they went down into the rain.

The descent took all day and cost them the last of the dry matches. Twice Tam navigated by memory of his grandmother's escape stories — *she crossed the high bridge at Serel's Bend, but the bridge won't be there anymore, so we go where the bridge used to be and look for the rope anchors, the smugglers drilled them into the living rock, they last a hundred years* — and once, miraculously, it worked: rusted iron rings, ancient and absurd, protruding from a cliff face above a river gone mad with rainwater, exactly where a dead woman's story said they'd be. Ines touched one of the rings and felt the strangest sensation: history as a handrail. The dead holding the living upright.

By late afternoon the clouds had lowered until they were walking inside them, visibility at fifty meters, the world reduced to wet grey wool and the sound of water everywhere, above, below, within. Ines's frostbitten fingers had gone from painful to numb, which she knew clinically was worse, and she flexed them inside her gloves and refused to think about it.

It was Tam who heard it first.

He stopped so abruptly she walked into his pack. He held up a hand — listen — and tilted his head, and beneath the roar of the monsoon Ines strained, and heard nothing, and said so.

"Not nothing," Tam said. "Downstream. Voices. Or—" he frowned, listening harder, rain streaming off his jaw. "Or a whistle. A signal whistle. Three short, one long."

Ines went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather. "That's not a smuggler's signal."

"No," Tam agreed quietly. "Smugglers don't signal. Smugglers hide." He was already moving, scrambling down through the wet scrub toward the river's edge, and Ines followed, heart doing something complicated and painful in her chest, because she recognized the pattern — three short, one long — from a night train east, from a young man who'd invented it as a joke, a way to find each other in crowds, *in case we get separated, which we won't, but in case.*

They broke through the trees at the riverbank, and the rain parted for half a second — the way it sometimes does, a curtain twitching — and through the gap Ines saw the far bank, and the figure standing on it, swaying, mud to the eyebrows, one arm raised, holding something dark and dripping that might have been a recorder.

Three weeks of cataloguing collapsed into a single, unscholarly thought:

*I take back every entry. The amendment stands.*

Then the curtain of rain closed again, and the river roared between them, wide and brown and rising, and getting across it was going to kill one of them, and Ines found she didn't care even slightly.
