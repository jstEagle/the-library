## 31. Reunion in the Rain's Shadow

The valley mouth was a mile away, maybe less, and it might as well have been a country I'd have to apply to visit.

I carried Voss on my back because there was no other way to carry him. He was lighter than I expected — a man built out of discipline and debt, and both of those weigh nothing once they stop working. His arms hung over my shoulders. His recorder was in the inside pocket of my jacket, wrapped in a plastic bag I'd found in his own pack, because irony is the one thing the mountains give you for free.

"Put me down," he said, for the fourth time.

"No."

"It's a waste of—"

"If you say *time*," I said, breathing hard, "I'm going to drop you in the scree and let the goats have you."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, in a voice scraped thin by the rockfall and the hours since, he said, "You'd feel bad about it."

"I'd feel something," I admitted. "It might be relief."

"That's the spirit."

We came around the shoulder of the last ridge and the valley opened below us — a wide grey fan of gravel and dead water channels running down to the frontier river, which was no longer a river so much as a rumor of brown muscle. The monsoon clouds had stacked themselves against the southern sky all morning, purple-black, organized, patient, like a creditor who knows exactly when your payday is. And now the first outriders of the storm were coming up the valley as a grey curtain of rain, half a mile out and closing, hissing on the stone.

And there, at the mouth, small in the distance but unmistakable — a blue tarp, a figure standing under it, and a second figure crouched by a fire that was losing its argument with the damp.

Ines. Tam.

I stopped. I don't know why. Some instinct that said: *look at this. You will want to have looked at this.* Two people I'd been ready to die beside two days ago, standing under a tarp at the end of the world, and I'd crawled out of a mountain to reach them. My eyes were doing something embarrassing. I blamed the altitude. I'd been blaming the altitude for everything since Kestrel Bend and it had never once complained.

"They'll see you," Voss said against my ear. "Walk."

"I'm composing myself."

"You've been composing yourself for three hours. It's a very long piece."

I walked.

The rain reached us before the tarp did — the first fat, cold drops, then the curtain, then the full commitment, the sky deciding that this valley in particular had been getting away with something. Within thirty seconds I was soaked through and Voss was shivering against my back, and I heard him make a small sound that I chose, generously, to interpret as a cough.

The figure under the tarp turned. I watched the recognition happen in slow motion across Ines Marchetti's face — the squint, the freeze, the hand that came up to her mouth and then pretended it had been adjusting her collar the whole time.

"*Dio santo,*" she said. "Dio *santo*, Dominic—"

"I know," I said. "I'm late."

She crossed the gravel at a dead run and hit me like a small determined landslide, and I staggered, and Voss made a noise of protest from my back, and Ines pulled away and stared at the dying man over my shoulder with an expression that went through surprise, recognition, hatred, and something almost like pity, all in about two seconds, which is a personal record even for her.

"You're carrying *Voss*."

"He's light," I said.

"He's a *vulture*."

"He's also dying, and he weighs about as much as a filing cabinet full of apologies, so if you could hold the eulogy until after we're under the tarp—"

Tam appeared out of the rain with the tarp's edge lifted like a doorman at a very bad hotel. "In," he said. "You're dripping. He's dripping. Everything is dripping. This is a tarp, not a miracle."

Under the tarp, I lowered Voss against a pack near the fire. Tam looked at him the way you look at a snake that's wandered into your kitchen — not attacking, not turning its back, just keeping the situation under active review.

"You," Tam said.

"Me," Voss agreed.

"You drowned my boat."

"I didn't personally—"

"You *represent* the people who drowned my boat. My grandmother crossed worse water than that boat ever saw. I want that noted for the record."

"Noted," Voss said. His voice was going papery. "For the record. That's — actually why I'm here."

I got the recorder out of my jacket and held it up, and the rain hammered the tarp above us, and Ines looked at the little machine, and then at Voss, and her face did the arithmetic fast, the way it always did. Rival to my mother for thirty years, and still the sharpest instrument in any room she walked into.

"That's a testimony recorder," she said.

"It is."

"You recorded a *statement*."

"He did," I said. "Four hours of it. Names, dates, accounts, shipping manifests he memorized, the works. The Halvard Collection's whole procurement pipeline, from the buyer down to the men who burn villages." I looked at Voss. "Tell them."

Voss tried to sit up straighter and failed at about forty percent of it. Tam, without being asked, wedged a rolled jacket behind his shoulders, and I want it noted that Tam did this while maintaining full eye contact and an expression of profound grievance, because that's the kind of man he is.

"The Collection doesn't buy objects," Voss said. "That's the first thing nobody understands. It buys *silences*. A provenance is just a story about where something's been. The Halvards learned you can own a story by owning everyone who could contradict it." He coughed, wet and long. "Mei-Lin Zhou understood it better than anyone. That's why she ran. That's why she buried herself in that valley. She wasn't hiding the archive from the world. She was hiding it from an *audience*."

"Her partner," Ines said quietly. "The dig in '09. You were—"

"I was the partner," Voss said. "I was the one who signed the paper that said the finds were unregistered, so the Collection could take them and the blame could land on her. I told myself they'd protect her. They protected the *acquisition*. She lost her license, her funding, and her name, and I got a salary and a debt I'm still paying." His eyes found mine. "Your mother never told you."

"She told me nothing," I said. "That was sort of her signature move."

"She told me once," he said, "that silence was the only inheritance she could keep clean."

The rain picked up. Somewhere up-valley, thunder rolled down the rock like a barrel of stones. Ines sat back on her heels, and I watched thirty years of rivalry go through some kind of internal audit — because Voss had just handed her the answer to a question she'd been sharpening her whole career, which was *what happened to Mei-Lin Zhou*, and the answer was uglier and more ordinary than any of the theories she'd built her grudges on.

"I spent a decade," Ines said, "assuming she beat me to things because she cheated. Every grant, every post, every dig. I built a whole architecture of resentment, and it turns out the foundation was a man with a fountain pen and a contract." She laughed, short and joyless. "I hate that. I hate that it was *small*."

"It's always small," Voss said. "That's the trick. Nobody guards against small."

Tam crouched by the fire, feeding it the last dry sticks. "The recording," he said. "It survives the rain? It survives the *crossing*?" He nodded south, where the frontier river was eating its banks in the dark. "Because that's what's between here and anyone who can use this. That water, and then the passes, and the passes are closing."

"The recorder survives water," Voss said. "The man carrying it is the weak component."

"Glad we're aligned on the risk assessment," I said.

"There's a copy function," Ines said, already holding out her hand for the machine. "Give it here. We split the recordings. Three carriers. Anything happens to one of us—"

"Nothing is going to happen to—"

"Your mother said the same thing in Ushba in 2011 and then fell through a bridge," Ines said, taking the recorder with the smooth authority of a woman who had been confiscating things from younger colleagues for decades. "Give. It."

I gave it.

She worked with numb fingers and the firelight, and Tam held the tarp flap closed against a gust, and for a few minutes the only sounds were the rain, the click of the recorder's case, and Voss breathing — a sound I found I was monitoring the way you monitor a candle in a draft. In, out. In, out. Each one a little more expensive than the last.

"Voss," I said.

"Mm."

"The rockfall. In the vault. You cleared the path for me. You could have gone first."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he hadn't heard. Then: "The Collection teaches you an order of operations. Asset first. Personnel second. I'd done it their way for nineteen years." A breath. "I wanted to do one thing in the other order. Just to know what it felt like."

"That's a hell of a retirement plan."

"It's a *terrible* retirement plan. I'd advise against it." But he almost smiled, and for a second, in the firelight, with the rain coming down like the sky was settling accounts, he looked less like a man who'd spent his life buying silences and more like the dig partner my mother might have once trusted, back before the fountain pen and the contract.

Ines finished with the recorder. "Done. Three copies. I keep one, Tam keeps one, you keep the original with his statement." She handed the machine back to me, and her hand lingered on it, and on mine, one second longer than the transaction required. "Dominic. I believed you were dead. For six hours, I believed it, and I — " She stopped. Restarted, the way she did, like a scholar revising a sentence she refused to get wrong. "I started composing what I would say at the memorial. It was very cutting. It was going to be devastating about your mother's parenting."

"I'm sorry I survived and deprived you of the material."

"I'm not sorry. I'm *furious* I'm not sorry. It's very confusing." She sat back and wiped her face, and it was impossible to tell how much of that was rain.

Tam put a tin cup in my hands — tea, scalding, tasting of smoke and glacier — and then, in a gesture I'll keep longer than most of what happened on that mountain, put a second cup in Voss's hands and folded the man's fingers around it.

"For the record," Tam said, "you're still a vulture."

"Noted."

"But you're a vulture who carried my pack through the scree when the kid was busy playing hero with a stranger's grandmother's route memorized wrong. So. Tea."

Voss looked at the cup. His hands were shaking now, and not from cold, or not only. He drank, small sips, like a man rationing a resource he'd finally run out of ways to price.

"It's funny," he said. "I've spent nineteen years around objects worth more than countries. Sealed rooms full of them. And the thing I keep thinking, sitting here—" he lifted the cup slightly, "—is that this is the first thing anyone's handed me in nineteen years that wasn't a payment or a bill."

The thunder came again, closer, and the rain turned from curtain to wall, and the fire hissed and shrank, and I sat down in the mud next to a dying man I'd hated for a month and a half, and found that the hatred had washed out with everything else, the way dye comes out of cheap cloth. What was left underneath was simpler and worse: just a person, and the rain, and the knowledge that some debts can't be paid, only witnessed.

"How long," I asked quietly.

He didn't pretend not to understand. "Hours. The internal bleeding — I know the signs. I've seen it before, on a dig in '07, a porter, a fall. There's a window where they're fine and then they're not, and the window is a lie." He took another sip. "Which is why we're not waiting for it to close. Your colleague with the recorder — she's right. We move now, while I can still talk, in case anything happens to the copies."

"You shouldn't move at all."

"Neither should you, medically speaking, and yet here we are." He looked out at the rain, at the brown violence of the river beyond it, at the passes already disappearing into cloud. "Dominic. When we get to the tribunal — because we *will* get to the tribunal, I've planned the route, I've planned everything, it's the last thing I'm good for — they'll ask you why you didn't sell. The archive, the index, the coordinates in your head. There are people in that room who will offer you more money than your mother's debts, times a hundred. They'll dress it up as preservation, as stewardship. They'll use the word *legacy*."

"And what do I tell them?"

"Tell them what you told me in the vault." He closed his eyes. "'Some things aren't currency.' I've been turning that sentence over for two days. Nineteen years of the Collection, and the whole philosophy comes apart on six words from a dredger with a dead compass." He opened his eyes, and they were clear, and for a moment the window he'd mentioned didn't seem like a lie at all. "She'd have liked you. Mei-Lin. She'd have found you infuriating and then she'd have liked you."

"She found me infuriating from a distance of four thousand miles for fifteen years, so we were halfway there."

He laughed — a real one, short and cracked, the first I'd ever heard from him — and the laugh turned into the cough, and the cough went on too long, and Tam's hand was on his shoulder, and Ines was leaning in with that fast face of hers already calculating, and when it passed, Voss was greyer, and the window was visibly narrower than it had been five minutes ago.

"We go," he said, when he could. "Now. Before the river takes the crossing."

Ines was already packing. Tam was already kicking the fire apart. And I sat there one more second in the rain's shadow, holding a recorder full of a dead nation's witness and a dying man's confession, feeling the two beats land together in my chest the way they'd been landing since the vault — *they made it, he's dying; I made it, she's gone; we made it, and nothing we made it out with is the thing we went in looking for.*

Relief and loss, sharing a tarp, splitting a pot of terrible tea.

I stood up. I put the recorder in my inside pocket, over my heart, which is where you carry things you intend to keep.

"Okay," I said to the rain, to the river, to the two people and one enemy under the tarp. "Which way's the crossing?"

Tam pointed south, into the wall of water, and grinned the grin of a man whose grandmother had crossed worse.

"That way," he said. "Try to keep up."

* * *

The river had doubled since dawn.

We heard it before we saw it — a sound like a mountain clearing its throat — and then the trees ended and there it was: the frontier, a quarter mile of brown water moving with the calm confidence of something that has never once been told no. The ferry crossing was gone. The ferry itself, Tam's backup boat, was a shape snagged in a tree forty yards downstream, and Tam looked at it the way you look at a relative in a casket.

"Rope crossing," he said. "There's a gorge narrows, half a mile up. Rock on both sides. My grandmother's route — she went *north* on this river in '54, but water's water, it reads the same in both directions." He was already uncoiling line from his pack. "We rig a tension line, we clip across one at a time. Ines first — she's lightest and she argues the least when she's scared."

"I do *not*—"

"Second lightest," Tam amended.

Voss was sitting on a rock, grey as the rain, watching the water with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already knows the answer to.

"I'm not crossing," he said.

Everyone stopped.

"I'm not crossing," he said again, calmer, "because I won't make it, and a body on a rope in flood water kills the whole operation. You clip a dead weight to that line, the river takes the line, the line takes all of you." He looked up at me. "So. The recorder. Give it here one more time."

I gave it. He held it in both hands, and his voice was steadier than it had any right to be, and the rain came down on all of us like the world's most patient interrogation.

"Final addendum," he said, to the machine, to me, to whatever tribunal was going to play this in a dry room with good lighting. "Edmund Voss, acting without the authority of anyone, because that's rather the point. The Halvard Collection's holdings include — " and he named them, one after another, pieces and places and names, his memory laying it all out like a man unpacking a crate he'd been forced to carry for nineteen years. When he finished he pressed record off and handed the machine back, and his hands were cold, and I held them a second longer than I needed to.

"Tell them everything," he said. "Tell them it was small. That's the part they'll never believe."

"Voss—"

"Zhou." He almost smiled. "Go. The river's rising, and I've kept you long enough, and — for the first time in nineteen years — I'm not keeping anything at all."

We clipped Ines onto the line first, then Tam, then me, recorder against my heart, and I looked back once from the middle of the crossing — brown water roaring underneath, the far bank dissolving into rain — and the rock where Voss sat was already just a shape in the grey, a silhouette keeping its own counsel at the edge of a country that had finally, after everything, let him stop paying.

Then the rain closed, and the river took the view, and there was nothing left to do but the one thing left to do.

"Move!" Tam shouted over the water. "South! The passes close tonight!"

And we went — three wet, half-broken witnesses and one machine full of voices — running into the monsoon with the whole drowned country of Kharvath at our backs, and the first real storm of the season coming down the valley like a debt collector who had finally, finally found our address.
