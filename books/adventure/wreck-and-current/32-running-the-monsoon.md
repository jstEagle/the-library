## 32. Running the Monsoon

My grandmother crossed these mountains going the other way, in the spring of '54, with a stolen horse, a bag of dried apricots, and a baby on her back. She told the story so many times that by the time I was eight I could walk it in my head with my eyes closed — the pass she took, the stream she drank from, the ridge where she buried her mother's ring under a cairn because a soldier was watching and grief is a luxury you hide.

She never told it as an escape. She told it as a joke. That's how survivors talk. The horse was stupid, the apricots were terrible, the baby — my father — cried at exactly the wrong moments, and every time death came close it tripped over its own boots and let her go. She laughed telling it. It took me thirty years to hear what was underneath the laughing, which was: *I ran, and I lived, and I do not know if I was brave or lucky, and I have decided not to find out.*

Now here I was running her route backwards, downhill, into the teeth of the monsoon, with two people who had no business being alive and less business being fast, and I understood her completely for the first time. You don't run from something. You run *toward* the idea that somewhere lower down there is water you can drink and a border someone else has to guard.

"Tam." Ines's voice, thin from the altitude but sharp as ever. "You're smiling."

"I'm remembering."

"Don't. Remember later. Walk now."

She was right, of course. She was always right, which was going to be insufferable once we were somewhere safe enough for me to enjoy it.

* * *

The storm caught us on the second morning, or maybe we caught it — hard to say who was chasing whom anymore. We'd come off the high shoulder of the range before dawn, following a trail my grandmother called *the shepherd's lie*, because it looked like it went nowhere and went everywhere instead, switchbacking down a face of grey shale that shed stones under your feet like a river shedding water. The sky to the south had been stacking clouds since yesterday, great blue-black towers building themselves in silence, and every hour they'd climbed a little higher and leaned a little closer, like men crowding around a card game.

By mid-morning the first drops came. Fat ones, cold, spaced far apart — the polite knock before the door comes down.

"That's it," I said. "That's the season turning. After this it won't be rain, it'll be the sky falling for four months straight."

Dominic looked up at the clouds with an expression I was starting to know well. It was his mother's expression — I'd seen it in the photographs Ines carried, the same furrow between the brows, the same way of looking at weather like it was a document to be read. "How long do we have?"

"Before the trails go? Two days, if we're lucky. Before the rivers go?" I shrugged. "The rivers are already going. Listen."

And they did listen, and heard it: a sound under the wind like a long animal breathing, coming up out of the valleys below us. Every gully and ravine between here and the frontier was filling. Water that had been snow three days ago was now moving downhill with intent, and everything it met — soil, scree, the washed-out ghost of a road — it took with it.

"The bridge at Sarn Khola," Ines said. It wasn't a question. She'd been reading my maps over my shoulder for two days, which should have annoyed me and instead felt like having a second pair of eyes bolted onto my skull.

"Gone," I said. "Or going. My grandmother's route doesn't use bridges. Bridges are for people who trust other people to build things."

"And your grandmother trusted?"

"My grandmother trusted a horse, some apricots, and God, in roughly that order."

Dominic almost smiled. He'd been doing that more since the mountain — since Voss, since the recorder he carried wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heartbeat. Grief had done something strange to him. It hadn't lightened him; it had *organized* him. He walked like a man with an errand now instead of a man with a debt, and there was all the difference in the world between those two walkers.

We went down.

* * *

The shepherd's lie earned its name that afternoon. A whole section of it — forty meters of trail cut into the hillside above a gorge — had simply left. Slid away in some earlier thaw, taken by gravity into the brown roaring water below, leaving a scar of raw earth and a gap you could have driven a truck through sideways.

I stood at the edge and did what my grandmother taught me to do when the way forward disappears: I turned around and looked behind, because escape routes are only ever read properly in reverse. And there it was, sixty meters back and twenty meters up — a seam in the rock face, a crack wide enough for a body, angling down through the cliff like a staircase built by water and patience.

"Through the rock," I said. "It'll be wet. It'll be tight. If anyone has developed claustrophobia since Tuesday, now is the time to mention it."

"I've been buried alive recently," Dominic said mildly. "I'm good."

Ines said nothing, just started climbing, which was her way of mentioning nothing.

The seam was everything I promised and worse. Water ran down its throat in cold ropes; the dark inside was total except where daylight leaked through cracks like the rock was grinning at us. At one point the passage narrowed until I had to exhale fully and turn my shoulders, and I heard Ines behind me doing controlled, furious breathing — the breathing of a woman cataloguing every reason to panic and declining each one individually.

Then Dominic's voice, echoing strangely off stone: "There are carvings here."

"There are what?"

"Scratched into the wall. Old. Kharvathi script, I think — same letterforms as the ledger."

I put my hand flat against the rock beside my face and felt them under my fingers, grooves worn shallow by centuries of hands and water. Escape routes get used by everyone eventually, I thought. My grandmother's people had come through here too, seventy years ago, running the other direction, and they'd marked their way the way frightened people do — small signs, scratched prayers, proof of passage.

"She came through here," I said quietly. "Or someone like her."

Nobody answered. There was nothing to answer with. We kept moving, and the rock closed behind us, and after a while the passage spat us out onto a grass shelf above a valley that was no longer a valley but a river wearing a valley's clothes.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and the rain arrived for real. Not the polite knocking anymore — the full assault, a white roaring curtain that turned the world into sound. We huddled under a tarp strung between trekking poles, eating cold rice, shouting conversation at each other across eighteen inches of space.

"You realize," Ines yelled, "that academically speaking, this is a catastrophic decision. Descending into monsoon terrain. No expedition board would approve it. No insurance company would touch it."

"We're not an expedition," I shouted back. "We're a rumor with legs."

"And you—" she rounded on Dominic, rain streaming off her chin, finger raised in the universal gesture of the about-to-lecture, "—are carrying the only copy of testimony that can burn down the Halvard Collection, wrapped in cloth, next to your skin, through flood country. Do you understand what happens if you fall in a river? It's not you I'm worried about. Well. It's also you. But the recording—"

"It stays dry," Dominic said. "It stays with me. Both facts are permanent."

Something passed over Ines's face then — quick, gone before I could name it. Later I'd understand it was the look of a rival realizing the student had become the subject, that Mei-Lin Zhou had built something in that exhausted, stubborn man that even she couldn't have built better. Rivalry, I've learned, doesn't die. It just changes what it's jealous of.

I slept badly, dreamed of my grandmother's horse, woke to the rain still hammering and the day already late, and made the call I'd been dreading: we weren't going around the rivers anymore. We were going to have to cross them while they were merely angry rather than murderous.

* * *

The first one was a tributary, maybe fifteen meters of churning brown water between stones, and it took us two hours to find a crossing. Not because the water was deep — because the water was *fast*, and fast water doesn't need to be deep, it needs only to be hungry. We linked arms, me upstream breaking current, and shuffled across on a submerged gravel bar with the water shoving at our knees and hips like a crowd trying to move past us. On the far bank we stood gasping, absurdly triumphant, three drowned rats holding a victory nobody had awarded.

"Two hours," Ines panted. "For fifteen meters."

"Now multiply," I said, "by every river between here and the border."

She looked at me. I watched her do the arithmetic. I watched her arrive at the number and decide not to say it out loud, which was the most diplomatic act I'd ever witnessed from her.

The big one came the next afternoon — the main stem, the one my grandmother had forded in spring at a place she called *the elbow*, where the river bends hard against a cliff and dumps its load of gravel in a long shallow fan. In fifty-four it had been knee-deep. Now it was a quarter kilometer of moving ocean, braided channels splitting and merging around gravel bars, the whole surface wrinkled and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways like matchsticks.

We stood on the bank and I read it the way she taught me. Find the braid that's slowest. Watch the floating debris — whatever speed the sticks travel, that's the speed of the channel. Never cross where the water is smooth, because smooth means deep. Cross where it's broken and noisy, because noise means rocks near the surface, and rocks mean footing.

"There," I said finally, pointing to a section where three channels braided over a wide gravel fan, white and chaotic. "Ugly water. Ugly is good. Ugly is shallow."

"And if you're wrong?" Dominic asked.

"Then we'll know very quickly."

He laughed — actually laughed, short and startled out of him, and for a second the rain didn't matter. Then he wrapped the recorder tighter against his chest, took my arm, and stepped in first, which told me everything about how far he'd come. Two weeks ago he'd have waited to be led. Now he led himself, and just needed a hand on the current.

The crossing took forty minutes and cost us feeling in everything below the waist. The middle channel hit chest-high and shoved hard enough that for three full seconds I was purely a passenger, feet skimming gravel, trusting the grip of the man on either side of me. Somewhere in that middle channel Ines slipped, and Dominic caught her pack strap, and she came up swearing in three languages, and none of us mentioned it again because that's the deal you make in moving water: what happens in the river stays in the river.

On the far side we collapsed on the gravel, shaking, half-hypothermic, laughing like idiots. The rain fell on us and we laughed anyway. There's a kind of laughter that only exists on the wrong side of a river, and if you haven't heard it, I hope someday you do, because it's the cheapest medicine in the world and the hardest to earn.

* * *

That evening, drying by a fire we barely coaxed alive under a rock overhang, Ines asked me about my grandmother. Really asked, not politely asked.

"The ring," she said. "You said once she buried a ring under a cairn on the ridge. Did anyone ever go back for it?"

I fed a stick to the fire. "My father went, in the seventies. Found the cairn. Dug under it. Nothing there. Either someone else found it first, or the mountain moved it, or she lied about the ring the way she lied about everything — to make the story better."

"And does that bother you? That the story might be better than the truth?"

"No," I said. "Because the story is what got us down the mountain. The story knew where the fords were. The story remembered which rock faces had seams through them. Whatever she buried or didn't bury, she wrote the route into me, and today it held." I looked at the fire. "That's the inheritance, Ines. Not the thing under the cairn. The map in the telling."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had that new steadiness in it. "That's what she meant. My mother. *Custodian.* It was never about owning the archive. It was about carrying the route."

"Look at that," I said. "Two weeks in the mountains and he's wise. Give him another week and he'll be unbearable."

"He's already unbearable," Ines said, and threw a pebble at him, and he caught it without looking, and the fire cracked, and outside the rain went on erasing the world behind us trail by trail, ford by ford, until everything we'd crossed was gone and only the way ahead existed.

Which was, I understood then, exactly how my grandmother had wanted it. You burn the road behind you not to trap yourself but to free yourself. Nothing back there can follow you if back there no longer has roads.

* * *

On the fifth morning we came over a low forested spur and saw the last obstacle: the frontier river, a mile-wide brown serpent in flood, and on the far bank, faint through the rain-haze, the neutral territory beyond — green fields, a watchtower, the ordinary world.

Between us and it: no bridge. Where the maps showed a suspension span, there was now only two frayed cables sagging into the water and a signpost leaning at a drunk angle, its warning board long since swept away.

Ines lowered her binoculars slowly. "Tell me you have a story for this one."

I studied the river, and the wrecked bridge, and the cables — one anchored on our side, still bolted to a concrete block, dipping into the flood mid-span. And I thought of my grandmother, and apricots, and a stupid horse, and every impossible crossing she'd laughed her way through.

"I have rope," I said. "And I have a grandmother. Between the two of them, we're getting across."

Dominic looked at the water — a mile of it, moving like it had a schedule and a grudge — and then at me, and I saw him weigh it, the way he weighed everything now, calmly, like a man reading a document.

"How," he said.
