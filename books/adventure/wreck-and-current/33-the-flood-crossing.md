## 33. The Flood Crossing

The bridge had been on the map. That was the first betrayal.

The second was the river itself — the same Kestrel-class brown water that had started all of this, except here it ran three times wider and moved with the kind of purpose that made Dominic's back teeth ache. It wasn't flowing so much as *commuting*, hauling half a mountain's worth of monsoon runoff toward the coast with the indifference of a freight train that had somewhere important to be.

"The map says there's a suspension bridge," Ines said, holding the ledger page at arm's length as if distance might shame it into accuracy.

"The map is forty years old," Tam said. He stood at the edge of the washed-out abutment, hands on his hips, surveying the gap where the bridge should have been with the expression of a man who had expected exactly this and was mildly annoyed at being right. "The map is also wrong about the trail from the col, wrong about the village being two days apart instead of three, and wrong about there being a hot spring." He paused. "I'm still upset about the hot spring."

"There was a hot spring?"

"There was *supposed* to be a hot spring, Marchetti. There was supposed to be a whole afternoon of hot spring. Instead there was snowmelt and your snoring."

"My snoring kept you alive. Altitude apnea is no joke above four thousand meters."

"It kept *me* awake, which is different, and I resent the implication that I enjoyed it."

Dominic let them argue. It had become the soundtrack of the descent — Ines and Tam bickering like an old married couple who'd never actually been married and would both be horrified by the suggestion — and he'd learned that as long as they were arguing, everything was fine. It was when they went quiet that you checked the sky for aircraft.

He looked at the river instead.

It was, he had to admit, a fitting opponent. Every meaningful thing in his life had happened near water lately. A flooded river in Kestrel Bend had given him a bronze chest and ruined him. A rope crossing in the high country had nearly killed them twice. And now this — the last water between them and neutral territory, running fast and fat and gray-green under a sky the color of wet slate.

The first monsoon rain had caught them two days ago, and since then the world had been damp in a way that felt personal. His boots had given up pretending to be waterproof around noon yesterday. Ines's frostbitten fingers were wrapped in strips of Tam's spare shirt, which she claimed didn't hurt anymore, which everyone knew meant the opposite.

"Options," Dominic said, and the arguing stopped, because he'd learned that too — that these two would generate options forever if you let them, and someone had to occasionally say the word out loud to close the meeting.

Tam crouched at the bank, picked up a stick, and threw it in.

They all watched it. The stick went maybe ten meters before the current took it under, and it did not come up again within sight.

"That's option one," Tam said. "Option one is we don't."

"I love option one," Ines said. "Option one has my full support."

"Option two," Tam continued, ignoring her with the ease of long practice, "is rope. We anchor on this side, run a line across to that dead pine — see it, the one leaning over the cutbank — and we go hand over hand, one at a time, above the water. The current's fastest mid-channel but if you keep your feet up and don't fight it, the line does the work."

"And if you fight it," Dominic said, "the line does the work anyway, just not in the direction you prefer."

"Now he's learning." Tam was already uncoiling rope. "Give me twenty minutes. And somebody find me something heavy to anchor with. Marchetti, you're a professor. Find me a rock with academic gravitas."

* * *

While Tam worked — and the man worked beautifully, loops and tensioning knots going down with the speed of someone whose grandmother had crossed borders for a living and taught him why — Dominic sat on a flat stone and took the ledger out of its oilcloth one more time.

He'd been doing this every night since the vault. Not reading it, exactly. The coordinates lived in his head now; he'd drilled them into himself during the whiteout, during the climb, during the long wet misery of the descent, until they were less like numbers and more like a heartbeat. No, what he did was hold it. Feel the vellum gone soft at the edges, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, the whole thing lighter than a paperback and heavier than anything else he owned.

His mother's handwriting covered it. Half of it was hers, anyway — the second half, the pages she'd mailed ahead and cached at the col, addressed to *my child, if you're reading this*. The first half was older, stranger, in hands he didn't recognize: Kharvathi archivists who had spent their lives preparing for erasure and had gotten the paperwork done early.

Two halves. Two keys. One door that had opened onto ten thousand sealed cylinders of a dead nation's memory.

And now the door was buried behind them under several hundred tons of deliberately dropped mountainside, Voss was dead, the recording of his confession rode in a waterproof case against Dominic's ribs, and the only remaining copy of the only remaining map to the archive was sitting on his knees getting rained on.

"You keep looking at it like it's going to apologize," Ines said, dropping onto the stone beside him. She'd stopped pretending she wasn't in pain days ago; now she just breathed through it, small controlled exhales, a woman negotiating with her own body.

"It got us here."

"Mm. And now?"

Dominic turned it over. Rain spotted the oilcloth. Somewhere behind them, up-valley, thunder rolled with the lazy confidence of weather that had all season to work with.

"When we get across," he said, "we walk into the first town with a tribunal office and I hand over Voss's recording. That part's decided. But this—" he tapped the ledger "—this doesn't go in any report. If the Collection has friends left, and it will, the last thing they need is coordinates. And the next thing after that—" he thought about spring, about the small sanctioned expedition taking shape in his head, Ines and Tam and the Kharvathi descendants Tam swore he could find through his grandmother's old networks "—the next thing after that, the people who dig it up properly shouldn't need a looter's map. They should need each other. The way we did."

Ines was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the oilcloth.

"You're going to destroy it."

"I'm going to stop carrying it. Those might be the same thing. I haven't decided."

"Mei-Lin carried it her whole life," Ines said. Not accusingly. Wonderingly, almost. "Twenty years. She built her entire career as camouflage around it. She sent away the person she loved most to protect it. And you've had it for six weeks and you're already drafting its eulogy."

"She also never got to sleep," Dominic said. "I'm told she was unbearable before coffee."

That surprised a laugh out of Ines — a short, cracked thing, but real. "She was unbearable *after* coffee. Before coffee she was a natural disaster with tenure." She looked at the ledger a long moment. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, because I photographed pages of it against your explicit wishes—"

"Against my *sleeping*, technically."

"—against your sleeping, then — I think she'd hate it. And I think she'd allow it. In that order. Within the same minute." Ines stood, wincing, and squeezed his shoulder once with her bandaged hand, careful as a bird landing. "Fifteen years I competed with that woman and the whole time she was writing letters to you she never sent. I'd have given a lot to read one." She walked off toward the rope, calling over her shoulder: "Don't drop it before you decide! Sentimental objects deserve a fair trial!"

* * *

Tam finished the rig with time to spare, which was fortunate, because the light was going the color of old pewter and the rain had graduated from polite to committed.

The anchor was a boulder the size of a washing machine, sunk into a natural socket Tam had spent twenty minutes improving with a pry bar and profanity. The line ran taut across the flood to the dead pine on the far side, where he'd thrown a weighted cord eleven times — "twelve," Ines corrected, "you missed once and hit yourself in the face" — "eleven *successful* throws, Marchetti, history is written by the survivors" — before getting a loop over a limb thick enough to trust.

"Order of crossing," Tam said, in his ferryman voice, the one that brooked no argument and had, over the past weeks, become weirdly comforting. "Marchetti first. Her hands are bad and I want daylight margin for her. Then the packs, on a pulley loop. Then Zhou. I go last, because I rigged it, and if it fails I want to be the one holding the story."

"That's not how stories work," Ines said.

"It's how *my* stories work."

She went. It was worse to watch than Dominic had expected — Ines swinging out over that brown violence, boots up, hands sliding along the wet rope, the current reaching for her ankles like something with fingers. Halfway across a gust shoved her sideways and she spun once, slowly, terribly, and then the pendulum brought her back and she was moving again, and then her boots hit the far gravel and she sat down hard and raised one bandaged fist in triumph.

The packs went across. Then it was Dominic's turn.

He stood at the edge with the ledger in its oilcloth inside his jacket, inside its inner pocket, buttoned. The rope hummed in the wind. Below him the river made a sound like a crowd that never stopped talking.

"Zhou." Tam's hand on his shoulder. "Feet up. Don't look down. Look at Marchetti. She'll make faces. It helps."

"It does not help."

"It helped me," Tam said solemnly, "when I tested it. Twice."

Dominic went.

The cold came up off the water like a wall. Mid-channel the spray found him even with his feet raised, and the rope flexed and swayed and the world reduced itself to wet hemp, burning shoulders, and Ines on the far bank making — she had not lied — truly inventive faces. Something in the current below him rolled, large and pale, and was gone. He did not think about it. He thought about the coordinates, steady as a pulse. He thought about a bronze chest coming up out of the Kestrel mud on a chain, streaming water, stamped with a seal from a country that had never existed, and how he'd felt the exact shape of dread and wonder at the same time and hadn't known yet that they were the same feeling.

His boots hit gravel. Hands pulled him up. Ines hugged him briefly, fiercely, then pretended she hadn't.

Then Tam crossed, fast and showy, walking his hands like he was strolling, and landed grinning, and the grin lasted right up until Dominic reached into his jacket.

"What are you doing."

Dominic had the ledger out. The rain fell on the exposed vellum and the old ink began, very slightly, to bloom.

"Making a decision," Dominic said.

"Zhou—" Ines started.

"Hold on." He looked at it one last time. His mother's handwriting. *My child, if you're reading this.* The coordinates he could recite in his sleep now, and had, once, out loud, during the whiteout, to the alarm of everyone present. All of it balanced on his palm, weighing nothing, having cost everything.

He thought about what he'd tell the tribunal. About what he'd tell the descendants in the spring. About the difference between a treasure and a trust, and how the difference was mostly whether you could put it down.

"Hey," Tam said quietly. "You don't have to be symbolic. Some things can just be heavy."

"I know." Dominic smiled. "This one isn't. Not anymore."

He threw it.

Not dramatically — he'd considered dramatic, briefly, some arcing farewell into the sunset, but the sun had set hours ago and the wind was wrong. He just threw it, sidearm, the way you'd skip a stone, out over the brown water. It turned once in the air, oilcloth flashing dark, and then the river took it the way it had taken the stick, the way it had taken the bridge, the way it took everything eventually, without malice and without ceremony.

It did not come up again within sight.

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence, professionally.

"Well," Ines said finally. "I hope you're satisfied. I hope you understand that I risked arrest, frostbite, and my professional reputation photographing that document, and you have now fed it to a river."

"I do understand that, yes."

"Fifteen years of rivalry with a woman whose son destroys primary sources for *fun*."

"For closure," Dominic said. "Fun would've been skipping it."

Tam was looking at the water with an unreadable expression. Then he laughed — a big, helpless, rolling laugh that bent him double. "You know what my grandmother would say? She'd say, *good.* She crossed a border with nothing, you know. Whole country in her head. Songs, recipes, the names of streets that aren't on maps anymore. Soldiers took her luggage at the crossing and she said it was the freest she ever felt." He wiped his eyes. "I used to think that was just something old people said to feel better. Turns out it's a technique."

"The coordinates," Ines said. It wasn't quite a question. She was watching Dominic with the sharp, cataloguing attention she usually reserved for stratigraphy.

"Right here." He tapped his temple. "All of them. Plus the landmarks, plus the cipher key, plus the sequence for the door. Ask me anything."

"Third landmark past the split rock."

"Dry riverbed, then the cairn of black stones, then the notch that looks like a broken tooth from the south."

Ines exhaled slowly. "And if you crack your head on the trail tomorrow?"

"Then I guess you'd better stay close to me," Dominic said, "and take notes."

"I have been taking notes since Kestrel Bend, you absolute—"

"Then we're agreed."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the dead pine, and for the first time since the vault, Dominic slept without the ledger's weight against his ribs. He woke once, deep in the black hours, certain he'd forgotten something — patted his jacket, checked the recording case, counted his companions (one snoring like a landslide, one breathing in careful measured rhythm) — and then understood that the missing weight was the point, and lay back down, and the river talked all night in the dark like a crowd that had finally, at long last, run out of arguments.

At dawn the rain had thinned to mist, and the valley ahead opened toward the lowlands, and the lowlands toward the border, and the border — Tam said, squinting at the light — was two days' walk if the trails held, one day if they didn't and they had to improvise.

"Improvise," Ines said. "With what? Our winning personalities?"

"With my grandmother's escape routes," Tam said, "run backwards, through country the patrols forgot about in 1954." He shouldered his pack. "Also with Marchetti's faces. Morale matters."

But fifty meters down the trail, where the mist hung thinnest over the wet grass, they all saw it at the same time.

Boot prints. Fresh ones, pressed deep into the soft ground, heading the same direction they were headed — toward the border, toward the lowlands, toward whatever waited downstream.

Three sets. Maybe four.

And beside them, half-dissolved by rain but unmistakable, the tread pattern of a boot Dominic had last seen pointing at him across a vault full of the dead.

"Voss's men," Ines whispered.

"No," Tam said slowly, crouching, touching the edge of a print. "These are hours old. Maybe less." He looked up, and his grin had gone entirely. "Somebody's running too."
