## 34. Border Light

The rain found them an hour before the border did.

It came down the valley in a grey wall, not falling so much as arriving, the way a debt arrives — suddenly and all at once. Ines Marchetti stood at the rope crossing with her frostbitten hands jammed into her armpits and watched the river that had eaten the bridge eat the last of the light too, and thought: *this is what Mei-Lin must have felt like, standing on the wrong side of everything she loved.*

"You're smiling," Tam said beside her. He was wringing out his shirt with the grim efficiency of a man who had done it forty times in six days. "That's either a good sign or you've finally lost your mind."

"Both," Ines said. "Probably both."

Below them, the river ran brown and furious where three days ago it had been a polite mountain stream you could hop across on stones. The bridge — a rickety timber thing the smugglers' maps called *the old lady* — was gone, its planks scattered downstream like a dropped hand of cards. Which meant the rope. Which meant Tam rigging a line across thirty meters of whitewater while the first true monsoon rain tried to peel his fingers off the rock.

And which meant Dominic Zhou, standing at the water's edge with his dead mother's ledger coordinates written nowhere but inside his skull, about to do something characteristically stupid and characteristically necessary.

"He's going to drop," Ines said.

"He's not going to drop."

"He's exhausted, half-starved, and emotionally compromised. I've known him for five weeks and I can already tell you he's the type to drop."

Tam glanced at her sideways. There was a bruise blooming along his jaw from the rockfall, and he wore it like a medal he hadn't asked for. "You've known him five weeks," he said, "and you talk about him like you raised him."

"I knew his mother for twenty years. It's practically the same thing." She paused. "Worse, actually. With Mei-Lin you always knew exactly what you were getting. With him I keep being surprised, and I *hate* being surprised."

Dominic clipped onto the rope.

He did it the way Tam had shown him — carabiner through the harness loop, both hands, weight forward — and then he stepped off the bank into the roar, and the river took his legs sideways immediately, and for one long horrible second Ines watched him swing like a pendulum over water that would kill him without malice or even interest.

Then his boots found the current's rhythm, hand over hand, and he began to move.

She counted. She couldn't help counting. Twenty-two meters of rope, and Dominic crossed nineteen of them before the river decided to have one more opinion. A wave broke over his shoulder — Ines heard Tam swear in three languages, two of them possibly invented — and Dominic went under to the chest, held, hauled himself up the rope with arms that were shaking visibly even at this distance, and dragged himself onto the far bank like something the sea had rejected for being too stubborn.

He lay there face-down in the gravel for a moment. Then he raised one arm, weakly, and gave them a thumbs-up.

"Oh, he's definitely his mother's son," Ines said, and heard her own voice crack on the word *mother*, and pretended she hadn't.

Tam went next, quick and low and economical, a man crossing water the way other men crossed rooms. Then it was her turn, and the rope was cold and slick and the river hit her like a crowd, and somewhere in the middle of it — halfway between one country and no country — she thought about how she'd spent twenty years competing with a woman who was now dead, and how the competition had turned out to be the closest thing she'd ever had to a friendship, and how she was now carrying that woman's son across a border on a rope, and how life was fundamentally a joke with excellent production values.

Her feet touched gravel. Dominic hauled her up by the elbow without a word, and they stood there dripping in the rain, two archaeologists and a smuggler on the wrong side of everything, and behind them the pass they'd come through was already disappearing under weather.

"Look," Tam said quietly.

Ines turned.

The Serrano Range had closed. There was no other word for it. The col they'd bled over, the goat path, the snow cave, the dry valley — all of it was vanishing behind a curtain of cloud and snowfall, the mountains pulling their white blanket up over their faces like an old man refusing to be argued with. By morning the passes would be sealed. By tomorrow night, nothing human would cross them until spring.

Whatever happened next could not be undone. And could not be followed.

"We made it," Dominic said. He said it the way a man says *I'm fine* after a funeral — technically true, completely insufficient.

"We made it," Ines agreed, because someone had to say it back to him.

* * *

They walked south through the rain for the rest of the day, following a track that Tam swore existed and that Ines mostly took on faith, which was itself remarkable, because Ines Marchetti had not taken anything on faith since 1997.

The neutral territory announced itself gradually. First the trail widened. Then a fence appeared, unmended, decorative. Then a shrine with fresh flowers, then a telephone pole, then — absurdly, gloriously — a road sign, faded blue, pointing toward a town whose name meant *warm springs* in a language Ines only half remembered from a conference in another life.

At the sign, all three of them stopped, as if by agreement, and looked back north one last time.

The mountains were gone. Not hidden — *gone*, swallowed whole, the monsoon clouds stacked against the range like a wall built by something with a grudge. Somewhere back in all that white and stone was a buried archive containing ten thousand voices of a nation that had been erased from every map, entombed now under Tam's avalanche channel, sealed by winter, guarded by altitude and distance and the fact that nobody alive except the three of them and a handful of Kharvathi descendants knew precisely where it lay.

Somewhere back there, too, was Voss. What was left of him. Ines had watched Dominic carry the man's testimony recorder against his chest for two days like a second heartbeat, and she had not asked him about it, because some questions are just doors you agree not to open until the house has stopped burning.

"He's quiet," Tam observed, nodding ahead to where Dominic walked with his hood up, shoulders squared against the rain.

"He's been quiet since the vault."

"That's different quiet. Vault-quiet was grief. This is—" Tam searched for it. "This is a man doing arithmetic."

Ines considered this. "What kind?"

"The kind where you count what you've got left." Tam shrugged. "My grandmother used to do it every year on the anniversary of the crossing. Sit by the window, count on her fingers. One boat, one language, one grandchild. When she got to three she'd make tea." He paused. "I used to think it was sad. Now I think it might be the most honest prayer there is."

Ines said nothing. But she filed it away, the way she filed everything, in the vast card catalogue of her mind — and noticed, with mild professional irritation, that the card she'd just filed had Mei-Lin Zhou's name on it. Because that was what Mei-Lin had done, wasn't it, in the end? Counted what she had left. One archive, one secret, one son. And when she got to three, she'd made her arrangements.

* * *

The town of warm springs was four houses, a guesthouse, a tea shop, and a bathhouse fed by a natural hot spring that smelled faintly of sulfur and salvation.

The guesthouse keeper, a broad woman named Odalys who looked at three soaked, hollow-eyed travelers and asked exactly zero questions — which, Tam murmured, was the local currency — gave them a room with a iron stove, three cots, and towels that had been washed so many times they'd achieved a state of philosophical softness.

Ines was in the hot spring within the hour.

She would defend that decision to any tribunal on earth. Her feet had been wet for eleven days. Her frostbitten fingertips had progressed from *painful* to *interesting* to *a problem for future Ines*. And so she sat in water hot enough to make her gasp, in steam thick enough to hide her face, and let the mountain leach out of her muscles one ache at a time, and stared at the grey sky through the bathhouse's broken roof, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since a bus depot in the lowlands, five weeks and a lifetime ago.

Freedom, it turned out, felt less like triumph and more like the absence of being hunted. Like a silence so complete you could hear your own pulse and didn't immediately assume it was footsteps.

Through the steam she heard the outer door, and then Dominic's voice, tentative: "Ines? Tam said—"

"Bathhouse is co-ed and I don't care," she called. "There's a wall. Be civilized."

He appeared at the edge of the pool a moment later, dressed, holding two glasses of tea, looking like a man who had aged a decade in five weeks and somehow come out younger on the other side. He set her tea on the stones within reach and sat down heavily on the bench, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the spring muttered its one long vowel, and the rain played percussion on the roof.

"My mother came here once," Dominic said finally. "To these springs. Or ones like them. There's a photo — storage unit, box four. Her and a man I never met, sitting in water like this, laughing about something. On the back she wrote *the only fieldwork worth doing*."

Ines closed her eyes. "That would have been Aldo Reyes. Her partner." The name still cost her something to say; it had cost everyone something, eventually. "We competed over a permit in '89 and she beat me, and I sulked for a year, and then I found out she'd split the site with the local university and published under a joint credit, and I realized I had spent twelve months losing to a woman who didn't actually want to win. She just wanted the *dig*." Ines laughed, briefly, at the bottom of the years. "I never forgave her for making me look petty. That's the truth of our rivalry, Dominic. She was generous, and it infuriated me."

"She sounds exhausting."

"She was *exhausting*. She was the most exhausting person I have ever met, and I once shared a trench with a man who narrated his own stratigraphy." Ines opened her eyes and looked at him through the steam — this lean, drowned-rat of a man who five weeks ago had been hauling scrap metal out of a flood-swollen river to pay a dead woman's debts, and who now carried a nation's memory in his head and a dead man's testimony against his ribs. "She would have hated what you did in that valley. The avalanche. Burying it."

"I know."

"And she would have understood it better than anyone alive."

Dominic turned his tea glass slowly on his knee. "That's the part I keep landing on. Everyone keeps asking me — well, no one's asking yet, but they will — *why seal it? Why not bring it out?* And the answer is that it isn't mine. It was never anyone's. It's theirs." He gestured vaguely north, at the mountains, at the erased country under them. "Ten thousand cylinders of somebody's whole civilization, packed away by people who knew they were about to be deleted from the record. You don't *take* that. You don't even *open* it, really. You just... make sure it's still there. Until the people it belongs to can come get it themselves."

Ines sat with that. Steam curled between them.

"When we met," she said, "at the bus depot, I told myself you were a liability. A grieving amateur with half a ledger and a target on his back. I told myself I was helping you because I owed Mei-Lin, or because I wanted the discovery, or because Voss needed stopping." She shook her head slowly. "All true. None of it the truth. The truth is I'd spent twenty years being the best at something, and it had made me small, and watching you refuse Voss's bargain in that vault was the first time in my career I saw someone hold history like it was *heavy* instead of like it was *priced*."

"Ines—"

"Don't interrupt, I'm having a moment and they're rare." She flicked water at him. "I'm telling you I was wrong about you. I don't say that often. Savor it. Frame it. I'll deny it later."

Dominic smiled — really smiled, the first full one she'd seen from him since the vault, and it transformed his whole face the way sun transforms a wet street. "Noted. Witnessed. Will be weaponized at the earliest opportunity."

"There he is," Ines said. "Mei-Lin's sarcasm. I wondered where you'd put it."

* * *

Dinner was stew, flatbread, and a bottle of plum brandy that Odalys pressed on them with the instruction that it was *for the nerves* and *not negotiable*.

They ate around the stove in the guesthouse room, and the brandy did its work, and somewhere in the second glass Tam started singing — low, half under his breath, a song in a language none of the words belonged to anymore. A walking song, he explained, a road song, the kind his grandmother had sung on the long march out of Kharvath, except she'd sung it in Kharvathi and he sang it in whatever pieces had survived the crossing into him.

"What does it mean?" Dominic asked.

Tam considered. "Roughly? *The road forgets us, but we remember the road.*" He shrugged. "Grandmothers are not subtle people."

"No," Ines said softly. "They are not."

Later — much later, stove burned to embers, Tam snoring with the dignified abandon of a man who had run rapids under fire and earned unconsciousness — Ines lay awake on her cot and listened to the rain, and did her own arithmetic.

One rival, dead, forgiven in the way that matters, which is privately and too late. One archive, buried, safe, waiting. One testimony recorder, against the law and the Collection and every map they'd ever bought. One spring coming, and with it an expedition, and with it the slow careful work of digging a country back into existence.

One pair of frostbitten hands that would probably always ache in cold weather now. She flexed them under the blanket, wincing. Fine. Mei-Lin had carried worse souvenirs home from worse mountains.

She was nearly asleep when she heard the cot across the room creak, and Dominic's voice in the dark, barely above the rain:

"Ines. Are you awake?"

"Unfortunately."

A pause. "When we get out. When this is over. Do you think anyone will believe it? Any of it? A country that isn't on the maps, a vault under a glacier, all of it?"

Ines stared at the ceiling, and smiled in the dark.

"Dominic," she said, "I have spent my entire career being believed by people who were wrong. Being *disbelieved* by people who turn out to be right will be a refreshing change." She rolled over. "Sleep. Tomorrow we walk out of here, and the world starts asking questions, and for once — for *once* — we'll have answers."

Across the room, quiet as the rain, Dominic said: "There's something else. Something I haven't told you. About what Voss gave me, at the end. On the recorder — there's a file Mei-Lin never mentioned. A name."

Ines sat up in the dark.

"A name," she repeated.

"Yeah," Dominic said, and even in the blackness she could hear it in his voice — exhaustion edged with something sharper, something almost like hope. "And I think she's still alive."
