## 35. Press Conference, Interrupted

The tribunal building in Geneva had the particular smell of institutions that have survived every scandal thrown at them: old carpet, new coffee, and a faint undertone of legal disinfectant. Dominic stood in the marble lobby with Voss's recorder in his jacket pocket and felt like a man carrying a lit match through a fireworks factory.

"You're sweating," Ines said beside him.

"It's October."

"It's Geneva in October. Nobody sweats." She adjusted her scarf — the frostbite on two fingers had healed to pink new skin that she touched compulsively, like checking a bruise. "You could still sell it, you know. One more time. The *Courier* offered you six figures before breakfast. I checked your phone while you were in the shower."

"You went through my phone?"

"I went through your offers. There's a difference, and the difference is that I was protecting you from your own worst instincts." She nodded at the recorder's outline in his pocket. "That is worth more as a bomb than as a story. You know this. Mei-Lin would have known this."

"That's exactly why I'm nervous," Dominic said. "Every time someone invokes my mother's judgment, it turns out she was right and I spent ten years being wrong about her."

Tam appeared from the direction of the coat check, where he'd been having an animated conversation with a security guard in a mixture of French, English, and hand signals. He was wearing a suit he'd bought that morning from a charity shop, and he wore it the way he wore everything — like a man who might need to run at any moment and had simply decided the suit could come along.

"The guard says the hearing room has a metal detector," Tam reported. "I told him we are not armed, only dangerous. He did not laugh. Swiss people do not laugh before lunch."

"It's after lunch," Ines said.

"Then he is a very serious man."

Dominic looked up at the directory board. *Committee on Cultural Property and Intentional Destruction — Session 14 — Public Gallery.* Somewhere behind those doors sat nineteen people whose job titles contained the word "heritage," who had spent years issuing strongly worded statements about looted antiquities while men like Aldous Voss moved whole civilizations through auction houses under cover of provenance papers so clean they gleamed.

Voss's recorder held eleven hours of testimony. Eleven hours of a dying man in a hospital bed in Kathmandu, hooked to oxygen, describing in a voice gone gravelly and precise how the Halvard Collection acquired what it acquired. The bribed customs officials. The dig sites bought outright and their local staff silenced. The archaeologist left on a collapsing trench wall in 2009 because she'd found something the Collection wanted buried along with her — except she hadn't died, she'd climbed out, and she'd spent the rest of her life making sure no one ever followed her back.

The Collection's lawyers had already sent three letters. The first threatened litigation. The second offered settlement. The third, which arrived yesterday, offered nothing at all, just a single line: *"We are aware of your location."*

"They know we're here," Dominic said.

"Of course they know we're here," Ines said. "They've known since the ferry. Knowing and stopping are different verbs. Come on."

* * *

The public gallery of the hearing room held perhaps forty seats, most of them occupied by interns, journalists, and one elderly man asleep with his mouth open. Dominic took a seat in the back row between Tam and Ines and watched the morning's business unfold: a dispute over underwater ruins off the Levantine coast, presented via PowerPoint by a man who pronounced "sediment" as though it were a small religious observance.

"He's going to have to wait four hours for his slot," Ines whispered, consulting the agenda she'd memorized. "Item nine. After the coffee break, after the closed session on repatriation claims—"

"I'm not waiting four hours."

"Dominic."

"Ines." He turned to look at her. Up close, in the flat institutional light, she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the mountains anymore — the tiredness of a woman who had spent thirty years being right in rooms full of people who didn't want to hear it. "My mother spent fifteen years running from these people. Voss spent twenty years working for them and the last month of his life paying for it. If I sit here politely until item nine, some lawyer will find a procedural reason to have the recording sealed 'pending verification,' and it will stay sealed until everyone in this room has retired. I've seen how archives work. I grew up in one."

Ines studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a small, sharp smile, the smile of a rival recognizing a rival.

"You're going to interrupt a session of an international tribunal."

"Yes."

"And you'd like me to create a distraction."

"I hadn't gotten that far in the plan."

"Amateurs," Tam said fondly. "Both of you. Give me your jacket."

* * *

What happened next was later described in three separate news reports as "a coordinated disruption," which flattered everyone involved, because the truth was that Tam simply stood up during the sediment man's conclusion and asked, in a carrying voice, whether the committee was aware that the Halvard Collection had purchased Levantine artifacts from the same wreck site in 2011 using falsified export documents — a fact he knew because his grandmother's smuggling route had once been used to move them, and because he had spent the last week reading everything Ines put in front of him.

The room turned. The chairman frowned. Two security guards began converging on Tam with the unhurried menace of men paid by the hour.

And in the doorway of the public gallery, holding up a small black recorder like a lantern, stood Dominic Zhou.

"My name is Dominic Zhou," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he let it crack, because he'd learned somewhere in the last six months that a cracked voice opens doors a steady one can't. "My mother was Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou. Some of you may remember her name from a misconduct case fourteen years ago — a dig collapse, a destroyed trench, a career ended. She took the fall for a betrayal that wasn't hers. The man who betrayed her worked for the Halvard Collection. His name was Aldous Voss, and before he died last month, he gave eleven hours of sworn testimony about what the Collection does to get what it wants. I have it here. I am not selling it. I am giving it to you, free, right now, before anyone's lawyers can make it disappear."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then the sleeping old man woke up, looked around, and said clearly: "About time."

* * *

They played the first forty minutes in the hearing room itself, after a junior committee member — a sharp-eyed woman named Okafor who had been trying to subpoena Halvard records for three years — made the procedural argument that evidence voluntarily surrendered in open session could not be seized by any member state's courts without triggering an international incident. The Collection's lawyers arrived within the hour, beautiful and terrible in charcoal gray, and discovered that the recording was already copied, already distributed to five national heritage ministries, and already, thanks to a journalist named Priya Raghavan who had been sitting in the gallery filing updates on her phone, partially quoted in a breaking story read by four million people before the coffee break ended.

Dominic watched it happen from the edge of the room and felt strangely calm. He had expected to feel triumphant. What he felt instead was the specific quiet of a man setting down something heavy and discovering his arms still remember how to be empty.

Voss's voice filled the chamber — dry, precise, unflinching even through the oxygen hiss. *"...the Collection does not steal. Stealing implies the existence of a legitimate owner. We operate on the principle that provenance belongs to whoever pays. Everything else — the permits, the papers, the museums' little certificates — is theater we commission..."*

An aide leaned toward Okafor and whispered. Okafor shook her head. On the dais, the chairman — the same man who'd frowned at Tam — sat very still, listening to a description of a bribe he himself had once, per Voss's testimony, declined and reported, and looking like a man watching his own obituary being written correctly.

*"Dr. Zhou,"* the recording said, and Dominic's chest tightened, *"was the only person in twenty years who beat us. Not outmaneuvered — beat. She understood that the archive wasn't treasure. It was a message. And messages only work if they arrive."*

* * *

By evening, the Halvard Collection's name was on every wire service, its director had resigned "to spend time with his family," and three governments had frozen its asset accounts pending investigation. Dominic learned most of this from the lobby television while eating a sandwich he couldn't taste.

A lawyer found him there — not one of the gray-suited ones, but a tired woman from the tribunal's own counsel office, carrying a folder.

"Mr. Zhou. A few administrative matters." She sat down across from him without asking, which he appreciated; everyone who asked permission these days seemed to want something. "First: the debt question. Your mother's estate carried obligations to several creditors, including two private lenders connected — indirectly, through two shell companies — to the Halvard Collection."

Dominic set down the sandwich. "Meaning?"

"Meaning that when the asset freeze hit this afternoon, both lenders' claims became subject to the insolvency proceedings. Their recovery prospects are, in technical terms, a rounding error. In practical terms—" she slid a document across the table, "—your debts are functionally extinguished. You'll receive formal notices within the week. I'd frame the first one, if I were you. People rarely get to watch their chains dissolve in real time."

He stared at the paper. Numbers he'd been drowning in for years — the dredging contracts, the interest, the slow arithmetic of a life spent bailing out a dead woman's accounts — reduced to a footnote in someone else's scandal.

"There's a second matter," the lawyer said. "The Collection's remaining counsel has filed one final motion — an attempt to claim the archive's contents as corporate property, based on a 1974 acquisition agreement signed by a Kharvathi trustee who, our researchers now confirm, was eight years old at the time and living in a refugee camp. The motion will fail. But it tells us they haven't given up. Men like that don't. They regroup." She stood, gathered her folder. "Enjoy the sandwich, Mr. Zhou. You earned a boring evening."

* * *

He found Ines on the terrace overlooking the lake, wrapped in her coat against the cold, watching the lights come on across the water. Tam was inside charming the cafeteria staff out of a second dessert.

"They're saying the hearings will run through spring," Ines said without turning. "Subpoenas, forensic audits, the whole machinery. Your mother's name is going to be rehabilitated in a conference room instead of a field. She'd have hated it. She'd also have won it."

"She hated conferences."

"She hated losing in conferences. Different thing." Ines finally turned, and her expression was complicated — pride and grief and something older, worn smooth by decades. "Fourteen years I competed with that woman. Grants, posts, the Meridian Fellowship. I told myself she was ruthless. Cold. That she chose the work over everything, over everyone." She paused. "I owe her an apology I can't deliver. So I'll deliver it to you instead, badly, on a terrace in Switzerland: I was wrong about her. And I was wrong about you, which took me considerably less time."

Dominic didn't trust his voice for a moment. When it came, it came rough. "She'd have said you were still wrong about half of it."

"Oh, absolutely. That's what made her worth competing with." Ines looked back at the lake. "Spring, then. When the passes open."

"Spring," he agreed.

Neither of them mentioned the valley. Neither of them needed to. It sat between them like a fourth person — the split rock, the cairn, the stair going down into warm dark, the avalanche that had fallen short of the inner chamber and left ten thousand voices of a dead nation sleeping under fresh stone. Waiting. The coordinates lived in Dominic's head now, and in Ines's notebook, and nowhere else that mattered.

Tam came out onto the terrace holding a plate with three pastries, having apparently negotiated, purchased, or stolen them. He handed them around and bit into his own with the satisfaction of a man whose grandmother's country had just become front-page news for the first time in seventy years — for rescue, this time, instead of erasure.

"So," Tam said. "Rich man now. No debts. Famous. What will you do with all that freedom? Buy a boat?"

"I'm going home first," Dominic said. "Kestrel Bend. There's a dredge operator there who's owed money, and a river that started all this, and I want to stand on that bank one more time and feel what it's like when the water isn't hiding anything."

"And after the river?" Ines asked.

He was quiet for a moment. Because there was one thing he hadn't told them yet — one thing he'd found that morning among the effects the tribunal had returned to him, going through his mother's last shipment of personal items with museum gloves and bureaucratic care. A roll of undeveloped film, tucked inside a hollowed-out book, labeled in her cipher with a string of characters Ines could surely read but which Dominic had already guessed at anyway.

It was dated three weeks before she died. It was addressed, in effect, to him.

"After the river," he said slowly, turning the small film canister over in his pocket, feeling its weight like a held breath, "I think my mother has one more letter for me. And this one, I think, she wrote in pictures."

Ines went very still. Then she held out her hand, palm up, and said, in the voice of a woman who had waited her whole career for exactly this:

"Give it to me. I'll get the darkroom."
