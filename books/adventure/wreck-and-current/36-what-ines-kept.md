## 36. What Ines Kept

The tribunal building in Geneva had the acoustics of a cathedral and the soul of a dentist's waiting room, which was why Ines Marchetti had stepped out of the hearing room for the fourth time that morning to stand by the tall windows and watch the lake do its patient, glittering nothing.

Behind her, through the doors, a lawyer for the Halvard Collection was explaining — in the tone of a man describing a minor clerical error — why forty years of purchased and buried archaeological sites should be understood as *private stewardship*. Behind her, also through the doors, a recording was playing: Voss's voice, thin and wet from the valley mouth where he'd died with his testimony running, naming names, dates, dig sites, graves.

Ines had heard it eleven times now. She kept coming back because each time she noticed something new — a hesitation before a certain name, the way Voss's breathing changed when he said *Mei-Lin*, as if the word itself were heavy equipment he'd been carrying too long.

She did not go back inside yet. Instead she took the little brass canister out of her coat pocket and turned it over in her hands for the hundredth time since the border crossing.

Thirty-six frames of 35-millimeter film. She'd shot them on a camera so old that Dominic had laughed at it on the night train east — an all-metal thing with a sticky shutter and a light meter that lied cheerfully. She'd photographed half the ledger's pages in a bus depot toilet stall outside Serrano Alto, holding pages up against the cistern lid while Tam stood guard outside humming something that was either a lullaby or a warning.

She had never told Dominic. Not on the train. Not in the foothills. Not when he let the original go into the floodwater with a look on his face that she still thought about more than she wanted to. Not during the tribunal, not during the weeks after, not once in four months of phone calls that had slowly, without either of them announcing it, become a habit.

Today she was going to tell him. Her hands were steadier than they'd been on the goat path, but only just.

* * *

Dominic arrived at her hotel at six, carrying a paper bag of chestnut pastries and wearing the expression of a man who had spent the day being cross-examined and had discovered, to his surprise, that he was good at it.

"You look terrible," Ines said, opening the door.

"I look like a man who has answered the question 'and how did you come to possess this recording' nine times." He set the bag on her table. "The answer is always 'a dying man gave it to me,' and every time I say it, the lawyers look at me like I've confessed to stealing it off him personally."

"Did you?"

"He tried to give me his boots too. I drew the line at the boots."

Ines laughed — a real one, which surprised her. Four months ago she would have filed that laugh under things she did not do in front of Mei-Lin Zhou's son. Four months ago Mei-Lin Zhou's son had been a debt-ridden dredger with mud under his fingernails and his mother's jawline, someone Ines had catalogued the way she catalogued everything: provenance, condition, value. She had looked at Dominic Reyes in that bus depot and thought, *the Collection will eat him alive, and I will be there to document the teeth marks.*

Now he was sitting on the edge of her hotel bed eating a pastry with the concentration of a man defusing it, and she found she could not catalogue him at all. He refused to stay in any category she built for him. It was infuriating. It was, she suspected, exactly what Mei-Lin had loved about the world and feared about her son in equal measure.

"The hearing went well," he said. "I think. The Kharvathi descendants' counsel is good. There's a woman, Adeyemi, who asked Voss's former employers a question about the 1979 site purchase that made three separate lawyers reach for water."

"I saw. I was in the back row being disapproving."

"You're very good at that. It's almost a superpower."

"It's a discipline. Like the piano." She poured two glasses of the indifferent wine the hotel provided and sat down across from him. And then, because she had rehearsed this in the shower twice and both times lost her nerve at the same sentence, she put the brass canister on the table between them and said, "Before you leave Geneva, there's something you should have. Something I should have given you months ago."

Dominic looked at the canister. Then at her. He had his mother's way of going very still when something mattered — not frozen, just *arrived*, fully present, the way she used to stand at the edge of a trench before touching anything.

"That's film," he said.

"It is."

"Of what?"

Ines took a breath. "The ledger."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a long moment. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed with diplomatic softness.

"The ledger went into the river," he said finally.

"Yes."

"At the flood crossing. You were there. You watched me let it go."

"I watched you let it go," Ines agreed. "And three days earlier, in a bus depot toilet that smelled like industrial lemon, I photographed thirty-six pages of it. Fronts and backs. Every page I could get my hands on while Tam hummed at the door like a badly tuned radio."

Dominic set down his pastry. That was how she knew it was serious; the man guarded those pastries like state secrets.

"Why?" he said. Not angry. Worse than angry — careful. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because at the time I told myself it was insurance." She held his gaze, because she owed him that much and had learned, somewhere on a mountain, that owing people things you didn't pay made you smaller. "Insurance against losing everything if the original was taken. And then the original wasn't taken — you gave it away, freely, and I sat on my film for four months telling myself a new story, which was that you'd want to be the one to decide what happened to copies. Which was true. But it wasn't the real reason I waited."

"What was the real reason?"

"Because I was ashamed of how easy it was." She turned the canister over. "Mei-Lin and I competed for twenty years, Dominic. Grants, sites, publications. We were cruel to each other in the specific, efficient way that ambitious women are permitted to be cruel when the field is small and everyone's watching. I told myself for two decades that we were rivals. And then I read her cipher on that night train, and I cracked the first layer using a memory only the two of us had — the bad wine in Tbilisi, the argument about stratigraphy that ended with us laughing until the landlady banged on the wall — and I understood that I hadn't been her rival. I'd been her colleague. Possibly her friend. And she'd never told me, because telling me would have meant trusting me, and she trusted no one, and I had just demonstrated why by photographing her son's inheritance in a toilet stall without asking."

Dominic was quiet. Then: "You could have sold those pages. After the tribunal, with the Collection burning — the archive coordinates alone—"

"I know what they're worth."

"Do you? Because I genuinely don't. I stood in that vault and I couldn't price a single cylinder. Ten thousand voices of a dead country and my first thought was *she kept all of it, she kept even the seed stock, she kept the boring maps,* and my second thought was that pricing it would be like pricing my mother's handwriting." He rubbed his face. "Sorry. Go on."

"So you do know," Ines said. "Good. Then hear the rest." She slid the canister toward him. "I didn't photograph it to sell. I need you to believe that, and I'll tell you why it's true. On the night train, when I finished the first cipher layer and read her field notes — really read them — I got to a page where she'd written, in the margin, next to a list of sites the Collection had already stripped: *no record survives. As if they were never here.*"

Ines stopped. The radiator ticked. She made herself keep going.

"I have spent my whole life making records, Dominic. That's the job. You dig, you draw, you photograph, you publish, and someday you die and the drawings are what's left. It's the only immortality anyone in our profession actually believes in. And Mei-Lin — who documented everything, who ciphered her grocery lists, God rest her paranoid brilliant heart — had found a nation that had been *unwritten.* Maps redrawn. Names struck. Archives burned. An entire country edited out of the world's memory, and the world went along with it because the world always goes along with it." She tapped the canister. "When I held those ledger pages up against that filthy cistern lid, I wasn't thinking about value. I was thinking: *not again.* Whatever else happens — whatever the Collection does, whatever the river takes — there will be a record. Even if it's only mine. Even if it's only thirty-six exposures of a dead woman's hand. I could not bear a world with no record. So I made one."

Dominic picked up the canister. Held it the way he'd held the compass at Kestrel Bend, she thought — like something that pointed nowhere useful and mattered anyway.

"She chose you as heir," Ines said quietly. "Custodian, second half. She wrote that years before she died, and she never sent it, and she never told me either, and I have decided to stop being hurt about that sometime around next spring, probably." A breath. "But she named *you*, and the ledger was yours, and I took pictures of it in secret. So this isn't a gift, exactly. It's a surrender. The film is yours. Do with it what you'd have done with the original — including throwing it in a river, though I'll mourn, because developing it took me three tries and a darkroom I rented under a false name, which I feel demonstrates commitment."

That got the almost-smile out of him. "You developed these already?"

"In a hotel bathroom in Trieste, with a changing bag and a great deal of swearing. Tam held towels over the window gaps. He says the experience has cured him of ever assisting an artist again."

"And the images?"

"They're good." She allowed herself the small vanity. "They're very good. Sharp enough to read her marginalia. Page nineteen has a coffee ring on it that I have chosen to interpret as intentional."

Dominic turned the canister in his fingers. When he spoke again, his voice had changed register — lower, the way it went in the vault, among the cylinders.

"You know what I thought, when I let the ledger go into the water?" he said. "Everyone assumed it was some grand gesture. Release. Letting go of the object, keeping the meaning. And it was that, partly. But underneath it I was terrified, because the ledger was the last thing with her handwriting on it that I actually possessed, and giving it to the river felt like burying her twice." He looked up. "You're telling me it isn't gone. That her hand survived. In your pocket, the whole time, while I walked around Geneva feeling noble about having nothing left of her."

"I'm telling you that, yes."

"And you sat on it for four months."

"I sat on it for four months," Ines said, "because I am a coward in precisely the ways my mother warned everyone I was not."

He laughed then — short, startled, real — and something in the room loosened that had been tight since a bus depot in the lowlands. He reached across the table and closed his hand over the canister, and over her hand, briefly, before letting go.

"Thank you," he said. "Not for the film. For telling me the truth four months late instead of never. That's more than most people manage."

"My mother would say that's a low bar."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She sounded wise mostly when she was insulting me, which is a genre I recommend."

* * *

They ordered food neither of them ate much of, and Tam arrived at eight with a bottle of something Georgian and a story about a taxi driver who had tried to convert him to a football club, and at some point the canister came out again and Tam went quiet in the way he only went quiet about Kharvath.

"My grandmother," he said, turning his glass, "kept one thing from the old country. A recipe. Written on the back of a train ticket. She sewed it into her coat lining and carried it through two borders and forty years, and when she died we found it, and none of us could read the language anymore." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which was how Tam handled everything that mattered. "My mother framed it anyway. Hung it in the kitchen. Said, *we don't need to read it. We need to know it's there.*" He nodded at the canister in Dominic's hands. "Same thing. You don't develop it. Or you do. Doesn't matter. What matters is somebody kept it."

"Somebody kept it," Dominic agreed.

"Two somebodies," Ines said, and then wished she hadn't, because it sounded like claiming kinship, which — she was learning, slowly, painfully, at fifty-three — was exactly what it was.

Tam raised his glass. "To records," he said. "And to the people who make them at inconvenient moments in toilets."

"To records," Dominic said.

Ines drank, and did not trust herself to speak, and found that this too was a kind of speech.

* * *

Later, when Tam had gone off to argue with the front desk about his bill in three languages, Dominic stood at her window looking out at the lake, the canister in his shirt pocket like a small brass heart.

"There's a letter," he said. "From the Kharvathi council. The descendants' group. They voted last week." He turned. "They want to open the valley properly. Sanctioned excavation. Slow. Community-led. Their terms, not the tribunal's, not the universities'. And they asked—" he paused, and Ines saw him choose the words carefully, the way his mother used to choose strata to cut, "—they asked if I would lead it. With whoever I trusted."

Ines felt the sentence land somewhere under her ribs. *With whoever I trusted.* Twenty years of rivalry, and the son of Mei-Lin Zhou was standing in a Geneva hotel offering her a trench.

"They understand it's a decade's work," he went on. "Maybe two. Bad weather, worse funding, and every government on the map wanting a piece the moment word gets out. Tam's already said yes — he claims it's for the money, which is a lie, because there isn't any. And I told them I'd need to ask you." He met her eyes. "So I'm asking."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. She thought about the young man in the bus depot with mud on his boots, whom she had assessed and dismissed and followed up a mountain anyway. She thought about Mei-Lin, who had trusted no one, and who had — in the end, in the ice, in a recorded voice Ines had now heard eleven times — trusted her son with the choice she never had herself.

"I have conditions," she said.

"Of course you do."

"One: I run the stratigraphy. Your mother and I fought about it for twenty years and I intend to win posthumously."

"Accepted with grief."

"Two: page nineteen gets published with the coffee ring intact. Provenance matters."

"Integrity in all things."

"And three." She picked up her glass, because her hands needed occupation. "Three: when we open the vault again in spring — when you record your message for whoever comes next, and I know you're going to, you have your mother's exact face when you're planning something sentimental — you let me be there. Not as a rival. Not as an auditor." She made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the only currency she had left that was worth anything. "As family. The kind you choose. The kind she never managed to choose, and paid for it, and so did I, and so did you."

Dominic was quiet for a moment. Outside, the lake went on doing its patient glittering, and somewhere below a tram sang past, and Ines Marchetti, who had built a career on never needing anyone, stood waiting for an answer with her heart going like a girl's.

"Deal," he said simply. "Spring. The valley. Both halves — yours and hers." He touched the canister in his pocket. "We'll develop the film together. First thing. Before the passes even open."

"Before the passes open," she agreed, and then, because warmth embarrassed her and honesty embarrassed her more: "And Dominic — if you ever photograph my field notes in a toilet stall, I shall have you exiled to a survey of drainage ditches."

"Noted. Though in fairness, you'd deserve it."

"I absolutely would."

He grinned, shouldered his bag, and paused at the door. "Geneva hearings end Friday. I'm taking the slow train south after — through the mountains. Want to see what the snowline looks like from the windows?"

Ines looked at the man who had walked out of a flooded river carrying a dead nation's chest, and who was now inviting her to look at mountains, and understood that the rivalry was over, had probably been over since a night train and a cipher of small cruelties, and that what remained was better and harder and hers.

"I'll bring the wine," she said. "Someone has to protect you from hotel vintages."

He laughed, and the door closed behind him, and Ines stood alone in the warm room with the empty canister's weight already missing from her pocket — and realized, with a start, that she had one more confession to make, a smaller one, one she'd been carrying since Trieste.

Because thirty-six frames had gone into that canister.

And she had shot thirty-seven.
