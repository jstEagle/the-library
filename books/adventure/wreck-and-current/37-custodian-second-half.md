## 37. Custodian, Second Half

The valley mouth in spring smelled like a promise kept.

Dominic stood where the rope crossing had been — the river was down to a polite murmur now, the kind of water you could wade without making peace with your gods — and watched the mules pick their way along the trail that had nearly killed all of them eight months ago. There were nine mules, four Kharvathi descendants, one archaeologist who had insisted on walking the whole way "for her knees," and one smuggler who had named every single animal after someone he owed money to.

"That's Voss," Tam said, pointing at a gray mare with an opinionated gait. "Because she bites and then acts like it was your fault."

"You named a mule after him?" Dominic asked.

"He'd have hated it. That's the point." Tam patted the mare's neck. "Also she carries the heaviest load without complaint, which he also would have hated."

Ines came up the trail behind them, walking stick in hand, surveying the valley like a woman checking whether a houseguest had rearranged her furniture. She'd spent the winter writing the site report — three hundred pages, footnoted within an inch of its life, and in the acknowledgments she'd written only two names besides her own: *Mei-Lin Zhou, who taught me that rigor is a form of love,* and *D. Zhou, custodian, second half.*

He'd read that line eleven times. He still didn't know what to do with it.

"You're smiling," Ines said, arriving beside him. "Stop it. It's unprofessional."

"It's spring."

"It's a work site. Smile on your own time." But she was smiling too, the small private version she thought nobody saw.

* * *

The expedition had taken seven months to assemble, and every month of it had been a lesson in what his mother's world actually cost.

There were permits — forty-one of them, from three governments, one of which technically did not recognize the existence of the other two. There was the tribunal fallout, which had made Dominic briefly famous in the way he least enjoyed: his face on news sites under headlines like THE CUSTODIAN SPEAKS, when all he'd done was hand over a dead man's recorder and refuse to answer questions about treasure. There were the Collection's lawyers, who had spent the winter trying to argue that a vault entombed by its own avalanche protocol constituted abandonment, and therefore ownership by right of salvage.

The tribunal had disagreed. Loudly. The ruling ran to sixty pages, but the sentence that mattered was the one Ines had framed and hung in her office:

*Provenance belongs to those who remember, not those who pay.*

And there were the descendants. That had been the part Dominic hadn't planned for, because he hadn't known they existed until the tribunal process flushed them out of hiding the way the flood had flushed out the chest. Kharvath hadn't been erased from maps so thoroughly as everyone believed. It had been erased from *official* maps. The people had gone underground — literally, in some cases — into diaspora communities scattered across four borders, keeping songs and recipes and a language that survived mostly in lullabies.

Their council had sent a delegation to meet him in a rented hall in a border town, and Dominic had prepared for suspicion, for demands, for the anger he felt he deserved.

What he got instead was an elderly woman named Sefa who looked at him for a long moment and said, "You have her hands."

"I never met anyone who knew my mother," he'd managed.

"We knew her letters." Sefa had opened a tin box — an actual tin, biscuit brand faded off the lid — and shown him forty years of correspondence. His mother had written to the scattered families of Kharvath every year. Coordinates disguised as poetry. Encouragement disguised as weather reports. Apologies, sometimes, for how slow the work went.

"She never told us your name," Sefa said. "She told us you existed. She said, 'I have a son, and one day he will finish this, and you must be patient with him, because I was not patient enough.'" She closed the tin. "So. We are being patient. Tell us what you need."

It had taken Dominic a full minute to speak.

* * *

Now the camp was going up at the valley mouth exactly where the old cairn of black stones had been rebuilt — stone by stone, by hand, by the descendants themselves, because Sefa had been firm on one point: nothing at this site would be moved by machine until the community said so.

"Slowly," she'd told the assembled team on the first morning, standing on a crate so she could be seen over the equipment. She was seventy-eight years old and commanded a room like a general. "My grandmother walked out of Kharvath with one shoe and a bag of seed wheat. She took eleven months to travel a distance we crossed in six days. She said the difference was that she arrived still knowing who she was. We will dig slowly. If any of you find that boring, the mountain is that way, and the monsoon will be back eventually."

Tam raised his hand. "Question. Is the mountain way open for lunch visits or just permanent departures?"

Sefa looked at him the way one looks at weather. "You are the ferryman."

"I'm many things. Ferryman is the one with references."

"The ferryman will carry supplies up the goat path," Sefa said, "and will enjoy it."

And here was the thing that kept catching Dominic off guard, weeks into the work: it *was* enjoyable. Not the hauling — his shoulders had filed formal complaints — but the pace of it. The old digs he remembered from childhood, trailing his mother, had run on urgency. Deadlines, funding windows, rival teams rumored to be two valleys over. This was different. They surveyed for a week before touching anything. They mapped the scree field twice because Sefa wanted the second map drawn by a descendant's hand. When they found the first artifact — a clay lamp, intact, sitting in the rubble of the collapsed channel as if it had waited politely — the whole camp stopped, and Sefa held it, and an old man named Rurik sang something low and wavering over it, and nobody wrote anything down for ten full minutes.

"What was the song?" Dominic asked afterward.

"A thank-you," Sefa said. "To the lamp for waiting. And to us for coming back."

"For coming back," he repeated. "Nobody left. Right? Everyone who built this place died in the purge, or—"

"Left." Sefa considered the word. "Yes. But leaving is not the same as not returning. Your mother understood that. It took her some years, I think. Letters get shorter when a person is learning something hard." She'd looked at him then, with that terrible gentleness old women carry like tools. "Yours will get longer. You'll see."

* * *

The inner chamber had survived.

They'd confirmed it in the first week, from outside — Ines had spent two days with a borescope and a borrowed endoscopic camera fed through a fissure in the rockfall, lying flat on her stomach in the cold, emerging with frostbitten elbows and an expression Dominic had learned to read as barely contained triumph.

"It's there," she'd announced, spitting out grit. "Cylinders. Hundreds visible from one angle alone. The collapse channel funneled the main slide past the chamber mouth — Mei-Lin's engineers built it to shed outward. The door took damage, the antechamber's half-full of rubble, but the archive itself—" She'd sat back on her heels and laughed, once, a short startled sound. "She buried it and it *held*. Of course it held. She never built anything that didn't."

"Except a family," Tam offered, then caught Dominic's eye. "Too soon? That joke has a shelf life and I keep missing it."

"Nah," Dominic said. "It landed. Mostly."

But they hadn't opened the chamber yet. That was the discipline of it — Sefa's discipline, really. The excavation of the antechamber rubble was proceeding by hand, bucket by bucket, documented by three separate cameras, with a conservation tent erected inside the corridor beyond. The Kharvathi families had voted — an actual vote, conducted over satellite phone across four countries — on the order of operations. First the antechamber cleared and stabilized. Then the door mechanism examined by conservators, not forced. Then, and only then, the opening, which would happen with the descendants present, all of them, however long it took to arrange.

"They waited seventy years," Sefa said, when Dominic suggested — gently, practically — that maybe a representative delegation would suffice. "We can wait one more season. What is patience for, if not the important things?"

* * *

On the nineteenth day, Dominic found the room.

Not the archive chamber — the little one off the antechamber, half-crushed, the one he recognized from his mother's recording. The booth. Where she had sat among the cylinders of a dead nation and spoken to whoever might come after.

The excavation crew reached it mid-morning, and the mood went quiet in the particular way quiet descends on dig sites when everyone understands a space at the same moment. Rurik touched the doorframe and murmured something. One of the younger descendants, a graduate student named Alba who'd been born in exile and spoke Kharvathi only to her grandmother, stood in the doorway with her hand over her mouth.

Dominic crouched at the threshold. The booth was small — two meters square, maybe — with a bench carved into the wall and a bracket where a recording mechanism had once sat. The mechanism itself was gone; his mother had taken it, or it had been removed deliberately, sockets empty, wires clipped clean.

"She used this," he said. Not a question. He'd heard her voice fill the vault; he knew the acoustics of the place even if he'd never stood in it.

"She used it more than once, I think," Ines said quietly, beside him. She nodded at the bench. "Look at the wear."

He looked. The edge of the stone bench was polished smooth in two patches — two places where a person had sat, over years, over decades. Two sizes. One broader than the other.

His throat did something complicated.

"Kharvathi practice," Rurik said, from the doorway. He was the oldest of the descendants, older than Sefa, with hands like tree roots. "Before the purge. Every family kept a memory room. You went in when you had something true to say and no one alive to say it to. You spoke to the ones not born yet." He tapped his chest. "We carried the practice out with us. My mother had a corner of a cellar in a country whose name I will not say in this room. Same idea. Smaller walls."

"So this was—" Dominic started.

"This was theirs first," Rurik said. "Your mother borrowed it. That is not a criticism. Borrowing is how anything survives." He smiled, and it transformed his whole face into something young. "Your mother recorded here?"

"Once. That I know of."

"Then she followed the old rule. Speak to the unborn." He looked at Dominic with mild curiosity, the way you'd look at a river to see which way it ran. "And you are the born. Strange, how the message travels backward through time to reach forward. But that is archives for you. Everything arrives late and exactly on schedule."

Alba laughed wetly. Dominic discovered he needed a minute with the scree field, and took it.

* * *

That evening, camp settled into its rhythm — generators humming low, the mess tent loud with the specific argument of eleven people deciding how much chili was too much chili (the answer, per Tam, was always "more"), the mountains going pink and then violet above them.

Ines found him at the survey table, where he was redrawing the day's grid plan for the third time because his handwriting deteriorated after altitude and emotion in roughly equal measure.

"Status report," she said, dropping into the chair opposite. "Conservation team says the door mechanism is intact. Sefa says the vote on opening is scheduled for next month. Tam says the mule named Voss has eaten two hats and a field notebook, and honestly the notebook deserved it, it was mine." She slid a paper across the table. "And I say you should look at this."

It was a photograph — a print, actual paper, because Ines distrusted screens the way other people distrusted rivers. The door mechanism, shot close. Around the keyhole assembly, cut into the bronze in characters worn nearly smooth, ran a line of Kharvathi script.

"The conservators photographed everything," she said. "Rurik translated it tonight at dinner. He made me write it down because he said I'd want it later, and he was right, because I want you to have it first."

Dominic read the transcription. Rurik's English was precise, a little formal, the English of a man who'd learned it from books and chose his words like stones for a wall:

*Two halves make one door. No hand opens what the heart has not joined.*

Below it, Rurik had added a note in pencil: *This is not a lock instruction. It is a warning, and a welcome. Both halves — the ledger, yes, but also the people. The door knows the difference.*

Dominic sat with that for a while. The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the dark, the mule named Voss was committing some new crime.

"My mother mailed the second half before she died," he said finally. "To a postal depot on the coast. I carried both halves up the mountain. I opened the door." He turned the photograph over, as if the answer might be stamped on the back. "So what does it mean, 'no hand opens what the heart has not joined'? We already did it. Mechanically."

"Mechanically," Ines agreed. She leaned back, chair creaking. "You know what I've been thinking about all winter? The recording. Her confession. You listened to it in that vault with Voss's men twenty minutes from killing you, and you listened to the whole thing, and then you made a choice — refused the bargain, let Tam bring the mountain down. And I keep thinking: the door opened for you *before* any of that. Before you'd proven anything. Which means either the mechanism is dumber than the inscription suggests—"

"Which you'll never accept."

"—which I will never accept," she said, "or the door wasn't testing whether you'd opened the ledger. It was testing whether you'd *carry* it. Both halves. The work and the cost." She tapped the photograph. "Rurik says the old families sealed these doors with ceremonies. Names spoken. Obligations stated aloud. Your mother bypassed all of it — she was a scientist, she trusted mechanisms over rituals. But the ritual was built into the metal anyway. Waiting."

"For what?"

"For someone to read it," Ines said simply. "Seventy years, and the first person to stand at that door with both halves and both hands was you. And tomorrow—" she glanced toward the antechamber, where the conservation tent glowed faintly against the rockfall, "—tomorrow the conservators reach the inscription panel itself. Full text, not just the line around the keyhole. Rurik thinks there's more. A longer passage, maybe. Maybe names."

"Names of whom?"

"Of the people who sealed it." She stood, gathering her papers, and paused. "Or, if your mother was thorough — and she was thorough the way avalanches are thorough — of the people permitted to open it. Custodians." A beat. "Plural."

She left him with the lantern and the photograph and the strange vertigo of a man discovering that a door he'd already walked through might have been, all along, an application.

Outside, the stars over the Serrano Range were ferocious and indifferent, and somewhere below the snowline, in a chamber built by people who refused to be forgotten, ten thousand voices waited to speak — and one voice, his mother's, waited among them, having already said her piece, having already chosen him.

Dominic picked up his pen. His handwriting, for once, was steady.

At dawn, the conservators would clear the last of the rubble from the inscription panel, and Rurik would read aloud whatever the metal had kept, and the expedition would learn whether the door had one more thing to ask of its custodian — or one more thing to give him.

Either way, he intended to be there. Slowly. On the community's terms.

With both halves.
