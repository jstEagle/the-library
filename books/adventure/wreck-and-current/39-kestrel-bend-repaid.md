## 39. Kestrel Bend, Repaid

The river had not missed us.

That was the first thing I understood, standing on the bank at Kestrel Bend in the last week of spring, my boots sinking into mud the color of old tea. Rivers don't hold grudges. They don't hold anything — that's their whole philosophy. Everything that goes in comes out somewhere downstream, eventually, rearranged. My grandmother used to say a river is just the world's way of refusing to keep secrets, and then she'd spit into the current for luck, because she was also a woman who believed in hedging her bets.

I spat into the current. For luck. Some inheritances you don't get to decline.

"You're doing it again," Dominic said behind me.

"Doing what."

"The spitting thing. You said it's a Kharvathi custom but Ines looked it up and she says there's no record of it anywhere."

"Ines looks everything up," I said. "That's her whole tragedy." I spat again, elaborately, to make my point. "My grandmother made it up on the boat. She said if we're going to be from nowhere, we should at least have customs. So now it's real. That's how countries start, Dominic. Somebody makes something up and then refuses to be embarrassed about it."

He laughed — the loose, easy laugh he'd found somewhere in the mountains and carried back down like contraband — and went back to arguing with the dredge operator about rates.

I watched him do it. I want that recorded somewhere, since apparently I'm the one who notices things: Dominic Zhou, who eight months ago hauled a bronze chest out of this same river with hands shaking from debt and dread, standing in the sun arguing about hourly fees like a man who expected to win. He'd grown into his shoulders somehow. Or maybe he'd just stopped apologizing for taking up space in them.

The dredge operator was a wide man named Oskar with forearms like mooring rope and the negotiating instincts of a man who could smell desperation through concrete. Eight months ago he'd have smelled it on Dominic from across the river. Today he smelled nothing, and it was visibly unsettling him.

"Full day rate," Oskar said. "Plus fuel surcharge."

"It's your barge," Dominic said pleasantly. "You're already floating on it. What's the fuel doing, swimming?"

Oskar blinked at him. I bit the inside of my cheek to keep from grinning. Behind them, Ines sat on an overturned crate with a thermos and a notebook, pretending to read sediment reports and actually watching the negotiation with the open delight of a woman who had spent thirty years around academics and never once seen one haggle well. She caught my eye and raised the thermos in a small salute.

We'd come back the long way, the three of us. There had been tribunals to feed, recordings to hand over, lawyers to endure — Ines called them "the migratory birds of justice, they arrive in flocks and leave nothing behind" — and a spring's worth of hearings where Voss's voice, dead and patient and damning, did more work than any living witness could have. The Halvard Collection was being taken apart bolt by bolt in rooms none of us would ever see. Dominic's mother's debts, which had been held as leverage by people who thought debts were leashes, had been dissolved in the scandal like sugar in rain.

But some debts aren't held by institutions. Some are just owed, plain and ugly, to ordinary people who lent money in good faith or bad and never got it back. Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou had died owing eleven separate creditors in Kestrel Bend alone. The tribunal didn't care about those. The tribunal cared about nations erased and archives buried and collectors who bought history with other people's bloodlines.

Dominic cared about the eleven.

"We'll take the full day," he told Oskar. "But we run the east channel first. And I want the same crew you had last autumn — the ones who were decent to me when I couldn't tip."

Oskar squinted at him. "You remember who was decent?"

"I remember everything about this river," Dominic said, and there was something under the pleasantness then, something quiet and total, and Oskar — who bargained for a living — decided not to test it. "Deal," he said quickly, and went to shout at his crew.

* * *

The east channel gave up its secrets the way rivers always do: reluctantly, and mostly mud.

I stood at the rail while the dredge clawed and sucked and coughed up the riverbed in gray-brown loads, and I thought about the last time I'd worked water like this. Not here — my grandmother's ferry ran a different river, a border river, before she ran it into history. She used to say the water keeps books too, better books than any ledger, because it never lies about what sank and why. It just makes you work for the reading.

The first load of the morning came up ordinary. Sand, silt, a bicycle frame gone to lace, a boot. The second load: more of the same, plus a catfish that objected loudly to its circumstances and was returned to them. By midmorning a small crowd had gathered on the bank — the same kind of crowd that had gathered the day Dominic pulled up the bronze chest, because towns remember where miracles happened and show up hoping for encores.

"Nothing today," someone called hopefully.

"Nothing today," Dominic agreed, cheerful as a man announcing good weather.

It went on like that all morning. Mud, gravel, a stove door, forty-one bottle caps (Ines counted; Ines always counts), a doll's head that made even me step back and look at it for a moment before deciding it was only sad and not haunted. The crowd thinned. The sun climbed. And Dominic paid the crane operator, and paid the fuel dock, and walked up the street to a tailor shop and put an envelope on the counter in front of a small fierce woman named Petra whose husband had lent Mei-Lin two thousand against a promise, and came out ten minutes later with his head slightly bowed and Petra's hand briefly on his arm.

Then the hardware store. Then the clinic, where the debt was to a doctor who had treated him once, for free, and been repaid in nothing for years. Then a room above the feed store where an old man took the envelope, counted not one bill of it, and set it unopened in a drawer, saying only, "Your mother sat where you're sitting. She had the same way of standing like she was waiting to be arrested."

"She wasn't wrong to stand that way," Dominic said.

"No," the old man agreed. "But she was wrong to think we minded."

I heard that exchange from the doorway, because I'd followed him — not because he needed guarding, though I'd have argued he did back in the winter, but because Tam does not let his people walk into rooms alone when the rooms involve money and grief. Money and grief together is how ambushes happen. I've seen it.

By late afternoon the ledger of Kestrel Bend was clean. Eleven creditors. Eleven envelopes. Eleven small doors closing on eight months of shame, and Dominic came out of the last one looking lighter and stranger, like a man who has set down a pack so heavy he'd forgotten his own posture.

Ines met him on the street. She didn't hug him — they weren't built for that, either of them; they communicated mainly through footnotes and insults shaped like compliments — but she fell into step beside him and said, "Petra's putting your mother's account in a frame. Apparently it's the first entry anyone's ever closed."

"Don't tell me that," Dominic said. "I'll cry in front of the hardware store."

"Too late," I said. "I'm telling everyone."

* * *

We went back to the bend at dusk, the three of us, when the crowd was gone and the dredge was tied up and the river had gone from brown to pewter to a deep moving dark. Oskar had offered one last hour free — sentiment, from Oskar, which shows you what kind of year it had been — and Dominic took it, though I think mostly he wanted to stand at the rail without an audience.

"One more pass," he said. "East channel. Where I found it."

The claw went down. The river gave up its load. We leaned over the rail and looked at what eight months and a nation's worth of trouble had bought:

Mud.

Ordinary, honest, unremarkable mud, with a few stones in it and a strand of weed and one very indignant crayfish. No bronze. No seals. No half-ledgers, no cipher, no country that never existed. Just the river's daily business, conducted as if nothing remarkable had ever sunk here, which — I suppose — was now true again.

"Well," Ines said. "That's that."

"That's that," Dominic said.

And here is the thing I noticed, because noticing is my inheritance, the one thing my grandmother left me that couldn't be seized at a border: he wasn't disappointed. I watched his face in the last light, expecting the flicker — everybody hopes for one more miracle, it's human, it's the engine of every bad decision I've ever witnessed — and there was no flicker. He looked at the mud the way you look at a finished sentence. With relief. Maybe even affection.

"The compass," Ines said quietly.

He took it out. The repaired compass, the one that had pointed uselessly and stubbornly toward nothing for the whole journey, spinning slow circles whenever anyone consulted it, useless as a drunk uncle at a funeral. He'd kept it anyway. Carried it through whiteouts and avalanches and a tribunal hearing room, of all the absurd places.

He held it over the rail, over the mud, over the place where the chest had come up.

The needle swung once, twice — and settled. Pointing north. Steady. Quiet. Like it had finally gotten where it was going, or finally given up trying, and either way had decided to rest.

"Huh," Dominic said.

"What does it mean?" Ines asked. She was leaning in, academic hunger all over her face, already composing the footnote. "A magnetic anomaly? Iron in the riverbed? Some residual field from the chest itself—"

"It means it's a compass," Dominic said, "and it's pointing north, and for the first time since I've owned it, that's enough." He pocketed it. "Some instruments don't need a mystery, Ines. They just need to stop being asked questions."

She stared at him. "That's not science."

"No," he agreed. "It's better."

* * *

We ate that night at the taverna by the dock, the one with the crooked sign and the owner who remembered Dominic as "the boy who owed everyone" and now couldn't stop bringing him things he hadn't ordered — olives, bread, a dessert nobody had requested, a second dessert as an apology for the first dessert being insufficient. Word had traveled up the street ahead of us, the way word does in river towns, faster than boats.

"To Dr. Zhou," Ines said, raising her glass, when the plates were cleared. "Who was a terrible correspondent, a worse colleague—" she paused, and I saw her choose the next words with the care she usually reserved for dating pottery shards, "—and the finest archaeologist I ever competed against. Which is a different thing from the finest archaeologist, period. But I'm working on honesty. It's a spring project."

"To Mei-Lin," Dominic said simply.

"To the custodian," I said, and clinked his glass, and he rolled his eyes, because he still hated the title, and I still used it, because somebody has to keep the customs alive. That's how countries start. Somebody makes something up and refuses to be embarrassed.

Later, when Ines had gone up to bed with her notebook — she was writing something, she wouldn't say what, but she'd started labeling pages *for the record* in a way that made me suspect a memoir with footnotes, God help the reading public — Dominic and I stayed at the table by the window with the last of the wine and the river going black and silver beyond the glass.

"Tam," he said. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've met me. All my questions are strange."

"When your grandmother left Kharvath. On the boat. Did she ever go back? Even to look?"

I turned my glass. The wine moved. Outside, the current moved, patient as arithmetic.

"She went back once," I said. "Forty years later. Stood on the far bank where the ferry used to land. There was a town there full of people who'd never heard of her country, using her grandmother's words for counting sheep." I shrugged. "She spat in the river and came home. Said the water remembered so she didn't have to carry it anymore."

"And you believe that? You can put something down in a river and it stays put?"

"I believe," I said, "that you can put something down anywhere, if you actually let go. The river's just the most convincing witness. It takes everything and tells no one." I looked at him. "You let the ledger go in the flood crossing. I watched you do it. You didn't even reach after it."

"I knew where it was going," he said. "Same place everything goes. Downstream. Rearranged." He smiled a little. "Besides, I had it memorized by then. Every coordinate. Every note in her hand. You don't need the paper, Tam. You just need to have read it honestly once."

"Spoken like a man whose family invented custody."

"Spoken like a man whose family invented nothing," he said, "and finally doesn't mind."

We sat with that. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, in a valley sealed under snow until spring, ten thousand voices of a dead nation waited in the dark with their seeds and maps and songs, patient as they'd been for seventy years, waiting for the small sanctioned expedition that would come for them slowly, properly, on the community's terms — descendants and rivals and smugglers' grandsons, a strange little nation forming itself around a door in a mountain. Somebody makes something up and refuses to be embarrassed. My grandmother would have liked them.

"One more thing," Dominic said. He set something on the table between us. Small. Brass. The compass.

"I'm not taking your—"

"I'm not giving it to you. I'm asking you to hold it." He pushed it across. "Next season. When we go back up. I want you navigating. Real navigation, with a needle that points somewhere. You ran your grandmother's river; you should run mine."

I picked it up. It was warm from his pocket, and light, and the needle lay steady under the glass, pointing north with the simple confidence of an instrument that had stopped being asked impossible questions.

"Your mother's?" I asked.

"Mine," he said. "Hers is buried with her, where it belongs. This one's the repair. New parts, old housing." He shrugged. "Seems right that it should go to the man who knows all the escape routes."

I closed my hand around it. Outside, the current kept its books, and the mud stayed mud, and the town slept off a day of settled accounts.

"All right," I said. "But I'm spitting in the river first. Custom's custom."

"Wouldn't dream of stopping you," Dominic said, and poured the last of the wine, and the compass in my fist pointed north, quiet as a closed ledger, toward the mountains where spring was already melting the passes — and where, come thaw, a door would be waiting to open again.
