## 1. The Last Open Line

The bridge over the Calloway Narrows had been dying longer than most of the people scheduled to watch it die. I'd been sent to survey it — officially to confirm what everyone already knew, which is the most honest work description I've ever held. The county wanted a demolition date. The railroad wanted a liability waiver. Nobody wanted the bridge, least of all the bridge.

I arrived on a Tuesday in October with a transit level, a notebook gone soft at the corners from sweat and river damp, and a duffel bag I could pack in ninety seconds flat. That last part isn't a skill you list on a résumé, but it's the one that's kept me employed and unattached for eleven years. Felix Reyes: reads steel like sheet music, leaves before the coffee pot cools.

The morning fog was still burning off when I walked out onto the deck. The Calloway ran green and slow beneath it, the kind of river that looks lazy right up until it takes something from you. The bridge was a through truss, 1908, riveted iron with a timber deck that had been patched so many times the patches had patches. Somebody had painted it once, decades ago, a gray that had faded to the color of old dishwater. Birds owned the upper chords now. Swallows, mostly. They didn't appreciate my footsteps.

"Go ahead, complain," I told them. "Get in line."

I set up the transit on the east abutment and started taking measurements, and this is the part where I should explain what I actually do, because "railroad engineer" makes people picture someone driving a train, and that's not me. I'm the person who decides whether the train can keep driving. Bridges, culverts, ballast, gradients — I measure the skeleton of a line and tell the owners whether it's alive or just hasn't fallen down yet. Most of my work lately has been the second thing. There's a whole economy growing up around infrastructure that's too expensive to save and too embarrassing to admit is dead. I'm its priest. I come, I take readings, I pronounce last rites, I move on.

By nine o'clock I'd found three things wrong with the Calloway span, and by ten I'd found seven, and by noon I'd stopped being surprised. Corrosion at the lower chord joints where the paint had failed — expected. Deck timbers rotted through at every expansion joint — expected. But the west pier had settled four inches since the last inspection, and nobody had noticed, because nobody had been out here, because the only trains that used this line anymore were the ones hauling scrap out of the mill at Harrow Bend, twice a week, slow enough that even a dying bridge could hold them if it held its breath.

I ate lunch sitting on the abutment with my boots hanging over the water. Sandwich, apple, thermos coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration. Downstream, a heron stood in the shallows doing its statue impression, waiting for the river to bring it something. That's the difference between me and a heron, I thought. The heron waits. I've never been any good at it.

Here's the thing about me that the employment file doesn't capture: I'm very good at leaving. Not running — there's a difference, though I'll admit the line between them got blurry somewhere around year six. Leaving is a discipline. You keep your possessions countable, your friendships seasonal, your forwarding address theoretical. You learn to love places the way you love weather: completely, briefly, without expectation that they'll be there tomorrow. It sounds sad when I say it out loud, which is why I don't say it out loud. Inside my own head it sounds like freedom. Mostly. On days like this one, standing on a bridge with an expiration date, it sounds like something else, but I don't have a word for it yet, and I've decided not to go looking.

* * *

The demolition crew showed up Wednesday morning, three men and a woman in orange vests and a flatbed with a excavator chained down on it. Their foreman was a broad, sunburned man named Dombrowski who shook my hand like he was testing it for structural integrity.

"You the engineer?"

"I'm the engineer."

"So how long she got?"

"That depends on how much weight you put on her and how fast."

He grinned. "We're gonna put a lot of weight on her real fast."

"Then I'd say Thursday. Maybe Friday if it doesn't rain."

We spent the day together, him showing me his staging plan, me showing him which members would let go first and where the debris field would land. This is the part of the job nobody thinks about: when a bridge comes down, it doesn't just fall, it *goes somewhere*. The truss will fold toward the upstream side because the current's scoured the pier footings unevenly. The deck will pancake into the channel. If they want to clear the wreckage without dredging the navigation lane, they need to drop it clean, and dropping anything clean requires someone who knows exactly how it's going to break.

That's my actual talent, if I'm honest. Not measuring. Predicting failure. I look at a structure and see the exact way it wants to die, and there's a strange comfort in that, a certainty most people never get. People fail messily and without warning. Steel fails along lines you can draw with a pencil.

Dombrowski's crew worked fast and sloppy-competent, the way demolition crews do, and by late afternoon I'd signed off on their plan and they'd invited me to drink beer off the tailgate of their truck. I said yes, which surprised me a little. Usually I say no. Saying no keeps the departure clean.

"So how long you been doing this?" asked the youngest one, a kid with a neck tattoo of a wolf howling at nothing.

"Eleven years."

"You like it?"

"It pays."

He laughed like I'd said something funny. Dombrowski didn't laugh. He looked at me the way foremen look at engineers, which is with a mixture of respect and suspicion, as if we're a species that hibernates underground and surfaces only to complicate things.

"You work alone?" he asked.

"Mostly."

"Must get lonely."

"Nah." I tipped my bottle at the bridge. "I've got company."

Which was a lie, sort of, but the good kind, the kind you tell so you don't have to hear yourself say the true version. The truth is that loneliness stopped bothering me years ago the way a bad knee stops bothering you — not because it healed, but because you built your whole life around favoring it.

* * *

Thursday night, after the crew knocked off, I stayed out on the bridge alone. This is another thing I do that I don't put in reports. Before a structure dies, I walk it end to end, slow, touching things. Rivet heads worn smooth as knuckles. A splice plate stamped with a foundry mark from a company that dissolved before I was born. Someone had carved initials into the timber railing near the north end — E.M. + R.T., 1931, cut deep enough to survive eighty years of weather. Two people stood here once and wanted the moment to outlast them, and it did, and neither of them knew it, and now I was the only person on earth who'd ever read it.

I took out my notebook and did the thing I always do, the thing that has no professional purpose whatsoever.

In the back of every notebook I carry, there's a map. Hand-drawn, ink on grid paper, corrected and re-corrected until the original grid barely shows through. It's a map of closed lines — every branch, spur, and mainline I've ever surveyed that's been abandoned, torn up, or scheduled for destruction. Railroads are the circulatory system of the continent, and for a hundred years the blood flowed outward, building everything, reaching everywhere. Now the flow has reversed. Lines close every year. Towns that exist only because a rail line reached them start to evaporate when it leaves, slowly, like water finding its way out of a cracked basin.

Nobody maps the closures. The official maps just quietly stop showing the lines, the way old photographs stop showing people who died. One year the line's there; the next year it's blank space, as if the trains never came, as if the towns grew wild from the soil. I find that unbearable in a way I can't fully justify. So I map them. Every dead line gets drawn in, dated, annotated. Cause of death: washout, bankruptcy, consolidation, neglect. It's a census of endings, kept by a woman who attends every funeral and stays for none of the reception.

I uncapped my pen and added the Calloway Narrows span. *Line closed at Narrows Bridge, Oct. Demolition pending. Last traffic: scrap, 2x weekly.* My handwriting on these entries is different from my field notes — slower, more careful, almost calligraphic. I've never examined why. Some things you're better off not examining.

Below the entry, after a hesitation I felt in my chest more than my hand, I wrote: *Good span. Held 116 years. Deserved better than scrap.*

Then I capped the pen and stood there feeling foolish, because it's a bridge, Felix, it doesn't know, it doesn't care, and the swallows certainly don't.

But I stayed anyway. The moon came up over the eastern ridge and laid a silver road across the river, straight as a rail line pointing downstream, toward everywhere I hadn't been yet. I stood on a dying bridge under a full moon and felt the specific ache that is my constant companion and closest colleague: the ache of a person who belongs everywhere temporarily and nowhere permanently.

Tomorrow the bridge comes down. Friday I catch the freight out of Harrow Bend to the next assignment — a flood-damaged yard two hundred miles south, culvert inspection, two weeks' work, maybe three. Then wherever after that. I had the route memorized already. I always have the next route memorized. It's how I sleep.

* * *

They dropped the bridge Saturday at dawn.

I could have left Friday night. I'd finished my report; my signature was on the demolition authorization; there was no professional reason for me to stand on the far bank at 6 a.m. with coffee going cold in my hands. But I told myself the crew might need engineering oversight for the charge placement, which was true for about twenty minutes, after which it became an excuse, after which I stopped pretending even to myself.

Dombrowski counted down on the radio. The charges fired in sequence, a stutter of white flashes and thunder rolling up the valley, and the Calloway Narrows Bridge folded exactly the way I'd predicted — upstream, clean, the truss collapsing into itself like a closing hand. The splash threw spray forty feet. The sound came back off the ridge a second later, doubled, mournful.

The crew cheered. Demolition crews always cheer; it's not disrespect, it's relief, the same cheer surgeons give when the patient survives. I didn't cheer. I watched the dust climb off the wreckage and drift downstream, and I watched the swallows — displaced, furious, circling the gap where their home had been — and I felt the map in my notebook grow heavier in my pocket, one entry heavier, one ending more.

"Well," Dombrowski said, coming to stand beside me. "She went easy."

"They usually do," I said. "It's the living that go hard."

He gave me a look — the look people give me when I say things like that, the look that says *you're younger than that sentence* — and then his radio crackled and he was gone, back into the noise and the machinery, and I was alone again, which was fine, which was the arrangement, which was the whole design.

My freight left Harrow Bend at 4:15. I was on it with ninety seconds to spare, duffel over one shoulder, notebook in the inside pocket where I keep the things I'd grab first in a fire. As the yard slid past — the rusted gondolas, the signal mast with one arm frozen at caution forever, the little brick depot where a station agent I'd known for exactly four days raised a hand to me without smiling, because we both understood the transaction — I pressed my forehead briefly against the cold window glass and did not say goodbye to any of it.

You don't say goodbye to things you're leaving. That's the rule. Goodbye is a root; it grows down into a place and holds you there, and I have spent eleven years making sure nothing holds.

South of Harrow Bend the line followed the river, and the river was high with autumn rain, brown and muscular, carrying branches and fence posts and somebody's lost dock toward the lowlands. I watched it from the caboose window — they let me ride in the caboose, one of the few remaining perks of a dying profession — and I remember thinking, with no particular urgency, that the water looked like it was in a hurry to get somewhere, and that I envied it, and that envy was probably a bad sign.

Two hundred miles south, there was a flooded rail yard with my name on the work order. Culvert inspection. Easy money. In and out in two weeks.

I had no way of knowing — sitting there in a swaying caboose, watching the river run beside the tracks like a rival traveler — that the water was bringing something toward me. Something wedged and waiting, stamped with a seal from a country that no longer existed, carrying a question that would make every map I'd ever drawn look like a child's sketch of the truth.

The river knew. Rivers always know first. They're the oldest couriers on earth, and they deliver whether you've packed or not.

But that's ahead of the story. For now there was just the caboose, the cold window, the fading hills, and a woman with a duffel bag and a map of endings, heading south at forty miles an hour, excellent at leaving, and — though it would take her another nineteen chapters to admit it — tired.
