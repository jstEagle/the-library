## 3. A Nation With No Address

The archive smelled like dust and old decisions.

It sat on the river side of Corben's Landing, a long low building of gray timber that had once been a freight depot and had been demoted, over the decades, to a place where paper went to be forgotten. I'd passed it a dozen times in my years working this stretch of line. I'd never gone in. Archives were for people who intended to stay somewhere — people who needed records because they planned to be held accountable to them later. I traveled light in every sense. My whole history fit in a duffel bag and a leather satchel, and the only archive I kept was the one I drew myself: closed lines, dead bridges, places the world had decided to stop going.

But the chest was sitting in my cab like a passenger who refused to pay his fare, and the seal on its lid kept looking at me.

The double eagle. Two heads, one body, wings spread over a border stamped into brass so old it had gone green at the edges. I'd spent two days telling myself it was municipal — some drowned county's ceremonial junk, a fire brigade crest, a brewery's idea of grandeur. Two days of wading through flooded yard debris with a pry bar while the chest sat locked in my locomotive cab under a tarp, and me pretending I hadn't already tried three keys from my own ring in it, and failed, and felt something in my chest do a slow, embarrassing roll every time I looked at it.

Curiosity is a bad trait in an engineer. An engineer is supposed to want things predictable: gradient, gauge, load, weather. The whole job is knowing what's around the bend before you get there. And here I was, forty-one years old, holding a cup of terrible coffee outside a building full of other people's pasts, about to go looking for something I couldn't see coming.

I went in anyway.

* * *

The archivist was a woman named Mrs. Aldous, or possibly she just tolerated being called that, and she was roughly the age of the building and roughly as welcoming. She looked up from a ledger when the door chimed, took in my coveralls, my boots still crusted white with dried river silt, and said, "Research fee's two dollars. No food, no drink."

I set the coffee on her desk anyway, out of spite, then picked it back up. "I need to identify a seal," I said. "Heraldic. Double-headed eagle, crowned, on a document chest. Brass stamp, probably official government issue."

Something moved behind her eyes — not recognition exactly. More like a person hearing a word in a language they used to speak. "Double-headed eagle," she repeated.

"Two heads. One crown between them."

She put down her pen. "How old?"

"The chest? No idea. The brass is old enough to have been underwater a while. The wax impression inside the lid is intact." I hesitated. "There were documents inside too. Charts. Survey work. Sealed with the same mark."

Mrs. Aldous stood up, slowly, the way old buildings settle. "Come with me," she said, and there was something in her voice now that I didn't like, because it wasn't boredom anymore. It was care. People who worked in archives got careful when you asked about certain things, the way engineers got careful near washouts. It meant there was weight there. It meant the ground moved.

She led me past the public reading room, past the county records, past shelves of plat maps and tax rolls, into a back room where the atlases lived. Big folios, cloth-bound, arranged by decade. She pulled one from 1938 first — before my time, before a lot of things — and laid it open on a table with both hands, gentle as a nurse.

"Show me where you found the chest," she said.

"Twelve miles downriver. Rail yard at Marrow Bend. It came down in the flood, wedged in the culvert debris."

"Upstream of that," she said. "Where did it come from *from*?"

I shrugged. "The river knows. I don't."

She turned the atlas toward me and tapped one finger on the upper river country — the high valley country north of the gorge, where the maps went pale and the contour lines spread out like they'd given up. "This region," she said. "What does your map call it?"

I leaned in. Contours, a river fork, a rail spur that died at a tunnel marked ABANDONED 1911. "Nothing much calls it anything. Highwater Basin, maybe. It's empty up there. Nobody runs lines past the gorge since the washout."

"Look again," Mrs. Aldous said. "Not at what's printed. At what isn't."

I looked. And I'll be honest — at first I saw nothing, because that was the problem. The map was fine. Roads faded to dotted lines and stopped; towns thinned to nothing; the river ran up into blank paper the way rivers do. It looked like every other edge-of-the-map page I'd ever studied, and I'd studied thousands. Empty country. Unsurveyed. Nobody's.

Then she pulled the 1952 atlas off the shelf and opened it to the same page, and the vertigo hit me like a change in grade I hadn't seen coming.

The river was still there. The contours were still there. But the names were different. Not missing — *different*. Where the modern atlas showed nothing, the older one showed a town called Vasko Polje. A station called Stari Most. A branch line running up the east fork where my own map showed only a dotted guess. And across the whole upper basin, printed in small confident capitals, a country name I had never once seen on any map in forty years of reading them:

**VASKARIA**.

"That's not possible," I said, and heard how stupid it sounded the moment it left my mouth.

"It's entirely possible," Mrs. Aldous said. "It happened in '61. The border commissions redrew everything after the war settlements. Vaskaria was dissolved — partitioned among three neighbors, officially absorbed, whatever the treaties wanted to call it. And the new cartographers did what new cartographers always do." She turned pages as she talked, decade by decade, and I watched it happen in fast-forward: VASKARIA shrinking, thinning, the name going from bold capitals to italics to parentheses to a footnote to nothing at all. Towns renamed. Stations re-lettered. By the 1970s atlas, the upper basin was blank. By the current one, it was as if nobody had ever lived there.

"They didn't just move a border," I said slowly. "They erased a—"

"A nation, yes." She said it without drama, the way you'd report a bridge load rating. That somehow made it worse. "Forty years ago. There are still people alive who were born there. There are exiles all over the river towns. And if you look at a map made after 1965, you will never learn any of that. The paper simply agrees they never existed."

I stood there with my coffee going cold and felt the floor tilt in a way that had nothing to do with architecture. I'm a mapper. It's the one true thing about me. I keep a map of every closed line in the territory because I believe — I have always believed — that the world is knowable, that if you walk enough miles and record enough grades you can trust the ground under you. Maps don't lie. Maps are the opposite of leaving, the opposite of forgetting. A map says: this was here, and here is how it was, and someone bothered to write it down.

And here was a hole in the map the size of a country. Not unmapped — *unmapped*. Deliberately. On purpose. Somebody had taken a whole nation of people off the paper, and every atlas I'd ever trusted had gone along with it, and I had gone along with every atlas.

"You said the chest had charts inside," Mrs. Aldous said. "Survey documents?"

"Rail surveys. Grades, alignments, water levels. Professional work." I paused. "Sealed with the double eagle."

She was quiet a moment. Then she walked to a cabinet, unlocked it with a key from a chain at her waist, and brought out a flat box of reference plates — heraldry, seals, official marks. She flipped through with the efficiency of someone who had done this exact lookup before, maybe more than once, maybe for other people carrying other pieces of a drowned country.

She stopped on a plate and turned it toward me without a word.

The double eagle looked back at us. Two heads, one crown, wings spread. Beneath it, in engraved capitals: **ROYAL SURVEY AUTHORITY OF THE KINGDOM OF VASKARIA — OFFICE OF WORKS AND WATERWAYS**.

"That's it," I said. "That's exactly it."

"Then you're holding government property of a country that no longer exists," Mrs. Aldous said. "Which makes it either priceless or worthless, depending entirely on who's asking." She closed the plate book. "May I ask what the charts show?"

"I haven't been through all of them. The ones I've read are rail alignments up the east fork. Toward the headwaters." I hesitated again, and then, because she'd shown me a hole in the world and I figured I owed her honesty: "One chart has a note across it. Red ink. It says FORBIDDEN SOURCE — DO NOT SURVEY PAST MARKER 40. Official stamp underneath."

Mrs. Aldous went very still.

It was a small thing, her stillness. But I've spent my life reading signals — flag signals, hand signals, the tiny human telegraphy of people standing next to heavy machinery — and I know what stillness means. Stillness means the ground just moved.

"You know something," I said.

"I know rumors," she said. "Rumors aren't records. I deal in records." But she didn't unlock the cabinet again, and she didn't meet my eye, and she added, carefully, "If the charts point upstream, and they carry that seal, and they carry that warning — then whoever sealed that chest didn't lose it in a flood. They hid it. And somebody, somewhere, has been waiting a long time for it to surface." She straightened up, and the archivist mask came back down over whatever had briefly shown through. "Two dollars for research. The atlas consultation is free. Consider it a professional courtesy between people who keep records."

I paid her. At the door, I paused.

"One more question. The exiles. You said there are Vaskarians in the river towns."

"There are. Quiet communities. They keep to themselves. They hold onto what they can hold onto." She looked at me over her glasses. "Why?"

"Because if I were a man whose country had been erased from every map," I said, "and a chest bearing its seal washed up out of the river into the hands of a stranger — I think I'd want to know about it."

Mrs. Aldous considered that for a long moment.

"Then I'd suggest," she said, "that you be careful who sees you carrying it. Countries that get erased usually get erased by somebody. And somebodies tend to notice when their erasure starts coming undone."

* * *

I walked back to the yard along the levee road with the afternoon going gold on the water, and I did something I almost never do, which is think about my mother.

Not because of her particularly. Because of a thing she used to say, back when I was small and we moved so often I learned to pack my toys by feel in the dark. She'd stand in another empty rented room and say, *Home is wherever the map ends, Felix. Past the last line, nobody can follow you.* I thought it was wisdom when I was eight. Later I understood it was just exhaustion wearing a wise costume — the philosophy of a woman who'd been followed one time too many, by bill collectors or worse, and had taught her daughter that safety meant being findable by no one.

I built my whole life on that sentence. Closed lines. Dead stations. Places past the last mark, where the paper gives out and nobody comes looking. I thought I was mapping freedom.

Standing on the levee with a drowned nation's seal burning a hole in my satchel, I understood for the first time that the blank spaces on maps aren't empty. They're full. Full of people who got wiped off, full of towns that got renamed, full of children who grew up speaking a language their own country's atlases denied existed. Every blank space I'd ever run to was somebody's erased home. The map I trusted had holes in it, and the holes weren't gaps.

They were shaped like people.

At the yard, the chest sat where I'd left it, under the tarp in the cab, patient as ballast. I climbed up, threw back the canvas, and unlocked it — third try, the small brass key I'd found taped inside the lid lining, which I had told no one about, because apparently even my curiosity had survival instincts.

The charts came out dry and crisp, rolled in oilcloth by someone who'd cared about them. I spread them across the cab floor and worked through them as the light failed, and the picture assembled itself the way a route profile assembles: not all at once, but inevitably.

Rail surveys. Dozens of them. Decades of them, by the dating codes. The eastern fork, the gorge, the high basin — surveyed and resurveyed by the Royal Survey Authority, alignment after alignment climbing toward the headwaters, each one more precise than the last, each one stopping dead at the same place.

Marker 40.

Every chart stopped at Marker 40. Forty kilometers up the east fork, right where the gorge narrowed and the maps went blank, every single survey terminated — and above that line, in the same red ink, the same hand: FORBIDDEN SOURCE. DO NOT SURVEY PAST THIS POINT. And beneath the warnings, in smaller script, annotations in a different hand — cramped, precise, engineering shorthand I recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall—

I stopped breathing.

I knew that handwriting.

I'd spent six years watching it fill the margins of my textbooks. I'd watched it draw tangent lines and calculate superelevation and correct my own sloppy figures with two economical strokes. Oren Vasquez. The man who taught me everything — how to read water, how to read steel, how to leave a town without anyone noticing you'd gone. The man whose last letter to me said *keep moving, kid, staying is how they find you.*

Oren Vasquez had annotated these charts. Oren Vasquez had written, in his own cramped hand, beside the forbidden source of a drowned country: *sealed per directive. Let it stay buried.*

I sat in the darkening cab with a dead nation's charts spread around me and my mentor's handwriting staring up from forty-year-old paper, and the vertigo came back, worse than before. Because it wasn't just the map that had holes now.

My whole life had one, shaped exactly like him.

Somewhere out on the levee road, a car engine started — the first vehicle to come down that road since noon — and its headlights swept the yard fence and held there, idling, not moving on.

I folded the charts along Oren's creases, exactly as they'd been folded, and I sat very still in the dark, and I listened.
