## 4. The Man Who Watches Trains

There is a particular skill to watching a person without being seen, and I have had forty years to perfect it. My mother taught me the first lesson of it on the last night Vaskaria existed, though neither of us knew that yet. *Stand where the light doesn't want you,* she said, holding my collar so hard it left a bruise shaped like her fingers. *A man who watches is never caught. A man who is watched is already lost.*

I was nine years old. I have been standing in bad light ever since.

The woman I was watching now — Felix Reyes, engineer, mapmaker, ghost of the branch lines — was doing something to a culvert grate with a pry bar and a vocabulary she clearly believed the river couldn't hear. The river heard. Rivers hear everything. That is why we trust them more than maps.

Let me explain what brought me here, because it will sound like madness and I would rather it sound like method.

* * *

My name is Dain Kovic, and I am from a country that no longer exists, which makes me a man from nowhere with an excellent memory. There are perhaps eleven thousand of us scattered across three continents — Vaskarians, though if you said the word aloud in most cities you'd get a blank look, or worse, a polite correction: *Don't you mean Vasquez Province? Don't you mean the Kessel Corridor?* The treaties that dissolved us were thorough. They redrew borders, renamed rivers, reprinted atlases. What they could not reprint was the eleven thousand of us, carrying our impossible country around inside our skulls like contraband.

We meet twice a year, when we can. In rented halls above restaurants, in the back rooms of churches whose saints are not our saints. We eat food our grandparents ate and speak a language the governments of four nations officially classify as extinct, which is a funny thing to be told about the words coming out of your own mouth. *Extinct.* As if I were a dodo with opinions.

At the last gathering — Rotterdam, a hall above a Greek restaurant, rain like a verdict on the windows — old Marta Brenner stood up during the coffee hour and did something unprecedented. She held up a photograph.

"You all know the story of the Source," she said. "The spring at the head of the Vaska. The one the old regime sealed, the one the new governments pretend never fed anything." She let the photograph travel hand to hand. "This chest was recovered from a river barge forty years ago. It went into a private collection. The collection was sold. And last month, a dealer in Corvane offered it to a buyer who turned it down — but not before photographing the seal."

I didn't need to see the photograph. I knew the seal the way you know your own face in a dark window. Double eagle, wings folded, crown between them. The arms of the House of Vaskar. The mark that had been on every survey document, every rail charter, every birth certificate in the country that raised me and then stopped existing.

"The chest is in the flood country," Marta said. "Somewhere along the lower Vaska. The storm season will move it or bury it. If any of you are near—" She paused, and her voice did the thing that old exiles' voices do, the thing where pride and grief share a sentence. "If any of you are near, it should not fall into stranger hands."

Eleven thousand people, and only one of us was near. Me. Because I am always near. Because for thirty years I have followed the dead lines of the Vaskan railway system as a track inspector, a signalman, a consultant, a drifter — whatever work keeps me close to the iron spine of the country they erased. The rails are still there under the gravel even where the trains stopped running. Rails outlast nations. I find this comforting in a way I cannot fully explain to people whose homelands are still on maps.

So I came to the flood country. And I asked questions the way I know how to ask them — sideways, patiently, buying drinks for yardmen and ferry pilots and the kind of old men who sit at the mouths of railyards watching trains go by and rating them out loud. And the answer, when it finally surfaced, was a name.

Reyes. A railroad engineer. Salvaged something out of the floodwater at the Meridian yards. Been asking after a double-eagle stamp.

And then I found her, and I watched her, and I hesitated.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Felix Reyes before I ever saw her, gathered from three days of listening: she worked alone. She took contracts nobody wanted on lines nobody rode. She kept a notebook of closed railways the way other men keep photographs of lovers. She had a mentor once — a famous one, Oren Vasquez, the man who wrote half the engineering canon for this whole watershed — and he died two years ago, and she had not stayed anywhere longer than a season since.

A woman who collects endings. Of course she found the chest. The river gave its dead thing to the one person alive who catalogs dead things.

I watched her for two more days before I decided how to approach her, which is to say I watched her and did not decide anything, because deciding requires a courage I was rationing.

She was easy to watch, in the way that competent people are easy to watch. There is a pleasure in it. She walked a bridge scheduled for demolition like a doctor examining a patient she liked too much to lie to. She talked to machinery — actually talked, low and continuous, apologies and encouragements, *come on, sweetheart, don't do this to me today* — and the machinery, against all mechanical law, seemed to listen. When she slept in the cab of her inspection car she slept like a soldier, boots on, one hand near the tools.

When she opened the chest — I saw her do it once, through the window of a rented room over the chandlery, by lamplight — she sat very still for a long time afterward. Hours, maybe. I know because I sat very still too, in the alley across the street, feeling like the worst kind of thief: the kind who steals nothing and takes everything anyway.

Because here is the thing I have not said yet, the thing I will say badly:

Those charts in that chest. The surveys of the upper Vaska. The annotations around the word *forbidden*. I grew up on those charts. Not literally — but my father helped draw their successors. He was a surveyor for the Vaskan State Railways, and when the dissolution came, the new authorities collected every original plate of the headwaters surveys and destroyed them, publicly, in a furnace, with newsreel cameras. *Redundant documentation,* the announcement said. My father watched his life's work burn on a cinema screen in a refugee camp in Corvane, and he never spoke above a whisper again.

The chest Felix Reyes pulled out of the river may contain the only surviving originals. The source itself — the spring, the valley, whatever the old regime sealed and the new states pretended wasn't there — may exist nowhere else on paper. Eleven thousand people remember a country. This woman is holding the receipts.

And I stood in an alley for three nights, rehearsing how to say hello.

* * *

You would think it would be simple. *Excuse me, madam, I believe you have my nation in that box.* But it is never simple, because I know what happens when strangers learn what you carry. I was six when the soldiers came through our town with lists. My uncle Tomas was on the list for the crime of having been a stationmaster, which made him infrastructure, and infrastructure belonging to a dissolved state gets decommissioned. We never learned which list he ended up on. There were several.

So I did what watchers do. I waited. I told myself I was gathering intelligence. I learned her schedule down to the minute — up at five, coffee at the chandlery counter standing up, six hours at the yard office with the chest's contents spread under blotting paper, evening walk along the levee with her hands in her pockets like a woman arguing with someone who isn't there.

On the fourth evening I followed the levee walk. Fifty meters back. Bad light, as trained. And I got close enough to hear her arguing with herself, because apparently she argued out loud when she thought the levee was empty.

"They're lying," she was telling the river. "Somebody burned these numbers. You don't misprint a whole valley. Somebody *chose*."

I stopped walking. The river said nothing, diplomatically.

She chose the wrong valley. Or rather, somebody else chose it for her, forty years ago, with a furnace and a newsreel camera. And she had found the seam in the erasure, and she was standing on it, and she didn't know there was a whole scattered nation holding its breath on the other side.

That was the moment I should have stepped forward. Any reasonable account of myself says that was the moment. Instead I stepped backward, off the levee path into the shadow of a flood gate, because my mother's bruise-shaped lesson lives deeper in me than my wants do, and my wants, I am ashamed to report, had begun to complicate things.

Because it wasn't only the chest anymore. Somewhere in those four nights of watching, the mission had developed a second objective, unauthorized and unlogged. I had started watching her the way you watch a train you want to be on.

* * *

Let me tell you about looking backward, since I'm apparently confessing things tonight.

Exiles live facing the past the way sunflowers face the sun; it's not a choice, it's heliotropism. Every conversation in the rented halls eventually bends toward Before. The bread in Before. The bells in Before. Your grandmother's kitchen in Before. We are archaeologists of our own childhoods, and the dig sites are closing, one death at a time. Marta Brenner is eighty-one. When she goes, the pronunciation of fourteen village names goes with her.

I spent my whole adult life believing my job was to keep the past from finishing its disappearance. Guard the language. Guard the recipes. Guard the memory of a spring at the head of a river that official geography insists is fed by snowmelt and nothing else.

But watching Felix Reyes argue with a river about the honesty of maps, I felt something I hadn't felt since I was nine years old and my mother gripped my collar: the pull of forward. Not *remember* — *arrive.* Not *guard* — *find.* If the valley exists, if the descendants of the hidden ones are still up there — and the old stories say some families went into the high country during the purges and never came down — then the past isn't behind me at all. It's upstream. It has a location. For the first time in my life, my country might be somewhere I could *go*, instead of somewhere I'm from.

That is a dangerous hope. Hope is dangerous for men like me precisely because it moves us. A watcher who wants something becomes a walker, and walkers get seen.

I stood in the flood-gate shadow and conducted an internal audit, which went approximately as follows:

*Dain, you coward, she is right there.*
*Dain, you fool, the Combine has been asking the same questions you have. Two of their men bought drinks at the ferry pilot's table yesterday and their questions were less sideways than yours.*
*Dain, you disaster, if the Combine finds her first—*

That third voice ended the audit. Fear, as usual, proved a better motivator than courage. Courage is a luxury good; fear is available to everyone.

* * *

I nearly managed it the next morning. Nearly.

She came out of the chandlery with her coffee, and I positioned myself at the corner where her route crossed mine, and I composed my opening line — something casual, something about rail gauges, the universal passport of our profession — and I had actually begun to inhale for the purpose of speech when a black sedan slid past us both, slow, windows down, two men inside wearing the particular blankness of men paid not to have expressions.

They looked at her. They looked at the chandlery. They drove on.

Felix watched the sedan go with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the flat stillness of a person recalculating exits. Then she drank her coffee in one swallow, went back inside, and came out ten minutes later with the chest in a canvas tool bag, walking fast, heading for the yard.

I let her get twenty meters ahead and followed, and hated myself with great efficiency, because following her was exactly what the sedan's occupants were also doing, and I had just become indistinguishable from the threat. Excellent tradecraft, Kovic. Blend in with the wolves.

At the yard gate she stopped. Turned. Looked directly at me — directly, the full weight of it, eyes the color of wet slate and about as welcoming — and said, "You've been behind me for four days. Either say what you want or find a different hobby."

I would like to report that I answered with dignity. What I actually did was freeze, mouth open, a man caught mid-inhale with his nation's entire case load balanced on an unspoken sentence. Four days of rehearsal and every opening line I'd composed evaporated at once, leaving only the truth, which arrived in my throat unbidden and came out as:

"The double eagle. Wings folded. Crown between them. It's not a heraldry curiosity. It's a country."

Her expression didn't change, which told me she'd already suspected I existed, and had probably known since the second day, and had let me keep following her either out of contempt or curiosity, and I genuinely could not tell which, and both options stung.

"Keep walking," she said quietly. "Yard office. Now. And if those men in the sedan are yours—"

"They're not mine. They're worse than mine. They're buyers." I fell into step beside her, heart going like a rod knocked loose at speed. "Meridian Combine. They've been asking after the chest since before the storm ended. If they reach you with an offer, understand what the offer is really purchasing."

"And what's that?"

"Not the paper," I said. "The ending. They buy things like this so the story stays finished."

She glanced at me sidelong, and for one suspended second I saw her decide something — weigh me, file me, shelve me somewhere between threat and tool.

"Who are you?" she said.

"Dain Kovic," I said. "Track inspector. Formerly of the Vaskan State Railways." Saying the name of it out loud, the whole name, the country-name, in open air, for the first time in decades — I won't pretend my voice was steady. "Which is to say: formerly of a place that no longer appears on your maps. And I believe you're carrying the proof that it should."

The yard gate stood open in front of us. Behind us, at the corner by the chandlery, the black sedan had come back around and was idling, patient as interest accruing.

Felix looked at the sedan, then at me, and made the fastest decision I would ever witness her make.

"Get inside," she said, "and start talking fast — because I've got questions, and you've got about as long as it takes me to finish this coffee."

I went through the gate. Forward, for once. Though I'll admit I checked the light first.
