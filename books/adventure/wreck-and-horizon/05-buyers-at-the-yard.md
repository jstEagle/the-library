## 5. Buyers at the Yard

They came on a Tuesday, which I have always considered a suspicious day for anything. Mondays you expect trouble. Fridays it arrives wearing cologne. But Tuesday is the day bad news dresses like good news, and sure enough, at nine in the morning, while I was elbow-deep in the chest's third drawer trying to make sense of a contour map drawn by someone who clearly hated rulers, a black car with government plates that weren't government plates rolled into the yard and stopped exactly one meter from my toolbox.

Nobody parks that precisely by accident. Precision like that is a résumé.

There were two of them. The driver stayed with the car — broad man, sunglasses, hands resting on his thighs in the universal posture of someone paid to be useless until he isn't. The other one got out and crossed the gravel like he owned it, or would shortly. Gray suit, gray hair, gray smile. He had the kind of face that gets remembered by witnesses as "pleasant" and nothing else, which is either a gift or a lifetime of practice.

"Felix Reyes?" he said, though we both knew he'd already checked my name against three databases before breakfast.

"That depends who's asking."

"Mr. Aldous Vane." He offered a hand. I looked at it. He put it away without embarrassment, which told me he'd been rebuffed by better people than me and worse. "I represent the Meridian Combine."

The name landed somewhere behind my ribs. You don't work rail salvage for eleven years without hearing about Meridian. They were the ones who bought up dead lines before anyone else realized they were dead, stripped the rails, sold the ballast, and vanished before the towns they'd hollowed out finished noticing. They didn't demolish things so much as *un-happen* them. A bridge Meridian touched wasn't torn down; it simply stopped having ever been worth saving.

"The Combine," I said. "I thought you people only worked in places with actual money."

"We go where there's value." He glanced at the open door of my workshop, where the chest sat on my bench under a drop cloth I'd thrown over it with the optimism of a woman hiding an elephant behind a napkin. "And value has come to you, Ms. Reyes. May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. That was another tell. Men who wait for permission are selling something; men who don't are buying.

He lifted the drop cloth and looked at the chest the way a jeweler looks at a stone — not with wonder, but with arithmetic. "Vaskarian survey chest, military pattern, double-eagle seal. Do you know what you have here?"

"A heavy box that clogged a culvert."

"Forty years ago, Vaskaria produced the finest hydrographic surveys in the world. Their engineers mapped water the way monks transcribed scripture. When the country was dissolved, most of those records were destroyed or scattered. A complete field chest from the upper Kestrel watershed—" he turned to me, and the gray smile widened by a professional millimeter "—is a significant artifact. Museums would want it. Collectors certainly would. The Combine collects."

"You collect chests?"

"We collect completeness." He said it lightly, but something underneath it didn't feel light at all. "Ms. Reyes, I'm authorized to offer you forty thousand for the chest, contents intact, delivered to our facility within the week."

I'll be honest with myself, because nobody else was going to be: forty thousand is the number at which my mouth went dry. Forty thousand is a small house in a town with a school. It's never working a demolition survey again. It's sleeping through the night because nothing downstream needs me awake. I had spent eleven years being excellent at leaving, and here was a man offering to pay me to stop — permanently, comfortably, in some place where nobody would ever need to find me.

That should have been my first warning. Anything that sounds like your dream when a stranger says it out loud deserves a second listen.

"I'll consider it," I said.

"Of course." He produced a card — heavy stock, embossed, the kind of card that costs more to print than my boots. "Take a day. Two at most. The offer reflects urgency on our side, and urgency doesn't improve with age."

"How did you know I had it?"

Something flickered across his pleasant face — not guilt, nothing so honest as guilt. More like a man deciding how much truth the transaction required. "Salvage is a small world, Ms. Reyes. Things wash up. People talk. We listen."

"People talk," I repeated. "Which people?"

"The ones who talk to us." He straightened his cuffs. "You'll find we're easy to deal with. Fair prices, clean paperwork, no complications. Our only request is discretion. There are others interested in Vaskarian materials, and some of them are less... organized than we are. Selling to us protects you from them, frankly."

"From whom?"

"Exactly." He smiled again, and this time I saw the teeth in it. "Two days, Ms. Reyes."

The car left the way it came, precise as a signature. I stood in the yard gravel for a long time afterward, listening to the river move through its channels, thinking about the word *discretion*, and about how men like Aldous Vane never say *we'll take care of you*. They say *no complications*. As if complications were things that happened to you, rather than things you did to other people.

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night, which was fine, because I'd planned to spend it working anyway. Sleep and I had been on thin terms since Oren died; the nights were when the running caught up with you, all those departures lining up outside your skull like trains waiting on a signal that never changes.

Instead I spread the charts across the workshop floor and actually looked at them.

I'd been treating the chest like cargo — inventory it, catalog it, decide its fate. But sitting cross-legged on cold concrete at two in the morning, I finally read the thing. And once I started reading, I couldn't stop.

These weren't just surveys. They were *corrections*. Page after page of the upper Kestrel watershed, drawn in a draftsman's hand so fine it looked printed, and every sheet carried annotations in red ink that didn't match the original cartography. Depths revised. Springs relocated. One entire tributary — marked on every modern map I owned as seasonal, dry eight months of the year — annotated in red: *perennial. flow verified 3 seasons. do not publish.*

Do not publish. On a hydrographic survey. Written by someone whose entire job was publication.

And then there was the handwriting itself. I'd been avoiding looking too closely at it, the way you avoid a word stuck in your throat, but around three o'clock I pulled out one of Oren's old training exercises from my bag — the slope-stability worksheet he'd made me redo four times when I was twenty-three, *again, Felix, again, precision isn't pride* — and laid it beside the red annotations.

The *f*s matched. The way he hooked his numerals. The little tick he put over every *t*, impatient, like even his letters were in a hurry to get somewhere else.

Oren Vasquez had held this chest. Had written on these charts. Had helped decide that a river's source — a *forbidden* source, someone had gone to great trouble to make it forbidden — stayed off every map I'd ever trusted.

I sat back on my heels in the middle of my own floor, surrounded by paper, feeling the particular vertigo of a woman who maps closed lines discovering that the open ones were never fully open either. My whole career was built on the belief that the world could be known if you walked it carefully enough. Oren taught me that. Oren, who also apparently knew things he'd decided the world shouldn't.

What else had he decided for me?

* * *

Vane came back the next afternoon, a full day early, which told me everything about how patient the Combine was and how closely they were watching.

"Have you reached a decision?" he asked, standing in my doorway without being invited, because of course he hadn't waited to be invited. Behind him, the broad man from the car leaned against the fender, and I noticed — filed away, the way you file away a sound your engine makes that it didn't make yesterday — that there was a second car now, parked down the road, occupied.

"I have questions first."

"Of course."

"Who owned this chest originally? Where was it coming from, and where was it going, when the river took it?"

Vane's smile performed patience. "Provenance is our department, not yours. You're selling an object, Ms. Reyes, not a story."

"Everything's a story. That's usually why people want the object."

"Then let me simplify." He stepped inside — uninvited again, and this time I moved deliberately to stand between him and the bench, and I watched him register that, and I watched him decide it didn't matter yet. "Sixty thousand. Final. The chest, contents intact, by Friday."

Sixty. He'd jumped twenty thousand in a day. Either the Combine was flush, or whatever was in this chest was worth far more than sixty thousand to somebody, and Vane was the discount rate.

"Why does a salvage syndicate care about old water charts?"

"Collectors are eccentric."

"Try again."

The pleasant mask held, but I felt it strain, like ice under a boot. "Ms. Reyes. I've been courteous. I've been generous. Let me be plain instead, since you seem to prefer it." He folded his hands. "The Meridian Combine acquires assets. Assets include objects, corridors, contracts, and reputations. We always pay fair value for the asset. What we cannot always protect is the *holder* of the asset, when the holder becomes an obstacle. There are parties interested in Vaskarian materials who are considerably less courteous than I am. I mentioned them yesterday. I mention them today as a courtesy, and as a warning: their interest does not expire Friday."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a weather report." He set a second card on my bench, next to the first, squaring the corners with a fingertip. "Sixty thousand. Friday. Or keep the chest, and keep everything that comes attached to it. Some attachments are heavier than they look." His eyes went, briefly, to the photograph I keep tacked above my bench — Oren and me, younger, standing on a bridge neither of us knew was scheduled for anything. "People who live alone, travel alone, answer to no one — they're very easy to lose track of. Nobody misses a person with no address."

He said it kindly. That was the worst part. He said it the way you'd tell someone their tire looked low.

They left. I stood in the doorway and watched the two cars go, one precise, one patient, and understood that I had just been measured — weighed, priced, and found movable.

Here's the thing, though. Here's what Vane didn't know, standing in my workshop doing his arithmetic.

I grew up around men like him. Not his suits, not his cars — but his *kind*. The buyers. The ones who show up wherever something valuable is attached to something breakable, and offer enough money to make the breaking feel voluntary. They bought my mother's building when I was nine. They bought the pension of every man on Oren's last crew. They buy things, and then they erase the people attached to them, and the erasure is always called something clean — *restructuring*, *acquisition*, *progress* — and the people always sign something first, because a signature makes the vanishing look like consent.

I had spent my whole life making sure there was nothing on me worth buying. No property, no contract, no address, no one. A woman with no attachments can't be leveraged. That was the whole design. Oren built it into me along with the surveying — *never own more than you can carry, never owe anyone, never stay* — and I'd obeyed so faithfully I'd mistaken the cage for a philosophy.

But now there was a chest on my bench with a dead nation's seal and my dead mentor's handwriting, and a man in a gray suit had just told me, politely, that I was an asset with a holder, and the holder was negotiable.

And I found — standing in the gravel, heart going hard, fear sitting in my chest like a stone I couldn't cough up — that under the fear, somewhere below it, was something hotter.

Defiance, maybe. Or its ugly cousin, spite. Or maybe just the plain animal refusal of a woman who has been leaving her entire life being told, finally, explicitly, that she has nowhere she's allowed to stand.

Because here's what else I recognized, watching that car shrink down the road. Vane thought he was negotiating with a lone woman holding a box. He thought solitude was leverage.

He didn't know about the charts. He didn't know about the red ink, the *do not publish*, the perennial tributary that some empire had drowned in paperwork. He didn't know that whoever wrote those annotations had risked something enormous to hide them in a river, betting that someday the water would give them back to someone stubborn enough to read them.

The chest hadn't washed up to me by accident. Nothing that heavy travels that far without intent.

I went back inside, took both of Vane's cards, and pinned them to the wall above Oren's photograph — side by side, like a warning label, like a dare.

Then I opened the chest, pulled out the deepest chart — the one with the source marked in red, the one with coordinates that pointed upriver into country no train had served in forty years — and I began, very carefully, to copy it.

If the Combine wanted the chest by Friday, then Friday was when I intended to be gone — upriver, up-gradient, toward whatever it was that Oren Vasquez had helped bury and the Meridian Combine was willing to kill to keep buried.

I just needed a train. A dead line. And, God help me, a crew.

I was still staring at the coordinates, doing load calculations in my head, when I noticed the figure across the yard — a man, tall, dark-coated, standing at the fence line where the gravel met the weeds. He'd been there, I realized, since before Vane arrived. Watching the trains. Watching me.

He raised one hand — not a wave, more like a question — and then, when I didn't answer it, turned and walked away along the riverbank, unhurried, as if he had all the time in the world and intended to spend all of it watching.
