## 6. Dain

I had watched Felix Reyes for eleven days before I spoke to her, which by the standards of my family is practically impulsive. My grandmother once waited out a border guard for three years, memorizing his lunch schedule, before she crossed a river with nothing but a sewing kit and a forged name. Patience is our inheritance. We are a people who learned that the fastest way to lose everything is to hurry.

But eleven days of watching Felix Reyes was testing even my inheritance.

She worked like a woman arguing with the world and winning on points. I saw her re-shim a warped coupler in the rail yard with two shims and a curse, then stand back and look at her own work with suspicion, as if the coupler might be planning something. I saw her eat standing up, always facing the door. I saw her sleep in the cab of her engine rather than rent a room, though she could afford one — I knew roughly what the Meridian Combine had offered her, because knowing things is what I do now that my country no longer exists to give me a job.

And every evening, when the heat broke, she walked through the night market on Kessel Street like a woman checking exits. She never bought anything. She looked at everything.

That was how I knew how to reach her. You don't approach a woman like Felix Reyes with a plea or a threat. You approach her with an inventory.

* * *

The market at Kessel Street assembles itself at dusk the way rivers assemble from creeks — nobody decides it, it simply happens where the ground allows. Lanterns strung between awning poles. A man selling grilled eels off a cart he swore was licensed in three countries, none of them real. Women from the coast with trays of candied ginger. Somewhere always a fiddle, and somewhere always a man claiming the fiddle player owed him money.

I bought two skewers of eel I didn't want, because a man holding food looks like a man with nowhere urgent to be, and I stationed myself near the map-seller's stall.

The map-seller was a fat, cheerful liar named Brannoc who sold reproductions of atlases to tourists and, in a drawer under the counter, sold the other kind of maps to people who needed them. I had bought from him before. He liked me because I paid in cash and never asked him to smile.

Felix came up the street at half past eight, as she did every night, hands in her jacket pockets, eyes doing their slow inventory: exits, crowds, rooflines. She stopped at Brannoc's stall — she stopped there every third night — and ran her finger along a framed reproduction of a coastal survey without seeing it, the way a gambler touches a rail.

I stepped up beside her and said, "The chest is elm. Not oak. Everyone assumes oak, because of the iron banding, but it's elm. Vaskarian cooperages used elm for anything meant to travel by water. It swells shut instead of rotting."

She didn't look at me. She looked at Brannoc's maps, and her hand drifted, very casually, toward the knife I knew she kept at the small of her back under the jacket.

"Keep talking," she said, "and find out what happens."

"Gladly." I set the eel skewers down on Brannoc's counter, partly as a gesture of peace and partly because they were terrible. "Inside the chest: forty-one survey sheets, linen-backed, rolled in three bundles tied with tarred cord. The top sheet of the middle bundle is a longitudinal profile of the upper Sereva gorge, drawn at a scale of one inch to four hundred feet, and someone has written *ne dolž* across the headwaters in pencil so hard it tore the paper. Below that, a sealed report with a broken stamp. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth, a brass theodolite with a cracked level vial and the initials O.V. scratched into the base."

Now she looked at me.

It was not a friendly look. It was the look of a woman recalculating every assumption she'd made about being unobserved, and finding the sum ugly. But she didn't reach for the knife, which I counted as progress.

"Who are you," she said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"My name is Dain Kovic." I gave her the name plainly, because a false name would have been an insult to us both, and because if the Combine was going to come for me anyway, I preferred they come for the correct man. "I'm Vaskarian."

"There's no such thing," she said. "I checked. Three archives. The seal doesn't resolve to any nation, any registry, any—"

"I know what you checked. I know which archivist you bribed with coffee and flattery, because I've bribed him too, and he told me about you the same day." I let that sit. "There's no such thing as Vaskaria anymore. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

We walked. She chose the route — away from the lanterns, along the canal towpath where the light came only from the refinery across the water and everything smelled of tar and cooling metal. She walked on my left, knife side toward me, and I noted it and let her see me note it, because pretending otherwise would have been its own kind of lie.

"Start with the seal," she said.

"The double eagle, crowned, holding a river in its talons instead of a scepter. Every other nation put weapons in the eagle's feet. We put a river. My grandfather said it was because Vaskaria never conquered anything it couldn't drink." I shrugged. "He also said the national bird was the mosquito, so take him with a grain of salt."

"And the country?"

"Dissolved forty-one years ago. Partitioned between two neighbors who signed papers in a hotel ballroom while our army was still technically fighting. The maps were redrawn within the year. The registries followed. Then the birth records, the land titles, the language in the schools. You can erase a country in about a decade if you're thorough and nobody stops you." I kept my voice level. Forty-one years of practice. "Nobody stopped them."

"And the chest? How does a dead nation's survey chest end up jammed in a culvert in a rail yard?"

"That," I said, "is the question I have spent nine years and most of my money answering. May I tell you a story? It's long, and you strike me as a woman who resents long stories, but this one has a payoff."

She said nothing, which from her I was learning was permission.

"In '39, the last government of Vaskaria knew the end was coming. They couldn't stop the partition, but they could hide things. There was a program — unofficial, deniable — moving archives out of the capital by rail, disguised as freight. Machine parts, agricultural equipment. One of those shipments was a survey expedition's entire output from the upper Sereva valley. Charts, reports, instruments. The expedition had found something upstream that the new powers very much wanted not to exist, and the old government wanted it preserved somewhere the new borders wouldn't reach."

"The train never arrived," Felix said.

I stopped walking. "How do you know that?"

"Because I know what happened to trains that didn't arrive." She nodded back toward the yards, the dark hump of the branch line. "Washout on the Sereva bridge, autumn of '39. Freight went into the river. Salvage crews pulled out what they could over the next two years; then the war ate the budget and they wrote the cargo off. The manifest listed machine parts." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Machine parts don't usually weigh four hundred pounds and get packed in elm."

"So the chest has been in that river for forty years."

"Forty years, minus one storm and one stubborn engineer." She turned and faced me fully for the first time, and across the water the refinery light caught her face and made her look carved rather than drawn. "Here's my problem, Kovic. Everything you just told me, I could have gotten from the same archives you clearly crawled through. The seal, the dissolution, the freight scheme — it's all recoverable, if you dig. What isn't recoverable is why you know the *contents*. Nobody alive has seen inside that chest except me. So either you're the best-informed liar on this continent, or—"

"Or I've seen the manifest," I said. "The original. Not the doctored one filed after the washout — the expedition's packing list, which survived separately, because the man who carried it out of Vaskaria was my grandfather's brother, and he hid it inside a Torah scroll for thirty years, which is either sacrilege or the highest use a book can be put to, depending on which rabbi you ask."

Her eyes narrowed. "You're telling me your family has been sitting on a packing list for forty years."

"We've been sitting on a *country*, Miss Reyes. A packing list was easy to keep in the pantry." I heard my own voice go thin and pulled it back. "Two hundred and six sheets were packed into that chest. You have forty-one. The rest were lost with the train — or scattered, or burned, we don't know. What matters is that the forty-one include the profile chart of the gorge and the sealed report, and those two documents together prove the source exists. Prove it in ink, at scale, with instrument readings. Proof is the whole game. Without proof, Vaskarians are a diaspora with a sad story. With proof—" I stopped, because my voice had started doing something I didn't authorize.

"With proof, what?" she said quietly.

"With proof, we exist again. On paper first. Paper is how it starts. It's always how it starts — theirs and ours."

* * *

We walked another quarter mile before she spoke again. The canal slid past, black and patient. A barge lamp moved somewhere upstream, going nowhere fast.

"You want the chest," she said finally.

"No."

"Everyone wants the chest. The Combine offered me more money than I've earned in ten years, and when I stalled, their man smiled at me in a way that cost him effort. You're the third party this month to know what I'm carrying. Why should you be different?"

"Because I'm not asking for the chest." I stopped and turned to face her, and made myself say the sentence I had rehearsed for eleven days and still nearly fumbled. "I'm asking for passage. Upstream. On your train, up the dead line, as far as the rails go. That's all."

She stared at me. "You want me to take you up a closed line, through territory the Combine wants empty, toward a source they'd kill to control — and you're not asking for the documents."

"I'm asking to be there when they're read. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. Documents can be copied, stolen, sold, burned. If I hold paper, the Combine takes paper. If I stand on the ground where the paper points—" I spread my hands. "You can't steal a man's boots off his feet while he's standing in them. Well. You can. But it's harder."

"That's a stupid plan."

"It's a Vaskarian plan. We specialize in stupid plans that take forty years to fail."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile. It vanished before I could confirm it.

"Why passage?" she said. "Why not buy your way upriver like everyone else? Hire a boat, hire guides."

"Because the river dies in six weeks." I watched her absorb that — she'd already done the arithmetic, I could see it land behind her eyes, and I felt a flicker of professional respect. "Dry season. The upper Sereva goes to gravel. Any boat that gets you up will not get you back, and the headwaters are a week's walk beyond navigable water anyway. Your train follows the old service road the whole way. It's the only machine that makes the trip possible, and it belongs to you." I paused. "Also, boats can be boarded by people with contracts and guns. Trains can too, but less comfortably, and I'm told you're good at making people uncomfortable."

"Flattery."

"Inventory. I told you — I know what I'm looking at."

She walked to the canal edge and stood looking at the black water, and I recognized the posture, because I'd seen it in mirrors and in my father and in every photograph of my grandmother: a person deciding how much of themselves it was safe to spend.

"Let's say I believe you," she said to the water. "Let's say I take a stranger onto my engine, up a dead line, into a fight I haven't agreed to yet. What do you bring? Because I don't haul passengers. I haul weight that earns its space."

"I bring the language. The charts are annotated in Vaskarian — you've noticed the marginalia you can't read. Survey terms, corrections, warnings. Half of them matter. I can read all of them." I held up fingers. "I bring nine years of research into who else is hunting this chest and what they know, which is more than you think and less than they think. I bring a community — scattered, poor, and watching, which means eyes in every yard between here and the headwaters. And I bring—" I hesitated, then decided honesty was the only currency she might accept at face value. "I bring a reason not to quit. You strike me as a woman who has never once needed a reason not to quit, which means you've never had one worth keeping. I have forty years of mine. It weighs nothing and it doesn't eat."

She was quiet a long moment. When she turned around, her face had gone careful, the way ground goes careful before a slide.

"Here's my problem with you, Kovic," she said. "Not the story. The story hangs together. It's that you want something I can't check. Money I can count. Cargo I can weigh. But you want to *be there*, and there's no scale for that. People who want to be present want it for reasons, and reasons change, and I've been left holding other people's changed reasons before." Something crossed her face — grief, briefly, badly hidden. "So here's the offer. You ride in the caboose, not the cab. You touch nothing on the charts without asking. You tell me everything you know about the Combine's movements, weekly, whether you think it's relevant or not. And if at any point I decide you're a liability, you walk. Wherever we are. However far upstream. Clear?"

"Perfectly clear."

"One more thing." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, and they were not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "If this is a con — if you're working for them, or anyone — I will know eventually. I always know eventually. And I won't be dramatic about it. I'll just be gone, and you'll be standing wherever we were, alone, with whatever you actually wanted. Think about whether what you actually wanted was worth that."

"It is," I said. "It's the only thing that ever has been."

She studied me for a moment longer, weighing, and I understood that I had passed some examination whose questions I hadn't seen — not the facts, maybe, but the manner of them. Liars perform. I had only grieved.

"Be at the east gate of the yard tomorrow, six in the morning," she said. "Bring gloves. If you're late, I leave, and I don't leave forwarding addresses."

"I know," I said. "I've watched you leave for eleven days."

She paused, halfway turned away. "Eleven days," she repeated. "That's either devotion or surveillance, Kovic."

"In my family those were always the same word."

This time the smile got all the way to her mouth before she killed it, and she walked off down the towpath with her hands in her pockets, a woman carrying a dead nation's proof in a duffel bag, heading home to sleep in the cab of an engine because rooms imply futures.

I stood by the canal a while longer, letting my hands shake now that no one could see. Nine years. Two hundred and six sheets, forty-one recovered, one grandfather's brother dead twelve years and still, tonight, useful.

Then I went to send word to the community that the passage was real, and that I would need everything they had on the Meridian Combine by morning — because I had watched the Combine's man watch her too, and I knew exactly what kind of patience *they* had inherited.

Six weeks of river. One derelict train. And at dawn, a crew of two.

It was, I reflected, walking home through the dying lantern-light, the most Vaskarian enterprise imaginable: insufficient, improbable, and absolutely necessary.

I only hoped the woman driving it was as good as her couplers.
