## 7. Mentor's Handwriting

I spent three days not looking at the charts.

This is a skill of mine, and I want it on the record that I'm excellent at it. Not looking at things. I once spent an entire winter not thinking about a woman in Cheyenne by working so many hours on a grade crossing rebuild that my hands forgot how to do anything but hold a spike maul. The trick is to give the rest of yourself enough work that the part that wants to look gets outvoted.

The rest of myself had plenty to do. The *Kestrel* — my inspection car, half motorcar, half junk drawer with a horn — needed her axle boxes repacked, and the riverside yard where I'd moored her leaked rain through three separate seams I'd been meaning to fix since spring. So I fixed them. I repacked bearings. I re-caulked. I organized my tool chest twice, which for me is the equivalent of pacing a hole in the floor.

On the third evening, sitting in the lamplight with my hands finally out of excuses, I opened the chest.

Not because I'd stopped being afraid of what was in it. Because I'd figured out what I was actually afraid of, and it wasn't the seal, or the syndicate, or whatever the double-eagle meant. It was this: the chest had come to *me*. Out of every culvert on every flooded river in this territory, the water had carried it to the one person who made a living reading dead lines. That wasn't luck. That was a summons, and I have never once in my life answered a summons without checking the exits first.

So I checked the exits. Both doors of the boathouse I was renting as a workshop. The window over the lathe. The gap under the east wall where a cat came and went and, presumably, so could anything else with sense enough to be small. Then I put the lamp between me and the door, which wouldn't stop a bullet but would make whoever fired it squint, and I unrolled the first chart across my workbench like a surgeon laying out instruments.

Vaskarian survey sheets. That much I'd already confirmed at the archive — the paper stock, the grid notation, the elevation stamps in a script that hadn't been printed anywhere official in forty years. What I hadn't done was read them closely. Reading closely is a commitment. You can't skim a survey the way you skim a letter; the numbers get into your hands. A good chart tells you where the ground *wanted* the line to go, and every deviation from that desire is a decision somebody made, and every decision has a reason, and reasons are where people live.

I started at the downstream end and worked up, the way you'd walk a river.

The first sheets were ordinary — beautiful, but ordinary. Bridge sitings, cut-and-fill estimates, a tunnel bore through a limestone shoulder with the strata drawn in colored pencil so fine it looked embroidered. Whoever surveyed this line loved it. You can tell. There's a kind of note-taking that's just transcription, and there's another kind where the surveyor argues with the terrain in the margins, little pencil conversations: *ridge unstable here, consider realign*, or *spring flow seasonal? verify in August.* The second kind is a love letter written in arithmetic.

Then I got to sheet fourteen, and the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. Not the way your own hand degrades when you're tired or cold or writing on your knee in a moving caboose. This was a different hand entirely, cutting across the original surveyor's notes like a second voice interrupting a eulogy. Blunter. Faster. The letters leaned forward like they were trying to get somewhere ahead of the sentence.

I knew that handwriting.

You spend eleven years learning engineering from a man, you learn his letters before you learn his methods. Oren Vasquez wrote numbers with a flat-topped 4 and a 7 he crossed twice, always twice, like once wasn't proof enough. He wrote fast and pressed hard enough to emboss the page, because he'd learned drafting on linen in a shop where erasing cost money. He used to say, "Felix, ink is a confession. Make sure you mean it." He said it while teaching me to initial every revision, and I have initialed every revision since, on every map I've ever kept, including the closed-line atlas nobody will ever see, because some part of me still wants him to check my work.

Oren Vasquez had been dead for two years. Heart, they said, though Oren's heart was the toughest organ in any room he stood in, so I'd always privately doubted the diagnosis.

And here he was, pressing hard enough to emboss, arguing with a dead nation's surveyor about a river I was holding in my hands.

I did what any sensible person does when the past climbs out of a chest. I made tea. The kettle took four minutes to boil and I watched every one of them, and then I sat back down and read sheet fourteen properly, because running away from a piece of paper is the one direction even I can't manage.

The annotation read: *Source access restricted per Directive 9. Survey party to record approach corridor only. Do NOT chart the valley floor. Seal copies A through F. Originals to Central.*

Directive 9. I didn't know what Directive 9 was. But I knew the cadence of the sentence, the way it compressed a whole policy into four words and a threat. Oren had dictated orders like that over radio a hundred times when we worked flood season together — short, load-bearing sentences, engineered like trestles. *Do not cross until I confirm the far abutment.* *Do not open the gate before the pressure equalizes.* Sentences you could stand on, which is why it took me a full minute to understand that this sentence was built to keep people *out*, and that Oren had been the one who built it.

I turned to sheet fifteen. More of the same, worse. The original surveyor had mapped the upper river in loving detail — side channels, gravel bars, a fan of springs feeding the main stem from a hanging valley — and someone had gone through with a heavier pen and struck it all. Not crossed out. *Struck*, the way you'd strike a name from a crew roster after a fatality. And beside each strike, in Oren's hand: initials. F.V. No — wait. Let me look again. O.V. Oren Vasquez. Initialing his revisions, the way he taught me to.

He taught me to initial revisions so that responsibility would never blur. I had assumed that lesson was about pride in craft. Sitting there with tea going cold, I understood it might also have been about something else: a man making sure that when the erasure was eventually questioned, everyone would know exactly whose hand held the pen. Maybe that's conscience. Maybe it's just good bookkeeping. With Oren I honestly couldn't have told you the difference, and that uncertainty hurt worse than the strikes themselves.

* * *

Here's the thing you need to know about Oren Vasquez, and I need to say it plainly or I'll start dressing it up in nicer language than it deserves:

He found me when I was nineteen and sleeping in the cab of a derelict speeder outside Harlow Junction, and instead of running me off, he handed me a grease rag and said, "If you're going to squat on railroad property, you'll work for the privilege." Three months later he was teaching me to read a level. Six months later he'd talked the division engineer into letting me sit the assistant's exam. Within two years I was the youngest licensed roadmaster's assistant on the district, and within five I'd left anyway, because Oren — this is the part I never examined too closely, the part I'm examining now with my whole chest caved in — Oren was the one who taught me that staying is a trap.

He never said it outright. Oren didn't deal in outright. He dealt in stories, told over coffee at 3 a.m. in section houses, stories about men who settled down and got comfortable and then got laid off, or got sick, or got their pension gutted by men with better lawyers. "The company will forget your name before your badge cools," he told me once. "Make sure you can leave faster than you can be left." I took that as wisdom. I built a life on it. Eleven years of platforms and depots and towns where I learned the names of exactly enough people to order breakfast, and then I moved on, light and free and untouchable, and I called it freedom because Oren had called it freedom first.

And now his handwriting was telling me that the man who taught me to run had spent some portion of his career helping bury a valley full of — of what? The archive clerk had given me the bones: Vaskaria, dissolved forty years back, borders absorbed by neighbors who preferred the word "reunification" because "annexation" has a smell to it. The people scattered. The maps scrubbed. Atlases don't delete countries with a heavy pen strike the way Oren struck those springs, but functionally it's the same operation, and apparently Oren had run it on the ground.

Unless.

I want to be fair to him. Fairness is a discipline I practice mostly on other people, but I was raised — badly, lovingly — by this man, and some filial reflex in me kept turning the pages looking for an exit sign in his handwriting. Sheet sixteen gave me something. A margin note, smaller, pressed lighter, almost private: *Valley population relocated per agreement. Terms honored. Verify annually.* And beneath it, in the same hand but different ink, older: *They were starving when we found them. Remember that before you judge.*

Remember that before you judge. Addressed, apparently, to himself. Or to whoever came after him. To me, maybe, though he'd been dead two years when the river delivered his sins to my boathouse, and Oren didn't believe in prophecy, only in preparation.

"Terms honored," I said out loud, to the empty boathouse, and the cat in the wall gap said nothing, which was more honesty than I wanted.

Because here's what gnawed: *verify annually.* That's not the note of a man who seals a valley and walks away whistling. That's the note of a man who sealed a valley and then spent years going back to check on it. I knew Oren's travel habits better than anyone alive — or I'd thought I did. Every autumn, two weeks, unexplained, no forwarding contact. "Visiting my sister," he'd say, and I'd never met the sister, and I'd never asked, because asking questions is a form of staying and I was his best student.

Two weeks, every autumn, for eleven years. Upstream. Toward the headwaters of a river whose source his own hand had struck from the record.

I set the charts down and walked out onto the dock and stood in the dark listening to the river move, which is the sound the world makes when it doesn't care what you've just learned. Somewhere upstream, in the dark, was a valley. In the valley were people who had been "relocated per agreement," and either the agreement was a kindness wearing a uniform or a cruelty wearing a kindness, and Oren had known which, and Oren was dead, and the only testimony he'd left me was a stack of struck-through springs and a note telling me not to judge him before I'd starved in his shoes.

The worst part — and I'll admit it now so I don't have to discover it later in front of witnesses — the worst part wasn't even the sealing. Governments erase things; water runs downhill; I've never expected the world to be clean. The worst part was that he'd taught me to run *because of this*. I could feel it now, fitting together with the ugly click of a coupling slamming home. A man who helps bury a secret learns exactly how secrets get dug up. He learns who comes asking, and what happens to the people who answer. Of course he taught me to travel light. Of course he taught me that staying is a trap. He wasn't passing down philosophy. He was passing down a survival protocol from a man with bodies on his ledger, real or figurative, and he'd dressed it up as mentorship because the truth — "they may come for me someday, Felix, and I won't have you standing next to me when they do" — would have required a conversation, and Oren Vasquez would rather emboss a lie into linen than have a conversation.

Or so I decided, standing on the dock, at one in the morning, alone, which is the ideal condition for deciding terrible things about the dead.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around four I went back inside and did the thing I should have done first, the professional thing, the thing Oren would actually have approved of: I stopped reading the annotations and started reading the *engineering*.

Strip away the politics and the grief and look at the line itself. That's the oath, more or less. The steel doesn't care who signed what.

And the steel told me something the handwriting hadn't. The upper-river sheets weren't just surveys. They were construction documents — grades, ballast specs, bridge loads, a spiral tunnel with ventilation calcs. Somebody had *built* this line, recently enough that the specs used modern rail sections. Which meant the "sealed" valley had a working railway into it, which meant the sealing wasn't abandonment. It was maintenance. You don't spec a tunnel ventilation system for a place you've erased. You spec it for a place you're keeping.

Keeping for whom?

I spread the last three sheets across the bench and there it was, tucked behind the title block of the final sheet, folded small, a separate paper entirely — not Vaskarian stock, not Oren's drafting linen. Cheap paper, typewritten, a carbon copy, the kind of thing that comes out of a railway accounting office. A waybill summary. Freight tonnages, quarterly, twenty-two years' worth, routed to a terminus code I didn't recognize: **VS-0**. Zero. A station number that isn't a station. Grain, seed stock, medical supplies, school books — I could read the commodity codes, I've shipped all of them — moving up a dead line to a station that didn't exist, quarter after quarter, year after year, long after Vaskaria stopped appearing in atlases.

Somebody had been feeding that valley for two decades. Quietly. Through the books. And the last entry in the waybill series was dated nine years ago, and the final line was a single shipment, flagged PERSONAL, sender listed only as **O.V.**, contents: *books (assorted), seeds (garden var.), one (1) violin.*

One violin.

I sat down on my stool, the good stool, the one with the busted caster I keep meaning to fix because fixing it would mean admitting I intend to stay in any town long enough for a stool to matter, and I laughed. It came out wrong, more like the sound a coupler makes.

He'd verified annually. He'd shipped books and garden seeds and a violin. He'd erased a valley off the face of the earth and then spent the rest of his life secretly keeping it alive, and he'd hidden both halves of the work so well that the only evidence of either was sitting in a flooded chest in a rented boathouse in front of a woman he'd trained never to stay anywhere long enough to find it.

Betrayal, I'd have called it yesterday. Now I wasn't sure what to call it. A lie can be a shelter — I'd heard Oren say that too, once, drunk, staring at a flood gauge, and I'd thought he was talking about weather forecasts.

Outside, the sky was doing the gray thing it does before dawn, and the river kept saying its one word over and over. And that was when I heard the footstep on the dock boards — one step, deliberate, placed like a man who wanted to be heard — and a voice I didn't know said, quietly, through the door:

"You've found the waybills. I can hear you laughing."

I put my hand on the wrench I keep by the door for exactly this genre of morning, and I said, "Whoever you are, you picked a bad week to know what's in my boathouse."

"I picked the only week," the voice said. "May I come in, Miss Reyes? I've been watching your trains for a very long time, and I'd rather talk than follow you again."
