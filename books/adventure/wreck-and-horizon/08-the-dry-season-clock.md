## 8. The Dry Season Clock

**FELIX**

There are two kinds of numbers in this world, my mentor used to say. The kind you write down, and the kind that write you.

I'd spent three days with the second kind.

The gauge board at Marrow Bend told the story if you knew how to read it, and I did — had been reading rivers since before I could legally drive a locomotive. The Vell was dropping four centimeters a day now, and the drop was accelerating as the high-country snowpack gave out early. Upstream of the Kettle Narrows, the channel braided out over gravel bars like spilled thread. By my figures — and my figures were good, they were the one thing about me that had never let anybody down — the upper river would be impassable for anything heavier than a canoe in six weeks. Five, if the heat held. Four, if it didn't rain, and the sky above the canyon had been the color of old tin for nine days running.

Six weeks to pilot a derelict work train up a dead line through flooded cuttings and broken trestles, past whatever the Meridian Combine decided to throw at us, to a source that might not even exist except on charts annotated by a dead man who'd lied to me his whole life.

I sat on the caboose steps at dusk with the survey charts spread across my knees and did the arithmetic again, because arithmetic is a prayer you can check your work on.

"Still six weeks?" Ilsa asked from the galley doorway, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last year.

"Five and a half, if we're lucky."

"We're never lucky."

"No," I said. "But we're still here, which statistically suggests otherwise."

She snorted and went back inside. That was the thing about Ilsa Voss — disgraced dispatcher, exiled from the Corridor Authority for an error that killed nobody but cost the railroad a fortune and cost her everything else — she didn't waste words on comfort. She just kept showing up. I hadn't asked her to stay after the night she'd found me cross-checking water tables by lamplight. She'd pulled up a crate, said "move your elbow," and started recalculating my flow rates without permission. They were better than mine. I'd have resented it more if I weren't so grateful.

The twins were in the engine shed arguing about the boiler, which was how the twins said good morning, good afternoon, and I love you. Pip was supposed to be asleep in the baggage car and was almost certainly not. And somewhere out there, Dain Kovic was watching our fires the way he watched everything — from outside the light, patient as erosion.

I looked down at Oren Vasquez's handwriting looping across the margin of the oldest chart. *Keep the water honest,* he'd written, in the same hand that had taught me to draft a gradient profile. *Honest water finds its level. So should you.*

He'd sealed the source. He'd helped bury a nation. And he'd taught me to run before teaching me anything else, because running was how you kept from being buried yourself.

I folded the charts away and went to check the culverts, because when the numbers start writing you, the only answer is work.

**ILSA**

I dispatched trains for nineteen years before the Authority took my license, and the first thing they teach you — before block signals, before timetable arithmetic, before anything — is that every schedule is a countdown wearing a polite mask. Trains don't run on time. They run out of it.

Felix Reyes ran out of time the way other people run out of money: quietly, methodically, with a ledger nobody else could read until the bill came due.

I saw it happen over the following week. The Combine's lawyers came to the yard office twice, then three times, then sent a man named Corliss who wore a suit worth more than my severance and smiled like a man quoting scripture at a funeral. Felix met him standing up, arms crossed, in the doorway of her own office because she wouldn't give him a chair.

"The corridor's for sale, Ms. Reyes," Corliss said, loud enough for the whole yard to hear. "Everything north of the Marrow Bridge. The Authority's already signed. You're working a ghost line on borrowed steel."

"Then I'm borrowing well," Felix said.

"You have a train that barely runs, a crew of—" he glanced at the twins, at me, at Pip loitering by the water tower with the studied innocence of teenagers everywhere, "—irregulars. And a piece of paper you found in a flood. Sell us the chest. Name a figure. Walk away rich. Everyone does, eventually. It's just a question of price."

I'll say this for Felix Reyes: she doesn't flinch, and she doesn't posture. She just looks at people like she's checking their gauge.

"My price," she said, "is that you leave."

Corliss smiled wider. "Then we'll buy around you."

And that's exactly what they did. Within four days the Combine had purchased the corridor easement, the bridge demolition contract, and — this was the elegant part, the part I had to admire even as it closed around our throats like a coupling — the salvage rights to every rail, tie, and spike north of Marrow. Which meant, legally speaking, that our own track now belonged to them, and any train operating on it was operating on stolen property.

"They can't stop us from *rolling*," Felix said that night in the caboose, pacing the narrow aisle while the rest of us pretended not to watch. "They can report us. They can have the line torn up ahead of us. They can put agents on the river. But they can't stop us from rolling."

"No," I said. "But they can make sure that when we roll, we're criminals. Every mile. It changes what happens when we're caught."

Felix stopped pacing. For a moment she looked very young and very tired, this woman who mapped closed lines because closing things couldn't hurt her the way opening them could.

"I know," she said. "I've done the numbers. Six weeks of water. A corridor full of people paid to erase us. And a crew that—" she gestured at all of us, at the stove and the bunks and the kettle going cold, "—that keeps *staying*."

"Terrible, isn't it," I said. "Staying."

She gave me a look that on anyone else would have been a laugh.

I stayed too, obviously. Nineteen years I'd believed the rails were the only honest thing left, and then the rails got bought out from under a woman who loved them more honestly than the men who owned them. If honesty had changed addresses, I'd follow it.

**PIP**

Okay so here's what nobody tells you about hiding on a train full of adults: they always know. Like, from day one. Felix knew before I even boarded, I'm pretty sure — she left the baggage car door unlatched and a blanket folded on the crates where I sleep, which is either kindness or a trap and honestly with her it's both.

Anyway. The Combine guys came back Tuesday. Not the suit guy — actual hard-looking ones, in a gray truck, and they walked the whole yard taking photographs of the train like it was a crime scene or a menu. I was up on the coal tender pretending to check the coal, which is a job I invented and no one has questioned yet, so I heard everything.

"This is the one?" the big one said.

"That's her train," the other one said. "Reyes's. Word is she won't sell."

"Nobody holds out forever. Corliss says soften the asset."

"Soften it how?"

"How do you think? Rails, water, crew. Make the road expensive. She's got no permits, no backing, no friends. Six weeks of river left, tops. We just have to be more patient than the weather."

I wrote it all down later, in the notebook I keep under my bunk next to the chalk I took from the station — the station with the drawings on the wall, the little houses and the double-eagle bird and the kid's name, MIRA, scratched into the plaster like she was afraid the world would forget her. I wasn't going to let it forget her either. That's why I keep the chalk.

I brought the notebook to Felix that night. My hands were shaking a little and I hated that, so I shoved the notebook at her fast, like it was nothing.

"Heard the Combine guys. They're gonna sabotage stuff. 'Soften the asset.' That's us. We're the asset."

Felix read the page twice. Then she looked at me for a long time, and I braced for the speech, the *you're fourteen, this is dangerous, go home* speech.

Instead she said: "Good ears. What direction was the wind?"

"Uh. West. Off the river."

"Then they probably didn't hear you." She tapped the notebook against her palm. "Pip. From now on, you don't listen alone. You hear something, you come get me or Ilsa *first*, then we decide together. Deal?"

Together. Nobody had ever put the word together in a sentence with my name in it before, not in a way that stuck.

"Deal," I said, and my voice cracked, and I decided we'd both pretend it didn't.

**DAIN**

I have spent forty years learning the shapes of things that are gone. The shape of Vaskaria in the negative space between borders on a map. The shape of my grandmother's kitchen in the smell of paprika in a foreign city. The shape of my father's voice in my own, saying words in a language the schoolmasters beat out of the children of everyone I grew up with.

So I know what it means when a woman stands in a rail yard at midnight, alone, holding a chart up to the lantern light, looking not at the lines drawn but at the country implied between them. Felix Reyes has been reading the holes in the map for weeks now. She reads them the way I do — like scripture, like accusation.

I should have spoken to her. I had rehearsed it a hundred times, in the old language and the new: *I am Dain Kovic, son of the valley, and the seal on your chest is the seal of my grandmothers.* Instead I stood behind the fence with the rusted wire and said nothing, because forty years of exile teaches you that hope is a currency you spend carefully, and I am not yet certain she will not spend it for me and hand me back the change.

But I watched the Combine's men mark her rails. I followed their gray truck up the valley road and noted where they stopped, what they measured, which culverts they photographed twice. Soften the asset, they called it. I know this phrase. In the old country, they softened assets too — villages, mostly. Records. Names.

That night I made a decision that felt like stepping off a platform into moving air.

I crossed the yard, past the sleeping engine, and set on the caboose step where she would find it at dawn: a strip of paper bearing the old script, the letters my mother taught me, spelling out the one sentence every Vaskarian child learns before any other.

*The river remembers what the maps forgot.*

And beneath it, in their alphabet, so she would understand:

*I know what you carry. I know who helped bury it. Ask me about Oren Vasquez.*

Then I went back beyond the fence, into the dark where I have lived my whole life, and waited for morning like a man waiting for a verdict.

**FELIX**

The note was waiting on the caboose step at dawn, weighted with a stone, and I read it three times while the river talked to itself below the bank.

Oren Vasquez. His name, offered up by a stranger who knew — who had known before I did, maybe, or at least before I admitted it — that the man who taught me everything had been one of the gravediggers.

I should have burned it. Caution said burn it; caution had kept me alive and unattached for thirty years. But caution also said take the Combine's money, and look how far that advice would have gotten me.

Ilsa read it over my shoulder. Pip read it upside down. The twins argued briefly about whether the handwriting was a threat or a prayer and settled, as they settled most things, on both.

"Trap?" Ilsa said.

"Maybe."

"Bait?"

"Definitely bait. Question is whether the hook has anything true on it."

"And if it does?"

I looked upstream, toward the narrows, toward the headwaters I couldn't see and the six weeks draining away like water through gravel. Somewhere up there was a valley Oren had sealed to protect people hiding in it — people whose grandchildren might be watching this same river right now, keeping a secret the size of a nation. Somewhere behind me was a syndicate buying my rails bolt by bolt. And somewhere out past the fence line, I'd have bet the chest itself, someone was watching to see what I'd do with a scrap of paper and a name.

"Fighting means staying," I said, mostly to myself. "Staying means roots. Roots mean—"

"Meaning," Ilsa finished. "Yeah. Terrible."

I laughed — actually laughed, short and rusty, like an engine turning over after years in the shed. Then I took out my pencil and wrote on the back of the note, in my best surveyor's hand, the only answer I had:

*Marlow Bend trestle. Tomorrow, noon. Come tell me about my mentor. Bring proof you bleed like the rest of us.*

I weighed it with the same stone and set it where the dawn had found the first one.

Because here's the thing about the trap closing: a trap only works on something trying to get away. And for the first time since I was nineteen years old and Oren Vasquez taught me that leaving was a skill, I noticed I'd stopped planning my exit.

The river dropped another four centimeters that day. I marked it on the chart, and under the number, for the first time, I drew a line pointing forward instead of back.
