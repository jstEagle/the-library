## 9. Crew of Strays

You can't run a train alone. That's the first lie they tell you in engineering school, right after *the schedule is your friend* and *nobody ever got hurt by a signal you double-checked*. A locomotive is a stubborn animal with a hundred opinions, and it takes at least three people to argue with it properly: one to drive, one to fire, one to stand behind the driver muttering that she's doing it wrong.

I'd planned on being all three. I'd done it before — solo runs down dead branches with a hand-fired boiler and a bad attitude — and I figured the run up the Vaskar headwaters would be the same, just longer and wetter and with more people shooting at me.

Then I took an honest inventory of the work train sitting in the old Meridian siding, and I revised my ambitions.

She was a maintenance consist from the era when railroads still believed in themselves: a locomotive built the year my mother was born, a water tender with a riveted belly, two flatcars stacked with rusted rail, a crane car whose boom hadn't swung since before the dissolution, and a caboose that some previous crew had painted a defiant green before somebody else had painted over it in institutional gray. Her boiler passed pressure test at sixty percent of rated working steam, which is engineer for *she'll do it if you ask nicely and never above a whisper*. Her air brakes held — barely. Her sanders were clogged with forty years of wasp nests.

I named her *Patience*, because I needed a lot of it and because naming things is how I pretend they're permanent.

The problem wasn't the engine. The problem was arithmetic. Firing a cold boiler uphill for six days through flooded cuttings, running a hand pump on the water tender, spiking and patching track as we went, keeping watch against a syndicate that had already threatened me once — that math didn't close with one pair of hands. It closed with five. Maybe six.

And here's the thing about hiring: I've spent my whole life avoiding it. Crews are obligations. Crews are people who notice when you don't come back. Every job I ever took, I took knowing exactly which day I'd walk away from it, and I never once let that day surprise me.

But the Combine had made it simple for me. They'd put a man in my yard office who smiled like a bank statement and said the word *retirement* out loud, and something in my chest had closed around the chest of charts like a fist. If I was going to fight them, I couldn't fight them the way I'd fought everything else — alone, fast, gone before anyone learned my name.

So I did the hardest thing I've done in this whole affair, harder than wading into floodwater, harder than telling the Combine no.

I went looking for people.

* * *

Ilsa Brandt found me before I found her, which should have been my first warning.

I'd posted a notice on the yard board — *crew wanted, one-way run, pay in salvage rights, discretion essential* — the kind of notice that attracts exactly two kinds of people: desperates and liars. She walked into the yard office at seven in the morning carrying her dispatch certificate in a folder soft as cloth from handling, set it on my desk, and said:

"You posted this wrong. Your window block says *run eight*, but the dead line's single-track past Kilometer 40 with no passing sidings until the quarry spur. You can't run eight on a single track unless you want to meet yourself coming back."

I looked at her. Fifty-ish, gray shot through dark hair cut short as a hedge, hands of a woman who'd spent thirty years moving other people's trains with a pencil and a telephone. Eyes that had seen something once and never quite blinked it away.

"You're a dispatcher," I said.

"I was." She sat down without being asked, which I respected and resented in equal measure. "Twelve years, mainline desk, Harrow Junction. Then there was a collision."

"There's always a collision," I said. "What happened?"

"A freight ran through a stop board and hit a work gang. Eleven men." She said it flat, the way you say numbers you've said ten thousand times. "The inquiry cleared the freight crew. Cleared the track circuit. Cleared the weather. Somebody had to be at fault, and the dispatcher's desk is where fault goes to live. They took my certificate. I've been signing on to section gangs under my sister's name for six years."

"And was it your fault?"

She looked at me then, straight on, and I understood why eleven men's families still wrote her letters, half of them angry and half of them — worse — grateful.

"No," she said. "But I signed the order that put them on that stretch, so it's mine anyway. That's the difference between fault and weight. Fault gets assigned. Weight you carry."

I should have sent her away. A woman carrying that much weight is a liability on a run where the whole point is to travel light. But she'd read my notice and corrected it in under a minute, and when I slid the dead line's survey across the desk she traced the route with one finger and said, "Water's your problem, not grade. The culverts at K-62 and K-90 are washed through; you'll need to slow for both or you'll lose the low cars. And whoever's hunting you will come at the trestle at K-75, because it's the only place you can't dodge."

"Come aboard," I heard myself say. "Pay's salvage rights and a share of whatever the truth turns out to be worth."

"I don't want money," Ilsa Brandt said, taking her ruined certificate back off my desk. "I want a desk again. Even a moving one."

* * *

The twins came next, and they came as a unit, because everything about them came as a unit.

Sorrel and Soren Vance — boilermakers, twenty-six years old, identical down to the burn scars on their forearms — were working the repair shed at the coalyard when I went to look at their work. Their reputation preceded them by about a decade: apprenticed at fourteen, certified at eighteen, fired at nineteen from the Meridian shops for what the record called *insubordination* and what Sorrel called, when I asked, "telling a works manager his weld was going to fail, and then being right about it in front of clients."

"It failed?" I said.

"Spectacularly," said Soren.

"Killed anyone?"

"No," said Sorrel, offended. "We'd have stopped the shift first. We're not monsters. We just don't lie about metal."

They interviewed each other while I watched. That's genuinely what it was — I'd ask one a question and the other would answer, seamlessly, finishing sentences, correcting each other's grammar mid-word, arguing about whether a boiler's throat sheet could be patched cold (Soren said yes, Sorrel said only if you hate the boiler, and they glared at each other like this was a lifelong dispute). When I finally got a question in edgewise — *can you keep sixty percent steam climbing six days of dead-line grade?* — they answered together, in unison, which was somehow more unsettling than either voice alone:

"If you let us retube the front flue sheet and re-pack every gland on the engine, yes. Otherwise you'll burst a tube on day two and walk home."

"How long for the work?"

"Four days," said Soren.

"Three," said Sorrel.

"Three," they agreed, and shook my hand simultaneously, one left hand and one right, and I swear the grip was identical.

Here's what I wrote in my logbook that night, because I write things down when I don't trust my own judgment: *Hired two people who are functionally one person. Risk: they'll finish each other's sentences during a crisis. Benefit: they'll also finish each other's work during one.* I underlined the benefit twice. I was already learning to need them.

* * *

Pip I did not hire. Pip hired himself, which is apparently a thing teenagers can do now.

I found him on the third morning, four days before departure, curled in the caboose cupola under a tarp he'd clearly stolen from my own stores, eating a tin of beans he'd definitely stolen from my own stores. He was maybe fifteen, all elbows and collarbones, hair the color of wet rope, with the specific watchfulness of a kid who has learned that adults are weather — mostly harmless, occasionally catastrophic, never reliable.

"Don't get up," I said.

"I wasn't sleeping," he said, not getting up.

"You were snoring."

"That was the caboose settling."

I climbed up and sat on the opposite bench. He tracked me the whole way, the way a cat tracks a dog too big to fight. "This is private property," he added.

"So are those beans."

"They were abandoned. Abandoned property reverts to the finder. That's maritime law."

"This is a train."

"Trains are maritime law adjacent."

Against every instinct I have cultivated over thirty-one years of successful solitude, I laughed. He pretended not to notice, but his shoulders came down half an inch, which from a kid like him is a standing ovation.

"Where are your people?" I asked.

He shrugged with the entire geography of nowhere in it. "Around."

"Pip—"

"It's not Pip. Pip's just—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Started over, quieter. "It's what I'm called lately. Names are easier when they're temporary."

I know something about temporary names. I've worn three of them myself, and I've kept every one of them loose enough to slip out of. So I didn't push. Instead I said: "There's beans in the galley locker. Real ones, not abandoned ones. And there's work, if you want it. Greasing, watching water levels, learning which end of a spike maul to hold. Pay's meals and a bunk and nobody asking where you came from."

"Nobody asks?"

"Nobody asks."

He studied me for a long moment, doing whatever arithmetic teenagers do instead of trust. "What happens when the run's over?"

That's the question, isn't it. That's the question I've been dodging my whole life, dressed up in different clothes. What happens when the run's over. I looked at this kid who'd stowed away on a train bound for a valley full of erased people, and I thought about all the times I'd answered that question by being already gone when it was asked.

"We'll figure it out," I said. "Together, probably. If you're still here."

He nodded slowly, like a man accepting terms he intended to renegotiate later. Then he held out the bean tin. "Want some? They're yours anyway."

I took one. They were cold and half-salt and the best thing I'd eaten all week.

* * *

Departure minus two days, the crew assembled itself around me whether I consented or not.

Ilsa had claimed the caboose desk and covered it with timetables, bridge ratings, and a hand-drawn water profile of the upper river annotated in a dispatcher's shorthand I could actually read, which felt like hearing my own language spoken in a foreign country. She'd also, without asking, established a rule that all radio traffic would be logged and coded, and had taught Pip the code in an afternoon, because apparently the kid absorbed ciphers the way other children absorbed measles.

The twins had the engine apart down to her throat sheet. I'd climb into the cab at dawn and find four arms buried in the smokebox to the elbow, arguing in stereo about tube expansion while sparks drifted off their rods like orange snow. By the second day they'd re-packed every gland, freed the sanders, and gotten *Patience* to hold seventy percent steam — Soren's number, disputed by Sorrel, conceded by both — and when they stood back to admire her, they did it in unison, arms crossed identically, and I caught myself smiling at the sight like a fool at a wedding.

And Pip — Pip had appointed himself lookout, quartermaster, and morale officer, roles nobody had voted on and everybody had accepted. He knew where everything was. He knew what everyone ate. He'd learned the brake wheel and the injector and, when he thought I wasn't listening, was teaching himself the throttle logic off a manual he'd found, mouthing the words.

On the last evening, I found them all in the caboose: Ilsa at her desk tracing tomorrow's run, the twins playing cards with such vicious symmetry that neither could win, Pip on the cupola bench with his legs through the railing, watching the yard lights. Nobody had invited anybody. We had simply accumulated, the way driftwood accumulates against a piling.

Ilsa looked up. "Run-through briefing at 0500. Water's high from the storm, which helps us and hurts us. First sabotage attempt will come inside forty-eight hours; the Combine won't let us clear the yard without testing us."

"You say that like it's certain," Soren said.

"It's certain," Ilsa and I said together, and the twins exchanged a look that meant *those two are going to be trouble.*

Pip swung down from the cupola. "So we're really doing this. All of us. Up the dead line."

"We're really doing this," I said.

"To find a country that isn't on the map."

"To find out why it isn't," Dain's voice said from the doorway — and I turned, and there he was, pack on his shoulder, knife at his belt, standing in the caboose door like a man arriving at a house he'd been circling for weeks. He looked at the five of us — the disgraced dispatcher, the twins, the stowaway, the woman who never stays — and something crossed his face that I'd learn later to call hope, though at the time I mistook it for indigestion.

"The Combine bought the corridor papers this morning," he said quietly. "All of them. As of tonight, officially, this railroad belongs to them."

Ilsa's pencil stopped. The twins' cards froze mid-deal.

"Then we leave at 0500," I said, "before their paperwork learns how to drive a train."

Pip grinned. Ilsa almost smiled. The twins began, in unison, to list everything that could go wrong.

And I stood there in the lamplight, surrounded by strays who had decided — without consulting me, without my permission, without any regard whatsoever for my carefully maintained solitude — to become my crew, and I felt the thing I'd spent my whole life outrunning settle onto my shoulders like a coat that fit.

It weighed more than I expected.

It was warm.
