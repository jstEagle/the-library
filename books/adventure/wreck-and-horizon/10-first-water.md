## 10. First Water

I've been a dispatcher for twenty-two years, and in that time I have learned exactly one universal truth: every disaster announces itself politely, in advance, to anyone willing to listen. A hot box will tick. A cracked rail will sing. A flood will send its warning downstream a full day ahead of itself, riding the current like a courier with bad news and good manners.

The Combine's sabotage did none of these things. It just sat there at Kilometer 41, quiet as a lie, waiting for us.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and dispatchers never get ahead of themselves — it's the first thing they beat out of you at the academy, right after cursive and despair.

* * *

We left the yard at dawn, which Felix insisted on because "the water's lowest before the sun hits the cuttings," and which Pip celebrated by being discovered — again — in the coal bunker, where he had apparently been sleeping since Tuesday. Nobody had the heart to put him off. He'd greased our journals without being asked, and a boy who greases your journals before you catch him is a boy you keep.

Felix ran the engine herself. Of course she did. She'd spent two days coaxing life back into a locomotive that had been rusting on a dead siding since before Pip was born, and you could see in her hands what the machine meant to her — the way a musician holds an instrument they've repaired themselves, tender and proprietary and slightly afraid of it.

"Cuttings are flooded through the first twelve kilometers," she announced over the cab's battered intercom, her voice crackling into the caboose where I rode with the twins and the charts. "Water's knee-deep on the ballast in places. We'll take it slow."

"Slow is fine," Brann rumbled from the fireman's side. The twins — Brann and Torren, identical down to their burn scars and opposite in every other particular — had drawn straws for who'd ride up front, and Brann had lost, or won; with the twins it was never clear.

"It is not fine," Torren said over the intercom from the caboose. "Slow means we're in the open longer. Slow means more time for anyone watching."

"Torren," I said, "if you say one more pessimistic thing before breakfast, I'm making you eat yours standing in the flooded cutting."

"I stand corrected," he said, and I heard Pip snicker somewhere under the coal dust.

That was the thing about this crew, in those first hours. We were still performing at being a crew. Felix gave orders like a woman testing whether anyone would follow them. The twins bickered like brothers who'd never learned any other language. Pip appeared and vanished like weather. And I sat in the caboose with my dispatch board and my timetables and my twenty-two years of professional habit, quietly convinced that all of this would come apart at the first real test.

I want it noted, for the record, that I hate being right.

* * *

The dead line earned its name inside the first hour.

This was a work railway once — built to haul timber and stone down from the high country when the valley above still had a name on maps. Then Vaskaria dissolved, the traffic died, and the line was left to rot with its stations shuttered and its signals frozen at stop. Forty years of rain and neglect had turned the cuttings into canals. Water poured down the rock faces in gray braids, pooled across the track bed, and swallowed the drainage culverts whole. In places the ballast had washed clean away, leaving the rails floating on mud like the ribs of something long drowned.

Felix took it at walking speed, then slower. Twice we stopped while she and Brann waded out to check the roadbed, and twice she came back soaked to the waist and grimly satisfied. "Ballast's scoured but the subgrade held," she reported the second time, wringing out her sleeves. "Whoever built this line built it angry. It wants to live."

"Everything worth keeping does," Dain said.

He was riding in the caboose with me, mostly silent, mostly watching the country slide past with an expression I couldn't read — hunger and grief wearing each other's clothes. This was his country once, or close enough to it. Every kilometer marker we passed, he touched the window frame lightly, like greeting someone.

"You knew this line?" I asked him.

"My father worked the upper section. Stone quarry traffic." He didn't look at me. "He used to say the river here was the only honest clock in Vaskaria. It never lied about the season."

"And now?"

"Now it's a weapon with a schedule." He glanced at my dispatch board. "You'll understand by afternoon."

I understood by eleven.

* * *

Kilometer 41 sits at the mouth of a gorge where the river pinches between rock walls, and the old builders spanned it with a trestle — timber legs, steel deck, ninety meters of nothing with a view straight down into white water. On a healthy line it would be routine. On our line, forty years unmaintained, it was a question mark with a train on top of it.

Felix stopped us a hundred meters short. Standard practice — walk anything questionable first. She took Dain and Brann forward on foot while Torren, Pip, and I held the train, and I remember thinking how strange it felt to sit still and wait on other people's judgment instead of issuing it myself. Twenty-two years of being the voice in everyone's ear, and here I was, voiceless, watching three small figures shrink against the green-gray bulk of the trestle.

They were gone maybe ten minutes. When they came back, Felix was moving differently. Not running. Worse than running — that flat, deliberate quickness of someone who has seen exactly how much trouble they're in and is calculating.

"Deck's intact," she said, swinging up onto the caboose steps, dripping. "Timbers are rotten but sound enough at walking pace, one car at a time. But look at this." She slapped a photograph against my dispatch board — she'd taken it with the little camera she kept in her coat, developed it in the caboose darkroom she'd rigged from a biscuit tin, because of course Felix Reyes traveled with a darkroom. "West approach. See the cribbing?"

I saw it. The stone cribbing that anchored the trestle's western abutment had been undermined — not by water. The excavation was too clean, too deliberate, a trench dug beneath the footing and packed back with loose rubble. To the eye, passing at speed, it would read as ordinary washout.

To the eye, yes. To the wheels, it was a trap. The abutment would settle the moment weight came onto it — maybe settle, maybe drop half a meter, maybe worse.

"That's not the river," I said.

"No," Felix said. "The river doesn't dig."

* * *

Here is what I know about the Meridian Combine, gathered over years of routing their freight and hating their invoices: they do not destroy things loudly. They don't blow bridges or shoot engineers out of saddles. They adjust conditions. They make the world slightly wrong in one specific place and let physics do the murder, so that afterward every report reads *structural failure, act of God, no foul play suspected.* I once watched them acquire a widow's orchard by buying up every access road around it until her trees rotted unpicked. Nobody threatened her. Nobody had to.

Somebody had come up this dead line ahead of us — days ahead, maybe weeks — and adjusted our conditions.

"They knew we'd come this way," Dain said quietly. "They let us buy the corridor fight knowing they'd already prepared the ground."

"Then why not just wreck the bridge completely?" Pip asked. He'd gone very still, perched on the coal scuttle, all the swagger gone out of him. "Why leave it standing?"

"Because a wrecked bridge tells us to turn back," Felix said. "A *subtle* wreck tells us the line is passable — right up until it isn't." She looked at the trestle, and I watched her do the arithmetic every engineer does: mass, momentum, margin. "One car at a time. Light engine first. If the abutment settles, we'll know by the sound."

"And if it drops?" Torren asked.

"Then we lose the engine, and we walk out, and the Combine wins by default." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "So we don't let it drop."

* * *

What followed was four hours of the hardest, finest railroading I have ever witnessed, and I include in that the flood year of '09, when I talked a stranded excursion train through six kilometers of rising water by lantern light.

Felix lightened everything. We unloaded the tender to a third capacity. The twins stripped every nonessential fitting off the engine — sand dome covers, tool boxes, even the cab's brass fittings, which Brann mourned aloud — and stacked them by the track. Then Felix took the locomotive forward alone, at less than walking pace, standing on the footplate with her head out the window listening to the roadbed the way a doctor listens to a chest.

I stood at the west approach with a flag, because somebody had to be the one who could signal stop, and because Ilsa Brandt does not ask others to stand where she won't. The abutment was at my feet. I could see the rubble-packed trench through the gaps in the cribbing, and I confess that standing over it while eighty tons of locomotive crept toward me was the longest quarter hour of my professional life.

She crossed. The abutment groaned — one long, low note, like a whale surfacing — and settled perhaps the width of my thumb. And held.

"Deck next," Felix called back, voice carrying flat across the water. "Brann, Torren — hand-spikes on the abutment as she comes. Pack that trench as she crosses. If it starts to go, spike it solid."

What the twins did then was not engineering so much as violence. They wedged iron bars under the settling cribbing and drove stone and tie-plugs into the undermined trench in real time, hammering like men building a foundation during an earthquake, while the empty flatcar rolled overhead at a crawl. Dust came off the trestle in sheets. The structure screamed in three different registers. Pip ferried tie-plugs on a handcart, pale as milk, doing the job of a man twice his size because there was nobody else.

And Dain — Dain stood at the far end of the deck, on the east side, in the open, visible to anyone watching from the heights, deliberately making himself a target so that any Combine spotter would shoot at a man instead of a bridge. Nobody shot. But he stood there the whole crossing anyway, arms spread slightly from his sides, facing the gorge, and I understood that this was not bravado. This was a man saying *this place is mine, and you will not have it.*

By late afternoon, engine, tender, flatcar, and caboose stood on the east bank, and the trestle behind us was a ruin we'd made honest — Felix fired a charge of leftover blasting powder from the tool locker and dropped the west span into the river, so no one following could use it, and so no later traveler would trust it by accident.

We camped that night on the east bank, engines cold, and nobody suggested pushing on in the dark. Some days you spend everything you have and stop.

* * *

It happened at supper, around the twin stoves, and I mention it because it matters more than the trestle.

Pip burned the rice. Badly. A whole pot, blackened to the bottom, because he'd been showing Torren how to rig a signal lamp and forgot he was cooking. He stood there holding the ruined pot with smoke still rising off it, braced for the kind of words boys like him have heard all their lives — useless, clumsy, get out.

Felix looked at the pot. Looked at him. And said, "Rice is fuel too. We'll just chew harder." Then she handed him a fresh ration bag and went back to her charts.

That was all. That was the entire event. But I saw the boy's shoulders come down from around his ears, and I saw Torren quietly scrape the burnt pot clean without being asked, and I thought: *there it is.* Not speeches. Not oaths. Just a woman who refuses to make people flinch, and a crew that has started arranging itself around that refusal like iron filings around a magnet.

Later, after dark, Felix came and sat beside me at the caboose door with a mug of chicory she didn't drink.

"Ilsa," she said. "When the abutment moved. When I heard it groan with the engine on it." She turned the mug in her hands. "My whole life, when things went wrong, I checked the exits. First instinct, always. Tonight I heard that sound and my first thought was — who's on the spikes? Are the twins clear? Is Pip clear? I didn't think about leaving once." She laughed, short and humorless. "I don't know when that happened."

"About the time you hired people nobody else would," I said. "We tend to attach."

She nodded slowly, looking out at the black gorge where our demolished trestle lay in pieces in the starlight. "The Combine thinks they're hunting a lone engineer with a chest. They haven't figured out yet that they're hunting a crew."

"Give them time," I said. "They adjust conditions. So do we."

She almost smiled. Then her eyes went past me, upriver, to where the night hid the next sixty kilometers of dead line, the closed stations, the shallowing water, and whatever else the Combine had already adjusted in our path.

"Get some sleep, Ilsa," she said. "Tomorrow we run the closed stations. And tomorrow night—" she tapped the dispatch board, on the route chart, at a small marked point upstream, "—we sleep indoors for the first time since the yard. Station Thirteen-Vesper. If the roof holds."

It did not hold. But that is tomorrow's entry, and a dispatcher learns early never to file a report before the facts arrive.
