## 11. Closed Stations

The station had a name once. You could tell because there was a bracket on the platform wall where a sign used to hang, and beneath it a rectangle of paint slightly less faded than the rest, like the wall was still holding the shape of the word out of stubbornness. I put my hand in that rectangle and felt like I was shaking hands with a ghost who'd already left.

"Kestrel Hollow," Ilsa said behind me. She had a paper timetable from 1961 folded in her coat pocket, because of course she did. Disgraced dispatchers don't travel light; they travel *archivally*. "Passenger stop, two platforms, water tower, freight spur. Closed in 'sixty-eight when the line went dead."

"It says all that on one timetable?"

"It says more. There were forty-one scheduled stops a week here. Forty-one." She said it the way other people say *can you believe it snowed in June*. "That's a town's heartbeat, right there. Eight times a day, something arrived that wasn't you."

I didn't say anything to that. I'm fourteen, and my policy is not to have opinions about things I can't steal or outrun yet. But I kept my hand in the empty sign-bracket a second longer than was cool.

We'd stopped because Felix said we had to. The river had gone shallow enough over the last two days that the locomotive was drinking mud, and the cuttings ahead were listed — in Oren Vasquez's cramped annotations, which Felix now read like scripture and hated like scripture — as prone to flash flooding if any weather came off the high desert. So: shelter, water the engine from the tower if the tower still held anything, let the twins re-pack a bearing that had started singing a note no bearing should sing.

And rest. That was the word nobody said out loud. We'd been running scared since the trestle, since the Combine's men had tried to drop us into the gorge, since Dain took a knife for us and stitched himself up with the same face he uses for reading timetables. The train needed rest. The people on it needed it worse, and would rather have admitted to needing the knife again.

The station building was long and low, brick gone the color of old bone, roof mostly honest except for one sagging hip over what had been the waiting room. The doors were swollen shut. Felix hit the main one twice with her shoulder, swore in three languages — I counted Spanish, railroad, and something else I didn't recognize, probably Vaskarian, because Dain nodded at it like a man hearing a song from home — and then we went in through a broken window instead, because that's how our crew does doors.

Inside smelled like dust and bird droppings and, underneath both, faintly, like woodsmoke that had soaked so deep into the walls it would never fully leave. Somebody had lived here. Not just waited here. *Lived.*

* * *

You could read the whole story of the place in its floors, if you knew how, and I know how, because reading rooms is most of what being a stowaway is. First layer: railway. Ticket window with its little hatch, benches bolted down in rows facing the tracks, a stove flue hole in the ceiling where the waiting-room iron monster used to breathe. Second layer: abandonment. Benches pried up — see the clean rectangles in the dust where they stood for years after everyone stopped coming — glass broken, timetable cases emptied, pigeon civilization rising in the rafters.

Third layer was the strange one. Third layer: beds.

There were bed frames in the waiting room. Four of them, iron, army-surplus by the look, arranged where the benches used to be, with straw ticks rotted down to lace. A curtain rod still hung across one corner on two bent nails. A child's shoe sat under the window, small, leather cracked like dry riverbed, laces still tied. Whoever tied them had been learning, because the bow was enormous and the loops uneven, the kind of knot you make when you've just figured out the trick and want to do it everywhere, on everything, forever.

Nobody else had noticed the shoe yet. The adults were doing adult things — Felix walking the building with a lantern, checking joists; Ilsa cataloguing; Dain standing very still near the ticket window with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

I picked up the shoe. It weighed nothing. It fit in my palm like it had been made for my palm, which was a horrible thought, so I put it back exactly where it had been, angle and all, because some things you don't move even when nobody's watching. *Especially* then.

"Pip." Felix's voice, from the far end. "Come look at this."

I went, glad of the errand, stepping around the bed frames like they were sleeping people.

* * *

It was in what must have been the stationmaster's office, or maybe a second waiting room, or maybe — and this is what my gut said, before my gut finished saying it and I wished it hadn't — a classroom. One big room, one window, plaster walls gone gray-green with damp but structurally sound, and on those walls, chalk.

Chalk drawings. Dozens of them. Hundreds maybe, layered and overlapping, in white and yellow and a faded blue that someone had clearly rationed like gold. A train, drawn with real care, wheels and coupling rods and a plume of smoke rendered in careful loops — drawn by somebody who'd looked at engines the way I look at wallets. A house with a crooked chimney. Mountains with little zigzag snowcaps. A sun with a smiley face, because every kid in history has drawn the same sun, and I felt weirdly moved that children who spoke whatever language this was drew their suns smiling too.

And letters. Chalk letters in an alphabet I didn't know — curvy, slanted, full of little tails and hooks. Rows of them, practice lines, the way you'd practice your ABCs. Some rows neat. Some rows wild. One row halfway down the wall just dissolved into scribbles, the way practice always does when the hand gets tired or the mind gets elsewhere, and next to the scribble someone older had written a single word in the same alphabet, gently, like a correction, like a hand on a shoulder.

"What does it say?" I asked.

Dain had come in behind us without making a sound, which is his whole personality. He stood looking at the wall for a long time. Long enough that I thought he hadn't heard me.

"*Learn,*" he said finally. His voice did something I'd never heard it do. It went quiet in the middle of itself. "It says learn. She wrote it — the teacher, probably. Or a mother. Someone patient." He touched nothing. He kept his hands behind his back the whole time, like the room was a church, or like he was afraid if he touched the wall the chalk would come off on him and he'd never get it off. Maybe both.

"Who were they?" I asked. "The kids?"

"Refugees." Felix answered, because Dain's voice had apparently used up all its words. She had her lantern up, moving its light slowly along the rows of letters. "After Vaskaria was dissolved. Forty years ago. People ran — north along this corridor, mostly, before the border closed. Stations like this became waystations. Then homes. Then—" she gestured at the rotting straw ticks, the tied shoe, "—memories."

"They lived *here?* In a train station?"

"For years, some of them. Until they found somewhere safer, or until—" Felix stopped. Felix doesn't usually stop sentences. She finishes them or she doesn't start them. This one she abandoned in the middle, and the abandoned half of it hung in the air like the smell of woodsmoke.

"Or until the Combine bought the corridor," Ilsa said from the doorway, flat as a rail. "And cleared it. You can't sell land with people living on it. Well. You can. But first you make them not be people."

Nobody argued with her. Ilsa knows things about paperwork the way I know things about rooftops, and neither of our knowledges is cheerful.

I turned back to the wall. And that's when I saw the thing I'm going to carry for a while, so I might as well say it properly.

Down low, near the floor, at the height of a small person crouching — hidden, deliberately, behind where a desk or a cot would have stood — someone had drawn a map. Not a good map. A kid's map. But a *map*: a wiggly line for the river, a boxy thing for the station, a stick-figure train, and then, upstream, past a mountain with angry eyebrows, a little circle with rays coming off it, and beside it, in that curvy hooked alphabet, two words.

"Dain," I said. "What's this say?"

He crouched. He was down there a while. When he straightened up, his face had done the thing it does — closed, careful, the exile-face, the face of a man who has spent his whole life carrying a country in a place with no pockets.

"'Home,'" he said. "'Still home.'"

* * *

We made camp in the waiting room, because the alternative was the rain, which had started an hour after dark with the enthusiasm of something with a grudge. Felix got the potbellied stove in the corner drawing — the flue was clear, miracle of miracles, or maybe pigeons are useful for something after all — and the heat came up rusty-smelling but real. The twins, Brann and Rennick, whose entire conversational output is normally conducted through each other, actually talked to the rest of us for once, telling a story about a boilermaker they'd apprenticed under who fell asleep inside a locomotive firebox and was only discovered because he snored in whistle-pitch. It was a lie, probably. It was a good lie. It made Dain laugh, one short bark, surprised out of him, and I noticed Felix noticing that, filing it away the way she files everything.

I couldn't sleep. Big surprise. I took my blanket and went back to the classroom, because I'd left the lantern there, and also because the room had gotten its hooks in me and I wanted to understand why.

Here's the thing about me. I've been on my own since I was eleven, and I've slept in a lot of places, and I have a theory that every empty building is a letter somebody wrote to the future without knowing it. Most of them say nothing. *Somebody was bored here. Somebody broke this here.* This room was shouting.

I held the lantern up and read the walls properly this time, slow, section by section, the way Felix reads a chart. Trains, houses, mountains, suns. A dog with too many legs. A game of noughts-and-crosses played to a draw by two hands with different pressure — one confident, one hesitant — and I sat with that a minute, thinking about two kids, maybe siblings, playing a game on a wall of a room that was the whole world, drawing ties because neither of them wanted to win alone.

Then, in the corner, lowest of all, almost worn away by decades of floor-scrubbing feet or floodwater or just time: a handprint. Chalk on the palm, pressed to the plaster. Small. Smaller than my hand, and I'm not exactly huge.

Kids do that. Press their hands to things. Proof of presence. *I was here.* It's the oldest signature there is.

Except this one had been traced. Over and over, I mean — the outline reinforced, fresh chalk dragged along the lines of an old print by some later hand, keeping it crisp, keeping it *there*, the way you'd keep a grave clear of weeds. Somebody had come back to this wall, year after year maybe, and redrawn a child's hand so it wouldn't fade.

I sat down on the floor under it. I don't know how long. The stove ticked in the other room. Rain worked the roof.

My mother left me at a junction town when I was eleven with a promise to come back that I believed for about nine months, which was eight months longer than I should have. Since then, buildings have just been buildings. Roofs are roofs. I don't read letters in them because I stopped believing anyone writes to the future. The future is just where you keep running.

But somebody had loved a kid in this room. Somebody had taught that kid letters on a wall because paper was precious and walls were free. Somebody had stayed behind, or come back, or sent someone, to trace that hand in chalk so the erasure — the Combine's erasure, the atlas's erasure, the whole world's polite agreement that these people had never existed — wouldn't take *this one small thing.*

The erasure wasn't abstract. That's what I kept coming back to, turning it over like a stone. Back in the yards, when Dain first told us about Vaskaria, it had sounded like a fairy tale, a country that vanished, sad but distant, a story with no smell. But this room smelled like woodsmoke. This room had a shoe with a giant proud bow in its laces. This room had bedrooms. The erasure had *bedrooms*, and beds in them, and kids in the beds, and a teacher writing *learn* on a wall in a language the world decided to forget, and a hand that somebody refused to let fade.

You can't un-know that. Once you've seen the bedrooms, every map with a hole in it looks different. Every hole has a shoe in it.

I heard the floorboard creak and didn't jump, because I knew the weight. Felix came in with her lantern, saw me sitting there, and instead of telling me to get to sleep — which is what an adult is supposed to do, and Felix is many bad things but rarely incorrect — she looked where I was looking.

She stood there a long moment. Then she crouched, the way Dain had, and read the two little words by the rayed circle. Her jaw did the thing it does when she's swallowing something too big.

"My mentor helped bury these people," she said. Not to me, exactly. To the wall. "Oren. He drew the charts that sealed the valley upstream. Kept it off every map. I thought—" She stopped herself. Second abandoned sentence in one day. The station was bad for her too.

"I know," I said, because what do you say. "It's got bedrooms."

Felix looked at me sideways. "What?"

"The erasure. Books make it sound like a line on a map going away. But it's got bedrooms. And shoes." I nodded at the doorway, toward the waiting room, toward the small leather ghost under the window. "Somebody tied those laces learning how. That's what got erased. Not a line. Laces."

For a second I thought she was going to tell me I was being a kid about it. Instead she sat down on the floor next to me, which Felix Reyes has never once done in the entire time I've known her, and we looked at the handprint together, two runaways and one traced hand, the rain ticking on the roof like a patient clock.

"We're going to open the valley," she said finally. Quietly. Like a promise she was testing to see if it would hold weight. "Whatever it costs. They don't get to be a hole."

"Okay," I said.

"And Pip — if we pull this off, if we get through the dry season and past the Combine — you'll need somewhere that isn't a rooftop."

"I've got somewhere," I said automatically, fast, the reflex of eleven-to-fourteen. "I always find somewhere."

"Yeah," Felix said. She reached out and, very lightly, tapped the wall just beside the chalk handprint, not touching it, honoring its border. "So did they."

* * *

I did sleep, eventually, in the classroom, with the lantern low and the handprint above me like a guardian with five fingers. Somewhere in the night the rain quit, and near dawn the quiet woke me — desert quiet, huge and rinsed — and I lay there listening to the building settle and thinking about letters to the future.

Then boots on the platform. Two sets, wrong rhythm, wrong weight — not Felix, not the twins, not Dain's soft ghost-step. Moving casual, moving deliberate, the walk of men checking a building they intend to own.

I was up and at the window crack before my brain finished the math. Down on the platform, gray in the first light: two figures in survey-company slickers, standing at the empty sign-bracket, and one of them holding a clipboard, and on the clipboard, catching the dawn, the little brass-colored logo I'd hoped to never see again at this range.

Meridian Combine.

The other one was prying at the classroom window frame from outside. My window. Ten feet below the handprint.

I backed away from the glass, heart going like a runaway, and went to find Felix — and nearly walked into Dain, already awake, already dressed, a knife appearing out of his sleeve with the calm of a man dealing cards.

"I know," he whispered. "Wake the others. Quietly."

Above us, faint in the dawn gray, the chalk handprint held the wall. Outside, the world that had erased it was climbing through the window.
