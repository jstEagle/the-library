## 12. Oren's Ledger

The rain came back that night, which was fitting, because everything in my life lately had come back — the Combine, the dead line, and now a dead man's handwriting.

I'd spread the charts across the caboose table under two lanterns and a jury-rigged lamp Ilsa had wired to the battery bank, because apparently when you hire a disgraced dispatcher you also get someone who treats lighting as a discipline. The crew had gone quiet around me without being asked to. Pip sat cross-legged on the bench with his chin on his fists. The twins — Brann and Sorrel, or possibly Sorrel and Brann; I'd given up distinguishing them by anything except that one of them hummed when he worked — leaned in the doorway smelling of coal smoke and cheap soap. Dain stood at the window with his back half-turned, watching the dark for things neither of us wanted to name.

And there on the table, forty years old and water-stained at the corners, lay Oren Vasquez's annotations.

I want to be clear about what this felt like. It did not feel like discovery. It felt like walking into your own kitchen at three in the morning and finding a stranger standing at the stove, making tea like they lived there.

I knew that handwriting. You don't forget the hand that taught yours. Oren wrote his numbers the way he did everything — leaning forward, urgent, the downstrokes heavy as if the pencil were trying to dig through the paper into whatever was underneath. He'd taught me surveying arithmetic on the back of freight manifests, sitting on coupling blocks in yards that smelled of creosote and rust, saying *show me your work, Felix, because someday somebody will need to know why you decided what you decided.* He'd said it like it was a moral instruction. It turned out it was. Just not the one I thought.

"Those are the marginal notes?" Ilsa asked. She'd taken her boots off, which was how she signaled she intended to stay a while.

"Some of them." I turned a chart toward the light. "Most of them are engineering. Bearing loads, drainage grades, culvert specs. Standard stuff for sealing a tunnel — if you were going to seal one."

"But?"

"But these aren't." I tapped a cluster of figures in the margin of the source chart, up where the river forked above the last surveyed station. They weren't load calculations. They were dates. Eleven of them, spaced over six years, each followed by a short string of characters I'd assumed were survey codes. "These are cipher groups. Book cipher, maybe. Or a running key."

"You can read them?" Pip asked.

"I can read *him*," I said. "That's not the same thing, but it's a start."

Here is the thing about Oren Vasquez: he never taught me anything without teaching me how to undo it. It wasn't sentimentality. He used to say a lock you couldn't open yourself was just a coffin with ambitions. So every cipher trick, every shorthand, every way of hiding a number inside an innocent-looking figure — he showed me the door out of it while he showed me the door in. I spent my twenties thinking it was paranoia dressed up as pedagogy. Sitting there in a caboose rolling up a river that officially didn't exist, I understood he'd been leaving me keys the whole time, and I'd never once checked whether any of his doors were locked.

His date notation was the key. He always wrote dates as day-month reduced modulo something — a habit he claimed came from working with foreign crews who couldn't agree on formats. Eleven dates. If I took each date's components as indices into the chart itself — line, column, character — I got letters. The first three gave me nothing. The fourth gave me a V. The fifth gave me an A.

Pip saw it before I said it out loud. He was fourteen and had the shameless pattern-recognition of someone who hadn't yet learned to doubt himself professionally. "It spells something," he said. "Keep going."

So I kept going, and the caboose got very quiet except for the rain and the click of the wheels and my own breathing, which I could hear because I'd stopped doing anything else.

*VASKAR LIVES. THEY ARE IN THE VALLEY. I SEALED THE TUNNEL TO KEEP THEM SO. FORGIVE ME THE MAP OR FORGIVE ME THE PEOPLE. I COULD NOT CARRY BOTH.*

I read it four times. Then I put the pencil down, because my hands had developed opinions about holding things.

"That's him," Dain said from the window. He hadn't moved, but his voice had changed — lower, rougher, like something long-silted shifting in a channel. "That's his voice. That's exactly how they talked about it. The ones who stayed behind. *Forgive us the map or forgive us the people.*"

"Who's *they*?" Ilsa asked carefully.

Dain turned around. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had that morning, and younger too, which shouldn't be possible but grief manages it. "When Vaskaria was dissolved, it didn't dissolve evenly," he said. "Cities were registered, relocated, absorbed. But there were valleys. Mining valleys, spring valleys, places the new borders drew straight lines across because nobody important lived there — according to the maps. Some people ran. Some people were run. And some people went up into the high country and simply... were not counted. My grandmother's sister was one of the uncounted. We thought they all died in the first winter. It was easier than thinking anything else."

"And Oren sealed the tunnel so no one would find them," I said.

"He sealed the source," Dain corrected. "The water, the valley, the road in. Everything. Look at his engineering notes again — not as notes. As a wall."

I looked. And once you looked at it that way, you couldn't unsee it. Every specification was sound. Every bearing load was honest. That was the part that made my teeth ache: he hadn't faked anything. He'd built a real, competent, permanent closure over a living secret, the way you'd build a bridge — with integrity, with care, with the full weight of his considerable skill. The lie wasn't in the work. The lie was in what the work was *for.*

"He falsified the hydrology," I said slowly. "Downstream reports. There's a page here — flow rates that don't match the catchment area. I noticed it days ago and thought it was sloppy surveying. It wasn't sloppy. It was deliberate. He made the headwaters look seasonal. Made the whole upper river look like it died every summer, so nobody would ever bother going up to check."

"The dry season," Ilsa said. Her eyes went to the window, to the north, where the river came from. "The one we're racing."

"Yes." I rubbed my face. "Except the river doesn't die. It just gets low enough that his numbers become true. He built a lie with a maintenance schedule."

Brann — or Sorrel — snorted from the doorway. "Sounds like every dispatcher I ever worked under."

"This isn't funny," I said.

"No," he agreed, not looking sorry. "But it's true, and true things get to be funny sometimes. That's how you know they're true."

*

I didn't sleep. I told the crew I would, and then I sat up with the ledger — because by then I'd stopped calling it a set of annotations and started calling it what it was, a ledger, a record of debts — and I worked through the rest of the cipher groups until the lamp burned low and the rain quit sometime before dawn.

There were more messages. Not many. Oren had been a careful man, and careful men write few words. But scattered through the eleven years of dates were fragments:

*A family came up in March. Seven of them. The youngest was born on the trail.*

*They ask me when the world will remember them. I tell them the world has a poor memory and good eyes. Someday the eyes will win.*

*I have become a liar the way a dam becomes a lake. Slowly, and on purpose, and it holds back more than water.*

*I taught a girl to survey. She runs the way I ran. I hope she never finds this. I hope she finds this. Both hopes are dishonest. I am out of practice being otherwise.*

That last one I read about nine times, and then I went outside and stood in the vestibule in the cold and let the wind off the canyon do something to my face that I'm going to describe as checking the weather, because I am a professional.

He'd known. Somewhere in those eleven years of sealing tunnels and falsifying flows and carrying medicine up a mule track to a hidden valley, he'd thought of me — the apprentice he'd taught to leave before breakfast, the girl whose map of closed lines was already getting thick — and he'd hoped I'd never find this, and hoped I would, and known both hopes were cowardice. He'd written *I am out of practice being otherwise* the way another man might write *I miss my daughter.*

I had spent five days hating him. Clean hate, efficient hate, the kind that fits in a pocket and doesn't weigh anything. Betrayal is tidy. You file it under his name and you move on, and moving on is the thing I was best at in the world.

This was not tidy. This was a man trading truth for lives, one year at a time, and keeping books on it, and hoping the auditor never came.

*

Felix," Dain said. He'd come out without me hearing him, which should have frightened me and instead just felt like the week we'd been having. He handed me tea from the caboose kettle, which meant Ilsa had made it, which meant it was terrible, which meant I drank it. "You've been out here an hour."

"I'm calibrating."

"To what?"

"To the idea that everything he taught me was also a shelter." I wrapped both hands around the cup. "You know what he told me the day he sent me off? Last time I saw him? He said, *Never stay anywhere long enough to owe anybody.* I thought it was wisdom. I built a whole life on it. Twenty years of branch lines and demolition surveys and leaving before the goodbye, and I thought I was free." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I was following instructions. His instructions. He taught me to run because running was safe, and safety was the whole point, because somewhere upstream there was a valley full of people whose survival depended on nobody asking questions, and the best protection he could give the world was a generation of engineers trained not to look."

Dain was quiet for a while. The train ticked and settled beneath us. Down the cut, the river muttered over its stones, lower than yesterday. I didn't say that out loud. Nobody had to.

"My father had a saying too," Dain said finally. "*A locked door protects whoever is behind it, not whoever is knocking.* He hated the men who sealed things. The officials, the cartographers, the ones who erased us. And then he grew old, and he understood that some of the locks were ours. Some of the erasure was mercy. He never forgave it. But he stopped demanding it be simple."

"I don't want it to be simple," I said. "I want it to be *load-bearing.* There's a difference."

"Explain the difference."

"A lie that's simple is just a lie. You kick it over and move on. A lie that's load-bearing — that's different. That's a lie with people standing on it. You knock it down, you'd better know what comes down with it." I stared at the dark shape of the canyon walls. "Forty years, Dain. Forty years of children born up there who've never been counted, never vaccinated, never schooled anywhere but each other. If that seal held because the world believed the river died every summer — and we blow it open next week with a whistle and a press release — what happens to them? Do they get their country back, or do they just get discovered?"

"That is not your decision alone," Dain said. It wasn't harsh. It was the gentlest thing anyone had said to me in years, and it landed like a dropped coupling. "You are right to ask it. But you are one person on a train of seven, and the people in that valley are not characters in your guilt. They get a vote too. Whatever you decide the truth costs, they're the ones who've been paying for the lie."

I looked at him. "When did you get wise?"

"When they took my country, they left me the arguing." Almost a smile. "It travels light."

*

Morning came up gray and thin over the cut. I called the crew together in the caboose, because there are conversations you can postpone and conversations that start costing interest, and this one had been accruing since midnight.

I laid it out flat, the way Oren taught me to lay out a problem: facts first, feelings after, decisions last. The cipher. The valley. The families. The forty years of engineered silence. The fact that the dry season was eating the river a foot a day, and that when it bottomed out, Oren's lie would become literally true — the upper river impassable, the valley sealed by nature instead of by concrete, for another season, maybe forever if the Combine bought the corridor and tore up the line.

"So here's what I keep turning over," I finished. "Every instinct I have says publish. Drag it into the light, redraw the map, hang the erasure out where everyone can see it. That's the justice version. But justice is easy from down here. Up there—" I pointed north, at the source none of us had seen, "—there are people who have survived forty years precisely by not being a story. And I learned everything I know from the man who built their hiding place, and I cannot tell anymore whether wanting to tear it open is principle or just me needing to stop owing him."

Silence. Then Pip, of all people: "My mom used to say a house isn't walls, it's who's allowed in." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Maybe the lie's a house."

"It's a dam," Ilsa said. "And I've seen what happens when you breach one without spillways. Everybody downstream drowns in the truth at once." She looked at me over her terrible tea. "You don't decide this alone, Felix. That's not how crews work. That's not how *this* crew works."

"She's right," said one twin.

"She usually is," said the other.

Dain said nothing. He just looked at me with that patient, backward-facing gaze of his, and I understood that he'd already voted, days ago, in the only way that mattered — by bleeding for a train that wasn't his, bound for a valley that was.

I gathered the ledger pages and squared their edges against the table, and my hands were steadier than they had any right to be.

"All right," I said. "Then we do this properly. Tonight, around the stove, everybody speaks — what the source means to them, what the truth should cost, and who pays it. No votes until everyone's said their piece. Including the two of you who think you're identical, because I know for a fact you're not."

The humming twin grinned. The other one elbowed him.

"One more thing," I said, and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized. "Oren wrote that he hoped I'd never find this, and hoped I would. I've decided that means he wanted me to finish it. Not his way. Mine." I looked around the caboose, at the strays I hadn't hired so much as failed to prevent. "So tonight we decide what *mine* looks like. And tomorrow—"

The brake whistle shrieked from the engine, three long blasts, the emergency signal, and the whole caboose lurched as Sorrel-or-Brann stood up so fast he knocked his tea flying.

"Bridge," he shouted. "Washout on the trestle ahead — and there are men on it."
