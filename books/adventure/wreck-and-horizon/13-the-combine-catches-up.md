## 13. The Combine Catches Up

I have been recognized by strangers in four countries and ignored by my own people in five, so I like to think I know something about faces. Faces are how I make my living now — or made it, before a railroad engineer with river-mud on her boots decided to stop running and drag the rest of us into her wake. Faces are also how I nearly got us all killed on the fourth day out of the yard, which I will get to, but first you should understand that I had already seen the man twice before he ever set foot on our train.

The first time was at the water stop below Kettle Gap, standing too far from the tower to be waiting for water and too clean to be waiting for anything else. The second time was through the observation slit of the caboose, walking the ballast line an hour behind us with the patient gait of a man being paid by the day. Both times he wore the same gray coat, and both times he looked at the train the way a butcher looks at a side of beef: not hostile. Appraising.

I said nothing for two days. This is not cowardice, whatever Pip tells people at parties. It is arithmetic. If I told Felix there was a man following us, she would either stop the train — impossible, we were racing the dry season down a corridor the Combine had bought out from under us — or she would change nothing, because there was nothing to change. And if I told her and I was wrong about the man, I would be the exile who cried wolves at a crew that had, against every instinct I possess and several I've invented, started trusting me.

So I watched. Watching is what I'm good at. Forty years of watching.

* * *

You should know what I was watching *for*, though, because it explains the mistake I made.

When Vaskaria was dissolved — "administratively reorganized," the communiqué said, as if a country were a filing cabinet — they did not just redraw borders. They redrew faces. My father's name became a number. Our street became a parcel code. The church where my sister was married became a grain depot, then a ruin, then nothing at all, because photographs of nothing require no explanation. I learned early that erasure has employees. Men in gray coats who carry clipboards instead of rifles, who ask for documents instead of names, whose whole terrible genius is making you feel that *you* are the irregularity, the smudge on their clean page.

Meridian Combine hires those men. Not all of them — some of Meridian's people are honest salvagers, and God knows honest work is thin on the ground these days — but when the Combine wants a thing buried, it does not send thugs. Thugs leave witnesses. It sends inspectors.

So when the radio crackled on the morning of the fourth day and a voice announced that two officials from the Corridor Authority would board at Havel Junction for a routine safety inspection, I felt the cold arrive in my hands before it arrived anywhere else.

"Routine," Felix repeated, looking at the radio as if it might blush. "Since when does any authority inspect a dead line?"

"Since someone paid them to," Ilsa said. She had the train moving at a careful forty kilometers an hour through a cutting that wanted to eat us, and she did not take her eyes off the rail ahead. "Authority paperwork can be bought same as anything. Question is what the paperwork says."

"It'll say we're carrying unregistered cargo," I said.

They all turned to look at me. Even Pip, who was supposed to be greasing the journal boxes and was instead lying across the tender like a cat in a sunbeam.

"You knew," Felix said. Not angry. Worse — interested. Felix Reyes interested is a force of nature with a clipboard.

"I suspected. Now I know." I told them about the gray coat, the water stop, the walk along the ballast. I told them what I knew of Meridian's methods, which was more than I wanted to know and less than I needed. "Two men will board. They will be polite. They will want to see the chest."

"And if we don't show it to them?"

"Then they will be polite somewhere else while other men are impolite here."

Pip sat up. "Can't we just — not stop? At Havel?"

"The junction switch is Authority-controlled," Ilsa said grimly. "They'll set it against us if we blow past. We'd end up in the gravel cut, and the gravel cut ends at a bridge that isn't rated for anything heavier than regret."

"So we let them aboard," Felix said. She was doing the thing with her jaw that meant she was building something in her head, some structure of steel and audacity. "We let them aboard, we give them a tour, and we don't give them the chest. Dain — can you tell?"

"Tell what?"

"If they're real inspectors or Meridian's kind. You said you know their faces."

I could have lied. It would have been easy; lying is the mother tongue of every exile. But she was looking at me with those surveyor's eyes that measure everything including the person measuring back, and I found — this is the strange part, the part I still turn over at night — that I didn't want to lie to her.

"No," I admitted. "Not for certain. I know the type, not the face. But I know one thing better than any inspector alive." I tapped the chart case at my hip, the one holding the Vaskarian surveys. "I know what's in these documents. Every watermark, every annotation, every correction Oren Vasquez made in his own hand. If they ask to see the charts, I'll hand them fakes, and I'll know within a minute whether they know the difference. A real Authority man wouldn't. He'd check load ratings and brake lines. A Meridian man will go straight for the paper."

Felix nodded slowly. "A trap built out of your own expertise."

"My mother always said my education would be useful someday. She imagined something with a pension."

* * *

Havel Junction was a stub of platform and a signal box sinking into the meadow like a tooth into gum. We came to a stop with the usual symphony of shrieking brakes and Ilsa swearing at the air gauge, and the two men were already waiting on the platform, which was my second warning. Real inspectors travel to inspections. These men had traveled to *ambushes* and simply dressed for the wrong occasion.

The tall one wore the gray coat I'd seen on the ballast walker. The short one was new to me — round-faced, pleasant, with the soft handshake of a man who has never lifted anything heavier than another man's future. Their credentials were laminated. Laminated! No genuine Authority inspector in this corridor had seen lamination since the floods took the regional office three years ago. Everything real out here was water-stained and photocopied twice. Only institutions that expected to survive issued plastic.

"Gentlemen," Felix said, meeting them on the platform with her best engineer's blankness. "This is a work train under private charter. What exactly do you need to see?"

"Standard safety review," the tall one said. His accent was a hundred kilometers wide and belonged nowhere, the way accents belong nowhere when they've been purchased from a course. "Brake systems, boiler certification, crew manifest. And cargo documentation, of course."

"Of course," Felix echoed pleasantly. "Boiler first. It's a climb."

And here is where I must confess the shape of my error, because pride is a debt that always comes due at the worst exchange rate. While Felix hauled the tall one up into the cab to admire gauges, and Ilsa answered questions about manifests with the serene obstruction of a woman who has spent thirty years losing paperwork professionally, I took the round-faced one aside and showed him my forged charts.

I had worked on those fakes for two nights. I aged the paper with tea. I copied Vasquez's annotations until my hand cramped. They were good. They were very good. And the round-faced man looked at them for perhaps four seconds, smiled his soft smile, and said:

"These aren't the ones."

Not *these appear fabricated*. Not *the seal is wrong*. He didn't examine them at all, because he didn't need to. He had seen the real charts. Which meant Meridian had seen the real charts. Which meant somewhere in the chain between the flooded rail yard and this meadow, someone had photographed or memorized or stolen a look at what I carried — and everything I had built my certainty on was a wall with a door in it that I myself had just opened.

He saw me understand it. I watched the understanding move across his pleasant face like weather.

"Well," he said, still smiling. "That's unfortunate."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I will render it honestly even though honesty costs me, because the whole point of what I am writing here — what I finally agreed to write, after Felix put the pen in my hand and refused to take no — is that I am done curating myself.

The tall one came down from the cab. Something passed between him and the round-faced man, some flicker, and the tall one's hand went inside his coat, and Felix — bless her, sharp as a fishhook — stepped backward off the cab ladder and shouted "Ilsa, NOW," and Ilsa, who had been waiting for exactly that word the way soldiers wait for a ceasefire, dumped the air and threw the reverser and the whole train lurched forward with two Meridian agents standing on a platform we were leaving behind.

Except we weren't leaving them behind. The tall one caught the grab iron of the tender. The round-faced one caught the ladder of the caboose, which is where I was standing, alone, holding forged charts, being exactly the kind of man I had spent forty years refusing to be: surprised.

He came up over the railing quick for a soft-handed man. He had a knife — not a thug's knife, a working tool, the kind of blade that opens crates and, apparently, exiles. I did the math I should have done on the platform. I had maybe ten kilos on him and none of it in the right places. I had survived purges, border crossings, two famines, and one marriage, and none of it had taught me to fight, only to endure, and endurance was about to be tested in a way I found frankly insulting.

"You're Kovic," he said. "Dain Kovic. The community in Marlowe's Landing talks about you. The one who keeps the lists."

"The lists," I said, backing along the caboose roof-walk, "are longer than you think."

"They won't matter. Paper burns."

"Paper," I said, "is the only thing that doesn't."

He lunged. I did the single most effective thing my life had trained me for: I fell. Deliberately, gracelessly, backwards off the walkway onto the deck, and he stumbled over my outstretched boot, and that bought me two seconds, and in those two seconds I did the second most effective thing: I grabbed the chart case and held it above my head like an idiot, like a man in a story, because it mattered more than my body and he knew it and I knew he knew it.

The knife went into my side instead of the case. Below the ribs, angled up, the way you'd open a crate. The pain was white and total and strangely clarifying, the way cold water is clarifying, and I remember thinking, with the absurd detachment of the newly wounded: *so this is what loyalty costs retail.*

Then the caboose door banged open and Pip came through it swinging a grease pot.

I want to be accurate about this, because Pip will deny it forever and embellish it everywhere else. He did not fell the agent with a mighty blow. He hit him in the shoulder with four liters of axle grease, which is not a weapon, it is a *condition*, and the round-faced man slipped in it, went down hard on the deck plates, and lost his knife, and Pip — fifteen years old, ninety pounds of stowaway courage — planted himself over me with the empty pot raised like a war club and screamed in a voice that cracked beautifully in the middle: "GET OFF OUR TRAIN."

Our train. He said *our*. I was bleeding on the deck of a machine I had boarded nine days ago as a passenger, a stranger, a man paying for passage with information, and a child called it *our* train with me inside the word.

Felix arrived at a run, took in the scene — the greased agent pinned under Ilsa's knee (she had come back along the catwalk like the veteran of a hundred boarding actions she never once mentioned), the knife, my blood spreading on the deck in a shape I chose not to interpret — and did the most Felix thing I have ever witnessed. She picked up the agent's dropped credentials, examined the lamination with professional distaste, and said:

"Counterfeit. Figures." Then, to the man under Ilsa's knee, conversationally: "Your friend ran at the trestle. Saw him jump. You'll want to catch up — it's a long walk to Havel." And she threw the laminated cards off the moving train into the meadow, one at a time, like a woman dealing a very bad hand.

* * *

They stitched me in the workshop car with thread meant for leather belting, because that's what we had. Ilsa did the sewing. Her hands were steady and her commentary was not, and Pip held the lamp and narrated my wound to himself in a whisper like a sports commentator, and Felix sat on a crate across from me and watched with an expression I couldn't read until she finally spoke.

"You gave him the fakes on purpose."

"I gave him the fakes because I believed they'd pass. That's not the same as on purpose."

"No." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "But you stood up when you could have stayed hidden. You knew what they were and you walked toward them. Why?"

It was a fair question and deserved better than the answer I almost gave, which was *habit*. Instead I heard myself say: "Because you all stopped for me. Back at the cutting, when the rockslide blocked the line — nobody ordered it, everybody just got out and moved stone. I've spent my whole life watching communities scatter, Mr. Reyes. Watching is what I do. And I watched yours, and I thought — if this is a thing that exists, then it is worth a knife."

"That's a terrible reason to get stabbed."

"It's the only reason anyone gets stabbed. The good reasons are all terrible."

She almost smiled. Then she reached over and took the chart case from beside me — gently, asking with her eyes first — and set it in my lap and closed my hands around it.

"Yours," she said. "It was always going to be yours. I just needed to hear you say why."

Outside, the canyon walls went gold with evening, and the wheels kept their rhythm, and my side burned with every beat of my heart, which was — I noted with clinical interest — beating faster than it had in years.

Ilsa tied off the last stitch and said, "You'll live. Unfortunately for all of us, you'll live." And Pip laughed, and Ilsa laughed, and even Felix laughed, low and unwilling, and I lay there on a workbench in a stolen country of rolling steel, bleeding and stitched and included, and understood that something had happened to me that no border crossing had ever managed.

I had stopped watching from outside.

Which was fortunate, because at dawn Ilsa came into the workshop car holding the telegraph flimsy the Havel operator had flagged for us, and her face told me the dry season had just gotten company. The Combine, it seemed, had stopped sending inspectors.

The next message read, simply: *Helicopter inbound.*
