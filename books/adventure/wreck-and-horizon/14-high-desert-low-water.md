## 14. High Desert, Low Water

I've been a dispatcher for twenty-two years, and in that time I learned exactly one universal truth: every disaster announces itself politely, in advance, on paper. The flood that drowned my career had filed its paperwork three weeks early. The derailment that ended my reputation had been flagged, highlighted, and initialed by four separate people who then chose to ignore it. Disasters are courteous like that. It's the small things — the slow things — that sneak up on you.

The dry season was a slow thing.

I noticed it first as a sound, or rather the absence of one. The river that had chattered alongside the track since the cuttings was going quiet. By the third day in the canyon it wasn't talking at all, just muttering over its rocks like an old man reading bad news. By the fifth day, stretches of the bed were bare — wide aprons of gray stone and stranded silt, cracked into polygons like something baked in a kiln. The water that remained ran thin and glassy in the deepest channel, so clear you could count the pebbles under it, which is another way of saying there wasn't enough of it to hide anything, including our mistakes.

Felix stood at the canyon rim that morning with her chart spread against the wind, doing arithmetic she didn't like. I could tell because of her mouth. The woman has a whole vocabulary of mouths, and this one said: *the numbers are winning.*

"Six inches," she said when I climbed up to join her. "Maybe five. The book says we need fourteen to float the shallow-draft gear across the ford at Kilometer 212."

"The book also says this line closed forty years ago," I said. "We're already off the page."

She gave me the mouth that says *you're not wrong but I hate it.* Then she rolled up the chart and we went back down to tell the others, which is the part of dispatching nobody puts in the job description: being the person who carries the bad numbers from the paper to the people.

* * *

The locomotive — we'd taken to calling her the *Mule*, on account of her temperament — labored through the canyon like a woman hauling a piano upstairs. Every gradient Felix had promised us would ease turned out to have opinions. The water for her boiler came straight from the river now, filtered through what Pip called "the world's saddest sock," and twice a day the shallows gave us more silt than steam. The twins — Bo and Bram Havel, boilermakers, exiles from three railroads and at least one marriage between them — took turns crawling into the firebox throat with scrapers, emerging looking like they'd lost a fight with a chimney.

Rations tightened the way rope does before it bites. We'd planned for eleven days of margin; the canyon had eaten six of them. Rice went first, then the good coffee, then the coffee that only pretended to be coffee. Meals became a ledger item, weighed on a little brass scale Felix kept in her kit like a relic. Two hundred grams per person per meal, no exceptions, including the engineer, including the stowaway, including me, though I'll confess I did the weighing and my thumb developed a generous streak I chose not to examine too closely.

It's remarkable how quickly hunger rearranges a family.

The first crack showed on the fourth morning, over eggs — powdered, reconstituted, and lying about themselves. Pip, sixteen years old and growing like a weed in fast-forward, took his portion, looked at it, and looked at mine.

"You get the same as everyone," he said, "and you weigh it."

"I do weigh it."

"Yeah, well." He pushed his tin across the table. "Weigh it again."

The caboose went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone drops glass. Bo and Bram found their boots fascinating. Dain, who had a knife scar along his ribs from the Combine's inspectors and a way of watching everything without seeming to, watched everything without seeming to.

"Pip." Felix's voice was level. Level the way ice is level.

"He gets extra because he's big," Pip said, jerking his chin at Dain. "I've seen it. And you don't say anything."

"Dain gives half his portion to you most nights," Felix said. "You just sleep through it."

That landed harder than any insult could have. Pip looked at Dain, who didn't confirm or deny, just kept buttering his hardtack with the patience of a man who had survived worse tables than this one. Something went out of the boy's face — not anger exactly, more like the specific humiliation of being caught needing people.

"I'm not a kid," he said, to no one in particular, and left.

Nobody followed him. That was the mistake, and I knew it was the mistake even as we all sat there making it together, because I've spent my life watching trains leave stations and I know the difference between someone going for a walk and someone going for good. But the water gauge needed checking and the firebox needed scraping and the canyon doesn't care about your interpersonal weather, so we let him go, and told ourselves he'd be back by supper.

He wasn't back by supper.

* * *

I want to say something about tempers, since history will probably record this chapter of our journey as the one where everyone yelled. History loves a shouting match. What history misses is the silence around them — the way scarcity works on a crew like rust works on rail, invisible, patient, structural. Nobody screamed at anybody that week. What happened was smaller and worse. People stopped saying thank you. People started eating a little faster, a little hunched, the way animals eat. Bram snapped at Bo over a valve packing and Bo didn't snap back, just went and sat on the tender and didn't talk for two hours, and somehow that was louder than snapping.

And Felix — Felix got quiet. Not her usual quiet, which is the quiet of a woman conserving words like fuel. This was the quiet of a woman running calculations she couldn't share, and I recognized it because I used to wear that same face in dispatch towers while storms stacked up on the horizon. She was doing the math on all of it: water, coal, food, distance, and the sixth variable, the one she never wrote down — whether the crew itself was a resource she was spending faster than she could replace.

On the fifth night I found her on the footplate, oiling parts that didn't need oiling.

"You should talk to him," I said.

"He doesn't want to talk to me."

"He's sixteen. He doesn't want to talk to anyone. That's not the same thing." I handed her the rag she'd been pretending to look for. "In the tower, when two controllers stopped speaking to each other, I made them share a shift. Nothing else worked. Not memos, not mediation, not me yelling, which I tried, extensively. You put people in the same small room with a job that needs four hands, and either they solve it or they starve. Usually they solve it."

"We don't have a four-handed job."

"You have a boiler scale problem and a boy who's been climbing into your machinery since Kilometer 90 when he thinks nobody's watching. Give him the scraper and the bad air and a reason to come back filthy and useful, and see what happens."

Felix looked at me for a long moment. In the firebox light she looked younger than her years and older than her face, both at once, which is a trick I've never managed to describe and won't attempt here.

"When did you start managing me?" she asked.

"Somewhere around the trestle," I said. "You were too busy driving to notice."

* * *

But the talk never happened, because the river finished dying.

We woke on the sixth morning to a canyon that had changed overnight, the way canyons do — not gradually, but all at once, in the dark, while you slept. The ford at 212 wasn't shallow. It wasn't there. A mile of riverbed lay exposed, boulders sitting in cracked mud like furniture in an abandoned house, and the water that had fed it now came down somewhere far below, audible but unreachable, a rumor of a river.

Felix walked the bed for two hours with her surveyor's eye and came back with the verdict we all already knew: the *Mule* could not cross dry. Not with her weight, not with our water, not with any amount of plating and prayer. We'd need to dam what flow remained, pond it, and run her through on a temporary causeway of ballast and cribbing — twelve hours of work, minimum, in heat that had begun to feel personal.

So we worked. That's the thing about crews that the novels get wrong: they don't hold together because of speeches. They hold together because there's rock that needs moving, and moving rock leaves no room for anything else. Bo and Bram built cribbing like they were born in it, which arguably they were. Dain hauled stone with the methodical patience of a man doing penance for sins he wouldn't name. I ran the timing — when to open the dam, when to close it, how long the pond would hold, how long the crossing would take — and discovered, somewhere around hour nine, that I loved it. God help me, standing in a dead riverbed shouting numbers at strong people, I felt more myself than I had in the five years since they'd taken my license and my tower and my name off the board.

Pip worked too. He'd come back the evening before, filthy, silent, and smelling of juniper smoke, and nobody asked where he'd been because asking felt like pressing on a bruise. He took the scraper job in the firebox throat without being offered it, which was as close to an apology as anyone was going to get, and Felix accepted it the same way, and if the two of them stood a little closer together at the end of the day than physics required, well. Rivers aren't the only things that run shallow and deep by turns.

We crossed at dusk. The *Mule* took the causeway at walking pace, wheels singing on wet cribbing, the pond draining behind us like a held breath finally let go. On the far bank, high and dry, Bo actually laughed — a great bark of a laugh that echoed off the canyon walls — and for one evening it felt like we'd beaten something.

Then Felix counted the rations for the next leg and stopped smiling, and I saw her do the new math: seven days of food, four days of canyon left, and a boy who'd been sneaking half-portions onto other people's plates for a week.

* * *

The fight, when it finally came, was almost a relief. Pressure wants somewhere to go.

It happened at the evening halt, in a widening of the canyon where the walls stepped back and let a little sky in. Bram accused Pip of shorting the coal measurements — unfairly, as it turned out; the fault was a sticking gauge, and Felix proved it inside of ten minutes — but by then the accusation had done its work. Words came out of the boy that had been queuing up for days: about being the youngest, the smallest ration, the last consulted, the first blamed. About how he'd been told he was crew and then treated like luggage. About how everyone here talked about family this and belonging that, but when the food got short, suddenly there was a scale, and thumbs, and a pecking order, and guess who was at the bottom of it.

"And where were you today?" he finished, rounding on Felix, voice cracking in a way that would humiliate him later. "Where were you when I was in the firebox? Off walking the bed. Off doing the important work. You always find something important to do when the talking starts."

Felix took that one full in the chest. I watched it land. I watched her open her mouth, and close it, and I understood — because I'd raised a son, once, badly, at a distance — that whatever she wanted to say, she didn't trust herself to say it yet, and silence from Felix Reyes reads as agreement.

Pip read it that way too.

"Right," he said, quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Right."

He took his bedroll and his canteen and walked up the scree slope into the blue dusk, and this time — this time — Dain stood up to follow him, and Felix said, "Let him go," and Dain, who had bled for this crew and earned the right to ignore her, ignored her, and went anyway.

They were gone an hour. When they came back down in the dark, Dain's hand was on the boy's shoulder, and Pip's face was blotchy and set, and nothing had been fixed, but something had been witnessed, which in my long experience is ninety percent of what fixing turns out to be.

Except.

At breakfast, Pip's bedroll was empty. His tin was gone. His canteen was gone. And on the caboose table, weighted under a stone, was his chalk — the stub of chalk he'd carried since the abandoned station, since the wall with the children's drawings, since Closed Stations and everything we'd found there — laid beside a single word in his careful, unlovely hand:

*Gone to find the shrine. Back in two days. Don't come.*

Felix read it twice. Then she folded it once, precisely, and put it in her breast pocket, and picked up her surveyor's kit, and I saw in her face the expression I imagine she wore at every station she ever left behind — except this time she was the one being left, and it fit her terribly.

"Two days," she said, to the canyon, to the crew, to herself. "The shrine's a half-day's climb. If he's not back by tomorrow noon—"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. We were already counting the hours, and the water, and the days of food, and the canyon ahead, and every number in the column was worse than the one above it.

Felix buckled her kit and started up the scree alone.
