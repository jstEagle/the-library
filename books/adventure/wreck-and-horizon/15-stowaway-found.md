## 15. Stowaway Found

Pip lasted eleven hours before I noticed.

That's not a boast about my attentiveness — it's an indictment of the whole operation. We were a crew of five running a hundred-year-old work train through a canyon that wanted us dead, and I still didn't clock the absence of the smallest person aboard until the dinner whistle. Eleven hours of shoveling coal and watching gauges, and somewhere in there a fourteen-year-old boy had simply stepped off the face of the earth between two water stops.

"He's sulking," Ilsa said, when I asked. She was rationing rice with the precision of a woman who'd once run forty trains a day on four hours' sleep and a grudge. "He'll turn up."

"He's not on the train."

The ladle stopped mid-pour. "What do you mean he's not on the train."

"I mean I've checked the tender twice, both cabs, the tool car, and every space under this locomotive big enough to hold a cat. He got off at the last stop and he hasn't gotten back on."

Ilsa set down the ladle. The twins — Bo and Bram, who communicated in a dialect of grunts and wrench-taps — went quiet in the way large men go quiet, which is to say the canyon itself seemed to lean in and listen.

"When did you last see him?" Dain asked. He was sitting on the caboose step, cleaning the knife wound in his forearm with the same unhurried care he gave everything, as if his own body were just another piece of rolling stock due for maintenance.

"Water stop. Two hours back. He was mad about the rations."

"Mad about the rations," Ilsa repeated flatly. "A boy raised in rail yards is mad about *rice*."

"He was mad about what the rations meant." I heard myself say it and wished I hadn't, because saying it made it true: Pip hadn't been angry at the food. He'd been angry at the arithmetic underneath it. Six weeks of water left, maybe less now that the dry season had stopped pretending to be a rumor. Every cup we poured him was a cup the engine would want later, and he was old enough and yard-smart enough to do that math. He'd done it out loud, actually, at the water tower, red-faced, shouting that we should leave him off the manifest because he drank more than he hauled.

We'd all laughed. That was the mistake. We'd laughed like family laughs, easy and unguarded, and apparently it had landed on him like something else entirely.

Dain stood up, wincing. "Which direction?"

"The cliffs, if I had to guess. He watches them. He's been watching them since the shrine country started."

"What shrine country?" Ilsa asked.

I looked at Dain. Dain looked at me. It was one of those moments where two people who trust each other exactly as far as a handshake realize they've been keeping the same secret from the same third party.

"Vaskarian herders used to work these slopes," Dain said carefully. "Before. They built little waystations up in the rock. Shrines, sort of. Places to leave offerings for safe passage."

"And nobody thought to mention," Ilsa said, "that our runaway stowaway might be climbing toward a pile of religious rocks?"

"We mentioned the shrines," Bo said.

"You mentioned the shrines," Bram agreed.

"To each other," Bo clarified. "In the shop. Last week."

Ilsa closed her eyes the way dispatchers do when the board goes sideways, counting to some number I've never personally reached. "Fine. Fine. Felix, you're fastest on broken ground. Dain, you're bleeding. Twins, nobody touches the engine. I'll keep the coffee warm and rehearse what I'm going to say to that boy, and none of you want to hear the draft."

I took a lantern, a coil of rope I mostly carried for the comfort of its weight, and the last thing I said before dropping off the tender steps, which was nothing, because my mouth had already decided we weren't making this a ceremony.

* * *

The canyon at dusk does something to sound that I've never trusted. Your own footsteps come back to you late, from the wrong direction, so that half the time you're walking toward the echo of yourself and calling it pursuit. I climbed the talus slope above the water stop with the lantern hooded to a coin of light, following the only trail a boy leaves on shattered limestone: scuffed edges, a handprint in the dust where he'd pulled himself over a lip too tall for him, one lace-tip from a boot sole gone bad. Yard kids learn to move light through freight cars. It doesn't translate to cliff faces. He'd left a signature on every obstacle, and the signature read *scared but going anyway*, which is the most honest handwriting there is.

The shrine found me before I found it.

There was no warning — no cairn, no marker. One moment I was hauling myself up a seam in the rock, and the next the wall simply opened into a shallow cave mouth, and inside it, in the last gray light, someone had built a small architecture of attention. Flat stones stacked into shelves. A niche swept clean. Little objects arranged with the care people give things they can't afford to replace: a tin cup gone green with age, a coil of braided wool faded past color, a child's wooden bird with one wing carved and one wing still waiting inside the wood. And chalked on the stone above, in a script I couldn't read but recognized on sight from the survey charts wedged under my bunk — Vaskarian letters, worn by weather but stubbornly legible, the way anything written by people determined to exist tends to be.

Someone had left bread here once. Long ago. There was a fossilized heel of it on a shelf, kept, not discarded. You don't keep old bread unless the leaving mattered more than the thing left.

"Pip," I said. "It's Felix. I'm coming up slow."

Silence. Then, from the back of the cave, in a voice scraped raw: "Go away."

"Noted. Coming up anyway."

"That's not — you're supposed to *respect* —"

"I respect plenty of things. Caves boys are freezing in aren't one of them." I ducked into the mouth and let the lantern up a little. He was curled against the far wall with his knees to his chest, and he'd been crying, and he was furious about having been caught crying, which meant he was going to be twice as difficult, which meant I needed to skip the part where we pretended this was about rice.

I sat down across from him. Not beside him — beside is a trap for a kid in that state; it reads as ambush. Across gives them a wall at their back and a person at a distance they can study. Oren taught me that without ever meaning to. Oren taught me most things without ever meaning to.

"It's cold," I said.

"I'm fine."

"You're shivering hard enough to generate electricity. If you could hook yourself to the dynamo we'd make better time."

Something twitched at the corner of his mouth and died there. Good. A dead smile means the door isn't fully shut.

"You laughed at me," he said finally, to the wall.

"At the water tower. Yeah."

"You all did. Like I was — like I was being cute. Like I don't know how it works." His voice cracked and he hated that too; I watched him hate it. "I've been cargo my whole life, okay? On every crew, every yard, every — wherever. I'm the extra mouth. I'm the reason the math doesn't work. And now the math really doesn't work, and everybody's doing it politely, not saying it, and then I said it out loud and you all *laughed* —"

"Pip."

"— because it's funnier when the cargo says it itself, right? Saves everybody the awkwardness of —"

"*Pip.*"

He stopped. The canyon filled the silence behind him, that dishonest dusk-echo, his own anger coming back at him from the wrong direction.

"I wasn't laughing at you," I said. "I was laughing because you sounded exactly like me, and hearing it out loud scared me."

He blinked. "What?"

"You stood there at the tower doing the water arithmetic out loud — cups against miles, mouths against tons — and it was word for word the speech I give myself every time a job ends and it's time to be gone before anyone starts needing me. I've been giving it since I was younger than you. So I laughed. It's what I do instead of flinching."

"That's a stupid thing to do instead of flinching."

"It's a terrible thing to do instead of flinching. I recommend almost anything else." I turned the lantern down a notch, because the light was making him squint and squinting was letting him hide. "But here's the part you got wrong, and I need you to hear it, because Ilsa will kill us both if you freeze to death before she gets to yell at you herself."

He waited. Kids always wait. It's the one negotiation they win every time.

"You're not cargo. Cargo doesn't get missed. We spent eleven hours not noticing you were gone, and I know how that sounds, so let me finish — we spent eleven hours not noticing because we've all been working ourselves stupid trying to get this wreck of a train up a river that's leaving, and not one second of those eleven hours was a second where you weren't part of the plan. You're in the ration count. You're in the watch rotation Ilsa drafted and pretends is voluntary. You're in the weight distribution for the last grade — Dain recalculated it Tuesday and put your mass in the column for *crew*, not *stores*. Nobody told you because nobody thought it needed saying. That's the failure. Not the laughing. The assuming."

His chin was doing the thing chins do when their owners are losing a fight against their own faces. "You didn't assume."

"I assumed worst of anybody. Assuming is my native language. I've never stayed anywhere long enough to find out what happens after the assuming." I looked around the little cave, at the shelves and the swept niche and the wooden bird with its unfinished wing, and I heard myself keep going, which surprised me, because I had planned to stop several sentences ago. "You want to know why? Why I run? It's not the romance of it. It's not the maps, even though the maps are good. It's that everyone I ever stayed for left first. My mother, when I was six. Oren, who didn't die so much as arrange his dying to be convenient. Every crew that folded, every town that dried up, every depot that closed while I was learning to love it. I got very fast at leaving so it would always be my idea. And then I put a fourteen-year-old on my train and forgot that he might have learned the same trick from watching me, and that when I laugh instead of answering, it teaches him the lesson I never meant to teach anybody again."

The silence after that was long. The canyon held it, echoed it back wrong, let it settle.

"You're afraid," Pip said at last, testing the words like loose rail.

"Terrified. Daily. Currently of: the dry season, the Combine, the grade ahead, and this conversation."

"Of this conversation?"

"Conversations where I say true things are the ones I've historically fled from. You'll notice I'm still here. That's new. Don't tell Ilsa; she'll want it institutionalized."

And then he laughed — wet, ugly, real — and the fight went out of him all at once, the way it does with kids, like a valve opening. He wiped his face on his sleeve with the total lack of ceremony that is childhood's one great gift, and scooted forward, and looked past me at the shrine.

"She left bread," he said quietly. "Whoever she was. You can still see it."

"I see it."

"Do you think she came back for it?"

I considered lying to him. I considered the kind version, the *of course she did* version. But he'd caught me admitting fear tonight; the account was open; I might as well spend honestly.

"I think," I said, "she left it knowing she might not. And left it anyway. Because the leaving was the point. You build the shelf, you sweep the niche, you put the bread where the next traveler can find it — and whether you come back or not, the road is warmer than it was. That's the whole religion, as far as I can tell. It's not the rocks. It's the deciding that somebody comes after you."

Pip looked at the wooden bird for a long time. Then he reached out and touched the unfinished wing, gently, the way you touch a thing you intend to finish someday.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. But I'm walking down in front, and if you laugh at me on the talus I'm pushing you off it."

"Deal. For the record, Ilsa has a speech prepared. It involves the phrase 'manifest integrity.' I'd brace."

* * *

We came down the last of the slope with the lantern swinging and the canyon giving back our footsteps from all directions at once, and I could see the camp fire below, small and orange and impossibly domestic, and three figures standing at its edge in attitudes of studied unconcern that fooled absolutely no one. Ilsa had her arms crossed. The twins had materialized on either side of her like bookends. Dain was on his feet despite the arm, and the look on his face when he saw the boy alive was the look of a man who has spent forty years mourning a country and cannot bear to lose a piece of one in miniature.

Pip stopped ten feet out. Squared up. Braced for the speech.

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Then she thrust out a bowl.

"Rice," she said. "Eat it. You're in the rotation at dawn, and if you ever scare me like that again I will reassign you to latrine detail until your beard comes in. Sit down."

He sat. The twins shifted to make room without appearing to move at all. Dain put a blanket around his shoulders with the fussy competence of a man who had clearly done it for younger siblings in another life, in another country, on the far side of an erasure.

I stood at the edge of the firelight, outside the circle, where I have historically lived.

"Felix," Ilsa said, without turning around. "Sit down. You're in the circle. That's not a vote, it's a finding of fact."

So I sat. The rice was terrible. The fire popped. Above us, invisible in the dark, a dead nation's shrine kept its swept niche and its unfinished bird, holding the warmth for whoever came next.

And that was when Dain, staring into the flames, said quietly: "Tomorrow we reach the Narrows. After that, the last gradient."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. We all felt the mountain lean closer in the dark, waiting to find out what we were made of.
