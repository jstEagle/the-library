## 16. The Offer

The radio started talking to us at dusk, which I thought was rude of it, because the caboose stove was finally warm and Ilsa had just produced a tin of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed through a boot, but a boot we'd all agreed to share.

We were tied down in a siding below the canyon narrows, waiting out the wind. High desert wind is a specific animal. It doesn't howl so much as it *insists*, like a salesman who's decided you two are friends now. The locomotive ticked and cooled. Pip was asleep in the corner with a wrench for a pillow, which I'd told him was a bad habit and then privately noted was exactly what I'd done at fifteen. Dain sat across from me sharpening a knife he had no practical use for. Ilsa had the radio log open on her knee, and she was the one who sat up first.

"Felix," she said. "We've got a carrier."

"Whose?"

"Strong signal. Directional. Somebody's got a set on the ridge above us." She turned the dial with two fingers, delicate, the way she did everything, like the equipment might apologize if she was gentle enough. "And they're asking for us by name."

"Which name?"

"Yours."

The static cleared, and a voice came through, male, unhurried, with the particular smoothness of a man who has never once had to raise it. It had the texture of good upholstery.

"Miss Reyes. Good evening. I hope I'm not interrupting dinner."

I looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at me. We had, in fact, been about to interrupt dinner.

"This is Meridian Combine, regional operations," the voice continued. "My name is Aldous Wren. I'm the director for this corridor. I understand you've had a difficult trip. The trestle, the agents who boarded you — I want you to know that wasn't sanctioned. I've handled it internally."

"Handled it how?" Dain said quietly, not into the mic. "The man with the knife?"

Wren either didn't hear him or chose not to. The radio is a coward's medium; you can say anything into it and nothing bleeds through except the words.

"I'll come to the point," Wren said, "because I respect your time, and because the dry season respects nobody's. You're carrying documents. Vaskarian survey charts, roughly forty years old, relating to the upper watershed. I'd like to buy them."

"Everyone would like to buy them," I said. "It's the season's fashion."

"Everyone else was offering you money. I'm offering you a life."

Ilsa wrote the frequency on the log. Dain had stopped sharpening the knife. Pip, miracle of miracles, was still asleep, and some protective animal part of me was glad, because I didn't want him hearing what came next, and a smaller, more honest part of me knew he'd have heard it anyway, asleep or not. Kids hear everything. That's their whole job.

"Talk," I said.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about me, since apparently I'm telling Wren, so I may as well tell you: I have wanted exactly one thing my entire adult life, and it isn't wealth, and it isn't adventure, whatever the novels would have you believe. It's a door that locks from the inside. A lease longer than a season. A place where the woman at the general store knows my name and doesn't mean it as a warning.

I learned early that staying is a thing that gets done to you by people leaving. Oren Vasquez taught me surveying, taught me gradients, taught me how to read a riverbed like a confession. And then, one spring, he taught me the last lesson without meaning to: he was gone. No note. A paid-up room and a gap on the roster and a fifteen-year-old girl with a head full of numbers and nowhere to put them. So I put them in trains, and I kept the trains moving, and I told myself motion was a kind of home.

Forty years old and I have never owned a chair.

So when Wren said *a life*, I want to be honest with you, the way I wasn't honest with him: my hands went still on the table. Not from fear.

"Here's the structure," Wren said, and I could hear paper moving on his end, the soft shuffle of a man who had rehearsed this. "The charts, and everything derived from them — copies, notes, annotations. In exchange: two hundred thousand in certified scrip, transferable anywhere. Plus a position. Meridian is reopening freight corridors across three territories. We need a chief surveyor. Salary, housing, pension. A house, Miss Reyes. With a door."

He paused, and I could have sworn I heard him smile.

"With a door that locks from the inside. Am I close?"

Ilsa's pencil had stopped. Dain was looking at me, and I made myself look at the stove instead, because there's a limit to how much of yourself you'll let a man see through a radio, and there's a different limit for the man sitting three feet away.

"You've done your homework," I said.

"I employ people whose whole job is homework. Here's mine on you: no fixed address in eleven years. No lease longer than ninety days. No emergency contact on any document, anywhere, until eight weeks ago — and then suddenly there are six names on your crew manifest." He let that sit. "You've been running a long time, Miss Reyes. I'm offering you the chance to stop. That's not a threat. That's the entire offer. The money is real. The house is real. The pension will outlive you."

"And the valley?"

"What valley?" he said, mild as milk. "There's no valley on any map I own. There's a watershed with anomalous flow data and a survey file that certain parties would prefer stayed buried, for reasons that are — I'll say this plainly — more complicated than greed. Some doors are closed because what's behind them is fragile. You of all people should appreciate that. You map closed lines. You know some of them stay closed because opening them kills people."

"And some of them stay closed because somebody got paid to close them."

"Sometimes," Wren agreed, pleasantly. "Both things can be true. That's what makes this offer generous rather than simple. I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to stop digging in a place where the digging hurts people you've never met, in exchange for a life you've wanted since you were a child." A pause. "I read your file, Miss Reyes. All of it. I know what Oren Vasquez did to you. I'm offering you the thing he never gave you."

The stove ticked. The wind insisted. And somewhere in my chest, that small, shameful, forty-year-old animal lay down in front of the fire like it belonged there.

* * *

I made him repeat the numbers. Not because I needed them twice — I have a surveyor's memory, I'd have them forever after one pass — but because I wanted to hear whether the voice changed. It didn't. That frightened me more than the knife man had. A man whose voice doesn't change when he names a fortune has named fortunes before, and will again, and considers them line items.

"Two hundred thousand," he said. "Scrip, certified, good in every territory that honors Meridian paper. Chief surveyor, corridor division. Housing in any division town you choose. And one more item, since I'm told you have a young man aboard."

The fire in my chest went out.

"Pip is fourteen," I said. "He's not on any offer."

"Everyone's on every offer; that's how offers work. Meridian runs a trade school in Corridor City. Real classrooms. Real beds. He'd learn locomotive work properly instead of picking it up from—" a pause I did not appreciate "—from whoever's been teaching him. He's a stowaway, Miss Reyes. Legally, he's a ward of whichever territory catches him first. I'm offering him a future. What are you offering him? A canyon and a war?"

I want to tell you I said something magnificent. I want to tell you the wry, unbreakable Felix Reyes came out swinging. Here's what actually happened: I looked at the kid asleep against his wrench, and I thought about a trade school with real beds, and my mouth went dry, because the offer had just stopped being about me, and I could have survived it being about me.

"Give me until morning," I said.

"Of course." No relief in his voice, no urgency. A man selling water in a desert doesn't chase the customer. "One consideration, though. The dry season. Your window on the upper river is — what, four weeks now? Five? I don't need to rush you. The river will. If you decline, I'll respect that, and we'll meet at the headwaters eventually, one way or another. If you accept, my helicopter can be at the narrows by noon." Another pause, and this one had weight in it, the kind you feel in a bridge cable. "Miss Reyes. One thing I'd ask you to hold onto tonight. Whatever the exile has told you, whatever the charts promise — Vaskaria is gone. It has been gone for forty years. Countries don't come back. People do, though. People come back all the time, when the price is right. Ask your crew what they'd come back for. Ask yourself. Then ask what happens to them if you're wrong."

He signed off clean, no static, a professional to the last, and the radio hissed back into the wind's frequency, and the caboose was very quiet.

* * *

Dain spoke first. "He knows about the valley."

"He suspects. There's a difference. Suspecting is his job." I rubbed my face. "Knowing is ours."

"You're going to take it." Not accusatory. Flat. A man reading a gradient.

"No."

"You went quiet when he mentioned the house."

"I went quiet when he mentioned the trade school," I said, and that landed on him harder than the house had landed on me, because Dain had been the one to pull Pip out of the water at the trestle, Dain had been teaching the kid Vaskarian letters on the long grades, chalk on a slate, *this is how you write home*. "That's the filthy genius of it, Dain. He didn't price the charts. He priced us. Every one of us, separately, at the thing we can't say no to. Ilsa—"

"Retirement," Ilsa said, from the radio log, without looking up. Her pencil had started moving again, tiny, precise. "He'd have found the disciplinary file from the Kessel junction. Twenty-two years of service, one mistake, dismissed without pension. He'd offer me my pension back with the word *restored* in the letter. I've imagined that letter." She looked up. Her face was calm the way a frozen river is calm. "I've imagined it for six years. That's how I know it's bait. You don't want the letter, Felix. You want the six years back. Nobody's selling those."

"And the twins?"

"Boilermakers with blacklisted union cards," Ilsa said. "He'll offer them a shop with their names over the door."

We sat with it. The wind leaned on the caboose. Pip slept on, and I found I couldn't look at him, and I found I couldn't stop looking at him, which is the same problem stated twice.

"He's right about one thing," I said finally. "I've wanted a locked door since I was fifteen years old. Two hundred thousand and a pension and a house. I have run the numbers on that life the way I run them on a grade — every night, some nights, without deciding to. It's not a temptation, Dain. It's a *plan*. It's the plan. It's been the plan my whole life, and tonight a man with a smooth voice read it back to me and offered to fund it."

"And?"

"And he made one mistake." I got up and put my hand flat on the charts case, the old Vaskarian tin, dented, river-stained, the closest thing I have to a heart. "He priced us separately. He thinks we're a crew because a manifest says so. He thinks loyalty is a line item you can outbid." I looked at Dain, and then at Ilsa, and I heard myself say the thing I'd been circling for two hundred miles, the thing that would have been unthinkable in chapter one of my life, if my life had chapters, which apparently it does. "So we don't decide separately. We don't decide at all — not me, not alone, not the way I've decided everything since Oren walked out. We put it to the crew. Every name on that manifest. What the source is worth to them. What Wren's offer is worth. And we vote."

Dain stared at me. "A vote."

"A vote. I'm a railroad engineer, not a queen. Nobody elected me to sell their future, and nobody elected me to spend it either." I almost smiled. "Besides, if I decide it myself and I'm wrong, I'll do what I always do. Leave. And I'm starting to suspect that's the one outcome Wren's really buying."

Ilsa closed the radio log. "Morning, then. Stove's warm. Wake the boy."

I looked at Pip, asleep with his wrench, fourteen years old, one trade school away from a bed that wasn't a boxcar.

"Yeah," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize. "Wake the boy. He votes too."

Outside, the wind shifted, and for a moment it carried a sound from up the canyon — faint, high, almost like water. Almost like the river, still running, still running, somewhere ahead of us in the dark, toward a valley that wasn't on any map, holding its breath to find out what we were worth.
