## 17. Night Vote

**FELIX**

The caboose smelled like woodsmoke, boiled coffee, and the particular despair of a man who had been eating Ilsa's cooking for two weeks. That last part was mostly Pip, who had made a face at the stew and then eaten three bowls of it, because teenage boys are constitutionally incapable of consistency.

We were camped on a shelf of cut bank below the canyon walls, maybe a day and a half from the headwaters if the gradient didn't kill us first, and everyone knew it. You could feel it in how quiet they were. Not scared-quiet. Weighed-down quiet. The kind of quiet that comes when people have been carrying something separately and finally realize they're all carrying the same thing.

I'd spent the whole climb up the dead line telling myself I'd decide alone. That was my method. It was practically a religion: Felix Reyes, patron saint of unilateral decisions made in moving vehicles. Oren taught me that too, though he never said it out loud. He just lived it — every problem solved before anyone else could be burdened with it, every door slipped through quietly, every goodbye pre-written.

But sitting there with five faces lit orange by the stove, I found I couldn't do it. Not because I'd gone soft. Because I'd done the math, and the math was ugly either way, and ugly math deserves witnesses.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we get to the top of this grade, and after that it's the valley. The source. And what we do when we find it — that's not an engineering problem."

Ilsa snorted into her mug. "First honest thing you've said since Salta."

"It's true," I said. "I've been pretending otherwise. Pretending there's a technical answer to whether we sell these charts or publish them. There isn't. So." I set the ledger down on the crate between us — Oren's ledger, the charts, the whole buried country in a bundle of oilcloth. "We vote. Everyone here gets a say. Including you, Pip, and don't make that face, you've earned worse privileges than this one."

Pip's face did something complicated. Thirteen-year-olds aren't built for being taken seriously; it short-circuits them.

"And me?" Dain asked quietly, from his corner by the door. He always sat by the door. Fourteen years of exile will teach a man to know where the exits are even in a moving train.

"You especially," I said. "It's your country."

"It stopped being a country," he said. "That's rather the point."

"Then it's your non-country. Vote anyway."

* * *

**DAIN**

I want to record, for whoever finds this account, that I did not expect her to do it. Put it to a vote. In my experience — and my experience is long, and mostly consists of committees failing to save anything — decisions about Vaskaria were always made by someone else. The treaty signatories. The border commissions. The men with the ink stamps who dissolved forty years of language and song between lunch and supper. Nobody ever asked a Vaskarian what should happen to Vaskaria. They asked each other, in rooms we weren't in, and then sent the bill.

And here was this railroad woman — a stranger four months ago, a person I followed for six weeks before working up the courage to speak to her — putting the fate of my homeland on a crate next to a dented coffeepot and asking a stowaway child to weigh in.

I spoke last, by choice. I wanted to hear the others first. This is also new for me; usually I talk first and regret it in the traditional order.

Ilsa went first. She held her mug in both hands like it was warm, though the coffee had been cold for an hour, and she looked at the stove instead of at us, which is how she looks at everything difficult.

"I was a dispatcher for nineteen years," she said. "You all know why I'm not anymore. I moved trains on paper while real ones burned. I signed the routing that put the Meridian ore run on the same stretch as the passenger special, and eleven people died, and the inquiry called it 'systemic failure,' which is a word they invented so no single name has to carry it. But I carried it anyway. Every day since." She turned the mug a quarter turn. "The source means a chance to put one true thing back on the map. One thing where the official record and the actual world match. I've spent nine years living inside a lie the paperwork tells. I'd like to spend whatever's left helping kill one." She paused. "Publish. Obviously. But I wanted to say why properly, because Ilsa Varga doesn't get to hide behind 'obviously' anymore. That was the old Ilsa."

Nobody clapped. Clapping would have ruined it. But the twins both reached over at the same moment, without looking, and refilled her mug, which among boilermakers is roughly equivalent to a standing ovation.

Then the twins. They insisted on going together, which they insist on for everything, including, once, a medical examination.

"Bran?"

"Borin."

"We're publishing," Bran said.

"Because our father worked the Vaskarian freight concession," Borin said, "before the dissolution, and he used to sing songs in a language he told us was 'just work slang.'"

"And it wasn't," Bran said. "It was Vaskarian. We found that out from Dain's phrasebook last month and neither of us has slept right since."

"There were words," Borin said, "for things we didn't have words for. Grief with weather in it. Home as a debt."

"So somebody kept those words alive somewhere," Bran finished, "up in that valley, or the charts are wrong. And if they're right, you don't sell the last speakers of a language to a mining concern for a retirement package."

"That's the whole statement," Borin said. "We're boilermakers. Brevity is structural."

Then Pip. And I confess I braced myself, because the boy treats gravity like a coat he keeps leaving on chairs. But he pulled his knees up and looked at the fire and was silent so long that Felix opened her mouth, and then he spoke.

"My mum worked domestic service in Corvane. For a family that had money from — she never said where from, but I did the reading, and it was Meridian money, same as always. She cleaned their floors and they paid her in a currency that got devalued twice, and when I was born she couldn't register me because her papers were tied to her employer, and the employer wouldn't release them, because that's legal there." He shrugged, too casually, the way kids do when casualness is load-bearing. "So I'm not officially a person anywhere. I learned trains because trains go places paperwork can't follow." He looked up. "If there's a valley full of people nobody could erase — people who just... stayed, and hid, and kept being Vaskarian for forty years under everybody's nose — then that means the erasure can fail. It means they didn't win. I want to see it with my own eyes. And I want everyone else to see it too, so the next kid like me knows it's possible." He swallowed. "Publish. And if anyone votes sell, I'll understand, but I'll be mad about it in a very mature way for a long time."

Then it was me.

I had prepared remarks. Of course I had. I am a man who has given eulogies in three languages for a country that still technically exists on no map at all; remarks are how I breathe. But sitting there, looking at the oilcloth bundle, I found the remarks had left me.

"When the dissolution came," I said, "I was twenty-two. My father was a surveyor — a real one, government-trained, the irony is not lost on me — and he was one of the men who drew the original charts of the upper river. Some of those sheets are in that bundle. His hand is on them. When the order came down to seal the source and strike the valley from the record, he obeyed, because obedience was survival, and then he drank himself to death over eleven years, which was also survival, of a sort." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it does. "I have spent fourteen years collecting fragments. Songs. Recipes. A dialect dictionary compiled by a schoolteacher who died before she could print it. I have a suitcase of a country, gentlemen and ladies. That is what exile is: you become luggage."

Pip made a small sound. I let it pass.

"If we sell, the Combine will bury the valley deeper than the treaty ever did. They'll buy the silence and amortize it. The people up there will spend another generation unseen, and I will add the sheets to my suitcase, and someday someone will inherit my luggage." I looked at Felix. "But I want to say something inconvenient, because a vote that only hears one side isn't a vote, it's a coronation. Selling would keep these people safe. That is a real argument. Publicity is a flood; floods drown things. Forty years of hiding is a kind of shelter, and we would be tearing the roof off, and some of them may curse us for it. I believe the truth is worth it. I believe a life in hiding is half a life. But I want it recorded that I know the cost, and that I would pay it with other people's roofs, and that I say so anyway, because the alternative is letting a mining company own the last word on my father's handwriting."

Silence, then. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the river ran low over its stones, getting shallower by the hour, keeping our schedule for us.

Which left Felix.

* * *

**FELIX**

They all looked at me, and I'll be honest: for about ten seconds I seriously considered opening the door and walking into the canyon. Just briefly. As a thought experiment.

"I found the chest," I said. "So I guess I start. Here's what it means to me, and I'm going to say it straight because you all just did me the courtesy of saying it straight, and Ilsa will mock me forever if I don't."

"Already drafting the material," Ilsa confirmed.

"The chest means Oren." I put my hand flat on the ledger. "Oren Vasquez taught me everything. How to read water. How to read rail. How to leave a town at dawn without waking anyone, which he framed as a professional skill and which I now recognize was a symptom. He was the closest thing to family I ever consented to having, and he's dead, and I can't ask him why he sealed a spring-fed valley full of living people and then taught a girl to run from every place she might have put down roots. Maybe running was his penance. Maybe he was training me to be free. Maybe he was just making sure I'd never stay anywhere long enough to dig up what he'd buried." My voice cracked on that last one and I let it, because we were doing this properly. "Selling the charts would pay me enough to stop. Forever. A house. A garden, even, God help me. Everything I've pretended I didn't want, priced out in one clean transaction. And I'd take it, except—" I looked around the caboose. At the dispatcher who'd rebuilt herself out of wreckage. At the twins singing grief-with-weather-in-it. At the boy with no country and the man with a suitcase full of one. "Except I already have the thing the money was supposed to buy. I just hadn't noticed, because I was moving too fast. So. Publish. That's my vote. And now we count."

"Before we count," Dain said, "procedure. Majority?"

"Majority," I said. "Felix Reyes, chairing her first committee. If it ties, the tie goes to the river, meaning we stall until the dry season makes the decision for us, which is the most engineer answer available."

Hands. Ilsa, first, no hesitation, arm straight up like she was answering a roll call at last. The twins, both hands each, because they don't understand how voting works or refuse to. Pip, both hands also, plus one foot, until Ilsa told him to stop showing off.

Dain raised his hand slowly, and I watched him do it — watched a man raise his hand for Vaskaria for the first time in fourteen years, in a rusted caboose that smelled of burnt coffee, and I saw him feel the weight of it land, and I saw him choose not to put it back down.

Five.

Six, counting mine, though I'd already voted out loud, because apparently I run a sloppy committee.

 unanimous. Nobody sold anybody. Pip whooped and Ilsa shushed him and the twins shook hands with each other across the stove, which settled nothing and pleased them immensely.

"One more thing," I said, before the noise could become permanent. "Whatever happens tomorrow — helicopters, lawyers, guns, whatever the Combine sends — this stays what it was tonight. Six people around a stove deciding together. Whatever they take from us, they don't get to take the fact that we decided it ourselves. Agreed?"

There was a general mumble of agreement, which in crew language is a solemn oath.

* * *

**DAIN**

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls and the stove had burned down to a red eye, Felix sat across from me sharpening a hand-spike with a file, which is not what hand-spikes are for, but I have learned not to question her methods, only their terrifying results.

"You knew they'd all vote publish," I said. "You stacked the room."

"I stacked nothing. I hired strays. Strays skew honest."

"You could have decided alone. It would have been faster."

She filed a few more strokes, considering. "My mentor decided alone," she said finally. "Look how that turned out. Forty years of hidden people and a dead man's guilt and a chest in a culvert. Alone-decisions have a half-life, Dain. They rot into other people's problems." She glanced up. "Besides. First decision I've ever made with people instead of ahead of them. Wanted to see if it worked."

"And?"

"And we'll know tomorrow." She stood, stretched until her spine crackled, and nodded west, toward the black shoulder of the canyon. "Get some sleep. Last gradient's steeper than the locomotive's got any right to attempt, and I've got an idea about how we're going to manage it that's going to require every pair of hands on this train and probably a miracle."

"What kind of idea?"

She smiled, and it was the smile she wore going into impossible grades, which is to say it had teeth in it.

"The kind," she said, "where we push."
