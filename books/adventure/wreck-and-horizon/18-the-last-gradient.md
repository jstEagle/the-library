## 18. The Last Gradient

The grade announced itself before we saw it — a change in the sound of the river, a tightening in the canyon walls, the way Pip stopped talking mid-sentence and just stared up the line like it had personally insulted him.

"Is that real?" he said.

"That's real," Ilsa said grimly from the cab.

The track ahead climbed out of the canyon floor at what my instruments insisted was eleven percent. Eleven percent. For context: mainline freight grades run one to two percent, and engineers write angry letters about those. Mountain logging spurs might touch four or five if the timber barons were feeling suicidal. Eleven was not a railroad grade. Eleven was a suggestion that gravity should mind its own business.

I stood on the pilot beam with the wind coming down the canyon cold enough to bite through my coat, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for three days. The *Meridian Star* — our derelict work train, our stubborn, rusting, beautiful argument against every reasonable person's advice — weighed forty-one tons dry. We were not dry. Water tanks full, because water was the whole point of everything now. Coal, food, tools, Dain's crate of Vaskarian documents he treated like scripture, Pip's hoard of scavenged brass fittings nobody had the heart to make him throw out. Call it fifty tons climbing an eleven percent grade.

Our little saddle-tank locomotive could pull maybe six hundred pounds of tractive effort per ton of her own weight on dry rail, and she weighed thirty-eight tons herself, and half our drivers were worn slick as river stones.

"You can't," Ilsa said, when I told her the number. Not cruelly. Just the way you tell someone it's raining.

"I know."

"Then why are you smiling?"

Because there was a version of this problem with a solution, and the solution was us. All of us. Every pound of human being willing to put a shoulder into iron.

* * *

We held a council of war on the footplate, which is to say six people crammed into a space built for three, arguing over the hiss of the safety valve.

"The math doesn't work," Ilsa said again. She'd been a dispatcher for nineteen years before one bad night ended her career, and she trusted numbers the way some people trust God. "Adhesion alone won't do it. We slip, we slide back, we roll into the canyon, and they'll find us in the spring all mixed together like stew."

"So we don't rely on adhesion." I'd been turning it over since the trestle, since the sabotage, since the night vote around the caboose stove where everyone said their piece and I learned that a decision made with people weighs different than one made alone. Lighter, somehow. Easier to carry. "We spike the wheels."

Dain frowned. He'd spent forty years in exile learning to be careful; improvisation sat on him like a wet coat. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning we stop pretending this is a train and admit it's a siege engine. Hand-spikes between the spokes. You lock a wheel, the engine strains against it, you advance the spike, lock again. Ratchet by ratchet. Old logging crews did it. Old mountain crews did it. It's slow and it's brutal and it works."

"It's insane," Ilsa said.

"It's also the only option that ends with us alive at the top," I said, "so I'm going to need everyone to be insane with me."

Pip raised his hand like we were in school. "What about weight? Fifty tons is too heavy, right? So make it less than fifty tons."

Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, fifteen years old and unbothered, the way only teenagers and cats can be.

"He's right," I said slowly, and the plan assembled itself in my head the way good plans do — not invented but discovered, like it had been waiting under the gravel. "We strip her. Everything that isn't needed for the climb comes off and goes up on foot ahead of us. Coal we carry by hand. Water we can't spare, so the tanks stay, but tools, crates, spare iron, the caboose itself — off. We lighten the engine until she's barely herself, and then we push her up the hill with our own backs."

"And the crew?" Dain asked. "Who drives while the rest push?"

"I drive. Ilsa fires. The twins and Pip and you push and spike." I looked around the footplate at them — the disgraced dispatcher who hadn't touched a throttle in years and would tonight, the exile who'd crossed an ocean and a lifetime to see a valley he'd never seen, two boilermaker twins named Brann and Bren who communicated mostly in grunts and finished each other's welds, and a stowaway who'd solved in ten seconds what I'd been chewing for three days. "Any objections?"

"Yes," said Ilsa. "Several. Well-founded ones." She rolled up her sleeves. "Let's go."

* * *

Stripping a locomotive at eight thousand feet as the sun goes down is a special kind of misery, and I recommend it to no one. We unbolted anything that would forgive a wrench. Brann and Bren took the caboose apart with cutting torches until it was flat pieces lashed onto a handcar, which they'd send up ahead on the flange of the grade where the line leveled for forty yards. Dain carried his document crate on his back like penance. Pip organized the coal into sacks sized to a boy's shoulders, then lied about how much each one weighed, then got caught lying, then kept doing it anyway.

By full dark the *Star* stood twenty-nine tons instead of fifty, naked and gleaming where paint had scraped away, looking less like a locomotive than a skeleton that had decided to keep working anyway.

I gave the safety briefing standing on a tie, lantern overhead, five faces turned up to me. It was the shortest speech of my life and the hardest to get through without my voice cracking, though I managed it, mostly.

"Rules are simple. Nobody stands downhill of a wheel. Nobody puts a hand where they wouldn't put their face. When I whistle twice, spike and hold. When I whistle once, advance. If she slips —" I paused. "If she slips, you let go and jump clear, and you do not be a hero, because dead heroes don't get to see valleys. Are we understood?"

Five nods. Somewhere below us, the river ran thin and cold, already shrinking toward its dry-season bones. Six weeks had become nine days somewhere along the line without anyone signing the paperwork.

I climbed into the cab. Ilsa stood at the firebox door, sleeves pinned, hair tied back under a rag, looking like she'd been born in a locomotive cab and had merely been exiled from it for nineteen years.

"Ready?" I asked.

"No," she said, and opened the firebox, and fed it like she'd never left.

* * *

Here is what pushing a train uphill by hand through the night actually feels like, for the record, since no ballad will ever get it right:

It feels like the world reduced to a single repeated decision. Shoulder against the timber baulk we'd braced across the pilot beam. Feet finding purchase on ties slick with frost. The count — Brann counting, always Brann, a low gravel rhythm, *push — hold — ease — set* — and the spike going in between wheel spokes with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then me opening the regulator a quarter turn, and the whole machine straining against its own locked wheels with a shudder you felt in your teeth, and then the spike advancing one notch, one notch, one notch, and the train moving upward at the speed of continental drift.

Forty yards an hour, at first. Then thirty, as the grade steepened and the air thinned and the frost turned to ice on the rails and we had to sand every foot of it by hand, walking ahead with buckets like sowers planting a crop of friction.

Nobody complained. That was the strange part. I kept waiting for it — the mutiny, the breaking point, the moment someone looked at the black drop beside the rails and asked the obvious question of why. Pip sang instead, terrible songs, songs he made up on the spot with lyrics about spikes and wheels and Ilsa's temper, and Ilsa threatened him with the shovel, and the threat was their love language and everyone knew it. Dain pushed in silence, steady as tide, and once, passing me at a spike-change, he said quietly: "My grandfather moved a church bell over three mountains this way. Forty men, six days. He said afterward that he could never again believe in impossible things, because he had personally carried one up a hill."

"What happened to the bell?"

"It rings in a village that isn't on any map anymore." He set his shoulder back to the timber. "Tonight it starts ringing again."

I didn't have words for that, so I whistled twice, and we spiked, and we held.

* * *

Hour four, the moon went behind cloud and the cold came down like a lid. Hour five, Bren's spike slipped — not his fault, the spoke was cracked inside where rust had been quietly eating the wheel for decades — and the wheel kicked backward a quarter turn and took the skin off his palm to the wrist before he let go, exactly like trained, exactly like I'd ordered, jumping clear while the baulk swung free.

We stopped. Bandaged him by lantern light. He insisted on staying on the crew, wrapping the hand in tar canvas and holding spikes with the other arm like a man loading a crossbow.

"Twins share pain," Brann said, deadpan. "He feels nothing. I feel everything. It's very unfair."

"You feel nothing either," Bren said.

"True. Carry on."

Hour seven, the regulator packing blew — a jet of scalding steam straight across the cab — and Ilsa, without hesitation, without a word, wrapped her forearm in a wet sack and reached past the jet to cinch the valve shut while I killed the fire. Afterward she sat on the cab floor breathing hard, and I crouched next to her, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we were both thinking the same thing: nineteen years ago, one bad night, one frozen interlocking tower, one career ended by a system that preferred scapegoats to repairs. And here she was, burned and bleeding and still standing watch over a machine everyone else had written off.

"They were wrong about you," I said.

She looked at me with steam-wet eyes. "So were you, when you hired me."

"I hired you because you were the best person available."

"You hired me because nobody else would come." A ghost of a smile. "Same thing, out here."

* * *

Hour nine. The grade eased for a blessed forty yards — the shelf where the handcar waited with the stripped cargo — and we collapsed against the warm side of the boiler and ate cold beans and watched the sky start to consider dawn.

That was when Pip found me sitting apart, the way I still sometimes did, old habit, checking the perimeter of my own company.

"Felix." He sat down next to me without asking, which was new, and which I allowed. "Can I ask you something dumb?"

"You've asked me plenty of dumb things. Consistency is a virtue."

"Why are you smiling? Like — actually. Everyone's bleeding and freezing and we're going like ten feet an hour, and you keep doing this—" he mimed my face, badly, "—like weird quiet smile thing."

I considered lying to him. I've talked myself out of tighter spots than a teenager's question. But the night had burned most of my lies off, the way a hot fire burns off scale, leaving bare metal underneath.

"Because three months ago I mapped closed lines for a living," I said. "Bridges scheduled for demolition. Stations nobody remembered. I wrote down endings for a living, Pip, and I told myself that was honest work, and really it was just — practicing leaving. Getting good at goodbye before goodbye ever asked." I turned my cup of beans in my hands. "And tonight I'm pushing a dead train up a hill that says it can't be done, with a disgraced dispatcher and a pair of grunting twins and a runaway kid and a man whose country lives in a crate, and every single one of them keeps showing up for the next spike. That's not a crew. That's the opposite of everything I ever mapped."

Pip chewed on that. "So the smile is 'cause you're happy."

"The smile is because I'm terrified and grateful and I can't tell the difference anymore, and I've decided that counts as happiness."

"Huh." He nodded seriously. "That's the dumbest thing I ever heard."

"Consistency," I said, "is a virtue."

* * *

The last mile broke something in all of us and made something else in its place.

The grade steepened past twelve percent in the final switchback cut, rock walls close enough to touch on both sides, the rails glazed with rime that sand couldn't fully beat. The *Star* stalled twice — truly stalled, wheels spiked, drivers slipping in place with a scream of steel that echoed off the canyon like something dying. Both times, we backed her down three feet by hand-spike, re-sanded, re-set, and tried again, and both times she took the grade on the third attempt because Brann changed the count — slower, longer holds, letting the boiler build pressure between surges — because a boilermaker knows engines the way shepherds know weather.

At the worst of it, all five of them pushed at once, timber baulk and hand-spikes and bare hands on the pilot beam itself, Dain shouting something in Vaskarian that I didn't understand and understood completely, Pip wedged at the twin's shoulder adding his whole small furious weight, Ilsa feeding the fire like the furnace was a hungry god that answered to her specifically.

And the train moved.

Not fast. Not gracefully. A foot at a time, a spike at a time, an act of collective stubbornness so pure it felt almost religious. Impossible things, I have come to believe, are rarely destroyed. They're dismantled — into hours, into shoulders, into individual decisions to take one more step — and if you refuse to stop reassembling them, they eventually have to give in.

The summit came at 5:47 in the morning, though none of us knew the time then. One last surge, one last spike pulled free and tossed down the bank like a discarded crutch, and the *Star* crested the divide and ran level for the first time in nine hours, and the momentum of it nearly knocked us all off our feet, laughing, staggering, catching each other.

Below us, the canyon we'd climbed out of filled with grey first light. Ahead of us, the land fell away toward the headwaters — toward the valley, toward the source, toward whatever truth we'd been carrying uphill in a rusting boiler all this way.

Nobody cheered, exactly. Brann sat down on the rail and started laughing and couldn't stop. Bren wrapped his bandaged hand around his brother's shoulder. Ilsa leaned out of the cab and let the cold air hit her burned arm and sighed like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried for nineteen years. Pip lay flat on the tender lid, arms spread, making snow angels in coal dust.

Dain stood at the head of the train, facing downhill into the light, and after a long moment he began, very quietly, to sing. A few lines, low and rough, in a language erased from every atlas I ever trusted. The others went still to listen, even the twins.

"What's it say?" Pip whispered to me.

I'd picked up fragments of Vaskarian over the weeks — enough road signs, not enough sermons. But I knew this much.

"It says the road was long," I said, "and we walked it anyway."

Then the radio crackled.

It had been silent for two days — I'd assumed the Combine had given up, or lost us, or run out of helicopters. The voice that came through was smooth and unhurried and terribly awake for the hour, and it belonged to Director Vane, and she sounded almost friendly.

"Good morning, Miss Reyes. Congratulations on the climb. Truly — I've been listening to your wheel-slip for six hours. Magnificent stubbornness." A pause, silk over wire. "Now, before you reach the valley — we should talk about what happens when you get there. I'm landing in ninety minutes. Bring the charts to the meadow, and this time, I'll make you an offer you can actually live with."

I looked at my crew. My impossible, bleeding, laughing crew, lit gold by a sunrise they had personally hauled up a mountain.

"Vane's coming," I said. "Ninety minutes."

Ilsa spat over the firebox. Pip sat up in his coal dust. Dain folded his singing voice away like a letter he'd read again later.

"Then we'd better get there first," he said.

And the *Star*, light as her skeleton, ran down the far slope toward the source with the whole sky opening ahead of her.
