## 19. Source

I have spent forty years — no, let me be honest, fifty-one years, since I was four and my mother carried me over a border in a flour sack — looking for a country that does not exist. That is the joke of my life, the one I tell at funerals and weddings when the wine is bad: *Dain Kovic, professional geographer of nowhere.* I have memorized the shape of Vaskaria the way other men memorize the faces of dead wives. I can draw its coastline on a napkin. I know where the mountains were before the treaty commission redrew them as someone else's mountains.

And this morning, at the top of a gradient that should have killed us all, I looked down into a valley that was not on any map I have ever drawn, and it looked exactly like home.

* * *

The last kilometer we walked, because even Felix Reyes would not ask a locomotive to do what came next. The track ended at a rockfall older than I am, granite spilled across the right-of-way like a dropped dinner, and beyond it the line simply stopped — rails vanishing into meadow grass, ties soft with sixty years of moss, as if the railroad had lain down to sleep and never been told it could wake up.

Felix went first, of course. She always goes first; it is her only real flaw, if you don't count stubbornness, secrecy, and the way she holds a coffee cup like she's daring it to betray her. She climbed the rockfall in her engineer's boots without once looking for a better route, which is either courage or contempt for geology, and at the top she stopped so suddenly that Pip walked into her back.

"Kovic," she said. "You'd better see this."

I want it recorded that I did not run. A man of my age and dignity does not run toward his homeland. What I actually did was scramble up the rocks on all fours like a man half my age and twice my desperation, tearing my good coat, and arrive at the crest breathing like a bellows, and there it was.

The valley opened below us like a held breath finally released. The river — our river, the one we had fought upriver for six weeks, the one that had shrunk from a beast to a thread in the canyon — came out of the ground here whole and confident, rising from a hundred springs that seamed the valley floor with silver. Water everywhere. Water standing in the meadows, water singing under tussocks of sedge, water welling up in pools so clear they looked like holes in the world. And around the springs, terraced gardens climbing the lower slopes in old, patient curves. Stone walls. Smoke from chimneys. Orchards of apples and something darker — plums, maybe, or the sour cherries my grandmother used to preserve in brandy until the jars themselves got drunk.

And people. Small figures in the fields, bent over rows of greens. A woman beating a rug on a fence rail. Two children chasing a dog along an irrigation ditch, their voices carrying up to us thin and bright as wire.

Nobody down there knew yet that they had been found. They were just living their Tuesday morning.

"Dain." Felix's voice was careful, the way it gets when she's watching someone stand near an edge. "Is it—"

"Yes," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, which was undignified, but I have decided to allow myself this one indignity per decade. "It's the upper Vaska. It's the headwaters. It's—" I gestured at all of it, the smoke, the orchards, the impossible ordinary morning, and discovered I couldn't finish the sentence, because the sentence was *it's still here,* and I had been saying *it's gone* for so long that the words had grown into the walls of my throat like roots.

Pip broke the silence, because Pip always breaks the silence; the boy has the emotional timing of a dropped wrench. "There's a lot of *people,*" he said. "You said hidden. I thought hidden meant, like, empty."

"So did I," I admitted.

That was the truth of it, and the ache inside the awe. For two generations I had imagined this place as a museum — a preserved country, a valley of ghosts keeping the language alive among the graves. I had built my whole exile around the idea that Vaskaria survived only as memory, carried by people like me who remembered it wrong and grieved it badly. I had prepared speeches. I had, God help me, rehearsed a eulogy for a nation, planning to deliver it to an empty valley.

Instead there were children arguing with a dog about a stick.

* * *

We went down. Of course we went down. Felix checked the rockfall for a passable route for the locomotive — "because I'm not leaving my engine up there to be snowed on by winter, Kovic, don't look at me like that" — and then we descended through the terraces, and the dog found us first.

It was a shaggy brown thing of indeterminate breed and total conviction, and it announced us to the entire valley with the authority of a border official. By the time we reached the lowest wall, a dozen people had come out of the fields and doorways to look at us, and I understood immediately that we were the strangest thing they had seen in years, possibly decades.

They were dressed like farmers anywhere. Boots, canvas, sun hats, a man in a cardigan with both elbows gone. But when the oldest of them spoke — a woman with a face like a walnut and eyes that missed nothing — she spoke in Vaskarian.

"*Ko ste? Otkud ste?*" Who are you? From where do you come?

My mother's tongue. The tongue I dream in and haven't spoken aloud to a stranger in thirty years, because strangers were dangerous and the language itself was evidence. I answered before I thought, the words coming out rusty and too loud:

"*Ja sam Dain Kovic. Iz Vaskarije.*"

From Vaskaria. Which no longer existed, except apparently it did, except apparently it was standing in front of me wearing a cardigan with no elbows.

The old woman looked at me for a long moment. Then she said something that undid me completely, something small and practical and devastating: "*Hladni ste. Dođite.*" You're cold. Come inside.

Not weeping. Not falling on my neck. Not singing anthems. Just — come inside, you're cold, there's soup. Because that is what survival looks like after two generations: it stops being a tragedy performed daily and becomes a habit of hospitality. They had kept the extraordinary secret alive by making it ordinary. The language wasn't a monument down here; it was just how you told someone the stew was ready.

I followed her into a stone house that smelled of woodsmoke and onions, and I stood in the doorway like an idiot while my eyes adjusted, and then I saw the shelf above the hearth.

On it, between jars of preserves and a chipped saint whose name I've forgotten, sat a double-headed eagle carved from lindenwood. Crude. Child-made, maybe. Wings uneven, one eye worn smooth by thumbs.

The seal from the chest. The seal from the charts. The seal that had gotten men shot and rivers renamed and a country deleted — sitting above a fireplace holding up nothing, guarding a shelf of plum jam.

"You know what it is," the old woman said, following my gaze. Not a question.

"I've spent my life on it," I said.

She nodded slowly, unsurprised, and ladled out soup, and that was the moment the ache arrived properly — not as grief but as vertigo. All those years I had mourned a nation, and the nation had been busy. Planting. Preserving. Teaching children letters in a script forbidden three borders away. Keeping the eagle on the mantel where it belonged: not on flags, not on chests full of doomed surveys, but over the fire, where a country actually lives.

* * *

By evening, word had traveled the valley faster than we had, and the low room of the largest house — a meeting hall, though they didn't call it that; they called it "the big kitchen," which tells you everything about how Vaskaria survived — held perhaps forty people, and every one of them wanted to know about the chest.

Felix laid the survey charts out on the long table, weighted at the corners with jam jars, and I watched the faces of the valley read them. These were the original documents, the ones Oren Vasquez had annotated in his careful hand, the ones the Meridian Combine had offered her a fortune for and would kill for now. To the syndicate they were an asset. To the cartographers who would eventually come, they were proof. But to these people they were something else entirely: they were the reason.

An old man named Stjepan — he claimed, with a straight face, to be ninety-four, and nobody dared check — traced the boundary lines with a finger gone yellow at the nail. "This is why grandfather never left," he said. "He was one of the ones who moved the stones. When the surveyors came, before the dissolution, the valley sent men up to move the boundary cairns. Made the source look like it rose outside the line. Outside anyone's line." He tapped the chart. "Your Mr. Vasquez drew what we told him to draw. Then he drew the truth underneath, where only we could find it."

Felix went very still beside me. I felt it more than saw it; she has a way of going still that pulls the air out of a room, like a train entering a tunnel.

"He hid it," she said. "In his own survey. He falsified the official record and buried the true one — and then he sealed the valley off the maps entirely, so no one would ever come looking for the correction."

"To protect us," Stjepan said simply. "A lie on paper. A life in the valley. We knew the trade we were making." He looked around the big kitchen, at the children dozing against their mothers' shoulders, at the young men who had never seen a city, at the women who had buried the old ones and taught the new ones. "Some of us thought it was a bad trade. Most of us thought it was the only trade. Nobody thought about a third option, because there wasn't one. Until your train came up our river."

I looked at Felix. She was staring at Oren Vasquez's annotations — the handwriting she'd known since she was nineteen, the hand that had taught her to read gradients and water tables and, God forgive him, how to leave — and I watched her arrive somewhere painful. Her mentor had not merely betrayed the truth. He had made a bargain with it, the same bargain this valley had made: safety purchased with silence. And she had spent six weeks cursing him for it, and now she was sitting in the house his lie had sheltered, eating bread baked in its shadow.

"I need to say something," Felix said finally, "and I need everyone to understand I haven't decided it yet." She straightened the charts, squaring their edges with a precision that meant her hands needed the work. "There are people behind us with money and guns and helicopters. They will come here. Soon. And when they do, they'll offer this valley a version of the same deal Oren made. Silence for safety. Or worse — they won't offer anything. They'll just take." She looked up. "I can sell these charts and buy you time. Or I can publish them and bring the whole world down on your heads — journalists, surveyors, governments, all of it. Light. And everything light attracts."

Silence in the big kitchen. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside the dog was losing an argument with a goat.

It was the old woman from the first house — Marta, I learned her name was Marta, and I learned it because she corrected me when I thanked her formally, saying *we're past formal, boy, you cried in my soup* — who answered.

"My mother hid in this valley for eleven years before I was born," Marta said. "She used to say: a secret kept is a debt owed to everyone who doesn't know it. We have paid that debt for sixty years." She folded her hands. "Publish it. Let the debt come due. We'll pay it together — but we stop paying alone."

Around the table, one by one, heads nodded. Not unanimously — I counted three faces that stayed doubtful, and I honored them silently, because doubt is also a Vaskarian tradition — but the valley had spoken, and it had spoken forward.

I put my hand flat on the oldest chart, over the spring marked in Oren Vasquez's secret ink, and I felt fifty-one years of looking backward pivot, creaking, toward something ahead.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the big kitchen had emptied and the valley slept the way valleys sleep, completely, having earned it — I walked out to the lowest spring and sat on the wall beside it.

The water came up out of the earth without effort, the way it must have for ten thousand years, indifferent to treaties and syndicates and the grief of exiles. I put my hand in it. It was cold enough to hurt, which felt correct.

Felix found me there. She does that — appears without announcing herself, moves quiet as rolling stock on greased rail. She sat down on the wall without asking, which I have decided to take as a compliment.

"You knew," she said. "All those years on the road, tracking rumors. You weren't sure this place existed."

"No," I said. "I hoped. Hoping is cheaper than knowing. Knowing costs everything."

"And now you know."

"Now I know." I watched the spring breathe. "Do you want to hear something absurd, Reyes? I spent my whole life drawing maps of a country that was erased, and I thought the work was mourning. Turns out the work was reconnaissance. Every dead-end rumor, every forged document, every night in some archive reading atlases with holes in them — it was all just the long approach to this wall. To tonight." I laughed, and it came out wet. "I'm a surveyor. I just didn't know what I was surveying for."

Felix was quiet for a while. Down the valley, a window went dark; someone checking on livestock, or unable to sleep for the same reason I couldn't.

"They'll come tomorrow," she said at last. "Or the day after. The Combine doesn't travel slow when there's this much money on the ground." She turned the coffee cup she wasn't drinking from — she carries one everywhere, empty, like a talisman; I've never asked, and now I never will, because some things you learn by sitting next to a person long enough. "When they come, they'll point cameras at me and contracts at you. Whatever happens tomorrow — whatever I decide, whatever gets torn up on camera — I want you to know the charts were never mine to sell. I figured that out somewhere in the canyon. Maybe you already knew it about yourself."

"I suspected," I said. "Suspecting is cheaper than knowing."

She almost smiled. In Felix Reyes, that's a parade.

Then she stood, brushed off her coat, and looked east down the dark valley, toward the river, toward the world we'd climbed out of. And her whole body changed — the stillness going taut, the engineer reading a grade she didn't like.

"Kovic," she said quietly. "How far is it, by rail, to the junction where the Combine's helicopter would stage?"

"Six hours' flight. Eighteen by road. Why?"

"Because Ilsa just signaled the caboose." She nodded back toward the black bulk of the mountain, where our train lay sleeping above the rockfall, and I saw it now — a lantern swinging at the caboose rail, three short, one long. The signal we'd agreed on, the one that meant *company coming.*

"Three short, one long," Felix said. "Headlights on the dead line, moving fast, and they're not ours."

The spring sang on, cold and indifferent, and somewhere down the valley a dog began to bark.
