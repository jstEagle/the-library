## 22. Wreck and Horizon

The valley town has a name now. That was the first thing they had to decide, before the journalists left, before the helicopters stopped coming — before anything, really, because you can't put a place on a map without a name, and the whole point, as Dain kept saying with the stubbornness of a man who'd spent forty years watching his country get erased, was to put the place on the map.

They argued about it for three weeks. The elders wanted to call it Vaskar's Rest. The younger families wanted something that didn't sound like a gravestone. In the end it was Pip — thirteen years old, permanently filthy, constitutionally incapable of letting a silence go unbroken — who said, "Why not just call it what it is? It's a spring town. Call it Source."

The elders hated it. The elders hated it so much they adopted it immediately, which is how you can tell a name is right. Nobody wants to admit a teenager solved it.

So: Source, population two hundred and eleven at the last count, two hundred and twelve if you count the dog, which Ilsa does, loudly, at every opportunity. One general store, one school with a bell salvaged from a closed station two hundred miles down the dead line, one garden that used to be a helicopter landing pad and is now, thanks to Dain Kovic and his criminal patience with soil, the most aggressively productive half-acre of tomatoes in the territory.

And as of this morning, one rail office. Mine.

* * *

I want to tell you about the office because I've discovered, at the age of forty-one, that I am a person who has opinions about office furniture, and I find this development both hilarious and slightly alarming.

It's a converted freight shed at the foot of the restored spur — the line we pushed a locomotive up on foot through the night, the gradient the survey charts said was impossible, the one Oren Vasquez had annotated in his careful hand: *not recommended, not impossible*. I built the whole reopening around that phrase. I had it stenciled on the side of the engine. Ilsa said it was sentimental. Ilsa, who cried into her dispatch log for an hour the day the first scheduled train made the climb without incident, said it was sentimental. The pot and the kettle have an arrangement, she and I.

The shed still smells like creosote and old iron, which I refuse to fix, because it smells like work, and I spent too many years in places that smelled like nothing at all. There's a desk — secondhand, scarred, one drawer that sticks unless you lift the handle exactly right, which Pip can do and I cannot and which he demonstrates with insufferable grace every single morning. There's a stove Ilsa installed without asking, because Ilsa does not ask, Ilsa installs. There's a window that looks down the line toward the canyon, and if you stand at the right angle you can see the trestle where the Combine's sabotage nearly killed us all, and I have decided to find that view bracing rather than haunted, and most days I succeed.

And on the walls, the maps.

The map of closed lines went up first. It's been with me for twenty years — every dead branch, every demolished bridge, every line the world gave up on, surveyed and recorded in my own hand because somebody ought to know where the wrecks are. It used to live rolled in a tube in my kit, because a map you carry is a map you're leaving. Now it's framed. Behind glass. On the wall.

Beside it, the other one.

The chart of open lines is new — new paper, new ink, and it's still mostly blank, which is the point of it. It has one line on it, the spur from the valley down to the junction, and one station marked, and a great deal of empty space that I look at the way other people look at the ocean. Pip thinks it's funny that I framed a mostly empty map. Pip also thinks it's funny to hide my chalk. We have an arrangement, he and I: he mocks the empty map, I dock his pay in chores, and neither of us would change a thing.

Because here's what the empty map means, and I'll say it once and then never again, because sentiment gives me hives: a closed line is a fact. An open line is a promise. Facts don't require anything of you. Promises do. I spent twenty years collecting facts because facts let you leave. I'm collecting promises now, and promises mean you stay, and staying — I have learned, slowly, expensively, with a knife scar on my arm that isn't even mine to be proud of — staying is the harder survey.

* * *

Pip arrived at the office at six this morning, which is forty minutes before the office opens, because Pip has no concept of time that isn't measured in trains.

He's fourteen now. He's grown four inches, which he mentions daily, and his voice has started doing a thing where it cracks in the middle of sentences, which he does not mention at all, and which the entire crew pretends not to notice out of a mercy that Pip would hate if he knew about it. He still has the chalk. He draws on everything — the shed door, the stove, once, memorably, the director of the Meridian Combine's replacement, a nervous man who came to apologize on behalf of the company and left with a small locomotive sketched on his briefcase and, I think, a genuine affection for this valley that he did not arrive with.

"Morning, boss," he said, letting himself in with his own key, which I gave him, which was a decision I made about six months ago at three in the morning when I realized I'd been lying awake worrying about him the way — well. The way Oren never worried about me. Or the way he did, maybe, in his locked-up, load-bearing-lie kind of way. Either way, the key felt right, and Pip carried it around for two days showing it to people like it was a medal.

"Office opens at six forty," I told him, the way I tell him every morning.

"And yet here I am," he said, "already better than you at everything," and he lifted the sticking drawer open with one finger, first try, and grinned at me with the grin of a boy who has never once in his life had to leave anywhere he loved.

I'm teaching him survey work. It started as a punishment — he stowed away on a work train, he could earn his keep — and it has become, without either of us announcing it, an apprenticeship. He's good. That's the thing that gets me. He has the eye. He can look at a grade and tell you what it'll do to a loaded engine before you've finished leveling the instrument. He can read water. Last month he flagged a culvert on the lower line that the county engineers had signed off on, and he was right, and the county engineers were wrong, and he was insufferable for a week, and I let him be, because being right about culverts is the only kind of insufferable I respect.

He asked me last week, out of nowhere, while we were walking a section of line with the level between us, "What happens if I'm bad at this someday? Like, what if I mess something up?"

"Then we fix it," I said.

"But what if it's bad?"

"Then it's bad, and we fix it, and the line stays open, and nobody has to leave." I said it before I thought about it, and then I heard it, what I'd actually said, the whole architecture of it — the difference between how I was raised in this trade and how I'm raising him. Oren taught me that a mistake was a reason to move on. I'm teaching Pip that a mistake is a maintenance item.

He looked at me for a second, and I could see him deciding whether to make a joke, and then he didn't, which from Pip is practically a hug. "Okay," he said. "Good. Because I already put you down as a reference for the rest of my life, so."

"Chores docked," I said. "Insufferable."

"Worth it."

* * *

Dain's garden is where the helicopters used to land. I want that on the record, because Dain wants it on the record, because Dain Kovic has spent his entire life making sure things are on the record.

He was out there when I walked down at midday, on his knees in the tomato rows with his sleeves rolled up, and I stood at the fence for a while before he noticed me, which is the kind of thing you can only do with a man who used to watch you from a distance for a living. We've come a long way from the night market, from his careful, rehearsed approach, from two people negotiating passage upstream like it was a hostage exchange. These days he mostly just tells me when I'm wrong, which is the truest form of intimacy I know.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're a railroad engineer. You know nothing about tomatoes."

"I know about gradients. That's a slope. Things roll down slopes. I'm concerned about your tomatoes' drainage."

He sat back on his heels and gave me the look — the one with the ghost of the old wariness in it, worn soft now, the way a handle wears smooth. "The drainage is fine. The drainage was fine last week when you told me. Come in or go away, Felix. The fence is not a supervisory position."

I went in. The garden is absurd. It's only been a season and a half and it already looks like it's been there forty years, like the soil was waiting for somebody with a memory to come tend it. Dain plants the way he does everything — deliberately, in rows, with a plan written in Vaskarian in a notebook he keeps in his breast pocket, the same notebook, I'd bet the office, where he once kept his lists of rumors about a river-locked chest. The notebook has changed jobs. Now it's planting dates and rainfall and which of the elders' grandchildren ate the strawberries, an offense he tracks with the gravity of a customs ledger.

He gave me a tomato. He always gives me a tomato. It's become a ritual neither of us acknowledges: I come to the fence to supervise, he hands me a tomato, I eat it standing right there in the row like a barbarian because I have never once had the patience to carry it home, and he shakes his head at me, and we are both, I think, quietly aware that a man who lost a country is feeding a woman who never had one, and that between the two of us we've assembled something that counts.

"The school bell came," I told him. "From Station Nine. Ilsa and the twins are mounting it Thursday. Pip wants to ring it first."

"Pip rang the bell at the junction when the first train arrived. And the bell at the store. And," Dain said, "I believe the church bell in the valley town below, though he claims that was the wind."

"It was the wind."

"It was Pip."

"It was Pip," I agreed.

He smiled and went back to his rows, and I stayed a while longer, eating my tomato in the sun, and thought about the man who'd spent his life looking backward and had, by the slow alchemy of dirt and patience, taught himself to look down instead — at things growing, at things that stay where you put them. He told me once, on the train, in the canyon, the night after he took the knife: "I don't want it back, Felix. Vaskaria. I wanted it *known*. There's a difference." The maps know it now. The atlases were redrawn in the spring, publicly, with the valley named and the people in it named, and Oren Vasquez's role told honestly — the shelter and the betrayal both, the sealed source and the two generations it protected, the whole load-bearing truth of it, set down in ink where nobody can bury it again.

I kept his annotations. Oren's. They're in a drawer in the office, the one that sticks. Some days I take them out and read his handwriting and I'm angry all over again, and some days I read it and I understand, and the days alternate in no pattern I've found, which I've decided is what grief actually is: not a wound that closes but a line you keep walking, in both directions, forever. He taught me to run. He also taught me to read a grade, and a river, and a man's intentions in the wear on a rail head. I'm not going to pretend the second half cancels the first. But I'm not going to pretend the first cancels the second, either. He's in the frame with the closed lines, in a way. He's why I know where all the wrecks are. Including me.

* * *

The office opened at six forty. By seven we had our first customer, which is more than I expected, because the valley's relationship with railroads is complicated — the last train most of them saw was the one that carried the world's forgetting up the line, forty years ago.

It was Marta, from the elder council, who wants freight rates for the spring wool clip and pretends she doesn't trust me and has, in fact, trusted me since the day I tore up the Combine's contract on camera, though she'd deny it under oath. She stood at the counter and looked at the two maps for a long time — the wrecks and the promises, side by side — and then at me.

"Your mentor," she said. "The one who sealed us in. You keep his papers here?"

"I do."

"Why?"

I'd thought about this. I'd thought about it a lot, in the months of journalists and hearings and the long slow public unwinding of Oren's secret, and I had an answer ready, and then I threw it away, because Marta is ninety-one and has earned the unedited version.

"Because throwing them out would be easier," I said, "and I'm done doing the easier thing."

Marta considered this. Then she said, "Good," in the tone of a woman granting a lease, and gave me her wool numbers, and that was our first transaction, and I wrote it in the ledger — a real ledger, a business ledger, the boring kind, the kind I never thought I'd want — and when she left I sat down at my scarred desk in my creosote-smelling shed between a map of every line the world gave up on and a map of everything still possible, and I felt something I'd been feeling for months now and had refused to name because naming it felt like tempting fate.

But I'm naming it now. It's this: I'm not restless.

I keep waiting for it — the itch, the horizon pulling, the certainty that it's time to go before anyone gets a chance to miss me. It comes some mornings, faint, like weather on the far side of the canyon. And then Pip lets himself in with his own key, or Ilsa bellows something about the Thursday schedule, or the morning train comes down the spur with its impossible-gradient whistle, and the itch passes through me like wind through an open window in a house with a lit stove, and it can't get a hold on anything, because I've filled the room.

Ilsa stuck her head in around noon. "Twins say the number two engine's ready for the Thursday run. Also your apprentice is on the roof."

"Why is he on the roof?"

"He says he's surveying the roof."

"Is he?"

"He's fourteen, Felix. He's drawing a locomotive on the shingles."

I went out and looked up. He was right — he was drawing a locomotive on the shingles, in chalk, in surprising and slightly worrying detail, and when he saw me he waved the chalk like a flag of surrender that wasn't.

"Chores," I called up.

"Worth it," he called back.

And that was the whole of it, that ordinary noon in Source: a boy on a roof, an engine ready for Thursday, a garden coming in heavy, two maps on the wall, one blank on purpose. The wreck and the horizon, side by side, and me standing in the doorway between them, not going anywhere.

The bell comes Thursday. Pip gets to ring it first.

I already know I'll let him.
