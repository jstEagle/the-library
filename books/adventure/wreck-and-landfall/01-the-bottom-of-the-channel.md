## 1. The Bottom of the Channel

The alarm went off at 4:40, and Casper Pemberton was awake before it finished its first ring, the way she was always awake before anything finished — before coffee finished brewing, before contracts finished printing, before men finished explaining what they wanted from her. She killed the alarm with her thumb and lay in the dark of the wheelhouse listening to the river slap the hull of the *Magpie* in a rhythm she knew better than her own pulse.

Fourteen years on this water. She could tell you the channel's mood by the sound it made against fiberglass.

She dressed without light: thermal layer, drysuit undergarments, wool socks with a darned heel she kept meaning to replace and never did. The kettle went on while she checked the tide table by the blue glow of the chart screen. Slack water at 6:12. That gave her ninety minutes of dead current on the wreck site, which was all anyone ever got down there, and less than anyone needed.

The navy had restricted the site eleven days ago. Buoyed it off with orange markers and a laminated notice zip-tied to each one: **NO SALVAGE. NO DIVING. NO ENTRY. BY ORDER OF THE RIVERINE COMMAND.** Somebody had gone to the trouble of laminating them, which told Casper the restriction wasn't temporary. Lamination was a commitment. She'd been running salvage out of Harrow Bend long enough to know that a paper notice meant nothing and a laminated one meant everything.

She also knew that the navy didn't pay divers. The navy paid contractors, who paid subcontractors, who paid her — eventually, minus fifteen percent, minus weather days, minus whatever the middlemen decided their patience was worth. And the navy's survey of the wreck had stalled twice already because the contracted dive team couldn't get mobilized before the season's first storm window closed. Casper had watched the requisition notices come through the harbor office fax like watching money circle a drain.

So she would dive it herself. Not for the navy. For the record.

That was the story she told herself, anyway, pouring the first cup of coffee and standing barefoot on cold deck boards while the sky over the eastern bluffs went from black to a bruised iron gray. The truth was simpler and less flattering: Casper Pemberton dove restricted sites for the same reason she did most things — because she was good at it, because nobody else was going to, and because there was a version of satisfaction available only to people who were very good at something nobody was watching them do.

She was forty-one years old and she had logged 3,112 dives. She knew the number the way other people knew their children's birthdays. Every dive in the logbook had a date, a bottom time, a max depth, a task, and a payment code. Her whole life reduced to columns. She sometimes thought if she died out here, the coroner wouldn't need to interview anyone. He could just read the logbook. *Diver, female, 41. Last entry: paid miles, channel mile 118, task: hull inspection, client: none.*

*Client: none.* That was today's code.

* * *

The wreck sat in thirty-one feet of green-black water where the channel narrowed between the old ferry pilings and the shoal they called the Widow's Teeth. It had been a coastal freighter once, the *Corvane*, listed lost with cargo in the spring flood four decades back — which put its sinking right around the year the maps changed, though Casper hadn't thought about that connection yet, and wouldn't for another day. Forty years of silt had buried it to the rail line. The navy's interest, as far as the harbor gossip went, was hazmat: fuel bunkers, maybe unexploded ordnance from the war years when the delta had been a supply corridor. Standard stuff. Standard enough that the restriction notice hadn't even slowed the fishing traffic; boats cut inside the buoys all day like the orange spheres were decorative.

Casper anchored the *Magpie* outside the marker line, which was a legal nicety she maintained mostly so she could tell herself she'd respected the letter of things. Then she kitted up on the transom in the gray half-light, checking her gauges twice because the water below was cold enough to kill a careless woman and she had no intention of being careless or dead.

The surface was smoked glass. She rolled backward off the transom and the river closed over her like a lid.

Down here the world was the color of strong tea held up to a lamp. Visibility ran eight feet on a good day, six today, enough to see the outline of the wreck rising out of the silt floor like a drowned cathedral roof. Her dive light carved a cone through the murk and the cone filled with drifting motes, each one lit up and falling, lit up and falling, an endless slow snowfall of the river's leavings. She had spent more of her life inside that snowfall than inside any building.

The *Corvane*'s deck was under two feet of soft gray silt, and the silt was alive in the way silt always was — shifting, breathing, erasing. She could see where the navy's survey team had fanned out weeks ago: a grid of small stakes with numbered tags, most of them already leaning, two of them swallowed entirely. The river was eating their work faster than they could do it. That was the thing about water that land people never understood. Water didn't preserve evidence. Water digested it.

Casper swam the port rail line, running her gloved hand along the steel, feeling for the seam of the forward cargo hold. Her breathing was slow and even, six breaths a minute, the rhythm of a woman who had learned to make her body cheap to run. Bottom time budget: forty minutes. Air plan: conservative. Task list, self-assigned:

One. Confirm hold access.
Two. Assess structural integrity at the bulkhead penetration.
Three. Photograph anything anomalous.

*Anomalous* was a word she used in her own logs precisely because it committed her to nothing. A hole was anomalous. A shape was anomalous. She had learned early, diving for her father, that the less you named a thing underwater, the fewer people came asking about it later.

Her father. The thought arrived the way it always did down here, uninvited, riding some current of association she couldn't trace — maybe the cold, maybe the dark, maybe just depth itself pressing on some old bruise. Denny Pemberton had taught her to dive when she was twelve, in a flooded quarry with a single regulator and his hand locked in her hair, and he had taught her the first rule before he taught her anything else: *the river gives to the quiet.* Not the brave. Not the strong. The quiet. She had been quiet for twenty-nine years since, and the river had given her a living, and taken everything else.

She found the hold hatch at the fourteen-minute mark. It was a cargo hatch, dogged shut, and the dogs were frozen with corrosion except one, which turned under her wrench with a grinding complaint that sounded almost human. She worked it loose in slow quarter-turns, the way her father had taught her — never force a seal, persuade it — and the hatch cracked open with a belch of trapped gas that bloomed black in her light and then dispersed.

Below was darkness stacked on darkness.

She descended into the hold with her light leading, and the beam swept across collapsed dunnage, rotted pallets, the shapes of crates slumped into each other like tired passengers. Ordinary. Forty years of ordinary. She photographed the grid reference, checked her gauge — twenty-two minutes — and was turning for the ascent line when her light caught something at the far end of the hold that made her stop mid-turn.

A shape too regular for wreckage.

It sat against the aft bulkhead, half-buried in silt, roughly rectangular, man-sized or a little larger. Not a crate — crates had slumped; this had posture. Its edges were sharp under the silt skin. And along its visible top edge ran a line of metal that threw her light back not with rust's dull brown but with a dull, dense, leaden gray.

Lead lining. On a river freighter's cargo. In a hold full of rotted wood.

Casper hung in the water above it and felt the particular stillness come over her that she had learned to trust more than any instrument — the stillness of a woman whose instincts had kept her alive in places that killed better-equipped people. Something was wrong with this picture. Lead-lined boxes went on ships for three reasons: radiation, corrosion, and secrecy. Two of those reasons belonged to laboratories. One belonged to men who wanted something to survive being lost.

She should have photographed it, logged it, surfaced, and reported it to the harbor office like a good contractor. That was the transactional life. That was the whole architecture of who she'd become: see the thing, name the thing, bill for the thing, forget the thing.

Instead she hung there in the falling snow of silt, breathing her expensive air, looking down at a box the river had kept for forty years, and felt the oldest part of herself — the smuggler's-daughter part, the part she kept locked below the waterline of her own personality — sit up and look back.

*The river gives to the quiet,* her father said in her head.

*And what does it give?* she'd asked him once, twelve years old, mask fogged, quarry water around them both.

*Whatever you're quiet enough to carry.*

She checked her gauge. Nineteen minutes. Enough to clear silt from one corner. Not enough to do anything foolish.

She did the minimum foolish thing. She fanned silt away from the near corner with careful sweeps of her glove until the metal showed clean, and there, stamped into the lead-sheathed corner plate, worn shallow but unmistakable, was a mark: a circle crossed by three wavy lines, like a sun over water.

She didn't know the mark. She was sure she didn't know the mark. But some animal ledger in her brain recorded it anyway, deep, indelible, the way you remember a stranger's face from a crowd you passed twenty years ago.

Then her low-air whistle chirped, rude as a doorbell, and the moment broke, and she ascended.

* * *

She surfaced at 6:51 into a morning that had decided to be beautiful, which felt like a personal affront. Mist stood on the channel in long white banners. The eastern bluffs had caught fire with sunrise, gold running down their faces into the water. Gulls wheeled over the buoy line screaming their idiot joy. Any other morning she'd have stood at the rail with her coffee and let the beauty happen to her for a full minute before getting to work.

Today she hauled gear, stripped the drysuit, started the dehumidifier on the regulators, and pulled the logbook across the chart table.

**Date:** [today]. **Site:** Corvane, restricted. **Bottom time:** 38 min. **Max depth:** 31 ft. **Task:** survey assist, unauthorized. **Findings:** hold access confirmed. Anomaly noted, aft bulkhead. **Payment code:** none.

She wrote *none* and looked at it for a while. Fourteen years of codes, and now *none*. A column with nothing in it. She almost liked the emptiness. It was the closest thing to freedom her ledger had ever contained.

Then she opened the camera and reviewed the shots, and there it was in the last frame, sharp despite the murk: the corner plate, the silt fan-marks of her own glove, and the mark. Circle. Three wavy lines. Sun over water.

She zoomed. She enhanced. She told herself she was doing due diligence.

She didn't know what it was. But she knew someone who might, because knowing things was the only currency the delta traded in besides cash, and Maren Ostrev at the ferry landing collected old things the way other women collected teacups. Old songs. Old names. Old grievances, probably. Casper had sold the woman engine parts twice and bought smoked fish once, and every transaction had come wrapped in conversation Casper hadn't asked for and couldn't quite bring herself to cut short — which, for Casper, was practically intimacy.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Today's problem was simpler: the box was still down there, in a restricted site, under a laminated notice, and the navy's real dive team was finally mobilizing — she'd heard the radio chatter at dawn, two boats out of the naval yard, ETA the site within the week. Whatever the *Corvane* carried in its lead-lined heart, the navy would have it in seven days, catalogued, classified, and erased from the river's memory forever.

Seven days. Casper looked at the photograph again. Sun over water. A nation's worth of wrongness packed into one stamped corner.

She closed the logbook. She poured the last of the coffee, cold now, and drank it anyway, standing at the rail while the mist burned off the channel and the *Magpie* swung gently on her hook, pointing upstream, the way Casper would soon find herself pointed whether she chose it or not.

She was good at being no one. She'd built the whole life on it — the unlisted mooring, the cash jobs, the logbook codes, the face that harbor officials squinted at and forgot. No one got followed home. No one got asked questions. No one's father got mentioned at parties, because no one got invited to parties.

But no one, she was beginning to suspect, doesn't stay no one forever. Not once the river decides you've been quiet long enough.

Tomorrow she'd run down to the ferry landing with a photograph and a tank of diesel and no particular questions. Just fish, and engine parts, and a stamped circle she'd claim she couldn't identify.

The river gives to the quiet.

It was about to find out how quiet she really was.
