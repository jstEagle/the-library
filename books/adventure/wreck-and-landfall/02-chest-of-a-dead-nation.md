## 2. Chest of a Dead Nation

The chest sat on the deck of the *Marrow* all day, under a tarp, while Casper did everything a woman could do to pretend it wasn't there.

She fueled at the municipal dock. She bought engine oil, a case of lamp oil, coffee, salt pork, and a coil of new line. She let the harbormaster's clerk copy her salvage permit twice because he was slow about it and she didn't want to seem slow herself. She ate a bowl of fish stew at the landing and paid with exact change and left no tip, because tips were memory, and memory was a trail.

All the while her eyes kept going back to the boat.

The tarp was good canvas, navy surplus, unremarkable. But she'd tied it wrong — she could see that from thirty feet away, the way you can see a crooked picture frame in someone else's house. The knot was a cleat hitch where she always used a bowline, and the corner nearest the stern sagged just enough to show the edge of what lay beneath: dull gray metal, lead-sheathed, furred with forty years of river silt except where her thumb had rubbed a circle clean.

A circle. A stamped seal. Two lines crossed by a third, like a river meeting its tributaries, inside a ring of lettering worn too smooth to read.

She had told herself on the tow home that she wouldn't look. That she'd photograph it, as planned, take it to the ferry landing, ask her no particular questions, and then decide whether the thing was worth the trouble of knowing what it was. That was the professional move. Her father's rule, back when rules were things he kept: *Don't name what you pull up until you know who's standing on the bank.*

But her father had also died face-down in this same current with his pockets full of unnamed things, so his rules deserved only so much reverence.

By late afternoon the light went copper on the water and the harbor emptied of working boats, and Casper cast off from the fuel dock and moved the *Marrow* upriver half a mile to her mooring in the reeds — not because she was hiding, she told herself, but because the municipal dock charged by the night and she wasn't made of money.

She was hiding. She knew she was hiding. Knowing it didn't stop her.

* * *

The dredge had hooked it three days ago, off-survey, in the silt shelf below the drowned pilings where the navy charts said nothing larger than gravel had settled since the war.

She remembered the moment exactly, the way divers remember the moments that change their accounts. The cable had gone taut wrong — not the steady strain of mud but a jerking, stubborn weight, like hooking a sleeping animal. She'd surfaced, checked her gauges, gone back down along the line hand over hand through water the color of steeped tea, and found her grapnel lodged in an iron hasp.

Not rock. Not wreck timber. A hasp. Forged iron, still hinged, still holding.

The chest had been wedged into a collapse of stone that might once have been a bridge footing or a customs house foundation — something built by people who expected to be there a long time, and weren't. The lead sheathing had kept the river out. Forty years of current had scoured everything around it to bare clay, and the chest had simply sat in the middle of the emptiness like a word someone forgot to erase.

She should have logged it. That was the procedure — find of unknown origin, restricted waters, notify the naval survey office at Kell's Folly, await instructions, accept whatever fraction of appraised value they deigned to pay, watch the thing disappear onto a barge under armed guard and never hear its name again.

She had written *gravel obstruction, cleared* in her log instead, in her flat, careful hand, and lied to a piece of paper for the first time in six years.

Now, moored in the reeds with the frogs starting up their evening argument, she untied the wrong knot and pulled back the tarp.

* * *

Up close, in lamplight, the seal was worse than a mystery. It was an answer to a question she hadn't asked yet.

She scrubbed the rim with a rag and fresh water, working in circles, and the lettering came up out of the grime letter by letter. Not navy script. Not any alphabet used on the current charts. The characters were older — rounder, denser, crowded against each other like people sharing a bench — and beneath them, smaller, a second ring in trade-common that she could actually read:

**VESANY — BY WATER BOUND, BY WATER KEPT**

She sat back on her heels.

Everyone on the delta knew the word Vessany the way everyone knows the name of a town that burned down before they were born. It lived in place-names that had never been renamed because renaming cost money nobody had — Vessan Reach, the Vessan Cut, Old Vessany Landing, which was now just Landing. It lived in the hymns some of the old river women still hummed at funerals, tunes with more notes than the words could hold. And it lived in the treaty, the one every schoolchild learned as a single dry sentence: *the Vessan territories were peaceably incorporated into the Delta Compact forty years ago.*

Peaceably. Incorporated. Two words that sounded like paperwork, because they were.

There was no Vessany anymore. There hadn't been since before Casper could walk. Maps showed the delta towns, the navy stations, the tollhouses, the glacier marked in blue hatch-lines at the head of the river with a printed warning — *RESTRICTED WATERS — SURVEY PERMIT REQUIRED.* No nation. No border. Just geography with new owners.

And here, dredged out of the dark under a collapsed foundation, was a chest wearing the dead country's name like a man wearing his own coat at his own funeral.

Casper looked over her shoulder at the reeds, at the black line of the far bank, at the empty water. Nothing moved. The frogs kept arguing. Somewhere upstream a night bird called twice and quit.

She got the pry bar.

* * *

The hasp fought her, then gave with a crack that seemed very loud in the reeds. The lid came up stiff on hinges that had waited four decades for the invitation.

Inside, on top, lay oilcloth packets bound with tarred cord, and beneath them, nested like eggs, three objects that stopped her breath.

The first was a ledger — real leather, swollen with damp but sound, its pages stiff as dried leaves. She turned them by lamplight with cotton gloves she used for handling silver recovery, and the handwriting inside was fine and regular, a clerk's hand, columns of names. Names, and beside each name a parcel of land described by river measure — *from the willow stand to the second channel mouth, thence along the deep water* — and beside each parcel, a signature line.

Every signature line was signed. Hundreds of them. Whole parishes of signatures, page after page, agreeing — the heading said — to *surrender all claims, titles, and inheritances within the ceded territories, in consideration of the protections herein granted.*

The second object was a document, folded in a separate oilcloth sleeve, heavier paper, official stock with a ribboned stamp. The Treaty of Incorporation itself, or a copy of it — the founding instrument, the one reproduced in every schoolbook, signed by the Vessan Council on behalf of a people who had supposedly agreed to stop existing.

She laid the ledger open beside it and compared signatures at random. The councilmen's names matched. The dates matched. Everything matched, neat as arithmetic.

Except.

Her father had run cargo up and down this river for twenty years, and before that he'd been a forger's apprentice in Kell's Folly, and before that — well, before that was a story he told drunk and she'd stopped listening to. But he'd taught her ink the way other fathers taught fishing. *Paper lies easy,* he used to say, holding a bill of lading up to the lamp. *Ink tells the truth if you make it tired enough.*

She made the ink tired. She tilted the ledger page under the lamp, low angle, raking light across the fibers.

The councilmen's signatures sat *on* the paper. The hundreds of others — the parish names, the little people surrendering their willow stands and channel mouths — sank *into* it. Different pens, different pressure, different hands pressed down over the same lines in the same session, which no honest signing produces, because honest signings are spread across weeks and weather and moods. These had been executed in a batch, fast, by a handful of writers grinding through a list.

Somebody had sat in a room and invented the consent of thousands of people, name by name, and bound it in leather so it would keep.

Casper set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

The third object was at the bottom of the chest, wrapped in waxed linen, and it was flat and heavy and when she unwrapped it she found a map — not printed, drawn by hand on skin-tanned vellum, the river rendered from its delta mouth all the way up to the glacier source in lines so fine they seemed stitched rather than inked. The delta towns were there under their old names. The tollhouses were absent. The navy stations were absent. The whole imposed geography of the last forty years simply wasn't, and underneath the present river ran another river — the same water, named differently, owned differently — and at the head of it, where the maps in the schoolbooks showed only hatch-lines and warnings, the vellum showed a symbol she recognized from the chest's own lid: two lines crossed by a third, inside a ring.

Beneath the symbol, in that dense round lettering, and beneath that in trade-common, a single line:

*THE ARCHIVE OF THE VESSAN NATION — SEALED AT THE SOURCE — LET IT OPEN TO THE RIVER'S OWN*

The map was sealed under a disc of wax the size of her palm, pressed with the same seal, unbroken.

She understood, dimly, the way you understand a current shift before your instruments confirm it, that the wax was the point. That somewhere there was a protocol, a law, a custom — something that said this map was not to be read until some condition was met, some door reached, some witness found. That she was holding a message addressed to a country that no longer existed, and she had no idea if she counted as a legal recipient or a grave robber.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick without looking away from the vellum.

* * *

She should sleep. She knew the arithmetic of her own body — four hours' rest or the morning dive would be sloppy, and sloppy was how divers died. She banked the lamp, lay down in the cabin berth with her boots off and her clothes on, and stared at the ceiling beams.

What she saw instead was the naval survey office at Kell's Folly. She'd delivered finds there twice. She knew the routine: the receiving clerk with his inventory book, the armed escort that materialized from nowhere the moment anything interesting crossed the scale, the windowless room where items of "strategic historical interest" were catalogued and crated and shipped downriver on the gray barge and never seen again. She'd watched a bell from a merchant wreck — brass, beautiful, worth real money anywhere else — go into a crate stenciled **DO NOT INVENTORY** and come out of the room with no crate at all.

That was for a bell. A bell was metal and a name.

This was a ledger proving that the ground under every farm and ferry landing and fish weir from the delta to the high water had been taken by forgery. This was a treaty whose true text might say something other than what the schoolbooks said. This was a map to a sealed archive at the glacier source, in restricted waters, in the one direction the navy patrolled hardest — because whatever was up there, they had decided forty years ago that nobody should ever read it.

And she had written *gravel obstruction, cleared* in her logbook in her own careful hand.

She thought about Aldous Vey before she knew his name — the contractor's men she'd seen at the landing, gray jackets, civilian guns, the ones who asked questions in a friendly voice and wrote your answers down. She thought about the phrase her father had used for men like that, the only vocabulary he'd ever offered her for the machinery of the state: *they don't buy the river, they buy the people who drink from it.*

If the chest went in over the side tonight, weighted, sunk into the silt where the navy's charts said nothing had ever settled — the problem would be gone by morning. She was good at sinking things. It was most of her profession.

If she kept it, she owned a secret that killed people. Not maybe. Not eventually. She had grown up on this river; she knew exactly how the machine worked; she had spent six years being no one precisely so the machine would never learn her name. The moment anyone connected Casper Pemberton to a Vessan seal, she would stop being invisible, and invisibility was the only armor she had ever owned.

She lay in the dark doing the arithmetic she hated: the value of silence against the price of knowledge, miles against years, the self she'd built against the truth sitting ten feet away under a tarp in the reeds.

The frogs went quiet all at once, the way they do when something moves on the water.

Casper was upright before she'd decided to move, knife in hand, at the cabin window. Out past the reed line, mid-channel, a boat slid downstream with its lamp doused — running dark, running quiet, the way she herself ran when she didn't want to be counted. It passed without pause. It didn't so much as slow.

It didn't have to. Whatever it was — patrol, smuggler, ghost — the river was small tonight, and word traveled on water faster than boats did. Somewhere between the wreck site and these reeds, the current had already begun telling people what she'd pulled up.

She sat back down in the lamplight and looked at the map, at the sealed wax, at the round patient lettering of a country that had trusted its future to whoever dredged it out of the dark.

*Let it open to the river's own,* the vellum said.

"I'm not yours," she said aloud, to a nation forty years drowned, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

At dawn she would run down to the ferry landing, as planned, with her photograph and her no particular questions. But she already knew, watching the gray water slide past the hull, that the plan had changed shape somewhere below the surface, in the dark, where the hasp had caught her hook.

Because there was a fourth thing in the chest. She'd almost missed it — a thin envelope tucked into the ledger's spine, addressed in that same clerk's hand, and the address was not a place.

It was a name. Written plainly, in trade-common, meant to be read by whoever opened the chest:

*Maren Ostrev.*
