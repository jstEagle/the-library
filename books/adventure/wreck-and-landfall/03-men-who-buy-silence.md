## 3. Men Who Buy Silence

The money came before the questions did.

Casper learned that the way she learned most things about the river: by watching the water. On the third morning after the chest came up off the *Coriander's* deck plates, a gray runabout with no name on the transom idled past her mooring at Halfway Dock, slow enough to be noticed, fast enough not to care. It made one pass. Then it was gone downstream toward the navy yard at Corren, and Casper stood on her deck with coffee going cold in her hand and understood that the clock had started.

She'd spent those three days doing nothing, which was itself a decision. The chest sat under her bunk, wrapped in the oilskin she'd used for the dive, lead lining still faintly cold to the touch even in summer. She hadn't opened it again. She'd told herself that was discipline — don't touch evidence, don't leave prints, don't make yourself a story — but lying awake at night with the river slapping the hull, she knew it was something closer to fear. She had lifted things out of dark water for eleven years and never once asked what they meant. Meaning was expensive. Meaning got people audited, subpoenaed, drowned.

So she dove other jobs. A sunken barge tender at Marker 44, a lost dredge chain, a routine hull inspection for a grain hauler whose captain paid cash and didn't talk. Her logbook filled with honest, boring entries. *0740, descent, vis two meters, silt. Recovered: nothing.* She wrote them in her careful block letters and felt herself reassembling into no one, and it almost worked.

Then the man in the good coat came to the dock.

* * *

He arrived at four in the afternoon, which told her he wasn't navy. Navy kept banker's hours; contractors kept whatever hours made their targets comfortable. He stepped off the gangplank onto her deck like he'd been invited, a broad man of maybe fifty with silver at his temples and a charcoal coat too warm for the season, and he looked around her boat with the frank appraisal of someone pricing salvage.

"Casper Pemberton." Not a question. "Aldous Vey."

She didn't offer a hand. He didn't seem to expect one. "You're a long way from Corren," she said.

"I'm exactly where I need to be." He smiled without deploying it fully, the way men do when the smile is a tool and not a feeling. "May I?"

He gestured at her cockpit bench and sat before she answered, settling himself as though the boat were already his. Up close he smelled of wool and something antiseptic underneath, like a hospital corridor. His eyes went to the tarp over her forward hold, then to the winch, then to her hands, and she realized he was reading her the way she read wrecks — systematically, looking for the breach.

"Eleven years licensed," he said. "Fourteen if you count the apprenticeship, which your file says we shouldn't discuss. Two hundred and six documented recoveries. Zero disputes filed against you. Zero complaints. Do you know how rare that is, Ms. Pemberton? In my line of work, silence is a commodity, and you are — forgive me — an extraordinary producer."

"What line of work is that?"

"Compliance." He said it the way a priest says grace. "Meridian Riverine Services holds the remediation contract for the naval district. When restricted sites are disturbed, when unlogged recoveries occur, when anomalies surface—" the faintest pause, priced precisely, "—we ensure the record stays clean."

"The record's clean."

"Mm." He reached inside his coat and produced a tablet, thumbed it awake, turned it toward her. On the screen was a survey chart of the channel below the old wreck site, and there, a single sonar return flagged amber, timestamped five days ago. Her dredge. Her coordinates. "This is an internal document, Ms. Pemberton. You will never see it again after today. What it shows is an anomalous contact adjacent to a restricted wreck. What our assessment determined — after considerable analysis — is that the contact is consistent with ballast stone and river debris. Nothing of interest. No recovery occurred."

"I recovered a chest."

"You recovered ballast stone," Vey said pleasantly, "and river debris. That is what the record will show, and that is what you will confirm if anyone ever asks, though no one will ask, because the record will be clean." He set the tablet face-down on the bench between them, and on top of it, unhurried, he placed a folded envelope. It was thick enough to stand slightly open at the fold. "Triple your standard day rate. For each day of the last week, and each day going forward that this conversation remains what it was — a courtesy call between professionals."

Casper looked at the envelope. She thought about her account balance, which was the kind of number that decided things rather than described them. She thought about the fuel bill, the hull survey due in autumn, the fact that she hadn't taken a real vacation since her father died because vacations cost money and money was the only language she'd ever been fluent in.

"And the chest?" she said. "The actual object."

Vey's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a shutter closing, or opening. "There is no chest. There was never a chest. Objects that do not exist cannot be surrendered, sold, photographed, or discussed. They simply are not." He stood, smoothed his coat, and for the first time looked directly at her with something that was almost warmth, which was worse than everything before it. "Ms. Pemberton. I've read your file. Your father ran cargo on this river. He was very good at not being seen. I imagine you inherited more than his boat." He stepped to the gangplank, then paused. "People who take this money live long, quiet lives. People who don't — well. The river takes all kinds."

He walked down the plank without looking back, and the gray runabout took him away downstream, and Casper sat alone in the late light with an envelope full of triple rate and the taste of copper in her mouth.

She counted it. Four thousand, in used bills, the kind of money that had been handled by many hands and could be traced by none. Then she locked it in the same drawer as her father's old river charts, and she made herself dinner, and she did not sleep.

* * *

In Corren, in an office on the fourth floor of the Meridian building where the blinds stayed down and the air conditioning hummed at a frequency that discouraged lingering, Aldous Vey logged the contact.

He worked from memory first — that was habit, that was craft — writing in a ledger bound in green cloth, an artifact from an earlier contract era that existed in no inventory anywhere. *Pemberton, C. Contacted 1600. Cooperative. Payment accepted without negotiation.* He paused over the last word. Negotiation was healthy. Negotiation meant the subject still believed she had leverage, still believed the transaction was the whole of it. Acceptance without negotiation meant either desperation or intelligence, and Pemberton's file argued against desperation. A woman who'd spent fourteen years being no one did not get that way by being stupid.

He opened the personnel system and pulled her file again, reading not the data but its shape. Father deceased, river incident, unsolved. Mother absent from every record after the girl was nine — not dead, just gone, erased so thoroughly it had to have been deliberate. No spouse. No partner listed in eleven years. No club memberships, no church, no union. A woman who had built her life the way you scuttle a ship: sealed compartments, nothing floating to mark where she'd gone down.

Vey had managed a great many such people. They were the easiest kind, right up until they weren't. The ones with nothing to lose were stable; the ones who believed they had nothing to lose were the ones who surprised you.

He drafted the follow-up order — standard surveillance tier two, passive only, no approach — and then, after a moment, added a second line in the comments field: *If subject initiates further inquiry into historical materials, escalate immediately. Do not offer second payment.*

Because that was the pattern, always. First payment bought silence. But silence, in Vey's experience, was like a hull patch: it held until the water found the seam. And somewhere downriver there was a seam. There was always a seam. An old woman at a ferry landing, a clerk in a records office, a cousin twice removed who remembered songs. Nations didn't die cleanly, whatever the treaties said. They leaked.

He closed the ledger, locked it in the drawer beneath the window that didn't open, and stood for a moment looking at the river through the blind slats — brown and patient, carrying everything downstream, keeping nothing.

*Escalate immediately,* he had written. He underlined it twice.

* * *

Casper tried to spend the money and couldn't.

That was the thing she hadn't predicted. She'd expected guilt, maybe — some moral static, easy to name and easier to ignore. What she got instead was mechanical failure. She fueled the boat and the pump clicked off and she stood there holding the nozzle thinking, *this is his money in the tank now,* and put it back. She ordered parts for the winch and canceled them before confirmation. She ate canned stew straight from the can over the sink because sitting at the table felt like a transaction she couldn't complete.

The chest sat under the bunk. She caught herself talking to it once, low, the way her father used to talk to engines — *all right, all right, I know* — and stopped, appalled.

On the fifth night she gave up on sleep, lit the lamp over the chart table, and pulled the documents out again. Just to look. Just to know what she'd been paid to pretend didn't exist.

The treaty paper was heavy stock, official watermark, forty years faded to the color of weak tea. She read the signatures slowly. Two governors. A Vessan delegation — five names, titles she half recognized from school lessons about the war nobody taught anymore. The terms were simple and total: the delta lands, the toll rights, the ferry charters, all ceded. All of it signed, witnessed, sealed.

Except.

She'd grown up watching her father forge harbor stamps. Not for anything large — landing fees, fish quotas, the small corruptions of a small man trying to keep a boat afloat — but she'd watched him do it a hundred times, and she knew what ink did when it was pressed in haste versus pressed with authority. The Vessan signatures were all pressed the same way. Identical pressure. Identical pen lift. Five different men, one hand.

And the dates. The treaty was dated the ninth of March. The Vessan delegation, according to the marginalia in Maren Ostrev's pamphlet — the one the old woman had pressed on her at the ferry landing weeks ago, the one Casper had thrown in a drawer unread until now — had been interned at Harrow Camp from February onward. Detained. Unable to sign anything, let alone surrender a nation.

Casper set the page down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was not.

They hadn't bought her silence about a curiosity. They'd bought her silence about a theft — thousands of people stripped of a delta, dispersed, renamed, deleted from maps, and the paperwork that did it was fake, and the fake paperwork was sitting on her chart table, and she had taken four thousand dollars to call it ballast stone.

She thought of Vey's voice. *People who take this money live long, quiet lives.*

She thought of her father, running the night current, competent and invisible, dead at thirty-eight in water he'd known better than anyone alive. Invisible right up until the river closed over him, and no one had ever even been charged, because no one had ever even looked.

"No," she said aloud, to the lamp, to the chest, to the man in the good coat who was probably at this moment logging her compliance in a green book. "No, I don't think so."

She swept the bills back into the envelope. She didn't burn them — burning money was a luxury — but she put them in the drawer with the charts, separate, quarantined, marked in her mind as *not mine.*

Then she did the thing she should have done on the first night. She unwrapped the wax-sealed map, broke the seal — and paused, because breaking it felt like a door closing behind her — and flattened the vellum under the lamp glass.

Rivers. The whole watershed, drawn in a fine old hand, the delta towns named in names she'd never heard on any modern chart. And upstream, at the top edge where the river began, at the glacier source where the maps went blank and the navy overlays said RESTRICTED WATERS in red block letters — a symbol. A square. An archive mark.

Her radio crackled. Just static, just the channel scan picking up the harbor band, ordinary noise. But it was nearly midnight, and the harbor band was quiet, and the static had a rhythm to it, deliberate, three short bursts and a pause, three short bursts and a pause.

Somebody was testing whether she was listening.

Casper sat very still in the lamplight, one hand flat on the map, the other on the radio, and listened to the river breathe around her hull while whoever it was counted her silences.
