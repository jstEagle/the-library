## 4. The Exile at the Ferry Landing

Casper took the chest off the boat the way you take a splinter out of your own hand — fast, before the nerve endings could file a full report.

She'd meant to leave it aboard. She'd told herself that twice on the run down from the wreck site, once in the voice of a professional and once in the voice of a coward, and both voices agreed: the thing belonged in the bilge, under the false floor panel, wrapped in her spare dive suit, until she decided what it was worth and to whom. But when she tied up at Ostrev's Ferry — a landing so small it was really just a suggestion of a town, three buildings and a fuel pump leaning over brown water — her hands went to the chest instead of the duffel. She carried it up the ramp like it weighed nothing, which was a lie, because lead-lined boxes weigh exactly what they weigh, and this one weighed forty years of something.

The tarp came with it. That was the mistake. She'd thrown the old canvas over the chest out of habit — habit being most of what she owned — and the Vessan seal she'd painted on that tarp six years ago, back when it was just a flag she'd lifted off a derelict because good canvas is good canvas, hung there in the morning light like a confession.

A blue circle. A white river running through it, forked at the top like a wishbone. She'd never thought about what it meant. She'd thought about whether the grommets held.

The ferry landing had one bench, one rusted sign reading OSTREV'S FERRY — CROSSING ON THE HOUR, MOSTLY, and one person: an old woman sitting on the bench with her hands folded in her lap as if she'd been waiting since the last century and intended to keep waiting with dignity.

"Crossing's not till ten," Casper said.

"I know." The woman didn't look up. She was looking at the tarp. "I know that mark."

"It's just canvas."

"It is not just canvas."

Casper shifted the chest to her other hip. Forty pounds of dead nation, pressing a bruise into her side. "Lady, I'm a salvager. I paint whatever's waterproof on whatever's left over. You want the crossing, come back at ten. Mostly."

The woman stood then, and standing changed her. She was small — five feet maybe, dried up like driftwood — but she rose the way things rise out of water, all at once, no negotiation. Her coat was army surplus two generations deep. Her hands were a working woman's hands, knuckles swollen, nails cut to nothing. Her eyes were pale gray and they did not blink enough.

"My name," she said, "is Maren Ostrev. This landing had my name before it had a road. And that mark on your tarp is the seal of Vessany, and I have not seen it flying anywhere in forty-one years, and I would very much like to sit down now, so please do not make me beg while standing."

"You're begging anyway."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's what forty-one years teaches you. Begging is just asking with the pride removed."

* * *

Casper should have walked away. She ran the arithmetic later, lying awake in the boat, and every column said walk away: an old woman at an empty crossing, a painted tarp, a coincidence wearing a coat. But there was a third voice in her head, quieter than the professional and the coward, the one that had made her open the chest alone at night instead of radioing it in, and that voice said: *she knew the word Vessany without being told.*

Nobody knew that word. Casper hadn't known it until she'd scraped the crusted silt off the chest lid and found the stamp beneath — the circle, the forked river, and letters worn nearly smooth: VESSANY. She'd spent an evening in the marina office pretending to check tide tables while actually searching a decades-old atlas for a country that wasn't in it. Not gone from the map. Never on it, anymore. Erased so thoroughly that the atlas's own page numbers skipped — 214, 216 — like a gap in teeth.

So they sat on the bench, because the third voice was a bad advisor and always had been, and Maren Ostrev looked at the tarp the way people look at graves.

"Where did you get that canvas?" she asked.

"A derelict hulk up-channel. Half-sunk barge, hull number filed off."

"The *Vessa's Daughter*?"

Casper kept her face still. It was a skill she'd paid for. "Could be anything."

"It could," Maren agreed mildly. "It could be anything at all. My father named that barge. I was nine years old and he let me break the bottle, and my mother cried, which confused me, because we were launching a boat, and boats are supposed to be happy." She smoothed her coat over her knees. "You have something else under that tarp. Under the canvas, in your arms. You've been holding it like a woman carrying a stove with the burner lit."

"I'm carrying salvage."

"Mm." Maren turned and looked at her directly then, and Casper understood that this was a woman who had been looked at by soldiers, by bureaucrats, by men with clipboards deciding where she was allowed to exist, and had survived all of them by refusing to look away first. "I am going to tell you a story, salvager, and you are going to listen, because you already know how it ends and you want to hear how it starts."

* * *

The story started, as those stories do, with water.

"There was a country here," Maren said, gesturing at the river with one crooked finger. "Not a grand one. A delta nation — mud and eels and grain barges and eleven thousand people who spoke Vessan and paid their taxes late and sang at funerals louder than at weddings. We had no army worth the name. We had the treaty, though. The Delta Compact. Signed between our council and the coastal powers, guaranteeing our rights to the riverlands in perpetuity. Perpetuity lasted thirty-one years."

"What happened?"

"What always happens. Someone upstream wanted what someone downstream had, and the paperwork got tired." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands had stopped smoothing her coat; they gripped the bench edge now, whitening. "There was a renegotiation. A new compact. And in the new document, we ceded everything — the channels, the floodplains, the ferry rights, the towns. All of it, signed by our own council head. Except he never signed it. None of them did. The signatures were forged, the ink was aged with tea and a warm iron, and within eighteen months the dispossession orders came down, and within two years there was no Vessany on any map, and the people who objected were relocated, which is a word they use for putting you on a barge and telling you not to come back."

Casper sat very still. In her lap, under the tarp, the chest pressed its cold weight against her thighs, and she thought about the documents inside — the ones she'd read by lamplight with her heart going like a diver's air alarm. The compact. The signatures. The elegant, looping hand that signed away eleven thousand lives.

"And you," Casper said. "You were relocated."

"I was twenty-two. I came back illegally four times. The last time they held me at the tollhouse for nine days." Maren smiled, and it was the worst smile Casper had ever seen, all the grief pushed down behind the teeth. "Now. You will show me what is under the tarp."

It wasn't a question, but Casper treated it like one. "Why would I do that?"

"Because you opened it already." Maren nodded at Casper's hands, at the way her thumb kept moving against the hasp, unconscious, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "You've been touching that clasp since you sat down. Whatever's in there, you've seen it, and it frightened you, and you're still carrying it instead of selling it or sinking it, which means some part of you hasn't decided whose it is." She leaned closer. Gray eyes, unblinking. "I can tell you whose it is. It's mine. It's ours. It belongs to everyone who ever sang too loud at a funeral in a language the maps forgot. Show me."

* * *

Here is what Casper Pemberton knew about belonging, in the year she turned thirty-four:

She knew it secondhand. Her father had belonged to the river the way other men belonged to churches, and it had killed him at the Kell's Folly rapids when she was nineteen, and she'd inherited his boat, his debts, and his talent for being forgettable. She'd built a life on the principle that the best thing to be, in any jurisdiction, was no one — no record, no flag, no name on any ledger except the ones that paid. Dive logs and paid miles. She could tell you the salvage value of a drowned ferry to the cent. She could not tell you the last time anyone had said her name in a tone that expected an answer.

And here is what happened at Ostrev's Ferry, at nine-forty in the morning, on a bench that smelled of creosote and old rope:

She pulled back the tarp.

Maren Ostrev made a sound Casper had never heard a human being make. It was small. It was the sound of a door opening after forty-one years, hinges seized, and it came out of the old woman like something being born rather than something being spoken. Her hands hovered over the chest lid — over the circle, the forked river, the worn letters — without touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping child you're afraid to wake.

"May I?" she whispered.

"It's your seal," Casper said. "You tell me."

Maren touched the lid with both palms flat, fingers spread, and closed her eyes, and for a long moment the only sound was the river slapping the pilings and the far-off grind of the fuel pump. Then she opened her eyes, wet and furious and clear, and said: "Open it. Please. I need to know if it's what I think it is."

Casper worked the hasps. The lid came up on the smell of lead and cold water and paper kept dry against all odds, and Maren leaned over the documents inside — the compact, the map rolled in its wax-sealed tube — and read them the way drowning people breathe, fast and desperate and not caring what it cost.

"This is the Compact," she said. Her voice shook. "This is the actual Compact. Do you understand? They burned the copies. They burned everything, every record, every registry, every parish book — there is not one sheet of official Vessan paper left in existence, they made sure of it, that was the whole point, you cannot claim a country that has no receipts—" She stopped. Pressed her fist against her mouth. Started again, lower. "And this map. The wax seal. This is the archive mark. This leads to the source."

"The source of what?"

"The river. The glacier. There was a vault, before the erasure — a records vault carved into the bedrock above the flood line, where the founding documents lived. Sealed. Everyone said it was a myth by the end. Everyone said a lot of things by the end." Maren looked up, and what was in her face then was not gratitude and was not grief and was not anything Casper had a word for. It was gravity. "Young woman. Casper, isn't it — the marina book says Pemberton. Listen to me. What you pulled out of that channel is not salvage. It is a country's whole testimony, and there are perhaps six people alive who know what it means, and I am one of them, and you are holding the other five in a box."

"That's dramatic."

"I am eighty-one years old. I have earned the right to be dramatic." Maren put her hand on Casper's wrist — light, dry, strong — and the touch landed somewhere Casper hadn't known was exposed. "Come with me. No — listen. Don't take it to the authorities; the authorities wrote the forgery. Don't sell it; whoever buys it will burn it. Bring it to the delta towns. There are Vessan grandchildren everywhere, salvager. They changed their names and learned new songs and they kept one hymn each, secretly, the way you keep a seed in your pocket through a winter in prison. Show them this and watch what wakes up."

The river slapped the pilings. Somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded, long and low, and Casper felt it in the bench, in the chest, in her sternum.

"I don't belong to anything," she said. It came out flatter than she intended. "I'm not Vessan. I'm not anything. I dive wrecks and I sell metal and I go where the work is. This—" she tapped the chest lid, once, "—is cargo. Heavy cargo. That's all it's ever going to be."

Maren withdrew her hand slowly, and Casper felt the absence of it like a draft.

"No," Maren said. "It isn't. But you're not ready to hear that, and I'm too old to wait for you, so I'll say the rest quickly and you can decide in your own time." She stood, unhurried, and looked down at Casper with the gray eyes. "The men who forged that treaty are dead, but the men who profit from the forgery are alive, well-funded, and patient, and when they learn this chest exists — and they will learn, they always learn — they will not offer you money twice. They will offer you the bottom of the channel you pulled it from. So decide faster than feels comfortable, Casper Pemberton. Belonging is a hand across water. You can refuse it. But the water doesn't care, and neither does the current, and neither, I promise you, do they."

She walked off up the gravel path toward the three buildings, small and straight-backed, and did not look back, and somehow that was worse than if she had.

* * *

Casper rewrapped the chest and sat with it on the bench for a long time after the old woman was gone.

She inventoried, because inventorying was what she did instead of feeling. One chest, lead-lined, sealed provenance unknown. Documents: one compact bearing signatures she now knew were forged, though she'd half-suspected it herself — the ink had sat wrong on the page, pooled at the ends of strokes like it had been coaxed. One map, wax-sealed, unread. One tarp with a dead nation's flag on it, which she had owned for six years without knowing what she was carrying, which felt suddenly like the defining fact of her life.

One old woman who had put her hand on Casper's wrist and left a warmth there that was still going, like a coal in wet cloth.

Belonging is a hand across water. You can refuse it.

She had refused it. Cleanly, professionally, the way she refused everything that couldn't be invoiced. So why did her hands keep drifting to the hasp?

At ten o'clock the crossing came — mostly — and Casper didn't take it. At ten-twenty she carried the chest back down the ramp and stowed it in the bilge under the false floor, and told herself that was refusal too, just the practical kind.

At ten-forty, the navy launch came around the bend.

Gray hull, twin diesels, running slow and official, the way government boats move when they're not in a hurry because they already know where you are. Casper stood on her deck with a coil of rope in her hands and watched it come, and behind her, in the bilge, forty pounds of stolen country sat quiet as a held breath.

The launch slowed. A young officer in the bow raised a hand — friendly, or dressed as friendly — and called across the water:

"Afternoon! Salvage vessel Pemberton? We'd like to ask you a few questions about your dive yesterday."

Casper smiled the smile she'd paid for, the one that made her no one, and picked up her mooring line as if she had all the time in the world.

"Sure," she called back. "Come aboard. Ask me anything."
