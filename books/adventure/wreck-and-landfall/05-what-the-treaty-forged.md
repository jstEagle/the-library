## 5. What the Treaty Forged

Maren Ostrev's hands shook, but not from age. Casper watched them move across the galley table — steady enough to lay the documents flat, careful as a surgeon — and understood that the tremor was something older than the woman. It was forty years of waiting arriving all at once.

"Turn the lamp," Maren said.

Casper angled the brass light over the treaty pages. The chest sat open behind them, its lead lining dull as a closed eye. Outside, the river moved in the dark the way it always did, indifferent, carrying its silt and its secrets down toward the delta towns that had never heard the word *Vessany* spoken aloud except as a joke or a curse.

"This is the signature of Chancellor Ivo Rennek," Maren said, touching one line of ink without touching it. Her finger hovered a hair above the paper. "He was eighty-one years old when this was signed. He had palsy so bad he could not hold a spoon. My mother served at his household before the drowning. She used to say his hand looked like a bird trying to land on a branch."

"So someone signed for him."

"Look closer." Maren produced a jeweler's loupe from her coat pocket — an old one, brass, worn smooth by decades of use. She held it over the signature. "A forger copies the shape of a hand. He cannot copy its hesitation."

Casper leaned in. Under magnification, the letters were beautiful. Too beautiful. Every stroke flowed into the next with a confidence no dying man's hand would have. And there — where the pen had lifted between two words — the ink pooled in a way that said the writer had paused not from weakness but from calculation, checking his work against a model.

"There are three signatures on this page," Maren said. "Rennek. Admiral Sost. The Vessan Land Commissioner, Deyra Kull. All three written in the same hand." She sat back. "One man forged a nation's surrender in an afternoon."

Casper straightened. The cabin felt smaller than it had an hour ago. "The navy has this treaty in their archives. Certified copies. They've had it for forty years."

"They have what they were given." Maren's voice stayed level, but something under it had begun to burn. "The delta was Vessan ground. Eleven thousand people held title to those banks — fishing rights, ferry rights, the toll roads along the levees. When the floods came and the old capital went under, the survivors were scattered upriver like seed. And while they were burying their dead, men with navy letterhead walked through the drowned districts and declared the land abandoned. Abandoned." She laughed once, a dry sound with no humor in it. "My father's boatyard stood above the waterline. His family had worked that bank for six generations. It was declared abandoned because he was in a refugee camp two hundred miles north when the surveyors came."

"And this document made it legal."

"This document made it look legal." Maren turned to the final page, where the wax seal sat in its protective sleeve — the seal Casper had first seen stamped into the chest's lead lid, a river delta inside a circle of stars. "The original treaty — the true one, negotiated in good faith between the provisional government and the navy commission — granted the exiles right of return and restoration of title. That document existed. Delegates saw it signed. But the copy that entered the naval registry is this one." She tapped the forged page. "And the original was sealed in the national archive at the glacier source, pending ratification, the same week the archive was closed. Sealed away. Forgotten. Or made to be forgotten."

Casper looked at the map beneath the documents — the wax-sealed sheet she'd photographed twice and hidden twice since pulling it from the channel bottom. The route it drew ran upstream through every checkpoint and restricted reach she knew, ending at a blue mark beneath the glacier's toe. An archive carved into bedrock, if the map told the truth. A vault full of paper that could unmake forty years of other people's paper.

"You want me to take you there," Casper said.

"I want you to understand what you pulled out of that river." Maren met her eyes. "You think you found salvage. You found eleven thousand people's lives folded into a box. Everything they lost is in there — and everything they can get back."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere out on the water, a night bird called, and Casper thought about her dive log, her paid miles, the clean transactional life she'd built one job at a time. She'd taken Vey's money three days ago. Triple rate to forget. The bills sat in a waterproof bag beneath her bunk, and every time she brushed her teeth she could feel them through the hull, humming.

"The navy will come," Casper said. "When they do, you can't be here."

"I have been nowhere for forty years," Maren said. "I can be nowhere here as well as anywhere."

* * *

Lieutenant Commander Elias Brandt had read the incident report four times, and each time the numbers refused to behave.

The dredge operator — Pemberton, C., licensed salvage diver, seventeen years clean record — had logged a find at the Channel wreck site: ferrous object, lead-lined, off-survey position. Standard procedure required notification within seventy-two hours. Pemberton had notified within six. That was the first thing that didn't fit. Contractors who found things off-survey usually didn't notify at all, or waited until they'd stripped whatever was worth stripping.

The second thing that didn't fit was the response. Within a day of the notification, Aldous Vey's office had cut a private settlement — triple rate, confidentiality clause, no navy oversight. Brandt had seen Vey's settlements before. They were how the navy's dirtiest work got done without anyone's fingerprints. Vey didn't buy silence because silence was valuable. He bought it because noise was dangerous.

Brandt stood on the dock in the gray morning light, cap brim low, and looked at Casper Pemberton's barge. It was a working vessel, nothing more — crane arm, dive gear, a tarp lashed over something amidships that might have been equipment and might not. A woman came out of the wheelhouse carrying two mugs of coffee, and stopped when she saw the uniforms.

"Lieutenant Commander Brandt, Naval Rivers Commission." He showed his credentials. "Routine follow-up on your Channel notification."

"Already settled," Casper said. "Your contractor handled it."

"He did." Brandt let that sit. "Which is why I'm here. The object you recovered — describe it."

"A chest. Lead-lined. Old."

"Contents?"

"I don't open other people's property. I notified, your man paid me for my time, and the chest went where he wanted it to go." She handed him one of the coffees, unasked, and the gesture was so smoothly practiced that Brandt understood it for what it was: a dock worker managing an inspector, a skill learned young.

"Where did he want it to go?"

"I don't ask questions I'm paid not to ask." She drank her own coffee. "Is there anything else, Commander? Tide's turning and I've got a mooring to check."

Brandt looked past her at the tarp amidships. The wind lifted its corner for half a second — long enough to show rope, packing crate, and a sliver of dark wood that could have been anything. Long enough for him to notice that the tarp was new, and the lashings were done in a hurry, and there was a second dinghy tied astern that hadn't appeared in any of the barge's registration photos.

"Ms. Pemberton. Do you know a Maren Ostrev?"

Something crossed the woman's face — fast, controlled, but not fast enough. "Should I?"

"She's been asking after you at the ferry landing. An old woman, Vessan-born, asks a lot of questions about salvage finds." He watched her. "The Commission keeps files on people who ask questions about restricted sites."

"Then you know more about her than I do." Casper set her mug on the rail. "If that's all—"

"That's all." He turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth — the Channel site wasn't just restricted. It was redacted. There's a difference. Restricted means keep out. Redacted means we're pretending it isn't there." He let the words hang in the river air. "You notified us in six hours. Most people wouldn't have. Whatever else you are, that tells me something."

He walked back down the dock feeling the weight of her eyes between his shoulder blades, and he did not write in his notebook until he was in the car, and even then he wrote only one line:

*Tarp too new. Ask again in 48 hours.*

He didn't report the second dinghy. He told himself it was because it proved nothing. He was almost sure that was the reason.

* * *

"They'll be back," Casper said, lashing the tarp down properly now, with time and care. "That officer. He saw the dinghy."

Maren sat in the wheelhouse doorway with the treaty pages sleeved in plastic, reading them the way other people read letters from the dead. "Then we should decide what you're going to tell him next time."

"The truth gets my license revoked and probably worse. A lie buys me a day." Casper cinched the last knot. "There's a third option. We're gone before he comes back."

Maren looked up. For the first time since she'd boarded, hope showed plainly on her face, unguarded, and it made her look younger and more breakable at once. "Upstream."

"Upstream." Casper said it like a diagnosis. "Through Kell's Folly, past the tollhouse, into water nobody runs without permits I can't get anymore. Because Vey owns the downstream exits, and the navy owns the paperwork, and the only thing in this whole river system that scares either of them is sitting in a vault under a glacier." She dropped into the pilot's chair and stared at the chart. Forty years of other people's lies, sealed in ice. If it was even there. If the map was real. If the ice hadn't eaten the door a century ago.

"If it's there," Maren said quietly, reading her face, "it ends everything. The dispossession voided. Titles restored. My father's boatyard returned to his grandchildren."

"And if it isn't, we're two women in a stolen boat running forbidden water with a forged treaty and a dead nation's map." Casper started the engine. It caught on the second try, coughing river mud. "I've run worse odds for less reason."

She took the barge out against the current as the sun came up, and behind them, small in the haze, a navy launch rounded the point toward her empty dock.

* * *

Brandt stepped aboard the barge at 0700 and knew within thirty seconds.

The coffee mugs were washed and inverted on the rail — two of them, and he'd drunk from one yesterday. The galley smelled of tea, not coffee; the tin on the shelf was a cheap Vessan blend sold at exactly one shop in the delta, the one near the ferry landing. On the chart table, a course was penciled and erased, but erasure leaves grooves, and he ran his thumb over them: upstream. Upstream through Kell's Folly, which no sane pilot ran, toward the source, which no permit allowed.

An old woman's shawl hung on a hook by the wheelhouse door. Gray wool, fringed, embroidered at the hem with a pattern of interlocking circles around a delta. Brandt had seen that pattern once before, in a deaccessioned file he should never have opened — the flag of a country that officially did not exist.

He stood in the empty wheelhouse for a long moment, hearing the river slap the hull.

His orders, if he reported this, were simple: flag the vessel, alert Vey's office, stand down. He had read those orders twice already this week, forwarded from channels that bypassed the Commission entirely, and both times the same question had lodged in him like a splinter: *why does a salvage chest need a paramilitary contractor?*

He took out his notebook. Wrote: *Vessel departed approx. dawn, upstream, two aboard.* Then, after a pause, underneath:

*No alert sent. Watching.*

He put the notebook away, climbed back into his car, and sat with the engine off, looking at the water going by. Forty years ago, men in uniforms like his had stood on docks like this one and watched eleven thousand people lose everything, and had signed the papers that made it official, and had gone home to their dinners.

Brandt started the car. He didn't know yet what he was going to do. But he knew, with the certainty of a man reading water before a rapid, that the past had stopped being past — and that he was already in the current, whether he meant to swim or not.
