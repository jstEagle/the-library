## 6. Burn Notice

The smell woke her before the sound did.

Diesel and burning varnish, thick enough to taste, seeping through the cabin boards of the *Maren's Luck* — no. Not that boat. The salvage barge. Her barge, the *Pemberton*, tied up at her own dock on her own stretch of river, and it was burning.

Casper came off the bunk with the dive knife already in her hand, a reflex she didn't remember reaching for, and hit the cabin door into the night. Heat rolled across the water. The barge was an orange skeleton against the dark, flames climbing the crane mast like they'd been paid to do it, and beneath the roar of the fire there was another sound — an outboard, low and unhurried, moving away downstream like a man strolling out of a room he'd finished with.

They hadn't even waited to see if she'd burn with it.

She stood on the dock in her bare feet and watched eleven years of work go up. The winch. The decompression gear. The dredge that had hooked the chest off-survey in the first place. Her father's brass compass, mounted on the wheelhouse bulkhead since she was nine years old, melting in its bracket. She catalogued it all the way you catalogue anything — item, condition, replacement value — because that was how Casper Pemberton survived the world: she priced things before she could grieve them.

Then she stopped pricing.

Because the chest was still aboard.

* * *

She'd moved it two nights ago without letting herself think about why. That was the thing about instinct — it worked best when you didn't interrogate it. She'd wrapped the lead-lined box in oilcloth, rowed it out in the dark, and sunk it under the third piling of the old ferry landing, thirty feet down in cold black water where no navy sonar operator would bother looking, because who hides treasure at a derelict dock nobody uses?

The map was different. The map was in the cabin of the *Maren's Luck*, tucked inside the false panel behind the water tank, because some part of her had understood — even while cashing Aldous Vey's check, even while telling herself triple rate was just good business — that the paper was worth more than the metal, and that the men who bought silence always come back for the receipt.

The fire lit the river orange for half a mile. She could see the heat shimmer standing off the water. And she could see, moored at the fuel dock two hundred yards downstream, a black-hulled patrol skiff with no running lights and two silhouettes aboard, watching.

Not fleeing. Watching. Making sure.

Her truck keys were in the barge office. Her cash roll was in the barge office. Her spare tanks, her logbooks going back eleven years, the photograph of her father standing on the deck of a smuggler's runabout with his arm around a girl who looked like nobody in particular — all of it in the office, all of it feeding the fire now, and the two silhouettes on the skiff were waiting to see whether she'd run toward it.

So she ran away from it.

Barefoot, silent, along the dock's underside walkway to where the *Maren's Luck* sat low on her mooring lines. Thirty seconds later she had the water tank panel open and the oilskin packet against her ribs, and forty seconds after that she was casting off with the engine at idle, no lights, sliding upstream on the current's edge while the barge that had been her whole visible life collapsed behind her into sparks.

The skiff's outboard coughed to life when the roof beams went. Too late. She was already past the point, hugging the shadowed bank, running dark like her father had taught her before she was old enough to understand what running dark meant.

* * *

Two miles upriver, in the reeds below the abandoned quarry dock, she finally let herself stop shaking. Not from cold. From the other thing.

She opened the logbook.

It was the only one she'd saved — not the business logs, the other one, the one she kept in the cabin and told no one about. Her father's. Water-stained, salt-crusted, half the pages fused. She'd pulled it from the wreck of his runabout herself, fourteen years old and breathing off a garden hose rigged to a compressor that should have killed her, and she'd carried it through every move, every sale, every fresh start since.

She clicked on the red lens of her headlamp and read by a light that wouldn't carry.

*June 3. Ran the Narrows dark again. Current's up 4 knots after the rain. Boy at the tollhouse waved us through — he knows Papa's money. Cargo dry. Mama says this is the last season. I say so every year.*

That was his handwriting at nineteen. She turned pages. Years of them. Fuel runs, cargo runs, names she'd never known, debts marked and crossed out. He'd been a boy once, running this same river, learning its moods the way she'd learned them later without knowing she was learning his life over again.

*Sept. 14. Vessan family paid double to move their goods past the checkpoint. Old woman kissed my hand. Papa laughed about it for a week. Says the river remembers who was kind.*

The old woman. Forty years ago. Maren Ostrev's mother, maybe. Or Maren herself, young, kissing a smuggler boy's hand in gratitude, and the river remembering.

Casper closed her eyes. Opened them. Kept reading.

*Oct. 2. Last entry. Navy's got new scanners at Kell's Folly. Papa says we go anyway — the Vessan cargo won't keep, and they're paying triple.*

Triple rate. Her own words to Aldous Vey, forty years later, and the price of them written in her father's hand a lifetime before she was born.

He died at Kell's Folly. Everyone said the rapids took him. She'd believed it her whole life — the river as a mindless god, indifferent, fair in its way because it killed everyone equally. But her father hadn't drowned at Kell's Folly. She knew that now the way you know things in your bones. His boat had been found burned. Burned, and sunk, and the official report said engine fire, and everyone nodded, and a fourteen-year-old girl learned to be no one because being someone got your father killed.

Vey's men hadn't invented anything tonight. They were just the latest hands on an old torch.

She sat in the dark cabin with the logbook open on her knees and let the grief come, and then she let it change, the way grief does if you hold it long enough — hot to cold, sorrow to something with edges. Fury, but not the wild kind. The kind that navigates.

"They burned him," she said aloud, to the empty cabin, to the river, to the man in the photograph who would never know his daughter had found the same papers he'd once carried for people who kissed his hand. "Same play. Same river."

And the answer, when it came, wasn't a voice. It was a heading. Upstream. Toward Kell's Folly, toward the tollhouses, toward the glacier source and the sealed archive and every checkpoint between here and there designed to stop exactly what she was about to do.

Every sensible route ran downstream — to the delta towns, to lawyers, to courts, to the slow machinery that had buried Vessany once already and would bury it again while the paperwork aged. Downstream was where Vey's money lived. Downstream was where her father's killers had always operated, comfortable, untouchable, buying silence at triple rate.

Upstream was suicide.

She started the engine.

* * *

Dawn found her at the mouth of the gorge above the quarry bends, the *Maren's Luck* riding the first white water, and Casper Pemberton standing at the helm with her father's logbook lashed to the console beside the chart of a drowned nation.

She'd done the math twice. Fuel for three days if she ran easy. Food for five. One change of clothes, the dive knife, the map, the chest still sleeping under the ferry landing pilings — she'd have to go back for it, which meant going back past whatever remained of her dock, which meant Vey's watchers, which meant a plan she didn't have yet.

One problem at a time. That was another inheritance from the logbook. *Fix the leak before you curse the sea,* her father had written once, in a margin, next to nothing in particular. It had stayed with her anyway.

The gorge walls rose on either side, grey stone streaked with the rust-red of old iron seams, and the river narrowed and quickened beneath her hull. Somewhere ahead was Kell's Folly, where the current had taken — or been blamed for taking — everything she'd ever loved first. Somewhere ahead was an old woman who had recognized a seal on a tarp and offered belonging like a hand across water, and whom Casper had refused, because refusing was what she did, because needing people was how you ended up watching things burn.

But her father had run Vessan cargo. Her father had been kind to exiles, and exiles had remembered. And somewhere in those water-fused pages there might be more — names, routes, the old paths past the checkpoints that the navy had never mapped because the navy only mapped what it wanted to own.

The first rapid hit hard, spray over the bow, and she rode it down with both hands on the wheel and her teeth set, and when the boat came level again she was laughing — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman who has just understood that she is no longer running from anything.

Let Vey send his skiffs. Let Brandt come with his questions and his careful doubt. Let the whole contracted machinery of the state come up this river after her.

They were all downstream thinkers. Every one of them. Men who bought silence, officers who followed orders, contractors who burned barges and called it insurance. None of them understood yet that the river ran both ways, and that the person coming up it had nothing left to lose and a map to a country that officially did not exist.

Behind her, far downstream, smoke still stood over the black ruin of the *Pemberton*. Ahead, the gorge narrowed, and the water quickened, and the first white throat of Kell's Folly roared somewhere beyond the bend like something waking up.

Casper opened the throttle.

She'd need crew. And there was exactly one person on this river who knew these waters from before the maps changed — one old woman with a seal on her memory and a grudge forty years deep, waiting at a ferry landing for an answer she'd already been refused once.

Casper turned the bow toward the landing.

"Hope you're still awake," she said to the morning, and to the dead, and to whoever was listening on the water.
