## 7. Downstream Is a Trap

Casper counted the exits the way she counted dive tanks: by what they'd give her and how fast they'd run out.

There were four ways off the river from Miller's Bend. South, past the tollhouse at Kell's Folly, where Vey's subcontractors ran a checkpoint dressed up as a navigation fee station — she'd paid it a dozen times without looking twice at the men in gray coveralls who logged hull numbers into a tablet. East, overland through the marsh roads to Halloway Junction, which meant abandoning the boat and every tool she owned, walking into a town with one gas station and three people who owed Aldous Vey money. West, down the delta channels toward the sea, where the navy kept its salvage yard and Brandt's questions were waiting like hooks left in the water. And north.

North was upstream. North was forty miles of whitewater, two portages, a canyon nobody ran anymore because the maps had been pulled after the Dispossession and never redrawn. North was the glacier source, the sealed archive, the place on Maren Ostrev's wax-stamped map that Casper had spent three nights telling herself was a dead woman's fantasy.

She sat in the wheelhouse of the *Maren's Luck* — no. The *Pemberton Girl*, her father's old name for her, the one she'd painted over eleven years ago and hadn't repainted since — and spread the river chart across the chart table with the chest locked in the hold beneath her feet.

Every exit south led through Vey's fingers. That was the arithmetic she couldn't argue with. The man had offered triple rate within eighteen hours of her surfacing with the chest, which meant he hadn't heard about it from her. Which meant someone else had been watching the channel that dawn. Which meant the network wasn't a net she could slip — it was the water itself.

She traced the chart with a blunt pencil. Tollhouse. Ferry landing. Fuel dock at Corren's Slough, where the owner leased his moorage from a holding company whose registered address matched, digit for digit, the shell firm on Vey's contract letterhead. She'd found that match last night at two in the morning, cross-referencing registry filings on a laptop older than some of her dive gear, and the feeling that had come up through her then was the same feeling she got at sixty meters when her light caught something moving in the silt: not panic, exactly. Recognition. The moment you understand the wreck is bigger than your plan for it.

Downstream was a trap. It had been a trap since the day she hooked the dredge line, maybe since the day she was born, if you believed her father had died running this same current away from men like Vey instead of just running stupid. She didn't believe that. Mostly.

That left north.

* * *

Maren Ostrev was sitting on the dock when Casper came out at first light, which meant she'd been there since dark, which meant the old woman had walked four miles from the ferry landing in the cold rather than wait to be invited.

"You've decided," Maren said. Not a question. She had a canvas bag at her feet and a wool coat buttoned to the throat, and she looked like a woman who had packed for a journey weeks ago and been waiting ever since for the boat to exist.

"I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided. You came out the forward door, not the cabin door. The cabin door faces downstream." Maren nodded at the chart roll under Casper's arm. "A woman facing downstream doesn't carry charts."

Casper looked at her. Seventy-some years old, hands like knotted rope, eyes that had watched a nation get erased and had apparently decided that watching was finished. "You don't know what's upstream."

"I was born upstream."

"The source is behind an ice dam that calving season opens for maybe six days a year, in a gorge the government closed in—" Casper stopped herself. Closed in the same year as the treaty. The same pen, probably. "It's restricted water. There are no charts. There are rapids at Kell's Folly that killed three rafters the last time anyone ran them, and that was twenty years ago with a full crew and current intel."

"And yet," Maren said.

"And yet nothing. I haven't said yes."

"You said yes when you rolled those charts." Maren picked up her bag. "I can stand here while you say no out loud, if it helps you. I'm good at waiting. I waited forty years."

Casper should have told her no. Every instinct she trusted — the ones that had kept her solvent and invisible and alive through eleven years of contract dives and quiet smuggling runs she didn't do anymore, mostly — said take the money, stay flat, be no one. Being no one had worked. Being no one had a price tag now, though, and the price tag was a burned barge and a contractor who knew her face, and invisibility, once punctured, didn't reseal. She understood that the way she understood pressure differentials. You didn't argue with it. You managed it.

"Rules," Casper said.

Maren went still, the way old people go still when stillness is a discipline.

"One: you don't touch the helm. Two: you don't talk to anyone at any stop — we're not stopping, but if we stop, you're my aunt visiting from upcountry and you keep your mouth shut. Three: the documents stay in the chest, the chest stays locked, and you don't ask me to open it again until there's a reason opening it won't get us both killed. Four—"

"I know rule four," Maren said quietly. "Rule four is that this isn't trust."

Casper felt the words land somewhere under her ribs. "You don't get to be offended. You followed me to my own dock."

"I'm not offended." Maren stepped onto the gunwale with a sureness that surprised them both, and stood there waiting to be permitted aboard. "I'm grateful. Trust is for people with time. We have a river." She paused. "The name of your boat. *Maren's Luck*. My mother's name was Maren. It's a common enough name among us that I shouldn't have flinched when I saw it on your tarp, but I did. Superstition." She smiled, small and dry. "I'm glad it's real."

"It's not real. I painted it last week."

"Why?"

Because a dead nation's seal on a tarp gets noticed, Casper almost said, and realized halfway through forming the lie that it wasn't a lie she wanted to tell, and that wanting not to tell it was its own kind of trouble. "Because names are free," she said instead, "and I was tired of mine."

Maren considered that with the patience of a woman who had spent four decades being patient at things. Then she stepped down onto the deck, set her bag by the wheelhouse door, and asked, "Which way is Kell's Folly from here?" — and Casper, before she could stop herself, pointed north.

* * *

They cleared Miller's Bend by seven, running the channel on the morning current with the engine at half throttle because noise traveled on cold water and Casper had no interest in announcing her heading to anyone with ears. The delta fell away behind them in gray-green slices — reed beds, stilt houses, the drowned orchard where the tops of apple trees broke the surface at low water like a congregation gone under. Maren stood at the rail and watched it all with an expression Casper couldn't read and didn't try to.

By nine they were into the narrows above Corren's Slough, banks closing in, current picking up, and Casper put the binoculars on the fuel dock as they passed — because even running north, you checked your back doors. Two gray boats at the moorage. Men loading crates. One of them had a radio handset pressed to his ear and was looking downriver.

Not at the dock. Downriver. Toward Miller's Bend. Toward her berth, empty since midnight.

"They'll find the slip cold," she said, mostly to herself.

"Then they'll look downstream," Maren said. "Everyone looks downstream. Water goes downhill; so does thinking."

"That's the problem." Casper handed her the binoculars. "Look east bank, next bend. Tell me what you see."

Maren raised the glasses, held them steady longer than Casper expected. "A track. Tire ruts. A culvert with new gravel on it."

"New gravel means maintenance. Maintenance means budget. Budget means somebody's spending money on a road that goes nowhere except a put-in ramp nobody uses." Casper took the glasses back. "Vey's network isn't a few men in gray coveralls. It's gravel and leases and holding companies and a tollhouse. It's infrastructure. He owns every way off this river that doesn't require paddling uphill through a war zone. If I run south, east, or west, I'm running inside his machine, and machines are built to process things like me."

"And upstream?"

"Upstream there's no machine. Upstream there's just water and ice and whatever's in that chest." She throttled up as the channel straightened. "And leverage. Because here's the thing he understands and I'm only now catching up to: the chest isn't worth anything destroyed. It's worth everything intact. Documents proving a treaty was forged — that's not treasure, that's a bomb with a fuse made of paper. He can't afford for it to reach anyone who'd act on it. Which means as long as I have it and I'm moving, I'm not prey. I'm a negotiation."

"You sound like a smuggler."

"I was raised by one."

Maren was quiet for a while. The gorge walls rose around them, basalt streaked white with bird lime, and the engine note flattened into the stone and came back doubled.

"My husband used to say something like that," she said finally. "He worked the ferries, before. He said a river doesn't care which way you're fleeing, only how well you read it." Her hands tightened on the rail. "He stayed when they came with the treaty papers. He believed paper. He believed that if the signatures were real, then real was enough, and if they were false, someone would say so loudly enough that it would matter." She turned her head, and her eyes in the flat morning light were the color of the water. "No one said so. Forty years, no one said so. So I will not tell you to trust me, Casper Pemberton. I will tell you that I have spent forty years learning exactly how much paper matters, and I will spend the rest of it making this paper matter. That is not trust either. That is arithmetic."

Casper held the wheel and did the arithmetic herself. An old woman with local memory of water nobody had charted in forty years, against a contractor with helicopters and a navy contract and every road south. It wasn't a hard equation. It was just the kind you didn't enjoy solving.

"Kell's Folly," she said. "You said you knew the water there. Before the closure. Before all of it."

"I ran it with my father every spring until I was nineteen. He called it the Folly because the mapmakers named it after a man who drowned proving it could be done." Maren's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "The trick is that there isn't one line through it. There are three, and they trade places with the season. The charts show last spring's river. I remember all of them."

"Then you're not cargo. You're crew. And crew takes orders."

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't call me that."

"What should I call you?"

Casper thought about it. The *Pemberton Girl* hummed under her hands, carrying a dead nation's chest and a living nation's witness, running north into water with no charts, pursued by a man who bought silence wholesale. Somewhere behind them, men in gray coveralls were finding a cold slip and starting their radios.

"Casper," she said. "Just Casper. Nobody's called me anything else in eleven years, and I'd like to keep at least that much of myself."

Maren nodded slowly, as if filing it somewhere permanent. "Just Casper," she agreed. "Now come see what the river's doing ahead, because the light on that water is wrong, and wrong light above Kell's Folly means the spring melt came early — which means the Folly is running high, and mean, and sooner than either of us planned for."

Casper looked north. The gorge bent away toward a horizon she couldn't see, and the water under the bow had gone from green to a pale, chalky, glacial white, and it was moving faster than it had been an hour ago, and it was going to keep getting faster all the way to the ice.

She pushed the throttle to full.
