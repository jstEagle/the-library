## 8. Rapids at Kell's Folly

The river told you things a day before it meant them. Casper had learned that from her father before she'd learned to read: the water going glassy and green where it should be broken, the birds lifting off in a single startled sheet, the sound changing — not louder, exactly, but deeper, like something clearing its throat downstream. By midmorning of their second day above the delta fork, the Kell was telling her everything at once.

She stood at the tiller with her weight low and her eyes on the far bank, where the tree line bent inward like hands cupping around a secret. The gorge ahead was narrow enough that the pines on either side seemed to lean over the water and whisper to each other. Between them, the river dropped.

"How much farther?" Maren asked from under the tarp amidships, where she'd spent the morning drying the documents they'd wrapped in oilcloth and nested in the chest like eggs.

"Listen," Casper said.

Maren listened. Her face changed — not with fear, Casper noticed, but with recognition. "Kell's Folly," the old woman said. "I haven't heard that sound in fifty years."

"You've *been* here?"

"I was born forty miles above it." Maren came forward on her knees, gripping the gunwale, her knuckles white but her voice steady. "My father ran this stretch every spring with timber rafts. He said the Folly was named for a man who tried to shoot it drunk on a bet."

"And?"

"He lost the bet. And most of himself." She squinted at the gorge. "Casper. How do you plan to run it?"

"The way anyone runs anything. Straight down the tongue, keep the boat pointed where I want to end up, pray between the holes."

Maren made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't atrophied in her decades of exile. "That is how the drunk did it."

* * *

They eddied out below a granite shelf to scout, which was more than the drunk had done. Casper climbed the shelf with a coil of line over one shoulder and found the whole ugly truth laid out beneath her.

Kell's Folly wasn't a rapid so much as an argument the river was having with itself. The channel split around a house-sized block of granite some ancient flood had torn off the wall and dropped in the middle of everything, and each side of the split was lying about being passable. The left looked open — a broad, inviting chute — but fed into a recirculating hole wide as her boat, the kind of hydraulic that took craft and held them, turning them over and over like laundry until the river got bored. The right side was a boulder garden, a chaos of gray teeth with maybe two feet of clean water threading between them, dropping fast enough that the spray at the bottom rose like smoke.

The tongue — the smooth V of water that marked the safe line — didn't exist. There was no safe line. There were only choices about how to die.

Casper crouched at the edge and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nine years old, running her father's smuggled cargo down black rivers with no lights. Water speed through the right channel: fast, eight knots at least, faster than she could correct with oars once committed. The entry gap: she could fit the boat through if she entered at the exact angle, no more than a few degrees off, at the exact moment. The exit: a lateral wave train that would try to broach her against the far wall.

It was runnable. On a good day, with a crew she trusted, with a boat she didn't need anymore afterward.

Behind her, gravel crunched. Maren climbed up onto the shelf with surprising sureness for a woman past seventy, moving like someone who'd grown up on rock before she'd grown old on flat land.

"That's the Widowmaker," Maren said, pointing at the left chute and its smiling hole. "My father called it that because it looks like the easy way. Four men went into that hole the spring after the Dispossession. They said the river was grieving too hard to be run." Her voice caught slightly on the word — *Dispossession*, capitalized in her mouth, a wound she carried like a name. "Three came out. Not the same three who went in, if you understand me. The river keeps what it likes."

"I'm taking the right side."

"I know. Look again. Where does the channel bend?"

Casper looked. "Right. It bends right at the bottom—"

"It bends right at the *middle*. Halfway down there's a ledge you can't see from here. My father called it the Liar's Shelf because the water pours over it so smooth you think it's just current. It's a four-foot drop onto rocks angled like a knife. Every raft that ever wrecked here hit the Shelf, not the entrance." Maren crouched beside her, and her hand came out — thin, spotted, certain — and traced a line in the air above the boulder garden. "Enter left of center. Hard left, harder than feels sane, because the current will push you right the whole way down and you have to spend your first hundred yards fighting it just to hold the middle. Then at the Shelf you go *with* the push, let it carry you right, and drop off the tongue of the Shelf itself — there's a slot, a boat-width, just left of the big splitter rock. You can't see it from up here. But it's there. It's always been there. The river hasn't moved in fifty years; only the names on the maps have."

Casper stared at the line Maren drew. It contradicted every instinct she had. Enter left of center in a channel that punished left? Fight the current instead of reading it?

But the old woman's finger didn't waver, and her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — somewhere fifty years upstream, to a father's voice and a raft deck and a country that still existed then, if only in the memory of people who ran its rivers.

"You've run this," Casper said slowly.

"I watched it run a hundred times. I steered it twice, when my father's hands were broken from the winter logging." She turned, and for the first time since the ferry landing, since the tarp and the seal and the begging, Maren Ostrev looked Casper dead in the eye without asking for anything. "You don't trust me yet. Good. Trusting me now would be foolish. But believe this: I have no reason to drown us. If I wanted the chest, I've had a hundred chances to take it while you slept. If I wanted you dead, I could have simply stayed quiet at the ferry landing and let Vey find you. All I want is to see my country's truth come down off that glacier. And I cannot do that at the bottom of a hole in Kell's Folly."

The wind came down the gorge and carried spray off the drop, cold as snowmelt, which it was. Casper thought about her father again — she'd been thinking about him too much since the fire, since the logbook interlude she couldn't stop rereading in her head — and about how he'd always said the river doesn't care whose blood is in the boat, only whether the hands on the lines know what they're doing.

Maren's hands knew.

"Okay," Casper said. "Left of center. Fight right. Drop the Shelf in the slot."

"Left of the splitter," Maren corrected. "And Casper — when we hit the wave train at the bottom, whatever happens, do not grab for the documents. Boats can be replaced. That paper cannot."

"I know what can't be replaced."

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "I think you learned it early, like me."

* * *

They stripped the boat to essentials before committing. Everything lashed down double, the chest cinched under the thwart with the oilcloth bundle inside it, bail bucket tied to a line, throw rope coiled on deck where Casper could reach it blind. Maren wedged herself low behind the center thwart, knees braced, both hands locked on the rails, and gave Casper a single nod that contained, somehow, an entire farewell and an entire faith.

Casper backed the boat out of the eddy and let the current take the bow.

The gorge swallowed sound first. The walls rose sheer on either side, wet granite streaked with orange lichen, and the roar built from a mutter to a argument to a shout, and then there was nothing in the world but water and the next three seconds.

Left of center. She pulled hard against the push, muscles burning, the boat crabbing sideways across the current while the boulders came up at her like a crowd pressing close. A gray fang slid past her starboard side close enough to touch. Another. The channel narrowed to a throat and the water accelerated and Casper stopped steering and started *choosing* — micro-corrections, half-strokes, the blade kissing the surface — fighting the river's shove with everything her shoulders had, holding the middle the way Maren had told her, harder than felt sane, insane, exactly right.

Then the Liar's Shelf appeared, exactly where the old woman said it would be, a smooth silver pour of water over an edge that meant violence. Casper let the current take her. It was the hardest thing she'd done in years — surrendering control on purpose, trusting a stranger's fifty-year-old memory over her own eyes — and the boat swung right, gathered speed, and dropped.

The slot was there. Boat-width, just left of the splitter, a ribbon of green in all that white. They went through it like a thread through a needle's eye, hull scraping stone on both sides at once, a shriek of fiberglass that Casper felt in her teeth, and then the world ended.

The wave train hit them broadside. The first wave lifted the stern and threw it; the second stood the boat nearly on its side; the third came down on top of them like a building falling. Casper was swimming in the cockpit, in the boat, in the river, all three at once, bailing with the bucket one-handed while the other hand held the tiller against nothing, against everything. Spray filled the gorge like weather. Somewhere behind her Maren was shouting — not screaming, *shouting*, words Casper couldn't hear over the water, words that were probably prayers in a language the maps had erased.

And then, all at once, it was over. The gorge opened, the gradient eased, and the Kell set them down in a long green pool as gently as a mother setting down a child. The boat sat low in the water, half full, but sitting. Floating. Moving.

Casper bailed until her arms shook. Maren wrung out her shawl with hands that trembled only a little, and when Casper finally looked at her, the old woman was laughing — actually laughing, a rusty, cracked-bell sound, tears cutting tracks through the spray on her face.

"My father used to say," Maren gasped, "that the Folly lets you know if you belong to the river. Fifty years I wondered which answer I'd get."

"We're not through the country yet," Casper said. But she was grinning. She could feel it on her own face, unfamiliar as a scar. "But I guess we belong to today."

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar in the lee of the gorge wall, and Casper did something she hadn't done since she was a child: she built a fire. Small, shielded by driftwood, invisible from the water — her father's rules, obeyed by reflex — but a fire nonetheless. The chest sat between them, opened, the documents spread on a tarp to finish drying in the heat. Maren turned the pages with tweezers improvised from a fishhook and a stick, handling each sheet like a relic, which, Casper was beginning to understand, it was.

"Tell me about the names," Casper said.

Maren looked up. "What names?"

"On the treaty. The signatories. You read them like a litany this morning. Who were they?"

For a long moment the fire cracked and the river murmured beyond the gravel bar, and Casper thought the old woman wouldn't answer. Then Maren began, softly, in the cadence of a woman saying evening prayers: "Aldous Renner, harbor master of Vessany-Port, who signed believing he signed a trade accord. Ilse Marchetti, who could not write and pressed her thumb, and whose thumbprint was copied onto the forgery after her death. Tomas Ostrev—" her voice caught, steadied, "—my grandfather, who was promised the delta ferries would stay in Vessan hands, and who drowned himself in the Kell the year the navy came to collect the tolls. Forty-one names. Forty-one lies. I learned them before I learned my letters, because my mother said a people who forget their dead get dispossessed twice."

Casper fed a stick to the fire. "My father had a list too," she heard herself say. "Not names. Bars. Every bar on every river from the delta to the headwaters, and which ones a man could drink in and which ones he'd get arrested in. He died with it memorized. Never wrote it down. Said paper gets you caught." She paused. "He was wrong, obviously. Paper is the only thing keeping any of us alive right now."

"Your father ran this river," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"Ran everything. Ran cargo, ran people, ran from the law and occasionally toward it when the money was right. Died at the Tollhouse Reach, going downstream, running a load he shouldn't have." Casper watched the fire. "I've spent my whole life going the other direction. Upstream. Away from where he died. And now look at me."

"Look at you," Maren agreed, and there was something in her tone — not pity, Casper realized, something better. Recognition. One keeper of lists acknowledging another.

They doused the fire before full dark and pushed off into the last hour of light, running easy water through a widening valley, the gorge falling away behind them. Casper took the tiller and Maren sat amidships with the dried documents rewrapped and stowed, and neither of them spoke, and it was the kind of silence Casper had never been able to afford before — the kind that assumed tomorrow.

The boat rode differently now. Lighter, somehow, though they'd lost nothing but sleep. She caught herself checking the lashings not out of fear but out of habit-as-care, the way you check on something you intend to keep. The chest under the thwart. The bail bucket on its line. The old woman with her closed eyes and her salt-and-ash hair drying by degrees in the evening wind.

Fragile. All of it fragile. But hers — theirs — for tonight.

Around the next bend, the valley pinched again, and Casper saw it before she understood it: a stone structure squatting on the right bank where the river funneled between bluffs, lantern-light in its windows, a chain stretched across the current marked with red buoys. A tollhouse. And standing on its landing, already watching them come, men with rifles slung across their backs and the particular patience of people paid to wait.

Maren opened her eyes and followed Casper's gaze.

"Tollhouse Reach," Casper said quietly. Her father's river. Her father's death. "Of course it is."

The chain waited. The lanterns burned. And the men on the landing raised one hand — not in greeting, Casper thought. In *counting*.
