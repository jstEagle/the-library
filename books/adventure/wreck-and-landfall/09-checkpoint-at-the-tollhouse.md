## 9. Checkpoint at the Tollhouse

The tollhouse sat where the river pinched between two bluffs, a stone throat with a chain across it and a man in a gray jacket standing on the near bank like he'd grown there. Behind him: a corrugated shed, a diesel generator humming, two skiffs tied bow-to-stern, and a radio mast that told Casper everything she needed to know before she could read the painted sign.

*HARLOW RIVER SERVICES — TOLL & SAFETY INSPECTION — ALL CRAFT STOP.*

Harlow River Services. She knew the name. Everyone who worked the water knew it — dredging permits, barge inspections, channel maintenance contracts. What most people didn't know was that Harlow had been bought out three years ago by a holding company with no name on its letterhead, and that the holding company's money smelled like navy subcontracting. Like Vey.

"That's not a toll," Maren said from the stern, where she'd spent the morning coiling line and pretending her hands didn't shake. "That's a filter."

"It's a toll," Casper said. "Tolls are what you call filters when you want people to pay for them."

She cut the engine and let the current carry them the last hundred meters, because arriving under power looked like intent and arriving adrift looked like fatigue, and fatigue was the costume she wanted. She pulled off her cap, dragged both hands through her hair until it stood up wrong, and unzipped her jacket halfway so she looked like every half-soaked salvager who'd ever begged a favor at a checkpoint. In the bilge beneath the deck plates, wrapped in oilcloth, the chest rode low and silent. The map was in her shirt, against her skin, where it had been for two days. It had stopped feeling like paper. It felt like a coal.

"Rules," she said quietly, without turning around. "You're my aunt. You don't talk unless someone talks to you first. If they ask where we're headed, I answer. If they ask about cargo, I answer. If anyone says the word *Vessany*, you look confused — bored-confused, not scared-confused. Can you do bored?"

"I've been bored for forty years," Maren said. "It's the one performance I can guarantee."

Casper almost smiled. She killed it before it reached her mouth. Smiling at checkpoints got you remembered, and being remembered was the same as being dead, just slower.

The chain came down as they approached — not raised, *lowered*, which meant the man in gray had already decided they were stopping whether they liked it or not. A second man stepped out of the shed carrying a clipboard and the particular posture of someone whose authority was rented. He had a scanner slung on his shoulder, civilian grade, the kind that read hull transponders and registration chips.

"Morning," the clipboard man said. "Harlow River Services. Toll's forty, inspection's free, refusal to inspect is a five-hundred fine and we turn you back." He said it like a liturgy, no eye contact, eyes already on the boat. "Name and registration."

"Casper Pemberton. Salvage vessel *Maren's Luck*."

She heard Maren inhale behind her and pretended she hadn't. Naming the boat after the old woman had been Maren's own dry joke three nights ago, back when jokes were still possible — *"Call it that and no one will ever guess."* Now it sat between them like a lit match.

Clipboard man wrote it down. "Salvage. You got a license?"

"Pemberton. License number four-four-one-seven." Her voice came out flat and practiced, because it was. Eleven years of checkpoints, harbormasters, insurance adjusters. Eleven years of being exactly legible enough to pass through. "Working the Kell's Folly stretch. There's a barge sunk below the second chute, pre-war hull, my client wants the winch hardware if it's still there."

"Lots of wrecks through Kell's Folly," the man said. "Lots of dead salvagers too. That water'll kill you this time of year."

"That's why I charge what I charge."

He looked up then, finally, and gave her the once-over that all checkpoint men gave women alone on the water — cataloguing, dismissive, concluding. She let him conclude. Underestimation was armor you could wear through any door.

"Forty for the toll," he said. "Then pull alongside the dock and keep your hands visible while Denny does the walk-through. Transponder scan too. Standard."

"Standard," she agreed, and handed over the cash, and thought: *this is the last time.* She didn't know how she knew. Some part of her had simply understood, somewhere back at the burning barge, that the woman who paid tolls and filed salvage reports and kept her head down was a woman who existed only inside a system, and the system had just tried to burn her alive in it. You couldn't be invisible to something that had already marked you. So the credentials were going into the fire today, one way or another. She just intended to get full value from them first.

They tied up at the dock. The one called Denny came aboard — big, slow-moving, boots that found the deck like he'd been born on one, which made him more dangerous than the clipboard man and less dangerous than whoever was listening on that radio mast. He walked the gunwales. He lifted the tarp over the forward locker. He glanced into the cockpit locker where Casper had staged, in plain sight, exactly what a guilty smuggler would hide badly: a case of untaxed spirits, half-wrapped, seal broken.

Denny looked at the case. Looked at her. Looked at the case again.

"Recreational quantity," Casper said, before he could speak. "Personal. I drink alone, it's cheaper than therapy."

Something moved at the corner of Denny's mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe — the look of one working man acknowledging another's small dishonesties. He closed the locker.

"Deck plates?" he asked.

"Bilge. You want to look at my bilge, be my guest. Bring a flashlight, it's black as sin down there and there's about six inches of river in it."

He did bring a flashlight. He did open the plate. And here was the thing Casper had staked two lives on: nobody wants to go into a stranger's bilge. The chest lay under thirty centimeters of cold water directly beneath where he crouched, lead-lined, dark, patient, and Denny played his light across the frame members and the stringers and the slime-slick limber holes, and saw nothing but what he expected to see, because expectation is the strongest material in shipbuilding. He dropped the plate. It clanged.

"She's ugly," he said, climbing out, "but she floats."

"That's the whole industry," Casper said.

The clipboard man ran the transponder wand along the hull and frowned at his reader. "Chip's registered out of Graywater Basin. That's a long way upstream of Kell's Folly."

"I move around."

"Client paying you to move around?"

"My client pays me to find things," Casper said. "Where they are is their business. Where I am is mine."

He held her gaze a beat longer than procedure required, and she watched him weigh it — the tired woman, the cheap liquor, the ugly boat, the forty dollars in his drawer — against whatever instinct was nagging him. Instinct lost. It usually did, when the other side had done its homework. She'd spent eleven years making sure her homework was boring.

"Clear," he said, and waved them off, and the chain rattled up, and the *Maren's Luck* slid through the stone throat of the river with a dead nation in her belly and a forged identity dying quietly in a government contractor's ledger.

Neither of them spoke until the tollhouse was a smudge behind the bluff.

"You used my name for the boat," Maren said at last. It wasn't an accusation. It was something closer to wonder, handled carefully, like an egg.

"It was funny three days ago."

"It was funny three days ago." Maren settled onto the thwart beside her, and her old hands found the tiller lines without being asked, trimming the sail they weren't even flying, just to have something to hold. "My mother named me for a saint who drowned. Did I tell you that? Maren of the Deep Water. They say she walked out into the flood to hold the church doors shut so the records wouldn't wash away. She drowned doing it. The records survived." A pause. "I used to think that was a tragedy. Now I think maybe it was instructions."

Casper watched the water. "We're past the tollhouse," she said. "No way back downstream now. Vey owns every landing between here and the delta. Every radio, every fuel dock, every pair of eyes that gets paid to squint at boats."

"So we go up."

"We go up." She said it and felt the word land somewhere below her ribs, heavy as the chest. Upstream meant the rapids. Upstream meant country where the maps went thin and the navy's jurisdiction went thinner and the river itself turned from employer to enemy. Upstream meant the glacier, eventually, and the archive, and whatever truth was worth when it was frozen into bedrock. "There's a pool above the second chute at Kell's Folly. If we make it by tomorrow night—"

The radio crackled.

It was the ship's set, the cheap VHF bolted under the dodger, tuned to the working channel the way every boat on this river was tuned, the way she'd been listening for eleven years without thinking about it the way you don't think about breathing. Static, then a voice, clipped and official:

*"All stations, all stations, Harlow River Services advisory. Be advised, vessel*Maren's Luck*, gray hull, single operator plus one, last logged passing the Tollhouse checkpoint at oh-eight-forty. Vessel may be operating on falsified credentials. Contractors are authorized to detain and hold for inspection. Reward posted. Repeat, reward posted—"*

Casper's hand was on the volume knob before the sentence finished. She clicked it dead. The river noise rushed back in — water on hull, wind in the rigging, Maren's breath.

"Falsified," Maren repeated softly.

"He read my name off the toll log." Casper's voice came out level, which surprised her, because underneath the level there was a roar. "Eleven years. Eleven years I built that name, license number, transponder chip, tax filings, the whole boring architecture of a person nobody looks at twice. And it took him eight minutes to burn it down and put a price on my head." She laughed, once, and it came out wrong. "Triple rate to forget. That's what he offered me. Turns out forgetting was the only thing he couldn't buy."

"What do we do?"

Casper looked upstream, where the bluffs drew together and the water went from brown to green and the first white thread of Kell's Folly showed faint and far, like a scar on the landscape.

"Now?" She reached into her shirt and pulled out the map, wax seal cracked, glacier-source markings faded but legible, and held it up between two fingers like a card she'd been saving. "Now we stop hiding it. Now we're exactly what they say we are."

* * *

Officer Dane Brandt arrived at the Tollhouse checkpoint ninety minutes later, driving a navy utility launch with the service's gray-and-white livery and a requisition chit in his pocket that authorized him to collect, by force if necessary, "salvaged materials of security interest" from any vessel matching the description of the *Maren's Luck*.

He was too late, and he knew it before he tied off.

The checkpoint men were nervous in the way men get when they've done something for money that they haven't yet explained to themselves. The clipboard man — a foreman named Gutch, according to the placard — met him at the dock with the logbook already open, already turned to the right page, already performing cooperation.

"Passed through at oh-eight-forty," Gutch said. "Woman and an older female, gray hull, twenty-six foot. We scanned her clean. Then the advisory came down from regional and we broadcast it, but—" he shrugged, a shrug that cost him something, "—she was gone. Current runs four knots through the narrows. Nobody catches four knots on foot."

Brandt flipped the logbook shut without reading it. "Show me the transponder record."

Gutch handed over the reader. Brandt scrolled. Registration chip: valid. License number 4417: valid, issued Graywater Basin, eleven years of clean renewals, zero flags. Salvage endorsement: current. The woman had passed every check his own office would have run, cleaner than most navy auxiliaries managed.

And Vey's office had flagged her as falsified within the hour.

Brandt stood on the dock a long moment, looking upstream at the green water and the distant white thread of the rapids, and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the burned barge. A salvager with a spotless record doesn't get flagged as a forger by accident. Flags like that come from somewhere. They come with paperwork, and the paperwork comes from contractors, and the contractors answer to a man who'd sat across from Brandt three weeks ago at a briefing table and said the words *chain of custody ends with me* in a voice that had made the room feel smaller.

"Your advisory," Brandt said. "It authorize detention by whom, exactly? Your men aren't sworn. You know that."

"Regional said—"

"Regional isn't sworn either. Regional is a procurement office." He handed the reader back. "If your skiffs intercept that vessel and someone drowns trying to 'detain' her, the inquest lands on this dock. On your logbook. Think about whether the reward covers that."

Gutch's face did something complicated. "You saying we shouldn't go after her?"

Brandt looked upstream again. Somewhere past that white thread, a woman was running a river that killed professionals, carrying something a billionaire's fixer wanted erased badly enough to burn a barge with people potentially aboard. He thought about the scorch patterns on the hull fragments he'd photographed, the ones that started at the fuel line rather than the galley stove. He thought about the requisition chit in his pocket and whose signature was on it and how many signatures stood between that signature and anything resembling law.

"I'm saying," Brandt said slowly, "that the advisory specifies *detain and hold*. It does not specify pursuit through Class Four water. Check your own regulations, Mr. Gutch. I'd hate to see you fined."

He untied, turned his launch upstream, and opened the throttle just enough to make honest wake.

Behind him, Gutch stood on the dock holding a logbook that recorded everything except the truth, and ahead of Brandt the river narrowed, and rose, and began to roar.

Upstream, at the mouth of Kell's Folly, Casper Pemberton lashed down everything that could float away and looked at the water and understood, with the calm of a woman who has finally run out of identities to lose, that from here on there was only one direction left in the world.

"Hold on to something," she told Maren, and pushed into the white.
