## 10. The Town That Remembers

The delta opened around them like a hand unclenching.

For three days the river had been braiding itself apart — one channel becoming two, two becoming five, the current slowing and spreading until Casper could no longer say where the water ended and the land began. Reeds stood in rows to the horizon. Herons lifted off the shallows on slow wings. The boat moved through a country that seemed to have been built by patience, silt laid down grain by grain over centuries, and Casper felt the strange loosening in her chest that always came when the river went soft: the sense that she had outrun something, at least for now.

"Slow down," Maren said.

Casper eased the throttle. Ahead, half-hidden by willows, a town sat low against the waterline — a cluster of stilt houses, drying nets, a church spire that leaned like an old man listening. No name on the charts. No fuel dock. The kind of place that existed only because nobody had bothered to remove it.

"There," Maren said again, and her voice had changed. Casper glanced over and saw the old woman gripping the gunwale with both hands, knuckles white, staring at the leaning spire as if it were a face.

"You know it?"

"I know the shape of it." Maren's accent thickened, the way it did when she forgot to guard herself. "Vessan towns all leaned east. Toward the sea we lost. The builders said it was so the dead could find their way home." She was quiet a moment. "My grandmother's village looked like this."

"It's not your grandmother's village."

"No." Maren didn't let go of the rail. "But someone built it."

Casper should have said no. Every instinct she'd sharpened over twenty years of running cargo and secrets said no — no stops, no witnesses, no towns where Vey's subcontractors might drink and talk. They had burned her barge, chased her through Kell's Folly, nearly caught her at the tollhouse. A delta town was a net waiting to close.

But they needed diesel, and food, and Maren hadn't slept properly since the narrows, waking with her hand pressed flat over her heart like a woman checking whether it still worked. And the current here was lazy and wide, and the light was going gold, and Casper heard herself say:

"One night. We keep the chest covered, we don't talk to anyone, we're gone before dawn."

Maren turned to look at her, and there was something in the old woman's face that made Casper look away — gratitude, or grief, or both wearing the same expression.

* * *

They tied up at a rotting public dock past the last of the fish racks. Casper stayed with the boat while Maren went for supplies, and for an hour nothing happened, which felt like a gift. She cleaned the intake filter and counted her remaining cash and watched egrets stalk the shallows, and told herself the tightness under her ribs was just exhaustion.

Then she heard the singing.

It came from up the bank, thin and high — a woman's voice, then others joining, a melody with a rising third at the end of each line that snagged something in Casper's ear. Not a hymn. Not anything she knew. It repeated, and layered, voices dropping out and coming back, and it sounded less like performance than like breathing, like something the whole town did without thinking, the way you breathe.

Maren came down the dock fast, a sack of groceries forgotten against her hip, her eyes wet and furious at being wet.

"They're singing the *Lament for the Drowned Quarter*," she said. "Do you understand? Forty years. Forty years since the deportations, and they're singing it at dusk like it's Tuesday."

"Maybe it's just a song."

"It is not just a song." Maren set the sack down with exaggerated care, the way she did when she was keeping herself from shouting. "That song was banned by decree. Singing it carried a prison term, once. So they buried it. They sang it inside, behind shutters, to their children as lullabies so the children would think it was theirs — and it worked, do you see? The children grew up thinking they'd invented it." She laughed, a cracked sound. "You cannot ban a thing people believe they made themselves."

Casper looked up the bank. Smoke from cook fires. The singing continuing, unhurried, indifferent to empires.

"Stay here," Maren said, and climbed the dock steps before Casper could answer.

She lasted four minutes alone. Then she followed.

* * *

The chapel was behind the fish racks, sunk into a hummock of old shells and packed earth, so small Casper would have taken it for a smokehouse if not for the door. The door was painted deep blue, and on it, in faded pigment, was a seal she knew better than her own handwriting now: the crowned fish of Vessany, coiled in a circle of water.

Inside, eleven people sat on benches facing a wall hung with river-worn stones, each one painted with a single letter of an alphabet Casper couldn't read. Candles burned in jars. And the singing — it was them, all of them, a whole congregation of ordinary people in work clothes, singing a song that had been illegal within living memory, in a language Casper had only seen on forged paper.

No one stopped singing when Maren stepped through the door.

But the song changed. The oldest man, white-haired, sitting nearest the door, faltered on a word — and then picked it up again, louder, and the melody bent toward Maren like a plant toward light. By the second verse everyone had turned. By the third, a woman in a yellow headscarf was crying openly, and no one minded, because half the room already was.

When the song ended, the silence was enormous.

Then the old man rose, crossed the floor, and took Maren's hands in his. He said something long and formal in Vessan, his voice shaking, and Maren answered in kind, and Casper stood by the door understanding nothing except that she was watching a woman come home to a country that no longer existed, in a room the size of a garage.

The old man turned to Casper. His eyes were pale blue and very direct.

"He asks who travels with you," Maren translated softly. "I told him you carry the chest. Word travels faster than boats in the delta, child. The tollhouse keeper has cousins everywhere."

Casper's stomach dropped. "Then Vey—"

"Has ears here, yes. But also—" Maren smiled at the old man, and he nodded gravely, "—has people who hate him more than they fear him. This is Tomas Ostrev. My husband's brother. I thought him dead forty years."

"He thought the same of me," Tomas said, in accented but fluent Common Tongue. He studied Casper with the unhurried attention of a man who had survived by reading faces. "You are the diver. The one who pulled our nation out of the mud."

"Just salvage," Casper said automatically.

Tomas smiled faintly. "Yes. That is what my people said too, for forty years. Just songs. Just names. Just a chapel behind the fish racks." He gestured her inward. "Come. Sit. Nothing is 'just' anything, diver. That is the first lesson of surviving an erasure."

* * *

They fed her fish stew and dark bread, and the room filled and emptied with neighbors who came to look at Maren Ostrev the way pilgrims look at relics. An old woman touched her sleeve and whispered a family name and asked if she was kin to the Ostrevs of the Lower Quarter, and Maren said yes, and the woman wept. Children were brought in and introduced formally, each one given a Vessan name alongside their registered one — *this is Ana, and this is Ana's true name, Annushka* — spoken quietly, like contraband, like treasure.

Casper sat at the edge of it with her bowl and watched.

She watched a girl of maybe nine correct her grandfather's pronunciation of a word and the whole room laugh with delight instead of shame. She watched a boy no older than that paint a letter-stone with careful strokes, learning an alphabet that existed nowhere official, passed hand to hand like a flame cupped against wind. She watched Maren surrounded, held, *claimed* — the old woman transformed, upright, luminous, forty years of exile burning off her like fog in sun.

And what Casper felt, sitting there, was not happiness for her.

It was hunger.

It rose in her slowly and then all at once, a physical ache under the sternum, watching a community hold its dead language alive in a hidden room behind fish racks. She thought of her father's logbook, the smuggler's shorthand, the miles and miles of entries in a code only two people had ever read — and how when he died, the code died with him, because there had never been anyone else. There had never been a room like this. There had never been anyone to teach her the words he used, or sing the things he sang, or tell her who she was beyond a name on a dive log. She had spent her whole life being good at being no one, and she had called it freedom, and here was a room full of people who had risked prison to be *someone*, together, and every one of them was richer than her.

"You're not eating," Maren said quietly, appearing beside her.

"I'm fine."

"You have the face of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

Casper almost laughed. "What's the sum?"

"That you are jealous." Maren said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Of this. Of us." She gestured at the candlelit room, the letter-stones, the children. "And you think that makes you small. It doesn't. Longing is just love with nowhere to land yet."

"I don't—"

"Casper." Maren's voice gentled further. "You pulled a drowned nation out of the riverbed and you've been carrying it alone ever since, refusing help from everyone, including me. Why do you think that is?"

Because help gets people burned. Because needing people gives them leverage over you. Because her father had needed crew, once, and one of them had sold him to the revenue cutters, and she'd learned at fourteen that belonging was just the distance at which betrayal became possible.

"Because I'm better alone," she said.

"Yes," Maren said. "So was I. Look how well it served me — forty years of safe, solitary, well-paid nothing." She squeezed Casper's arm. "Eat your stew, child. Inheritance isn't only paper. Whatever else that chest holds, this—" she nodded at the room, "—is also what was stolen. Remember that, when we decide what it's for."

* * *

Casper slept badly on the boat, the chest beneath her berth like a sleeping animal. Near midnight she gave up and went up on deck, and found Tomas Ostrev standing at the end of the dock, looking at the water.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked.

"In my experience," he said, "the river talks loudest at night. I was listening." He didn't look at her. "May I ask you something honest, diver?"

"People usually do anyway."

"The contractor. Vey. His men bought fuel at the trading post two bends downstream yesterday. Three of them, asking after a boat with a patched hull." He turned then, and his pale eyes were calm. "I am telling you because my niece asked me not to — she believes you must run before dawn and never owe anyone. But I watched you tonight, sitting at the edge of my chapel like a cat at a window, wanting in. And I think a woman who wants in will need somewhere to stand. So." He shrugged. "Stand here. If you choose."

Casper's mouth was dry. "Why help us? You'd be safer forgetting you ever saw us."

"We tried forgetting for forty years." He smiled, and it was an old, tired, immovable smile. "Look what it got us. A blue door behind the fish racks." He started up the dock, then paused. "One more thing. The song you heard — the Lament. Do you know why it rises at the end of every verse?"

"No."

"Because it isn't finished. It was written to be continued. Each generation adds a line." He tapped his own chest. "My grandmother added hers. I added mine. The song is still growing, diver. That is what a living country does." He walked away into the dark, humming.

Casper stood at the rail a long time, listening to the water slap the hull, thinking about a song designed to be unfinished, and about the entry she'd never written in any log: *belonging, location unknown.*

Then she saw the light.

Downstream, around the far bend, moving slow against the current — a searchlight sweeping the reeds in methodical arcs, and beneath it the low black silhouette of a hull riding heavier than any fishing boat had a right to ride.

Gunboat. Coming upstream. Toward the town, toward the dock, toward the blue door and the singing and the old man who had just handed her his safety like a gift.

Casper was moving before the thought finished, down into the cabin, shaking Maren awake.

"Maren. Get up. We're leaving now — and not downstream."
