## 11. Brandt's Doubt

The barge was still smoking when Brandt arrived.

Not burning — the fire had done its work in the night and moved on, leaving a blackened hull sitting low in the gray water like something that had drowned in place. Steam rose off the scorched deck planking in slow ribbons, and the smell was everywhere: diesel, char, and underneath it the sweet chemical tang of an accelerant that had no business being on a working salvage boat.

Brandt stood at the end of the dock for a long moment before stepping aboard. He'd been a navy officer for nineteen years, long enough to know that the first sixty seconds at any scene were the ones you never got back. He spent them looking, not touching. The mooring lines had been cut, not slipped. Cut from the water side, by someone in a low boat, which meant they'd come in quiet and left the same way. The burn pattern started aft, near the fuel locker, and climbed forward with the wind off the delta. Whoever set it had known boats. Had known *this* boat.

"Officer Brandt." A harbor constable met him at the gangway, young, nervous, holding a tablet like a shield. "We got the call at 0340. Owner wasn't aboard. Neighbors say the diver — Pemberton — she keeps odd hours, sleeps ashore sometimes."

"Does she sleep ashore last night?"

"No sign of her. Her truck's gone from the lot."

Brandt nodded and stepped past him onto the deck. His boots left prints in the wet soot. Somewhere downriver a dredge was running, its pump thudding through the morning fog like a slow heart.

He crouched at the origin point. The fuel locker door hung open, hinges warped, but the padlock hasp was intact — opened with a key or a shim, not forced. Inside, the jerry cans were melted into abstract shapes, but one had survived half-frozen in its own slag, and Brandt could see the manufacturer's stencil through the carbon: a military surplus mark. Navy-issue cans, five-gallon, the kind that came palletized out of three depots in this district alone. Not proof. Not yet. But a thread, and he had spent nineteen years learning to pull threads gently, because threads were all the truth ever gave you before it started lying again.

He photographed everything. Then he did the thing he'd been told not to do, in the meeting two days ago, in the room with the long table and the contractor's man sitting at the far end with his hands folded like a man at prayer: he looked for what he hadn't been sent to find.

* * *

The meeting came back to him in pieces as he worked.

It had been called by Aldous Vey's office — not the navy's, though it was held in a navy conference room, which was itself a small obscenity Brandt had chosen not to object to at the time. Vey ran river security under a services contract, which meant his people wore no uniforms and answered to no chain of command except an invoice. Brandt had been ordered to attend as liaison. Twelve officers in the room, and Vey standing at the head with a laser pointer he never used, talking about salvage irregularities in the lower channel.

"A restricted wreck site was disturbed," Vey had said. "Diver unknown. Recovered material may include items of historical sensitivity with ongoing legal ambiguity." He'd said it the way a man reads a grocery list. "Our interest is containment. Nothing recovered leaves the channel system. Anyone holding such material is to be considered in possession of government property. Questions?"

Brandt had asked one. "What's the legal basis for treating private salvage finds as government property?"

Vey had smiled at him then — a thin, patient smile, the smile of a man who has already priced your objection and found it cheap. "The basis is the contract, Officer. You're welcome to read it. Page forty-one."

Brandt had read page forty-one that night. It was worse than he expected. It wasn't a security contract at all; it was a suppression instrument dressed in security language, with cost-plus incentives tied to "incident resolution rates" — a bureaucratic machine that paid better the fewer questions anyone asked. And buried in an annex, referenced but never explained, was a single line item: *Vessany-era materials: disposition directive pending.*

Pending. For forty years, apparently, the disposition of a drowned nation's paper trail had been *pending*, and now a diver had hooked a chest off the bottom of the channel and the pending had come due.

He'd told himself it wasn't his problem. He was good at telling himself things. Nineteen years of practice.

Then the barge burned, and the telling got harder.

* * *

By noon he'd found the second thread.

It was in the water, actually — snagged on a piling under the dock, a scrap of synthetic fabric, dark green, torn free by whatever low boat had carried the arsonist away. Brandt bagged it with a gloved hand. Green was not a color Pemberton wore; her gear, per the harbor records he'd pulled, was orange and rust. Green was the color of Vey's subcontract crews. He knew this because he'd seen them at the tollhouse checkpoint last month, running "inspection" details in matching jackets, checking papers with a thoroughness that had nothing to do with customs law and everything to do with knowing who moved on the river and when.

He sat in his car at the dock's edge and laid it out flat on the passenger seat, the way he used to lay out evidence when he was a junior officer who still believed the chain of custody was a sacred thing.

Facts: A salvager disturbs a restricted site. Within days, a contractor offers her money — Brandt had seen the transfer authorization in Vey's expense queue, flagged only because the amount was absurd, triple rate for "site consultation." She takes it or she doesn't; either way, within a week her boat burns with military-surplus accelerant cans and a subcontractor's fabric left at the scene. And the officer assigned to investigate the fire is the same officer who asked the wrong question in the conference room.

That last part was the part that made his hands go cold on the steering wheel. Because it meant Vey hadn't just ordered the fire. Vey had ordered the fire and then handed the investigation to the one man in the district who might make trouble — not out of carelessness, but as a test. Or a trap. Either way, Brandt understood the shape of it: if he filed a clean report, he was manageable. If he didn't, there would be a page forty-two somewhere with his name on it.

He thought about his pension. He thought about his daughter, who was at university studying hydrology, of all things, and who had once asked him over dinner, aged nine, whether the navy ever caught the bad guys or just the ones who couldn't afford lawyers. He'd laughed then. He wasn't laughing now.

He took out his phone and photographed the fabric scrap, the burn pattern, the melted can, the cut mooring lines. Then he opened a folder he'd created two nights ago at his kitchen table, after reading page forty-one, a folder with no name and no sync enabled, stored locally on a device that had never touched the department network. He added the photographs to it.

The folder already contained three documents. Now it contained four kinds of evidence and one growing certainty.

* * *

In the afternoon he went to see the harbormaster, an old woman named Quill who had run the delta docks since before the treaty and remembered when the maps still said Vessany on them.

"Pemberton," Quill said, when he asked. "Good diver. Keeps to herself. Pays her fees early." She wiped her hands on a rag that had been black longer than Brandt had been alive. "You here about the fire?"

"I'm here about the fire."

"You're here about who set the fire." She said it without inflection, watching him. "Because everyone on this dock knows who set the fire, Officer. They've known since dawn. What nobody knows is whether saying it out loud gets their own boats burned next."

Brandt held her look. "If you tell me, I can protect—"

"You can't protect a fish from the river." She tossed the rag down. "But I'll tell you anyway, because I'm old and my boat's paid off. Two men came in the night before last asking about her schedule. Wore green jackets. Paid cash for fuel and didn't take a receipt. One of them had a tollhouse pin on his collar." She turned back to her work. "That's all you got from me. Write it down or don't."

He wrote it down. In the unnamed folder, later, in a note file titled with a string of numbers that meant nothing to anyone but him.

Walking back along the dock, he passed the charred hull again and stopped. Something about the stern bothered him. He climbed aboard a third time, ignoring the constable's questioning look, and knelt at the transom where the fire had peeled the paint back to bare fiberglass. Beneath the registration numbers — the official ones, current, issued to Casper Pemberton — there was a ghost. Older paint, sanding marks, the shadow of a previous name scraped off but not erased. He tilted his head until the low sun raked across it.

Letters. Half-legible. An old name, from another life.

He photographed that too, and sat back on his heels, and understood suddenly that he was not investigating an arson anymore. He was investigating a woman the river had tried to erase twice — once by fire, once by whatever had made her sand her own name off her boat — and he found, to his surprise, that he cared. That the caring frightened him more than Vey did.

* * *

At 1800 his orders arrived.

They came encrypted, standard routing, signed by Vey's operations desk with a navy countersignature that made Brandt's jaw tighten — the contractor signing through the chain of command like it was his own limb. The orders were simple. *Proceed upstream. Locate subject Pemberton. Recover government property. Detain if necessary.*

Detain. A civilian woman, detained by a contractor's asset with a naval liaison attached, on the strength of "government property" language that had no statute behind it. Brandt read the orders three times. On the third reading he noticed the routing: copies to Vey's desk, copies to the district legal office — and a blind copy line that went nowhere he could trace. Someone else was watching. Someone above Vey, or beside him, or behind him. The machine had many hands, and he couldn't tell yet which ones were clean.

He drafted his acknowledgment. His thumbs hovered over the screen for a long moment while the fog came up the channel and the dredge thudded somewhere in the dark.

Then he typed: *Acknowledged. Proceeding upstream at first light.*

And beneath it, in the local folder, in the note file, he typed something else:

*Subject is not the criminal here. Neither am I. Not yet.*

* * *

He drove home through the delta towns with their lights coming on along the levees, and he let himself think the thought he'd been refusing for two days.

The Vessans. He'd been a child when the nation was erased — he remembered the maps changing at school, the teacher peeling the old chart off the wall and putting up the new one, and no one explaining why a country the size of a river valley simply stopped existing between one semester and the next. He remembered the exile families at the ferry landing, selling what they could carry. He'd assumed, the way children assume, that it must have been necessary. That grown-ups in rooms with long tables would not dissolve forty thousand people's citizenship unless the paperwork demanded it.

Now he'd read page forty-one. Now he'd seen the annex line: *disposition directive pending.* Forty years of pending. You don't keep a directive pending for forty years unless the document it concerns is dangerous — unless somewhere in a sealed archive at the top of the river there sits a piece of paper that, if it ever saw daylight, would unmake every signature, every deed, every fence line in the valley.

And a contractor was burning boats to keep it buried.

Brandt parked outside his apartment and sat with the engine ticking down. His phone buzzed. A message from the duty desk: Vey's team had mobilized two gunboats and a helicopter, upstream tasking, effective immediately. Whatever Pemberton had taken, Vey intended to take it back with overwhelming force, and the navy's name would be stapled to the operation like a receipt.

He thought about his daughter again. Hydrology. She'd told him once, home for the holidays, gesturing with a wine glass: *"Water always finds the level, Dad. You can dam it, you can pipe it, but it finds the level."*

He got out of the car. Before going inside, he stood in the cold and did the thing that could not be undone: he copied the unnamed folder onto a second drive, and the second drive into his coat pocket, and he began — quietly, methodically, in the way of a man who has spent nineteen years following procedure and has finally decided which procedure matters — to build a case against his own employer.

Then he went in, packed a bag, and set his alarm for 0430.

Upstream, somewhere in the dark above the rapids, a burned-out salvager and an old woman were running a river that wanted them dead, pursued by men in green jackets. And tomorrow morning, Officer Elias Brandt would point his boat the same direction, with his orders in one pocket and the evidence against them in the other, and decide — mile by mile, rapid by rapid — which pocket weighed more.

At 0430 the alarm rang. At 0451, casting off, he radioed the district a single routine-sounding line, logged and unremarkable, that would matter enormously later:

*"Brandt, proceeding upstream on recovery tasking. Will report position at each waypoint."*

A lawful man, reporting faithfully. Every waypoint a breadcrumb. Every report a door he was leaving open behind him.
