## 12. Ambush at the Narrows

The gorge announced itself an hour before they reached it. Casper heard it first as a change in the river's voice — the broad mutter of the middle channel tightening into something harder, a hiss with teeth in it. Then the walls rose on either side, gray granite streaked white with old high-water marks, and the sky narrowed to a ribbon of late afternoon blue.

"Kestrel Narrows," Maren said from the bow. She'd been quiet most of the day, hands folded around a tin cup of tea gone cold hours ago. "My father portaged this once. Said the river runs faster than a man can pray."

"We're not portaging." Casper kept her eyes on the water ahead, reading it the way she read everything — depth, speed, threat. "We'd lose six hours on the scree and Vey's helicopter would find us standing in the open like tourists."

"And if he's already past us?"

"He's not." She was fairly sure. The helicopter had swept the valley twice that morning, a black insect against the peaks, but it had gone north, back toward the tollhouse, which meant Vey still thought they were behind him. Which meant they had until dark, maybe less.

The *Merganser* ran light now. They'd burned through fuel at Kell's Folly and again at the weir country above it, and every mile upstream cost more than the last. Casper had done the arithmetic at dawn and didn't like the answer: two days to the glacier source if nothing went wrong, and nothing had gone right since the night her barge burned.

The chest sat under her feet in the bilge, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sin. The map rode inside her jacket, next to her heart, where she told herself it was safest and knew it was really so she could feel it there.

"Slow down," Maren said suddenly.

Casper throttled back. Ahead, the gorge bent hard to starboard, and beyond the bend —

She saw it the way you see a reef: not the thing itself but the wrongness of the water over it. A smooth green tongue of current sliding out from the far wall, unnervingly flat, and beneath it a shadow the length of a bus. Sweepers hung off the left bank, dead pines leaning out over the flow like drowned men reaching for a handhold.

"That's not on any survey," Casper said.

"Rivers change." Maren set down her cup. "That one didn't used to be there. Rockfall, maybe. Or the spring floods moved it."

Casper eased the boat along the near wall, hugging the slack water, giving the shadow its due respect. As they slid past, she looked down into it — a slab of granite lying just proud of the surface, maybe eighteen inches under, waiting for some fool running fast at dusk.

"Good eyes," she said.

"I grew up on this water." Maren said it without pride, the way you'd say *I grew up breathing*. "You forget that about me."

Casper hadn't forgotten. She just hadn't decided yet whether it made Maren an asset or a liability. Forty years of exile, forty years of nursing a grievance like a coal under her coat — Casper trusted competence, but she wasn't sure yet what Maren would do when the choice came between the chest and something else. Something older.

They cleared the narrows as the sun dropped behind the western rim, and the gorge went cold all at once, shadow pooling in the bottom like spilled ink.

* * *

They made camp in a gravel pocket two miles on, where a side creek cut a notch in the east wall and the trees came down close enough to hide the boat's outline. Cold camp — no fire, no lamp, dinner eaten out of tins in the dark. Casper took first watch because she never slept well anyway, and sat on a rock above the waterline listening to the river and doing what she did instead of praying: counting assets.

One boat, half a tank. One rifle, twenty rounds. Food for four days if they were careful, three if they weren't. One chest full of a nation's stolen birthright. One old woman who talked in her sleep in a language Casper didn't know.

And somewhere behind them, closing at whatever pace Aldous Vey considered efficient, a gunboat.

She heard the engine at 11:40.

Not close — a low diesel thrum coming down the gorge, bouncing off the walls so it seemed to come from everywhere. Casper was on her feet before her mind finished the sentence, boots on, hand on Maren's shoulder, shaking her awake without a word. The old woman surfaced instantly, no grog, no questions. That frightened Casper more than the engine did. You only woke like that if you'd spent years sleeping in places where waking slowly got you killed.

"Downstream," Maren whispered.

"Coming up." Casper crawled to the edge of the gravel and looked back the way they'd come. Two miles of black water. And then, rounding the bend below the narrows, a running light — one, then a second, riding low and wide apart. Gunboat. Not Vey's helicopter money this time; this was a hull built for work, probably requisitioned through one of his subcontractors, crewed by men whose names weren't on any payroll she could subpoena.

"They'll run floodlights when they get to the straight," she said. "We're lit up like a stage the second they sweep this pocket."

"Can we outrun them?"

"In the dark, upcurrent, at half fuel? No." Her mind was already moving, though, sliding down channels of possibility the way her father's boats had slid down channels of water. "But we don't have to outrun them. We have to be somewhere they can't fit."

Maren followed her gaze to the side creek. In daylight it had been a modest thing, knee-deep over gravel. But the day had been hot on the glaciers, and evening melt came down hard, and even now Casper could hear it running fuller than it should, a rushing note under the river's murmur.

"That's not a channel," Maren said. "That's a chute. It'll be whitewater all the way up into the trees, and God knows what's fallen into it."

"It's also four feet narrower than their beam and going the wrong direction for their draft." Casper was already untying the painter. "Vey knows we're in the gorge by morning either way. Right now he doesn't know exactly where. If we sit here, we're caught. If we run downstream, we're caught slower. Up the chute is the only door in the room."

"You'll swamp us."

"Probably not."

"*Probably.*"

"Maren." Casper kept her voice level. "I've run water nobody surveyed since I was fourteen years old. I'm not asking you to trust the chute. I'm asking you to trust me."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere behind them the diesel note deepened, rounded the bend, became real.

"That's the first honest thing you've offered me," Maren said, and got in the boat.

* * *

The gunboat's floodlight hit the gravel pocket ninety seconds after they cleared it, a white blade sweeping the trees where the *Merganser* had been, finding nothing but cold ashes and the print of two sets of boots. Casper heard a voice crackle across the water, amplified and flat, and then the engine note changed — rising, searching.

She didn't look back. Looking back was how people died on rivers.

The side creek took them in like a throat. Three feet of churning meltwater over unseen rock, banks closing to boat-widths, alder whipping the gunwales, and the pitch steepening every ten yards so the little outboard screamed and clawed and the hull banged off stones she felt through her boots more than saw. Maren crouched low amidships with both arms locked around the chest, and Casper stood at the tiller reading the water by sound and starlight, feeling for the deep lanes the way her father had taught her before she could write her own name: *the river tells you where it's deep, girl, it can't help itself.*

A sweeper loomed out of the dark directly across the current — a whole spruce, rootball and all, wedged between the banks. No room under it. No room around it.

"Grab line!" Casper threw the tiller hard and the boat slewed sideways into the eddy behind the rootball, wallowing, shipping water over the transom corner. For one lurching moment they hung there in the foam, pinned, the main current roaring past a foot away, and Casper got her hand on the trunk and shoved — shoved with everything her shoulders had, the boat grinding free, scraping through a slot barely wider than herself, alder raking her scalp, and then they were through and the chute swallowed them again.

Behind, faint through the trees, the floodlight swept the mouth of the creek and passed on. The amplified voice said something she couldn't make out. Then the diesel faded, hunting onward up the gorge, chasing a ghost that was no longer there.

Casper let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the barge burned.

Then the chute ended.

It didn't narrow or shallow — it simply stopped being a creek and became a staircase. Meltwater pouring down a shelf of bedrock in a series of drops, each one taller than the last, white noise filling the gorge wall to wall. No boat alive was going up that.

"No," Maren said quietly. It wasn't protest. It was grief.

Casper killed the engine and let the silence rush in around them, broken only by the falls. They floated in a black pool at the base of the shelf, hemmed by walls and water, safe from Vey and trapped by geography, and the arithmetic she'd done at dawn tore itself up and started over.

"We walk from here," she said. "Haul the boat up the moraine on the east side, line it through the shallows above the falls, keep moving north. He'll figure it out by morning, but by then we'll have—"

The pool shuddered.

Casper felt it through the hull before she understood it — a long, rolling tremor, the water lifting and dropping like something enormous turning over underneath. Above them, up the dark face of the gorge, came a sound she knew from wrecks and salvage jobs and one bad night off Cape Verrell: the deep structural groan of a mass deciding to move.

"Landslide," she breathed. "The melt undercut the slope. Maren, get us to the bank, NOW—"

The side slope of the gorge let go.

It came down the west wall in the dark — not a single slide but a whole hillside shrugging loose, tons of glacial till and shattered rock pouring into the upper chute, and the creek, blocked, backed up with terrifying speed. Casper saw it happen in flashes of starlight: the white water stuttering, stalling, then beginning to rise, the pool swelling, the current reversing, debris riding a growing bulge of water back down the chute toward them like a fist closing.

"The dam's going to burst," Maren shouted. "When it goes, everything it's holding comes down this chute at once!"

"Bank! Get to the bank!"

Casper rammed the throttle open and drove the bow at the east shore, the same shore they'd planned to climb. The boat ground onto gravel and Maren was over the side, chest held high, water to her thighs, and Casper grabbed the go-bag — fuel, food, the rifle, the medical kit — and then the dam failed.

It didn't burst so much as dissolve. The whole improvised barrier slumped at once and the pent meltwater came down the chute as a single brown wall, eight feet of it, carrying trees and rock and the entire argument of the mountain. Casper heard it rather than saw it — a roar like the gorge clearing its throat — and she threw the bag up the bank and turned to grab Maren and Maren wasn't there.

The water had her.

Casper saw her for half a second — a pale shape in the flood, one arm up, the other still wrapped around the chest strap, tumbling — and then the wall hit the pool and the world became noise and cold and motion. Casper went down. The river rolled her like laundry, slammed her into rock, gave her a lungful of ice water, and some animal part of her brain that predated language took over: curl, protect the head, find the surface, breathe when it lets you.

She surfaced thirty yards downstream in the flooded lower creek, gasping, and clawed for the alder-choked bank. Behind her the flood was still running high and furious, and in it — there, snagged against a half-submerged spruce — a shape. An arm. Moving.

Casper went back into the water.

She'd tell herself later it wasn't a decision. She swam the eddy line, got a fist into Maren's collar, and hauled, and the old woman came around coughing, still strapped to the chest, still holding it, her grip having outlasted her breath. Together they fought the shallows and collapsed onto the gravel as the flood peaked and began, slowly, grudgingly, to fall.

For a while neither of them spoke. Maren lay on her back, chest heaving, gray hair plastered across her face, and Casper knelt beside her counting her breaths the way she counted everything, waiting for them to steady.

"You saved the chest," Casper said finally. It came out half accusation.

Maren laughed — a wet, cracked, astonishing sound. "Forty years I waited for it. Let the river try to take it back."

Then the laugh folded into coughing, and the coughing into shaking, and Casper got the emergency blanket out of the ruined bag and wrapped them both in it, sitting hip to hip on the gravel like survivors of a shipwreck, which is what they were.

The inventory, when she forced herself to make it, was ugly. The *Merganser* was gone — she'd seen the flash of its hull going over the falls inside the flood surge, or imagined she had; either way it was downstream and done. The fuel was gone. Most of the food was gone. The rifle had vanished somewhere between the pool and the bank. What remained: the chest, soaked but sealed; the map inside Casper's jacket, dry because she'd ziplocked it against sweat months of habit ago; one knife; one lighter; one emergency blanket; two women.

Below them, far down the gorge, a searchlight swept the water. Slower now. Methodical. Vey's people had found the flood scar and were working it, patient as gulls after a gutting boat.

"They'll find the boat wreck by dawn," Maren said. Her voice was scraped raw. "They'll assume we died in it. Men like that prefer tidy stories."

"Men like that check." But Casper stared at the light, and something shifted in her chest — not hope exactly. Hardening. She had spent her whole life believing that the smart move, the survivable move, was to be small, to be no one, to pay the toll and keep the wake clean. She'd paid Vey once. She'd watched her father pay the river once. Both times the price had been a lie, because the price was never final; they always came back for more.

Her father had died running *downstream*, trying to get home. She was done running home.

"Up," she said. She stood, legs shaking, and pointed up the gorge into the black where the glacier source waited, another sixty miles of moraine and cold. "On foot. Tonight. While they're picking through the flood debris and telling themselves a tidy story."

Maren looked up at her, water dripping from her chin, seventy-some years old and half-drowned, and grinned like a girl. "You know," she said, "for someone who takes money to forget things, you have a terrible memory for letting go."

Casper shouldered the bag. "Move."

They climbed. And behind them, in the wet gravel at the flood line, half-buried where the water had stripped the bank open, something pale caught the starlight — a seam of worked stone, square-cut, ancient, pointing upriver like a finger.

Neither of them saw it. Yet.
