## 13. The Map's Second Layer

The side channel had taken nearly everything. Casper inventoried their losses by lantern light in the lee of a rock overhang two miles above the Narrows: half the food gone to the flood, the spare fuel canister spinning away into black water before she could even swear at it, Maren's coat soaked through and stiffening with cold. The chest was still lashed under the thwart, water-stained but sealed. The map—

The map was the problem.

She'd pulled it from its oilskin sleeve too fast in the chaos, and the river had found it anyway. The lower third was pulp now, fibers fused into a gray felt where the delta country should have been. The upper portion — the glacier source, the archive's approach — had survived better, ink ghosting through the damp like a face behind frosted glass.

"We dry it slow," she said. "Fast heat curls it. Curls it, it cracks."

Maren sat with her feet wrapped in a blanket, her ankle swollen where the channel had slammed her against stone. She'd refused to complain about it, which Casper was learning was simply how the woman did everything. "You've dried documents before."

"I've dried everything before." Casper strung a line between two rocks near the small fire and began pegging the map flat, working from the center outward, smoothing each fiber with the back of her thumbnail the way her father had taught her to smooth bills before counting them. *Paper remembers what you do to it,* he used to say. *Same as people.* She'd hated that kind of talk when she was fourteen. She heard it in her own head constantly now.

They'd made camp late, running on adrenaline and the last of the coffee, and for a long while neither of them spoke. The river talked instead — lower here, more patient, gathering itself for the high country. Somewhere upstream a bird called once and gave up.

It was Maren who finally broke the quiet. "In Vessany we had a word for paper like this. *Vesht.* It means both wound and door." She watched Casper work. "My grandmother was an archivist's daughter. She said every damaged document is a document trying to tell you something twice."

Casper grunted. She wasn't in the mood for exile poetry. Her hands were shaking — not from cold, or not only from cold, but from the arithmetic she kept doing without meaning to: three days of food, forty miles of unknown water, one gunboat somewhere behind them and possibly ahead, and a woman with a bad ankle who moved through the world like she had all the time the drowned nation hadn't given her.

But the old woman's words stuck, the way splinters do.

*Trying to tell you something twice.*

* * *

By morning the map had dried to a brittle, wavering sheet, and Casper laid it on a flat stone and weighted the corners with pebbles and studied it the way she studied wrecks — not looking for what was obvious, but for what didn't belong.

Forty years of salvage had taught her one law above all others: nothing underwater is ever only one thing. A hull is cargo over ballast over keel. A wreck site is a story told in layers, and the amateurs read the top layer and leave. The professionals dig until the layers start arguing with each other.

The map was arguing with itself.

She saw it first as a texture problem. The surviving parchment had a grain to it, a faint ridging under the lamplight, and along the ridge lines the ink sat differently — slightly raised, slightly darker, like writing pressed down onto something already written. She'd assumed watermark. Mills did that; she'd seen it on a hundred salvaged ledgers.

Except watermarks repeated. This didn't. This ran in long, deliberate strokes beneath the printed survey lines, and when she tilted the lamp low across the surface, the strokes caught shadow and held it.

"Casper." Maren's voice came careful, the way you speak to someone standing on ice. "What are you seeing?"

"Come look. Slowly. Don't breathe on it."

Maren lowered herself beside the stone, and for a full minute neither of them touched anything. Then Casper took the finest brush from her salvage kit — camel hair, meant for cleaning corrosion off coins — dipped it in the meltwater kettle, and drew one wet line across a patch of the ridge-texture near the archive's marked approach.

The top layer of ink, the navy surveyor's crisp cartography, softened and lifted. And underneath, dark and patient, another hand surfaced.

Letters. Old ones. The same angular script stamped on the chest's seal.

Maren made a sound Casper had never heard from her — small, cracked, involuntary, like a hinge on a door locked for decades. "That is *Old Registry hand.* That is how the archivists wrote. My grandmother wrote like that. Nobody has written like that in forty years because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because there was nobody left with the right to."

Casper barely heard her. She was already working, brush moving in slow strokes, lifting the modern layer section by section, blotting with cloth, revealing the older text beneath in patches like frost melting off a window. Her heart was doing something unprofessional. She made herself breathe the way she breathed at thirty meters: measured, boring, mechanical.

"It's not a survey map," she said. "Or it is, but it's printed *over* something. Someone reused an official chart. Navy stock, probably seized after—" She caught herself. After the erasure. After Vessany stopped being allowed to have maps of its own country. "Someone hid the real document inside their paperwork. Hiding in plain sight. Same trick I'd use."

"You would hide a nation inside a shipping manifest," Maren said, and there was something almost like laughter in it, ragged at the edges.

"I'd hide anything inside a shipping manifest. Nobody reads those." Casper worked faster now, and the revealed text assembled itself in fragments: a heading. A list of names — signatories, seven of them, each name followed by titles in that angular script. A date. She knew the date. Everyone on the river knew the date, the way everyone knows the year of a war.

It was four days *before* the treaty. Four days before the forged signatures that had signed away the delta.

"This isn't a copy," she said slowly. "Maren. Look at the wax residue — here, along this fold. There was a seal affixed to this. An original seal, broken and flattened when it was folded." She looked up. "This is the treaty. The actual one. The one they forged the copy from."

The fire popped. Downstream, faint but persistent, the river kept up its one long argument.

Maren had gone very still, the way deep water goes still. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something formal and old, a register Casper hadn't heard from her — the voice, maybe, of kitchens and chapels and hidden songs. "The original instrument. If it exists, if it can be authenticated — the forgery doesn't just become known. It becomes *void.* Every land grant issued under the false treaty collapses with it. Not compensation. Not apology. *Title.*" Her hands, hovering over the map without touching, trembled. "Do you understand? We wouldn't be asking anyone for anything. We would be holding the paper that says it was never theirs to give."

"That's why Vey wants the chest burned so badly." Casper sat back on her heels. The picture assembled itself with the ugly clarity of a wreck coming into dive-light: the restricted sites, the triple-rate silence, the barge in flames. Vey hadn't been protecting a secret. He'd been protecting a *legal structure* — forty years of deeds and leases and navy charters, all of it standing on one forged signature, all of it load-bearing. Burn the original, and the forgery became the only document in the world. Became, effectively, the truth.

"He doesn't need to convince anyone the treaty is real," she said. "He just needs the real one to never exist. One match."

"And he thinks he has it." Maren's eyes went to the chest, lashed under the overturned boat where they'd dragged it above the flood line. "He took the chest at the weir. But the map—"

"The map stayed with us because the guide didn't know it mattered." Casper almost laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "He sold us out for debt forgiveness and never looked twice at a piece of wet paper."

Then the laugh died, because the next thought arrived, and it arrived cold.

The map's lower third — the delta, the towns, the legal geography of everything the treaty had stolen — was pulp. Gone. But the upper third, the part that mattered, the part showing the glacier source and the archive's approach, had survived. And layered beneath the surveyor's clean lines, the Old Registry hand continued in annotations: depth marks, a route description, and one phrase that Casper lifted free of the modern ink stroke by stroke, holding her breath so the lamp flame wouldn't gutter against it.

She translated it with Maren leaning close, reading aloud over her shoulder in a whisper, the two of them bent together over a dead nation's handwriting like women warming themselves at a small fire.

*"The vault holds the instrument entire. Let no copy stand in its place."*

"Not records," Casper said. "Not just records. The whole thing. Sealed, witnessed, original." She straightened, and her knees cracked, and she realized she'd been crouched over the stone for hours. "We're not carrying evidence anymore. We're carrying the deed."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day preparing, and it was only that evening, boiling meltwater for the last of the rice, that Casper did the other math — the math she'd been avoiding since the Narrows.

"The calving season," she said.

Maren looked up.

"The archive sits under the ice-dam at the source. Your stories put the entrance behind the summer melt channel — accessible a few weeks a year, when the lake drains and the gorge opens. Right?" Maren nodded. "And Vey knows the window too. He's not chasing us upriver for fun. He's racing the same calendar." Casper turned her tin cup slowly. "Here's what I know about ice, from wrecks under it. It doesn't open on schedule. It opens when it decides. Early some years. Late others. And every pilot on this river is betting on the average."

"So we bet on the ice."

"We bet on being *early.*" She met the old woman's eyes. "If the gorge opens early and we're not there, Vey goes in first, and the original treaty burns before lunch. If it opens late and we're there waiting, we freeze waiting, and he finds two bodies and a map. Either way—" she tapped the fragile sheet, pegged and drying between its stones, the hidden letters surfacing and sinking again as the light shifted, "—we have maybe six days of margin. Maybe less. And forty miles of river that gets worse the higher you climb, with half our food at the bottom of the Narrows."

Maren was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "When I was a girl, my father fished the melt channels below the dam. He said the glacier always gives one warning before it calves — a sound like a door unlocking, far away, days before. He called it the house opening its rooms." She pulled her blanket tighter. "I have spent forty years telling myself I imagined hearing it once, the spring we left."

Casper fed the fire another stick. Above the tree line the peaks were going pink and then gray, and somewhere up there, under a hundred feet of blue ice, a room was waiting with a document inside it that could give a drowned country back its name.

Six days. Less, if the river had opinions.

She banked the coals, checked the lashings on the chest, and stood the first watch facing upstream, into the cold wind coming off the ice, listening — though she'd have denied it if asked — for the sound of a door unlocking.

Near midnight, far up the valley, so faint she might have made it out of hope and exhaustion, something boomed. Once. Deep. Like a house settling. Or opening.

"Maren," she said quietly. "Wake up. I think your father was right."
