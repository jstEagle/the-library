## 14. Cold Country

The river changed its mind about being a river somewhere above the last town.

Casper noticed it first as a sound — or rather the absence of one. For eleven days the water had talked to them, muttered and roared and gossiped against the hull, and now, pulling into a wide gravel flat where the current went glassy and pale, she realized it had gone quiet. Not still. Quiet. The way a man goes quiet when he's made up his mind about something.

"Glacial till," Maren said, crouching at the waterline. She dipped her fingers in and held them up: milk-white, gritty with rock flour. "The melt's carrying the mountain down to you. Every drop of this is a glacier deciding what to keep."

"You say things like that on purpose," Casper said.

"I'm eighty-one years old. I've earned the right to be strange."

They hauled the boat out on rollers cut from drift alder, because from here the map said nothing useful and Maren's memory said everything. The channel above the flats braided into dead arms and gravel shoals that would ground them in an hour of poling. Past this point, Vessan country was walking country. The old woman had told her that back at the ferry landing, in another life, when Casper had refused her hand across the water. Now they were both holding the rope.

The moraine began a mile inland from the flat — a black spine of broken stone running parallel to the valley like the keel of some drowned ship. They'd portage along its flank, Maren said, three days' walk to the high bench where the source lake sat under the calving face. Casper had divided the load the night before with a salvager's arithmetic: chest and map in the dry bag on her own back, because she trusted no one else's shoulders with it, not even the shoulders of the woman whose nation it belonged to. Fuel, rope, the tarp, the tent. Food for six days stretched to nine if they were careful.

Maren carried her share without complaint and without slowing, which Casper found herself watching for. She kept expecting the old woman to flag, to become a burden she'd have to factor in, and Maren declined to do it. She walked the way she did everything — deliberately, upright, as if the ground had been arranged for her benefit.

* * *

The helicopter came on the second afternoon.

They heard it before they saw it — a wasp-whine bouncing off the valley walls, directionless, everywhere. Casper had the tarp off the packs and over them both before Maren finished turning her head, dragging them into the shadow of a house-sized boulder the ice had dropped a thousand years ago and couldn't be bothered to explain. The gray-green canvas blended with the lichen. Casper pressed Maren down against the cold stone and put her hand flat on the old woman's back, feeling the bird-bone ribs working underneath, quick but steady.

The helicopter came up the valley at altitude, swung wide over the braided flats, and hung there. Even from half a mile Casper could see the sensor pod under its nose, the slow deliberate sweep of it, reading the ground line by line like a man going through a desk drawer.

"He'll fly the moraine twice," Maren breathed. "Then the gorge. Then he'll go away and come back tomorrow."

"You know his flight plan?"

"I know Aldous Vey." Her voice was dry as the rock flour. "He is a man who checks the same drawer four times because he cannot believe anyone would hide something in the fifth."

The helicopter crossed the flats again, lower now, and Casper felt the sound in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her palms, in the place behind her sternum where fear lived rent-free. It passed. The whine faded downstream toward the tollhouse country, and after a long time neither of them moved, and then Maren started laughing — silently, shaking, her shoulders jumping under Casper's hand.

"What?"

"He swept the same gravel bar three times," Maren said. "There are ten thousand acres of rock out here and he is looking at one gravel bar. Because that is what money does. It looks where it has already looked."

Casper let the tarp off them and sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something undignified. "You find him funny."

"I find him *small*," Maren said. "That is different, and it is better. Funny men get forgiven. Small men just get in the way until the mountain deals with them."

* * *

They walked until the light went bronze and then blue, and camped in a hollow the wind had carved out of the moraine, walls of stacked stone on three sides. Casper got the stove going low, shielded by the pack, and heated water for the barley and the last of the dried fish. Above them the peaks had gone the color of old bone, and between two of them, far up the valley, the glacier showed itself for the first time — a white shoulder, then a white face, catching the last light like something enormous keeping watch.

"That's it?" Casper said.

"That's it." Maren didn't eat so much as negotiate with her food, small precise bites. "My mother could see it from our kitchen window. In Vessany proper, I mean. Before." She chewed. Swallowed. "She used to say the ice was the country's bank. Everything we ever lost got deposited there. Everything we'd need later."

"And you believed her."

"I was six. I believed the moon followed us home too." A pause. "I believe it now, though. Isn't that a foolish thing? Sixty years of exile and I end up agreeing with my mother."

Casper fed a twig to the stove. The flame took it greedily and died back to business. Out here, above the tree line almost, fuel was rationed to twigs and patience. "Tell me about her. Your mother."

"Why?"

"Because we've got three days and nothing to do but walk and lie to each other about being fine. Might as well spend one evening telling the truth."

Maren looked at her across the stove, and in the blue dark her eyes caught what little light there was. "You first," she said. "You know everything about me and I know nothing about you except that you dive for pay and sleep like a soldier and flinch when anyone says the word *home*. So. You first."

* * *

Casper hadn't planned to tell her anything. That was the thing about her life — she'd built it so that nothing needed telling. Dive log, invoice, paid miles, move on. But the wind was coming down off the ice with a sound like someone breathing at the mouth of the hollow, and the old woman waited with the terrible patience of someone who had outlasted a nation, and Casper heard herself start talking before she'd decided to.

"My father ran cargo," she said. "Not the kind with bills of lading."

"The other kind."

"The other kind. Downriver mostly, out to the coast. I crewed for him from the time I was nine. He taught me to read water the way your mother taught you—" she gestured vaguely at the glacier, at all of it, "—whatever that was. Where the current lies to you. Where the rocks wear a collar of white. He used to say the river keeps honest books; it's only people who cook them."

Maren nodded slowly, encouraging without interrupting, and Casper hated how much she wanted to keep going.

"There's a stretch below Kell's Folly — you know it, you called the line through it better than I could. He died there. Running heavy, running late, running scared, probably all three. I was fourteen. I wasn't aboard." She turned the spoon over in the pot. "I've spent thirty years diving wrecks and telling myself it's salvage. It's not salvage. It's me going down and checking every hull to see if it's his."

The words came out level, which surprised her. She'd expected them to cost more, or maybe she'd been paying on them so long the principal was gone and this was just interest.

"After that I stopped belonging anywhere on purpose. Belonging gets people killed, or worse, it gets *you* killed and leaves somebody standing on a dock. So I became very good at being no one. Paid miles. Clean logs. No name on the ledger worth remembering." She looked up. "And then I pull a chest out of the mud with your seal on it, and within a week my barge is ash and I'm hauling a nation uphill on my back, and the funny part — the genuinely funny part — is that I can't remember the last time I felt this much like myself."

Maren reached across the stove. Her hand was small and dry and strong, and she laid it over Casper's wrist the way you'd steady a compass.

"There it is," the old woman said softly. "There's the girl who was on that dock."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn because the cold woke them anyway, breath frosting inside the tent, and walked the long gray morning along the moraine's spine. The country up here didn't care about them and Casper found that restful. No checkpoints, no informants, no subcontractors. Just rock and meltwater and the white face growing by degrees, the way a truth grows when you finally walk toward it instead of away.

It was Maren who talked that afternoon, in pieces, handed over like stones passed hand to hand on a climb.

Her father had been a lockkeeper on the delta — the third Ostrev to hold the same gate. When the treaty came, forty years ago, the family was given seventy-two hours and one cart. Her mother had stood in the doorway of the house and refused to leave until her husband physically lifted her across the threshold, and even then she walked backward the whole way to the ferry, facing the house, so it would be the last thing she saw.

"She never once looked forward after that," Maren said. "Sixty years facing backward. And I loved her, and I swore I wouldn't become her, and look at me." She spread her hands at the moraine, the ice, the whole cold country. "Walking toward it. At eighty-one. With a stranger carrying my nation's paperwork on her back."

"You're not facing backward."

"No," Maren agreed. "But I'm carrying her with me. Both things can be true. That's the lesson nobody teaches you — that grief and forward motion fit in the same boat." She glanced sideways. "Your father taught you the river keeps honest books. My mother taught me the ice does too. Slow books. Books that take a lifetime to read. But honest."

* * *

On the third day the weather turned, because weather up here considered itself obligated to. Wind came off the calving face in pulses you could hear coming — a low boom rolling down the valley, then thirty seconds later the gust, cold enough to make the eyes water. They leaned into it with their heads down, and between pulses Casper listened to the glacier talking to itself, deep structural groans like a ship working in a swell.

"It's early," Maren said, and there was something new in her voice. Not fear exactly. Urgency wearing fear's coat.

"What's early?"

"The calving. Listen to it. That's not summer noise, that's a face getting ready to let go." She stopped on the trail and squinted up-valley, reading the ice the way Casper read water. "My grandfather's generation called this season the Opening. When the face calves big, the ice dam at the source lake lets go, and the outwash gorge floods open for a few days — the only days in the year anyone can reach the vault approach from the water side. We're early. Days early. Which means either we wait on the bench for the burst—"

"—or Vey gets there first and waits for us," Casper finished.

They stood in the wind and looked at each other, and Casper watched the arithmetic run behind the old woman's eyes and knew it matched her own. Waiting meant exposure. Going early meant the gorge might still be sealed, or worse, half-open — a throat full of moving water and moving ice.

"We go when it opens," Casper said. "We don't get to choose the window. We only get to be ready for it."

Maren smiled, and for a moment, in the white light off the glacier, she looked forty years younger and sixty miles closer to home. "Spoken like a lockkeeper's granddaughter."

"You're claiming me now?"

"I claimed you at the ferry landing," Maren said. "You were just slow taking delivery."

* * *

They came down off the moraine at dusk onto the high bench above the source lake, and that was where they found the sign that the quiet chapter was over.

A cairn, fresh-built, stones still dirt-crusted. Beside it, staked under a rock, a strip of orange flagging tape — surveyor's tape, contractor's tape, Vey's tape. And scratched into the flat face of a boulder with a knife or a geologist's pick, in a casual hand that thought it owned the world:

**VEY PARTY — 4 DAYS AGO — 6 MEN — RETURNING WITH GEAR**

Returning. Which meant they'd gone on ahead toward the vault approach and were coming back this way, and somewhere between here and the lake, on a trail barely wide enough for one tired diver and one stubborn old woman, their paths were going to cross.

Casper crouched by the cairn and read the ground the way her father taught her. Boot prints, several sets, heading down-valley. Fresh enough that the wind hadn't yet softened their edges.

"Maren," she said quietly. "How do you feel about meeting people?"

The old woman pulled her pack straps tight and looked down the trail into the gathering dark.

"I feel," she said, "that we should get off this path. Right now."
