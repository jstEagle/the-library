## 15. Betrayal at the Weir

The guide's name was Teodor, and Casper should have known from the first hour that something was wrong with him.

Not wrong the way a man is wrong when he's lying — she'd have caught that. Wrong the way a man is wrong when he's already decided. He poled their hired skiff through the braided shallows above Cold Landing with an ease that looked like skill and was actually rehearsal. He knew every channel before he sounded it. He never asked where they were going twice.

"You've run this water before," Casper said, watching his hands on the pole.

"Born here." He didn't turn around. "Before the navy drew its lines."

Maren sat amidships with the chest between her knees, her hands resting on the lead lid the way other old women rested hands on a Bible. She'd barely slept since the Narrows. The flooded side channel had taken most of their food, half their fuel, and very nearly her — Casper still woke seeing the moment Maren went under, the gray water closing over her white hair, the two full seconds before Casper's grip found her wrist. Two seconds. She'd started measuring things in seconds now instead of miles, and she hated it.

"The weir's three bends up," Teodor said. "Old salmon weir, pre-annexation. Nobody uses it. We can portage the left flank and be at the moraine trail by dark."

Casper nodded and kept watching his hands.

Here was what she knew about men who sold things: they always touched what they'd sold one last time. Her father had taught her that on the night runs down this same river, twenty years ago, when she was small enough to hide among the cargo crates. *A buyer shakes your hand, Casper. A seller checks his pockets.* Teodor's right hand kept drifting to his breast pocket, touching it, letting it fall. Touching it. Letting it fall.

She said nothing. There was nowhere to say anything *to*. They were committed to this stretch of river, committed to the guide, committed to the plan, and the only alternative to trusting him was turning back into Vey's gunboat country with no fuel and no food and Maren's cough getting worse by the day.

So she trusted him the way you trust weather. Knowing it could kill you. Knowing you'd walk into it anyway.

* * *

The weir appeared around the fourth bend like a row of rotten teeth.

It spanned the river in a broken diagonal — cedar pilings black with sixty years of water, the old fish-gates long collapsed, the whole structure canted downstream as if trying to leave. Above it the river pooled wide and slow, green-gray, deceptively calm. Below it the water tore itself white over the fallen gates.

"We portage left," Teodor said, nosing toward the bank. "There's a trail. I'll take the chest first — it's the heavy thing."

"No," Casper said.

He turned then, finally, and looked at her, and she saw it in his face — not guilt, exactly. Something sadder than guilt. The look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like but had already finished.

"Casper," Maren said quietly.

"I'll carry the chest." Casper stepped onto the shingle, boots crunching, and took the towline. "You take Maren up the trail. Slow pace. She twisted her ankle at the Narrows."

"She did not—"

"Maren." Casper didn't look at her. "Trail."

The old woman understood. Casper felt it pass between them — not agreement, because there was nothing agreed, just the shared animal knowledge of a trap springing. Maren let Teodor hand her out of the skiff, let him offer his arm, let herself be steered toward the treeline with the careful gait of a genuinely tired seventy-year-old.

Casper lifted the chest. It weighed maybe forty pounds with the water damage, lead and oak and everything a dead nation had thought worth drowning for. She got ten steps up the bank.

Then the trees spoke.

"Stay where you are, Pemberton."

Four men came down through the alders in gray river-clothes, no insignia, carbines slung low but present. Behind them, upstream, a boat engine she recognized — the low diesel growl of Vey's patrol craft, idling somewhere out of sight around the bend. Not chasing them. Waiting. It had been waiting all along.

Teodor stood at the treeline with his arm still half-extended toward Maren, and he wouldn't look at Casper. He studied the ground, the weir, the water — anything with no eyes in it.

"How much?" Casper asked him.

He flinched.

"How much did my father's river cost?" She set the chest down in the gravel without taking her eyes off him. "Or was it debt? It's usually debt. Vey buys debts the way other men buy drinks."

"Fifty thousand," Teodor said to the ground. "Owed since the flood year. My boat, my house, my mother's medical—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They weren't going to forgive it. They were going to take the boat either way. At least this way—"

"At least this way you get to watch," Casper said.

One of the gray men stepped forward. "The chest. Step away from it."

Casper looked at the chest. Forty pounds of lead and oak and the only un-forged signature in forty years of lies. She thought about running. There was no version of it that worked — open ground, four rifles, Maren already in the trees with men behind her. She thought about the river. The pool above the weir was deep enough to drown the documents forever, and Vey knew it, which was why his men were positioned on the bank side, why they'd waited until the chest was ashore.

They'd done this math too. Everyone on this river was doing math except the people the math was about.

She stepped away.

* * *

Aldous Vey came up the bank himself, which surprised her. She'd expected him to stay on the boat, let his men do the wet work. But he climbed the shingle in polished boots that had no business on a riverbank, immaculate gray field jacket, and he looked at the chest the way an auditor looks at a discrepancy.

"Ms. Pemberton." He crouched, ran a gloved thumb across the Vessan seal, and something moved behind his eyes — not greed. Relief. The relief of a man closing a file. "Do you know what this is worth? Not in money. In *stability*. Forty years of settlements, water rights, three towns' legal standing — all of it rests on a treaty everyone agrees to remember correctly." He straightened. "You found a document that would make thousands of people litigious and hopeless at the same time. That's not treasure. That's a wound."

"It's the truth," Maren said.

Casper turned. Maren stood at the treeline between two of the gray men, hands visible, spine straight as a piling. Her white hair had come loose from its braid. She looked, Casper thought, like a photograph of someone — like the one image that survives of a drowned town.

Vey regarded her with something almost like courtesy. "Madam Ostrev. You've been a great deal of trouble for a woman your age."

"I've been a great deal of trouble for seventy years," Maren said. "You're just the latest weather."

Vey gestured, and two men took the chest. Casper watched it go — watched it swing between them up the bank toward the sound of the idling diesel — and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very cold. Not despair. She knew despair; her father's death had taught her despair at fourteen. This was colder. This was the feeling of a ledger being closed against you.

"They'll burn it," Maren said. Not a question.

"There's a furnace facility at the tollhouse," Vey said mildly. "Industrial incineration, documented chain of custody. By Thursday there will be no chest, no map, and no evidence that any of it existed, and Ms. Pemberton will return to salvage work with a generous severance and a permanent prohibition notice." He looked at Casper. "You're good at being no one, Pemberton. I'm offering you the career."

The words landed harder than he could have known. Because he was right — that had been her whole life, the dive logs and paid miles, competence as camouflage. And some traitor part of her, the part that had taken his money in chapter three of her own mistakes, whispered that she could survive this. Walk away. Be no one again.

Then she looked at Maren, and Maren looked back, and there was no survival in that look at all. There was only the question: *what are you, now?*

"Bind them," Vey said. "Above the flood line. Someone will come through eventually — fishermen, a survey crew. A week, maybe less. They'll have a story to tell." He paused, and gave Casper a thin smile. "Make it a boring one."

* * *

They zip-tied Casper's wrists behind her and walked them both fifty yards up the bank to a stand of dead cedars killed by some old flood, silver-barked, stripped of limbs below eight feet. They tied Maren to one trunk sitting, and Casper to another standing, and checked the knots with professional indifference, and then they were gone — down through the alders, the diesel growl swelling briefly, then fading upstream toward the tollhouse and the furnace.

The river kept talking below the weir. That endless white noise, patient as time.

Casper tested the zip ties once, twice, and stopped. Standard issue, cinched tight, doubled. Her shoulders were strong from twenty years of salvage work but wrists don't work that way; you can't pull a tie apart, you can only cut or shimmy it, and she had neither blade nor slack. The tree bark was smooth. Dead cedar, silvered, nothing to abrade against.

"Stop fighting it," Maren said. "You'll dislocate something and then we're truly finished."

"What's your plan, then?" Casper heard her own voice crack on it — fury looking for somewhere to live. "We had it. We *had* it, Maren. One more portage, one more day, and we're at the moraine, and now—" She pulled against the ties anyway, uselessly, hating herself. "I should have read Teodor in the first hour. I *did* read him. I saw his hand going to his pocket and I said nothing because there was nothing to say."

"You said nothing because hope makes people stupid," Maren said. "It's not a character flaw. It's a feature of hope." She shifted against her trunk, settling herself with the deliberate economy of a woman conserving strength. "Now stop. Listen to me. How did they tie me?"

"Sitting. Wrists to the trunk behind you. Why?"

"And you?"

"Standing. Same."

"Good." Maren's voice changed — dropped into something older, drier, the voice Casper had heard at Kell's Folly when the old woman had called the channel by a name that wasn't on any chart. "My father fished this weir. Before annexation, before the lines. Every family on this reach had a claim-stake in it — you bought shares, like a boat. Our share number was eleven." A pause. "When the treaty dispossessed us, they didn't just take land. They took *infrastructure*. Weirs, ferry rights, millponds. And when the navy surveyed, they catalogued every structure — including this one."

"So?"

"So the survey teams needed access points. Maintenance paths. And a weir that's still legally a shared asset — even a dead one — has to have a *tool cache*. For gate repairs, chain work, clearing debris." Maren's voice took on a grim satisfaction. "Survey cache, north end of the structure, marked with a brass plate. Bolted to a piling. Probably never opened since the annexation. Probably full of rusted junk."

"Probably," Casper said.

"But possibly bolt cutters. Possibly a pry bar. Possibly a hacksaw, because salmon weirs need chain work and chain work needs saws." Maren leaned her head back against the cedar. "I spent forty years memorizing a drowned country, girl. Every asset schedule. Every survey notation. You think I learned the names of towns. I learned the *bureaucracy*. The occupation's paperwork is my homeland now. And the paperwork says there is a tool cache at this weir."

Casper stared at her. The white noise of the river filled the silence.

"The north end is thirty yards downstream," Maren said. "Can you walk with your hands bound?"

"I can walk anywhere."

"Then walk carefully. The bank drops off past the third dead cedar. And Casper—" Maren's voice softened by a degree, which from her was a shout. "The plate will say ASSET 11-C. Read it to me. I want to hear our number."

* * *

Getting down the bank with her hands tied behind her took Casper twenty minutes and cost her one fall, shoulder-first into moss, breath knocked loose. She worked her way along the base of the weir's north piling, feet in cold shallow water, and found it exactly where forty years of bureaucracy said it would be: a steel box the size of a bread loaf, bolted to the wood six inches above the normal high-water line, its lid furred with lichen.

The brass plate beneath it was green with age. She bent close.

"Asset Eleven-C," she read aloud, loud enough to carry upstream. "Vessan Shared Fishery. Claim-share holders: registered families of the Kell Reach." She swallowed. "Maintenance access guaranteed in perpetuity."

"*In perpetuity,*" Maren repeated from up the bank, and her voice broke on the word, and Casper pretended not to hear, because some things you grant a person the privacy of.

The box latch was a simple hasp, rusted nearly solid. Casper braced it against her knee and hit it with the heel of her bound hands, once, twice — and on the third blow forty years of oxidation gave way with a crack like a knuckle popping. Inside: a coil of chain gone to orange lace. A wooden mallet dissolved to mush. And wrapped in oilcloth that had actually held —

A hacksaw. Blade pitted but intact. And beside it, miracle of miracles, a fresh-sealed replacement blade in wax paper, the brand name still legible.

Casper pressed the saw frame against a piling and snapped the old blade free, then wedged the new one in with her teeth and her chin and a fury that had finally found its job. Then she sat, put the saw between her boot soles, and began to cut.

Forty strokes. Sixty. The blade sang against the plastic, a thin industrial whine under the river's roar, and Casper found herself thinking of her father's logbook, the line she'd read by flashlight while the barge burned: *The river gives back what you carry to it.* She'd thought it sentiment when she was young. Now it sounded like engineering.

The tie parted. Blood rushed back into her hands like a returned tide.

She cut Maren free next, and the old woman stood, stamped feeling into her feet, and flexed her fingers, and looked down at the ruined weir with an expression Casper couldn't read.

"Well," Maren said. "Asset Eleven-C. Still in perpetuity." She picked up the hacksaw, considered it, and handed it back to Casper. "Keep it. You may need to cut more ties before this is done. Possibly metaphorical ones."

"The chest is upstream at the tollhouse," Casper said. "Furnace by Thursday. And we have no boat, no food, no fuel, and Vey thinks we're a week from being found."

"Yes," Maren agreed. "Terrible position." She pointed downstream, past the broken fish-gates, to where the river pooled slow and deep below the weir before quickening again. "Except that Teodor's skiff is still tied at the landing, and Vey's men took the *patrol boat*, because a skiff can't carry four armed men and a chest comfortably, and because nobody guards a prisoner they've tied above a flood line in dead timber nobody visits." She smiled, and it was the first time Casper had seen her do it, and it rearranged her whole face into something fierce. "And except that the calving window opens in nine days, and the moraine trail starts one bend downstream, and I have spent forty years learning the exact weight of the paperwork that owns this river."

Casper looked at the skiff, small and patient at the landing. Looked at the river, running north toward the glacier like it had been waiting for them all along.

"He sold us for fifty thousand dollars," she said.

"He sold us for a debt," Maren corrected. "Which means Vey owns the paper, not the man. Remember that, Casper. Everything on this river comes down to paper — who holds it, who forged it, who burns it." She started down the bank toward the landing, steady despite her ankle, and threw the last words over her shoulder like a line tossed across water:

"Now come on. We have a nation to steal back."
