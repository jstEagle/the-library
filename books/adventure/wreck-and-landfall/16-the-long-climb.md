## 16. The Long Climb

The rope the guide had left them with was forty feet of wet poly, and Casper coiled it like it was the last honest thing in the world.

They'd walked out of the weir gorge before dawn, Maren's wrists raw where the zip ties had bitten, Casper's ribs aching from the kick that had put her down. Behind them, Vey's convoy was already moving — she'd heard the diesel on the switchbacks, heard the winch cable singing as they hauled the chest up onto a flatbed. Forty years of a drowned nation, strapped down and tarped like any other salvage.

"They'll take the road," Maren said. She was standing at the treeline, looking up. Not back. Up.

"The road's faster."

"The road is for people who know where they're going." Maren turned, and there was something in her face Casper hadn't seen before — not grief, not the old woman's patience. Something harder. "I know where I'm going."

Casper looked at the mountain. The river came out of it a quarter mile above them, white and furious, pouring from under a tongue of ice that glowed blue-green even in the grey light. Above that, the valley narrowed into moraine and scree and then the glacier itself, miles of it, climbing toward the source no map showed because no map was allowed to show it.

"There's no trail," Casper said.

"There was. When I was nine." Maren shouldered her pack — half of what they'd owned, everything else gone with the guide's betrayal or sunk in the flooded channel. "My father carried me over the shoulder of it on his back. He said the mountain forgets nothing. He said the mountain doesn't care about us either." She started walking. "He was right about both."

Casper stood one more second with the rope in her hands, measuring — miles to the source, hours of light, the calving window opening sometime in the next six days if the warm front held, closing forever if it didn't. It was how she'd always thought: in distances, in rates, in what could be bought and what could be lost. Her whole life a dive log.

Then she put the rope away and followed the old woman uphill.

* * *

Maren counted in songs.

She didn't mean to. It was what her mother had done on the long walks — the dispossession marches, the ones that weren't in any treaty because treaties didn't record the people who walked out of them. Four beats to a step, a verse to a switchback. The words were Vessan, old Vessan, the dialect from before the delta towns softened it, and Maren sang them low so the girl behind her wouldn't hear all of them.

Not the girl. Casper Pemberton. Salvage diver, smuggler's daughter, the woman who'd pulled a dead nation out of the muck and then taken money to forget it and then couldn't. Maren had watched her do all three things and decided, somewhere in the middle of the night river, that this was the kind of person you could build something with. People who broke and kept walking were worth ten people who never bent.

The scree field began at midmorning. Stones the size of skulls, loose as ballast, sliding under every footfall. Maren climbed it the way her father had taught her — weight low, steps deliberate, never trust the rock that looks solid, always trust the rock that moves a little. Behind her she heard Casper learning the same lesson by falling twice.

"You're fighting it," Maren said, without turning around.

"I'm *climbing* it."

"You're fighting it. The mountain wins every fight. You have to ask it." She found the line she remembered — there, the seam of grey stone between two ridges of black, exactly where her father's shoulders had carried her past — and moved along it. "My mother sang on this slope. The whole way. Twenty-two verses. By the last one we were at the top."

"What were the songs about?"

"Water." Maren smiled at the stone in front of her nose. "Everything Vessan is about water. We were a nation of the delta before anyone drew lines through it. Our word for home and our word for river are almost the same word. You'd hear the difference only if you grew up inside it."

"Say them."

"The words?"

"The difference. Say it."

Maren stopped. Turned. Casper was three meters below her, breathing hard, hair plastered to her forehead, watching with that flat salvager attention — the look of a woman appraising something to decide its value. Maren had been appraised her whole life. Refugee. Relic. The last Vessan-born, a museum piece with a heartbeat. She knew what it was to be weighed.

"*Domal*," she said. "Home. And *domala* — the river that makes a home. One letter. One breath at the end." She held Casper's eyes. "You say *domala* when you mean that the place would still be a place without you in it. You say *domal* when you mean you are part of what makes it one."

Casper was quiet for a moment. Then she climbed on, and Maren resumed her song, four beats to a step, and did not sing the twenty-second verse until they crested the shoulder at dusk.

* * *

The wind at the top came off the glacier like a wall of knives, and they made camp in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and Casper sat with her arms around her knees doing arithmetic she hated.

No chest. No documents. No proof of anything except what Maren carried in her skull and Casper carried in her memory of having read it once, by lamplight, in a boat that was now ash. Vey had the original treaty papers, the forged signatures, the wax-sealed map. Even if they reached the archive first, even if the vault opened, what did they walk out with? Stories? An old woman's childhood?

"We're going to get there," Casper said, "and we're going to have nothing."

Maren was laying out their food on a cloth: half a block of cheese, a bag of oats, a tin of fish, a bar of chocolate gone soft. She portioned it with the care of someone who had eaten rationed her whole life. "We'll have the truth."

"The truth needs paper."

"So make paper." Maren looked up. "You told me yourself — the archive holds the original. Un-forged. Signed by men whose names are still in the land registries downstream. Brandt can verify it. Brandt *wants* to verify it. You saw his face at the tollhouse." She pushed the chocolate across the cloth. "Vey has a box. Boxes burn. You've seen that too."

Casper took the chocolate. Ate a square. The wind screamed past the boulder and the glacier below them groaned — a long tectonic complaint, ice settling against ice, the sound of a thing too large to hurry.

"How far tomorrow?" she asked.

"To the calving face? Two days, if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't." Maren wrapped herself in the emergency blanket, silver side out, and became a small bright ghost against the dark. "And then the lake."

The ice-dammed lake. Casper had seen it on the map's second layer — a body of meltwater held back by the glacier's toe like water behind a fist, and the archive route running through the gorge beneath it, passable only in the days after a major calving event cracked the dam and dropped the level. Nature's lock. Nature's schedule. And nature, as Maren kept saying, did not care.

"If it bursts early," Casper said, "we ride the surge or we miss it."

"Yes."

"And if we ride it wrong, we die."

"Yes," Maren said again, comfortably, and closed her eyes. In a minute she was asleep, the way only the very old and the very certain sleep.

Casper stayed up and watched the glacier breathe.

* * *

In the morning there was snow.

Not falling — blowing, horizontal, off the icefields above, filling the air until the world was white static and ten feet of visibility. Casper led, because Casper had the compass and Casper had spent her life navigating by instruments in zero visibility, and Maren followed holding the hem of her jacket like a child in a market crowd.

They climbed for six hours and gained perhaps a mile.

At noon they stopped in a hollow between moraine ridges and ate standing up, because sitting meant cold soaking into your bones, and Casper caught Maren looking at her hands. At the rope burns. At the scar across the left palm she'd never explained.

"My father ran this river," Casper said. It came out before she decided to let it. "Cargo, mostly untaxed, sometimes worse. I was twelve when he went down at Kell's Folly. They never found the boat. I've dived that reach eleven times." She flexed the scarred hand. "Twelfth time I found him. Or enough of him."

Maren said nothing. The wind filled the silence.

"He taught me the river the way yours taught you the mountain," Casper said. "Read it, don't fight it. Ask it." She laughed, short and ugly. "Then he fought it. One bad night, one overloaded hull, and he fought it, and the river won every fight."

"And you stopped asking it after that," Maren said. "You started buying it. Miles, dives, contracts. Numbers don't drown."

Casper stared at her. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know everything about you," Maren said mildly. "You are me, forty years ago, if I had decided the water owed me nothing." She pulled her hood up. "Come. The weather's lifting. I can smell it."

* * *

By late afternoon the snow thinned to spits and the world reassembled itself piece by piece — the ridge, the sky, the glacier rising ahead of them like a frozen sea stood on end. And there, cut into the valley wall where the map's second layer promised it, the gorge: a slot of black rock descending toward the buried route, plugged at its mouth by the glacier's toe and, above it, the pale green gleam of the ice-dammed lake, held, waiting, indifferent.

They made camp on the moraine overlooking it. Below, a mile down the valley, they could see the road — and on the road, small as toys, the lights of Vey's convoy, camped at the base of the access spur. Close. A day behind them, maybe less, and they had machines and ropes and men.

Casper lay awake listening to the glacier.

It had been talking all evening — cracks like rifle shots echoing down the valley, deep groans that she felt through the ground more than heard. Maren had said the calving window might open early. The warm front had held three days longer than forecast. Every hour the ice groaned louder.

"Maren," she whispered, into the dark. "Are you awake?"

A pause. "I am ninety-one years old, child. I am always awake at some level."

"When the dam goes — how much warning?"

"My father said the mountain sings before it shouts." Maren's voice drifted from the silver blur of the blanket. "Hours, sometimes. Sometimes minutes. You will know. Everyone in the valley will know."

"And Vey's men will know too."

"Yes." A rustle; the blanket shifted; the old woman's eyes caught the starlight. "So we sleep fast and wake faster. And tomorrow we go down to the gorge mouth and wait at the door of my country." She settled back. "Forty years I have waited to stand on Vessan ground. I did not think it would be this cold, or this high, or that I would arrive empty-handed in the company of a smuggler's daughter."

"Sorry about the company."

"Don't be." Maren's voice was already going under, thick with sleep. "Of all the people alive, you are the one who keeps showing up where the truth is buried. That is a strange profession, Casper Pemberton. But it is mine now too."

Casper lay back and watched the glacier glow faintly against the stars, blue-green, patient, a nation's worth of water and time held behind a fist of ice.

Sometime past midnight, the mountain sang.

It started as a hum she felt in her teeth, then a crack that rolled down the valley like thunder dragged across stone, then another, closer, and another — a rising litany of splitting ice, and beside her Maren was already sitting up, eyes wide open, saying quietly, "*Domala*. It's opening. Child — it's opening *now*."

Down in the dark, the glacier's toe shuddered, calved, and the lake behind it began, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, to let go.
