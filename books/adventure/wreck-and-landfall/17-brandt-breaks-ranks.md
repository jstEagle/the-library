## 17. Brandt Breaks Ranks

The convoy left the tollhouse at 0600, and Brandt counted it the way he counted everything now: like evidence.

Four vehicles. Two flatbeds with tarped cargo riding too high for salvage equipment, one fuel truck, and Vey's black SUV with its government plates and its antenna farm. Brandt had requisitioned a helicopter for "upstream site verification" and gotten it, because nobody at the regional office questioned Aldous Vey's subcontractors asking for things. That was the whole architecture of the operation — nobody questioned anything, and the questions that got asked were routed back through the people who shouldn't be answering them.

He sat in the helicopter's jump seat with a clipboard he didn't need and watched the river unspool below them through the morning haze. It ran green-gray here, braided around gravel bars, patient. Forty miles upstream it would turn white, then turn to ice, and somewhere in between it kept a drowned nation's paperwork in a vault carved by people who had believed, right up until the treaties, that the law would protect them.

Brandt had believed that too, once. It was embarrassing how recently.

"You're quiet this morning," said Reyes, the pilot, over the intercom.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a man doing math."

Brandt looked down at the convoy threading the valley road, dust trailing it like a fuse. "Just counting," he said.

* * *

He'd found it on a Tuesday, in the ash of Casper Pemberton's barge.

Officially the fire was an insurance matter — unattended fuel stove, structural failure, the kind of report you could write in your sleep because you'd written it a hundred times. Brandt had walked the burned hulk with a fire marshal who wanted his lunch more than his diligence, and while the man photographed the galley for the third time, Brandt had knelt at the waterline beneath the engine housing, where the char didn't reach, and found the transponder.

Salvage vessels carried them by regulation. This one had been pulled from its mount — the bracket screws were sheared, not unsheared, torn out in haste — and stowed in the driest corner of the bilge, wrapped in a waxed cloth. Someone had disabled it before the fire. Someone had known the fire was coming.

But the transponder had a buffer. Thirty days of position logs, and the last twelve hours of them showed a second signal — a chase boat running dark, no AIS broadcast, holding position off Pemberton's dock for six hours before the barge burned. Six hours of a ghost sitting in the current, watching, and then the fire.

He'd run the ghost boat's engine signature through the contractor fleet registry because he had access, because he was navy, because access was the one currency they'd never thought to take back from him. The signature matched a vessel chartered to Meridian Resource Recovery. Meridian Resource Recovery held a sole-source contract administered by — he'd laughed, actually laughed, alone in his office at midnight — Aldous Vey.

That had been eleven days ago. He'd spent eleven days doing what a lawful man does with evidence: he'd drafted a report. He'd attached the transponder logs, the fleet registry cross-reference, the fuel purchase records from the tollhouse that showed Vey's convoy buying enough diesel to reach the glacier and back. He'd written it clean and neutral, the way reports were supposed to be written, no adjectives, no accusations, just the arithmetic.

And then he'd sat with it for eleven days, because he knew exactly what would happen to it.

It would go to his commanding officer, who reported to the regional director, who took his marching orders from the same interagency working group that had contracted Vey in the first place. The report would be classified under some environmental-security exemption he'd never heard of, and Brandt himself would be reassigned, or investigated, or simply erased from the file the way Vessany had been erased from the maps. He'd seen it happen to a fisheries officer upriver two years ago, a man named Okonjo who'd asked one too many questions about dredging permits. Okonjo now managed a marina in a town whose name Brandt couldn't remember. That was the gentle version.

The report sat in his desk drawer like a loaded thing.

And Pemberton was upstream with an old woman and a chest that could unmake forty years of dispossession, and Aldous Vey was driving toward her with four vehicles and, Brandt was now nearly certain, weapons that no salvage contract authorized.

* * *

The convoy stopped at midday at a wide bend where the valley narrowed, and Vey's SUV pulled off the road onto the gravel bar. Brandt told Reyes to set down on the far bank "for a terrain assessment," which was the kind of phrase that made paperwork instead of questions.

He crossed the shallows on foot, clipboard under his arm, and found Vey standing at the water's edge with his coat collar up, studying the current the way other men studied stock tickers.

"Officer Brandt." Vey didn't turn around. "I wondered when you'd catch up."

"You filed a route plan with the regional office. I'm verifying compliance."

"Compliance with what?"

"Environmental protection protocols for the upper corridor. Restricted access above the tollhouse." Brandt stopped beside him. The river talked between them, cold and constant. "Your convoy is carrying more fuel than the round trip requires, Mr. Vey. And your cargo manifest lists survey equipment that doesn't match the weight distribution on your flatbeds."

Vey turned then. He had the kind of face that aged well because it had never been asked to feel anything. "You've been busy."

"I do my job."

"Your job," Vey said, "is to make sure the river corridor stays closed to unauthorized traffic, so that authorized traffic can conduct its authorized work without interference. That's the arrangement. It's been the arrangement for a long time, and it has worked very well for a very long time, including for the navy whose uniform you're wearing." He smiled without warmth. "You found something on that burned barge. I can tell. You've got the look. Men who find something always have the look — like they're holding it behind their teeth."

Brandt said nothing.

"Here's what I'd like you to consider," Vey went on, turning back to the water. "Whatever it is, it's already in a drawer somewhere, and drawers are where things go to be handled properly. Slowly. By the right people. If you try to force it, it stops being a drawer and becomes a fire, and fires burn the men who light them far more often than the men they're aimed at. I've watched it happen. I've helped it happen." He shrugged. "I'm not threatening you, Officer. I'm describing the weather."

"I know the weather," Brandt said. "I've been reading it for years."

He walked back across the shallows with his pulse loud in his ears, and Vey watched him go, and Brandt understood that he had just been measured, and that the measurement had come back insufficient, and that from this moment forward he was either useful or in the way, and he had just chosen which.

* * *

That night, in the helicopter's parked cabin at the tollhouse landing, Brandt took the report out of his case and read it one more time under the dome light. Clean. Neutral. True.

Then he did the thing he'd been circling for eleven days. He opened the radio cabinet.

The set was a standard fleet unit, encrypted, channel-locked to navy frequencies — but the tollhouse relay, the one Vey's subcontractors ran, sat on an open marine band, because the subcontractors were cheap and the tollhouse operator needed to talk to the fishing boats. Brandt had listened to that band for three nights. Fishing chatter, weather, a woman on a boat called *Kestrel* who never gave a position but whose voice he'd have known anywhere now, because he'd spent a week reading her salvage filings and her father's old smuggling-era incident reports, and because she was the only person in this whole rotten corridor trying to move the truth upstream instead of drowning it.

He couldn't use the encrypted set. He couldn't use anything logged. So he did it the way his grandfather had done it, the way river men had always done it: he wrote it down and waited for a hand to carry it.

The hand came at 2300, in the form of the tollhouse operator, a gray-bearded man named Sool who took bribes from Vey's people and, Brandt had learned from the tollhouse ledger, took smaller bribes from everyone else too, which made him not an ally but a market. Brandt bought him with the only coin he had left.

"I need a message passed on the open band tonight. Fishing traffic. No log entry."

Sool looked at the money, then at Brandt's face. "This about the fire?"

"This about the fire."

"The old woman," Sool said. "Ostrev. My mother sang her songs. We all did, before it got complicated." He took the money. "What's the message?"

Brandt had written it in fishing shorthand, the clipped code river men used when the water was high and the wardens were near. He read it once, made Sool read it back, then burned the paper in the cabin stove.

*Convoy departs 0600. Route: valley road to the Weir, then north fork trail to the high lake. Two days to the glacier. Four vehicles, armed. They have your chest. Move.*

* * *

At 0540 he stood in the cold watching the convoy load, and Vey came over to him with two cups of terrible coffee and handed him one, and Brandt took it, because refusing would be a statement and he needed one more hour of being nobody.

"Sleep well?" Vey asked.

"Like a stone."

"You know," Vey said, sipping, "in another life you'd have been good at this. You've got the discipline. Most men can't hold a thing behind their teeth for more than a season. You've held yours for years." He looked out at the river, pink with dawn. "That's all this country is, in the end. Men holding things behind their teeth. It's why it works."

"It's why it's rotten," Brandt said.

Vey glanced at him, and something in his face shifted — not anger, something more like an accountant finding an error he'd have to correct. "Careful, Officer."

"I'm always careful," Brandt said. "That's my whole problem."

The convoy pulled out at 0600 exactly, four vehicles and a fuse of dust, heading for the Weir and the north fork and the high cold country where a drowned nation kept its receipts. Brandt stood on the gravel bar until the dust settled, then walked to the helicopter, and told Reyes they'd take a slow patrol loop downriver first — "checking for unauthorized traffic" — which would put them an honest two hours behind the convoy and no closer than that.

Reyes gave him the math look. "You're letting them go."

"I'm following procedure," Brandt said. "Procedure takes time."

They lifted off into the bright morning, and Brandt looked down once at the river — green-gray, braided, patient, running the wrong way for everyone who'd ever tried to own it — and then at the valley road where four vehicles crawled north carrying a nation's stolen grave goods toward the ice.

Two hours. Maybe three, if the Weir road washed out like it always did.

He'd done everything a lawful man could do inside the law, and the law had looked at him with Vey's face and offered him coffee. So now he was doing the other thing. The thing his oath had actually been about, before the oaths got routed through working groups.

He keyed the radio — the open marine band, unlogged, the tollhouse relay already bought and burning — and sent the second message in the clear, in plain words, consequences be damned:

*All stations, all stations. Meridian convoy, four vehicles, armed, heading Weir to north fork, ETA high lake tomorrow night. Any vessel upstream, any hand on the water — they're coming for the archive. You have two days.*

He set the handset down. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Somewhere downstream, if the river was kind, a woman in a salvager's boat was going to hear that and turn her bow into the current, and an old woman was going to pray in a language the maps had forgotten, and the two of them were going to race a convoy and a calving glacier for the truth at the source.

And somewhere behind him, by nightfall, his own report would be discovered missing from its drawer, and the machine would turn around and look at him, and Aldous Vey would stop describing the weather and start making it.

Brandt watched the north fork glitter through the windshield and found, to his astonishment, that he wasn't afraid.

"Change of heading," he said to Reyes. "Take us upriver. Slow."

"Slow," Reyes repeated. "That's new."

"Everything's new," Brandt said, and the helicopter banked north into the cold country.
