## 18. Calving

The mountain spoke first.

Not a sound you heard so much as one you felt — a deep, tectonic groan rolling up through the bedrock and into the soles of Casper's boots, into her teeth, into the marrow of her. She stopped mid-stride on the moraine ridge, the pack straps biting her shoulders, and turned toward the glacier's face two miles up the valley.

Maren stood a few paces ahead, and she had stopped too. She was facing the ice with her head tilted, the way a person listens to music from another room.

"You hear it," Casper said.

"I remember it." Maren's voice was thin with age and wind, but steady. "Forty years, and the mountain remembers me."

The glacier's terminus filled the head of the valley like a wall of dirty blue glass, three hundred feet of compressed time, and behind it — hidden, dammed, patient — lay the meltwater lake that had no name on any current map. On the old Vessan charts, the ones Maren had recited from memory through six days of portage, it had a name. *Vess-Kald*, the held water. The lake that waits.

Casper had been counting on it waiting another four days. The calving season was late this year; Brandt's radio message had said Vey's convoy wouldn't reach the valley floor for two more days after that. The whole plan had been built on a schedule written in ice, and ice, she had told Maren a dozen times, keeps no appointments.

Now the ice was breaking them.

* * *

They had come up the last of the moraine at dawn, following the river's ghost — the dry channel that, in the weeks before calving, ran nearly empty while the glacier held its meltwater back like a fist. The Vessan archive lay in the gorge beyond the terminus, carved into bedrock where the river's source burst free each summer. For a few days — sometimes only hours — after the lake burst, the flood scoured the gorge clean and the water dropped, and the vault's entrance, sealed behind a curtain of falls for most of the year, stood open to anyone mad enough to be there.

Maren had been that mad once. Sixty years ago, as a girl, before the drowning of the nation, before the forged treaty and the exile barges. She had stood in that gorge. She had seen the door.

"Four days," Casper said now, watching the glacier's face. "That's what the melt pattern said. That's what every chart said."

"The charts were made by people who wanted the gorge closed," Maren said. "Maybe the ice is more honest than the mapmakers." She shaded her eyes. "Look at the face. See how it's leaning?"

Casper saw it. The whole terminus had a bulge to it, a swelling at the waterline, the way a river swells before it takes a bridge. And along the crest of the dam — the saddle of ice where the lake pressed against the mountain's shoulder — the surface had gone the gray-white of rotten snow. Crevasses had opened in the night; she could see them even at this distance, blue-black seams like the beginnings of a sentence the glacier had been trying to write for forty years.

"It's going today," Casper said.

"Within hours." Maren turned to her, and in the flat white light her face looked carved from the same material as the mountain — old, weathered, immovable. "The flood will come down the dry channel. The gorge will open. And Vey is two days behind us with the chest and the map and thirty men." She paused. "We can be through the gorge before the water drops. They cannot."

"Because we'll be dead."

"Because we'll be *fast*," Maren said. "There's a difference. My father took the flood channel twice, in the old days, when the Vessan pilots ran the source run. He said the water is not your enemy. The water is a door that opens once. Your only job is to be standing in the doorway."

"Your father also died on this river."

"No," Maren said quietly. "Your father died on this river. Mine died in a courtroom, of a broken heart. Different rivers. Same current." She shouldered her pack. "We need the boat."

"We don't have the boat. We have a raft made of driftwood and two dry bags and a prayer."

"Yes," Maren said. "The Vessans called that a *keld-boat*. It means 'enough.'"

* * *

They built it in the lee of a moraine mound while the mountain groaned at intervals — longer groans now, deeper, like a sleeper turning toward waking. Casper lashed driftwood with the last of their cordage, waterproofed the seams with pine pitch they'd boiled out of a downed snag two valleys back, and rigged the dry bags as flotation in a frame that would have embarrassed her old salvage crew. Six feet of raft. Two paddles cut from green alder. Enough.

Her hands worked while her mind ran the channel. She had run water her whole life — smuggler's daughter, salvage diver, a woman who read currents the way bankers read ledgers. She knew what a flood surge did in a confined gorge. It didn't flow. It *arrived* — a wall of water, ice, and boulders moving at the speed of a falling object, filling the channel wall to wall, and everything in it became part of the water or part of the gorge's new architecture. There was no eddy-hopping a flood. No ferry angle. No technical line.

There was only riding it, and staying on top, and praying the gorge's floor hadn't moved since the last map.

"Maren." She drove a lash tight with her thumb. "The gorge. When your father ran it — how deep was the water after the surge?"

"Deep enough to float the raft over the boulder fields. That's the point. The flood lifts you over what would kill you at low water." Maren fed her the next length of cord without being asked; they had been doing that for days now, the wordless economy of two women who had learned each other's hands. "But the surge drops fast. The lake empties in — he said — the time it takes to boil tea. Maybe an hour of good water. Maybe less."

"An hour." Casper looked up the valley. The dry channel wound down from the glacier's base, a mile of gray boulder garden currently wearing a thin ribbon of seep-water. Beyond it, the gorge proper: two miles of granite slot, walls two hundred feet high, the sealed vault somewhere in its middle reach behind the falls that would be falls no longer. "An hour to run three miles of channel that's actively trying to redesign itself."

"Yes."

"And if we miss the window, the gorge seals behind us and Vey walks in whenever he likes."

"Yes."

Casper tied off the last lash and sat back on her heels. Somewhere above, the glacier made a sound like a rifle shot, then another, then a long tearing roar that went on for ten seconds and stopped as if a door had closed on it.

"Then we don't miss the window," she said.

* * *

They carried the raft down to the channel's edge and waited.

Waiting was the worst of it. Casper had spent her whole life converting waiting into work — dive checks, gear maintenance, log entries — because idle time let in the things she kept out. Now there was nothing to maintain. The raft was done or it wasn't. The channel was empty or it wasn't. She sat on a boulder with the alder paddle across her knees and watched the glacier, and the glacier watched her back, and between them the dry channel lay like a held breath.

Maren sat beside her and, of all things, sang.

It was low, barely more than breath — a Vessan river-song, one of the ones the delta town had taught them, the melody worn smooth like a stone in a current. Casper had heard it a dozen times since the town. She had never asked what the words meant. She asked now, because if she was going to die in an hour she wanted to know what the old woman was humming over her.

"It's a launch song," Maren said. "Boatmen sang it when they put in at the source. It says: *The water remembers every boat, and lets go of none.*"

"That's supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be true." Maren's eyes stayed on the glacier. "Comfort is a different country, Casper. I gave up my citizenship there a long time ago."

The next groan came and did not stop.

It built the way a wave builds — rising pitch, rising pressure — and then the whole face of the glacier moved. Not a chunk. Not a calving. The *face*, two hundred yards of it, leaned forward out of itself with a slowness that was obscene, that was wrong, ice the mass of a town detaching from ice the mass of a mountain, and for one full second the world held its breath.

Then it fell.

The sound was not a sound. It was a blow to the chest, a pressure, a white detonation that sent a plume of spray and shattered ice five hundred feet into the air and a shockwave down the valley that knocked grit off the moraine and into Casper's open mouth. The wave from the impact raced back across the hidden lake, and the lake — dammed, full, forty years patient — took the blow and passed it to the dam.

The dam held for eleven seconds. Casper counted them without meaning to.

Then the saddle of ice sagged, split, and let go, and the held water of Vess-Kald came down the valley like the end of the world changing its mind.

* * *

"NOW!"

They were moving before the word finished leaving her mouth — raft down the bank, into the thin ribbon of water that was already thickening, already climbing the stones. Casper shoved off with both paddles as the first tongue of the surge came around the bend upstream, and it was not a wall, not yet, it was a rising — the channel filling like a bathtub with the tap wide open, water climbing the boulders hand over hand, and the raft lifted, spun, and caught.

The current took them at twenty miles an hour and accelerated.

The dry channel's boulder garden vanished beneath them in the space of a breath. Where an hour ago Casper had picked her way on foot between gray stones the size of trucks, now there was only brown violence, a single fluid muscle of meltwater, ice chunks, and pulverized glacier flour, and the raft rode its crest like a leaf on a boot. Spray hit her face hard enough to bruise. The cold was absolute — snowmelt cold, the cold of things that had been frozen since before her grandmother — and it went through her jacket and into her bones and she welcomed it, because cold meant awake.

"STEER!" Maren screamed, and Casper steered, or made the gestures of steering, dragging the alder paddle through water that had the density of wet concrete. The raft didn't so much answer as forgive her.

The channel bent. The gorge mouth came at them — a black slot in the granite, and the river, all of it, every gallon the mountain had held for a year, funneled into that slot with a roar she felt in her ribs. The walls narrowed. The water rose. And the raft, impossibly, rose with it, lifted on the compression wave at the gorge's throat and shot inside.

The world became stone and noise.

Walls two hundred feet high, close enough to touch, streaked with the high-water marks of ten thousand forgotten floods. The channel inside was a series of drops and reversals, and the flood turned each of them into something new — a hole the size of a house here, a collapsing pillow of water there, and everywhere the ice: boulder-sized chunks of the calved terminus riding the surge like wrecking balls, and the raft threading between them at the mercy of a current that did not know they existed.

A chunk of ice the size of a car surfaced off the port bow. Casper dug the paddle, leaned, screamed something that wasn't a word, and the raft yawed just enough. The ice passed close enough that she could see the bubbles frozen in it, forty years old, a winter that had happened before she was born, preserved and rushing past at thirty miles an hour.

Maren was in the bow, on her knees, one hand locked in the lash-line, and she was *singing*. Full voice now, the launch song ripped wide open, and Casper realized through the terror that the old woman was steering too — calling the water the way her father's generation had called it, reading the surface the way Casper read dive charts. "*Left!* Left and hold — *now right* — the falls, Casper, the falls—"

The falls. The vault.

The gorge bent hard to starboard and the granite wall on the outside of the bend was black with a vertical seam — the falls' channel, dry now, its curtain of water gone with the lake that fed it. And at its base, half-hidden behind a fin of rock, a shape that was not geology. Squared edges. Cut stone. A door.

The archive.

"THE FIN!" Casper screamed. "AIM FOR THE FIN — WE HIT THE EDDY BEHIND IT OR WE'RE GONE!"

The eddy behind the fin would be a pocket of slack water in the flood's shoulder — if it existed, if the flood hadn't erased it, if the raft could reach it before the current carried them past into the gorge's lower reach where no eddy would save them. It was a salvage maneuver, the kind she'd done a hundred times on wrecks in current — the last-moment grab, the one attempt, no second chances.

She dug both paddles. Maren threw her weight to starboard. The raft slewed, wallowed, and for one endless second hung on the flood's shoulder like a held note —

— and the fin of rock slid past close enough to touch, and behind it the world went suddenly, shockingly quiet.

The eddy held. A circle of churning, breathing slack, maybe fifteen feet across, spinning slow as a drain, and the raft sat in it going nowhere while the apocalypse roared past six feet to their left.

Casper put the paddle down. Her hands were shaking so hard the alder chattered against the thwart. Maren knelt in the bow, soaked, gray-lipped, sixty-eight years old, and she was laughing — a cracked, gasping, disbelieving laugh that turned halfway through into something else, into weeping, into the launch song's last line sung to no one.

"The water remembers every boat," she said, wiping her face with a shaking hand. "And it let go of us. Casper. Look."

Casper looked.

The door stood twenty feet up the dry falls channel — cut granite, Vessan characters chiseled deep, the seal of the drowned nation worn but legible, exactly as it had been worn on the chest she'd pulled from the bottom of the channel a lifetime ago. Above it, the cliff where the falls had lived was bare wet stone, streaming with residual spray, and the gorge beyond the vault's landing was already dropping — the surge moving through, the lake emptying, the window beginning to close.

Behind them, downstream, faint under the river's roar, came a sound she knew from her smuggling childhood: engines. Many of them. Far off, but coming.

Vey had heard the mountain too.

Casper took the coil of rope off her shoulder, looked up at the door her companion had been exiled from for forty years, and said, "Then we'd better be inside before they're outside. Climb."
