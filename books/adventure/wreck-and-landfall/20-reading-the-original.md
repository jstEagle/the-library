## 20. Reading the Original

The vault smelled of cold stone and old iron, of a century held its breath.

Casper had expected darkness to feel like defeat down here — the last chamber of a drowned nation, lit by three camping lanterns and a salvager's headlamp propped on a crate. But the light did something strange in the carved rock. It pooled warm on the walls where Vessan masons had chiseled lines of script four generations dead, and the letters caught the glow like water catches sun, and for a moment the room was not a tomb at all. It was a library waiting for readers.

Brandt stood at the entrance with his sidearm holstered and Vey's two contractors zip-tied to an iron ring set into the floor, their protests worn down to sullen muttering. Vey himself sat against the far wall, wrists bound, one eye swelling shut from where he'd gone into the rock during the standoff. He hadn't spoken since Brandt read him the charges off a folded sheet Casper had pulled from the ashes of her own barge. He just watched. That was worse than talking. A man like Aldous Vey didn't stop working when he stopped moving; he was already composing the report that would make this night a rounding error in someone's ledger above them.

Maren Ostrev knelt at the center of the vault, before the stone coffer they'd broken open with a pry bar and twenty minutes of Maren's shaking hands. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay the original Treaty of the Delta Confluence — signed, witnessed, sealed in lead the way Vessans sealed things that were meant to outlast empires.

"Forty years," Maren said. Her voice came out cracked, like ice under load. "Forty years I've told people we lost fairly. Signed away. Agreed." She looked up at Casper, and in the lantern light her face was all the ages she'd ever been at once. "We never agreed to anything."

Casper crouched beside her. The oilcloth crackled as she peeled it back. Beneath lay a document the length of her forearm, dense with copperplate script, the ink browned but unbroken. Two signatures anchored the bottom margin — and beneath them, six witness marks, each with a name and a date, each countersigned by a notary whose seal still held its embossed teeth.

She'd spent her life reading documents underwater, judging what paper could survive and what it couldn't. She knew forgery the way a fisherman knows bad knots. And she knew, before anyone said a word, that this was real.

"The copy in the national registry," she said slowly, "has five witnesses. Not six."

Maren's breath caught.

"And the fifth signature is different. I've seen both now — the forged version was in the chest, remember, filed with the navy's suppression order. They kept the fake close because it was the only thing keeping the theft legal." Casper traced the air above the sixth line without touching it. "This one has a witness the other doesn't. A delta notary. Ilse Brannoch."

"Ilse Brannoch," Maren whispered. "My grandmother sang about her. Said she refused to sign the lie, so they erased her from the record entirely. Cut her out of the copy and called the cut a clerical error." Tears were running freely down the old woman's face now, and she made no move to stop them. "They didn't just steal the land. They stole the proof we'd ever owned it. They stole the *witness*."

"That's the whole architecture of it," Casper said. "You don't need to burn a nation if you can edit it. One signature removed, one notary vanished, and forty years later nobody even remembers there was anything to argue about." She looked over at Brandt. "Except the navy remembered. Somebody high enough up remembered enough to pay a contractor to drown the original in a restricted wreck site and pretend it was ballast."

Brandt's jaw worked. He was a lawful man standing inside the biggest lawlessness he'd ever seen, and Casper could see the cost of it running through him like current through a cable. "The registry copy is the legal instrument," he said. "Courts work from registries."

"Then courts have been working from a forgery for forty years," Casper said. "And here's the thing about a forgery — it isn't a different opinion. It's a crime. It voids itself the moment the true instrument surfaces. This—" she touched the oilcloth at last, gently, the way you touch something sleeping, "—this doesn't *argue* with the treaty on file. It *is* the treaty on file. Everything else is a counterfeit that's been spending our money."

*Our* money. She heard herself say it and didn't correct it.

Maren heard it too. The old woman turned and looked at her — a long, level look, the kind Maren gave the river before deciding whether to trust it — and then she nodded once, and turned back to the document, and began to read aloud.

* *

Her voice filled the vault the way water fills a channel — finding every low place, settling into every hollow.

She read the preamble first, and it was poetry the way old legal language is poetry: formal, patient, certain of its own permanence. *Let it be known to all who hold these waters in trust.* The treaty granted the delta lands to the Vessan communes in perpetuity — not sold, not ceded, *held*, with the river itself named as guarantor. There was a clause about the glacier source, a right of access written so plainly it sounded almost casual: *the archive at the ice-head shall remain open to any Vessan-born soul, in perpetuity, without toll or permission.*

"They built the vault after signing this," Maren said, pausing. "Carved it into bedrock specifically so no government could seal it. My father used to say the mountain was the only honest clerk in the country." She laughed, wet and brief. "He was right. It took a calving flood to open it, but it opened. On schedule. In perpetuity."

She read the land grants. Names Casper half-recognized from the songs in the delta town — the Ferrow holdings along the lower channels, the Ostrev fields near the ferry landing, the Brannoch notary's parcel at the confluence. Forty years of towns built on top of those names, of deeds issued by a government that had inherited stolen paper and called it law. Casper thought of the river towns downstream, the ones they'd carry copies to, the ones who would have to decide what to do with a truth this size. Some of them sat on land their grandparents had taken without knowing it was taken. That reckoning wasn't hers to schedule. But it would come, and it would come because this page existed.

Then Maren reached the signatures, and her voice failed her.

It took her three tries. Casper put a hand on her shoulder and left it there. Behind them, one of the zip-tied contractors shifted, and Brandt said, quietly, "Sit still," in a voice that had nothing negotiable left in it.

On the fourth try, Maren read the names of the signatories. Then the witnesses, one by one, each name given full weight, each name a resurrection. When she reached the sixth line she stopped breathing for a moment.

"Ilse Brannoch, notary of the confluence, who saw all parties sign freely and attests the same under her seal and her life." Maren pressed her palm flat against the stone floor. "They killed her for it, you know. Or disappeared her; same thing. My grandmother said the soldiers came for her the week the fake treaty was published, and no one ever said her name again out loud. Saying it was dangerous. So we sang around it. We sang *the notary of the crossing* instead, for forty years, like cowards, like survivors." She lifted her head. "Ilse Brannoch. There. Out loud. In the room your mountain kept safe. Let them come disappear me too."

Nobody came. The vault held its silence, and the lanterns burned, and the name stayed said.

Casper found she was crying too, which annoyed her distantly, the way weather annoys you when you're trying to work. She let it happen anyway. There was no one down here to be competent for.

* *

Vey spoke for the first time in an hour.

"You understand what you've accomplished." His voice was hoarse but level, the voice of a man giving notes on a draft. "Nothing. You've read a piece of paper in a hole in the ground. Courts don't take instructions from bedrock. The registry governs. The registry says the land transferred legally. You're holding a historical curiosity, and in six months my principals will have buried this site under a hundred meters of new restrictions, and in ten years it'll be a story exiles tell their grandchildren, and it will change exactly nothing."

Casper looked at him for a long moment. Swollen eye, bound wrists, and still that terrible patience, that institutional calm that treated human beings as line items. She'd met men like him her whole life — at salvage auctions, at customs desks, across her father's card table. Men who believed that ownership was whatever the strongest paperwork said.

"You're half right," she said. "A court might not care. Yet." She stood, joints aching from the climb, and picked up the treaty with both hands. "But you've been downstream, Vey. Have you ever actually *looked* at the river towns? Not surveyed them. Looked. There are forty thousand people living on water that was theirs before your principals' grandparents learned to spell. They've been singing Vessan songs in locked chapels and teaching their kids the old names under their breath. They don't need a court to tell them who they are. They need a court to catch up." She turned the document so the lamplight caught the seals. "We're going to give them this. Every town. Every landing. Copies, translations, the whole instrument, read aloud on the water where it was signed. Let the people who live on the river decide what their inheritance is. If the courts want to follow, fine. If they want to stand in front of forty thousand people holding a forgery and explain themselves — also fine."

"That's not how law works," Vey said.

"It's how rivers work," Maren said. "Everything comes down eventually."

Brandt stepped between them before Vey could answer, and his voice had changed — Casper heard it, the sound of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been circling for weeks. "Aldous Vey. Acting under authority I am formally exceeding, I'm placing you under citizen's arrest pending transfer to civil jurisdiction. Evidence: arson of a registered vessel, conspiracy to suppress a legal instrument, attempted homicide at the narrows, and the destruction order recovered from your own field files, which I have photographed and transmitted." He paused. "To three addresses. Not all of them friendly to your employers."

Vey stared at him. For the first time all night, something moved behind the calm — not fear exactly, but recalculation, the ledger refusing to balance. "You'll be finished," he said. "Career, pension, clearance. All of it."

"Yeah," Brandt said. "I did the math on that back at the burned barge." He glanced at Casper. "Your fire gave me the numbers."

* *

Later — much later, when the contractors had been marched to the vault's outer chamber and secured, when Brandt had rigged a signal mirror to flash the valley station at dawn, when the adrenaline had drained out of the stone and left only exhaustion — Casper sat alone with the treaty and the map, side by side on the coffer lid, and finally understood what she'd been carrying since the bottom of the channel.

She'd thought she'd found a treasure. Then she'd thought she'd found a weapon. Then a burden. All wrong, or all incomplete. What she'd found was a *handoff*. Forty years ago, a notary named Ilse Brannoch had died rather than let a lie become the record, and someone — some Vessan hand, some desperate midnight — had sealed the truth in lead and sunk it where only the river could keep it. The river had kept it. The river had handed it to a dredge hook at dawn, to a woman who measured her life in paid miles because measuring it any other way hurt too much.

Inheriting wasn't getting something. Casper saw that now, clear as depth readings. Inheriting was being *trusted* with something — trusted by the dead, who couldn't check your references. Her father had run this current with contraband in his hold and a daughter on the deck, and everyone in the delta towns had called him a smuggler, and maybe he was, but he'd also ferried people the law didn't want ferried, carried letters, kept mouths shut, kept small boats moving through big dangers. He'd never once explained it to her. He'd just done it in front of her, mile after mile, until the doing was in her hands.

She'd been inheriting all along. The chest hadn't started anything. It had just finally told her what she already owned.

Maren settled onto the crate beside her, wrapped in both their blankets, her eyes red-rimmed and steady. "You're doing the thing," she said. "Where you stare at the paper like it owes you rent."

"It doesn't owe me anything." Casper smoothed the edge of the treaty. "It's not mine. None of this is mine. That's the whole point."

"No," Maren agreed. "It's yours to *carry*, though. There's a difference, and it's the most important difference there is." She leaned her shoulder against Casper's, an old woman's weight, unapologetic. "Tomorrow we go down. All of it, every town, read aloud. Ilse Brannoch first. Then the grants. Then the names."

"Forty thousand people," Casper said. "Some of them are going to be furious. Some of them are sitting on land this says was never sold."

"Some of them are going to be furious," Maren said comfortably. "Good. Fury means they believe it. Belief is the hard part; we've got the paper." She closed her eyes. "Wake me when the mirror starts flashing. I intend to sleep in my homeland tonight, and I intend to enjoy it."

Casper sat a while longer after her breathing slowed. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere above, through a hundred meters of ice and rock, the glacier groaned and settled, the mountain keeping its books.

She took out her logbook — the old habit, the last one she had left — and uncapped her pen, and under the coordinates and the dive times and the miles, she wrote a single line:

*Day 1. Cargo: the truth. Destination: everywhere.*

Outside the vault mouth, far down the black gorge, the river was already running, carrying everything toward morning.
