## 21. The River Decides

They went downstream the way water goes — without argument, without hurry, taking every channel offered to them.

Casper had copied the treaty four times on the archive's own paper, using the vault's iron-gall ink that Maren mixed from a recipe she hadn't spoken aloud in forty years. The copies were wrapped in oilcloth, then in canvas, then in Casper's oldest dive bag, the one with the patched shoulder strap she'd carried since she was nineteen. Four documents. Forty years of theft, folded flat.

"Which town first?" Maren asked. She stood at the tiller of the rebuilt *Kestrel* — not the same boat, but the same name, and Casper had decided that mattered more than planking.

"Doesn't matter," Casper said. "All of them. Same day if we can manage it."

Maren looked at her. "You want them to hear it together."

"I want them to hear it before anyone can tell them what it means. Before Vey's lawyers, before the navy's press office, before some minister calls it an administrative error." Casper watched the current take them past the first of the delta's braided bars. "Let the river read it first."

Brandt came aboard at Graywater Landing, two days later, out of uniform, carrying his resignation letter folded in his breast pocket like a splinter he hadn't yet worked loose.

"They'll call it sedition," he said. "A gathering on the water. Unlicensed assembly."

"It's a regatta," Casper said. "Towns hold them all the time."

"Not with legal testimony."

"Then it's a very well-attended regatta."

He almost smiled. It sat strangely on his face, like a knot tied wrong. He'd spent three weeks building the case — Vey's burn orders recovered from the barge wreckage, the subcontractor ledgers, the guide's confession at the weir, all of it cross-indexed in a navy clerk's careful hand. The network was already bleeding. Two of Vey's tollhouses had simply closed, their operators gone inland before the warrants landed. But warrants weren't the same as truth, and Brandt knew the difference better than anyone.

"The courts will take years," he said. "The dispossession claim has to be filed, validated, appealed—"

"So we don't wait for the courts." Maren said it from the stern, her voice level, the way she said everything now. "The land was taken by paper. So we answer with paper. Read to them. Let them decide what they own."

"And if they decide nothing?"

Maren turned to look at him fully. "Then they decide nothing. That is also theirs to choose." She paused. "That is the whole point, officer. We are not delivering a verdict. We are returning one."

* * *

They started at Sallow Bend because it was smallest, and because Maren remembered it — remembered it from before, when it had another name that no one used anymore except in songs sung too quietly.

Twenty-three boats gathered on the water below the bend: fishing skiffs, a gravel barge, two houseboats lashed together with someone's laundry strung between them. People stood in their boats because there was nowhere else to stand, and the water held them all level, and Casper thought: this is what a court looks like when it belongs to itself.

She read the treaty aloud from the bow of the *Kestrel*. Not the forged one — the original, the true signatures, the clauses that named the delta towns as Vessan commons held in perpetuity for those who lived on and worked the water. Her voice cracked once, on the word *perpetuity*, and she let it crack. These people had watched their grandparents' names get struck from rolls and maps. They could watch a stranger's voice shake.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a long moment. Then an old man in a green skiff — Casper recognized him distantly, a net-mender from the lower channel — said, "Read the part about the ferry rights again."

She read it again.

"And the mill levies," someone called from the houseboats.

She read those too. And then they began to argue — good, loud, river-town arguing, the kind that runs all day and ends with shared soup — about what the clauses meant, who counted as a water-worker, whether the old boundaries followed the 1849 channel or the present one. A surveyor's daughter produced a chart. A woman whose family had kept Vessan burial customs in secret for two generations stood up in her skiff and sang four lines of something old, and half the boats went quiet, and the other half joined in badly, and it didn't matter that it was bad.

Brandt took testimony from the bow, writing in his clerk's hand while the river rocked him. Names, claims, memories, grievances forty years deep and older. By dusk the green-skiff net-mender had been elected — elected, on the water, by raised oars — to carry Sallow Bend's copy of the treaty and convene the next gathering himself.

"We're not waiting for your courts," he told Brandt, not unkindly. "But you can come. As a witness."

"I'd like that," Brandt said, and meant it.

* * *

They ran the copies down through the delta like seed.

Colderon, where the copy was read from the roof of the cannery to six hundred people standing on the mudflats at low tide. Harrow Mouth, where the ferrymen declared the crossing free until the inheritance question was settled, and the tollbooth — one of Vey's, once — was dismantled plank by plank and rebuilt as a reading room on pilings. Little Ash, where nobody argued at all; they simply listened, and then a boy of maybe twelve asked, "So do we have to give anything back?" and an old woman answered, "No, love. For the first time in forty years, nobody has to give anything back," and the whole fleet blew their horns at once, a sound that rolled upriver for miles.

Vey's network didn't collapse so much as evaporate. With the treaty public, the tolls became indefensible — crews refused to collect them, then refused to guard them, then locked the gates and left the keys in the locks. A contractor's power was always contractual, Casper realized, watching an empty checkpoint drift loose off its moorings at Colderon; take away the willingness of the paid, and the whole structure floats apart. Vey himself sat in a navy brig awaiting trial, his lawyers fighting over jurisdiction while the river towns simply proceeded without him, which was, Casper thought, the cruelest possible outcome for a man who had built his career on controlling access.

Brandt testified at three gatherings. At the fourth — Graywater, the biggest, nearly a hundred boats — he did something else. He stood before the assembled fleet in his dress uniform, the last time he would ever wear it, and read his own statement into the record: what the navy knew, when it knew it, and what it had ordered done about it. His voice never wavered. When he finished, he took off his cap, folded the resignation letter open, and laid both on the table beside the treaty copy.

"I served the institution," he said. "I'm testifying for the people. I've learned those aren't the same thing, and I'm sorry it took me so long."

The raised oars that answered him made a sound like rain on a roof.

* * *

On the last night, moored off Harrow Mouth with the reading room's lamplight falling yellow across the water, Maren sat beside Casper on the deck and didn't say anything for a long time.

"You keep waiting for me to tell you what to do with it," Maren finally said.

"I keep waiting for it to feel finished."

"It won't. Homelands don't finish. They just get lived in." Maren pulled her coat tighter. "I wanted territory my whole life. Borders. A flag at the source. And instead—" she gestured at the lamplit water, the anchored boats, the sound of someone practicing the old song badly and getting it slightly less bad each verse, "—instead I got this. Memory with a population."

"Is that enough?"

Maren considered it honestly, the way she considered everything. "It's more than I had yesterday. Ask me again tomorrow."

Casper laughed, and found the laugh came easy, and understood that something in her had changed its units of measurement. She had spent her life counting miles — paid miles, logged miles, miles between herself and other people. Tonight she wasn't counting anything. She was just here, on the water, among people who had chosen each other, holding a document that proved belonging could be written down after all.

Below them, the river ran on toward the sea, carrying everything it carried — silt, memory, the reflections of a hundred anchored lights — and giving it all, freely, to everyone who lived along it.

Tomorrow they would reach the delta mouth, where the towns had called a final gathering, the largest yet, to sign something new: not a restoration of the old nation, but an inheritance declaration, drafted by the water-people themselves, claiming the river as commons for all who worked it.

"You should sign it," Maren said quietly. "Your real name. In the ledger."

Casper looked at the lamplight on the water, at the town that had fed them and sheltered them and asked nothing back, and felt the last of the old loneliness go slack in her chest like a line cut clean.

"Yes," she said. "I think I will."
