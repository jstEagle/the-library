## 22. Landfall

The river had changed its voice by the time Casper brought the *Kestrel* down through the last bend of the delta. Upstream it had been all argument — water shouting against stone, against ice, against the hull of every boat foolish enough to argue back. Here, in the wide brown reaches where the current forgot itself and spread out like a hand opening, the river only murmured. It sounded like a town talking to itself after dark.

She came in slow, engine at an idle, one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the gunwale where the new wood met the old. The rebuilt sections of the barge were paler than the original planking, raw as scar tissue, but a season of sun had begun to even them out. She'd done the work herself, plank by plank, in a boatyard two hundred miles downstream, and she had learned something in those months of hammering that no dive log had ever taught her: that repair was not restoration. The *Kestrel* would never be the boat she'd been before Vey's men put her to the torch. She was something else now — a vessel with a history written into her seams, like everything else that had come down this river.

The delta town had a name she was still learning to say without irony: Landfall. It had been called that for a century, back when it was a shipping head and barges from the coast unloaded here before the rail took their cargo inland. Then the rail died, then the shipping, and the name became a joke the town told about itself — landfall, as if anything landed here anymore except silt and driftwood and the occasional drowned man the river gave back. Forty years ago the treaty had redrawn the boundaries so that Landfall sat on the wrong side of a line nobody could find on the ground, and the people who lived here had gone on living, fishing, dredging, and burying their dead in ground that three governments insisted belonged to someone else.

Now the line was gone. Not moved — erased. Voided, abrogated, struck through in ink that was older and truer than the forgery, by courts that had taken eleven months to do what a lamplit night in a vault beneath a glacier had made possible in a single reading.

Casper eased the *Kestrel* toward the public moorage on the north bank, past the fish cannery with its new scaffolding, past the chapel whose bell they'd finally been allowed to ring in the open. Maren stood at the bow, and Casper watched her take in the waterfront the way other people took in weather — reading it, measuring it, finding it changed and unchanged at once.

"There," Maren said, pointing with her chin at a strip of ground behind the chandlery. A wedge of dirt maybe forty feet long, hemmed in by a board fence, currently hosting nothing but dockside weeds and the skeleton of someone's abandoned skiff. "That will do."

"For what?"

"The garden." Maren turned and looked at her, and there was something in her face that Casper had learned over the long months to recognize as the old woman's version of trembling — not weakness, but pressure held behind glass. "I told you I would plant one when we had ground that was ours. You said you would believe it when you saw it."

"I said I'd believe it when the lawyers stopped arguing."

"The lawyers stopped arguing in March." Maren's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "It is June."

Casper killed the engine and threw the lines to the boy waiting on the dock — Tomas, thirteen now, son of the woman who ran the ledger office, a kid who had spent the whole winter asking Casper questions about diving until she'd finally agreed to teach him to hold his breath underwater, which she suspected was less instruction than initiation. He caught the lines with the easy competence of a child raised on water and made them fast without being told how.

"Welcome home," he said, and Casper felt the word go through her like a hook set deep.

Home. She had moored boats in thirty towns on this river system and never once heard the word aimed at her. She'd been *the salvager*, *the diver*, *Pemberton*, *that woman with the barge*. Useful names. Transactional names. Names that let her arrive and leave without leaving anything behind.

"Thanks," she said, because she still didn't know what to do with the other thing.

* * *

The ledger office occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, a brick building the color of dried blood that had spent four decades flying the wrong flag. Inside, it smelled of paper and lamp oil and the particular dust of records nobody had expected to matter again. The town ledger itself lay open on a counter worn smooth by generations of hands — arrivals, departures, births, deaths, claims, disputes. Before the treaty, every soul on this stretch of river had been entered there. After the treaty, the entries had thinned to nothing, because the people of Landfall had understood, in the wordless way communities understand things, that signing your name into a book meant acknowledging whose book it was. And the book, after the forgery, had not been theirs.

Maren had explained it once, on the portage above Cold Country, when they were eating cold rice and watching for the helicopter: *"A ledger is not just a list. It is a claim that you exist in a place, and that the place agrees. My mother's name is in that book. My grandmother's. When the treaty came, the clerk was ordered to close it. Do you understand? They didn't erase us. They just stopped writing us down. As if a nation could be unmade by running out of ink."*

Casper stood at the counter now while the clerk — a broad, patient woman named Odile Ferrant whom Casper had first met standing waist-deep in floodwater hauling copies of the true treaty from boat to boat — turned the ledger toward her and laid out a pen.

"You've been putting this off since March," Odile said.

"I've been busy."

"You've been hiding." Odile said it without malice, the way you'd note the weather. "You delivered the truth to every town on this river. You stood up in front of four hundred people at the convening and read the original treaty aloud in a voice that didn't shake. And you won't write your own name in a book."

Casper looked at the page. The most recent entries ran down in careful script: families re-registering claims that had lapsed or been seized; the chapel congregation entering itself formally for the first time in forty years; Tomas's mother recording his birth retroactively, because he'd been born in the years when the book was closed and the hospital had kept no record anyone trusted. Page after page of people choosing to exist on paper again.

Below them, a blank line waited.

Her whole life, Casper Pemberton had understood the arithmetic of invisibility. Her father had taught it to her before he taught her to swim: *a smuggler's daughter is safest when she is no one, when her name opens no doors and closes none, when the checkpoints wave her through because there is nothing about her worth remembering.* She had built a life on that foundation — dive logs instead of friendships, paid miles instead of roots, a boat that was a shell she wore instead of a place she lived. She had been good at it. She had been so good at it that when Aldous Vey offered her triple rate to forget a chest full of a dead nation's truth, the money hadn't even been the point. The point was that she'd believed, right down to the bedrock of herself, that she was the kind of person who could be bought into silence, because she was the kind of person silence had made.

The river had disagreed. The river, and an old woman with a seal on a tarp and seventy years of memory in her hands.

"What name?" Odile asked gently.

Casper picked up the pen. Her hand was steady. Diving had taught her that too — that the body stays calm even when the mind is screaming, if you've trained it, if you've been down deep enough times to know that panic is just air wasted.

"Casper Yarrow Pemberton," she said.

Odile's eyebrows rose a fraction. Everyone on the river knew the name Yarrow by now — knew that Casper's father had been Eli Yarrow, the smuggler who died running this same current with a hold full of medicine for towns the treaty had cut off from supply, drowned in the rapids at Kell's Folly trying to keep people fed through a winter the government had decided they could survive without help. Knew that his daughter had grown up stateless in practice if not quite in law, running salvage under a name scrubbed clean of his, because his name was a warrant waiting to happen.

"You're claiming him," Odile said.

"He claimed me first." Casper wrote it out, letter by letter, in a hand that was not beautiful but was entirely her own. "He just never got to sign anything."

When she finished, Odile blotted the line, read it back aloud for the record — the old formality, restored along with everything else — and then, breaking formality entirely, reached across the counter and gripped Casper's wrist.

"Welcome to Landfall, Casper Yarrow Pemberton."

Outside, the light off the river came through the door, and Casper walked out of the customs house into it feeling lighter and heavier at once, the way you feel surfacing from a deep dive — the weight gone, the pressure gone, and the strange nakedness of a body learning to live at a new altitude.

* * *

Maren had already started on the garden.

Casper found her behind the chandlery on her knees in the dirt, having somehow, in the hour since they'd moored, produced a spade, a rake, and a wooden crate of seed packets from somewhere — Casper had stopped asking where Maren produced things sometime around the third week of the portage. The old woman worked with the unhurried precision of someone performing a rite she remembered in her hands if not her head.

"What grows in a Vessan garden?" Casper asked, sitting down on the fence rail.

"Everything that grew in Vessany." Maren didn't look up. "Which is to say, everything that grows anywhere, because Vessany was never a special country. That is what they never understood, the men who forged the treaty. They thought a nation was borders and signatures. But a nation is what people plant." She held up a packet. "Beans. My mother grew these on our terrace in Ostrev, in boxes, because we had no ground either. Exiles learn to garden small." Another packet. "Dill. For the fish. There is a soup my grandmother made with dill and river carp that I have not tasted in fifty years, and this summer I am going to make it, and you are going to eat it, and you are going to tell me it is good even if it is terrible."

"It'll be terrible."

"Then you will lie, as family does."

The word landed between them and neither of them flinched from it. Family. It had taken the whole river to get them here — the rapids, the tollhouse, the narrows where the side channel nearly took Maren and Casper had gone in after her without deciding to, the way you go in after your own blood. It had taken the vault, and the lamplight, and Brandt's handcuffs closing on Vey's wrists in the dark while the glacier groaned overhead like a ship changing course. It had taken eleven months of courts and convenings and copies carried downstream in every hull that would carry them. But somewhere in all of it, without ceremony, the thing had become what it was: two women who had each survived by being alone, learning to be two.

"How much ground do beans need?" Casper asked.

"Less than a nation. More than patience." Maren pressed a seed into the soil with one blunt finger. "Help me with the far row. Your hands are younger."

Casper climbed down off the fence, pulled off her boots, and knelt in the dirt of a town she'd signed herself into, planting beans in ground that had been stolen and given back and was now, finally, simply ground — belonging to whoever tended it, which was the oldest law there was, older than any treaty, forged or true.

* * *

Evening came down the river the way it always did in the delta, gold going to copper going to violet, the water holding the last of the light longer than the sky. Casper sat on the deck of the *Kestrel* with her feet on the rail and watched the town light its lamps. The chapel bell rang the hour — freely, officially, the sound carrying across the water to the moored boats and the lit windows and the strip of freshly turned earth behind the chandlery where Maren had covered her seeds and said she'd check them tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, because gardens were promises made in installments.

Tomas came by at dusk with a basket of bread his mother had sent, and stayed to sit on the dock with his legs hanging over the water, and asked Casper when the next diving lesson was, and she said Sunday, and he grinned like she'd handed him a chest of gold. Down the dock, a couple of the cannery workers argued amiably about the new cold storage. Somewhere a radio played a song with a melody Maren had hummed once, crossing the moraine, and Casper recognized it now — a Vessan river song, the kind that used to be sung quietly, behind closed shutters, and was sung loudly now, from boats, at festivals the towns had begun holding on the water itself, rafts lashed together, whole communities afloat on their own decision to stay.

Her logbook lay open on her knee. Old habit. She'd kept logs since she was nine years old — miles run, dives made, days paid. The last entry in the old book, the one that had survived the fire, ended mid-sentence the night the barge burned, and she'd never finished it. Some sentences you don't finish; you start new ones.

She uncapped her pen and wrote today's date, and under it:

*Moored at Landfall. No miles logged. None owed.*

She looked at the words for a long time. Her father had measured life in miles — miles ahead, miles owed, miles between him and the checkpoint, the payment, the safe water. She had inherited the arithmetic without ever being taught it, had counted her way through thirty years of rivers and never once arrived anywhere, because arrival wasn't a number. It was a ledger line with your real name on it. It was a garden planted in installments. It was a boy on the dock calling Sunday back over his shoulder like a promise he intended to collect.

Maren came down the dock as the last light went, moving slowly, her hands still dirty, and stood at the base of the gangplank looking up at the boat.

"You are not counting," she observed.

"No."

"This is new."

"It's the whole point," Casper said. "Turns out the map was never to the archive. The map was here. Took me twenty-two chapters of river to read it."

Maren snorted — she had never acquired a taste for Casper's jokes but tolerated them the way one tolerates weather — and came aboard, and they sat together in the darkening while the lamps of Landfall multiplied on the black water, each one a household, each household a name in the book, the book open now and staying open.

Far upstream, a hundred miles and another lifetime away, the glacier calved on its own schedule, indifferent and magnificent, sending a long low thunder down the valley that reached the delta hours later as barely a tremor in the mooring lines. Casper felt it through the deck, through the soles of her feet, through the hull that had burned and been rebuilt and was hers.

"That's the ice," Maren said softly. "Saying goodnight."

"Or keeping watch."

"Same thing, in Vessan." Maren settled back against the rail. "There is a word for it. *Vessk.* It means the mountain that remembers you."

Casper listened to the river murmur against the hull, the town murmuring beyond it, the mountain remembering them both from a hundred miles off, and found that for the first time in her life there was nowhere she needed to be by morning, no current she owed, no name she was hiding from.

She was home. She had signed for it.

And the river, which decides everything in the end, flowed on.
