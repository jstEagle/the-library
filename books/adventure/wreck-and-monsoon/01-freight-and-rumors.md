## 1. Freight and Rumors

The morning fog sat on the Chilkat River like a lid on a pot, and Isandro Yarrow flew through it the way he did everything else — half by instrument, half by stubbornness, and all of it on credit.

The Cessna 180 bucked as the valley walls closed in around him, gray granite sliding past his wingtips close enough to count the lichen. He'd been flying this run for eleven years and he still didn't love it in weather like this. He respected it. There was a difference, and the difference was that respect kept you alive long enough to hate it properly.

"Yarrow Two-Niner-Seven," he said into the radio, out of habit more than hope. "Crossing the narrows at nine hundred."

Static answered him, the way it always did out here. The interior villages had radios the way old men had teeth — some of them worked, most of them hurt to use, and nobody could afford replacements. He keyed off and let the silence come back, that particular cockpit silence with the engine's heartbeat underneath it, the only conversation he'd had in three days that wasn't with himself.

In the seat behind him, wrapped in tarps and bungee cords: forty pounds of amoxicillin bound for Klawock, a crate of insulin that had already burned through two of its ice packs, a mail sack fat with catalog orders and government letters, and — because gossip was freight too, out here — three newspapers two weeks stale and a letter from a woman in Haines to her sister in Angoon that Isandro had promised not to read and hadn't.

That was the business. That was Yarrow Air Service, one airplane, one pilot, one airstrip mortgaged down to the gravel. He flew medicine and mail and rumor into the interior, and he flew fur pelts and fish and fury back out, and at the end of every month the numbers met somewhere near zero and shook hands like old enemies.

His father had owned two airplanes once. His father had owned a reputation too, before the *Meridian* took both away.

Isandro pushed the thought down where he kept it, below the fuel gauges and the weather, in the place where a man stores the things that fly up at him when he's tired. The fog thinned. The river unspooled below, braided and brown, and the mountains stood up out of the murk one shoulder at a time, white-capped, indifferent, older than any name anyone had ever given them.

He loved them anyway. That was the joke of it. They'd ruined his family and he loved them anyway.

* * *

Klawock was less a village than an argument between the forest and a handful of buildings the forest was winning. Eleven houses, a cannery bunkhouse gone silver with rot, a dock that walked itself into the water plank by plank each winter, and a church whose steeple leaned like a man listening hard for something he didn't want to hear.

He set the 180 down on the grass strip with the tires kissing the ground so soft the geese didn't even bother to move. Old-timers said you could tell a pilot's soul by his landings. If that was true, Isandro's soul was broke but polite.

Elias Teng was waiting for him at the strip's edge, standing beside a handcart with the patience of a man who'd been waiting for things his whole life — for salmon, for the Indian agent, for death — and had learned that everything came eventually or didn't, and either way you kept your coat on.

"You're late," Elias said.

"I'm early. You moved the calendar."

"The calendar moves. The insulin doesn't." But there was no heat in it. Elias Teng was seventy-nine years old and dying in the slow, orderly way that men die when they've made their peace with the paperwork, and the only thing he'd ever been impatient about was other people's comfort. He helped unload the crates with hands that shook like leaves in a wind only he could feel, and Isandro pretended not to notice, because that was the courtesy they owed each other.

"How's the breathing?" Isandro asked.

"Loud. Like the whole village is snoring inside me." Elias grinned, gap-toothed, unbothered. "You eat anything today?"

"Coffee."

"That's not food, that's a rumor about food." Elias pressed a foil-wrapped bundle of smoked salmon into his hands with the force of a man settling a debt. "There. Now you've eaten. Don't argue with your elders, it makes my chest worse."

Inside the bunkhouse, while Elias counted out pills under a lamp and read the labels with a jeweler's squint, the news of the interior arrived the way it always arrived: sideways, in pieces, wearing different faces in different mouths.

The price of copper was up, which meant the mine at Tulsequah might reopen, which meant three families might stay another year instead of moving to Juneau where their kids would forget how to gut a fish. The ferry was running late again. Somebody's daughter had taken first prize at the Southeast Alaska State Fair for a quilt that everyone agreed was ugly but ambitious. And down in Petersburg, the salvage fleet was fitting out early this season — bigger than usual, three vessels instead of one, working the coast north.

At the word *salvage*, something turned over in Isandro's chest, quiet and cold, like a stone shifting at the bottom of a stream.

"Which fleet?" he asked, too casually, sorting mail that didn't need sorting.

Elias looked up over his glasses. He had eyes the color of creek water and the same quality of seeing straight to the bottom. "The *Cormorant* outfit. Vess. You know him?"

"No."

"You knew his name fast enough."

"Everybody knows Vess. Thirty years dragging the Inside Passage for other people's shipwrecks." Isandro taped a box shut with more attention than it deserved. "Salvage men are like gulls. Wherever there's a wreck, they show up, and they're loud about it."

"Mm." Elias went back to the pills. Then, as if the conversation were a fish he'd decided to keep on the line after all: "Your father ran salvage, didn't he. Before."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a raven said something rude and profound.

"He ran a lot of things," Isandro said. "Before."

Elias didn't push. That was the thing about the old ones out here; they knew that some cargo you don't ask a man to open in front of company. He finished counting, capped the bottles, and said only: "Fleets that size don't fit out for nothing. Somebody's heard something. Coast's full of rumors this spring."

"Rumors are free," Isandro said. "It's the truth that costs."

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing the arithmetic that was slowly killing him, sitting at the bunkhouse table with a ledger soft-cornered from handling. Fuel for the round trip: $212. Hangar fee, insurance, the annual inspection due in six weeks that would cost more than the plane had earned since March. The mortgage payment on the airstrip, which the bank in Juneau had begun to describe in letters as "our continued flexibility" — a phrase Isandro had learned to read the way sailors read weather, knowing that calm language often preceded the worst blows.

Eleven years ago he'd inherited the business and the debt together, twins born the same bad winter. Everyone had told him to sell. Sell the strip, sell the plane, take a wage flying for someone else, be a man with a paycheck instead of a man with a dream and a lien against it. But the strip had been his father's before him, cut out of the alder brush by hand, and selling it would have felt like the second time the world took something of Eli Yarrow's and called it justice.

Because that was the story everyone knew. The *Meridian*, 1928, outbound heavy with salvaged cargo, storm in the strait, and the second mate — Eli Yarrow, thirty-one years old, a man who'd never cursed in front of ladies or missed a tide in his life — had put the ship's boats over the side and pulled away from a sinking vessel with her owners' fortune still aboard. Three men lost. One man blamed. The inquiry had called it mutiny, or something close enough to ruin him, and the owners had called it nothing at all, because owners never have to call anything anything.

Eli Yarrow had spent the rest of his life flying freight into places roads couldn't reach, as if altitude could wash a man clean, and he'd died with the word *mutineer* still nailed to his name like a claim marker. Isandro had grown up in the shadow of that stake, in schoolyards and hiring halls and the quiet arithmetic of neighbors deciding whether to trust him. He'd learned to answer questions about it with a flat face and a short voice, and beneath the flat face he'd built a room where the question lived, and he visited that room more often than he admitted, even to himself.

Had his father done it? Had Eli Yarrow really cut boats loose and left men drowning? Isandro had been eight years old. All he had left was a memory of his father's hands — rope-scarred, careful, the hands of a man who tied knots that held — and the certainty, bone-deep and useless, that those hands had never abandoned anybody.

But certainty wasn't evidence. Certainty didn't clear a name. And the sea keeps its paperwork better than any archive.

He closed the ledger. Outside, the light had gone long and gold, and Elias was sitting on his porch watching the water with the expression of a man keeping an appointment.

* * *

That evening, Elias sent for him.

Not sent for — summoned, in the village way, which meant a ten-year-old appeared at the bunkhouse door and said, "Grandpa says come, and bring nothing," which was either a great honor or a warning, and with Elias it was usually both.

The old man's house smelled of woodsmoke and spruce pitch and forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the walls. He sat in a chair by the window with a blanket over his knees, and on the table beside him lay a tin box, dented, older than Isandro, with a rusted hasp.

"Sit," Elias said. "You want tea? Don't answer, I only have tea."

Isandro sat. The window framed the channel, iron-gray now, the far shore dissolving into dusk. Somewhere out there, past the islands, past the glaciers, the *Cormorant*'s fleet was fitting out, and the coast was full of rumors, and Isandro felt the day tilting toward something he couldn't name yet, the way you feel a change in pressure before the barometer admits it.

"I'm dying," Elias said, conversational, the way he'd say the ferry was late. "Doctor in Juneau says spring, maybe summer if I cheat. I intend to cheat."

"Elias—"

"Hold up. Let me get through this part, I've rehearsed it." He drew a breath that whistled going in, like wind under a door. "In 1931, I was seventeen. Worked the beach crews, saltery at Point Agassiz. That fall, a Tlingit hunter named George Kadashan came down from the glacier country with a story. Said he'd been up the fjord after goats and seen a cave in the ice where no cave should be. Big enough to walk a boat into. Said there was iron in there, and timber, and a smell of old rope. Said the glacier was giving something back."

Isandro kept his face still. "Glaciers do strange things."

"They do. And then they undo them." Elias's eyes glittered. "George went back the next summer with his brother-in-law to look proper. The cave was gone. Ice had advanced fifty yards in a year, sealed it like a mouth closing. He told the story his whole life and everybody smiled at him the way people smile at stories. But George didn't drink, Isandro. Never touched it. And he drew what he saw."

The old man's hands, steady now with the terrible steadiness of the nearly dead, lifted the lid of the tin box.

Inside, on top of a folded oilcloth pouch that clinked with a sound Isandro knew in his bones — gold dust, beach-mined, heavy as sin — lay a sheet of paper gone amber with age. A hand-drawn chart. Ink lines fine as spider silk, fjord and shoreline rendered with a surveyor's care, depths penciled along the channel, and there, up-ice of the tidewater line, a small shape inked and re-inked until the paper wore thin: a cave mouth. Beneath it, in faded pencil, in a hand that pressed hard:

*She went in whole. The mountain swallowed her. Ask the ice.*

Isandro didn't touch it. He was afraid that if he touched it, it would turn out to be a dream, and he'd been having fewer dreams lately and couldn't spare one.

"What is this?" His voice came out lower than he intended.

"A chart. George gave it to me the winter before he died, along with that gold, and told me: give this to a flyer someday. Not a boat man — a flyer. Because a boat can't get up the fjord anymore, the ice floes choke it in summer. But a man with wings can see what the mountain's hiding." Elias leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the wet in his eyes without shame. "I've had this paper for sixty years, boy. I watched you grow up hauling medicine through weather that grounds eagles. I watched you land on river bars and glacier flats and that mudflat at Angoon that isn't a landing strip, it's a rumor with mud on it. I know what you are."

"I'm broke," Isandro said.

"You're a pilot. Same thing." Elias pushed the tin across the table. "Take it. The gold pays your fuel. The chart pays something else."

Isandro finally looked at the drawing again, really looked, and his heart began doing something slow and enormous in his chest. Because he knew that fjord. Every pilot on the coast knew that fjord — the big tidewater glacier, the one the charts showed as solid ice from the moraine to the headwall, unbroken, surveyed a dozen times over a century, featureless white on every map ever printed. There was no cave on that glacier. There had been no cave on that glacier in living memory.

And yet the retreating ice — everyone talked about it now, how fast she was pulling back, how the old-timers said she'd never been this far up the valley in their fathers' time —

Ice that retreats gives back what it took.

"Sixty years," Isandro said quietly. "Why me? Why now?"

Elias smiled, and for a moment the dying fell away from him and he was just an old man looking at a young one with something like tenderness. "Because I asked George that same question once, about why he told me. He said: the ice doesn't show herself to everyone. She shows herself to whoever's supposed to find what she's keeping." He tapped the chart once, with one crooked finger. "And because whatever's in that cave has waited ninety years, and I don't think it'll wait much longer. Glaciers that pull back that fast don't linger. She'll seal again, or she'll calve the whole face into the sea, and then it's gone till the next ice age."

Outside, the last light drained off the channel. A raven crossed the window, black against blacker, heading inland toward the ice.

Isandro picked up the chart. The paper was warm from the tin, from sixty years of a dead man's keeping and a dying man's patience, and it trembled very slightly in his hands, though he told himself that was just the lamp draft.

"If there's a wreck in there," he said, "a real one — salvage law says—"

"I know what salvage law says. Finders, keepers, lawyers, liars." Elias waved it off. "I'm not giving you a treasure, boy. I'm giving you a question. What you do with the answer is between you and the ice."

Isandro folded the chart along its old creases and put it inside his jacket, against his chest, where it lay like a coal.

He stayed another hour, drinking Elias's terrible tea and listening to the old man talk about salmon and weather and the proper way to cheat at solitaire, and when he rose to go, Elias caught his wrist with surprising strength.

"One more thing." The whistling breath. "Whatever you find up there — and you'll find something, I've known you since you were knee-high to a propeller and you've got your father's look, the one where a man decides something and then the world has to live with it — remember that wrecks keep more than cargo. They keep accounts." His grip tightened. "Somebody's been waiting ninety years for somebody to settle one."

Isandro walked back to the bunkhouse under a sky just beginning to show its stars, and the gold dust clinked in the tin under his arm like a promise, and the chart rode against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

He didn't sleep. He spread the chart on the table under the lamp and studied it until midnight, then dug out his own charts — the official ones, the surveyed ones, the ones that showed nothing but unbroken white — and laid them side by side, and the discrepancy sat between them like a door standing open in a wall where no door should be.

Solid ice for a century, the surveys said.

*Ask the ice,* the dead man wrote.

Isandro looked at the fuel gauge of his life — the mortgage, the inspection, the bank's patient letters — and then at the fjord drawn in a dead hunter's careful ink, and he did the only arithmetic that had ever made sense to him.

Lowest spring tides of the season came in nine days. He'd need every hour between now and then to make the plane ready, and every ounce of courage he had left after that.

Because the mountain had swallowed something whole, and the ice was letting go, and Isandro Yarrow — broke, alone, and carrying his father's name like ballast — was going to be there when she opened her mouth.
