## 4. Six Tides

The dark in the cave was not like other darks. Isandro had flown night freight over the interior for eleven years; he knew the dark of cloud decks and the dark of spruce country and the dark that lived under a wing when the alternator died. This was older than all of them. It had weight. It pressed against his eyes like a hand testing fruit.

He did not move for a long time after the ice bridge came down.

He heard it settle — a long, granular sigh, the sound of a mountain exhaling through its teeth — and then nothing but the drip of meltwater somewhere deep in the ship's bones, ticking like a clock in an empty house. The route he'd come in by was gone. He'd seen the last of it: a curtain of blue ice shearing off the wall above the entrance, dropping across the gap with a crack he felt in his sternum rather than heard, and then the pale rectangle of daylight narrowing, folding, closing like an eye.

He was inside the *Meridian*.

He was inside his father's ghost.

"Okay," he said aloud, because the silence demanded payment. "Okay."

His headlamp found the deck again. The salvage ship lay on her side at maybe fifteen degrees, frozen mid-death, her rail buried in glacier ice that had flowed around her hull like wax around a candle. Cargo crates stood in their lashings as if the stevedores had just stepped away for lunch. Frost furred everything — the winch housing, the companionway ladder, the brass letters on the stern house that read MERIDIAN, Juneau registry, 1923. And there, where he'd first seen it before the world fell in: the letter. Still wedged in the split seam of a chart locker, still sealed in its oilskin wrapper, the ink showing through the paper in mirror-writing.

*E. Yarrow.* His father's name. Elias Yarrow, boatswain, mutineer.

Isandro reached for it and stopped himself, hand hovering. Superstition, maybe. Or the sense that once he took it, this became real — not a dream, not a hallucination born of bad fuel and worse debts, but a thing with consequences. He took it anyway. Of course he took it. The oilskin was stiff as bark, cold enough to burn. He stowed it inside his jacket, against his chest, where his heartbeat could keep it company.

Then he made himself do the arithmetic, because arithmetic was what kept pilots alive when wonder wanted to kill them.

He checked his watch against the tide table he kept folded in his breast pocket — habit from years of beach landings — and worked backward from what he'd seen on the approach. Spring tides. The fjord breathed twice a day, and each breath the glacier exhaled a little more ice into the channel. He'd watched the calving face on the way in: seracs leaning like drunks, a berg the size of a barn already grounded on the shoal. Calving season ran hard through late summer. When the first big autumn storms pushed the ice pack down-fjord, the entrance would choke solid, and no plane, no boat, no prayer would get through until spring thaw — if the cave even survived the winter's grinding.

He counted on his gloved fingers, muttering the numbers like a rosary.

Six tides. Six high waters before the window shut. Maybe five, if the weather turned mean. He had food for two days if he stretched it. Water, all he wanted, dripping off the ceiling of the world. And a radio that had taken a knock when the bridge came down.

That was the part that made his stomach drop past the ice, past the wreck, past the bedrock itself. He'd been standing near the entrance when it collapsed — close enough that the blast of displaced air had thrown him flat against a crate, close enough that a shard of ice had opened his scalp above the left ear. The headset was still around his neck. Whether the radio survived was another question entirely.

He dug the unit out of his pack. A VHF handheld, the good kind, the kind he'd skipped two mortgage payments to buy. The casing was cracked along one corner. The antenna bent at an angle antennas were not meant to hold.

He keyed the transmit switch.

Nothing. Not even the click.

"No," he told it. "No, you don't."

He worked by feel and lamplight, prying the casing apart with his Leatherman, breathing on his fingers to keep them working. The battery pack had shifted; one contact was fouled with a sliver of ice-melt and grit. He cleaned it with the edge of his shirt cuff, reseated the pack, taped the casing shut with a strip from the roll he carried for patching cowls. Held his breath. Keyed it.

A hiss. Faint, watered-down, but alive.

"Mayday, mayday, mayday." His voice sounded strange in the cave, too loud, swallowed instantly by all that ice. "This is Isandro Yarrow, Cessna one-eight-zero, call sign Yarrow Air, trapped in a glacial cave, north arm of the Sustina fjord. I say again—"

He gave it all: position by dead reckoning, the bearing lines he'd shot off the peaks on approach, the color of the cave mouth, the shape of the shoal. He said *Yarrow* twice, deliberately, though saying it out loud in this place felt like knocking on a door he'd spent thirty years avoiding.

Static answered him. Long, grainy static, the sound of the whole indifferent North rubbing its hands together.

He tried again. And again. He learned the rhythm of the cave while he worked — the drip-clock, the occasional groan of the glacier flexing overhead, a sound like a whale thinking. He cleared a space among the crates and built himself a camp without meaning to: pack opened, spare socks aired, the emergency blanket unfolded to reflect his lamp light back at him so the dark wouldn't feel quite so total. He ate half a candy bar and saved the rest and hated himself for how carefully he chewed.

On the fourth try — or the fourteenth; he'd lost count — the static changed.

It didn't clear. It *organized*. Somewhere inside the wash of noise, a voice assembled itself syllable by syllable, arriving across miles of granite and ice like a man speaking through a wall.

"—reading you, Yarrow Air. Say again your position. I say again, say your position."

Isandro's heart slammed. He keyed up. "North arm, Sustina fjord! Trapped in a cave in the glacier face — east side, above the shoal, you'll see fresh rockfall at the waterline. I've got six tides before this whole fjord ices shut. Do you copy?"

Silence. Then, distorted, almost amused:

"I copy." A pause full of static, and then something that raised every hair on Isandro's neck. "I know exactly where you are, son. I've been looking at that spot on my charts for thirty years."

* * *

The voice belonged to a man named Vess.

That much came through in the first ten minutes, along with the essentials: Captain Aldous Vess, motor vessel *Cormorant*, salvage fleet, currently forty nautical miles down-fjord and making best speed. The rest came in fragments, sentences chewed up by distance and stone. Isandro sat on an 1890s crate of whatever the *Meridian* had been carrying and held the radio to his mouth like a lifeline, which is exactly what it was, and tried to build a picture of the man out of voice alone.

The voice was old. Gravel over gravel. But underneath the age there was something else — a coiled attention, a hunger held on a very short leash. Isandro had spent his life around men who wanted things: miners wanting strike weather, fishermen wanting one more set, creditors wanting their pound of interest. He knew the sound of want being kept polite.

"You're telling me," Vess said, for the third time, "that you can see the ship's name."

"Brass letters on the stern house. M-E-R-I-D-I-A-N. She's lying on her beam ends, frozen into the ice maybe two hundred yards inside the cave mouth. Cargo's intact. Crates still lashed down." Isandro hesitated, then decided the truth was currency here. "There's quinine markings on some of the cases. Medicine grade, if the labels are honest."

The silence that followed had texture. Isandro could hear the man breathing, slow and deliberate, the way you breathe when you're trying not to let the other person hear what you're feeling.

"Thirty-one years," Vess said finally. "I've chased that hull for thirty-one years. Every rumor from Cordova to Skagway. Every drunk fisherman's story. Every chart anomaly." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "And a bush pilot stumbles into her because the ice moved."

"The ice is moving again," Isandro said. "Captain, listen to me. The entrance I came through doesn't exist anymore. The bridge collapsed behind me. I'm counting six tides before calving season seals the fjord — less if the wind comes up from the southeast. If you're coming, you need to be coming now."

More static. Then, briskly, all business: "Eighteen hours to the north arm at best speed. We'll make it inside the fourth tide, maybe the fifth if we push. What's your situation? Injuries?"

"A cut head. Nothing else."

"Food?"

"Two days if I'm careful."

Another pause, longer this time. When Vess spoke again, his voice had shifted into something lower, more careful, the way a man's voice changes when he's about to name a price.

"Here's how this goes, Yarrow. My fleet has the equipment to open that cave properly — saws, charges, a dive team, a crane barge. We can have you out of there inside two days. In exchange—" the pause stretched, "—you found her. Salvage law being what it is, finder's rights attach. I'm offering you a partnership. Ten percent of recovered value, plus full rescue costs covered. That's fair. That's generous."

Ten percent. Isandro did the math without wanting to. A hold full of medicine-grade quinine, in this century or the last, plus whatever silver and specie a 1920s salvage ship might carry — it was a number with commas in it. Enough to save the airstrip. Enough to pay off the hangar loan, the fuel contract, the two seasons of bad winters. Enough to stop hearing his landlord's voice in his sleep.

"And if I say no?" he asked.

"Then you can stay where you are and keep counting tides," Vess said, without heat, without malice, which somehow made it worse. "But you won't say no. Nobody says no with six tides left and a dead radio battery. You called Mayday, son. You already asked for help. Help has a price. It always did."

Isandro looked at the letter inside his jacket. He thought of his father's face in the one photograph that had survived the family's scattering — Elias Yarrow squinting into a Ketchikan sun, hands like tree roots, wearing the expression of a man who knows what people say about him when he leaves the room. *Mutineer.* The word had followed Isandro through school, through flight training, through every small-town bar between Juneau and Whitehorse. *Son of the man who stole the boats and left three men to drown.*

Except now he was sitting inside the ship those boats had come from, and the letter against his ribs weighed more than any cargo manifest.

"Ten percent," Isandro said. "Plus you document the wreck properly. No dynamite near the hull. No stripping crates before they're recorded. I've got—" he caught himself, "—there are historical materials aboard. Papers. They matter."

Something flickered across the static — surprise, maybe, or suspicion.

"Papers," Vess repeated.

"Ship's papers. Personal effects. Whatever the ice kept dry."

"Historians," Vess said, and the word dripped contempt like condensation off a cold pipe. "Fine. Your ten percent buys you a say. We'll log the pretty stuff before we lift the valuable stuff. Now listen close, because I'm only going to say this once. Stay away from the calving face. Don't touch the ice bridge remnants — the whole roof is under tension and it will kill you without meaning to. Keep your lamp low, conserve your batteries, and mark your position with something bright so my boarding party finds you fast. Can you do all that?"

"Yes."

"Then sit tight, Yarrow. Cormorant is coming." A last rasp of static, and then, quieter, almost to himself, almost lost: "Thirty-one years. By God. Thirty-one years."

The channel clicked off.

* * *

Isandro sat in the dark afterward for a long time, listening to the glacier breathe.

He should have felt relieved. He'd done the right things — secured rescue, negotiated terms, given a position. In eighteen hours men would come with saws and ropes and hot food, and he'd walk out of this cave with ten percent of a fortune and his father's letter hidden against his chest, and everything after that would be paperwork and lawyers and, God willing, a clean name carved back onto the family record.

Instead he felt the way he'd felt at nine years old, standing in a courtroom hallway while adults decided his father's fate in words too big for him. The sense of doors opening onto rooms he couldn't see into. The sense that the price of rescue was never printed anywhere, only discovered later, itemized.

*Help has a price. It always did.*

He got up and made himself useful, because uselessness was the fastest road to despair in a place like this. He rigged the emergency blanket as a signal panel near where the entrance used to be, weighted with chunks of ice. He inventoried his supplies honestly and wrote the numbers on his palm in marker: nine candy bars' worth of calories, one flare, one first-aid kit, forty hours of lamp battery if he rationed. He drank from a meltwater pool and the water tasted of stone and centuries.

Then, because there was nothing left to arrange, he took out the letter.

He didn't open it. Not yet. That was a different chapter, and it needed a different kind of courage, and besides — his fingers were too numb to trust with seventy-year-old paper. He simply held it, oilskin crackling faintly, and let himself imagine what it might say. An apology. An explanation. Names. He'd spent his whole life assembling his father from other people's accusations; the idea that Elias might speak for himself, in his own hand, from inside this frozen coffin of a ship —

The glacier groaned overhead, long and tectonic, and a fine sifting of frost drifted down through his lamplight like snow falling indoors.

Somewhere beyond all that ice, a man named Vess was coming with thirty-one years of hunger in his pockets. And Isandro understood, with the cold certainty of a pilot reading weather he doesn't like, that he had just invited the wolf into the fold — and that the only question left was whether the wolf wanted the sheep, or the shepherd, or something else entirely.

He keyed the radio one more time, just to hear a human voice.

"Cormorant, this is Yarrow Air. Radio check."

Static. Then, faintly, from far down the fjord, gravel over gravel:

"Loud and clear, son. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we make history — or money. Whichever pays better."

The channel went dead. Isandro laughed — one short, involuntary bark that echoed off the hull of his father's ship and came back sounding like someone else entirely.

Outside, invisible behind a hundred feet of ice, the sixth tide began to turn.
