## 5. The Archaeologist

Sefina Okafor had learned to read men by the way they held their hands.

Vess held his like a man gripping a wheel in weather that hadn't arrived yet — knuckles pale, thumbs hooked, always braced against something. His divers held theirs loose and greedy, fingers already curling around things that weren't theirs. And the pilot — the one they'd pulled off the ice shelf half-frozen, three days after his Mayday had crackled across a frequency no one was supposed to be listening to — the pilot held his hands very still, folded in his lap aboard the Cormorant's mess deck, as though stillness were the only thing he owned.

She watched him from the galley doorway with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking.

He was younger than she'd expected. Thirty, maybe thirty-two, with the wind-scoured face of someone who'd spent a decade arguing with weather and losing just often enough to stay humble. Frost had chewed the tips of two fingers on his right hand; the ship's medic had wrapped them in gauze that already needed changing. He sat where Vess had put him — aft corner, back to the bulkhead, eyes on the door — and he hadn't touched the food they'd given him except to move it around the plate like a man performing appetite for an audience.

*He's counting exits,* Sefina thought. *Good. So am I.*

"You're staring," said Wren beside her, not looking up from the disassembled water pump spread across the galley table like an autopsy. The girl was seventeen at most, all elbows and engine grease, her hair hacked short with what looked like scissors used on rope. She'd appeared out of the engine room three days into Sefina's time aboard, and when Sefina had asked Captain Vess why there was a child on his salvage vessel, Vess had said, *She's crew,* in a tone that ended conversations. Wren had a way of existing without being acknowledged that Sefina recognized from her own years in the field — the survival skill of people who'd learned early that visibility was a cost, not a gift.

"I'm observing," Sefina said. "There's a difference."

"Sure." Wren fitted a gasket into place with her thumbnail. "You've been observing him for forty minutes. That's staring with a degree."

"I have a doctorate."

"Two, I heard. And you still can't fix a pump."

Sefina almost smiled. It was the closest thing to friendship the Cormorant had offered her in six weeks, this sharp-mouthed mechanic who asked nothing about the scandal that had followed her off the *Alecto* and out of every journal and university post in the hemisphere. *Dr. Sefina Okafor, who refused to authenticate a forgery and paid for her integrity with her career.* The papers had made it sound noble for exactly one news cycle before the funding dried up and the invitations stopped. Vess had hired her because blacklisted archaeologists came cheap and asked no questions about provenance — and because, she suspected, a name on the manifest, even a disgraced one, made a salvage operation look legitimate to insurers.

She crossed to the mess deck and set her coffee down across from the pilot.

"Isandro Yarrow," she said. "The radio operator finally gave us your name. You're the one who's been flying medicine into Kaltag and Dot Lake for the last four years. Half the villages upriver know your tail number better than their own senator's."

"Senator's don't land on river bars." His voice was rougher than his face suggested, scraped raw by cold and smoke. He didn't reach for the coffee. "You're the archaeologist."

"Sefina Okafor."

"I know." Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, yes, but also a careful, deliberate blankness, the look of a man deciding what not to say. "You wrote the monograph on the *Bering Star*. The one that got the whole wreck site protected instead of salvaged."

"And cost me everything I had," Sefina said evenly. "You've done your reading."

"I do my homework." He glanced toward the porthole, where the fjord's grey water slid past, streaked with the milk-blue melt of the glacier at its head. Three days of sailing south from where they'd found him, and still the ice came down the channel in drifting islands, grinding softly against the hull at night like something enormous turning over in its sleep. "Why is a fleet like Vess's carrying a preservationist?"

"Because Vess likes the appearance of legitimacy, and I like eating." She let that sit. "And because whatever's in that cave, he wants it documented well enough to sell. Documentation is my price for passage. Everything gets recorded. Every crate, every context, every layer of silt. If I'm going to help strip a site, I'm going to do it properly."

Yarrow studied her for a long moment, and she felt the examination go both ways — two people weighing each other's silences, each aware the other was keeping something back.

"That's not how Vess described you," Yarrow said at last. "He said you were the best in the business and left it at that."

"Vess doesn't describe people. He deploys them." She leaned forward slightly. "You've been aboard twelve hours. You haven't eaten. You haven't slept more than twenty minutes at a stretch — I checked, because I sleep badly and the bunk next to yours creaks every time you turn over. And when the divers mentioned the word *manifest*, you went white under the frostburn." She kept her voice low, conversational, pitched beneath the clatter of the pump parts. "I'm not asking what you're hiding, Mr. Yarrow. I'm telling you I know you're hiding something, so we can skip the part where you pretend otherwise and waste both our time."

For a moment she thought he'd deny it. She'd seen a hundred men take that road — the reflexive lie, the smooth deflection, the wounded innocence performed for a woman they assumed couldn't tell performance from fact. But Yarrow only looked down at his bandaged hands, and when he spoke, his voice had dropped to nearly nothing.

"How much did Vess tell you about the Mayday?"

"Coordinates in a fjord that isn't on any chart. A cave exposed by the retreating glacier. He wouldn't say more over open radio." She paused. "He's been hunting something in these waters for thirty years. The crew says he keeps a photograph above his bunk of a ship going down. He won't say which ship."

Something flickered across Yarrow's face — too fast to name, gone before she could catalogue it. Grief? Fear? Recognition?

"The Meridian," he said quietly.

Sefina went still. The name landed in her chest like a dropped tool. Every archaeologist in the North Pacific knew the story the way seminary students knew Genesis: the salvage ship lost with all hands in '26 — no, not all hands, that was the poison in it — lost with her cargo of medicine-grade quinine worth a fortune then and a legend now, and one man who survived, who took the ship's boats back into the storm against orders and came back alone, and who spent the rest of his life called a mutineer and a coward by every newspaper from Seattle to Skagway.

*Yarrow.* The surname detonated quietly in her memory. There had been a trial transcript in the archives, once, in another life, another career. A young officer named Yarrow who testified that he'd gone back for survivors and found none, and whose testimony no one believed because believing it would have meant admitting the owners had ordered the abandonment.

"You're his son," she said.

Yarrow's jaw tightened. Outside, a growler scraped along the hull with a sound like a fingernail drawn the length of a coffin.

"I'm nobody's son," he said. "My father was a mutineer. Ask anyone."

"I don't ask anyone. I ask wrecks." Sefina held his eyes. "Wrecks don't lie, Mr. Yarrow. They get buried, they get salvaged, they get sold — but they keep their records. Silt keeps time better than courts do. If your father went back into that storm, the *Meridian*'s hull will remember it." She stood, retrieving her untouched coffee. "Which is why you're here. Not for the quinine. Not for Vess's silver. You're here because there's something in that ship with your family's name on it, and you'd rather freeze on an ice shelf than let Vess's divers get to it first."

He said nothing. But he didn't deny it either, and in the economy of the Cormorant, where every man aboard lied professionally, the absence of a lie rang louder than any confession.

"Get some sleep," Sefina said. "You'll want your hands working when we make the fjord tomorrow. And Mr. Yarrow—" she paused at the doorway, "—whatever it is you're hiding, decide soon whether it's worth more to you than the truth. On a salvage ship, those are usually different currencies, and everyone aboard gets paid in one or the other."

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep, which was usual, so she climbed to the wheelhouse, which was also usual, and found it occupied, which was not.

Mara Vess stood at the chart table with a red pencil and a tide almanac, and she didn't look up when Sefina came in. The captain's daughter was twenty-six and carried herself like a woman serving a sentence — precise, silent, always positioned near the door. Sefina had catalogued her the way she catalogued everything: *estranged, competent, contemptuous of her father, staying anyway.* The question of why she stayed was one of the more interesting problems aboard, ranking just below the mystery of a stowaway mechanic and well above the identity of the buyer Vess kept radioing in coded bursts from the aft aerial.

"Can't sleep either?" Mara said, without inflection.

"I never sleep before a dive. Bad luck." Sefina leaned against the chart table's far edge, careful not to touch the papers. "What are you plotting?"

"Tides." Mara tapped the almanac. "Spring series peaks in nine days. After that the window starts closing. My father's telling the crew we have until the equinox." Her pencil stopped. "We don't. We have six workable tides, maybe seven if the weather holds, and he knows it, because I showed him the same tables three days ago."

Sefina filed that away. *Six tides.* The number had a weight to it, the specific gravity of a deadline. "And the dynamite?"

Mara's head came up sharply. For the first time she looked directly at Sefina, and Sefina saw the family resemblance not in the face but in the fury — the same banked heat she'd seen in the captain's eyes, pointed in the opposite direction.

"You've heard about the charges."

"I heard the bosun requisitioning blasting caps from the magazine and lying about what for. I have ears. It's most of what's left of my profession." Sefina lowered her voice. "Blasting a frozen wreck inside an ice cave is not salvage, Ms. Vess. It's demolition with witnesses. The contextual record will be destroyed. Whatever's in that hold — and I suspect it's more than cargo — will come up as scrap with paperwork."

"My father doesn't want a record. He wants a payday." Mara rolled the pencil between her fingers. "Thirty years he's chased this wreck. Do you know why?"

"I assumed profit."

"Profit's the story he tells the crew." Mara looked out the wheelhouse windows at the dark water, the pale smear of the glacier miles astern, faintly luminous even at midnight, like a scar that glowed. "There's a photograph over his bunk. A ship going down. I asked him about it when I was nine. He told me it was the day he learned what men were worth." Her voice flattened. "He's never said another word about it in seventeen years. But he checks the tide tables for this fjord every spring like other men check lottery numbers."

Sefina turned this over. Two generations of Vess, bound to a dead ship by opposite chains — the father by obsession, the daughter by the exhausting arithmetic of loving someone consumed by it. And now a third element entering the system: a pilot with a disgraced father and a secret, a girl in the engine room with no papers and no history, and somewhere ahead, a cave full of 1926 waiting under a hundred feet of ice.

The Cormorant was a wreck herself, Sefina thought. Everyone aboard was salvage of one kind or another — people pulled out of their own disasters and pressed back into service, seaworthy enough, never quite trusted.

"Dr. Okafor." Mara's voice cut through her reverie. "The pilot. What's his angle? My father thinks he's just grateful. Grateful men eat."

"He's hiding something," Sefina said. "Something with weight. He carries it like ballast — you can see it in how he sits."

"Everyone hides something."

"Yes. But most people hide things they're ashamed of. He's hiding something he's *protecting*." She remembered the way Yarrow had flinched at the word *manifest*, the way his stillness had a white-knuckled quality to it, the discipline of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "There's a difference. Shame wants to stay buried. Protection is waiting for its moment."

Mara considered that, and something in her expression shifted — the wary assessment of a navigator sighting a new star and deciding whether to trust it.

"Then we should find out what it is," she said, "before my father does. He has a way of turning other people's secrets into leverage. It's the only inheritance he ever gave me."

* * *

They raised the fjord mouth at dawn, and Sefina stood at the bow rail and watched the glacier come down to meet them.

She had worked ice sites in the Baltic and the Beaufort, had dived under lake ice in February with her breath freezing in her regulator, but she had never seen anything like this: a river of ice a mile wide, descending from a country of white silence, its calving face sheer and blue-veined and older than every nation she held citizenship in. Meltwater poured from its base in braided silver threads. The air coming off it was cold enough to ache in the teeth, and it smelled of stone and deep time — the smell of the Pleistocene, of a world that kept its own records and surrendered them rarely.

Behind her, the crew mustered in the well deck: divers checking gauges, the bosun overseeing a crane, Vess at the rail with binoculars and an expression Sefina had last seen on a man opening a safe. Wren had emerged from below decks and stationed herself at the rail two meters from Isandro Yarrow, close enough to be accidental, and the two of them stood in a silence that had the texture of conspiracy — or the beginning of one.

"There," Vess said, and his voice carried the way voices carry when a man has waited thirty years to use it. "Starboard shore. The notch."

Sefina raised her own binoculars. At first she saw only ice — the glacier's lateral margin, a rampart of dirty blue — and then the light shifted and the notch resolved into an arch, a black mouth bitten into the ice perhaps forty feet above the waterline, rimmed with old moraine and dripping meltwater in long glassy strings. A cave. A cave that could not exist, because every survey since 1931 showed solid ice at that elevation, which meant the glacier had retreated further in three years than in the previous ninety, which meant—

Which meant the ice was giving something back. And the ice did not give gifts. It gave debts.

"Okafor." Vess lowered his binoculars and looked at her, and for one unguarded second she saw not the employer but the believer — a man standing before the object of a thirty-year religion. "You'll go in with the first party. Document everything. I want that hold recorded before a single crate comes out."

"Every context, every layer," Sefina said. "As agreed."

"As agreed." He turned back to the glacier, and his knuckles whitened on the rail, and Sefina understood suddenly that she was looking at a man holding his hands the way the pilot held his — still, braced, guarding something behind a closed door.

Beside her, unnoticed by anyone else, Isandro Yarrow had gone very quiet. When she glanced at him, she found him staring at the cave mouth with an intensity that had nothing to do with treasure and everything to do with grief, his bandaged hand pressed flat against the rail as if steadying himself against a swell only he could feel.

Wren saw it too. The girl's eyes moved from the pilot's face to the cave and back again, quick and shrewd as a bosun bird, and then she caught Sefina looking and gave a small, deliberate shrug: *I see him too. What do we do about it?*

Sefina turned back to the ice. Somewhere in that black arch lay a ship, a manifest, a fortune, a letter, a lie ninety years old — and three people on this deck who each wanted a different piece of the truth, and a captain who wanted none of it at all.

The Cormorant's engines slowed. The anchor chain began to run.

"First party suits up in twenty minutes," Vess called, and the well deck erupted into motion, and Sefina Okafor stood at the rail watching the cave mouth widen as they approached, thinking: *Every wreck I've ever entered told me the truth eventually. The question has never been whether the dead will speak. It's whether the living can bear to listen.*

Behind her, quietly, Isandro Yarrow whispered something to the glacier that she couldn't quite hear — a word, or a name, or a prayer — and the wind took it, and the ice gave nothing back.
