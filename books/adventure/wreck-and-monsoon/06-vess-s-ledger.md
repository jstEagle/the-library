## 6. Vess's Ledger

The Cormorant was not a ship so much as a floating argument — a hundred and forty feet of riveted steel that had been patched, welded, re-patched, and argued with for three decades until the vessel and her captain had grown into the same stubborn animal. Isandro saw it from the tender as they crossed the gray water of the fjord: rust bleeding down from the hawseholes like old wounds reopened on purpose, a crane arm swung out over the stern like a question nobody wanted answered, and the bridge windows lit amber against the failing light, smoke curling from a galley stack that smelled, even at two hundred yards, of burnt coffee and diesel.

Vess stood at the rail as they came alongside. He had not gone below after hauling Isandro off the ice shelf. He had not changed out of his soaked gear. He simply watched, arms folded, the way a man watches weather he has already decided to sail into.

"Yarrow," he said, when Isandro climbed the pilot ladder with Sefina's sample bags slung over one shoulder and Wren clattering up behind him like a loose tool in a drawer. "You look like something the tide rejected."

"Your diver said the same thing."

"My diver is an optimist." Vess turned and led them aft without waiting to see if anyone followed. That, Isandro would learn, was how everything worked aboard the Cormorant: you followed or you were cargo.

* * *

They gave him a bunk in the forward crew compartment, a steel locker that smelled of mildew and someone else's cigarettes, and a towel stiff enough to stand on its own. He washed the glacier silt out of his hair with water hot enough to remind him the Cormorant had a working boiler, which put her one full luxury ahead of his airstrip. Through the porthole, the fjord lay in that particular northern dusk where the water goes the color of a knife blade and the icebergs glow faintly blue, like coals banked for the night.

Somewhere below him, the Meridian sat in her tomb of ice, and somewhere above him, Captain Aldous Vess was awake too. Isandro could feel both facts pressing on the deck plates.

He found the galley by following the light. Wren was already there, cross-legged on a counter she'd been told twice to get off of, eating stewed beef straight from the pot while a cook the size of a door pretended not to notice. Sefina sat at the corner table with her documentation crates around her like a fortress, cataloguing by lamplight, her pen moving in small precise strokes.

"You should eat," she said without looking up. "Shock does its best work about six hours after you think you're fine."

"I'm fine."

"Mm." She wrote something. "That's what everyone says right before they do something stupid."

The galley door opened and Mara Vess came in.

Isandro had seen her only at a distance before — a tall figure on the bridge wing during the rescue, calling soundings through a megaphone, her voice cutting through the static like wire through cheese. Up close she was younger than her voice suggested and older than her years required: mid-twenties, dark hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of signal flag, eyes the flat gray-green of the sea outside. She carried a chart tube under one arm and a mug in the other hand, and she looked at Isandro the way a surveyor looks at a rockslide — assessing, unimpressed, already calculating where the debris would land.

"So," she said. "The man who found my father's white whale."

"I didn't find anything. I crashed."

"You crashed onto it." She set her mug down. "Thirty years he's chased this wreck. Do you understand what that means? I learned to read tide tables before I learned to read books. I've heard the name *Meridian* at every dinner table I've ever sat at. And you stumble into it because your engine quit." There was no malice in it. That was almost worse. It was arithmetic.

"Your father pulled me off the ice," Isandro said. "I'm grateful."

"He'll collect on that." She picked up her mug again. "He always collects. Eat your stew, Yarrow. You'll want your strength for the ledger meeting."

"The what?"

She paused at the door. "Tomorrow morning. He'll show you the salvage plan, offer you a share, make you feel like a partner. He's done it before." Her face did something complicated and then did nothing at all. "Just remember which side of the ledger you're standing on when he turns the page."

* * *

The salvage plan was laid out on the chart table in the after cabin at 0700, under a swinging lamp, with four men and Sefina crowded around it and Vess standing at the head like a minister at a funeral he intended to profit from.

The plan was good. Isandro hated how good it was. Vess had the flooded hold mapped from sonar runs going back fifteen years — contour lines drawn and redrawn in different inks, each generation of the glacier's retreat noted in the margins like a diary. The cargo deck lay behind a collapsed section of hull plating that could be cut away in two tides. The quinine crates were stacked amidships, exactly where a prudent master would have lashed medicine above the waterline. Estimated recovery value, written in Vess's blocky hand at the bottom of the sheet, was a number with enough zeros to buy Isandro's airstrip outright, fuel it for a decade, and still leave change.

"Twenty percent," Vess said. "To the man who found her."

Isandro kept his voice level. "I didn't find her. I fell into her."

"Falling into things is a profession, in my experience. Most men never fall into anything worth twenty percent." Vess tapped the chart. "You know these waters. You can fly spotter for the dive windows, run our shore caches, keep us supplied. In exchange you walk away solvent for the first time in your life. I've seen your kind of operation, Yarrow. One bad winter from the boneyard. This is the winter that doesn't come."

Around the table, faces turned toward him. Sefina's expression was carefully blank in the way of someone who had been offered partnerships before and had learned what lived inside them. Wren, who was supposed to be in the engine room and was instead wedged into the doorway pretending to check a valve, went very still.

It would be easy. That was the thing that frightened him. Twenty percent of that number was everything he'd spent six years scraping toward, and all he had to do was say yes and stop asking questions.

"There's something I want to ask first," Isandro said. "About the original wreck. 1927. The boats went back into the storm —"

Something moved across Vess's face. Not much. A tightening around the mouth, the way ice shifts before it groans. But Isandro had spent his life reading weather in small changes, and he read this one clearly.

"History is below decks," Vess said. "We're here for cargo."

"Three men died in those boats. My father —"

"Your father." Vess said the words slowly, tasting them, and now the whole room had gone quiet except for the lamp swinging on its chain. "Elias Yarrow."

"You knew him."

"I knew what he cost." Vess planted both fists on the chart table, and the lamp swung shadows across his face, carving him into someone older and harder than the man who had hauled Isandro off the ice with efficient, almost gentle hands. "October 1927. The *Meridian* founders on the reef in a gale. Boats launched. And then Elias Yarrow — second officer, twenty-nine years old, golden boy of the owners' circle — takes the number two boat and goes *back*. Back into the gale, against the master's order, against every law of the sea, with three volunteers who never came home. The boat was found smashed on the rocks a week later. Empty."

"They were going back for survivors —"

"There *were* no survivors!" The word cracked off the steel bulkheads. Vess straightened, breathing hard, and for a moment Isandro saw not the fleet captain but a boy somewhere inland, decades ago, being handed a telegram. "My grandfather was bosun on that ship. Thomas Vess. He was one of the three who rowed back with your father. Three men, Yarrow. My blood among them. And the inquiry gave the world a mutineer to hang it on, because the owners needed a villain and your father was dead and couldn't argue." His jaw worked. "I have spent thirty years telling myself the man who killed my grandfather at least paid for it. Now you stand in my cabin wearing his face and asking me to feel sorry for him."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water. Wren had stopped pretending about the valve entirely. Sefina's pen had stopped moving.

Isandro heard his own heartbeat. *They were going back for survivors.* The sentence sat in him like a splinter working toward bone — because it was true, he had always believed it was true, it was the single article of faith his mother had left him — and yet Vess's grief made it suddenly, horribly arguable. What if the letter in his jacket pocket, sealed and patient in its oilskin wrapping, said something else? What if his father had been reckless, proud, wrong?

What if the truth cost more than the lie ever had?

"I'm sorry about your grandfather," Isandro said quietly. "I mean that. But I need to know what happened on that ship. All of it."

"Why?"

"Because my family has been paying for a story we never got to read."

Vess studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw there seemed to settle something, or maybe just file it away; with this man Isandro suspected the two acts looked identical.

"Partnership stands," Vess said finally. "Twenty percent. History stays below decks until the cargo is safe. Then we'll see what the ice wants to give back." He rolled the chart closed with a snap. "Dive window opens on the next tide. Dismissed."

* * *

He found Mara at the stern rail an hour later, watching the bergs drift down-fjord in the last of the light. She didn't turn when he came up beside her, but she shifted slightly — an inch of space made, which from her he understood was practically an embrace.

"You asked him about the boats," she said.

"You were listening."

"I'm always listening. It's the only inheritance he gave me that works." She wrapped both hands around the rail. "My grandfather's father was Thomas Vess. Did he tell you that part? He tells everyone eventually. It's load-bearing."

"He told me your father blames mine for killing him."

"And do you believe it?"

Isandro looked out at the water. An iceberg the size of a church went by, slow and serene, carrying its blue interior like a secret. "I don't know. That's the problem. I've built my whole life on one version of that night, and he's built his on another, and neither of us was there."

Mara was quiet for a while. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped, pitched under the wind, meant for him alone.

"When I was fourteen I found a second book in his cabin. Not the salvage ledger — everyone knows about that one, the money book, he shows it off like scripture. This other one. Old, oilskin-wrapped, hidden behind the spare alternator of all places." She turned and looked at him directly, and up close her eyes were not flat at all; they were the color of the sea over a shoal, which is to say beautiful and dangerous and hiding something underneath. "A grudge book. Every name that ever crossed him, every debt he decided was owed, going back forty years. Interest calculated. Some entries decades old. Your father's name is in it, Yarrow. Written in ink so fresh it might as well have been yesterday."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying my father rescued you off that ice because you're useful, and he offered you twenty percent because you're cheap, and neither of those things means what you think they mean." She pushed off the rail. "He keeps two ledgers. One for money and one for grudges, and he settles them separately, and he never, ever confuses which one he's holding. Tonight he's holding the money ledger. Make sure you know which night he switches."

She was three steps away when Isandro spoke.

"Why tell me? He's your father."

Mara stopped. For a moment the wind filled the pause, and the fjord creaked and muttered below them, ice talking to ice in a language older than either of their families' griefs.

"Because whoever wrote that entry in the grudge book got the story wrong," she said, not turning around. "And I've watched what a wrong story does to a person for twenty years. I'd like to see one corrected. Even if it costs him." She glanced back once, gray-green eyes catching the last light. "Especially if it costs him."

Then she was gone down the companionway, and Isandro stood alone at the rail with the letter pressed flat against his ribs inside his jacket, warm as a coal, heavier than any cargo the *Meridian* had ever carried.

Below his boots, thirty fathoms down, the truth waited in the dark with the quinine and the silver. Above him, in the amber-lit after cabin, Aldous Vess was writing tonight's date into a book Isandro had never seen.

Two ledgers. Two stories of the same storm. And six tides — no, five now — to learn which one was true before the glacier closed its mouth forever.
