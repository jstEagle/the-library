## 7. Mara's Charts

The Cormorant's chart room smelled of diesel, old varnish, and her father's pipe tobacco — a smell that had followed Mara Vess from childhood like a dog that wouldn't be called off. She sat at the drafting table with the tide tables spread before her and did not look up when the door opened, because she already knew who it would be. Isandro Yarrow had a way of walking through a ship: quiet on the soles, loud in the air behind him, as though the space he occupied were still deciding whether to accept him.

"You wanted to see me," he said.

"I wanted someone to check my arithmetic." She turned the table toward him without ceremony. "You fly. You understand weather and water better than most of these deckhands understand their own hands."

He came around the table slowly, the way men do when they suspect a trap but can't name its shape. She watched him read. He was younger than she'd expected when Vess first spoke of him — thirty, maybe thirty-two, with the wind-scoured face of a man who'd spent his whole life at altitude, and eyes that kept flicking to the porthole as if the fjord might do something interesting while his back was turned. It might. That was the thing about this place. The glacier was in a mood.

"Your arithmetic's fine," he said after a moment. "That's the problem."

"Yes." She let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. "Say it plainly. I want to hear you say it."

"The window isn't six tides." His finger traced the column of figures — the lunar corrections, the barometric allowances, the glacial rebound data she'd pulled from three separate surveys and reconciled by hand because nobody else aboard could read them. "It's four. Maybe five if we're lucky with pressure. Your father's telling the fleet six because six is what keeps the divers diving and the investors calm."

"And you believe me. A stranger's numbers, over the captain's."

"I don't know you," Yarrow said. "But I've spent twenty years reading ice, and your numbers match what my own eyes have been telling me since the day I crashed into that cave. The meltwater's running brown. The seracs above the entrance are leaning. When a glacier starts shedding its eyebrows, it doesn't wait for anyone's schedule."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. There it was again — that flicker at the mention of the cave. She'd seen it twice now, once when he described the crash to the assembled crew and once just now, a small contraction around the eyes, like a man guarding a wound he didn't want counted among his injuries. Everyone aboard sensed there was something Yarrow wasn't saying. Sefina Okafor had said as much over coffee, in that dry academic way of hers: *Our pilot is a locked drawer, and I intend to pick the lock eventually.* Wren, the stowaway girl, had simply said, *He's scared of something bigger than the wreck,* which from a fifteen-year-old was either naivety or unnerving precision.

Mara didn't care about his secrets. Not yet. What she cared about was the dynamite.

* * *

She'd found the crates two nights ago, in the aft hold beneath a false floor of netting and spare line. Forty sticks of gelignite, sweating in their wax paper, wired into primers that had no business being assembled on a vessel carrying fuel drums. Her father's handwriting on the manifest: *demolition charge, salvage use, authorized.* Authorized by whom? Vess answered to no one since Halloran's money had started arriving — Mara had done the books long enough to know the shape of outside capital even when her father laundered it through three shell companies and a fishing lodge in Sitka.

Dynamite against a glacier. Against a flash-frozen ship sealed inside one. It was not salvage; it was butchery. And worse — far worse — it was suicide dressed in work clothes. The cave mouth was a throat of ice held together by luck and cold. Set charges inside it and the whole architecture would come down like a cathedral in an earthquake, with divers in the water and the Meridian crushed under ten thousand tons of calving ice before anyone could log so much as a rivet.

Thirty years the ship had waited under the ice. Thirty years, and her father meant to crack it open like an oyster and leave nothing behind but a hole in the charts and a bonus in his account.

Mara had grown up on stories of the Meridian. That was the bitter joke of it — the wreck that had ruined Yarrow's father had built hers. Vess had chased the rumor across two decades of debt and divorce, mortgaging boats, marrying badly, dragging his daughter through every port from Ketchikan to Kodiak while he listened to old men talk about a ghost ship full of quinine and silver. Mara had learned navigation on those voyages, learned tides and currents and the grammar of coastlines, learned that her father loved a treasure more than he had ever loved anything warm. By the time she was old enough to leave, leaving felt less like escape than like amputation.

She'd come back anyway. Two years ago, after the blacklist ended Sefina Okafor's career and after her own marriage quietly starved to death in Juneau, she'd signed aboard the Cormorant because the alternative was a desk job plotting other people's courses, and because some traitorous part of her still believed the old man could be reached. That part was dying now, day by day, charge by charge.

"So," Yarrow said. "What do you want from me?"

* * *

They walked the deck while she told him, because the chart room had ears — Wren had rewired half the ship and knew where every microphone and loose bulkhead panel lived, and Vess's bosun had a habit of materializing near doors. The evening was doing what evenings did here: pouring violet light down the fjord until the ice walls glowed like the inside of a lamp. Somewhere up-valley, the glacier groaned, a sound like a whale grieving, and every hand on deck paused for half a second before pretending not to have heard.

"Vess is going to blow the eastern shelf," Mara said. "Day after tomorrow, if the dive schedule holds. He thinks a controlled fracture will open the cargo hold without flooding the upper decks. He's wrong. The ice above the wreck is under compression — you saw the crevasse field yourself when you flew in. One charge goes deep enough, and it won't be a controlled anything. It'll be an avalanche with a fuse."

Yarrow stopped at the rail. Below them, the dive tender bobbed at its mooring, and beyond it the cave mouth showed as a black seam in the blue-white wall, patient as a closed eye. "I know," he said. "I've been trying to figure out how to slow him down without getting thrown off the boat. I need this salvage, Vess. My airstrip is collateral on a loan I can't service if the season goes bad. If he cuts me loose, I lose everything."

"Then we're both negotiating with a man who doesn't keep his word."

"He offered me partnership."

"He offers everyone partnership. Ask the last three captains who worked for him." She gripped the rail. The metal was cold enough to bite. "My father keeps two ledgers, Yarrow. One for money and one for grudges, and the second one is longer. Whatever he's promised you, somewhere in that book there's a page with your name on it, waiting. I don't know what you did or what he thinks you did. But it's there."

Something moved behind his eyes — that same guarded contraction, deeper this time. For a moment she thought he'd tell her whatever it was, right there, with the glacier light going gold around them. Then a winch clanged on the foredeck and the moment shut like a drawer.

"What do you actually want?" he asked instead. "Not for me. For you. Nobody walks back onto their father's ship after two years away just to check arithmetic."

The question landed harder than she expected, because it was fair. She made herself answer it honestly, or as honestly as she could bear.

"When I was nine years old," she said, "my father took me to the maritime museum in Vancouver. There was a model of a wreck in a glass case — a steamer, raised off the coast of Ireland. Every plank catalogued. Every artifact numbered and labeled. I stood in front of that case for an hour, and my father stood beside me, and I remember thinking he was bored, and later understanding he wasn't bored, he was hungry. He didn't see a story. He saw a price list." She turned to face the fjord. "There are maybe two ships in the world preserved the way the Meridian is preserved. Flash-frozen mid-voyage. The crew's kit still in the lockers, probably. The galley still set. It's not cargo, it's a century standing still — and it belongs in a museum, documented properly, by people who understand what they're looking at. Not blown apart for quinine bales by a man who thinks history is inventory."

"Sefina," Yarrow said.

"Sefina," Mara agreed. "Okafor lost her career because she refused to sign a provenance report she knew was forged. She's the best marine archaeologist of her generation and she's down in that hold right now taking photographs with borrowed equipment on a ship owned by the kind of man who destroyed her. If Vess sets his charges, everything she's documenting dies with it. Everything any of us could have saved." She paused. "I'm not asking you to mutiny, pilot. I know how that word sits with you."

His head came around sharply. "Who told you—"

"Nobody told me anything. I read manifests. Your father's name has been attached to the Meridian story for thirty years; it's in every archive I ever searched. Elias Yarrow, first mate, condemned as the mutineer who abandoned the ship." She watched him absorb it, watched the careful blankness strain. "I also know my father blames him personally. There's a photograph in his cabin — three men, a dock in Petersburg, 1929. My father's young in it. On the back, in pencil: *the ones we couldn't bring home.* He looks at it the way other men look at graves."

The silence between them filled with the sounds of the ship — halyards ticking, the tender's engine coughing to life below, Wren's voice drifting up from the engine room in cheerful profanity. Yarrow stared at the cave mouth for a long time.

"My father died owing eleven thousand dollars and a good name he never got back," he said finally. "I used to think if I could just find the Meridian, I'd find out what really happened. Now I'm standing on top of it, and the man who owns the salvage is the same man whose money bought the lie, and I—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Later. Maybe. Right now: what's your plan?"

* * *

Her plan, laid out that night in the chart room with the door wedged and Wren ostensibly fixing a light fixture that needed no fixing, was simple and dangerous: document first, dive fast, and steal the schedule out from under Vess.

"The tide tables give us four safe windows," Mara said, tapping the paper. "He's planning five operations across six. We compress. We run the documentation dives concurrently with the cargo dives — Sefina works the hold while his divers work the crates. We log everything, photograph everything, and we get a complete record out of the fjord before he ever lights a fuse. Then, when he does light one — and he will — there's a public record worth more than the silver. Museums, universities, the press. Men like my father fear exposure more than drowning."

"He'll catch us," Yarrow said. "He counts everything. Boats, hours, air bottles. It's how he caught every partner who ever crossed him."

"That's why there are four of us." Mara glanced at Wren, who was grinning around a mouthful of electrical tape. "The girl can make a gauge read anything she wants. Compressor hours, depth readings, tank pressures. Give Vess a false picture of the dive schedule and he won't know we've doubled the windows until the record's already ashore."

"Ma'am," Wren said, "I once made a fish buyer's scale read light for a whole season. This is easier. This is fun."

"It's fraud," Sefina said from the doorway.

Everyone froze. The archaeologist stood with a mug of coffee and the expression of a woman who had been listening longer than politeness allowed. She came in, set the mug on the chart table directly atop Vess's master plan, and studied the tide tables for a full minute before speaking.

"It's falsified operational data," she amended. "Which I have gone to prison-adjacent lengths to oppose, professionally speaking. So understand what it costs me to say this." She looked up. "If the alternative is watching that ship dynamited into gravel with no record at all — then yes. Falsify the gauges. But we do it my way. Every artifact photographed in situ before it moves. Every context logged. If we're going to commit crimes, we'll commit crimes that leave a proper archaeological record, because that record is the only thing that survives us. The silver will end up in someone's vault. The documentation is the actual treasure."

Mara felt something loosen in her chest — a knot she'd been tying tighter for two years without noticing. Four people around a chart table, none of them loyal to the same things, all of them loyal, suddenly, to the same idea. It was the closest thing to family she'd felt since she was nine years old in front of a glass case.

"There's one problem," Yarrow said quietly. "Vess watches me closest. I crashed his prize out of the sky; he hasn't decided yet whether I'm a partner or a liability. If I'm seen with you three—"

"Then don't be seen." Mara rolled the tide tables, squared them, and handed them to him. "Memorize them. The numbers live in your head, nowhere else. We meet only when the ship's asleep, and we speak only in the engine room, where Wren controls the acoustics." She hesitated, then added, because fairness demanded it: "And Yarrow — whatever you're carrying that you won't show us, decide soon whether we're people you trust with it. Because in about forty-eight hours my father is going to do something irreversible, and when he does, everyone aboard picks a side. There won't be time left for deciding."

He took the roll of paper. Their hands didn't touch, but something passed between them anyway — the wary, provisional trust of people who have each been burned by the same fire from different sides.

Above them, faint through the hull, came a sound none of them mistook for anything ordinary: a deep, cracking boom from up the fjord, followed by the long rumble of ice finding new shapes. The glacier, clearing its throat.

Wren looked at the ceiling. "Was that close?"

"Two miles," Mara said. "Give it a week, it'll be close."

Sefina picked up her coffee. "Then we'd better be fast," she said, "and we'd better be quiet — and somebody had better tell me why our pilot just went white as the ice wall."

All eyes turned to Isandro Yarrow, standing in the chart-room lamplight with a roll of stolen tide tables in his fist and a secret pressing against his teeth.

"I found something in the cave," he said. "The first day. Something I haven't told anyone."
