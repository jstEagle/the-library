## 8. First Descent

The water in the flooded hold was the color of old bottle glass, and Sefina Okafor had been staring at it for an hour before anyone went in.

She stood at the edge of the cutting Vess's divers had sawed through the Meridian's hull, her dry suit zipped to the throat, her camera housing cradled against her chest like a child. Below her, forty feet down, the lamplight of the first diver wavered against something that might have been a bulkhead or might have been a dream. The ice above them all was eleven feet thick, blue-black, groaning at intervals like a ship itself — a slow, tectonic complaint that Sefina had learned, over three days aboard the Cormorant, to stop hearing. She hadn't stopped hearing it. She'd just stopped letting it show.

"Dr. Okafor." Vess's voice came through her earpiece, flat as a ledger line. "You're up after Ruiz. Remember your place. You document; we recover."

"I remember," she said.

It was a lie of a kind she had told before, in other years, on other wrecks, for men who wanted provenance papers signed with dates that made looted cargo look like licensed salvage. She had refused once, cleanly, at the cost of everything — her post, her funding, her name in the field. And here she was again, at the bottom of another man's greed, telling him *I remember* when what she meant was *I remember everything, Captain, including you.*

She checked her gauges. Checked her mask. Checked the little waterproof notebook strapped inside her forearm, where she'd written, in pencil, the words she intended to live by for the next four tides: *Record first. Argue later.*

Ruiz went in with barely a splash, his lamp sinking into the green dark like a coin dropped down a well. Then it was her turn.

The cold hit her face first — not cold exactly, but a kind of pressure, a presence, as though the water were a solid thing wearing liquid as a disguise. Forty-one degrees. Her regulator chattered. She equalized, descended hand over hand along the guide line Vess's crew had rigged from the cut to the hold's broken skylight, and the world narrowed to the circle of her own light.

And then the Meridian opened beneath her like a held breath finally released.

Sefina had dived wrecks on five coasts. She had swum through ships that had died violently — burned, holed, scattered across reefs by storms that tore masts off like splinters. This was nothing like that. The Meridian had not died violently. She had died *mid-motion*, caught by the glacier's advance like an insect in amber, and every object in her hung suspended in the attitude of her last living minute. A coil of rope still lay faked neatly on deck plating. A tin cup rested upright on a shelf, its handle turned outward, waiting for a hand. Cargo netting draped over crates in the hold below, sagging under ninety years of ice load but intact, orderly, patient.

Her hands were shaking. Not from the cold. From the terrible intimacy of it — this was not a wreck site, it was a *room*, and someone had left the door open expecting to come back.

She began to work. Photographs first: wide shots of the hold, then detail frames of the stenciling on the crates — *MERIDIAN SALVAGE CO., SEATTLE — MEDICINAL CARGO — KEEP DRY* — the irony so perfect it felt scripted. She shot the companionway ladder, the brass fittings uncorroded in the anaerobic chill, the ship's bell still hanging in its bracket with the clapper lashed down for silence. Someone had lashed that bell. Someone had wanted quiet, that last night, and Sefina photographed the lashing twice because her eyes kept blurring behind the mask.

Through her earpiece, muffled and distant, came the sounds of the recovery operation: Ruiz grunting, the scrape of pry bars, Vess's voice counting crates. "Two more. Mark them. That's quinine — look at the seals, boys, that's medicine-grade, that's a hospital's ransom in one box."

Sefina filmed him prying the lid. Inside, nested in straw gone to brown lace, lay rows of amber glass bottles, their contents settled into sediment like sleeping blood. Intact. Ninety years sealed in ice, and intact. She thought of the villages Isandro flew medicine into, the ones he talked about when he thought no one was listening — fevers treated with willow bark and hope because the real thing cost too much to fly. That cargo could have saved thousands of lives if it had arrived in 1926. Instead it would make one man rich in 2024, and the arithmetic of that made her want to put her fist through her camera housing.

Instead she photographed the bottles. All of them. Every serial number, every seal, every crate mark. If Vess wanted to sell history by the pound, then history would have receipts.

* * *

They found the silver an hour into the dive, in a strong-room aft of the hold, and the mood on the comm channel changed the way weather changes over open water.

"Well, hell," said Ruiz, and then said nothing else for a long time, which from a salvage diver meant reverence.

Sefina swam to the doorway and looked in. The strong-room's door stood open — it had been open when the ship froze, unlocked, as though the captain had been mid-transaction — and inside, racked in wooden trays, lay bullion. Silver bars, trade silver, stamped ingots, and coins fused into gray-green bricks by time. In her lights it did not gleam; it *glowed*, a soft submarine radiance, like moonlight that had gotten lost and decided to stay.

"That's the manifest weight," Vess said quietly. "That's all of it. Thirty years I've looked for—" He stopped himself. Even over the comm, Sefina heard him reassemble his captain's voice, brick by brick. "Log it. Two divers per trip. Nobody touches anything without my say."

*Thirty years,* Sefina thought, filming the trays. *Thirty years, and you never once asked what the ship was carrying besides money.* Because the answer was in the papers, and the papers would tell a story Vess did not want told. She had read enough of Sefina's own blacklisted file to know how men like Vess handled stories.

It was on the return pass, near the captain's cabin, that she saw the safe.

It sat bolted to the deck beneath a collapsed chart table, a squat iron cube the size of a sea chest, its dial plate green with age but whole. Ruiz had already marked it with a tag. On the deck beside it, half-buried in silt that was really just rock flour and time, lay a canvas dispatch case, its leather straps rotted to ribbons, its flap fallen open.

Inside the flap, visible even in the poor light, was a packet of letters tied with wire. And on the top letter, in faded iron-gall ink, a name she recognized from three days of watching Isandro Yarrow pretend he wasn't haunted:

*E. Yarrow.*

She looked at it for perhaps four seconds. Long enough to photograph it — she was, whatever else, always a scientist first — and long enough to see Isandro, working the far side of the cabin on Vess's orders, drift toward it with the studied casualness of a man approaching a stove he's been told is cold.

Their lights crossed. Through their masks, across ninety years and forty feet of drowned ship, they looked at each other.

Sefina did not move toward the case. She turned her camera deliberately away, framing instead the chart table's rotting drawers, and gave Isandro the only gift her conscience could afford: her back.

She heard, faintly, the whisper of canvas. The click of a zippered pocket. When she turned around again, the dispatch case was empty of its top letter, and Isandro Yarrow was measuring a bulkhead with a tape measure, his face composed into professional blankness, his left hand pressed flat against his thigh as though holding something there.

*Record first,* she reminded herself. *Argue later.*

But she wrote it in her notebook anyway, in pencil, small: *Y. took one item. Letter? Personal. Did not intervene. Why?*

Because she knew why. She had spent her whole career among people who lied about cargo. Isandro wasn't lying about cargo. He was carrying something heavier than any crate in that hold, and she was beginning to suspect it had a name, and a date, and the word *mutineer* attached to it like a rusted tag.

* * *

Wren nearly died twenty minutes before the end of the dive, and afterward Sefina could not stop thinking about how ordinary it had looked, right up until it wasn't.

The girl was supposed to be topside. She was sixteen, she was a stowaway Vess tolerated because she could keep the Cormorant's ancient compressor alive with baling wire and blasphemy, and she had begged — begged — to be allowed on the dive line as a tender. Vess had grunted assent, mostly, Sefina suspected, because Wren worked free.

So Wren was on the ice shelf above the cut, paying out Sefina's safety line, when the surge came.

Sefina felt it before she understood it: a pressure change in her ears, a shudder through the hull, and then the entire drowned ship rolled — gently, horribly, like a sleeper turning over. Above, somewhere in the eleven feet of ice, something had shifted. The glacier settling. The fjord breathing. A berg grinding past the cave mouth outside, maybe, dragging a wall of displaced water through the opening and into the wreck.

The effect inside the hold was a tide. Their tide, indoors, out of schedule. Debris that had lain undisturbed since Calvin Coolidge was president lifted off the deck and began to travel.

Sefina grabbed a stanchion and rode it out, camera hugged to her chest, watching the silt cloud swallow her lights. On the comm, chaos: Ruiz shouting positions, Vess shouting over him, and Wren's voice — young, cracked, trying so hard to be professional — calling *"Line's fouled, line's fouled, Dr. Okafor, your line's snagged on something moving—"*

Then the pull came.

Sefina's harness snapped taut and yanked her sideways off the stanchion, into the black, and for three full seconds she was nothing but a body being dragged through freezing water by a line wrapped around a shifting crate. Her regulator ripped at her teeth. Her shoulder screamed. She had a lucid, academic thought — *this is how it happens, this is exactly how it happens, the paperwork always says entanglement* — and then the line went slack, then taut again, worse, jerking her downward toward the cargo netting where the crate had rolled and was rolling still, taking her line with it, taking *her* with it, down into the lattice of rope and timber where divers go and do not come back.

She got her knife out. Cold hands, thick gloves, the blade like a toy. She sawed at the line while the current dragged her across the netting's surface, feeling the mesh rise up to meet her like a mouth—

—and then the tension changed. Not slack. *Directed.* Someone above was hauling, hauling hard and fast and smart, taking up the slack she'd cut rather than fighting it, and the drag reversed, and she came up out of the netting's reach like a fish landed. She kicked for the guide line, found it, followed it up through the silt-cloud toward the pale wound of the hull cut, and broke the surface gasping in the air pocket beneath the ice shelf with her heart going like a compressor at full bore.

Above her, framed in the cut, Wren's face appeared — upside down, white-lipped, both hands locked on the line, gloves shredded, blood diluting pink in the meltwater.

"You cut it," the girl said. "I felt you cut it. So I hauled the rest."

"You hauled the rest," Sefina agreed. Her voice sounded like gravel underwater.

"You're heavy."

"I'm buoyant. It's the camera."

Wren laughed — a single high, shocked note — and then her face crumpled and she turned away fast, pretending to check the cleat, shoulders shaking. Sixteen years old and she'd just pulled a grown woman out of a drowning machine with two bleeding hands, and she needed very badly for nobody to see her cry about it.

Sefina let her pretend. Some mercies are just looking elsewhere.

* * *

Topside, in the Cormorant's wheelhouse, the price of the day was counted out in the ship's own currency: silence and hot drinks.

Ruiz reported the tally to Vess like a man reading scripture: nine crates of quinine confirmed, the strong-room inventoried, the safe located and tagged for the next dive. Vess received it standing at his chart table, and Sefina watched him watch the numbers, and saw the thing underneath the satisfaction — not joy, never joy, but the closing of a thirty-year account, and beneath *that*, faintly, something that looked almost like fear. Men who chase a thing for three decades are afraid of catching it. The chase is the part that keeps other feelings out.

Isandro stood apart, by the wheelhouse door, saying nothing. His right hand had stayed in his jacket pocket since the dive. Sefina caught his eye once, and he held it — steady, unreadable, a man asking a question without asking it — and she gave him the smallest nod she could make mean *your secret is not mine to spend.*

He exhaled. Barely. But she'd spent ten years reading liars, and she knew relief when she saw it.

Wren sat on the engine room hatch with her hands bandaged by her own instruction — she'd refused help, of course — drinking cocoa that trembled in the mug. Mara Vess appeared beside her without ceremony, knelt, and began re-wrapping the bandages properly without asking permission either. Wren stiffened, then didn't.

"You hauled her out," Mara said.

"Somebody had to. The line rigging's garbage. Your dad's rigging is garbage."

"My dad's rigging has killed fewer people than most," Mara said, tying off the wrap. "Give it time."

Sefina, peeling out of her dry suit in the corner, catalogued the scene the way she catalogued everything now: the girl bleeding and laughing, the navigator kneeling in the bilgewater, the pilot guarding his pocket, the captain counting his silver, herself writing it all down. Four tides left. Maybe fewer, if the ice had opinions — and today it had made its opinion known, in a surge that should not have happened on a calm day.

She opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote, under the day's date:

*Hold surveyed. Cargo confirmed. One near-drowning (Okafor, self; rescue, W.). Surge event unexplained — ice is moving early. Vess counts money. Yarrow carries paper. Window shrinking.*

Then, after a moment, she added the sentence she'd been circling all day, the one that had started as observation and was becoming something closer to a vow:

*Everything in that hold is evidence. Including us.*

Outside, the glacier calved — a single deep report, like a door closing in another room — and every person in the wheelhouse stopped talking at once, and listened, and pretended they hadn't.
