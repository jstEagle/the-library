## 9. The Letter

The Cormorant's aft bunkroom smelled of diesel, wet wool, and thirty years of other men's dreams. Isandro lay in the lower berth with the letter against his chest, feeling its weight the way you'd feel a stone you intended to swallow.

He had waited two hours after the last of Vess's divers turned in. He'd waited through the clank of the anchor watch, through the generator's rhythm changing pitch as the night cold deepened, through Wren's footsteps overhead — she slept in the engine room, curled in the warmth of the boiler like a cat that had learned not to trust beds. He'd waited because he had known, with the certainty of a man who has flown into weather he shouldn't have, that once he opened this envelope he would not be the same person who closed it.

The letter had come out of the captain's safe in the second descent, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin document case, and it had passed through four sets of hands before reaching his. Sefina had catalogued it — *Correspondence, personal, sealed, addressee: T. R. Yarrow* — and had looked at him for one long moment over her clipboard, saying nothing, giving nothing, before passing it along the chain of custody to Vess's manifest clerk. Isandro had signed for it as "unopened, pending assessment." Vess, flush with the quinine crates and the silver ingots stacked in the forward hold, had waved it through. Paper, Vess had said. Paper doesn't pay for diesel.

Isandro turned the envelope over in the light of his headlamp. His father's name in faded violet ink — *T. R. Yarrow, First Officer, s.s. Meridian* — in a hand Isandro recognized with a jolt that went through him like turbulence. He knew that hand. He had seen it on the flyleaf of the navigation primer that was the only thing his father had left him besides the name and the disgrace. The letters were tall and narrow, the crossbars decisive, the hand of a man who wrote the way he flew: no wasted motion, no second chances.

*Thom.* His father had been Thom to the people who loved him, Yarrow to the people who feared him, and *the mutineer* to everyone else. Isandro had been nine years old when the inquiry's findings came down like a verdict from a higher court — *First Officer Yarrow, having abandoned the Meridian in deteriorating conditions and taken the ship's boats with him, is judged to have deserted his post and his shipmates, and is hereby stripped of his certification* — and he had been thirty-one years old before he understood that a verdict was just a story told by the people who survived to tell it.

He broke the seal. The wax crumbled like old bread.

*My dear Elena,*

Isandro's breath caught. His mother. Elena Yarrow, who had died believing — or at least never saying otherwise — that her husband had done what the papers said he'd done. Who had raised her son under the name like a weather that never lifted.

*If you are reading this, then the sea has given back what I could not carry, and I am past explaining myself in person. I will be brief, because I have never been good at long goodbyes, and because I trust you to understand what I cannot make elegant.*

*They are calling it a mutiny. You will have heard it by now, and I will not insult you by pretending the word doesn't fit the shape of what they describe. Six men took the Meridian's boats out of a dying ship. Three came back. I was one of the three, and I was the one with the rank, and so I am the one who will hang for it in every newspaper from Ketchikan to Seattle.*

*But hear me, Elena, because you are the only one who ever heard me. The Meridian was lost before the storm. Mr. Greer's agents loaded her past every line in the Plimsoll mark, and when she began to ship water in the blow off the glacier coast, the owners' man aboard — Mr. Halloran, who signed the manifests — ordered the boats lowered and the cargo abandoned rather than the crew saved. I refused. I said we would go back for the men in the water. Halloran said the insurance would not cover a rescue attempt, only a loss. He said it in those words. I have written them in the logbook, which I have sealed in the captain's safe, and God willing the safe is as stubborn as the man who bought it.*

*We went back anyway. Three times. We pulled six men out of that water and we could not pull out the other three, and I have their names written here so that you will know them, because their families deserve to know that their husbands and sons did not die of my cowardice but of a cargo manifest. Their names are: Aksel Brandt, mate. Samuel Okafor-Ley, donkeyman. And the boy, Peter Rasmussen, seventeen years old, who had lied about his age to get aboard.*

Isandro stopped reading.

He sat up in the bunk, and the bunkroom seemed to tilt around him the way the world tilts when a plane drops into air nobody warned you about. He read the names again. *Aksel Brandt. Samuel Okafor-Ley. Peter Rasmussen.*

*Okafor.* He thought of Dr. Sefina Okafor, standing in the wreck's flooded hold with her camera and her clipboard, documenting a ship that had killed — or been killed with — a man who might have shared her name. It could be coincidence. Names travel; the sea scatters families the way it scatters wreckage. But the word *donkeyman* sat in his chest like a swallowed hook. He would ask her. Not tonight. Tonight there was only this.

He read on.

*Elena, I did not desert that ship. I went back into the storm for my shipmates, and when the boats were smashed and the owners needed someone to blame for the loss of their cargo, they chose the man who had disobeyed them. Halloran's statement to the inquiry was a work of fiction, and Greer signed it, and the board believed them because the board was made of men who own ships and have never drowned in one.*

*If I do not come home, do not let them make our son ashamed. Tell him his father went back. Tell him the water was black and full of ice and we went back anyway, because that is the whole of what a man is — not whether he lives, but whether he goes back.*

*There is money owed to us. Greer's company settled with the insurance underwriters on a fraud — I have seen the papers, double-claimed cargo, a ship written off as a total loss while half her manifest sat dry in a Juneau warehouse. I could not prove it then. I have put what I could in the safe. If the sea ever gives it back, give it to a lawyer who is not afraid of Greer, and there are few enough of those.*

*I love you. I loved you badly and at a distance, the way a man loves when his work is the horizon. Tell Isandro —*

The ink stopped there. Not faded out, not water-damaged — stopped, mid-word, the pen lifted and never set down again, because somewhere between that sentence and the next, the Meridian had gone down far enough, or the storm had come close enough, or the man writing had simply run out of the one resource no salvage operation can recover: time.

*Tell Isandro.*

Isandro sat in the dark of the bunkroom with the unfinished letter in his hands and did not move for a long time.

He had spent his whole life as the second half of that sentence. He had filled in the blank himself, a thousand times, in a thousand bitter drafts: *Tell Isandro his father was a coward. Tell Isandro not to follow him to sea. Tell Isandro the name is a debt.* He had built an airstrip on the edge of the map partly because it was the only ground he could afford and partly because it was the farthest point from Juneau's courtrooms where a Yarrow could still hold his head up. He had taken the medicine contracts nobody wanted because the villages at the end of them didn't read newspapers. He had made a life out of distance and called it freedom.

And all along, the answer had been sitting in a tin box under forty feet of ice, three miles from his own airstrip, waiting for the glacier to give it back.

*Tell him his father went back.*

Isandro pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. He was thirty-four years old and he was crying in a bunkroom that smelled of diesel, and he let himself do it the way you let a storm cell pass — no fighting it, just holding course until it moved on. He thought of his mother, who had died with the question still in her face. He thought of the boy Rasmussen, seventeen, who had lied about his age. He thought of Wren, who was probably seventeen herself and had lied her way aboard the Cormorant, and something in his chest turned over.

* * *

He read the letter twice more before dawn, and then he read the enclosure — a single page in a different hand, cramped and official, that his father had tucked inside the envelope like evidence in a trial. It was a copy of a settlement memorandum, *E. Greer & Associates, Marine Underwriting Division*, dated eleven months after the Meridian's loss, itemizing a payment of $40,000 to *Halloran, J., consulting services, salvage assessment* — a payment made, Isandro understood with a cold, clarifying fury, to the man who had ordered the abandonment, for services that could only have been the fiction of the inquiry itself.

*E. Greer.* Elias Greer. Isandro knew the name the way you know the name of a mountain that has killed people you loved. Greer Shipping had become Greer Marine, then Greer Consolidated; the founder had died rich and eulogized, but the son — *Aldous Greer* — was still alive, still in Seattle, still on the boards of three banks and a hospital and, if the gossip that came up the airways with the mail plane was worth anything, still quietly buying up salvage rights all along the coast. Still powerful enough that a bush pilot with a mortgaged airstrip was an insect against his windshield.

Still powerful enough, Isandro thought, to fund a salvage fleet.

He lay back and stared at the overhead and made himself follow the arithmetic to its end. Vess had hunted this wreck for thirty years — that was what he'd said over the radio, and men don't lie about the number of their obsessions. Thirty years. A fleet the size of the Cormorant's didn't run on quinine and silver; it ran on capital, and capital like that came from somewhere. Vess had a partner. Vess had always had a partner. And if Aldous Greer had spent three decades paying to keep the Meridian exactly where she was — or, no. Isandro turned it over. If Greer wanted her lost, she'd have stayed lost. Which meant Greer wanted something out of her. The safe. The logbook. The papers that could still, after all these years, hang him.

Which meant Vess wasn't hunting a wreck. He was hunting the same letter Isandro held in his hands.

And Isandro had signed for it. It was on Vess's manifest. Sooner or later — probably sooner, probably the moment the silver was secured and the divers had nothing left to risk their necks for — Vess would come down the manifest line to *Correspondence, personal, sealed, addressee: T. R. Yarrow*, and he would want to know why a bush pilot's name was on the most dangerous paper in Alaska.

The generator changed pitch. Somewhere above him, the anchor chain shifted as the tide turned, and the Cormorant swung at her moorings like a compass needle finding north.

Isandro folded the letter along its original creases, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and then — thinking of Wren, thinking of flooded holds and snagged lines and the sea's habit of taking back what it lends — he did not put it in his duffel. He slit the lining of his flight jacket, the one with the patched shoulder his mother had sewn, and slid the oilcloth packet flat against the lining, and closed the seam with three stitches from his emergency kit. It sat over his left breast like a second heartbeat.

Then he got out his logbook — his own, the one he'd kept since his first solo — and on a blank page at the back, in the tall narrow hand he'd inherited without ever meaning to, he wrote the three names: *Brandt. Okafor-Ley. Rasmussen.* Under them he wrote: *They did not die of my father's cowardice. They died of a cargo manifest.*

He was blowing out the headlamp when he heard the footsteps on the aft ladder — light, quick, deliberately loud the way Wren's footsteps always announced themselves, a stowaway's habit of never startling anyone who might shoot first.

"Skipper?" Her whisper came through the curtain. "You awake? There's a light out on the water. Been circling the last ten minutes. And Mara says it's not one of ours."

Isandro was already reaching for his boots, the stitches over his heart pulling tight, the letter riding against his ribs like ballast.

"Get Sefina," he said. "And wake Mara. I want to know who's flying at three in the morning over a fjord that's supposed to be sealed."

The light out on the black water swung once, twice — patient, methodical, a man counting boats — and went dark.
