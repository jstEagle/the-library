## 10. Wren's Secret

The Cormorant's engine room smelled of diesel and hot iron and the particular sourness of bilge water that had been sitting too long in a warm tank. Wren had decided, somewhere around hour two of her self-appointed watch, that she liked it better than any room she'd ever been in. It was honest down here. Everything made a sound when it was failing — bearings whined, pumps gurgled wrong, belts squealed their complaints before they snapped. Nothing in an engine room lied to you and smiled while it did it.

She sat cross-legged on a grating beside the port generator with her father's old multimeter balanced on her knee, its cracked case held together with electrical tape gone gray and soft. The needle swung lazy across the dial. She'd been pretending to check grounds for twenty minutes. Really she was listening to the ship.

The Cormorant talked all night if you let it. Hull popping against the ice in the fjord like knuckles cracking. The anchor chain ticking as the tide turned. Somewhere above, boots on deck plating — Vess's crew walking their rounds, heavy men moving heavy loads of other people's history. And under all of it, the generator's heartbeat, which was hers now, whether anyone aboard knew it or not.

Nobody knew. That was the whole architecture of her life these days: nobody knew.

She'd come aboard folded into a crate of spare impellers at Ketchikan, three days before departure, with a duffel bag, a stolen lanyard, and a forged work order she'd typed herself on the library computer using a font she'd matched from a real requisition slip. Fourteen years old and five foot one, she'd learned early that people saw what they expected to see, and what they expected to see in an engine space was a man's back and a man's hands. She'd worn her hair stuffed under a watch cap for the first two days until the chief engineer — a wheezing ex-trawlerman named Duff who drank his coffee through a straw because of his teeth — caught her re-packing a stuffing box and said, "You're either the best deckhand I never hired or the worst thief I ever met," and then, after a long look at her work, "Either way, hold this light."

That had been eleven days ago. Duff hadn't told Vess. Wren suspected Duff didn't tell Vess anything he didn't have to, including how much the starboard generator hated cold starts.

The needle on the multimeter jumped. Wren frowned and put her palm flat on the grating. Through the steel she felt it before she heard it: a stutter in the port generator's rhythm, a hitch like a skipped breath. She'd been hearing it for two days, fainter, and each time she'd told herself it was harmonic vibration off the hull ice. It wasn't getting fainter anymore.

She crawled under the fuel line bracket, cheek almost against the housing, and listened with her mouth open the way her father had taught her — *open your mouth, kid, your jawbone hears things your ears miss.* The stutter came again, and beneath it, a thin metallic whisper, high and wrong.

Injector pump. Number three cylinder, most likely. A scoring plunger, or a piece of something that shouldn't be there, riding where nothing should ride. Left alone it would gall the bore, then the bore would gall the plunger, and then some night when the temperature dropped and the whole fleet was leaning on its generators to keep the ice off the decks, number three would seize and take the governor with it, and the Cormorant would go dark in a fjord full of calving glaciers.

Wren lay there under the fuel line with her heart going hard, and thought about what she'd do with that information.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out in eleven days of listening: information was the only currency on this ship that couldn't be confiscated. Vess kept ledgers — Mara had said so, half-joking, in the passageway, not knowing Wren was wedged in the linen locker with a head full of rivet patterns. One ledger for money. One for grudges. Well, Wren had a ledger too, and hers had exactly one entry, written in her father's handwriting on the last page of his last logbook:

*Vessel: F/V Annelise. Seized by order of Meridian Salvage & Recovery, R. Vess, Master. Debt assessed: $41,300. Vessel sold at auction: $6,100.*

Her father had fished the Sound for nineteen years. He'd been a careful man, a boring man, a man who read tide tables at breakfast the way other men read the sports pages. He'd lost the Annelise not to weather or bad luck but to a salvage lien Vess had filed over a mooring dispute — a dispute Vess manufactured, Wren was nearly certain, because her father had once refused to dive on a wreck site Vess wanted stripped quietly, out of season, without permits. Her father had told the harbormaster. The harbormaster had told Vess. Six months later the Annelise was gone and her family was living in her aunt's garage, and her mother stopped singing in the kitchen, and her father started taking jobs on boats whose names he wouldn't say out loud, and then came the winter he went out on one of them and didn't come back, and the Coast Guard found only the wheelhouse door floating.

Leverage. That was why she was here. Not treasure. Not adventure. She'd read enough about Vess in eleven days of snooping through the ship's office at night — manifests, correspondence, a locked drawer in his cabin desk that Duff had once mentioned held "the real books" — to know that a man who kept two ledgers kept evidence in both of them. Somewhere aboard was paper that proved what he'd done to the Annelise, and maybe to other boats, other families, other winters. Wren meant to find it and take it home and make it into a hammer.

She hadn't counted on Isandro Yarrow.

* * *

He came down the ladder at 2300, which was itself strange, because pilots didn't wander engine rooms at 2300. He moved differently than the crew — quieter, weight on the balls of his feet, a man used to judging surfaces before trusting them. He stood at the bottom of the ladder for a moment, looking at the generators the way he probably looked at weather: reading it, deciding whether it was going to kill him.

"You're up late," Wren said, not moving from under the fuel line.

"So are you." His voice was low, roughened by the engine noise. "Duff says you've been down here every night since Ketchikan. Says you sleep in the tool crib."

"Duff talks too much."

"Duff says you're the best mechanic he's seen in thirty years and he can't figure out where you came from." Isandro crouched beside the grating, forearms on his knees. In the work light his face was all planes and shadow, the kind of face the interior carved out of people. "I've been wondering the same thing. So I checked the crew manifest."

Wren's stomach dropped through the deck plates. She kept her voice flat. "Find anything?"

"Forty-one names. None of them yours." He wasn't angry. That was the strange part. He sounded almost tired, and underneath the tired, something else she couldn't name yet. "No Wren. No last name. No emergency contact. You don't exist on this ship, officially."

"Neither does half of what goes on aboard it," she said. "I've read the office files."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, quickly banked. "Have you."

"I pick locks. It's not hard. Vess uses a padlock older than I am." She slid out from under the fuel line and sat up, wiping her hands on a rag that was already black. If he was going to throw her off at the next port, she wanted him to understand first that she wasn't cargo to be offloaded. "I'm not stealing anything. I'm looking for something specific. And before you ask — no, I won't tell you what, and no, I won't leave, so you can save us both the argument."

Isandro didn't argue. He lowered himself the rest of the way to the deck plates, sitting across from her with the generator humming between them like a third presence, and for a long moment he just looked at her — not the way adults usually looked at her, measuring how much trouble she was, but the way he looked at weather systems and wreck sites. Reading. Deciding.

"The port generator," he said finally. "It's missing. You hear it?"

Wren blinked. "Number three injector. Scoring plunger, I think. It'll gall within — "

"— forty hours, give or take, depending on load." He finished it for her, and the corner of his mouth moved, not quite a smile. "I flew freight for six years. Half my maintenance budget went to a Cummins shop in Fairbanks. I know that sound the way I know my own cough."

"It'll take the governor when it goes. Night seizure, cold snap, whole ship dark in the ice." She hesitated. "I could fix it. I'd need parts off the spares shelf — there's a rebuilt pump in crate fourteen, I've already checked. But if I touch it, Duff logs the repair, and the repair gets traced to me, and then Vess asks who I am, and then I'm on a dock in Ketchikan with my duffel bag and no ship."

"And whatever you're looking for stays locked in that drawer."

"Yeah."

Isandro nodded slowly. He picked up a bolt from the deck plates and turned it over in his fingers, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, more careful, like a man stepping onto river ice and testing each stride.

"My father's name was Elias Yarrow," he said. "You might have heard of him. Mutineer of the Meridian. That's what they called him, anyway — the papers, the owners, every barroom from Juneau to Seattle. Took the ship's boats and abandoned his captain and three crewmen in a storm, 1928. That's the story. My mother died believing a version of it. I grew up believing a version of it. Every debt I owe, every airstrip lease I can barely hold, every door that closed in my face — it all traces back to a story somebody wrote about my father, and I'm starting to think the people who wrote it are on this ship right now, eating good food and sleeping warm, while he's been dead and disgraced for fifty years."

Wren didn't say anything. The generator hummed. Somewhere above them, the ice groaned against the hull, long and slow, like the fjord clearing its throat.

"So when I ask you what you're looking for in Vess's drawer," Isandro said, "understand that I'm not asking as the ship's guest. I'm asking as somebody who knows what it is to want a piece of paper more than you want air."

Wren looked at him for a long time. She thought about her father's logbook, the last entry, the careful handwriting gone shaky near the end. She thought about the garage apartment, and her mother's silence, and the four years she'd spent being angry in a way that had nowhere to go. She had planned to trust nobody on this ship. Plans were allowed to change. That was what her father had said about weather, anyway.

"He ruined us," she said. "Vess. Four years ago. He filed a salvage lien on our boat over a mooring dispute that he made up, because my dad reported him for diving a wreck out of season. Forty-one thousand dollars in fake charges. They auctioned the Annelise for six thousand and kept the difference. My dad spent two years working boats he wouldn't name, trying to buy us back a hull, and then one winter he went out and—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish it. "They found the wheelhouse door. Just the door."

Isandro's face didn't do the thing she dreaded, the soft pitying thing adults did. Instead something hardened in it, quiet and total, like water freezing.

"So you stowed away to get the proof."

"I stowed away to get leverage. Proof, blackmail material, whatever works. He keeps two sets of books — Duff let that slip once, drunk. The real ones are in a locked drawer in his cabin. I've been waiting eleven days for a night when the ship's busy enough that nobody counts footsteps." She pulled her knees up. "I'm not a thief. I just want what's ours. What he took."

"That's not thieving," Isandro said. "That's salvage."

And Wren laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse — because it was true, and because nobody had ever made her feel like what she was doing was legitimate, and because this stranger with his dead disgraced father had just handed her the only word in the language that made her whole plan make sense.

* * *

They fixed the generator together that night, by lamplight, without logging it.

Isandro stood watch at the ladder while Wren worked — small hands, quick, sure, pulling the injector pump with a socket wrench wrapped in tape so it wouldn't ring against the housing. The rebuilt pump came out of crate fourteen. She swapped plungers in forty minutes, torqued everything to spec from memory, and bled the lines while Isandro watched her with an expression she decided not to comment on.

When she hit the starter, the port generator caught and settled into its rhythm, and the stutter was gone, and the heartbeat of the ship ran clean.

"There," she said, wiping her hands. "Good for another ten thousand hours, if anybody aboard deserves it."

"Doesn't deserve it," Isandro agreed. "But we need it. This ship is the only thing standing between me and that cave, and between that cave and my father's name." He paused. "Yours too, now. Whatever's in that drawer — if it touches the Meridian, it touches my family. We're after the same paper, Wren. Different pages, same book."

She studied him across the work light. He was maybe thirty, maybe thirty-five, with frost-cracked lips and eyes that had done too much squinting into glare, and he carried himself like a man who'd been alone so long that company was a skill he was having to remember. But when he'd talked about his father, his voice had cracked in exactly the place hers cracked when she talked about the Annelise. Same fracture line. Different wreck.

"If I get the drawer open," she said slowly, "and there's stuff in there about the Meridian — about what really happened — you get copies. First. Before I take what's mine."

"Why first?"

"Because you'll be gone when this is over. Back to your airstrip, your plane, your life. I've still got to live on the water, in towns where Vess's name means something. I need you gone and clean and loud about it, wherever you land. You're my alibi, Yarrow. Make your truth so big nobody has time to wonder about a missing girl and a missing drawer."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment, and then he did smile, fully this time, and it changed his whole face — made him look briefly like someone who hadn't been ground down by fifty years of other people's story about his family.

"You drive a harder bargain than Vess," he said.

"Vess never fixed your generator."

"No," Isandro said. "He didn't." He reached out and took the rag from her hand, folded it, set it on the workbench with a precision that struck her as oddly formal — a pilot putting away tools, closing out a flight. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we dive again, and tomorrow night, when the ship's busy counting silver, you and I are going to have a look inside that drawer together. Two sets of hands. Half the exposure."

"Half the blame, you mean."

"That too." He started up the ladder, then stopped, one rung down, and looked back at her. "Wren. Your dad sounds like he was a good man."

"He read tide tables at breakfast," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Every morning. Like the sea was a person he was being polite to."

Isandro nodded like that meant something to him — like it explained everything about her — and climbed out of the light.

Wren sat alone in the engine room afterward, listening to the generators run clean, both hearts beating steady, and felt something she hadn't felt in four years: not safe, exactly, but accompanied. The ship talked all night. For the first time since Ketchikan, she talked back — a low hum under her breath, matching pitch with the port generator, her father's habit, jaw open, hearing with her bones.

Above her, through the deck plates, she heard boots cross the overhead — heavy, deliberate, pausing directly above the engine room hatch. Pausing there a long moment. Then moving on toward the stern, toward the cabin with the locked drawer.

Toward the ledgers.

Tomorrow night, she thought. Two sets of hands. Half the exposure.

She just hoped neither of them understood yet what Vess kept in the second book — because Wren had read enough of the first one to know that men who write down grudges also write down debts, and somewhere in that drawer, she was suddenly certain, was a page with her father's name on it, and a page with Yarrow's, and maybe — the thought arrived cold, uninvited, certain as ice forming — a page describing exactly what the Cormorant had been sent to this fjord to find, and what Halloran's money had really paid for.

The generator hummed. The ice groaned. Wren clicked off the work light and sat in the dark, keeping the watch.
