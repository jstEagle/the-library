## 11. Tide Three: The Crack

Sefina Okafor had learned to read water the way other people read faces. Twenty years of it — first in the warm silt-brown rivers of her childhood, then in the cold archives of shipwrecks from Zanzibar to the Baltic — had taught her that every body of water carried a mood before it carried a current. The fjord outside the Cormorant's porthole was telling her something this morning, and she did not like its tone.

The water lay too still. That was the first wrongness. A tidewater glacier breathed; it exhaled meltwater all night and swallowed the tide twice a day, and a healthy fjord shivered with the exchange. This one had gone glass-flat, green-black, holding its breath.

The second wrongness was the color of the ice. She stood at the rail with her coffee going cold in her hands and watched the cave mouth across the water, that impossible cathedral arch the retreating glacier had exposed after a century of secrecy. The ice above the arch had been blue-white two days ago, dense and old. Now a seam of gray ran through it like a vein of bad metal, and where the gray showed, the surface had gone chalky, crazed with hairline fractures that caught the low sun and threw them back as a faint, dirty glitter.

She had seen that chalkiness once before, off South Georgia, three days before a shelf the size of a parish let go and took a research tender with it. Two men had died. She had written eleven pages about the warning signs afterward, pages nobody read because by then everyone was writing about the inquiry into why she'd refused to sign off on a donor's provenance papers, and science made such dull copy next to scandal.

"Doctor Okafor."

Vess's voice behind her. She didn't turn. She had learned that much about herself on the Cormorant: you didn't give Captain Vess your face until you knew what yours was going to say.

"Captain." She set the coffee down on the rail. "Your charges went in at four this morning."

"Six sticks. Placed per my dive plan. You were given a copy."

"I was given a copy, yes." She turned now, and gave him the face she'd chosen: flat, professional, the face of a woman reading instruments. "And I'm returning it with annotations. Captain, the ice above the wreck has changed structure since dive one. The freeze-thaw cycle we've had these last three nights, plus the vibration loading from your first two detonations — you've been ringing that shelf like a bell. What you have up there is no longer an ice bridge. It's a keystone with the mortar knocked out of it."

Vess was a big man gone lean the way old rope goes lean, all his mass drawn inward toward some harder core. He looked past her at the cave, and for one unguarded moment she saw something almost like grief cross his weathered face — thirty years of hunting this wreck lived in him somewhere, under everything else — and then it closed over, the way water closes over a dropped coin.

"The Meridian's hold is worth more than every building in Juneau," he said. "You know what's down there. Quinine alone, pre-war pharmaceutical grade, sealed tins — collectors pay by the ounce. Silver bullion. And the safe." His jaw worked. "We're not here to admire her, Doctor. We're here to open her."

"And I'm here to make sure there's a *her* left when you're done." Sefina stepped closer, kept her voice level, pitched for him alone. "Dive three can proceed. I'm not asking you to stop work. I'm asking you to halve the charge and move it forty meters east, off the fracture line, into the pressure ridge where the ice is thick enough to take it. You lose nothing but an hour."

"You lose an hour," Vess said. "I don't. Tide tables don't negotiate, and neither do I." He picked up her annotated dive plan, considered it for a long moment — long enough that she thought, absurdly, that she'd reached him — and then he folded it once, precisely, and dropped it over the rail into the black water. "My ship, my plan, my charges. Document whatever you like, Doctor. Just stay clear of the fall line when they fire."

He walked aft toward the dive station, calling for Halloran's men to get the skiffs moving, and Sefina stood at the rail and watched her annotations float and spread and sink, ink blooming off the page like a small dark wound opening in the fjord.

*Fall line,* she thought. As if ice fell in lines. As if a glacier were a building you could stand clear of.

* * *

By nine the dive station was organized chaos. The Cormorant anchored a quarter mile off the cave mouth, riding a swell so slight it was more suggestion than motion. Two skiffs ferried gear. Wren — the stowaway mechanic, sixteen years old and all elbows and competence — crouched on the fantail splicing a new air line, her hands moving with a speed that embarrassed the grown men around her. Isandro Yarrow checked his own kit in silence, methodical, a man who trusted equipment more than people and was learning, slowly, against his will, to revise that order.

Sefina worked at the folding table they'd given her, which wobbled on one shortened leg and which she had come to think of as an accurate portrait of her position aboard this vessel. Her camera housing sat open beside her slate. On the slate, in her cramped underwater shorthand, she'd logged the morning's survey targets: frame stations along the Meridian's port side, the cargo manifest stencils she'd found on the lower crates, the captain's cabin bulkhead where the safe waited behind a door furred with a century of ice.

Three dives in, and the Meridian still stopped her breath.

A salvage ship flash-frozen mid-sinking, held in the glacier's grip like an insect in amber — she had published papers on wrecks that lay in anoxic mud, wrecks preserved by cold and darkness, but she had never seen anything like this. The ship hadn't decayed. She had been *interrupted*. List hard to starboard, boats davits swung out empty, a coil of line still hanging off the rail where some man's hand had dropped it ninety years ago. Diving her felt less like archaeology than like trespassing in a photograph.

"Dr. Okafor." Isandro appeared at her elbow, voice low. "You argued with Vess this morning."

"I annotate. Arguing implies he listened."

"He doesn't listen. But he counts." Isandro glanced aft, where Vess stood over the charge crew. "He counted your argument. Whatever you said about the ice, part of it landed. He moved the firing point twenty meters east instead of ten."

"Twenty isn't forty."

"No," Isandro said. "It isn't." He looked at her then, directly, and she saw again what she'd seen the day they pulled him off the ice shelf: a man carrying weight he wouldn't name, distributing it through his body the way a mule distributes load. "If things go wrong down there — if the shelf starts talking — I want you topside. Not documenting. Topside."

"The documentation *is* the work, Mr. Yarrow. Everything else down there is theft with better manners."

"Everything else down there is also what's paying for your bottom time." He said it without heat, and without apology, and she hated him a little for being right. "Just — hear me. Ice doesn't warn twice."

"It warned twice off South Georgia," Sefina said quietly. "Nobody listened then either."

Isandro held her gaze a moment longer, and something passed between them that wasn't agreement but was adjacent to it — two professionals acknowledging the same arithmetic. Then the divemaster's whistle cut the morning, and the first pair went over the side, and the fjord closed over them without a ripple, still holding its breath.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble usually does, with success.

Sefina descended at slack water with the second pair, camera housing clipped to her harness, and the cave received her the way it always did — a slow dimming, then the blue-green gloom, then the Meridian rising out of it, vast and patient, her hull furred with anchor ice that glittered in their lights like a coat of crushed diamonds. Somewhere above, filtered through a hundred feet of glacier, daylight came down the color of old bottle glass.

She worked her stations. Frame twelve: the port rail, davits empty. Frame thirteen: the number two hatch, crates stacked inside like a library no one had opened. She shot the manifest stencils again because the first images had come back soft, and while her strobe recycled she drifted a moment in the enormous quiet, listening to the wreck breathe — the tick and groan of ice settling, the deep slow percussion of the tide working the hull.

That was when she heard the new sound.

Not a groan. A *crack* — high, sharp, singular, like a rifle shot fired through wool. It came from above and forward, from the ceiling of the cave itself, and every diver on the site froze simultaneously, five lights snapping upward in unison, five beams crossing in the murk like searchlights over a prison yard.

Above them, the underside of the ice shelf hung in sculpted folds, blue and white and beautiful. Along one fold, maybe sixty meters overhead, a crack had opened — a dark thread, thin as a hair, running laterally for what looked like thirty meters.

Then a second report, deeper, and the thread became a seam.

Sefina's hands were moving before her mind finished the sentence. She grabbed the nearest diver — one of Vess's contract men, big through the shoulders — and shoved the emergency ascent signal against his chest, jabbing it twice, three times, the agreed sequence. The man stared at her, then up, then understood all at once, and his own arm came up waving the signal wide, light swinging in great urgent arcs.

They rose together. Protocol said controlled ascent, rate limits, safety stops. The protocol lasted eleven seconds. When the third crack came — a sound like the whole sky tearing along a seam — discipline dissolved, and five divers kicked for the surface with the ungraceful desperation of fish fleeing a net, gear banging, bubbles boiling, Sefina clutching the camera housing to her chest with both arms like a child.

She broke the surface into chaos.

The cave's air space echoed with shouting. The skiffs were already hauling divers over their gunwales. And above them all, the ceiling was *moving* — not falling, not yet, but shifting, the great folds of ice flexing with a slowness that was somehow worse than speed, the way a glacier moves, the way continents move, a violence operating on its own timescale and indifferent to human urgency.

"Off the wall! Get off the wall!" someone was screaming, and Sefina realized the skiff driver was steering her boat away from the ice cliff at the cave's edge, out toward the open fjord, because everyone had understood at the same instant what came next.

The bergs came next.

The first one went with a boom she felt in her sternum rather than heard — a section of the shelf the size of a house simply detaching, tilting, and dropping, and the splash wave it threw raced across the cave and lifted the skiffs like toys in a bath. Sefina went to her knees on the deck plates, arms locked around the camera housing, and rode the surge up and down and up, spray cold as needles, watching through salt-blurred eyes as the calving spread along the ceiling like fire spreading along a fuse — another detachment, another, the cave shedding its skin in slabs, each impact a drumbeat, each wave stacking on the last.

And beneath the waves, below the surface, she could see the Meridian's superstructure flashing white as the surge rolled over her, the old ship shrugging under the blows like a sleeper struck in her sleep.

* * *

They got everyone out. That was the sentence Sefina repeated to herself, standing dripping on the Cormorant's deck an hour later, wrapped in a blanket she didn't remember taking, counting heads the way she'd counted frames: Yarrow, present. Wren, present, gray-faced, gripping the rail with both hands. Four contract divers, present. Divemaster, present. Herself, present. Everyone out.

But the camera housing —

She'd had it in her arms. She could feel the phantom weight of it still, the cold bite of the handles. Somewhere between the third wave and the skiff, between one drumbeat of falling ice and the next, it had torn free of her grip or she had released it — she would never know which, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound — and it had gone down into the green-black water, down past the flashing superstructure, down toward the deck of the Meridian, taking with it:

Frames one through fourteen. The manifest stencils. The hull survey. The structural baseline of the wreck, shot over three dives, the only complete photographic record in existence of a ship no one had seen in ninety years. Fourteen hours of bottom time. Six years of her professional redemption, weighed against a single grip strength, and found wanting.

She stood at the rail and looked back at the cave.

It was still there. That was the obscene thing. From a quarter mile, in the afternoon light, the arch looked almost unchanged — a little raggeder at the crown, a litter of new brash ice fanning out from its mouth on the ebb tide, bergy bits drifting down the fjord like the debris of a parade. Inside, though, she knew what the eye couldn't see from here: the seam running lateral, the flexing folds, the whole ceiling reorganized around new weaknesses. The Meridian's roof was failing, and every hour of melt and tide would widen the failure.

"Doctor Okafor."

Vess. Of course.

She turned. He stood a few feet away, and she catalogued him with the cold efficiency she saved for specimens: dry clothes, steady hands, eyes bloodshot but level. A man who had nearly lost five divers an hour ago and was already past it.

"Everyone's accounted for," he said. "Minor hypothermia, one twisted knee. Your diver — the big fellow, Kessler — he'll dive again tomorrow."

"There won't be a tomorrow inside that cave."

"There'll be as many tomorrows as I need." Vess stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and she gave him credit for one thing: he didn't perform sympathy. "I know what you lost down there. Your camera. Your survey work. I'm sorry for it — genuinely. But you're looking at this wrong. The shelf coming down isn't a disaster, Doctor. It's a deadline. It means the window is closing, which means we stop being careful and start being fast. Smaller teams, bigger charges, straight down to the hold and the safe. Strip what matters and get clear."

Sefina heard herself speak from a very great distance, in a voice she barely recognized as her own — low, precise, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Do you know what you've done? Not today. Today is just the invoice arriving. I mean the last three days. You rang a hundred-year-old ice shelf with explosives to save yourself hours, and now the roof is coming down on the most intact wreck of her era — a time capsule, Captain, a *one-of-a-kind* — and your answer is to blow it apart faster." She stepped toward him, close enough to see the fatigue under his composure. "In my profession we have a word for a man who destroys the thing he claims to love in order to possess it. We call him a looter. But you're worse than a looter, because a looter only robs the dead. You're gambling with the living. Five divers under that ceiling today. If that first slab had come down ninety seconds earlier — if the surge had caught anyone against the wall —"

"But it didn't."

"*Murder-by-geology.*" The words were out before she chose them, and once out she did not take them back. "That's what you're practicing, Captain. You don't pull the trigger yourself. You just arrange the conditions and let the glacier do the killing, and then you stand on the deck afterward and tell everyone how sorry you are. Three men died on the Meridian because owners made cheap decisions in warm rooms. How many die on *your* Meridian?"

The silence that followed had texture. Around them the deck crew had gone very still, pretending to work, listening to everything. Wren had stopped mid-task, a wrench frozen in her hand. Isandro stood by the dive locker, half-turned, watching.

Vess's face did something complicated — rage and something older underneath it, something that might have been grief wearing rage's clothing. When he spoke, his voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting.

"You think I don't know what's down there. You think I haven't spent thirty years knowing exactly what's down there, and who put her there, and why." His jaw tightened. "You want to document a tomb, Doctor. Fine. Document it. But understand what you're standing in. That ship has already killed once, and she's not finished, and the only question left is whether she takes her secrets with her when she goes." He turned away, then paused. "Tide four is in nineteen hours. Be on the dive station or be off my deck. Those are your choices."

He walked aft. The crew resumed moving, carefully, like people leaving a room where an argument has just ended in a draw.

Sefina stayed at the rail. The fjord was breathing again finally — she could see it now, the slow pulse of the ebb, the brash ice streaming seaward — and the cave mouth across the water gaped in the fading light like a held breath about to end.

A presence beside her. Isandro, saying nothing, offering a thermos. She took it. The coffee was terrible and hot and exactly right.

"She's right, you know," Wren said from the other side, too young to be included in this conversation and present anyway, the way she was present everywhere on this ship. "About the ceiling. I ran the numbers on the charge vibrations. It's not going to hold through tide four. Maybe not through tonight."

"Then we have a problem," Isandro said quietly, "bigger than the ceiling." He was looking not at the cave but at the stern, where Vess stood with Halloran's men, their heads bent together over something small and bright that caught the sun — a phone, Sefina thought, or a chart, or a ledger of some kind. "Because I don't think dynamite is the worst thing Vess is planning to use."

Across the water, the glacier groaned — a long, deep note, like a ship's horn sounding in fog — and shed another piece of itself into the darkening sea.
