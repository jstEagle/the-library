## 12. The Owner's Man

The seaplane came in on the morning of the fourth tide, and Isandro knew before it touched down that nothing good arrived in a machine that clean.

He was on the Cormorant's aft deck, coiling line out of habit more than need, when the sound reached him — not the rough cough of a working bush plane but the smooth turbine purr of money. The aircraft slid down through the fjord's gray light like a knife through oiled silk: a twin-engine amphibian, white hull unscratched, registration numbers fresh enough to read at two hundred yards. It flared over the water with the delicacy of something that had never been asked to survive a bad landing, kissed the surface once, and taxied toward the ship as if the ice-choked fjord were a private marina.

Wren appeared beside him, wiping grease from her hands onto a rag that had given up being blue sometime last week. "That's not Coast Guard," she said.

"No."

"That's not anybody who works for a living."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. "Go tell Mara. And stay out of sight after."

The girl hesitated — he could see the question forming behind her eyes, the same question he'd been chewing since he'd pocketed the letter three nights ago: *what are you hiding?* But she went, boots ringing on the wet deck, and Isandro turned back to watch the seaplane's hatch swing open.

The man who stepped out wore a coat that cost more than Isandro's annual fuel budget and wore it the way some men wear weather — without noticing it. He was sixty-odd, silver at the temples, with the tanned, unhurried face of someone who had spent decades being waited on. He came down the Cormorant's boarding ladder without asking permission, and Captain Vess met him at the rail.

That was the first thing Isandro marked. Vess — who had made Sefina sign a liability waiver before she was allowed to touch a crate lid, who barked at his own divers like a man driving mules — Vess *greeted* him. Not warmly. There was no warmth in it. But there was deference, folded flat and hidden the way a card player hides a hand, and Isandro had spent thirty years reading men across airstrip fences. He knew what he was looking at.

"Yarrow." The stranger said his name before anyone introduced them, and let it sit there, tasting it. "Isandro Yarrow. Your father flew the interior too, didn't he? Same routes. Same stubbornness."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Halloran. I represent certain interests." He extended a hand, and Isandro took it because refusing would have told the man more than any answer. Halloran's grip was dry and precise, a handshake like a receipt. "Certain interests who've been following this project with great attention. May we speak somewhere private?"

"The mess is occupied."

"I'd hoped so. It saves me finding you later." Halloran smiled, and it was a good smile, maintained the way his coat was maintained. "Walk with me instead. I find decks make honest rooms."

* * *

They walked the port side while the Cormorant's crew pretended not to watch. Ahead of them the glacier filled the head of the fjord, a wall of compressed centuries, blue-green and groaning. Somewhere inside it lay the Meridian, and somewhere inside the Meridian lay a letter that had been burning a hole against Isandro's ribs for three nights, even now, even folded in oilcloth in the inner pocket of a jacket he'd stopped taking off.

"You've been aboard four days," Halloran said conversationally. "You've made five dives' worth of useful observations, you've charmed my — forgive me — *Captain* Vess's divers into showing you the hold layout, and you've spent an unusual amount of time in the chart room with his daughter." He glanced sideways. "I do my homework. It's cheaper than mistakes."

"What is it you want, Mr. Halloran?"

"Straight to it. Good. Bush pilots always are — no time for pleasantries when the weather's coming." He stopped at the rail and looked out at the ice. "Do you know what quinine goes for, per crate, medicine grade, provenance documented? Neither did I, until this week. The number has several zeros in it. Do you know what a 1920s captain's safe sells for at auction, contents intact?" He shook his head admiringly. "More zeros."

"I'm not a salvager."

"No. You're a debtor." Halloran said it gently, which made it worse. "Your airstrip carries a second mortgage. Your Cessna needs an engine overhaul your mechanic described to a mutual acquaintance as — I'm quoting — 'a prayer with wings.' You fly medicine into villages because nobody else will, and you charge less than it costs you, which is noble and also ruinous." He turned, and his eyes were pale and patient, like a fisherman watching a line. "A man in your position doesn't get offered money often. When it happens, he listens. So listen."

The wind came off the glacier, cold enough to make the moment feel like a confession neither of them had made yet.

"There's a letter," Halloran said. "Written by the Meridian's captain in the last hours of the ship. Addressed to a pilot named Tomas Yarrow. I'm told it never reached him. I'm told it may be aboard still, or may have left the wreck recently, in a jacket pocket, in the possession of a man who's been very careful about where he puts his hands." He paused. "I'm not here to accuse. Accusation is for people with leverage they don't intend to pay for. I intend to pay."

"For a letter?"

"For a letter. Paper. Seventy-year-old ink. Worthless to anyone but a sentimentalist — and yet my principal wants it very badly, and my principal pays well for wanting." Halloran produced an envelope from inside his coat, thick as a sandwich, and set it on the rail between them. "Fifty thousand dollars. For one piece of paper, delivered to me before the sixth tide. No questions about how you came by it. No record. The owners' estate considers the matter closed and wishes it to remain closed."

The word *estate* landed strangely, and Isandro caught it. "Estate. I thought your principal was living."

Halloran's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly banked, like a lantern shuttered. "Figuratively. Men like my principal are institutions. Institutions don't die; they're succeeded." He tapped the envelope once. "Fifty thousand clears your mortgage. Overhauls your engine. Flies medicine all winter. All for paper that means nothing to you."

*It means everything to me.* Isandro kept his face still, the way he'd learned to keep it still over caskets and foreclosure notices and every other delivery bad news required. "And if I say no?"

"Then I'll assume you haven't found it yet, and I'll keep looking myself, and we'll part friends." Halloran shrugged, easy as a shrug could be in forty-knot wind. "But Yarrow — a word of professional courtesy, since you've been polite. Ask yourself why Captain Vess answered your Mayday within minutes, from a fleet that was already equipped for salvage, already provisioned, already *here*, eighty miles from anywhere, in a fjord his own charts show as solid ice." He leaned in slightly. "Vess didn't find this wreck. My principal found it — thirty years ago, in old survey photographs — and Vess has been on retainer ever since, waiting for the ice to give it back. Everything you've seen aboard this ship — the schedule, the dive order, the dynamite — was decided long before you crashed into the middle of it. You weren't rescued, Mr. Yarrow. You were *collected*."

The glacier boomed then, far off, a sound like a door closing in a very large house, and neither of them moved.

"Why tell me this?" Isandro asked quietly. "If Vess is your man, why burn him in front of me?"

"Because men serve whoever pays them most recently, and I'd rather you owed me than hated me. Because if you have the letter, I need you to *want* to sell it, and fear is a poor motivator compared to arithmetic." Halloran straightened his collar with two fingers, unhurried. "And because — call it sentiment — my principal has carried the weight of the Meridian affair for seventy years, and he'd like it to end cleanly. Whatever you think you know about your father, Mr. Yarrow, the history was written by survivors, and survivors write generously about themselves. Consider whether a letter written by a drowning man is really the more reliable document."

"He wasn't drowning," Isandro heard himself say. "He was going back for people."

Something crossed Halloran's face then — gone in half a second, but Isandro caught it, filed it: not surprise. *Recognition.* As if Isandro had confirmed something rather than revealed it.

"Yes," Halloran said softly. "So the story goes." He picked up the envelope and pressed it into Isandro's hand, closing the fingers around it himself, an intimacy that felt like a trap springing shut. "Keep it whether you sell or not. Consider it a retainer. Fifty thousand either way — but only fifty thousand *more* if the letter comes to me by the sixth tide. After that, the glacier closes, the fleet leaves, and whatever's inside that ice belongs to the ice." He stepped back toward the ladder. "Oh — and Yarrow. The daughter. Mara reads her father's mail. Be careful what you leave lying about, and be careful who you trust with what you can't afford to lose. On this ship, that's nobody."

* * *

He stood at the rail a long time after the seaplane lifted away, the envelope heavy in his jacket, heavier than paper had any right to be. Fifty thousand dollars. His father's name in a drowned man's ink. A captain bought and paid for. A navigator whose loyalty he'd started counting on. An archaeologist whose documentation might be the only honest thing in the fjord. A stowaway kid who trusted him.

Every alliance suspect. Every kindness possibly an invoice.

Below him, faintly, he heard Wren laughing in the engine room — real laughter, unpriced — and above him the glacier groaned again, closer this time.

In his pocket, the letter seemed to shift against his chest like something alive, and Isandro realized with cold clarity that he had exactly three tides to decide which truth he could afford to keep — and that Halloran, walking up the Cormorant's ladder an hour ago, had known about the letter before Isandro had ever spoken its name.

Which meant somebody aboard had already sold him.
