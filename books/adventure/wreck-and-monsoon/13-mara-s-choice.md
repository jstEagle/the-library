## 13. Mara's Choice

Mara Vess had learned to read her father's silences the way other navigators read weather. A silence could be a squall line building on the horizon, or it could be the dead calm that came before something worse. Tonight, in the chart room of the *Cormorant*, with Halloran's seaplane gone and the generator humming its tired note through the bulkheads, the silence was the second kind.

She found him at the big table, alone, a tumbler of whiskey untouched beside his elbow — which was itself a warning, because her father never poured a drink he didn't intend to finish. He was studying the fjord charts, but his hands were flat and still on the paper, not moving, not measuring, not doing any of the small restless things he did when he was actually working a problem.

"You met with Halloran," she said.

Vess didn't look up. "I meet with a lot of people."

"In my chart room. On my tide tables." She came around the table so she could see his face. Thirty years of salt and sun had carved it into something like driftwood, handsome once, now just weathered down to what would last. "He flew out of here at 0600. I watched him go. So don't tell me you meet with a lot of people."

Her father's jaw worked. For a moment she thought he'd give her the old stone face, the one he'd worn since her mother left, the one that said *this is captain's business*. But then his eyes came up, and what Mara saw in them frightened her more than anger would have. He looked tired. He looked *old*. And underneath the tiredness there was something she had spent her whole life refusing to name whenever she caught glimpses of it: shame.

"Sit down, Mara."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down." His voice cracked like ice underfoot. "Please."

That *please* undid her more than anything else could have. She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, arms folded, and waited.

"Halloran represents Aldous Wren—" no, that wasn't right, she corrected herself silently, "—represents the estate interests of the Meridian's surviving owners. One owner, actually. The others are dead. This one is very much alive, very rich, and has been funding this operation since before you were born."

"Funding us." The word came out flat. "The *Cormorant*? Our fuel contracts, our permits, the refit in Ketchikan two years ago?"

"All of it," Vess said. "Every plank."

Mara felt the deck tilt beneath her, though the ship was riding at anchor, steady as a church. "Since when?"

Her father took a long breath, and when he spoke again, the captain's voice was gone. What remained was just a man in a chart room at night, confessing.

"Since 1971. I was twenty-six. I'd lost my first boat, my first crew, everything, in a blow off Cape Spencer. I was drinking myself to death in Juneau when a man came to my table and bought me another round and told me he knew where the *Meridian* was."

"The *Meridian*." Mara's mouth had gone dry. "The wreck Yarrow's father—"

"The wreck nobody could find for fifty years because it isn't where any chart says it is." Vess turned one of the charts over, tapped the fjord, the glacier, the impossible cave. "The owners wrote her off. Insurance paid, salvage rights lapsed, everyone moved on. Except one owner didn't move on. Because the *Meridian* didn't sink where they said she sank, and what she was carrying when she went down doesn't appear on any manifest the insurance company ever saw."

"The quinine."

"The quinine. The silver. And papers." Her father's voice dropped. "Papers that could hang a man who is still alive, Mara. Still alive, still giving money to hospitals, still getting his name on buildings. That's who Halloran works for."

Mara sat very still. Outside, somewhere down the fjord, a berg shifted against the hull with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "And you've been looking for this wreck for thirty years. For him."

"At first I was looking for it for me." Vess laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "I thought I'd found the salvage of the century and I'd be rich by forty. Then I learned how the arrangement worked. I find the wreck, I get a finder's fee. A generous one. Enough to retire on, he said. Enough to buy back everything the sea took from me." He finally picked up the whiskey and drank half of it in one motion. "Thirty years, Mara. Do you know what thirty years of almost costs a man? Every spring we'd get a lead, every summer it would die. Meanwhile the checks kept coming. Fuel, permits, gear. All I had to do was keep looking and keep quiet about what I knew."

"What did you know?" she asked quietly. "About the sinking. About Yarrow's father."

Her father wouldn't look at her. "Enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have that won't make you do something stupid." He set the glass down with exaggerated care, the way he did when his hands wanted to shake. "Yarrow's boy is trouble. You know it, I know it. He's got some idea about his father, some letter or logbook fantasy, and if he gets hold of papers from that safe—"

"So it *is* true." Mara stood, the chair scraping back. "There are papers in that safe. Papers about the mutiny. About Elias Yarrow."

"Mara—"

"And Halloran wants them destroyed. That's why he's here. That's why the dynamite charges keep getting bigger. It was never about the cargo, was it? The quinine is a bonus. The silver is a bonus. He's here to burn the evidence, and you've been helping him."

For a long moment her father said nothing. Then, in a voice so low she had to lean across the table to hear it:

"He ordered the boats away, Mara. Back in '23. The storm, the third night. There were men in the water — survivors from a fishing schooner the *Meridian* had come alongside. Elias Yarrow wanted to go back for them. The owner's representative aboard countermanded him. Ordered the ship to run on. Three of those men were our own crew who'd gone over the side on lifelines trying to reach the swimmers." Vess's eyes were wet and furious. "Then the ship foundered anyway, in the ice, and the owners needed someone to blame. They needed their insurance story clean. So they made the man who'd tried to save lives into the man who'd stolen a ship. Mutiny. It's been on his grave thirty years, and the man who put it there sends me a Christmas card every December with a check inside."

Mara's legs gave out and she sat back down. She thought of Isandro Yarrow's face when he talked about his father — that careful blankness, the way he changed the subject, the way he flew like a man with nothing to lose and everything to prove. She thought of the letter she'd seen him slip into his jacket during the first descent, and said nothing about it, even now. Even to herself.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because Halloran's coming back tomorrow with more men and bigger charges, and because you're going to figure it out anyway, and because—" her father's voice broke, actually broke, on the words— "because I'm tired, Mara. God help me, I'm sixty-one years old and I'm tired, and I told myself for thirty years that when we found her I'd finally be free. And now we've found her, and all I can see is the check. And I hate myself for it. But I'm too far in. If I stop now, everything I was — everything *we* were — was for nothing."

Mara looked at her father, this man who had taught her to box compass bearings before she could ride a bicycle, who had named her after the star he steered home by, who had loved her mother and lost her and never learned how to say so. She loved him. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. She loved him, and he had sold thirty years of his life — and three dead men's memory, and an innocent man's name — for a bounty that had never once been paid, because the wreck had never been found until a bush pilot with his father's eyes stumbled into a melting cave.

"Dad," she said. "The tide tables."

He frowned. "What about them?"

"The ones you posted in the wheelhouse. The window. Six tides, you told everyone. Six tides before calving season seals the fjord." She held his gaze. "I ran the numbers myself, Dad. Twice. With the current year's lunar data and the glacier survey benchmarks. The real window is four tides. Maybe five if we're lucky. You've been posting tables that give us two extra days we don't have."

Something flickered behind her father's eyes — guilt, confirmation, or both. "Halloran's schedule runs on six tides," he said. "His demolition plan needs six."

"His demolition plan is going to kill somebody." Mara stood again, and this time she didn't sit back down. "Those charges are already fracturing the shelf above the cave. Sefina Okafor documented it — before her work was flooded out, conveniently. Wren nearly drowned on dive one. And you're going to let Halloran set charges big enough to bring the whole ice wall down on top of divers, to destroy papers that would clear a dead man's name, and then collect your retirement check from the man who killed three sailors and framed a hero."

"Watch your tone."

"No." Her voice didn't rise; it dropped, which was worse, and she saw him register that. "You don't get to pull rank on me for this one, Dad. Not for this."

She walked to the chart drawer where she kept her own working copies — the honest numbers, the ones she'd been keeping in a locked folio for two weeks, telling herself she'd show him when the time was right, telling herself there had to be an explanation. There had been an explanation. It was worse than the one she'd feared.

She took the folio out. Held it against her chest. Looked at her father across the lamplit table, at the wreckage of a thirty-year bargain spread out between them like flotsam.

"If you walk out that door with those," Vess said quietly, "don't come back."

Mara stopped with her hand on the latch. She had imagined this moment, in the dark watches of the night watches, and in every version she had cried, or shouted, or begged him to come with her. In none of them had he said *don't come back*. In none of them had he chosen the check so completely.

"You taught me to steer by Polaris," she said. "You said a navigator's job is to know where true north is when everything else is lying to you." She opened the door. Cold air rolled in off the fjord, smelling of ice and kelp. "This is me knowing, Dad."

Behind her, she heard the whiskey glass set down empty, and her father say nothing at all.

* * *

The tender crossed the black water toward the shore camp with the folio zipped inside her jacket, against her heart, and Mara kept her eyes on the running lights and let herself shake only when the engine noise covered it.

Isandro was waiting on the shingle beach beside his plane, a lantern at his feet turning the gravel orange. Wren was with him, cross-legged on a crate, rewiring something with her teeth clamped around a flashlight. They both looked up as the tender grounded.

Mara stepped out into ankle-deep water and walked up the beach, and found that every speech she'd rehearsed had evaporated. So she just unzipped the jacket, pulled out the folio, and held it out.

"Real tide tables," she said. "The window is four tides, not six. Your father's letter, the logbook, whatever else is in that safe — if you want it, you've got less than two days, and Halloran's next charge goes in tomorrow at slack water."

Isandro took the folio without opening it. He looked at her for a long moment, and there was no triumph in his face, only a kind of grim recognition — one orphan of other people's decisions meeting another.

"Your father?" he asked.

"On the *Cormorant*," Mara said. "With his whiskey and his thirty years." Her voice held until the end, and then, despite everything, it didn't quite. "He told me not to come back."

Wren got up off the crate without a word, crossed the gravel, and put a hand on Mara's shoulder — a fifteen-year-old mechanic's hand, grease-blackened, steady as a cleat. Isandro set the folio down on the crate and opened it, and the lantern light fell across the honest numbers, the true window, the narrow closing door of the fjord.

"Four tides," he said softly. Then he looked up at Mara, and past her at the dark water where the *Cormorant* rode at anchor, and his jaw set the way she was beginning to learn it set when he'd stopped calculating and started deciding.

"Then we'd better get the safe open tonight," he said. "Because at slack water tomorrow, Halloran's men are going to find this cave empty — and I intend to be gone before the fifth tide ever comes."
