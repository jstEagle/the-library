## 14. Tide Four: Running the Fjord

The tide tables Mara had smuggled off her father's desk said they had thirty-six hours. The glacier said otherwise.

Isandro stood on the salt-crusted deck of the *Cormorant*'s tender at first light and watched the fjord close like a fist. Overnight, a shelf of ice the size of a church roof had calved from the eastern wall and now lay across half the channel, blue as stained glass, shedding meltwater in a hundred silver threads. Where yesterday he could have flown the tender through with his eyes shut, today there was a gap no wider than a barn door — and beyond it, the fuel cache at Cache Point Two sat stranded in a widening lagoon of brash ice.

"Thirty-six hours," he said to Mara, who stood beside him with her binoculars raised, her breath smoking.

"The table says thirty-six." She lowered the glasses. Her face was pale in the flat gray light, and there was a bruise of sleeplessness under each eye. "The table also said we'd have until tide six. My father has been shaving the margins since before you got here. He wants the safe up before Halloran's next plane comes in, and he doesn't care what the water does after that."

Isandro looked down the length of the tender — forty feet of riveted aluminum, a diesel engine Wren had coaxed back to life twice already, and a cargo deck stacked with empty drums meant to be filled at the caches and ferried back to the ship. Without those drums, without that fuel, nothing left this fjord. Not the *Cormorant*, not the seaplane, not any of them. Vess understood that even if he pretended not to; it was why he'd finally agreed to the cache run when Isandro laid it out on the chart table the night before, tapping the narrowing channel with one finger while Vess glowered over his coffee like a man being told his own funeral arrangements.

"You fly it," Vess had said at last, which was as close to trust as the old man came. "You know the ice. But you take my daughter's numbers, not your guesses."

"My guesses are better than his numbers," Mara had said quietly, and for once nobody argued.

Now she handed him the binoculars and pointed. "Gap's holding. Maybe ninety meters wide at the narrows. Brash is moving south with the ebb, so go with it, don't fight it. And Isandro—" she caught his sleeve, "—the wall above Cache Two. Look at it before you tie off. If the ice above the cache is fractured, you don't stop. You come home empty and we figure out something else."

"And if the fuel's the only thing between us and walking out of here?"

"Then we walk," she said, "and live to hate him properly."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was something in her voice these last two days — a looseness, a willingness to say aloud the things she'd only thought before — that was new, and fragile, and he handled it the way he handled anything fragile: carefully, and without looking at it too long.

Wren came up the companionway two at a time, hair stuffed under a wool cap, a wrench still in one hand and grease to the elbow. "She'll run," the girl announced, patting the engine housing like a horse's neck. "Third time's the charm. I re-seated the raw-water impeller and bypassed the corroded fitting with hose clamp and prayer."

"Prayer's not load-bearing," Isandro said.

"It is on this boat." Wren grinned, then sobered, glancing at the ice wall across the channel. Behind her, Sefina climbed down into the stern with a waterproof case hugged to her chest — her rebuilt documentation kit, third incarnation, everything from the flooded cabin dried page by page over the galley stove and copied out again in her small fierce handwriting. She'd slept four hours in two days. She looked like a woman carrying her own funeral pyre and intending to light it herself rather than let anyone else do it.

"All right," Isandro said. "Mara navigates. Wren on the engine. Sefina rides ballast and keeps her head down. We take the outer channel past the narrows, top both caches, and we're back inside twelve hours or we're not back at all. Questions?"

"Yeah," Wren said. "Why do we keep going back into the thing that's trying to eat us?"

"Because it's the only way out," Isandro said, and pushed off from the *Cormorant*'s hull.

* * *

The fjord at slack water was a held breath.

They ran slow at first, the tender's bow shouldering through loose brash with a sound like breaking crockery, and Isandro kept his eyes moving the way his father had taught him to read weather — not at the thing in front of you but at the edges of it, the places where change announces itself early. The western wall was old ice, gray and slumped, trustworthy in the way of things that have already fallen as far as they can fall. The eastern wall was the killer: clean-faced, under-cut at the waterline by three decades of warming water, hung with seracs that leaned out over the channel like drunks over a railing.

"There." Mara pointed to a seam high on the east wall, a dark line running laterally for a quarter mile. "That wasn't there yesterday. That whole face is hanging on by the seam."

"Then we hug the west side and we're quiet," Isandro said, and eased the throttle back until the engine was a mutter instead of a shout. Sound traveled on ice. Everyone aboard knew it; nobody said it. They moved through the cold like trespassers in a church.

The narrows took twenty minutes that felt like twenty years. The gap between the calved shelf and the west wall was exactly as wide as Mara had called it — ninety meters, no more — and the current funneled through it hard enough to carry the tender sideways if he let it. He didn't let it. He came in quartering, bow into the set, letting the ebb do half the work while Wren feathered the engine on his hand signals, and the walls of ice slid past close enough on either side that he could see the bubbles frozen in them, thousands of years old, rising in columns like trapped prayers.

Sefina watched the bubbles the whole way through, her hand pressed flat against the glassy blue as they passed. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Isandro had begun, without deciding to, to understand her silences — the ones that meant grief, the ones that meant fury, and the rarest kind, the ones that meant wonder so large it crowded out every other feeling she owned. This was the third kind. He found himself glad, absurdly, that she was there to have it.

Then they were through, and the channel opened into the upper reach, and Cache Point Two came into view around the shoulder of a moraine spit — and Isandro's stomach dropped.

The cache was intact. The drums were lashed where he'd left them, tarped against the melt. But the ice wall above them had changed overnight from a wall into an argument. A fracture ran diagonally across the entire face, wider than a man's shoulders in places, and below it the ice had begun to slump, a bulge the size of a house pushing slowly outward over the beach where the drums sat.

"Mara," he said.

"I see it." Her voice was very level. "Bulge is maybe sixty meters above the cache line. It goes, everything below it goes into the water, and the wave takes the drums with it."

"How long?"

"If I knew that, I'd be rich." She was already re-plotting, pencil scratching. "We take what we can carry and we don't linger. Fifteen minutes on the beach. Fifteen."

They went in fast. Wren threw lines, Isandro backed the tender onto the shingle, and they worked the drums down the beach in pairs — two hundred liters apiece, rolling them on their rims, the metal burning cold through gloves. Sefina hauled with the rest of them, case abandoned in the boat, her scholar's hands blistering on the drum rims without complaint. Nobody spoke except to count. Four drums. Six. Eight. The bulge overhead creaked once, a long groaning note like a bowed string, and everyone froze, and the mountain of ice said nothing more, and they went back to counting.

Twelve drums loaded. Isandro was casting off the second lash line when he heard it.

Not a crack. Something worse — a hiss, deep and building, from the base of the wall where the meltwater ran. He'd grown up around glaciers the way other kids grew up around highways, and he knew the sounds the way other men knew engines. Water finding a new path was a whisper. Water finding a *big* path was a roar. This was neither. This was the sound of the plumbing behind the wall rearranging itself, and it meant the bulge was no longer resting on solid footing.

"Off the beach," he said. "Now. Leave the lashings."

Wren didn't argue, which frightened him more than the hiss did. She scrambled aboard, Mara cast off, and Isandro brought the tender around hard with the current just as the wall exhaled.

It didn't fall so much as pour. The bulge detached along the diagonal seam and came down in a single sliding mass, hitting the shallows with a concussion he felt through the soles of his boots, and the wave it threw ahead of itself was a gray-green cliff, five meters high, moving faster than the tender could ever hope to.

"Can't outrun it!" Mara shouted, already reading the water. "Beam-to! Beam-to and ride it!"

He understood in a flash what she meant — you couldn't beat a calving wave head-on or flee it from behind; you turned your beam to it, throttled into it as it passed under you, and prayed the boat surfed instead of rolled. He swung the wheel, Wren slammed the throttle full forward, and the tender leapt like a startled animal just as the wave arrived.

The world went vertical. Then sideways. The port rail buried, green water over the deck, drums tearing loose from their lashings — one gone over the side instantly, its tether snapping like thread — and Isandro had one arm hooked through the wheel and one hand fisted in Wren's collar as the boat climbed the wave's face, hung at the crest for a heartbeat with the whole ruined fjord laid out below them, and came down the far side in a spray of white that hammered the deck like hail.

And then the engine died.

* * *

Silence, except for the water. The tender wallowed in the aftermath chop, listing, taking green water over the transom in lazy gulps. Ahead of them the wave spent itself against the moraine spit in a long thunder of white. One drum bobbed fifty meters astern, going away from them with the ebb.

"Wren." Isandro was already moving. "Talk to me."

She was under the engine housing before he finished the sentence, wrench in hand, hair plastered to her skull, and when she spoke her voice came out muffled and furious. "Fuel starvation. The wave hit the starboard quarter and the pickup line — the pickup line let go. It's torn off at the tank fitting."

"Can you splice it?"

"Not with the tank sloshing. Every time I get a clamp near the fitting, a liter of diesel comes out with it and the bilge is already—" She stopped. He heard her thinking, that rapid mechanical clicking he'd learned to listen for. "Unless I don't use the pickup at all."

"Meaning what?"

She surfaced, grease-streaked, eyes lit with the particular madness he was coming to recognize as her best quality. "Gravity feed. Off the reserve drum. We've got eleven drums lashed amidships — I tap one directly, run a line over the engine, straight into the filter housing. No tank, no pickup, no problem. It'll run rough and it'll die the second that drum empties, but the drum holds two hundred liters and Cache One is—" she looked at Mara.

"Nine kilometers," Mara said. "Downstream. With the ebb."

"So we float it," Isandro said. "Do it."

What followed was fifteen minutes of the strangest surgery Isandro had ever witnessed. Wren stripped the fuel line from the dead tank, jammed the free end into the bung hole of a reserve drum wedged upright against the engine mount, wired the drum to the rail with two lashings and a belt off her own waist, and primed the line by mouth — spitting diesel and swearing with a fluency that made Sefina raise her eyebrows — until the filter housing gurgled full.

"Crank it," Wren said.

Isandro hit the starter. The engine coughed, caught, coughed again, and settled into a ragged, hiccupping roar that sounded like a walrus with grievances. The whole boat shuddered. The drum rocked against its belt. Wren knelt beside it with one hand steadying the improvised feed and the other still holding the wrench, and she was grinning like a jack-o'-lantern.

"She lives! Ugly, but she lives!"

"Ugly counts out here," Isandro said, and put the helm over for Cache One.

They ran the nine kilometers with Wren kneeling at the drum like a priestess at an altar, occasionally adjusting the wire, occasionally thumping the drum when the feed air-locked, the engine missing and surging beneath her hands. Sefina wrung out her documentation case and discovered, to her visible relief, that the oilcloth had held. Mara stood at the bow reading the water, calling the sets of the swell, and somewhere in the middle of the run Isandro realized that none of them had given an order in twenty minutes. The boat was simply running itself through four people who each knew their part — Wren keeping the heart beating, Mara reading the road, Sefina guarding the record, himself holding the wheel — and the knowledge settled into him with a warmth that had nothing to do with the diesel stove.

His father had flown alone for thirty years. Isandro was beginning to understand what the old man had actually lost, beyond the name and the license. He'd lost this. The crew in the boat. The wordless machine.

Cache One sat in the lee of a gravel bar, untouched, its wall behind it old and gray and honest. They topped the remaining drums, transferred fuel, replaced Wren's gravity-feed rig with a proper spare line she'd cannibalized from the ship's stores, and ate cold biscuits and tinned salmon while the ebb carried them home. The sun never quite rose; it hovered at the horizon like a promise someone kept making and kept breaking, gilding the ice walls pink for ten minutes at a time.

On the return run through the narrows, the gap had narrowed again. Seventy meters now. By tomorrow, fifty. By tide six, nothing at all.

* * *

Vess met them at the gangway with Halloran's schedule written all over his face. "Fuel's topped?" was the first thing he said. Not *are you alive*, not *good work*. Isandro had expected nothing else and was still disappointed, which he supposed said something about hope's stubbornness.

"Topped," he said. "Both caches. Minus one drum to the fjord."

"One drum." Vess's jaw worked. "One drum is two hundred liters, Yarrow."

"One drum is also the price of doing business with a glacier," Mara said, stepping past her father with a drum dolly. "You're welcome for the other eleven."

Vess looked at her — really looked, for the first time in days, Isandro thought — and whatever passed across his face was gone too quickly to name. He turned back to Isandro. "Halloran's plane comes tomorrow with the blasting gel. After the safe comes up, we're done with caution. I want the hold stripped and the *Cormorant* loaded inside two tides."

"Two tides," Mara repeated flatly. "From the real tables or yours?"

"Mine," Vess said, and walked up the gangway.

Isandro watched him go, then looked at Mara. She was watching her father's back with an expression he'd seen on survivors reading wreckage — cataloguing, grieving, calculating all at once.

"He knows the window's shorter than he's telling Halloran," she said quietly. "Which means either he thinks he can beat it, or he doesn't plan to be aboard when the math comes due." She turned to Isandro, and her voice dropped further. "The safe comes up tomorrow. Dive five. Whatever's in it — the logbook, the insurance papers, whatever your letter points to — that's the moment everything changes hands. Be ready."

Below them, in the engine room, Wren's voice drifted up through the deck plates, singing something tuneless and triumphant to the machinery she'd beaten back to life. Sefina was already at the rail, drying her rescued pages in the last pink light, guarding the record the way she guarded everything — fiercely, and alone, and no longer quite alone, though Isandro doubted she'd noticed yet.

Tomorrow the safe. Tomorrow the truth, or whatever pieces of it the ice would surrender.

He went below to check the fuel manifest, and to sit for a moment with the letter against his chest, and to try not to hear, underneath the engine room song, the glacier groaning in the dark outside like something counting down.
