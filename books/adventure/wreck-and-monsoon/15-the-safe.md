## 15. The Safe

The cold in the flooded hold had a texture to it, Isandro thought — not the clean cold of altitude he'd lived with all his life, but a wet, patient cold, the cold of something that had been waiting thirty years and could wait thirty more. He hung from the dive line at the edge of the air pocket, his lamp cutting a cone through water the color of old bottle glass, and watched the divers work the captain's safe free of its cradle of rust and ice.

Five dives. Five tides spent inside a ship that had been his father's coffin before Isandro was born. The *Meridian* lay on her beam ends in the flooded lower hold, her deck plates furred with silt, her brass fittings still showing threads of gold where the glacier's grip had polished them clean. Above them, forty feet of ice. Above that, a fjord that was running out of patience.

"Slings are set," Halloran's lead diver said, his voice flattened by the comm unit into something insectile. "Ready to lift."

Isandro checked his gauge, checked the line, checked the ceiling of ice where hairline fractures had been spreading since dive three — since the dynamite, since Vess had decided that patience was a currency he could no longer afford. The fractures looked like river deltas seen from the air. They looked like the end of everything, drawn in white on blue-black.

"Copy," he said, and gave the signal.

* * *

Vess stood on the ice shelf above the dive hole with his hands jammed in his coat pockets and his eyes on the water, and he thought about his father teaching him to read tide tables off the back of a salmon boat, forty years and one lifetime ago. The old man had said: *the sea doesn't lie, boy. It's the men who sail her that lie.*

Thirty years he'd hunted this wreck. Thirty years of charts and sonar sweeps and dead-end rumors, of mortgages signed and marriages lost, of a ledger in his cabin with two columns — one for what the *Meridian* owed him in silver and quinine, one for what she owed him in blood. Three men. Okafor didn't know the names. His daughter didn't know the names. Isandro Yarrow didn't know the names, though the boy's father had known them, had carried them into the storm like ballast.

The winch groaned. The safe came up through the hole in a cage of rust and barnacle, streaming water that steamed faintly in the cold, and the men on the ice surged toward it the way men surge toward anything that glitters.

"Back," Vess said, and they backed. "Nobody touches it until it's on the deck and dry. Nobody."

Halloran's man — the one called Deitz, with the flat eyes and the shoulder holster he thought nobody had noticed — didn't back. He stood at the edge of the hole with his arms crossed, and Vess read the posture the way he read weather: a front was coming in.

"Mr. Halloran's instructions," Deitz said, "were that the safe comes to him. Unopened."

"The safe comes to my deck," Vess said, "because it came out of my water, on my charter, with my divers and my daughter's tide tables and that boy's plane. Mr. Halloran can look at it through the bars of his own cage."

Deitz smiled without moving anything but his mouth. "You want to think about that, Captain."

Vess thought about it. He thought about the first column of his ledger and the second, and about how, somewhere over the last ten years, he'd stopped being able to tell which column he was living in.

"Take it to the hold," he said. "And Deitz — if you or any of Halloran's men come near it before I open it, you'll swim home."

* * *

They cracked the safe in the *Cormorant*'s forward hold, under work lights, with the whole strange crew of them crowded in a semicircle: Vess with the cutting torch, Mara with her arms folded and her face arranged into neutrality, Sefina with her camera and her notebooks and her hunger barely leashed, Wren perched on a crate with a wrench across her knees like a talisman, and Halloran's men at the edges of the light, three of them now, since the seaplane.

Isandro stood where he could see everything and be seen by no one in particular. The letter rode against his chest, inside three layers of wool and oilskin, where it had ridden for two days. He'd read it a dozen times by lamplight in his bunk. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat. But a letter was a voice. A letter could be called a forgery, a fantasy, a son's grief dressed up as evidence. What it needed was a witness.

The safe's door peeled back with a shriek of tortured metal and a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were two things.

The first was a ledger-bound book, its cover stamped with the *Meridian*'s name and the year 1927. The captain's logbook. Isandro's hands shook when he took it — actually shook, and he was a man whose hands did not shake on a stick in crosswind — because he could feel through the oilcloth the weight of a hundred pages of handwriting, and somewhere in that handwriting was his father's name written by a man who had no reason to lie.

The second was a steel document case, and inside the case, bundled in rubber bands that had fused to paper, were insurance papers. Bills of lading. A telegram, yellowed to the color of weak tea. Isandro read the telegram twice, then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense and then, all at once, arranged themselves into something worse than sense.

*ABANDON AND SCUTTLE STOP INSURANCE SETTLEMENT PREFERABLE TO SALVAGE RISK STOP BOATS TO BE WITHDRAWN BEFORE SECOND TRIP STOP*

The owners had ordered the abandonment. Had ordered the boats pulled before the last run, before the three men — the three men Vess counted in his second ledger — had gone down with the cargo they'd been told was worth saving. And then the owners had blamed the man who'd taken the boats back in anyway.

His father hadn't been a mutineer. He'd been the only man aboard who'd disobeyed the order that killed three people.

"Yarrow." Vess's voice, low. "You've gone white as the glacier. What is it?"

Isandro closed the case. "Nothing. Shipping records. Routine."

He was a good liar — thirty years of flying freight and gossip into the interior had taught him that most of what people said was cargo they were trying not to drop — but Vess had spent thirty years hunting this wreck and could smell a hidden hold through a hull. His eyes narrowed. He said nothing.

* * *

Vess lay awake in his cabin that night with the logbook open on his bunk and a bottle of rye open beside it, and he read by lamplight the account of the last voyage of the *Meridian*, and he drank, and he did not weep, because he'd spent the tears for these three names thirty years ago and had none left.

*October 14. Wind rising from the southeast. Owners' wire received. Captain's note for the record: I do not intend to leave men on this ship to satisfy an insurance ledger.*

*October 15. Yarrow has taken the boats back in against my order and against the owners'. God forgive us both, whichever of us is right.*

Yarrow. His father. The mutineer of the *Meridian*, the man whose name was a curse in every harbor bar from Ketchikan to Juneau — and here it was, in the captain's own hand, written as the only man who'd gone back.

Vess set the book down. He picked it up. He read the passage again.

He had spent thirty years hating Elias Yarrow. He had built the second column of his ledger on that hatred, mortared it stone by stone, and it had held up his whole life the way a keel holds up a boat. And now the keel was gone, and Vess lay in the dark of his cabin feeling the whole structure of himself roll in a swell with nothing underneath.

The boy. Isandro. The boy had gone white as the glacier over those shipping records. The boy had the look of a man holding something in the exact place where a man would hold a letter.

Vess got up. He took the logbook, wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it in the safe in his own cabin — the real safe, not the ship's. Then he sat back down with the rye and did not drink, and thought about Deitz's shoulder holster, and about the two columns, and about which one he would die in.

* * *

At two in the morning, Isandro came back for the documents.

He told himself it was for copies — photographs, Sefina's camera, a record that couldn't be seized or burned or lost. He told himself he wasn't stealing, that the papers belonged to the truth and the truth belonged to everyone. He told himself a lot of things at two in the morning. He was a pilot; he knew what it was to hold a heading in weather that argued otherwise.

The *Cormorant*'s hold at night was a cathedral of shadow and machinery, the work lights doused, the diesel generators breathing in their sleep. Isandro moved through it the way he'd moved through his father's old boathouse as a boy, knowing where the boards creaked. The document case was where they'd left it, lashed under the netting with the quinine crates. He cut the lashing, lifted the case, and set Sefina's camera on his knee, and the shutter's click was very small and very loud.

"You always photograph other people's property at night, Yarrow?"

The voice came out of the dark behind him, and with it the cold circle of a muzzle against the back of his neck.

Isandro went still. His heart did not race; it did the opposite, went slow and enormous, each beat a tide coming in. "Careful with that," he said. "Trigger's cold. Metal shrinks. It'll go off before you mean it to."

"I've been meaning it for two days." Vess stepped around into the dim light, and his face was gray with something that wasn't the lamplight. "The telegram. What did it say?"

"You read the logbook."

"I read the logbook." Vess's hand didn't waver. "I asked what the telegram said."

Isandro set the camera down, slowly, and turned. "You've known for thirty years that the boats were pulled early. You told me the storm took them. You told everyone the storm took them."

"The storm took them," Vess said. "The order put them in the storm's way. There's a difference, and I've had thirty years to learn it, and it hasn't once made the water any warmer." He stepped closer, and Isandro could smell the rye on him and, under it, the salt of an old grief. "The papers. Give them to me."

"And do what with them, Vess? Hand them to Halloran? Because we both know who signs his checks now. We both know whose name is on that wire."

The muzzle pressed harder. "You think I don't know? You think I signed on with Halloran blind?" A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh in a younger man. "I've been on that man's payroll since before you were flying, boy. I told myself it was the only way to get to the wreck. Thirty years of telling myself that. And now the wreck's open, and the papers are in it, and every way out of this fjord runs through a man who needs them burned."

"Then don't give them to him."

"Or what?" Vess's voice cracked like ice underfoot. "You'll fly them out? On six tides of fuel and a plane that can't lift its own shadow in this wind? You'll publish them and clear your father and hang the owners, and what happens to the rest of us standing on the dock when it lands?" He was breathing hard now, the gun trembling at the edge of its arc. "I have a daughter on this ship. I have a debt in every port from here to Seattle. I have three names I've carried so long I don't know if I'm avenging them or just using them."

"Then put the gun down," Isandro said quietly, "and find out."

For one moment — Isandro would remember it all his life, would see it in the moment before sleep for years after — the muzzle dropped an inch. Vess's eyes went somewhere far away, to a salmon boat and a father saying *the sea doesn't lie*. His finger eased off the guard.

The hold lights came on.

"Evening, gentlemen." Deitz stood at the foot of the ladder with six men behind him and a shotgun broken open over his arm like a bird he was about to eat. "Captain. Mr. Yarrow. Lovely night for a little light reading."

Vess turned, and the gun in his hand was suddenly a small, foolish thing. "Deitz. This is my ship."

"Was." Deitz came forward, unhurried, his men fanning out to the netting with the ease of men who'd done this before, in other ports, on other people's property. Two of them took the document case. One took the logbook from Vess's cabin safe — Isandro saw it later, saw the moment in his mind, though in the moment itself he only saw Deitz's man come back up the ladder with the oilcloth bundle and Deitz's small nod. "Mr. Halloran sends his compliments. He says the salvage phase is concluded and the disposal phase is beginning. He says the charges for tomorrow have been increased to—" Deitz consulted a folded paper with theatrical care— "considerably more than Captain Vess authorized. He says the Captain's contract has been fulfilled. He says thank you."

"Thirty years," Vess said. The words came out of him like something breaking up through ice. "Thirty years, you gutless—"

"Twenty-two," Deitz said mildly. "He's been paying you twenty-two years, Captain. He showed me the ledger. You're in it." He smiled. "You're in everybody's ledger somewhere. It's just that most men never find out which column."

Two of Halloran's men took Vess by the arms. He didn't resist; Isandro watched him not resist, watched the fight go out of the old man like air out of a punctured wing, and thought: *there but for a letter.*

"The papers," Deitz said, turning to Isandro last, and the shotgun closed with a click. "All of them. And the letter, Mr. Yarrow. Mr. Halloran is quite specific about the letter. He knows your father's name is on it. He knows what's in it. He's known since before you did, I'd guess — men like him write the first drafts of other people's histories." He held out his hand. "The letter. And then you can go back to being a freight pilot, and in a month nobody will remember there ever was a *Meridian*, and your airstrip will keep its roof, and everyone lives."

Isandro looked at the hand. He looked at the shotgun. He thought about the letter against his chest, warm from his own body, the last true words his father had ever spoken to anyone.

And above them all, through the steel of the hull, through the dark water, through forty feet of ancient ice, came a sound.

A crack. Long, deep, and rising — the sound of the glacier clearing its throat, or beginning to.

Deitz's men looked up. Even Deitz looked up.

"Sixth tide's coming early," Wren said from the top of the ladder, her wrench in her hand and her eyes wide. "You might want to hold that thought."
