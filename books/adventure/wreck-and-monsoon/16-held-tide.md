## 16. Held Tide

Wren had learned engines by listening to them fail. Her father's boat, the *Kestrel*, had a Perkins diesel that coughed in a particular way when the fuel line was sucking air — a dry, stuttering hiccup, like an old man clearing his throat before he lied. She was twelve the first time she diagnosed it from the galley without looking up from her homework, and her father had laughed so hard he'd burned the fish.

She thought about that laugh now because there wasn't much else to think about that didn't hurt.

The hold of the *Cormorant* smelled like bilgewater, rope tar, and old canvas — the same smell as her father's engine room, which was either a mercy or a cruelty, she hadn't decided. The padlock on the hatch above was new brass, bright as a coin. Halloran's men had locked it with the ceremony of men who believed locks were permanent.

"They're loading another charge," Mara said. She'd found a gap between two hull planks where the caulking had rotted out, and she'd been at it for an hour, one eye pressed to the dark like a woman reading tea leaves. "Bigger than the last. I can hear the crane."

"I can hear everything," Wren said. "That's the problem."

It was true. Wren heard machines the way other people heard music — every note, every wrong note. Through the deck above her she could track Halloran's operation the way a doctor tracks a heartbeat: the compressor cycling, the generator lugging under load, the winch paying out cable in its uneven, greedy rhythm. And underneath all of it, muffled through forty feet of ice and water, the *thump* of the charges going off against the Meridian's bones.

Each one made the hold lights flicker. Each one made Sefina flinch.

The archaeologist sat against the port bulkhead with her knees drawn up, and for the first time since Wren had known her, she looked exactly like what she was: a woman watching her life's work get blown apart a piece at a time. Her documentation — three years of methodical photography, sonar mapping, provenance notes written in handwriting so careful it looked printed — had gone into the fjord on tide three, drowned in the surge. She'd salvaged nothing but her field notebook, half-soaked, its pages warped into little waves.

"They cracked crate fourteen today," Sefina said quietly. Nobody had asked her to report it. She reported anyway, because reporting was all she had left. "That was the medical chest. Pre-war quinine, still sealed in glass. There were enough doses in that one box to treat a whole district for a decade. He's selling it to a collector in Seattle who wants the *bottles*."

"Collectors want bottles," Isandro said from the shadows near the stern. He hadn't moved much in hours. Wren kept catching herself watching him — the way a man sits when he's carrying something breakable and can't put it down. "Bottles don't testify."

Mara pulled back from the gap in the planks. "Nobody's testifying to anything if we're still in this hold when the sixth tide comes. Have you done the math?"

"I've done the math," Wren said. "Everyone's done the math. We've been doing math for two days."

Because they had. It was the only game left in the hold: count the charges, count the tides, count the hours. Tide four had come and gone while they listened to Halloran's divers strip the captain's cabin bare. Tide five was coming tomorrow night, and after tide five came tide six, and after tide six the glacier would calve, and the fjord would fill with bergs the size of courthouses, and no plane, no tender, no swimmer was getting out until spring — if the *Cormorant* itself even survived the calving, moored where Halloran had moored her, too close to the ice face because closer meant faster loading and faster loading meant money.

"He's going to kill us by accident," Mara said. "He doesn't even have to mean it. He just has to keep being cheap and greedy in exactly the way he's always been."

"That's how my dad went under," Wren said, before she could stop herself.

The hold went quiet. Even the compressor above seemed to hold its breath.

She hadn't told them. Not really. They knew pieces — Isandro knew the shape of it, because orphans recognized each other the way dogs did, by smell. But she'd never said it plain, and now it was out, sitting in the bilgewater smell between them, and she found she didn't want to take it back.

"The *Kestrel* was a thirty-eight-foot gillnetter," she said, staring at the padlock because it was easier than staring at their faces. "My dad ran charters in the shoulder season to keep the permit. One September the weather came down fast — you know how it does, one minute it's flat, next minute the sky falls over. And the fleet radio — the big boats, the ones with the good antennas — they heard him calling. I know they heard him, because I was on the dock at Cordova when they came in, and I watched them unload their holds full of fish they'd stayed out to catch while he was going down." Her voice did something strange, went flat and mechanical, like she was reading someone else's logbook. "Coast Guard got there too late. Eleven minutes, the report said. Eleven minutes. A boat with a working radio could have made it in eight."

"And Vess?" Sefina asked softly.

"Vess was one of them. His fleet was in that year. My mom had the letter he sent afterward — insurance thing, everybody writes letters afterward. He wrote that conditions had been 'outside the margin of prudent response.'" Wren laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Outside the margin. That's what my father was to him. A margin."

Isandro shifted in the dark. "And that's why you stowed away."

"I stowed away because I wanted to burn his fleet down," Wren said honestly. "I was fifteen and furious and I had a wrench. But then I got aboard and I started listening to the engines, and I realized something better was down here." She tapped her own temple. "He keeps his real books in his head, but his *radio* — his radio traffic goes through the wheelhouse set, and the wheelhouse set logs everything. Every coded transmission to Halloran. Every schedule. Every payment confirmation. If we ever get off this boat, I can pull that log, and it won't be a letter about margins. It'll be thirty years of receipts."

Sefina lifted her head. For the first time in two days there was something in her face besides grief — the sharp, hungry look Wren had seen her wear over a good artifact. "You can access the log?"

"I can access anything with a circuit board," Wren said. "That was never the hard part. The hard part is being locked in a hole."

* * *

The lock stopped being the hard part around midnight, though not in any way Wren would have chosen.

Footsteps on the ladder first — heavy, unsteady, the particular arrhythmia of a man navigating by memory and whiskey. Then the scrape of a key, badly aimed, twice, three times, and the padlock dropped open and Captain Aldous Vess fell more than climbed down into the hold, carrying a bottle by the neck and thirty years of ruin in his shoulders.

He stood swaying in the middle of the deck plates, blinking at the four of them like a man who'd forgotten why he'd come.

"Well," he said. "Look at this. My whole crew. My whole *life's* crew, locked up like herring."

"Captain—" Mara started.

"Don't." He held up a hand, and the bottle sloshed. "Don't you 'Captain' me, girl. You haven't called me Captain in six years, and you were right to stop." He lowered himself onto a coil of spare mooring line with the grace of a collapsing mast. "Halloran took the cache tonight. All of it. Logbook, silver, the safe contents, everything we bled for. Put it on his floatplane and flew it to his ship before I finished my dinner." He drank. "Thirty years I chased this wreck. Thirty years. And do you know what he said to me? At the gangway? He said, 'Aldous, you were always better at finding things than keeping them.'"

Nobody spoke. Above them, faintly, the compressor cycled.

"So I thought," Vess continued, and his voice had gone somewhere past drunk into a cold sober country that drink only borders, "I'd come down here and tell the people I ruined the rest of it. Since nobody else will ever ask."

"Vess," Isandro said carefully, "the letter—"

"The letter, yes. Yarrow's boy and his letter." Vess smiled, and it was the worst thing Wren had ever seen on a human face, that smile — grief wearing a grin as a disguise. "Your father. Elias Yarrow. Do you know I hated that man for thirty years? Three of my men went into the water the night the Meridian grounded, and the story I was handed — the story *everybody* was handed — was that your father cut the boats loose and saved himself. Mutineer. Coward. I built a whole cathedral of hate on that foundation. Hunted this wreck because I was sure the truth was down here, and the truth was going to be *worse*, and I was going to be right about him forever."

He drank again. His hand shook.

"And then I found the logbook. Five days ago. Before Halloran took it. I read it." He looked up at Isandro, and his eyes were wet and terrible. "Elias Yarrow didn't mutiny. The order to abandon came from *aboard* — no. No, that's not true either. Let an old drunk tell it straight for once in his life."

He set the bottle down on the deck plates with exaggerated care, like landing a plane in crosswind.

"The owner ordered it. Halloran's principal — the man whose money bought this whole fleet, whose name is on the insurance settlement, whose signature is in the logbook ordering the *Meridian* abandoned with three men still below, because waiting for them would have cost him the salvage window and the insurance deadline both. Your father refused. Your father took the boats *back into* the storm against direct orders, trying to reach them. That's what the owners couldn't forgive — not mutiny. Disobedience that made them look like what they were. So they buried him instead." Vess's voice cracked. "They buried him, and I carried the shovel for thirty years and called it grief."

Wren watched Isandro. She'd expected rage — she'd have been raging, she *was* raging on his behalf, her hands curled into fists so tight her nails bit her palms. But Isandro just sat very still, the way he sat in the cockpit when the weather turned, hands light on controls that were trying to kill him.

"The signature," Isandro said finally. "In the logbook. You read it yourself."

"I read it myself."

"Then Halloran has it now."

"Halloran has paper," Vess said. "Paper burns. Paper gets lost. Paper gets *bought*." He picked the bottle back up. "But I'm an old man with nothing left, and a confession weighs nothing and can't be stolen." He looked at each of them in turn — Mara last, longest, and Wren saw something pass between father and daughter that neither of them would ever say aloud. "Ask me anything. While I'm still honest. It won't last."

* * *

He talked for two hours.

Wren didn't write it down — there was nothing to write with, nothing to write on — so she did what she'd always done with things that mattered. She memorized it like a wiring diagram. Every connection. Every junction. The name of the owner — Aldous Vess saying it out loud at last, the syllables falling like stones down a well. The date of the original abandonment. The insurance claim number. The payments, the schedule, the coded channel Halloran used, the frequency, the rotation of the codes — and at that part Wren sat up straight despite herself, because Vess recited the code rotation from memory, all of it, thirty years of a paid man's diligence, and she realized with a cold electric thrill that the drunken old captain had just handed her the key to his own cage.

Sefina asked the questions an archaeologist would ask — dates, chain of custody, corroboration. Mara asked the questions a navigator would ask — positions, times, tides, the exact sequence of the original disaster reconstructed hour by hour. Isandro asked almost nothing. He sat with his father's name being rebuilt in front of him, plank by plank, and Wren understood that he was afraid to touch it, the way you're afraid to touch a thing you've wanted too long.

And Wren asked the questions a mechanic would ask.

"How many charges does Halloran have left?" she said, near the end.

Vess blinked at her. "What?"

"You heard me. How many sticks, how many detonators, how much primacord. You supervised the loading. You counted everything your whole life — that's what you told us, that's what the ledgers were *for*. So count."

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes — the old competence surfacing through the booze like a fish rising. "Forty pounds of surplus military explosive. Sixteen electric caps. About two hundred feet of det cord. Enough for maybe five more shots at the size he's been running." He frowned. "Why does a sixteen-year-old want to know the blast inventory?"

"Because," Wren said, "he's not going to use five more shots on the wreck."

The hold went still.

"Think about it," she said, and her voice came out steady, and she was surprised by how steady, how much it sounded like her father's voice, the voice he'd used talking her through a rebuild. "He's got the cargo. He's got the logbook. The wreck is worth nothing to him now except as a problem — a documented wreck, in a cave that shouldn't exist, with an archaeologist and a pilot and a navigator who all know what it is. He doesn't need to salvage it anymore. He needs it to *stop existing*. And there's a glacier hanging over it with a few hundred thousand tons of ice that's been waiting a century for an excuse." She looked around at their faces, lit dim by the flickering hold lamp. "Five more shots. He doesn't need to aim them at the ship. He needs to aim them at the *cave roof*, and the mountain will do the rest, and when the Coast Guard asks what happened, there was a calving, a tragedy, the poor trapped souls, so sad, case closed. Four problems solved with somebody else's geology."

"He wouldn't," Mara began, and stopped, because they all watched him do exactly that kind of arithmetic every day.

"He'd sleep fine," Vess said quietly. "I know. I slept fine for thirty years."

Above them, muffled through steel and water and ancient ice, a charge went off — but wrong, Wren thought, wrong pitch, wrong place, higher and further aft than the wreck should be. The hold lights died completely. In the darkness, dust sifted down from the deckhead like snow.

When the emergency lamps buzzed on, Sefina was already on her feet.

"That wasn't the wreck," she said. "That was the cave mouth."

Wren pressed her ear to the hull plating and listened the way she'd been taught by a dead man in a warm engine room — past the compressor, past the generator, down to the deep grammar of the fjord itself. And beneath everything she heard it: a long, groaning exhalation, vast and patient, the sound of a glacier shifting its weight after ten thousand years of holding still.

The ice was awake.

"We're out of tides," Wren said. "We're out tonight. Somebody find me a wire, a spark, and a door — because I'm getting us off this boat before Halloran finishes his coffee."
