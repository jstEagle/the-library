## 17. Breakout

Mara had learned to read her father's ship the way other people learned to read weather. The Cormorant spoke in creaks and footfalls, in the particular drag of a tired man's boots on the deck plates above her head, in the rhythm of Halloran's men coming off watch — always eleven minutes late on the graveyard shift, because the man named Cutler who ran it took his coffee in the wheelhouse first and lingered over the radio log he wasn't supposed to read.

Eleven minutes. She'd counted for three nights, lying in the dark of the compartment they'd been locked into since the safe was seized, listening through a vent grate that carried sound the way a conch carries the sea. Three nights of counting, and mapping, and saying nothing while Isandro paced like a wolf in a pen too small for him, while Sefina sat perfectly still with her eyes closed as though she could memorize her way out of captivity, while Wren — sixteen years old and furious at the world in a way Mara recognized from her own mirror — dismantled the door's hinge pins with a spoon.

The spoon had been Wren's idea. Everything mechanical was Wren's idea. The girl had come aboard the Cormorant as a stowaway and had apparently spent the intervening weeks learning the ship's guts the way a surgeon learns anatomy, and now she lay on her back beneath the door with grease to her elbows, whispering numbers to herself like prayers.

"Third pin's sheared," she said. "Somebody's been through this door before us. Years ago. They left the top one rusted in place because they couldn't be bothered."

"Can you do it or not?" Isandro asked. He'd been asking versions of that question all night, and Mara understood it wasn't really about the door. It was about the tide tables folded against his ribs, the ones she'd copied for him in the wheelhouse what felt like a lifetime ago — six tides, and they were living inside the fifth now. The fjord would begin to seal with the next slack water after this one. Whatever they were going to do, they would do it in the dark, tonight, or become permanent residents of a museum that was about to close forever.

"I can do it," Wren said, with the flat certainty of someone who had never once been wrong about a machine. "I can't make you quiet going through it."

"We don't need quiet," Mara said. "We need eleven minutes."

She laid it out again, because laying things out was what she did, because a plan spoken aloud became real in a way a plan kept in the skull never did. They had gone through it nine times. She went through it a tenth.

Cutler came off watch at 0300 and took his eleven minutes. During those eleven minutes, the generator deck was unwatched; Wren would cut the main breakers, and the ship would go dark, and every man aboard would spend the first ninety seconds of darkness being angry instead of being useful. Ninety seconds was enough to cross the well deck to the aft hold, where Sefina's duplicate documentation — the film plates, the logbook tracings, the provenance records she'd hidden weeks ago inside a hollowed-out sounding lead — waited behind a false bulkhead only Sefina knew. From the aft hold, the cargo hatch opened onto the ice shelf on the seaward side, where Halloran's men didn't post a watch because they believed the glacier itself was a wall. Then down the shelf's spine, two miles of blue ice in full dark, to the salt flat at the fjord's elbow where the *Tern* sat on her floats under a tarp, fueled, untouched, dismissed by Halloran as a toy belonging to a dead man's son.

"And if the ninety seconds isn't enough," Sefina said quietly. It wasn't a question. It never was. Someone always asked it, and Mara always answered it the same way, because the answer was true and truth was the only currency any of them had left.

"Then we're caught, and there is no sixth tide, and Halloran flies out rich and we stay here until the ice decides what we're worth." She looked around the compartment — at Isandro with his father's letter pressed flat inside his jacket like a second heartbeat, at Sefina with her ruined hands and her unbowed spine, at Wren upside-down under the door with a spoon in her fist. "So don't be slow."

Nobody laughed. But something passed between them anyway, something that had been building since the hold, since the dynamite, since Vess had stood in front of them with a gun and then been discarded by the men who'd paid him — something without a name that Mara suspected was the closest thing to family she'd felt since she was twelve years old and her mother's boat went out one last time. You didn't laugh, in a family like that. You just made sure nobody got left.

Wren pulled the last pin free.

* * *

The door swung inward with a groan that sounded, in the silence, like a shout. They froze. Above them, boots crossed the deck plates — Cutler, right on schedule, heading for the galley and his coffee. Eleven minutes started now.

Mara went first, because she knew the Cormorant's passages better than anyone alive except maybe her father, and her father was drunk somewhere below with a bottle and the ruins of thirty years. She moved by touch and memory: companionway, ladder, the low pipe at head height that she'd cracked her skull on at fifteen and never forgotten. Behind her came the others, breathing hard, Isandro carrying Wren's tool roll because the girl's hands needed to be free, Sefina carrying nothing but the small steel case she'd slept with curled against her body for three nights like a child.

The generator deck smelled of hot oil and salt. Wren went to the main board the way water goes downhill — no hesitation, no wasted motion — and Mara watched her small hands hover over the breakers, reading them, choosing.

"Not all of them," Wren murmured. "If I kill everything, the emergency lights come up in ten seconds and we've bought nothing. I take the lighting circuit, the radio, and the winch power. Leave them their engines." A ghost of a grin in the dark. "Let them chase us. They'll need light to do it."

"You're enjoying this," Isandro said.

"I'm sixteen and I've been locked in a box for three days," Wren said. "Yes. I'm enjoying this." And she threw the breakers.

Darkness fell like a dropped curtain. For one heartbeat the whole ship held its breath — and then the shouting began, exactly as promised, men waking angry, boots hammering, someone falling over something metal and swearing with real poetry. Somewhere aft a flashlight beam swung wild across the bulkheads, painting brief white slashes through the black.

"Ninety seconds," Mara said. "Move."

They moved. Through the aft passage with its smell of diesel and old fish, past the coiled lines and the spare propeller lashed to the overhead, Isandro's hand on her shoulder guiding when the dark confused even her — and then the aft hold, and Sefina already at the false bulkhead, prying it open with fingers that shook not from fear but from something older, something like grief.

"It's here," Sefina whispered, and her voice broke on it. "It's all here. Every plate. Every page." She lifted out the sounding lead and opened it, and even in the dark Mara could see the shape of what she held — the record of the *Meridian*, the truth of her, photographed and traced and preserved by a woman who had lost her career rather than falsify so much as a single line of provenance. "They can strip the wreck. They can take the silver. They cannot take this anymore."

"Then it's done," Isandro said. "It's done. Go."

Behind them, forward, a generator coughed and caught — Halloran's engineer, good and fast, bringing auxiliary power up. Light bloomed at the far end of the passage, yellow and hard, and with it voices, and with the voices the flat crack of a shotgun rack being worked.

"The cargo hatch," Mara said. "Now. Run."

* * *

The night outside was the kind of cold that didn't feel like temperature so much as intention. The ice shelf stretched away from the ship's stern like a road built by something enormous and indifferent, blue-black under a sky scoured of stars by low cloud, and the glacier at their backs groaned — a long, deep sound, tectonic, the sound of a thing shifting its weight in its sleep. Mara had grown up on this coast. She knew that groan the way she knew her own pulse. The glacier was talking in its sleep, and what it said was *soon*.

They ran. Two miles of ice, and Halloran's men spilling out of the Cormorant's stern behind them with lights that bobbed and swung and multiplied — four beams, then six. Shots cracked across the ice, wild, the reports flattening and rolling off the glacier walls, and a round skipped off the surface twenty feet to Wren's left with a sound like a struck bell.

"Spread out!" Isandro shouted. "Single file's a gift!"

They spread. Mara cut toward the fjord-side crevasse field because she was the only one who'd mapped it, because during her father's survey years she'd walked this shelf with a compass and a notebook while he drank, and she knew — the way she knew tide tables, the way she knew her own name — that the pursuers' lights would follow the smooth center line and die at the first pressure ridge. She heard them hit it ninety seconds later, heard the shouting turn from pursuit to argument, and allowed herself one breath of something that wasn't quite triumph.

The crevasse field was a city of blue chasms underfoot, bridged by snow that lied about its strength. She called the safe line in a low voice — *left of my voice, always left* — and they threaded it in the dark, Sefina's hand on Mara's pack strap, Wren ahead with the fearless balance of youth, Isandro at the rear walking backward half the time with the pistol he'd taken off a sleeping guard, watching the lights behind them dwindle and rage.

And then the glacier spoke again, louder, and did not stop speaking.

Mara felt it through her boots before she heard it fully — a rising vibration, a note too deep for ears, climbing through the ice into her bones. Ahead and to starboard, high on the glacier's face where the fjord bent, a section of the serac field let go. Even at two miles the sound was apocalyptic: a roar like the sky tearing, followed by the long thunder of a hundred thousand tons of ice hitting tidewater.

The wave would reach the shelf in under four minutes.

"Off the low ice!" Mara screamed, and pointed to the pressure ridge ahead, a rampart of jumbled blocks twelve feet high. "Up! Everyone up! Now!"

They climbed. There was no time for care, no time for the crevasse rules; there was only hands and boots and Wren hauling Sefina up the last ice block by both wrists, and then the wave arrived.

Mara had seen calving waves before, from safe distances, from boats. She had never stood on the thing they struck. The whole shelf shuddered and tilted and groaned like a ship running aground; gray water surged up through the crevasse field below them, filling the blue chasms in seconds, geysers erupting from cracks that had been dry all night, and the air filled with spray that froze as it flew. Something enormous ground past below the ridge — a bergy bit the size of a house, riding the wave's shoulder — and then the water drew back, sucking and gurgling, and the night was quiet again except for the four of them breathing like bellows and the glacier, far off, settling back into its sleep.

Sefina was laughing. It was a strange, ragged sound, half hysteria and half joy, and nobody told her to stop.

"That," Wren panted, "was the best worst thing I have ever survived."

"The plane," Isandro said. His voice was steady, but Mara saw his hand shake as he checked his watch against the faint glow of the cloud ceiling. "Nineteen minutes to slack water. If we're not off the flat by then, the current through the narrows will pin us against the moraine. Move."

* * *

The salt flat appeared out of the dark like a promise kept — a broad apron of gravel and crusted mud at the fjord's elbow, firm enough to bear the *Tern*'s weight at low water, which was why Isandro had chosen it and why no one else had ever thought to. The tarp came off the plane under four sets of hands, and Wren went straight to the engine cowling with a flashlight clenched in her teeth, running her preflight by touch and memory while the others loaded the documentation cases through the cabin door.

Mara took the copilot's seat and began her own preflight, the one nobody had assigned her: charts, tide table, stopwatch, the pencil she'd sharpened with a knife an hour before the breakout because a navigator without a sharp pencil was a passenger. The narrows lay a mile east, a quarter-mile-wide throat between the moraine shoals where the ebb would soon run eight knots through drifting brash. In daylight, with an engine warm and a crew rested, it would be a careful passage. Tonight, in the dark, overloaded, pursued —

"How much fuel?" she asked.

"Enough for Juneau if I lean it and the wind cooperates," Isandro said, sliding into the pilot's seat. "Not enough for mistakes."

"Then we won't make any." She said it lightly, and meant it completely, and saw him glance at her with something that might have been gratitude or might have been the beginning of trust, which amounted to the same thing.

The engine caught on the third swing, coughed, smoothed into a rumble that rolled flat across the salt and out over the black water. Behind them, far back along the shelf, the pursuers' lights had stopped at the flooded crevasse field and could come no farther; the wave had done what walls and guards could not. Mara swept the flat once with the flashlight — gravel, kelp wrack, the tide line, nothing moving — and put her hand on Isandro's shoulder.

"Take us out," she said.

The *Tern* lifted off the salt flat with the particular reluctance of an overloaded seaplane, wheels of spray peeling away, the stall horn singing its thin complaint until Isandro eased her nose down a degree and let the air build under her wings. Below, the fjord unrolled — black water, white ice, the vast pale shoulder of the glacier sliding past to port, calving in slow silent pieces that glowed faintly, impossibly, like embers buried in snow.

Ahead lay the narrows, and beyond the narrows the open sea, and beyond the sea Juneau, and beyond Juneau a courtroom and a headline and a name scrubbed clean at last. Mara felt none of it as victory. She felt it as ballast — the weight of the woman beside her, the girl behind her, the man at the controls flying on nerve and inheritance, all of it holding the aircraft steady against the dark.

They were halfway through the narrows, brash ice ticking and scraping along the floats, when Wren leaned forward between the seats, radio headset clamped to one ear, and went very still.

"Mara," she said. "You need to hear this."

She handed the headset back. Mara pressed it to her ear. Through the static, on the guard frequency, a voice was repeating — calm, clipped, professional, and unmistakable:

*"Any station, any station, this is salvage vessel Cormorant, position fifty-seven degrees—"*

Her father's ship. Broadcasting a Mayday.

And beneath the Mayday, half-buried in static, another voice — young, male, frightened, saying words that turned Mara's blood to ice water:

*"—taking water fast, the charges went off wrong, she's down by the bow, and Captain Vess is still aboard—"*
