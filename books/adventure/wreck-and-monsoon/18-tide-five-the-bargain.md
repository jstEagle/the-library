## 18. Tide Five: The Bargain

Sefina Okafor had spent eleven years learning to read water, and the fjord was telling her something she did not want to hear.

She stood at the Cormorant's rail in the gray half-light before dawn, a mug of coffee gone cold in her hands, and watched the tide come in wrong. Not wrong exactly — the sea did not make mistakes; men did — but wrong for them. The flood was running higher than it had any right to run on the fifth day, pushing up into the ice-choked narrows with an appetite that made her stomach tighten. The bergs that had grounded themselves on the shoal below the glacier's face were lifting free one by one, rotating like slow animals waking, and the channel they'd been using to run the tender back and forth to Isandro's plane was narrowing by the hour.

By tomorrow's tide, it would be gone.

Behind her, the ship woke in pieces: the groan of the generator, Wren's boots hammering down the ladder from the engine room where she'd slept — if you could call it sleeping — beside the main pump she didn't trust, the smell of burnt coffee and diesel and, underneath it all, the sweet mineral breath of the glacier itself, which got into everything now, into their clothes, their hair, their dreams.

"You've been up all night," Wren said, arriving at the rail with a second mug she pressed into Sefina's hands without asking whether she wanted it. Sixteen years old and already she had the particular authority of someone who fixed things for a living. "I could hear you typing."

"I wasn't typing." Sefina turned the mug in her fingers. "I was writing."

Wren considered this distinction with the seriousness it deserved. "Is there a difference?"

"There is to me." There always had been. Typing was what she'd done for the journals and the grant reports and the provenance papers other people had wanted signed; writing was what she'd done at three in the morning in a cold cabin, reconstructing four days of documentation from memory because Halloran's men had flooded the cave and taken her originals and she refused — *refused* — to let the Meridian exist only as cargo manifests and invoices. She had written until her hands cramped: hull measurements, cargo positions, the stratigraphy of the ice, the position of every crate Vess's divers had cracked open, the serial numbers off the quinine tins, the captain's name stenciled inside a locker door. It wasn't the archive. But it was testimony.

"Where's Isandro?" she asked.

"Chart table. Mara's with him." Wren hesitated, and Sefina heard the hesitation for what it was — the girl had learned, somewhere between the engine room and the calving waves, when not to say a thing out loud. "Halloran's boat came alongside twenty minutes ago. He asked for Isandro by name."

*

They met in the Cormorant's saloon, which Halloran seemed to regard as his own property already. He sat at the table like a man at a bank closing, gray suit unwrinkled despite the tender ride across, a leather folio squared in front of him, and he smiled at Sefina when she came in with the smile of a man who had looked up her file.

"Dr. Okafor," he said. "I read your monograph on the Tasmanian wreck sites. Impressive work. A shame about how your career went."

"A shame about how other people's careers went," Sefina said, and took the chair opposite him deliberately, so that he would have to decide whether that was confrontation or courtesy. Isandro stood by the chart table with his arms folded, and Mara leaned in the doorway behind him, and Wren had appeared at the companionway as if summoned by the smell of trouble, and Halloran registered all of them the way a man registers weather — noting it, discounting it.

He opened the folio. Inside were two documents and a set of figures. He turned the documents around first.

"The letter," he said. "And the logbook pages you copied. I'm told you have them."

"I'm told a lot of things," Isandro said.

"What you have is a fuel problem." Halloran tapped the figures. "Your plane sits on a salt flat forty minutes' run through a channel that will not exist after tomorrow's tide. Even if it did, you're carrying four people and no range. The nearest strip with aviation gas is Ketchikan-side, and my tender carries nine hundred gallons of it, sitting in drums, doing nothing." He folded his hands. "The letter and the logbook for the fuel, the tender's escort through the narrows this afternoon, and my personal word that no one aboard this vessel ever hears from me again. That is the trade. It does not improve with time, and time is the one thing the glacier isn't selling."

Sefina watched Isandro's face while Halloran talked. She had known him six days and she could already read him the way she read sediment layers — pressure lines, deposition events, the strata of a man deciding. His jaw was set but his eyes moved: to the folio, to the porthole and the white wall of the glacier beyond it, to Mara, to Wren in the doorway. He was weighing the letter against the girl. Against all of them.

His father's name against their lives.

"No," he said.

Halloran didn't blink. "Mr. Yarrow—"

"It's not a negotiation. You want to know why? Because a man offered my father a trade once, sixty years ago, and my father took the boats back into the storm instead, and everyone who's paid for that decision since has been people who weren't in the room." Isandro's voice stayed level, which Sefina recognized as its own kind of effort. "You can keep your fuel. We'll walk out over the ice if we have to."

"You'll die on the ice."

"Probably slower than we'd die being the kind of people who hand you our father's grave to burn."

The silence that followed had texture. Sefina felt it settle over the saloon like cold air off the glacier. Halloran closed the folio without hurry, squared it again, and stood, and as he passed Sefina's chair he paused — just long enough.

"We should talk, Dr. Okafor. Before the tide makes us all honest." And he was gone up the ladder, his men falling in behind him, and the Cormorant rocked gently in his wake as though relieved.

*

Mara found Sefina an hour later in the hold, kneeling among the crates that remained — the ones too heavy or too broken to be worth Halloran's dynamite — cataloguing by hand into a waterproof notebook because her laptop had drowned on tide three and she had stopped trusting electronics that hadn't personally earned her forgiveness.

"He'll come to you," Mara said, without preamble. She was coiling line as she talked, her hands working on their own, her eyes on Sefina. "My father says Halloran always works the flank. He offered Isandro the front door so he'd know where it was, and now he'll come around the side."

"And what does he want with me?"

"What do you want with him?" Mara set the coil down. "You've been writing all night. Documentation doesn't fly planes. Whatever deal you're building in your head, build it fast, because the tide tables don't care whose name gets cleared."

Sefina looked at the girl — the navigator, the defector, the daughter who had chosen truth over blood and was still paying the interest on it daily — and decided that lying to her would be both pointless and ungrateful.

"Halloran has radio codes," she said. "Fleet frequencies, Coast Guard liaison channels, the whole network his principals use to move ships and money without anyone reading the traffic. If I broadcast on those frequencies — if I get a message out on *his* channel, timestamped, authenticated — the Coast Guard won't just intercept a distress call. They'll intercept proof of who he answers to. Proof of the insurance fraud, the abandonment order, thirty years of it. My word alone is a blacklisted archaeologist shouting into the wind. His own codes are a confession."

"And what does he get?"

Sefina closed the notebook. "Me."

*

He came at dusk, when the fjord turned the color of pewter and the glacier began its evening argument with itself — deep concussions rolling down the valley, ice dust hanging in the air like smoke after artillery. He found her at the stern rail, alone, which was not an accident; she had made sure of it the way she made sure of everything now, measuring twice, cutting once, because the last time she'd trusted a site to stay put it had tried to kill her.

"Dr. Okafor." He settled beside her at the rail, unhurried, a man who had never once been cold. "You'll forgive the theater of meeting out here. Walls have opinions."

"Walls also have ears," Sefina said. "That's rather the point of me."

He laughed — genuinely, she thought, which was worse. "Yarrow won't trade. I respect it. Stupid, but respectable; the two travel together more often than people admit. So the question becomes what happens tomorrow when the tide closes the channel and there are five people on this ship with no way home and one man holding nine hundred gallons of gasoline." He looked out at the ice. "I don't actually want anyone to die. Dead people generate paperwork. But I am paid to be indifferent to the alternative, and I'm very good at my work."

"Then make your offer."

"Simple. The letter and the logbook copies come to me tonight, quietly, without Yarrow's knowledge or blessing. In exchange, my tender carries your people and their plane's worth of fuel through tomorrow's tide — the last usable one — and everyone lives to argue about history in a warm room." He paused. "And there's a second clause. For you specifically."

"There it is."

"I know what happened in Fremantle. You wouldn't sign the provenance papers, they ruined you, and every journal in your field has a door marked *Okafor* that no longer opens. I can open those doors. Rehabilitation, reinstatement, a named chair if you want it — the people I represent buy universities the way other men buy boats. One signature from you confirming the salvage was conducted under proper archaeological supervision — mine — and your career resumes where it stopped, with interest." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were patient and gray and entirely without malice, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Everyone pays something, Dr. Okafor. That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic. The only question is what currency."

Sefina let the silence stretch until it became hers. Below them the tide turned; she felt it in the ship's motion, the long shudder as the current reversed, the water beginning its last great push toward the glacier face.

"You want my signature," she said. "Fine. I want yours."

Something flickered behind the patience. "Go on."

"Your radio codes. The fleet frequencies, the authentication tables, the liaison channels — all of it, written down, delivered before we sail. That's the price of my signature and my silence on the crossing. You buy the letter, you buy the logbook, you buy a clean salvage report with a disgraced archaeologist's name legitimizing it — and I go back to a career, and you go back to your owners, and nobody reads anybody's mail." She held out her hand. "But the codes first. I don't sign anything I can't verify, and I certainly don't board a tender with a man whose promises are the only ballast aboard."

Halloran studied her hand as though it might contain a mechanism. Then he laughed again, shorter this time, and shook it — his grip dry and cool and precise.

"You understand," he said, "that if you betray me, I'll know within the hour, and the fuel stays in my drums."

"You understand," Sefina said, "that if you betray me, I'll have memorized your codes, and I type very well under pressure."

*

She wrote the letter that night in the galley, by lamplight, while Wren pretended to repair a pump that didn't need repairing ten feet away and mercifully said nothing. The words cost her more than she'd expected. *Conducted under proper archaeological supervision* — she wrote them and felt each one go in like a splinter, because she had built her whole life on the refusal to write exactly that sentence, and here it was, in her own hand, in exchange for a set of radio frequencies and a boat ride.

*Everyone pays something.* Halloran's arithmetic. She hated that it balanced.

What she did not write in the letter — what existed nowhere but in the waterproof notebook strapped against her ribs — was the rest of the transaction. Because Halloran had given her the codes an hour ago, a single typed sheet passed across the rail like contraband, and she had read it three times before folding it away, and she had noticed what a man who bought universities apparently never needed to learn: that authentication tables cut both ways. A code that proves a message genuine can also prove, later, in a hearing, under oath, exactly who sent what, to whom, on whose behalf. He thought he was buying her signature. He was handing her the receipt for everything his principals had ever signed.

She sealed the envelope. Tomorrow, before the tender sailed, it would be in Isandro's hands — the letter, the logbook copies, and her false confession, all of it surrendered to Halloran on the last tide, while the true cargo rode out of the fjord sewn into the lining of a teenage mechanic's jacket.

Wren finally looked up from the pump. "You're going to give it to him," she said. "The real stuff. Aren't you."

"I'm going to give him exactly what he paid for," Sefina said. "And then I'm going to make sure he pays for it forever."

Outside, the glacier calved — a sound like the world's largest door closing — and the sixth tide began to rise.
