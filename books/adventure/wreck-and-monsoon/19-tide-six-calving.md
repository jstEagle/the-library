## 19. Tide Six: Calving

The fjord told them first.

Isandro heard it before he understood it — a sound like a giant clearing its throat deep under the water, a groan that came up through the gravel of the salt-flat strip and into the soles of his boots and settled somewhere behind his sternum and stayed there. He had been loading the last of the silver when it happened, crates of it, bars wrapped in oilcloth from the Meridian's strongroom, each one heavier than a sin. He stood in the gray pre-dawn light with a crate in his arms and listened to the glacier speak, and the crate suddenly felt like what it was: ballast on a sinking thing.

"Mara!" His voice cracked across the flat. "Mara, get up!"

She was already out of the tender's cabin, hair wild, tide tables in one hand and a flashlight in the other, and she didn't ask what. She'd been sleeping in her boots for three days the way navigators do when they can smell weather before instruments confirm it. She spread the tables on the wing of the plane and pinned them with stones, and her finger went down the column of times and stopped.

"It's early," she said. "It's not supposed to be until noon. It's—" She looked at her watch, then at the glacier at the head of the fjord, a pale blue wall two miles off that had been groaning all week like an old man climbing stairs. "It's four hours early."

"Glaciers don't read your tables."

"No," Mara said quietly. "They write their own." She was already folding the paper, already moving. "Get Wren. Get Sefina. We load now or we don't load at all."

* * *

The Cormorant was gone by then — Halloran's men had taken her down-fjord two tides ago, heavy with quinine and silver and the stripped bones of a ship that had waited ninety years to be robbed, and Vess with them, drunk or broken or both, a captain carried off his own bridge like cargo. What remained on the salt flat was Isandro's battered de Havilland, the tender, four people, and everything they'd managed to hide from Halloran's inventory: fuel drums, the letter, the logbook, Sefina's copies of the documentation sealed in waterproof sleeves, and eleven hundred pounds of silver that had seemed, three days ago, like the difference between survival and ruin.

The airstrip was a river bar that froze hard enough in winter to hold a plane and thawed into a bog by June. Right now, in the cold snap ahead of the calving, it held. But the plane was overloaded — Isandro had known it since the second crate went in, had felt the struts complain, had done the arithmetic in his head and hated every line of it. Gross weight over max by a margin that would matter on a short bar with a rising wind. He'd told himself the ice would hold, the air would be dense, the gods of marginal aviation would look kindly on a man who had suffered enough.

The gods of marginal aviation, he thought now, listening to the glacier groan again, longer this time, were about to send their answer by iceberg.

Wren came running from the tree line where she'd rigged the radio antenna, sixteen years old and all elbows, breath steaming. "Sefina got a signal out. Coast Guard acknowledged. They said eighteen hours to reach the mouth of the fjord." She paused, catching her breath, and her eyes went to the glacier wall, which had begun, very faintly, to smoke — mist blowing off its face where the morning sun found it. "Eighteen hours is too long, isn't it?"

Isandro looked at the ice. Ninety years he'd flown these coasts; his father before him had sailed them; and in all that inherited knowledge there lived one iron law, passed down like a family Bible: *the glacier does not negotiate.* Calving season didn't arrive on a schedule. It arrived when the tide undercut the face and the meltwater lubricated the base and a hundred tons of ice decided, all at once, that it was tired of standing.

"It might be too long," he said. "So we're not waiting for it."

* * *

They loaded in the blue half-light like people fleeing a house fire — because they were. Fuel first, then the documentation, then the people. The silver went in last, crate by crate, and each crate was a decision Isandro kept not making, sliding it into the fuselage and telling himself the next one wouldn't fit, knowing perfectly well it would.

Sefina stood at the open door with her sleeves rolled, passing crates, and her hands slowed as the pile shrank. She was a woman who had lost her career once already for refusing to lie about where things came from, and Isandro watched her look at the silver — the Meridian's silver, documented, photographed, catalogued in her careful hand — and he saw her doing the same arithmetic he was doing, arriving at the same ugly sum.

"The provenance case dies without physical evidence," she said. Not accusing. Just stating the weather.

"The name lives if we get out," Isandro said. "My father's name. That's the evidence that matters."

"That's *your* evidence that matters." She said it without heat, and that was worse. "I've spent nine years being told my work doesn't count because I wouldn't play the game. This wreck was going to be the thing that proved me right. And now I'm going to watch it get eaten by ice while I fly away holding a piece of paper."

"Sefina—"

"No." She set the last crate in the door and left her hands on it. "You say it. Say what we both know you're going to do. Don't make me find out at altitude."

He looked past her at the glacier. The face of it had changed overnight — he could see it even from here, a new lean in the old wall, a fresh fracture line running diagonally like a scar across a cheek. The tide was coming in beneath it, warm water gnawing at the footing of something that had stood since before either of their grandfathers.

"I'm going to do the math," he said finally. "Honestly. All the way through. And if the math says the silver goes overboard, the silver goes overboard."

Sefina studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face must have satisfied her, because she nodded once and climbed into the cabin and started strapping down her document cases like a woman securing a lifeboat.

Mara appeared at his elbow. "Math's done," she said. "We're four hundred pounds over. Maybe five hundred with full fuel. The bar gives us maybe six hundred feet of run before the soft ground, and the wind's coming down-glacier, which means a tailwind on takeoff unless we turn around and take the crosswind off the ridge, and the ridge turbulence in a loaded plane with calving waves hitting the shore—" She stopped herself. Breathed. "I can get us airborne at max gross in still air. I cannot get us airborne five hundred pounds over it in a fifty-knot outflow."

"So we burn fuel or drop weight."

"We don't have time to burn fuel. Look at the face."

He looked. Even in the few minutes since Sefina had spoken, the glacier had shifted — a low white dust hanging off the face where small seracs were letting go, the way snow slides off a roof before the roof itself comes down. The fjord water below it had gone strange, choppy in flat calm, disturbed from underneath by pressure waves.

"Wren," Isandro called. "How fast can you have the engine warm?"

"Already warm," Wren said, sliding out from under the cowling, grease to the elbows, grinning the fierce grin she wore when she was scared. "Been warming since the first groan. I figured you'd want to leave in a hurry. People always do."

* * *

They were twenty minutes from ready when the mountain moved.

Not the glacier — the mountain behind it, or so it seemed, a sound that came rolling down the fjord like freight cars coupling, and then the face of the ice let go. Not the whole wall, not yet, but a section of it the size of a village hall, a blue-white slab that leaned, hung, and dropped, and the crash of it reached them a full ten seconds later, rolling across the water, and behind the crash came the wave.

Isandro had seen calving waves before, from safe altitudes, from other fjords, white lace spreading out from a fallen berg. He had never seen one come at him down a narrowing channel with nowhere to go. The wave stood up as the fjord narrowed — two feet, four, six — a moving wall of gray water carrying ice debris ahead of it like a battering ram, and it hit the salt-flat strip with a sound like the end of the world conducted by an amateur.

The tender went first. It simply ceased to exist as a boat, flipped and folded against a driftwood log, and was gone. The fuel drums they hadn't loaded skated thirty yards inland. The wave ran up the bar, found the wheels of the de Havilland, and lifted the tail — Isandro felt the whole plane pitch forward on its nose gear, felt Wren's hand clamp his arm, felt the water suck back out with a hiss, dragging gravel, dragging the unloaded drums back toward the sea, dragging everything loose toward the water like the fjord collecting payment.

When the water withdrew, the strip was half what it had been. The soft ground at the south end was gone entirely, eaten into a new channel. The run available to them was four hundred feet, maybe less, littered with driftwood.

Nobody spoke. Then Wren said, in a small voice, "Good thing we were almost done loading."

And Isandro, absurdly, laughed — one bark of it, adrenaline finding the only exit it could — and then the glacier groaned again, deeper, and the laughter died in his throat.

* * *

They finished in silence. There was nothing left to say that the numbers hadn't already said. Mara stood at the wing with the tide table flapping in the outflow wind and called the figures like a surgeon calling for instruments: fuel state, wind speed, temperature, runway remaining. Isandro sat in the pilot's seat and did the final arithmetic with the honesty he'd promised Sefina, line by line, no mercy anywhere in it.

Max gross weight: exceeded by 620 pounds.

Available run: 400 feet, downhill toward a new channel, tailwind component 15 knots and building as the glacier exhaled.

Climb performance at overload: insufficient to clear the ridge at the fjord mouth before the sixth tide closed it.

Climb performance at legal gross: marginal. Possible. If the wind held steady. If the bar held hard. If the glacier gave them ninety more seconds than it wanted to.

There was only one variable left that he controlled.

The silver sat in the cabin floor, eleven hundred pounds of it, lashed down in oilcloth-wrapped crates. Bars stamped with the Meridian's own mark, salvaged fair and square from a wreck everyone else had given up on, worth enough to save the airstrip, pay the debts, buy Wren a future that wasn't stolen, put Sefina's expedition on solid funding, buy back in one morning everything the world had taken from him across one lifetime.

His father had stood on the deck of that ship and walked away from it, into a storm, in a boat, for strangers. And the world had called him a mutineer for ninety years.

Isandro unhooked the tie-downs.

He worked fast, before his hands could learn what his heart was doing, hauling crates to the door and rolling them out onto the gravel. Each one landed with a sound like a coffin lid. Sefina watched from her seat and said nothing, but her eyes were wet, and she put her hand flat against the window of her document case — her photographs, her notes, her catalogue of a ship that would now exist only on paper — and Isandro understood that she was making the same sacrifice in miniature, keeping the story and losing the proof, and that made it bearable, barely, that they were all paying together.

Wren jumped out to help with the last crates, no questions, just muscle, and when the final bar hit the gravel she looked up at him with her chin trembling and said, "For your dad," and he couldn't answer, so he nodded, and she scrambled back aboard.

"Sixty seconds," Mara said from the right seat, straps tight, voice level as a spirit bubble. "Wind's gusting forty down the channel. When that face lets go all the way, the outflow will be a hundred. We go now or we ride it out on the ground, and I don't recommend riding it out."

"Going now." Isandro ran the engine up. The propeller bit the cold air. Behind them, up the fjord, the glacier's groan had become continuous, a note held too long, the sound of something deciding.

The plane rolled.

Four hundred feet of gravel. Tailwind pushing the wrong way. The de Havilland heavy as a guilt, sluggish, wallowing, airspeed creeping — forty knots, forty-five — and the soft ground and the new channel rushing up and the airspeed stuck at forty-eight and not enough, not enough—

"Drop the nose," Mara said, calm as scripture. "Fly it onto the water. Ground effect. Skip it."

There was no runway left and no altitude and one move left on the board, and Isandro made it, easing forward, letting the wheels kiss out of the gravel and the floats — the amphibious floats Wren had rebuilt in a fever of work two winters ago that he'd cursed the cost of ever since — touch the flat water of the fjord beyond the bar, the hull slapping, skipping, skipping again, the plane hydroplaning on its own desperation while the airspeed built and built, fifty, sixty, and then the floats came off the water for good and the ridge at the fjord mouth rose up dead ahead, gray rock under white ice, and Isandro hauled back on the stick and the old girl climbed the way old things do — reluctantly, complaining, but climbing.

They cleared the ridge with fifty feet to spare. Below and behind them, the fjord opened up in the windshield like a wound.

The glacier let go.

All of it. The whole face, the whole leaning century of it, came down at once with a roar that they heard through the airframe, through their bodies, through the fillings of their teeth. The wave it threw was not a wave but a moving hill, and Isandro banked hard to keep it in view because some part of him demanded witness — demanded that someone, somewhere, see what the ice took.

The wave ran down the fjord and met the salt flat and erased it. It met the scattered silver on the gravel and took it, every bar, every crate, folding them into the gray water without ceremony. It met the place where the Meridian lay frozen in her cave, and the cave was gone — the ice bridge, the walls, the ninety-year vault — all of it collapsed into rubble and surge, and the sea poured in over the ruins, and the great bergs from the calving ground themselves into the channel like tombstones dropped by a careless gravedigger, and the water closed over the whole of it, churning white, then green, then smooth, then still.

In under four minutes it was over. The fjord lay quiet in the morning light, sealed at the head by a new wall of fresh ice, and somewhere beneath a hundred feet of water and a thousand years of future glacier, the Meridian rode out the rest of eternity with her holds empty and her secret gone ashore in a floatplane.

Isandro flew north in silence. In the cabin behind him, nobody spoke. He could feel them back there — Sefina with her documents, Wren with her grease-stained hands folded, Mara beside him watching the weather build over the pass — and the silence among them was not empty. It was the silence of people who had paid together and knew it.

His father's letter was in his breast pocket, over his heart, dry as bone. The logbook was in the bag between his feet.

That was the whole cargo. That was everything. Eleven hundred pounds of silver traded for a handful of paper, and Isandro Yarrow, flying into the teeth of a building storm with four hundred pounds of fuel and no airstrip to land on anymore, found — to his genuine astonishment — that his hands were steady on the yoke.

"Mara," he said. "Find me a river bar. Any river bar. We're going down somewhere in about forty minutes, and I'd prefer it to be soft."

Mara unfolded her charts with steady fingers, and outside the glass the first rain of the monsoon began to fall.
