## 20. Aftermath of Ice

The plane came down the way a tired animal lies down — not all at once, but in stages, each one more final than the last.

Mara felt it through her whole body: the stall warning's thin complaint, Isandro's hands steady on the yoke even as the engine coughed its last over the river bar he'd been hunting for since they cleared the fjord mouth. The Beaver settled tail-low into gravel and silt with a grinding shudder that went on far too long, propeller chewing mud, fuselage rocking like a boat coming off a wave, and then stillness — the enormous, ringing stillness that follows any violence.

Nobody spoke. Outside the frost-rimed windows, dawn was only a rumor behind the mountains, a gray suggestion of light on the water. The river ran shallow and braided past them, silver threads over dark stone, and the rain had softened to something almost gentle.

"Everyone report," Isandro said at last, his voice scraped raw.

"Alive," Wren said from the back, already moving, checking Sefina's harness, then her own, hands quick and certain. "Sefina's bleeding a little. Head."

"I'm fine." Sefina pressed a sleeve to her temple. Her voice was hoarse from the coded broadcast she'd hammered out on Halloran's own frequencies an hour before everything came apart. "I'm fine. Did it transmit? Tell me it transmitted."

"It transmitted," Mara said. She'd been watching Wren's face when the acknowledgment came back — a clipped Coast Guard callsign out of Sitka, repeated twice, asking for position. "They have us. They have *Cormorant*."

Isandro let his forehead rest against the yoke for one long breath. Then he unclipped his harness and turned around, and Mara saw him do what he always did: count his people. His eyes went from Wren to Sefina to her, cataloguing damage the way another man would count money.

"Out," he said. "Before the cold gets opinionated."

* * *

The river bar took them in without ceremony. It was a wide shelf of gravel and greywacke stone, half a mile of it exposed by the season's low water, edged with alder scrub going gold at the margins. Isandro walked the length of the plane twice, checking the landing gear, the prop strike, the belly skin where the gravel had scoured it, while Wren crawled underneath with a flashlight clenched in her teeth and pronounced the fuel lines intact.

"We're down hard but we're down whole," Wren reported, emerging with mud to her elbows. "She'll fly again if someone loves her enough."

"Then you're hired," Isandro said, and Mara watched the girl flush with something that wasn't embarrassment — it was belonging, arriving all at once after a lifetime of its absence.

They built a fire from alder and driftwood above the high-water line, and Sefina sat close to it with a compress against her head, dictating notes into a waterproof journal by firelight because her hands were shaking too badly to trust with anything finer. Documentation, always documentation. Even now, half-frozen and concussed, she was building the record that would outlive all of them.

Mara stood apart, as she had stood apart her whole life, and looked back upriver toward the fjord.

The sound reached them every few minutes now. Not a crack, not a shot — something older than either, a groan like the earth clearing its throat, rolling down the valley and across the water. Each time, the others flinched or looked up. Mara just listened. Somewhere beyond that wall of mountains, the glacier was finishing what it had started. The Meridian, the silver, the quinine, thirty years of Vess's hunger and three weeks of their labor — all of it going under the ice, sealed past any salvage, past any greed, forever.

Isandro had jettisoned the silver. She'd watched him do it, watched him kick the last crate off the float ramp into black water with his own hands while the calving wave chased them down the fjord, and he hadn't hesitated, hadn't even cursed. He'd kept the letter and the logbook wrapped in oilskin inside his jacket, and he'd thrown away a fortune like a man throwing away ballast.

She understood now that this was the whole man. Everything else was commentary.

"You should sleep," Sefina said beside her, without looking up from her journal. "You've been awake longer than any of us."

"So have you."

"Yes, but I'm concussed, so my judgment is medically compromised." A ghost of a smile. "Yours is just stubborn."

Mara almost smiled back. Almost. Then the groan came again from upriver, deeper than before, and beneath it — fainter, threaded through it — something else.

A voice.

She went still. The others heard it a heartbeat later: a human cry, thin and ragged, carried down the current on the wind.

"There," Wren said, pointing. Out on the braided shallows, where the river spread wide and shallow over the bar's downstream tail, something pale was snagged against a gravel spit. A body. Moving. Trying to crawl.

Mara was running before she decided to run.

* * *

He was facedown in six inches of water, one arm hooked over a chunk of glacial ice no bigger than a suitcase, the remains of the Cormorant's tender swamped and wrapped around a boulder twenty yards upstream. His parka had kept him alive; the water had taken everything else. His lips were gray. His hands, when Mara hauled him over, were the hands of a dead man, blue-white and clawed.

Captain Edmond Vess, thirty years on the water, master of the salvage fleet that had hunted the Meridian since before Mara was born, reduced to flotsam on a river bar sixty miles from anywhere.

"Dad." The word came out of her strange, unused, like a coin from a pocket she'd sealed years ago.

His eyes found her. They were clouded, unfocused, but they fixed on her face with the last of whatever held him together, and his mouth worked.

"Mara." Barely a whisper. "Mara-girl."

"Don't talk." She got her arms under him. He weighed nothing, which frightened her more than the cold did — when had her father become light? When had she stopped noticing?

Isandro arrived at a run and dropped to his knees in the shallows. "Together — lift on three—"

"He's hypothermic," Sefina called from the bank, already tearing open the first-aid kit. "Get him out of the water, get him dry, don't rub the limbs — warm the core, slowly, *slowly*—"

They carried him up the bar like something fragile and shameful. And there, kneeling in the gravel with her dying father's head in her lap, Mara arrived at the place the chapter of her life had been driving toward since she first read her father's second ledger — the one for grudges — and understood what kind of man he was.

Because she knew what he'd done. She knew about Halloran's payroll, about the dynamite, about the original abandonment ordered decades ago that had killed three men and ruined another — Isandro's father, blamed for the rescue Vess himself had refused to make. She knew, and here he was, and the river had already delivered its verdict. All she had to do was nothing. Stand up, walk back to the fire, let the cold finish the accounting. No one would call it murder. No one would even call it choice.

The wilderness forgives nothing, Isandro had told Wren once, out on the water. But people — people were allowed to forgive. That was the whole difference between them and the ice.

Vess's eyes were closing. His breathing had gone shallow and odd, stuttering.

"No you don't." Mara slapped his cheek, gently, then less gently. "Look at me. Look at me, Dad."

"Mara-girl." A bubble of blood at the corner of his mouth, or maybe just river water. "Should've... should've listened. About your mother. Should've..."

"Save it." Her voice cracked. "You can apologize when you can feel your hands."

Wren brought the fire closer — actually carried burning branches and built a new, smaller blaze right beside them, improvising a windbreak of gravel and driftwood with the speed of someone who'd grown up fixing things in emergencies. Sefina worked with hot water bottles improvised from canteens, tucked under Vess's arms and against his groin, counting his pulse with two fingers at his throat, murmuring numbers to herself like prayers.

Isandro knelt on the other side. For a long moment he said nothing. Then, quietly, so only Mara could hear:

"You know you don't owe him this."

"I know."

"And you know what he did. To my father. To yours, honestly, in a different way."

"I know that too."

Isandro looked at the old man's gray face, at the wreck of the fleet captain who had hunted a ghost ship for thirty years because he couldn't bear to be the man who'd once walked away from drowning sailors. Mara saw it land on Isandro then — the understanding, arriving late and all at once. Vess hadn't hunted the Meridian for the silver. He'd hunted it for the same reason Isandro had flown into a cave marked on a dying man's chart. Every one of them had been trying to salvage the same wreck: the day they failed someone.

"Then why?" Isandro asked.

Mara pulled the emergency blanket tighter around her father's shoulders. Downriver, the light was strengthening, turning the braided channels to hammered tin.

"Because I'm a navigator," she said. "And he's off his chart. Somebody has to bring him in."

* * *

Dawn came up slow and colorless, and with it came the helicopters.

They heard them first — the flat percussion of rotors echoing off the valley walls — and then two white-and-orange Coast Guard birds swung around the bend of the river, low, searching. Wren fired a flare from the kit, and the lead helicopter banked, waggled its wings in acknowledgment, and put down on the bar's widest stretch in a hurricane of gravel.

The next hours passed in the way hours pass after survival: in fragments, in shouted questions and blanket-wrapped answers. There was a corpsman for Vess, who pronounced him "lucky and stupid" in the same breath and loaded him onto a litter. There was a lieutenant with a clipboard who took their statements one at a time, and whose eyebrows climbed steadily higher as Sefina laid out the documentation — her salvaged copies, the photographs, the tide tables in Mara's hand, the coded broadcast log confirming the Mayday relay and the trafficking evidence.

"*Cormorant* is aground on the middle bar at the fjord entrance," the lieutenant said. "Hull breached. Hallorans' crew tried to scuttle her with the cargo aboard — your broadcast brought the cutter *Anacapa* down on them mid-effort. They're in custody. All of them." He looked at Sefina with something close to awe. "Whatever code you were using, it cut through their jamming like it wasn't there."

"Sefina Okafor," she said, offering her hand. "Marine archaeologist. Formerly disgraced. Currently available."

The lieutenant didn't smile, but his eyes did.

They flew Vess out first. At the helicopter door, strapped to the litter, he caught Mara's wrist with fingers that could barely close around it.

"The ledger," he rasped. "The real one. In my cabin — behind the chart drawer. Names. Dates. What Halloran paid. What the owners ordered in 'twenty-six." His eyes, clearer now, searched hers. "It won't clear Yarrow's father. Only the boy's papers can do that. But it'll bury the men who deserve burying."

Mara leaned close. She could have said any number of things. She thought about saying *this doesn't forgive you*, or *you chose this*, or simply *goodbye*. Instead she said the truest thing she knew:

"I pulled you out of the water, Dad. That's the salvage. The rest is up to you."

Something moved through his face — grief, maybe, or the beginning of an understanding thirty years overdue. Then the corpsman tapped the pilot's shoulder, and the helicopter lifted, and her father became a speck, and then nothing.

* * *

By afternoon the cutter's launch had come upriver for the rest of them, and the Beaver was slung beneath a Coast Guard helicopter like a caught fish, bound for Juneau. Mara stood at the rail of the launch with Isandro, watching the river bar fall astern, the fire pit they'd leave behind like a signature, the alders bending in the wind.

Wren was below, asleep in a bunk she'd claimed within four minutes of boarding, one boot still on. Sefina was on deck, writing. She never seemed to stop writing now.

"Juneau," Isandro said. He said it the way a man says the name of a country he's never visited.

"Nervous?"

"Terrified." He laughed, short and humorless. "Twenty-two years I've been the mutineer's son. It's a terrible job, but at least I knew the duties. I don't know who I am if the name comes clean."

Mara watched the water slide past, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream eventually.

"You'll figure it out," she said. "You've got a mechanic who worships you, an archaeologist who'd walk into fire for your paperwork, and a navigator—" she paused, and found she was smiling, really smiling, for the first time in longer than she could remember, "—who's already redrawn your charts. You're not doing this alone anymore. That's the part nobody tells you about being saved. You don't get to be alone afterward."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned back to the water, and she let him have his silence, because some cargoes take time to unload.

Ahead, around the last bend, the sea opened out — gray, enormous, glittering — and somewhere beyond it lay Juneau, courthouses, archives, a dead man's name waiting to be made clean.

Mara took out her father's ledger, the real one, and held it in both hands, feeling its weight.

Some things you pull from the water, she thought. Some things you carry the rest of the way yourself.
