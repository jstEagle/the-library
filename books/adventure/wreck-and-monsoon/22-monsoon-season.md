## 22. Monsoon Season

The rain came in off the Gulf the way it always did in August — not falling so much as arriving, a gray wall of it that Isandro Yarrow had learned to read like a second language. He stood on the gravel apron of Yarrow Field and watched it march up the fjord, swallowing the water, the treeline, the black shoulders of the mountains one ridge at a time. A year ago he would have counted what such weather cost him: grounded hours, canceled contracts, the interest accruing on a debt that never slept. Now he just stood there with his collar up and let it soak him, because there was nothing in it to fear anymore.

That was the strange arithmetic of survival. You could lose everything you'd measured your life against — an airstrip, a name, a father's honor — and discover that what remained was heavier than all of it, and worth carrying.

Behind him, the hangar door rattled open on its bad track, the way it had for thirty years, and Wren's voice came out ahead of her like something thrown.

"Radio's fixed. Again." She emerged into the gray light wiping grease from her hands with a rag that was itself mostly grease, sixteen going on forty-five, her braid frizzed out with humidity until she looked like she'd been struck by lightning and enjoyed it. "The moisture switch you rigged was a good idea right up until it wasn't. I bypassed it. If the set fries during monsoon, that's on you."

"You bypassed my moisture switch?"

"I *improved* your moisture switch." She tossed him the rag. "There's a difference. Ask anyone."

He asked no one. There was no one to ask who hadn't already taken Wren's side in every argument since the day she'd stowed away in his cargo bay with a stolen wrench and a grudge, and anyway she was usually right, which was the most irritating thing about her. The girl had a gift for machines that bordered on the theological. He'd stopped being surprised by it around the time she rebuilt his starter generator on a river bar at two in the morning by flashlight, swearing steadily in three languages, none of them learned anywhere reputable.

"Fuel's topped," she said. "Manifest's loaded. And before you ask — yes, I checked the quinine twice, and yes, it's the good stuff, and no, I didn't drop any of it this time."

"That was one crate."

"One crate of *medicine*, Isandro. People still bring it up at Klawock. Mrs. Tenakee made me a pie with a note in it."

"What did the note say?"

"'Next time, swim down and get it yourself.'"

He laughed, and the sound of it surprised him a little, the way it still did sometimes — easy, unguarded, a thing he owned outright. A year ago his laugh had been a currency he hoarded. Now he spent it freely and somehow had more.

Mara came out of the office with a clipboard and the particular expression she wore when the sky was about to disappoint everyone. She'd been reading weather the way other people read scripture for eleven months now, and she'd gotten good enough that fishermen three inlets over called the field asking for "the navigator's take" before they'd trust their own barometers. She stood beside him at the apron's edge and studied the wall of rain coming up the fjord with the narrowed eyes of someone negotiating with it.

"Window closes at fourteen hundred," she said. "Opens again maybe tomorrow night, maybe the day after. The low's sitting over the icefield like it paid rent." She glanced at him. "You can make Chenega and back if you leave inside the hour. After that you're sleeping on somebody's floor, and I know how you feel about Mrs. Tenakee's floors."

"They're honest floors."

"They're *sloped* floors. The whole house leans. You wake up feeling like you're still banking."

"That's how you know they're honest."

She almost smiled. With Mara it was always *almost* — a woman who rationed her expressions the way her father had rationed fuel, though where Vess had hoarded out of greed, she hoarded out of habit, and the habit was loosening. It loosened most, Isandro had noticed, when Wren was talking, or when Sefina sent postcards, or on evenings when the three of them sat in the hangar eating whatever Wren had decided to cook and burning half of it. The ledger of her face kept fewer grudges these days. Some entries, though, she'd never strike. Some mornings he'd find her at the north window looking up-fjord toward the glacier, toward the place where the Meridian lay under a hundred feet of new ice and ten thousand tons of collapsed serac, and her jaw would do a certain thing, and he would say nothing, because there was nothing to say that the silence didn't say better.

They had pulled Vess out of the water themselves. That was the entry Mara never struck.

"Fourteen hundred," Isandro agreed. "I'll be wheels-up in twenty."

"There's mail," Mara said, and held out the clipboard, and beneath the manifest and the fuel ticket and a bill from the parts house in Juneau that Wren had already annotated with the word *EXTORTION* in red pencil, there was a single envelope with no return address.

* * *

He flew the run to Chenega through rain that came sideways and light that came from everywhere and nowhere, the kind of silver-gray diffusion that made the mountains look like charcoal sketches of themselves. The Beaver hauled the load without complaint. She was a 1952 de Havilland he'd bought with salvage money — real money, finally, the settlement and the story rights and the small fortune Sefina's book had earned — but he'd kept her old on purpose. New planes didn't forgive. This one remembered every mistake he'd ever made and let him live through them anyway, which was more than most people managed.

On the seat beside him, wrapped in a garbage bag against the damp, sat the galley proofs of Sefina's book. *What the Ice Kept: The True History of the Salvage Ship Meridian.* Four hundred pages. Eleven years of her career destroyed and rebuilt inside those pages, her name cleared the same season his father's was, her blacklist dissolved by the same scandal that had ended Halloran's principal owner in a courthouse instead of a yacht. She'd inscribed the proof copy to him in handwriting that slanted hard to starboard: *To Isandro — who chose the letter over the silver. Nobody believed me that men like you existed. — S.*

He'd read it four times. Not the whole book — though the whole book was good, better than good, the kind of careful furious scholarship that made old men in academic departments write letters they'd regret — but the chapter about his father. Chapter nine. *The Boats Went Back.* In it, Elias Yarrow was not a mutineer. He was a man who took two ship's boats into a storm the owners had ordered everyone to abandon, and pulled six men out of the water, and was hanged in the newspapers for it while the owners collected the insurance. Sefina had written it with documents, with the logbook, with the letter in Isandro's own keeping, and she'd written it with a restraint that hurt worse than fury would have. The last line of the chapter was just his father's name, standing alone, restored.

Isandro had cried over that line exactly once, alone, at altitude, somewhere over the icefield, and had told no one, and knew perfectly well that both women in his life had guessed anyway and were kind enough never to confirm it.

Chenega came up out of the rain on schedule. He dropped the quinine, the antibiotics, the school supplies Wren had insisted on adding to the manifest out of her own pocket, drank coffee with Mrs. Tenakee on her sloped floor while she told him the pie incident had been Wren's idea of humor and the note had been meant affectionately, and lifted off again at noon with a hold full of fish and furs and one elderly passenger heading to town for a hip replacement, who spent the whole flight telling him about the winter of '49 as though Isandro hadn't heard it four times.

By the time he turned for home the ceiling was dropping and Mara's voice was in his headset, calm as tide tables.

"Yarrow One-Seven, you're looking at the front edge. Come in over the saddle, not the pass — the pass is full of weather and lies."

"The pass is always full of weather and lies."

"Yes, but today it's also full of wind shear. Saddle. Descend on the river. I'll talk you in."

She talked him in the way she did everything — precisely, without wasted words, with a competence so total it had become its own kind of tenderness. He broke out under the cloud base at four hundred feet and saw the field materialize below him, gravel gray, hangar roof green, and two figures standing at the edge of the apron in the rain, one tall and one short, waiting. Nobody had waited for landings at Yarrow Field in thirty years. Now there were always two figures. On days when Sefina flew in from Juneau there were three.

He touched down, taxied in, and found the envelope waiting on his desk where Mara had left it, weighted under a stone from the beach below the calving face — a stone he recognized, dark and veined, the kind the glacier gave back only when it was done with things.

He poured coffee, sat down, and opened it.

Inside was a chart. Hand-drawn, on oilskin paper gone soft with age, ink faded to the brown of old blood. It showed a stretch of coastline he knew the way he knew his own hands, and marked upon it, in a fine draftsman's hand, was a cave. No name. No date. No return address on the envelope, no note, nothing but the chart itself and, folded into it, a single sheet of modern typing paper bearing five words:

*The tide gives back again.*

Isandro sat very still for a long time, listening to the rain on the hangar roof, listening to Wren arguing cheerfully with someone over the radio in the next room, listening to the sound of his own breathing.

He thought about the last chart. About a dying old man paying him in gold dust and a hand-drawn map, and a cave the ice had hidden for a century and given back for six tides, and everything the cave had held — the ship, the crates, the letter, the truth, the four people who had walked out of the fjord together and never quite walked apart since. He thought about the price. He thought about the silver going into the calving wave, ten thousand pounds of it, swallowed whole, and how little he missed it. He thought about what Sefina would say, and what Mara would say, and what Wren would say, which would be some version of *when do we leave.*

And then he smiled — slow, private, the smile of a man who has learned the difference between a burden and a calling — and did not crumple the chart, and did not frame it either.

He opened the flat drawer of his desk, the deep one beneath the trays, where his father's letter lived in its oilcloth sleeve alongside the logbook pages and the photograph of Elias Yarrow that the historical society had finally reprinted with the caption corrected. He laid the new chart beside the old ones. Two maps now, ninety years apart, drawn by different hands, marking caves the ice kept and returned.

The wilderness forgets less, he thought. But family remembers more.

From the next room, Wren's voice floated through the wall, pitched to carry the way hers always did when she wanted to be overheard pretending not to be: "So are we going to talk about the envelope, or am I supposed to keep pretending the radio needed fixing?"

Mara's voice, drier than the inside of a drum: "Let him read it first."

"He's been reading it for ten minutes. I can hear him *not* moving."

Isandro looked at the chart a moment longer. Then he closed the drawer, stood, and called out through the doorway, loud enough for both of them, his voice steady and warm and entirely without dread:

"Wren — check the Beaver's range on a full load. Mara — pull the tide tables for the spring series next month."

A pause. Then Wren's whoop, and the scrape of a chair, and Mara's quieter voice saying something he couldn't catch but that made Wren laugh harder.

Outside, the monsoon rolled on across the water, patient as everything else the wilderness kept. Isandro Yarrow buttoned his coat, picked up his coffee, and went to find out what the ice was giving back this time.
