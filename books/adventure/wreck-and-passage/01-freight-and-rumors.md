## 1. Freight and Rumors

The fog came off the water at Pelican Narrows like something alive, and Hazel Joubert watched it from the cockpit of her Cessna 180 while the sky went the color of old pewter. She had been up since four, loading by headlamp: a crate of insulin packed in dry ice for the Kettle River camp, two sacks of mail, a case of shotgun shells somebody's wife had ordered in September, and forty pounds of flour that smelled faintly of the fuel drum it had been stored beside. Nobody signed for any of it. That was the point.

She kept no logbook that mattered. The one she carried was a fiction of round numbers and vague destinations, written in a hand slightly different from her own. Her registration papers named her Hazel Joubert, which was true enough now—ten years true—but the trail behind that name stopped dead at a border crossing in 2014 and started again three years later with a pilot's license earned under an instructor who had since drowned and could not be asked questions. Between those two points there was nothing. No lease, no bank account older than five winters, no photograph of her anywhere that she knew of. She paid Ruthie Okafor in cash for maintenance and Ruthie never asked, because Ruthie understood that some people's histories were like ballast—you dumped them to fly lighter.

"Weather's lifting," she told herself, or maybe told the plane, because after ten years alone in a cockpit you talked to something, and the airplane was the only thing that never wanted anything back.

She lifted off the gravel at first light, wheels leaving the ground before the fog had finished deciding whether to swallow the strip, and climbed into a morning so cold and clean it hurt to look at. Below her the territory unrolled: black spruce to the horizon, rivers stitched through it like silver wire, lakes still holding their ice in the shadowed bays even this late in the spring. Somewhere out there were eleven hundred people scattered across a country the size of a sea, and every one of them depended on somebody willing to fly into weather that grounded the mail planes and the medevacs and everybody else. That somebody was her. It was a good arrangement. It asked nothing of her but skill and silence, and she had both in quantities that would have frightened anyone who measured them.

The first stop was Kettle River, a mining camp of nine men where the cook met her on the beach with a rifle over his shoulder—not for her, but for the bear that had been getting into their garbage. He handed up a list scrawled on butcher paper and a jar of pickled eggs as payment-in-kind, and Hazel took both without comment, because refusing gifts out here was worse than rude; it was suspicious.

"Any word?" the cook asked, squinting against the prop wash.

"Word about what?"

"Anything." He shrugged. "It's been six weeks."

"That's the way I like it," Hazel said, and meant it, and saw him file that away somewhere behind his eyes—the way they all did, the quiet ones, the ones who noticed that the bush pilot never lingered, never drank with them, never said where she wintered. They didn't press. Out here privacy was currency and everyone respected another person's hoard. But they noticed. Noticing was free.

* * *

By midday she had made four stops and collected, in the ordinary way of things, more rumor than freight. Rumor moved faster than her airplane and needed no runway. At Two Moose Lake, an old trapper named Ferrier told her that the government survey crews had been spotted staking out the Sable Tidal Flats again, third time this year, and that men in town were saying Brase Salvage had the inside track on the concession.

"Brase," Hazel repeated, keeping her voice flat the way you keep your hands flat when a strange dog comes near.

"Corwin Brase himself flew in last month. Big floatplane, twin engines, the kind of machine that costs more per hour than I make in a year." Ferrier spat tobacco juice into the shallows. "Man looked at the flats like he already owned them."

"Flats are just mud," Hazel said. "Mud and tide."

"Mud and tide and whatever's buried in it." Ferrier looked at her a moment too long. "You ever hear about the Meridian Star? Went down out there before your time, girl. Supply ship, ran aground in fog, broke apart on the ebb. Some say the cargo was never all recovered."

"I don't listen to stories about wrecks," Hazel said. "Wrecks are just work for lawyers."

But the name went into her like a splinter, and she carried it aloft with her an hour later, turning it over during the long leg south toward Amos Vane's claim, and did not know yet why it itched.

* * *

Amos Vane's claim sat on a shelf of rock above a creek that ran orange with iron stain, ninety minutes' flying from anywhere with a nurse in it. He had worked it for thirty-one years, alone except for a dog that had died two winters back, and he was the kind of client Hazel valued most: he paid cash, asked nothing, and forgot her face between visits, not out of rudeness but because his memory had begun shedding things it couldn't afford to keep.

She brought him his order—cartridges, salt, yeast, a new blade for his filleting knife—and the usual gossip, which she dispensed freely since it cost her nothing and bought goodwill. She told him about the bear at Kettle River. She told him Ferrier's story about the flats. She watched his face when she said the word *Brase*, and saw something flicker there, quick as a fish under ice, gone before she could name it.

"You look thin," she said instead, unloading the crates onto his porch.

"I'm always thin." But he was thinner than always. His hands shook as he signed—or didn't sign, since signing was for people with paper trails—for the cartridges, and when he turned to lead her inside for coffee, an old courtesy, she saw that his gait had changed, the rolling sailor's walk replaced by something careful and stiff, like a man walking on a frozen lake he no longer trusted.

Inside the cabin it was dim and close, woodsmoke and kerosene and the particular sourness of a man who has been sick and hiding it. There was a chart on the table, half-rolled, weighted with a tin cup, and Hazel's eyes went to it the way a pilot's eyes go to weather. Charts were her scripture. This one was old, its edges soft as cloth, marked in faded ink with soundings and bearings in a hand that wasn't Amos's.

"Coffee's fresh," Amos said, and then coughed, a deep wet sound that went on too long.

Hazel stood in the doorway with her cap in her hands and did the arithmetic pilots do without meaning to: the sweat on his forehead though the stove was low, the gray of his lips, the tremor, the smell of sickness under the smoke. Fever. Something systemic. Days, maybe less.

"When did you last eat?"

"I eat."

"Amos."

He looked at her then, and his eyes were clear in a way that frightened her more than the fever, the terrible clarity of a man who has finished arguing with himself. "Sit down, girl. Coffee's getting cold."

She sat. She drank the coffee, which was terrible, burnt twice, and listened to him talk about nothing—the price of cartridges, the ice going out of the creek—while her mind ran checklists. Nearest airstrip with road access: six hours' drive from here, if the road was open, and it wouldn't be until June. Nearest hospital: the coast. Weather due in tomorrow night, a front she'd felt in her teeth all morning, the barometer falling like a stone down a well. And between here and the coast, three hundred miles of country that killed people who made mistakes.

"You need a doctor," she said at last.

"I need nothing." He poured himself coffee with both hands around the cup, steadying it, an old man's trick. "I've been dying for a year. Today it's just gotten chatty about it."

"How long have you known?"

"Long enough." He smiled, and there was blood on his lip where he'd bitten it coughing. "You'll take me out."

It wasn't a question, and Hazel felt the floor of her carefully constructed life tilt a degree, the way a deck tilts when the tide turns underneath you. "That's not how this works. You know that. I can't—"

"You can. You do. You've done it before, for people who deserved it less." He set the cup down. "Tonight. Before the front comes."

"Tonight is suicide. Tomorrow at dawn, if the ceiling holds—"

"Dawn." He considered this, and she watched him weigh it, a man bargaining with his own body. "Dawn, then. But you'll take me, Hazel Joubert. Because I've got something worth more than money, and I'd rather give it to you than let it rot in this cabin with me."

"Keep your money. I don't want—"

"Not money." His voice dropped, and the fish-under-ice thing moved behind his eyes again. "Something better. Something you've spent ten years thinking was lost."

Outside, the wind had come around to the southeast, and the light through the window had gone the color of brass. Hazel sat very still in a stranger's cabin, holding a tin cup in hands that had flown through everything the sky could throw at her and never once shaken, and felt them shake now.

"What are you talking about?" she asked, and heard her own voice come out small, stripped of the flatness she wore like a coat.

Amos Vane smiled, and began, wheezing, to laugh. "Your father," he said, "would be proud of how careful you've been. And furious at how much you've given up to be nobody."

The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the front edge of the coming storm, and Hazel Joubert—who had no father, who had buried that word a decade ago in a grave with no body in it—sat in the gathering dark and waited for a dying man to tell her why.
