## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The fever had Amos Vane by the throat when Hazel pushed open his cabin door, and it was not letting go.

She'd known something was wrong from the air. His smoke—thin, gray, the wrong color for spruce, more like wet paper than dry wood—had risen crooked from the chimney of a man who always burned clean. Ten years of flying this territory taught her to read smoke the way other people read faces, and Amos's smoke had been telling her *come* since she cleared the ridge line six miles out.

Now, standing in the doorway with her delivery satchel still on her shoulder, she understood.

He lay on his bunk in his long underwear and one wool sock, the other foot bare and bluish at the toes. The cabin smelled of woodsmoke and sour sweat and the sweetish rot of infection. A tin cup sat overturned on the floorboards, its contents long since soaked into the grain. His rifle leaned against the wall where he could reach it, though his hands trembled too badly now to have held it in days.

"Hazel Joubert." His voice came out like gravel poured slow. He tried to smile and managed half of one. "Knew you'd come. Smelled your engine over the lake."

"Amos." She set the satchel down—antibiotics for his trapline cache, as if medicine were what he needed now—and crouched beside the stove, feeding it two splits of birch because his hands couldn't be trusted with the axe. "You look like hell froze over and then thawed wrong."

"Felt like it too." A cough took him, deep and wet, and when it passed he lay with his eyes closed, breathing in shallow pulls. "Mail?"

"Mail. Medicine. And the news that the Hendersons' girl got married in Fort Nelson to a fellow nobody's met." She kept her voice light, the way you keep a lantern steady in wind—not because the wind isn't there, but because the flame matters more. "That's the whole paper."

"Good girl." He opened his eyes. They were yellowed, the whites gone the color of old ivory, and behind the yellow was a brightness that frightened her more than the fever did. It was the brightness of a man who has made up his mind about something. "Sit down, Hazel."

"I've got four more stops before dark."

"Sit down."

There was a register in his voice she'd never heard from him. In ten years of deliveries—flour and cartridges, letters and batteries, the small freight of isolated lives—Amos Vane had been one of her easiest customers. Gruff, punctual, paid in furs and gold dust weighed out on a scale he kept oiled like a church relic. He'd never once asked her to stay longer than it took to unload.

She sat on the stump he used for a chair.

"The weather's turning," he said. "You know it. I know it. That front coming down off the divide is going to sit on this country for three days, maybe four."

"It'll move through."

"It won't. I've watched forty years of fronts, girl. This one means to stay." He pulled himself up onto one elbow, and the effort cost him; she saw the tremor run through him like current through wire. "I need to be in Fort Meridian tonight. At the hospital."

Hazel looked at the window—at the sky already stacking itself into gray shelves above the western peaks, at the first fine snow ticking against the glass like fingernails. Then she looked back at him.

"No."

"You haven't heard my offer."

"I don't need to hear it. Amos, that front is a killer. I've flown through killers before and I'm not going to pretend I walked away clean either time. You're feverish, you're weak, and if we go down—and with you aboard, in that air, we would go down—there's no hospital for either of us. There's just the wreck and whatever the spring thaw gives back." She stood. "I'll radio ahead. Get the medevac fixed-wing out of Fort Meridian to stand by. The moment this breaks—"

"There is no *moment*, Hazel. There's tonight or there's nothing." He swung his legs off the bunk, wincing, and planted his bare foot on the floor. "Listen to me. I've got the sickness in my gut. Doc saw it last spring when I went in for my teeth. Told me straight, because Amos Vane doesn't want pretty lies. Months, he said. Maybe weeks." He laughed, a dry rattle. "It's been weeks. And now it's turned mean, and I can feel it moving, and I will not die in this cabin waiting for a front to blow through so some government pilot can carry me out in a plane with a nurse and a stretcher and paperwork. I want to die looking at the ocean. Or I want to live a while longer looking at it. Either way—I want to be *moving*."

"That's not a reason to kill us both."

"It's the only reason there is!" For a moment the fever-brightness flared into something fierce and whole, the man he must have been at thirty, at twenty, running rapids and staking claims while other men filed reports. Then it guttered, and he sagged back against the wall, breathing hard. "Name your price," he said finally, quieter. "Whatever you want. I've got gold dust. I've got a claim on Miller Creek worth more than the creek deserves. Name it."

"I don't want your gold, Amos."

"Everyone wants something."

"Not me." She said it flatly, and meant it, and saw him understand. That was the whole architecture of her life: she wanted nothing that left a mark. No debts, no favors owed, no name attached to anything that could be traced. She flew freight for cash and barter and never for credit, kept no hangar lease under her own hand, signed nothing but fuel slips in a handwriting that wasn't quite hers. Wanting things was how people got found.

Amos studied her for a long time. Outside, the snow thickened, and the light in the cabin went the color of pewter.

Then he said, in a voice entirely changed, quiet and deliberate, as if setting each word down on ice and testing whether it would hold:

"What about Elias Joubert?"

* * *

The name went through her like a crack through lake ice—that single sharp report, and then the long spreading shudder, the whole frozen surface of ten years groaning and shifting beneath her feet.

She did not move. She made herself not move. But the cabin seemed suddenly very far away and very close at once, and she could hear her own heart, and somewhere in the walls the fire popping, and Amos watching her with those yellowed eyes that missed nothing, that had never missed anything in forty years of reading country and reading men.

"Where did you hear that name?" she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Saw it," Amos said. "Thirty-one years ago, on a manifest, in the office of a shipping agent in Fort Meridian who owed me money and paid me in gossip instead. The *Meridian Star*, coastal supply run, listed crew." He paused. "Elias Joubert, deckhand, age twenty-six. Born the same year as a bush pilot I later got to know who never talks about her family and signs her name like she learned it from somebody else's letters."

The stove ticked. Snow whispered against the glass. Somewhere outside a raven called once, twice, and fell silent, as if even it understood the weight of the quiet.

"My father drowned," Hazel said. "Before I could walk."

"That's what the record says."

"*Is* what happened."

"That's what the record says," Amos repeated, gently, terribly. "And I've been carrying what else it says for thirty-one years, girl, waiting to find out whether you were woman enough to want it. Because most people aren't. Most people would rather their dead stay dead, tidy and buried, than learn the ground was disturbed." He worked himself upright again, slower this time, and nodded toward the iron-strapped chest at the foot of his bunk—the one she'd seen a hundred times and never once seen him open. "In there is a chart. Oilskin-wrapped. Marked in my own hand. And on that chart is a wreck in the Sable Flats that the tide has been keeping like a secret, and cargo enough under those ribs to buy any life a person cared to buy—including a life with no running and no hiding in it. And alongside that cargo—" his voice dropped, and the fever seemed to fall away from it, leaving only the truth, bare and cold as bedrock— "is proof of who was aboard that ship when she went down. And who signed the papers after. And why your father's death record reads the way it reads."

"Amos—"

"I'm dying, Hazel. Tonight, tomorrow, this week—it doesn't matter which, because it's soon, and we both know it. So here is my bargain, and it's the only one I've got left." He gripped the edge of the bunk, knuckles bloodless. "Fly me out. Not for the gold, not for the claim—you don't want those, and I respect it, God knows I do. Fly me out because the chart is yours by right, and I am the only man alive who knows where it leads, and I will not give it to you sitting in this cabin waiting for a front. I'll give it to you on the coast, with my own hands, with time enough to tell you what it means. All of it. Every name. Every date. Everything the record doesn't say."

"And if we crash?"

"Then we crash knowing." He smiled, that half-smile again, and it was terrible and kind at once. "Better odds than most men get."

Hazel turned to the window. The front had arrived in earnest now—she could see it eating the western ridgeline, snow streaming off the peaks like smoke from a burning ship, the ceiling dropping by the minute. Every instrument in her body, every hard-earned scar of judgment, said *no*. Stay grounded. Wait it out. Keep the nameless little life intact, the one built carefully, plank by plank, out of everything she declined to be.

But underneath the ice, something was moving. Something thirty-one years drowned.

"When does the wind back around?" she asked.

Amos's eyes gleamed. "Midnight. Comes around to the northwest, blows the front's teeth out. By dawn there'll be a window—a narrow one, four hours, maybe five, down the valley and over the pass before the next system stacks up behind it."

"Dawn," she said. "Not tonight. I don't fly that pass in the dark with a sick man and a dying engine temperature gauge, and you know it, and if you weren't half-delirious you wouldn't ask. Dawn, Amos. First light. We ride out the night here, I keep you warm and get fluids into you, and at first light we go."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he lay back against the wall, exhausted, and the fight went out of him like water out of a cracked hull.

"Dawn," he agreed. "First light." A pause. "Your father would've argued harder. He argued like a lawyer and flew like a poet, they said."

"I wouldn't know what he'd have done."

"No," Amos said softly, closing his eyes. "But you're about to."

* * *

She did not sleep.

She fed the stove through the small hours, boiled snow for water, propped Amos up and got broth and antibiotics into him by lamplight while he drifted in and out of fever-talk—names she didn't recognize, tides and soundings, once, unbearably, a fragment of a song she thought she might have heard before she was old enough to remember hearing it.

And between the feedings, while he slept, she sat on the stump by the stove and let herself do the thing she had forbidden herself for ten years: she said the name.

*Elias.*

Just once, silently, inside the closed room of her skull. And the ice cracked again, all the way across, and she felt the cold dark water of everything underneath—her mother's silence, the forged signatures, the decade of being no one in particular anywhere in particular—and beneath all of it, faint as a lamp in fog, the possibility that her father had not simply been taken from her, but *taken*.

Outside, near midnight, the wind backed around to the northwest exactly as Amos promised, and the front began to tear itself apart against the new current, ragged gaps opening in the cloud like wounds. Through one of them, for a moment, she could see stars.

At 4:40, the eastern sky went the color of a healing bruise. Amos opened his eyes without being called, as if he'd been waiting behind them all night.

"Well?" he said.

Hazel shrugged into her jacket, picked up her satchel, and looked out the window at the narrow, dangerous, impossible gift of a clearing sky.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're going to see the ocean."
