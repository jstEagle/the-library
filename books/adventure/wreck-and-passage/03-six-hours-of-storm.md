## 3. Six Hours of Storm

The barometer at Amos Vane's claim had been falling all afternoon, and Hazel had watched it fall the way she watched everything—sideways, without commitment, the way a woman watches a river she has no intention of crossing.

She'd told him dawn. She'd told him the front coming down off the divide was a wall of granite with weather painted on it, and that she didn't fly walls of granite at night, not for money, not for mercy, not for anything. He'd lain on his cot with the fever coming off him in waves she could almost see, a shimmer in the lamplight like heat over summer gravel, and he'd said, *Dawn's a gamble I might not live to collect.*

Now it was 11:40 at night and she was standing in the drizzle at the edge of her own airstrip—a mown strip of sedge she kept on a homestead lease under a name that wasn't quite hers, in a district where nobody asked twice—and she was breaking a promise to herself so old it had grown bark.

The Beaver's nav lights glowed red and green through the wet. She'd already pulled the covers off. That was the tell. There was a version of her, the sensible one, the one who had survived ten years by never committing to anything that could be traced, and that version had walked back to the cabin three times already. Each time she had turned around at the door and come back to the plane, and each time the distance between the door and the aircraft had felt shorter, as though the ground itself were conspiring.

Because Amos Vane had said a name.

She hadn't answered him. She'd stood in his stinking cabin with the woodsmoke and the sourness of sickness and she hadn't answered, and he had watched her not answer with eyes yellowed as old ivory, and he had smiled with the half of his mouth that still worked, and he'd said, *I thought so. I'll pay you at dawn. Bring the plane at dawn, Miss Joubert—or whatever it is you're calling yourself these days.*

So here she was, four hours early, because a man who was dying had said a name like a key turning in a lock she'd welded shut a decade ago, and she could not sit in her cabin one more hour with that sound in her ears.

She ran the preflight by feel. Fuel caps, oil, control surfaces, the drain cup of fuel tasting for water. Rain beaded on her forearms. The front was visible now even in the dark—a blackness deeper than the sky's ordinary black, sitting on the western hills like something waiting to be fed. Lightning moved inside it, slow and silent, the kind of lightning that meant business rather than theater.

She loaded Amos's stretcher—she'd rigged it an hour ago, lashing it into the cabin where the second and third seats usually lived—and then she went and got him.

He weighed nothing. That was the worst of it. Ten years ago Amos Vane had been a slab of a man who could carry a half-ton of gear on his back up a cutbank. Now he came up out of his bunk in her arms like driftwood, all bone and wet canvas skin, and he tried to help and couldn't, and he apologized between breaths in the flat, unembarrassed way of men who have spent their lives too far from anyone to be ashamed of anything.

"Shouldn't oughta be doing this," he said as she settled him in. "Bad night for it."

"Best night for it," Hazel said, buckling straps. "Nobody else stupid enough to be up."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that went on too long, and she stood there holding a mug of water he was too weak to lift, and she thought: *he's going to die in my airplane.* She had carried freight and mail and gossip and once, memorably, a drugged wolverine. She had never carried a dying man, and the weight of what she was about to do settled on her chest like the pressure before a storm.

She climbed in, pulled the door shut, and the cabin became small and close and real. Headset on. Master on. Fuel pumps check. She ran the start, and the old radial caught on the third blade and settled into that ragged, magnificent clatter that had been the truest sound of her life for ten years. Every instrument needle swung up and agreed to work.

"Amos," she said over the intercom. "Talk to me. If you go quiet, I can't tell if it's you or the engine."

"I'll holler," he said. "I holler good."

She taxied out, ran up the engine against the brakes, checked the mags—both rough but even—and turned her into the wind. The strip was nine hundred feet of sedge and old frost heave, and she used all of it, and the wheels left the ground with maybe forty feet to spare, and the black wall of the front was dead ahead.

She turned south instead. There was a gap. There was always a gap, if you knew where to look, and she had spent ten years learning where to look, because a woman who couldn't be found also had to be a woman who couldn't be cornered.

* * *

The first hour was almost kind. She flew the river valley south, down low where the air was smooth and cold, following the black ribbon of water by the pale smudge of its gravel bars, and Amos dozed against the stretcher straps and the Beaver droned and Hazel let herself believe, for whole minutes at a time, that she had outfoxed the weather again.

Then the valley bent east, and the front was there.

Not ahead of her. *There.* It came down the valley like a door closing, and she saw it first as a loss of stars, then as a loss of the far ridge, then as rain hitting the windshield so hard it sounded like gravel thrown by a hostile hand. The wind came with it, quartering across her course, shoving the Beaver's tail left, and she corrected, and corrected again, and the corrections started coming faster.

"Amos." She keyed the intercom. "We're going to have a rough stretch. You strapped in good?"

"Strapped like a Christmas goose," he said faintly. "Go on, girl. Fly."

The lightning was inside the cloud now, all around her, sheeting white through the rain so that for half a second at a time she could see the whole valley—river, banks, hills, the boiling underside of the cloud—and then it would go black again, blacker than before, and she would be flying on instruments and memory and nerve.

She had done this before. Not this bad. But before.

The trick, she'd learned years ago in a winter she did not talk about, was to stop arguing with the airplane. Let it do what it wanted within the envelope. The Beaver wanted to wallow and yaw and climb in the gusts, so she let it wallow and yaw and climb, keeping the needle centered and the nose where it belonged, hands light, eyes moving—airspeed, horizon, turn needle, back again—a rhythm like prayer, and the storm hammered the airplane and the airplane shrugged, because the Beaver was built by people who understood that the world is mostly weather.

Then the updraft took her.

It came out of nowhere, a fist of rising air off the ridge on her left, and the altimeter spun like something possessed—two hundred feet in four seconds, three hundred—and she pushed the nose over and the airplane went light and the rain went vertical past the windows, and then the downdraft on the other side of the gust front caught her and slammed her down five hundred feet and the river, which had been a comfortable black smudge below, was suddenly *close*, close enough that she could see white water on the riffles, and she hauled back and the stall horn sang its thin note and the Beaver wallowed, wallowed, and then the air thickened and she was flying again, climbing, climbing, hands shaking so hard she had to press them flat against the wheel to make them still.

"HAZEL." Amos's voice, cracked and enormous over the intercom. "HAZEL, GIRL, I AIN'T DEAD YET, DON'T YOU DARE."

"I'm not," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was a struck bell.

The radio was useless. Static like frying fat on every frequency. She tried the coast weather frequency twice, got nothing but the hiss of a sky full of lightning talking to itself, and gave up. She was alone with the storm, the way she had arranged her whole life to be alone with everything, and for the first time she understood the arrangement as a kind of poverty.

The second hour was worse.

* * *

Somewhere past two in the morning the engine started missing.

Not badly. A stutter, a skipped beat, like a heart with a murmur. Hazel's whole body went cold around the heat of her own fear. She checked the mags—rough, rougher—and the fuel pressure, which was fine, and she leaned the mixture and fattened it and the stutter eased and came back and eased again, and she made herself think it through the way her father had taught her to think things through, back when she was a child with braids sitting on a workbench watching his hands.

*One system at a time. One problem at a time. Panic is just doing all your worrying at once.*

Carb ice, maybe. She put the heat on full and held her breath and for ninety seconds the engine ran rougher, rougher, and then cleared, and the Beaver surged forward like a horse remembering its strength, and Hazel exhaled for the first time in two minutes.

"Still with me?" she asked.

"Still with you," Amos said. He sounded smaller. "Hazel."

Nobody used her name. That was the rule. Ten years of *miss* and *pilot* and *hey, you* and once, memorably, *the ghost of the Kettle River.* She had trained the whole territory out of her name, and here was a dying man saying it into the dark like it belonged to him.

"What."

"When we get in—" a breath, a long wet pull of air, "—when we get in, I got something for you. For the fare. You'll want it. Don't argue."

"I don't want your money, Amos."

"Didn't say money."

Lightning filled the cockpit white. She counted the seconds out of old habit—one, two, three, four—and the thunder came rolling after, and the front's heart was still somewhere ahead of them, and she had maybe an hour of fuel and a hundred and ten miles of night and a passenger whose breathing she could hear changing, getting shorter, wetter.

"Stay awake, Amos."

"Trying."

"Stay *awake*. Talk to me. Tell me something. Tell me about the coast. Tell me anything."

So he talked. It cost him air and he talked anyway, in fragments, between breaths—about the coast hospital and a nurse named Odile who kept peppermints in her pocket; about a dog he'd had in '09 that could swim the channel at flood tide; about a ship he'd seen once, wrecked on the flats, ribs sticking up out of the sand like the skeleton of a whale, and how the tide went out a mile and a half there and came back like it had a grudge—

—and then he stopped, mid-sentence, and didn't start again.

"Amos."

Nothing.

"AMOS."

A wet, dragging breath. "Here. Here. Just—resting my eyes. Keep talking yourself, girl. You got a good voice for the dark."

So Hazel talked. She told him about the river, about the wolverine, about the first plane she'd ever flown and how she'd groundlooped it in front of forty people at a fall fair and gotten up out of the wreck and walked to the beer tent as if she'd meant to do it. She talked and talked and flew and flew, and the storm came down off the hills and met them in the middle of the dark, and she flew through the middle of it with both hands and her whole heart and the sound of her own voice, because the sound of her own voice was the only proof that either of them was still in the world.

* * *

She saw the logging road at 3:40 in the morning, by accident, by lightning.

It was a straight pale line across a clearcut, running north–south on the flank of a hill, and she had flown over it a hundred times in daylight without a second look. Now it was the only thing in the territory flat enough, straight enough, and long enough, and the coast hospital was still forty minutes away through the worst of the front, and the engine was missing again, and Amos had stopped answering two minutes ago.

She made the decision the way she did everything—fast, and without telling herself why.

"Amos, I'm putting her down. Hang on."

She came in downwind of the hill to kill some speed, flaps half, into the wind along the cut, and the rain hit the windshield like a decision being unmade. The lightning gave her the road in stroboscopic flashes: gravel, crowned, a culvert crossing at the far end, a bend she couldn't see past. Wind quartering left at maybe twenty gusting thirty-five, and the trees on both sides standing up like walls.

She flew the approach on faith and flashes. Off the lights of nothing, on the sound of the engine stuttering, and at the last she saw the road rise up at her out of the black and she flared by instinct and the main wheels touched with a bang that went through her spine, and the Beaver bounced, and bounced again, and then she had it down, three-point, heavy, sliding on wet gravel with the tail swinging left and her boots full of rudder, holding it, holding it, and the plane rocked to a stop with the prop still turning and the rain coming down and Amos Vane not moving in the cabin behind her.

She got the engine shut down and was out the door before the blades stopped, into the rain, around to the cabin door, hauling it open, her hands doing the work while her mind stood back and watched in horror and awe.

He was breathing. Shallow, fast, the breath whistling. His eyes opened when she touched his face, and found her, and something in them was almost amused.

"Knew you could fly," he said.

"Shut up and stay with me."

She got the emergency beacon on. She got the survival kit out and the tarp rigged over the cabin door. She got on the handheld radio and called the coast on every frequency she had, and for twenty minutes she got nothing but static, and then, faint, broken, a voice—*say again your position, say again*—and she gave it: the road, the clearcut, the hill, the coordinates she knew by heart because she knew every inch of this country by heart, because knowing it was the only home she had.

The ambulance came at 4:55, chains clanking, lights sweeping the cut, and two paramedics who looked at the airplane sitting on a logging road in a storm like it was a thing out of a story, and then at Hazel, soaked through, and then at Amos, and got to work.

They took him on the stretcher down the hill to the ambulance, and he caught her wrist as they lifted him. His grip was nothing. A bird's grip. But he held on.

"At the hospital," he said. "You come in. You hear me? You come in after."

"You worry about breathing."

"You come in after," he said, "and I'll pay my fare."

* * *

The ambulance bay at the coast hospital smelled of bleach and diesel and old rain. Hazel stood dripping on the concrete with her cap crumpled in one hand, watching them wheel Amos through the doors into the light, and she had every intention of walking out to the truck stop, calling Ruthie Okafor about the shredded wingtip—she'd found the tear when the lightning flashed at shutdown, a six-foot rip in the leading edge of the left wing, fabric flapping like a wound—and getting her plane trailered and her life back under wraps before anyone asked her name.

She got as far as the bay doors.

Then she turned around and went in after him.

They'd put him in a curtained bay off the resus room, and a nurse with gray eyes was doing three things at once, and Amos's hand came out from under the blanket when he saw her, and she took it, and it was like holding a handful of cold sticks.

"Fare," he said.

"Amos—"

"Shut up and take it." His other hand came up from under the blanket, and it was holding something wrapped in oilskin, tied with tarred twine, the kind of package a man keeps oiled and ready for years because he has spent years being ready to die. He pressed it into her hands and folded her fingers over it with his own, and his eyes were bright and feverish and completely, terribly lucid.

"You'll want to look at that," he said. "Not here. When you're alone. And Hazel—" the grip tightened, the sticks flexing, "—when you see what's on it, you'll want to burn it. Don't. Whatever you do. Don't you burn it."

"What is it?"

"It's the truth," Amos Vane said. "It's the truth about the *Meridian Star*, and it's the truth about a man who signed a paper he had no business signing, and it's—" a cough took him, long and wet and awful, and the gray-eyed nurse moved in and Hazel stepped back, "—it's about your father, girl. It's about what really happened to your father."

The machines around the bed changed their song. The nurse said a word to someone behind the curtain and the word was a name Hazel didn't catch, and hands were suddenly everywhere, and she was being moved back, back, out of the bay, with the oilskin package hard against her sternum and the rain still dripping off her onto the hospital floor.

She stood in the corridor under the fluorescent hum, soaked, shaking, a six-foot tear in her wing and a dying man's package in her hands, and she understood that the life she had built—the nameless, traceless, careful life, ten years of invisibility—had ended somewhere back on that logging road in the dark.

She looked down at the oilskin. Water beaded on it and would not soak in.

*Don't you burn it.*

Outside, the storm was moving off down the coast, taking its lightning with it, and the first gray suggestion of dawn was coming up over the water, and Hazel Joubert—who was not Hazel Joubert, who had not been anyone for ten years—turned toward the door with the truth wrapped in oilskin held against her chest, and walked out into the beginning of a day that would change everything.
