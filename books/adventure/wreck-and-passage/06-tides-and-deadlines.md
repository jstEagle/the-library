## 6. Tides and Deadlines

The tide chart came free with the coffee at the Pelican Narrows diner, a flimsy pink sheet the harbormaster printed every month and left in a wire rack by the register. Hazel had taken one for years without reading it—tides were for boat people, for fishermen who thought in fathoms and moon phases while she thought in barometric pressure and crosswind components. She'd never needed to know when the water went out.

Now she spread it flat on the diner table at six in the morning, before anyone else was up except the cook, and read it like scripture.

The Sable Tidal Flats emptied twice a day. That much she'd known since childhood—everyone on the coast knew the flats breathed, that twice daily the sea withdrew for miles and left behind a landscape of gray mud and sandbars and channels that rewrote themselves with every cycle. What she hadn't known, what she was learning now with her finger tracing the columns of times and heights, was how little time the sea actually gave you. Low water lasted maybe ninety minutes of true exposure. The rest was transition—the flats either drowning or emerging, the channels running fast and brown, the sandbars going under an inch at a time until the whole country was sea again.

And printed across the top of the sheet, in the harbormaster's blunt block capitals, was the notice she'd been dreading since Ruthie first mentioned it:

**CONCESSION PERMIT 44-C (SABLE FLATS SURVEY & SALVAGE) EXPIRES END OF SIXTH TIDE FOLLOWING NOTICE. ALL SALVAGE RIGHTS REVERT TO CROWN PENDING RENEWAL OR REISSUE.**

Below it, someone had penciled in the dates. Six tides. Three days. After that, the permit lapsed into a gap—a window where technically nobody held rights—and then the reissue process would begin, and everyone in the district knew who had the money and the lawyers and the political reach to win a reissue. Brase Salvage and Concessions had been bidding on the Sable Flats for two years. The only thing that had stopped them so far was the cost of surveying twelve square miles of nothing for a wreck nobody could prove existed.

Until Amos Vane's chart proved it existed.

Hazel folded the tide table along its creases, carefully, and put it in her jacket pocket over her heart, which was where she'd been carrying everything important lately—the chart, the letters' promise, the photograph of the manifest page she'd copied in the records office. She finished her coffee. It had gone cold without her noticing.

* * *

The motel room still looked like a crime scene of paper. Charts overlapped charts on the bed; the desk lamp had burned all night; the ashtray held three cigarettes she'd lit and forgotten, which was unlike her, because Hazel Joubert did not waste anything, least of all attention. But last night she'd sat up until three reading the death record photocopy until the words *filed by: C. BRASE, SALVAGE & CONCESSIONS LTD.* had worn a groove in her mind, and then she'd sat longer, doing what she did best and worst: thinking about flying.

She stood at the window now, watching the harbor wake up. Fishing boats coughed out through the narrows. Gulls argued over something on the breakwater. And out past the point, visible even from here as a smudge of rust and white on the far anchorage, sat the survey vessel that had arrived yesterday evening flying no flag she recognized but broadcasting on a frequency she'd picked up by accident—a working channel, men's voices, coordinates being called back and forth in clipped professional cadence.

They were already surveying. Not the right grid—they were working the eastern flats, miles from where the chart put the *Meridian Star*—but they were here, they were equipped, and they had divers aboard. She'd watched their tender launch at first light, a black inflatable with two men in drysuits, running a search pattern as neat as stitching.

Six tides. Maybe five and a half by now. And somewhere out there under all that gray water lay a ship with her father's name in its crew list, his letters in its hold, and enough registered cargo to make a salvage fleet lie, cheat, and possibly kill.

Hazel turned from the window and looked at her airplane—or rather, at the memory of it, because the plane was forty miles south at Ruthie Okafor's strip, tailwheel assembly opened up like a patient on a table, waiting for parts Ruthie had promised to drive to the city for. The Beaver had carried her through ten years of invisible flying. It would have to carry her through this too. The question was whether it would be ready in time, and whether she would be.

She sat down at the desk and did the thing she always did before a difficult flight: she planned it until planning became a kind of prayer.

* * *

The chart itself she unrolled for perhaps the hundredth time. Amos Vane's hand was shaky but precise—an old prospector's drafting, learned in an era when a man's map was his reputation. The *Meridian Star*'s position was marked not with modern latitude-longitude but with bearings off three landmarks: the split rock on Cape Verrell, the old cannery chimney on Denby Island, and a stake Amos himself had driven into a sandbar twenty years ago, if it still stood. Triangulation. The way you found things when you couldn't trust instruments or other people.

Hazel had transferred the fix onto her own chart in pencil, light enough to erase, though she was beginning to understand she would never erase it. Then she'd done the arithmetic that frightened her most.

The wreck lay in the middle of the flats—not in a channel, thank God, but on a rise of hard sand the old charts called the Middle Ground, which meant it exposed at low water. Exposed was good. Exposed meant she might not need divers or cranes or any of the machinery Brase had and she didn't. Exposed meant a man on foot with a crowbar and a strong back could get into a hold.

But the Middle Ground was eleven miles from the nearest place a wheeled airplane could land—unless you counted the gravel spit at the mouth of the Serpent Channel, which the chart marked with a warning symbol and the words *unstable, shingle.* A spit of loose stone that shifted with every tide. Landing on shingle was a gamble even in a bush plane with big tires. Landing on shingle that moved was a gamble with worse odds than the storm she'd flown through to save Amos.

And she'd have to do it at low water, which meant dawn or dusk depending on the tide table, which meant either a night approach or a race against failing light, and the forecast for the next three days was building southwest wind with rain arriving behind it, because the coast was the coast and the weather owed nobody anything.

Hazel laid it all out on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments: tide table, chart, fuel plan, weight and balance. She spoke the numbers aloud to the empty room because saying them made them real.

"Eleven miles from the spit to the wreck. Forty minutes each way on foot over wet sand, carrying gear. Ninety minutes of low water, minus safety margin, call it seventy usable. Fuel for the flight plus two hours on the ground plus reserve. Weight limit means I take the crowbar, the pry kit, the camera, the waterproof bags, and nothing else." She paused. "Not even room for doubt."

The doubt came anyway. It always did. It sat on the edge of the bed in the shape of every bad landing she'd ever walked away from, every time she'd misjudged a strip or trusted a forecast, and it said, in a voice that sounded like her own: *You've spent ten years making sure nobody could find you. Now you're proposing to fly to the most conspicuous spot on the coast, in daylight, during a salvage operation, and dig up a shipwreck. You'll be visible from the moment your wheels touch. If Brase has a boat within thirty miles, you're done.*

And underneath that voice, quieter, older, another one—the one that had kept her alive through storms and engine trouble and ten thousand hours alone above the tree line—it said: *You've also spent ten years learning exactly what this airplane and this pilot can do. Nobody knows these skies better. Nobody.*

Fear and resolve, she was discovering, were not opposites. They were the same current, running different directions, and you chose which way to swim.

* * *

She spent the middle of the day walking the harbor, doing what she'd done her whole adult life: gathering information without leaving fingerprints. She bought rope and a pry bar at the chandlery, paying cash, chatting about crab season. She topped off two jerry cans at the fuel dock and let the dockhand talk about the survey vessel—"Brase's people, everybody says, though they won't admit it yet"—and how they'd chartered the *Northern Maid* as a dive platform and how the skipper was telling people they'd be on station through the week, weather permitting.

Weather permitting. Everyone said it like a joke. Hazel filed it like a fact.

At the harbormaster's office she asked, casually, about Permit 44-C, and got the full story from a man who clearly enjoyed having someone to tell it to. The permit had been issued eighteen months ago for a general survey of the flats—geological work, mostly, a cover for Brase's long game. It expired at the end of the sixth tide after formal lapse notice, which had been posted four days ago. After expiry, there was a statutory gap of one full tidal cycle—twenty-four hours and change—during which the flats were legally nobody's. Then the reissue competition opened, and Brase Salvage and Concessions was the only bidder with a survey vessel already on site.

"So whoever wants anything off those flats," the harbormaster said, "has got six tides to get it. After that it's Brase's backyard, fair and square." He laughed. "Man must be counting the hours."

"He probably is," Hazel said.

Walking back along the waterfront, she counted them herself. The tide table said low water tomorrow at 0547. That was Tide One. Then 1812. Then the next day's pair, then the next. Six chances. Six windows when the sea pulled back and showed what it had kept.

She wouldn't need all six. She needed one good one—one low water with tolerable weather, a serviceable airplane, and no patrol skiff between her and the Middle Ground. But she'd learned long ago never to bet her life on getting exactly what she needed on the first try. So she planned for six, and hoped for one, and prepared for none, because preparation was the only prayer that ever got answered.

* * *

Evening brought the radio call she'd been waiting for. Ruthie's voice came through the motel room's cheap set with the crackle of distance and the particular flat calm Ruthie used when she wanted you to know she was annoyed.

"Your tailwheel's fixed. New axle, new tire, repacked the bearings. Also found a hairline crack in the port lift strut fitting that would've killed you inside a month, so you can thank me whenever you're ready."

"I'm thanking you now. How bad was the crack?"

"Bad enough that I ordered the part from the city instead of welding it, which means you owe me for the part and the drive. Plane's airworthy as of an hour ago. She's tied down on my north pad." A pause, filled with static and judgment. "You're going out to those flats, aren't you."

It wasn't a question, so Hazel didn't answer it as one. "Ruthie—"

"Don't. I've known you ten years, Hazel, and in ten years you've never once told me what you're actually doing, and I've never once asked, and that arrangement has worked fine for both of us. But I'll say one thing and then I'll shut up." The static hissed. "Those Brase scouts were back in town today. Two of them. Asking at the fuel dock about a Beaver that flew a medevac out of the Vane claim last week. Asking who owned it. Nobody told them anything, because nobody around here tells strangers anything, but they wrote down tail numbers off the board at the strip, and yours is on that board."

Hazel closed her eyes. N-4173-K. Ten years of careful anonymity, cash deals, borrowed hangars, registrations layered through a holding company in a territory with lax records—and it could unravel because somebody's tail number was chalked on a public board in a town with one road.

"They know the number," Ruthie said. "They don't know the name yet. Records here are slow. But slow isn't never. Whatever you're going to do, do it before they finish their paperwork."

"Understood."

"And Hazel?" Ruthie's voice softened by a single degree, which from Ruthie was equivalent to an embrace. "Check your strut bolts before every flight. I torqued them, but torque doesn't survive a landing on shingle."

"You know about the shingle spit."

"I know everything about this coast. That's my curse and your blessing. Get some sleep. You sound like a woman who hasn't."

Hazel signed off and sat for a long moment with the radio's dying hum, looking at the chart spread on the bed, at the small penciled cross that was her father's grave and her inheritance and her deadline, all in one mark of graphite.

* * *

She slept badly and woke at three, and by four she was standing at the window watching the last stars burn off over the harbor, running the plan one final time.

Tide One, low water 0547. Too soon—she wasn't fueled, the plane was forty miles south, and flying a night approach into an unfamiliar gravel spit on her first attempt was the kind of error that ended careers and pilots together. Tide Two, 1812 tonight—dusk, falling light, wind due southwest at fifteen gusting twenty-five, a direct crosswind for the spit's orientation. Possible. Ugly. A coin flip.

Tide Three, 0631 tomorrow morning. Dawn light, wind forecast to ease overnight before the front arrived. That was her window. That was the one.

Which gave her today: fly south to Ruthie's strip, preflight the Beaver bolt by bolt, load the gear, study the approach, and be airborne before first light with the tide going out beneath her like the sea opening a door.

She packed in the dark, methodical, each item checked against the list in her head. Crowbar. Pry kit. Camera. Waterproof bags. Rope. The chart, wrapped in oilskin—Amos's oilskin, she'd kept the wrapping, it seemed right. Her father's letters stayed behind, hidden in the room's floor vent, because some cargo you didn't risk twice.

Before leaving, she stood at the window one last time. Out past the breakwater, the survey vessel's lights made a loose constellation on the dark water, and somewhere among them men were sleeping who would spend tomorrow hunting the same wreck she was. They had sonar, divers, money, and a boss who had erased a man's death with a signature. She had a patched airplane, a gravel spit, ninety minutes of falling tide, and a name she hadn't spoken in ten years.

It occurred to her, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that she would trade her odds against theirs any day of the week.

Hazel shouldered her bag, left cash on the dresser, and walked out into the cold blue hour before dawn, toward the strip, toward the plane, toward Tide One—because even if she couldn't use it, she intended to watch it go out, and see, for the first time in her life, what the sea kept hidden on the Sable Flats.

What she saw, when the water finally fell back that evening and the flats emerged gray and gleaming to the horizon, was not the wreck.

It was a boat. Small, dark, running without lights up the Serpent Channel, straight toward the Middle Ground—and someone aboard it was sweeping a flashlight across the wet sand, searching.
