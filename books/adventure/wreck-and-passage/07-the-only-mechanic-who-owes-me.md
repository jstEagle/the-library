## 7. The Only Mechanic Who Owes Me

The fog came off the water at Pelican Narrows the way it always did, slow and gray and smelling of kelp and diesel, as if the sea itself were exhaling its secrets into the town's lap. Hazel brought the Cub in low over the breakwater, watching the runway lights bleed through the murk like candles behind wet paper. Her wingtip patch held. It had better hold. She'd sewn it herself at a logging camp two days ago, by lantern light, with thread she'd traded a carton of cigarettes for, and every minute of air since had been a negotiation between her nerve and that fabric.

The strip at Pelican Narrows was gravel and mud and optimism, three hundred meters of it wedged between a fish plant and a hillside of alder. She flared, touched down soft, and let the plane roll out while the fog closed around her like a hand.

Ruthie Okafor was already there. Ruthie was always already there; it was one of the mysteries of the coast, how the woman seemed to know an engine was coming before the engine did. She stood at the edge of the apron in coveralls the color of old rust, arms crossed, a cigarette burning forgotten between two fingers, and when Hazel shut down and swung out of the cockpit, Ruthie looked at the patched wingtip for a long moment without saying anything at all.

"Don't," Hazel said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to."

Ruthie dropped the cigarette and ground it under her boot. "That stitching's going to walk right off the spar in anything over twenty knots crosswind. You flew through weather on *that*?" She shook her head, but her hands were already moving, unclipping the cowling latches, running her palm along the fabric like a doctor palpating a wound. "You owe me, Joubert. Again."

"I know what I owe you."

"Nobody knows what they owe me. That's the trick of it." But there was no bite in it. There never was. Ruthie Okafor had pulled Hazel out of trouble twice before—once with a magneto, once with a favor to a customs man whose name Hazel had made a point of forgetting—and the ledger between them was written in a currency neither of them named aloud. "Hangar three. I've got a tailwheel seal that'll take me till morning, so you can have the space. Bring your bill inside when you're ready to be honest about it."

Hazel hauled her bag out of the baggage compartment, and felt the weight of it—the oilskin-wrapped chart riding at the bottom, wrapped now in a second layer of shirt, pressed against the letters she hadn't read again because reading them twice would make them real. She'd slept four hours in two nights. The fog tasted like iron.

"Hazel." Ruthie's voice stopped her halfway across the apron. "You look like a woman being followed."

"I'm not."

"No," Ruthie said. "But you look like one who expects to be. There's a difference, and I've seen both."

* * *

Ruthie Okafor kept her books the way she kept her tools: everything in its place, nothing labeled for anyone else to understand. The hangar office smelled of solvent and cold coffee and the particular metallic sweetness of hydraulic fluid, and she sat at the desk after midnight with the lamp turned low, doing sums she didn't need to do, because the numbers were only something for her hands while her mind worked on other arithmetic entirely.

Three men had come through Pelican Narrows in the past week asking questions. Not together—that would have been stupid—but with the same smell on them, the same way of standing, weight forward, like dogs on a scent line. The first had been pleasant enough, buying fuel he didn't need, asking who'd flown in lately from up-country. The second had flashed a laminated card from Brase Salvage and Concessions and asked about a medevac flight, a bush pilot, a sick prospector. The third hadn't asked anything at all. He'd just sat in the café by the window for six hours drinking coffee and watching the strip, and when Ruthie had walked past him he'd caught her eye and held it a beat too long, the way a man does when he wants you to know he's counted you.

She'd told them all the same thing: nothing. She didn't keep records of transient traffic, she didn't gossip about customers, and if they wanted flight logs they could take it up with the territorial aviation board, which had no jurisdiction over her hangar and knew it. They'd smiled and thanked her and left tips too big, and each of them had left something else behind too—a pressure in the room, a change in the air, the way a barometer falls before weather nobody can see yet.

Now Hazel Joubert had come in on a patched wingtip with a face like a woman carrying a secret the size of a shipwreck, and Ruthie was doing math she didn't like.

Because here was the thing about owing someone: it cut both ways. Ruthie owed Hazel for the winter three years back when the fish plant burned and the only reason Ruthie's own little house above the shop hadn't gone with it was that Hazel had spotted the smoke from the air, landed on the road, and dragged a hose line until the volunteer crew arrived. And Hazel owed Ruthie for the magneto, and the customs favor, and for the simple fact that in ten years of flying invisible, Hazel Joubert had trusted exactly one person on this coast with the knowledge of where she slept, and that person was sitting at this desk.

Trust was a debt that collected interest. And somebody was coming to collect.

Ruthie heard the footsteps on the gravel outside—Hazel's, she knew them, that uneven rhythm of a woman who'd spent a decade walking like she might have to run—and watched through the window as Hazel crossed toward the hangar, paused, and glanced back at the fog line twice before going in.

Then Ruthie opened her desk drawer, took out the small notebook where she kept the names of everyone who'd asked about anyone, and tore out the page.

Some debts you pay before they're called.

* * *

Hazel found the hangar cold and cavernous, lit by two work lamps that threw more shadow than light. Ruthie had the Cub's tail up on sawhorses already, the wheel half-disassembled, parts laid out on a canvas sheet in a geometry only she could read. A space heater ticked in the corner, fighting a losing war against the damp.

"You work fast," Hazel said.

"I work scared. Different thing." Ruthie didn't look up. "Sit down or make yourself useful. There's coffee in the thermos, and before you ask—the good kind, not the kind that eats the cup."

Hazel poured a cup and sat on an oil drum, cradling it, letting the heat soak into her palms. For a while neither of them spoke. This was another thing about Ruthie: she understood silence, treated it like a tool rather than a void. The sounds of the hangar filled the space between them—the tick of cooling metal, the fog pressing against the roof, somewhere out in the dark the low horn of a boat feeling its way up the channel.

"The wing first," Ruthie said finally. "Then the tailwheel. Then whatever it is you're actually here for."

"I'm here for repairs."

"Mm." Ruthie held a cotter pin up to the lamplight, squinted at it, discarded it. "I've known you nine years, Hazel. In nine years you've never once come to me with a straight story, and I've never once needed one. But I'm telling you now, free of charge: something's changed. You're carrying weight in that bag that isn't clothes. You checked the apron twice walking in. And you came in through fog on fabric I wouldn't trust to wrap a sandwich." She set down her wrench and looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were steady and dark and very hard to lie to. "So either you tell me what's changed, or I decide for myself, and my imagination is meaner than your truth."

Hazel looked into her coffee. The surface trembled faintly—from her hands, she realized. When had her hands started shaking?

"If I tell you," she said, "you're in it. Whatever 'it' is. You understand that. These men—if they're who I think they are—they don't leave loose threads."

"I've been a loose thread my whole life, girl. Nobody's pulled me yet." Ruthie wiped her hands slowly on a rag, giving Hazel time, the way she always did. "Who is it?"

"Brase."

The name dropped into the hangar like a wrench onto concrete. Ruthie went still—not dramatically, just completely, the way water goes still before it freezes. "Corwin Brase. Salvage and Concessions."

"You know him?"

"I know his money. Half the new equipment on this coast comes through his leasing outfit, and every piece of it comes with a hook in it." Ruthie stood, walked to the window, and looked out at the fog for a long moment. "Three men came through here this week asking about you. I told them nothing. One of them was Brase's—had the card, the voice, all of it. Asking about a medevac flight. An old prospector. A pilot who doesn't exist on paper."

Hazel's stomach went cold. "What did you tell them?"

"That I don't keep records and don't gossip." Ruthie turned back. "Which is true. But Hazel—men like that don't ask questions because they want answers. They ask questions so you know they're asking. It's a message. *We're here. We see you.*"

"They traced the flight." Hazel said it flatly, working it through. Amos's cabin empty, the stove still warm, the neighbor who'd seen the Cub take off at dawn. A radio call here, a fuel purchase there. Ten years of invisibility undone by one act of mercy. "They traced the flight, and now they're following the trail backward, camp by camp, strip by strip. Looking for whoever flew him out."

"And they'll end up here, because everything ends up here eventually." Ruthie came back and crouched by the tailwheel, but her hands stayed quiet on her knees. "So. What did Amos Vane give you that's worth this much smoke?"

Hazel hesitated. Then she set down the coffee, dug into her bag, and unwrapped the oilskin.

The chart lay open on the canvas between them, lamplight pooling on the old paper, the inked lines of the Sable Tidal Flats sharp as scars. Ruthie leaned over it and said nothing for a long time. Her finger hovered above the X, above the notation in Amos Vane's cramped hand, and Hazel watched her read the marginal note without touching it—the date, the tide tables copied into the border, the single word *Meridian*.

"Sweet suffering God," Ruthie breathed. "That's the *Star*. That wreck's been a ghost story on this coast for thirty years. Every drunk in every harbor claims to know where she sits, and none of them ever found her." She looked up. "And Vane gave this to *you*."

"He paid me with it. For the flight."

"For the flight." Ruthie laughed, short and disbelieving. "Man pays with a fortune and you're the only one holding the receipt." Her laughter died as fast as it came. "You know what Brase's concession covers. The flats. If those permits renew next month, everything out there is his by law. Which means that chart isn't worth a fortune, Hazel. It's worth exactly as long as it takes him to learn it exists."

"He already knows it exists. He just doesn't know I have it." Hazel rolled the chart carefully, rewrapped it. "Or where."

"Yet." Ruthie stood and paced once, twice, the way she did when an engine wouldn't tell her what was wrong with it. "Listen to me. Those scouts aren't guessing. Brase doesn't guess—he buys information. Somebody talked. Maybe the man who saw you take off, maybe a radio operator, maybe—" she stopped, and Hazel saw her arrive at the thought at the same moment Hazel did.

"Maybe somebody here."

"Maybe somebody here," Ruthie agreed quietly. "I told them nothing. But I'm not the only pair of eyes on this strip, and I'm sure not the cheapest. The café keeper saw your tail number. The fuel dock logs every arrival whether I like it or not. You want to know who's trustworthy in this town? Nobody. Including me, if the price gets high enough—and I'd like to believe otherwise, but I've lived here forty years and watched better people than me sell out friends for less than Brase would offer." She met Hazel's eyes. "I'm telling you this because you deserve to hear it plain, not because I plan to betray you. I don't. But you should plan like I might."

It should have stung. Instead it settled over Hazel like something almost like relief—the cold comfort of honesty after weeks of calculation. "That's the most generous thing anyone's said to me in years."

"I'm a generous woman. Ask anybody. Don't actually ask anybody." Ruthie picked up her wrench again, and some of the tension went out of her shoulders as her hands found their work. "Tailwheel seal tonight, wing properly tomorrow—I've got fabric in stock and a sewing machine older than both of us. You'll be airworthy by tomorrow evening. After that—" she pointed the wrench at Hazel like a verdict— "after that you fly out of here and you don't tell me where, and I don't ask, and if those men come back I will look them in their expensive faces and swear on my mother's grave that I haven't seen you since spring."

"Your mother's alive."

"She'll forgive me. She raised me to lie to salvage men." Ruthie bent to the wheel. Then, without looking up: "One condition."

"Name it."

"When this is done—whatever 'done' looks like—you come back here, and you sit in that chair, and you tell me the whole story. Start to finish. Not because I'm owed it." She tightened a bolt with a sharp quarter-turn. "Because you've been alone in this too long, and I've watched what that does to people, and I'm tired of watching."

Hazel looked at her—at the rust-colored coveralls, the gray threading through her close-cropped hair, the hands that had kept half the coast's aircraft alive through sheer stubbornness—and felt something crack a little in her chest, some wall she'd built so long ago she'd forgotten it was load-bearing.

"Deal," she said.

* * *

Ruthie worked through the small hours, and Hazel slept in the hangar office chair with her boots on and the chart zipped into the bag beneath her, one hand resting on it like a child on a blanket. Twice she woke—once at a sound outside that proved to be wind, once at nothing at all—and both times Ruthie was still under the plane, lamplight catching the shine of her tools, humming something low and tuneless.

Near dawn, Ruthie sent her to the café for breakfast, against Hazel's better judgment, because Ruthie argued that a woman who never showed her face drew more attention than one who bought eggs like a normal person. So Hazel sat in the corner booth with her collar up and ate fried bread and watched the window, and the fog was lifting off the harbor in rags, and the fishing boats were going out one by one into the brightening gray.

That was when she saw the boat.

It wasn't a fishing boat. It sat wrong in the water—too clean, too new, too well-found for this harbor of working scowls—and it moved up the channel at a surveyor's pace, unhurried, deliberate. Painted on its bow, small and neat: a stylized crane hook inside a circle. Brase Salvage and Concessions.

Two men stood at the rail. One of them raised binoculars—not toward the water, not toward the fleet heading out for the morning haul, but toward the strip. Toward the hangars. Toward the café window, though Hazel couldn't say whether he saw her, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She left money on the table and walked back along the shore road at an easy pace, counting her breaths, keeping her stride even, while every instinct screamed at her to run. At the hangar, Ruthie was waiting in the doorway, arms crossed, and Hazel didn't need to say a word.

"Saw it," Ruthie said. "Been sitting off the point since midnight. They're not scouting anymore, Hazel. Scouting's done. That boat's posted up like it's waiting for something to move."

Hazel looked at the Cub—tailwheel rebuilt, wing stripped bare on the table, half-sewn, hours from airworthy. Hours she wasn't sure she had.

"How long till she flies?"

Ruthie chewed the inside of her cheek, calculating, hating the answer before she gave it. "Six hours. Seven if you want the wing done right."

Outside, the fog was burning away, and the boat in the channel sat patient as a spider, and somewhere beyond the headlands the tide was already turning.
