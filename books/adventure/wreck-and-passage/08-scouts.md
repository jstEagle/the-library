## 8. Scouts

The morning after the second night at the motel, Hazel woke with the chart's outline burned into the backs of her eyelids. She had traced it so many times in the dark—her finger following the penciled channels, the little cross Amos had drawn where the ribs of the Meridian Star lay buried in the flats—that she could have redrawn it from memory on a napkin, on frost, on skin. That was the trouble with secrets. They didn't stay folded. They unfolded *you*.

She dressed in the blue-gray light, counted her cash twice, and left the room key under the mat because she'd never signed anything at the desk. The Gullwing sat on the tie-downs at the far end of the strip, dew silvering its fabric like a fish just netted. She walked a slow circle around it out of habit and stopped halfway through the second circle.

There was mud on the step. Not her mud.

Hers was the gray silt of the coast road, dried to a chalky film. This was darker, richer, river-bottom black, still holding the shape of a boot heel with a deep-cut lug pattern—the kind of tread that came off a boat deck, not an airstrip. Someone had climbed up, looked inside, and climbed down again sometime between midnight and dawn. Nothing taken. Nothing left. Just the visit itself, the way a wolf leaves tracks at the edge of a camp without touching the meat: a message written in the only language the north speaks fluently.

Hazel crouched and studied the print for a long moment, then wiped it away with her palm, which was foolish and she knew it. You couldn't erase a footprint by smoothing the ground. It was already inside the house of her skull.

She flew out before the coffee shack opened, climbing east into a sky the color of unpolished tin, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the cold air at altitude. She knew better. For ten years she had been a rumor with a propeller—a plane that appeared on no schedules, filed no flight plans when she could avoid it, paid in cash, slept in rooms booked under three rotating names. Invisibility wasn't luck. It was craft, honed daily like edge tool. And someone had just put their boot on her aircraft in the night, casual as a man stepping onto his own porch.

*They're not looking for a plane,* she thought, banking over the tree line toward Vane's claim country. *They're looking for a story. And I'm the only chapter in it.*

* * *

Amos's cabin sat in a fold of hills two hours' flight inland, at the end of a creek that braided itself into nothing among the muskeg. Hazel had delivered medicine there twice a year for six years, always landing on the gravel bar downstream, always walking up through the alders with her collar up. She came in low now over the ridge and saw immediately that something was wrong with the shape of the place.

Smoke should have been rising from the stovepipe. There was none. And on the gravel bar, where her own tires had pressed twin furrows four days ago, there were other marks now—a heavier plane, twin floats or big tundra tires, had set down hard enough to gouge. Beside the cabin, a boat was beached. Aluminum, flat-bottomed, the kind salvage crews ran up creeks. Two men stood near it, and even from three hundred feet she could see they weren't prospectors. Prospectors moved like men carrying weight; these two moved like men carrying authority—one gesturing at the cabin door, one photographing something on the ground.

Hazel did not land. She pulled up over the ridge, throttled back to let the engine mutter instead of shout, and swung wide through a gap in the clouds where she could watch without her shadow crossing the clearing.

The taller one went inside. Came out. Went in again with a flashlight, though it was full morning. The other one knelt by the fire pit, lifted the grate, looked underneath. Looking for what? Ashes? Papers? A dying man didn't leave much behind but the smell of fever and a floor strewn with crushed ration tins. But they searched anyway, thorough as tax collectors, and after twenty minutes they walked the creek bank in slow parallel lines, reading the ground the way she read weather.

Then the tall one found what he was looking for. He held it up—a scrap of paper, pale against the dark gravel—and his partner crossed to him fast, and they stood head-to-head over it for a long time. Even at this distance Hazel felt the moment like a change in barometric pressure. A delivery slip. Her own handwriting, probably, on some carbon copy she'd left in the cabin years ago. *H. Joubert. Freight & Meds.* A name she'd worn so long it had grown into her like a splinter the body decides to keep.

The tall one took out a radio handset.

Hazel turned away and climbed, putting the ridge between herself and the clearing, her heart going hard and shallow. She flew the next hour on instruments of pure discipline—heading, altitude, fuel, breath—while her mind ran the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the boot prints. They had the cabin. They had the name. The medevac flight would have been logged somewhere, some tower, some nurse's intake sheet at the coastal hospital—*patient transported by private charter, pilot Joubert, H.* Ten years of careful erasure, undone by one act of mercy. She thought about that with a bitterness that surprised her: she had finally done something visible, something good, and it was the exact thread that would unravel her.

By the time she set down at Pelican Narrows, she had made three decisions. She would not sleep at the motel again. She would move the Gullwing to Ruthie's back strip, behind the timber, where it couldn't be photographed from the road. And she would stop telling herself the story that nobody was coming.

Somebody was already here.

* * *

In a rented office above the chandlery in Pelican Narrows, a man named Dreever spread photographs across a table scarred by fifty years of cargo manifests. He was Brase Salvage's advance man, and he had the gift of being forgettable—medium height, medium build, a face like a form filled out correctly. The two men who'd searched Vane's cabin stood dripping by the stove while he arranged the images in sequence: the cabin exterior, the empty bunk, the fire pit, the gravel bar with its overlapping tire marks, and last, held delicately by one corner, the freight slip.

"Joubert," said the taller searcher. "Hazel Joubert. Runs freight up the whole western drainage. No hangar, no office, no listing."

"There's always a listing," Dreever said. "It's just not where civilians look." He tapped the slip. "Medevac flight, four days ago, coastal hospital. Nurse's log will have the tail number if the pilot didn't file. Check with the harbormaster too—floatplanes have to take on fuel somewhere, and fuel is money, and money leaves receipts."

"And Vane?"

Dreever considered the photograph of the empty bunk, the blanket thrown back, the pillow dented by a head that would never press it again. "Dead or dying. Either way he gave the chart to somebody before he went. That somebody flew him out." He squared the photos into a neat stack. "Mr. Brase wants the site located before the fourth tide. He doesn't especially care how. But he does care about one thing more than the wreck itself."

The younger searcher waited.

"The pilot." Dreever smiled without any warmth reaching his eyes. "There's a history there Mr. Brase has a personal interest in. If you find her, you don't spook her, you don't touch her, and you don't say her real name out loud. You call the office. Understood?"

They understood. Outside, the rain had started again, fine as sifted flour, and down at the docks the first masts of the salvage fleet were beginning to show along the horizon like a row of needles threading toward the flats.

* * *

Ruthie Okafor found Hazel in the hangar at dusk, lying on a mechanic's creeper under the Gullwing's patched wingtip with a trouble light balanced on her sternum, though there was nothing left to fix. The fabric patch Ruthie had sewn was holding beautifully. Hazel was under there because it was the closest thing to a hiding place with a roof.

"You want to tell me why there's a stranger asking the harbormaster about floatplane fuel purchases?" Ruthie said. "Or are we doing the thing where you pretend you didn't hear me?"

Hazel slid out. Her hair had picked up a cobweb and a smear of grease, and she wore the expression of a woman doing math she hated. "What kind of stranger?"

"Forgettable kind. Bought diesel for his skiff, asked who'd flown in this week, tipped too much. Harbormaster says he wrote down your tail number off the tie-down before I moved you." Ruthie leaned against the workbench and crossed her arms. "I moved you like you asked. Back strip, behind the trees. But Hazel—men like that don't write things down for fun. They write them down for other men."

"I know."

"Do you?" Ruthie's voice stayed level, but it gained that iron undertone she saved for engines and emergencies. "Because you've got the look of somebody building a plan out of hope. Hope is not structural, girl. I've seen planes built on worse."

Hazel sat up on the creeper and looked at her hands. They were steady, which annoyed her; she wanted them to shake, wanted some outward proof that the fear churning in her gut was real and proportionate. "Amos gave me a chart," she said. "And the chart is worth more than either of us understood, and the people who want it know my face now, or will by tomorrow. That's the whole of it. That's everything I can say out loud."

Ruthie studied her for a long moment. Twenty years of fixing other people's airplanes in a town that forgot her name taught a person to read silence the way pilots read sky, and she read Hazel's now—read the grief under the fear, and the stubbornness under the grief.

"All right," she said finally. "Back strip's yours as long as you need. There's a cot in the loft and a lock on the hangar door that actually works, unlike the motel. And Hazel—" she paused at the door, rain hissing behind her, "—whoever's hunting you, they'll try the soft places first. The people who help you. That's how it's done. So decide now, before they ask me something, exactly what you want me to say. Because I'll say whatever keeps you flying. But I'd rather it be the truth than a lie I have to remember."

Hazel looked at her, and something in her chest cracked—not painfully, the way ice cracks underfoot, but the way a window cracks in a fire, letting in the very air that feeds it.

"The truth," she said. "From now on. All of it."

"That's going to cost you," Ruthie said.

"It already has."

* * *

Hazel didn't sleep in the loft. She slept badly in the Gullwing's seat, reclined against her pack, the hangar smelling of dope and fabric and cold metal, waking at every sound—the settling of the roof, a dog three streets over, the rain changing its mind. Toward three in the morning she gave up and walked out into the wet dark to check the strip, and that was when she saw the light.

It was out on the water, beyond the breakwater—a running lamp moving where no honest traffic moved at that hour. Pelican Narrows' channel was well marked and well known; boats ran it in daylight or with local pilots. This light was outside the channel, standing off the point, motionless except for a slow, patient drift. Then a second light joined it, lower, sweeping—a searchlight, playing across the water near the mouth of the creek that led to Ruthie's back strip.

Not searching the water. Searching the shore.

Hazel stood in the shadows of the hangar eave with rain gathering on her shoulders and watched the beam swing past the tree line, once, twice, methodical as a lighthouse. They hadn't found the strip yet—the trees were doing their work—but they were narrowing. Tonight the point. Tomorrow the creek. The day after, the clearing.

She thought of all the years she'd spent perfecting the art of not being seen: the unlisted everything, the cash, the false ease of the name Joubert worn like a borrowed coat. She had believed invisibility was safety. Standing in the rain, watching the light crawl along the shore toward everything she had left, she understood at last that it had never been safety. It had only been delay. A man alone and unnamed can vanish, yes—but he can also be erased, and no one will ever come asking. Her father had been invisible too, in the end. Filed away by a man with a stamp, mourned by no registry anywhere.

The searchlight swept past the trees one final time and died. The running lamps turned, unhurried, back toward the fleet anchorage down-coast.

Hazel went inside, lit the trouble light, and unrolled Amos's chart on the workbench, weighting the corners with socket wrenches. Six tides. The permits expired at high water on the sixth, and after that the flats belonged to whoever held the next license, and everyone in the territory knew whose signature was already inked on it. She had maybe three usable low-water windows before then—three chances to reach the Meridian Star, to find whatever her father had left in the hold, to hold proof in her hands before the fleet's divers got there.

Three chances, and a hunter's light already sweeping her shore.

She took out her notebook and began, in a hand steadier than she felt, to plan the first run—fuel load, tide tables, the channel Teo-less and unknown, every sandbar she'd have to read from the air at speed. Halfway down the page she stopped, pen hovering, and wrote one line at the top in letters larger than the rest:

*They know the name Joubert. They don't yet know what she's flying for.*

Outside, the rain thickened toward dawn, and down the coast, unseen, the first crane mast of the salvage fleet began to rise against the gray water like a gallows being built in reverse—for hanging, or for hoisting treasure, depending entirely on who reached the flats first.
