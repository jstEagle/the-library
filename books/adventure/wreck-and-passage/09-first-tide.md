## 9. First Tide

The tide table said low water at 4:17 in the morning, and Hazel was airborne by three.

She flew without lights except the faint amber glow of her instrument panel, out over the black shoulder of the coast where the Sable Flats began their slow exhale. The moon had set an hour before, and the world below her was not water and not land but something older than either—a darkness so complete it seemed to press up against the floats of the plane like a hand testing its weight. She had flown these flats a dozen times in daylight, hauling survey crews and fishery inspectors who never asked why she kept no fixed address, and she knew them the way you know a scar: by feel, by memory, by the particular way the channels braided and unbraided with every tide.

But night was different. Night stripped away everything except sound and instinct, and so she flew by both—by the hiss of water over gravel bars she could not see, by the smell of kelp and cold mud rising through the vent, by the memory of Amos Vane's shaking finger tracing the chart in the ambulance bay while machines counted down what was left of him.

*There,* he had said. *Where the ribs show at half tide. She's been there sixty years and nobody's looked.*

The engine droned. The air was cold enough that her breath fogged the windshield in small ghosts, and she wiped it with the back of her wrist and thought about nothing, which was a discipline she had practiced for ten years and was very good at until recently.

Below her now, the flats were beginning to give themselves up. She could hear it—the tide going out made a sound like a long sigh, like something enormous settling into sleep—and as the eastern sky loosened from black to iron to a bruised gray-green, the shapes came up out of the dark one by one: sandbars like the backs of sleeping animals, channel cuts gleaming like veins of mercury, the humped islands where stunted spruce clung sideways to whatever soil the wind had allowed them to keep.

Hazel throttled back and let the plane sink toward the flats, flying low the way she'd flown since she was nineteen years old, when a man named Callum Joubert put his hand on the back of her head and told her *the ground will tell you if you listen*. Her father's voice. Ten years buried and still it arrived uninvited, riding in on the cold air through every crack in the fuselage.

She listened anyway.

* * *

She found the wreck at first light, exactly where the chart promised.

The Meridian Star lay in the throat of a channel the chart called the Cut Behind Gull Island, and when the tide dropped far enough, she rose out of the mud like the skeleton of something that had once been magnificent. Hazel circled her twice, then three times, banking wide so she could take her in whole. Sixty years of tides had stripped her to her bones. The hull planking was long gone, rotted or salvaged or simply surrendered, but the ribs remained—dozens of them, arcing up out of the tidal silt in perfect parallel curves, black with age and furred with barnacle, so that from the air she looked less like a ship than like the fossilized ribcage of a leviathan that had lain down here to die and been slowly eaten by the sea.

At her stern, the keel still stood, and part of the transom, and Hazel could just make out the ghost of lettering beneath the weed—faded almost to nothing, but there. *MERIDIAN STAR.* Her father had touched that wood. Her father had walked that deck, or slept below it, in the last days of a life that ended—according to a piece of paper signed by Corwin Brase—in some other place, some other manner, some lie so clean it had held for a decade.

Her hands were steady on the yoke. That surprised her. Inside, everything was moving—grief and hope and fury churning together like the sediment the tide stirred up—but her hands stayed level, because that was the trade she'd chosen at nineteen: keep your hands level, keep your name quiet, keep flying.

She keyed the camera she'd rigged to the passenger-side window mount and began her passes.

Low and slow, engine at half song, holding two hundred feet off the flats while the shutter clicked. Wide shots of the whole channel, showing the wreck's position against the sandbars. Closer passes along the ribs, where the morning light raked sideways and threw long shadows between the frames—shadows that might be hiding a hold entrance, a cargo hatch, anything. She shot the transom twice. She shot the mudflats around the wreck in overlapping strips, building a picture of the whole site the way Amos had taught her to build a picture of weather: piece by piece, patiently, trusting the pattern to emerge.

And it was on the fourth pass, the closest one, eighty feet above the stern section, that she saw the boot prints.

* * *

They were there and then they weren't, depending on the angle of the light, and that was what saved them from being dismissed. At first glance the flats looked untouched—smooth gray-brown silt stretching away from the wreck in every direction, unbroken except for the wandering script of gull tracks and the delicate stitched lines left by the tide itself as it withdrew. But as Hazel banked and the sun came around behind her, the shadows reversed, and there they were: a line of footprints, deep and deliberate, coming out of the channel cut to the north, crossing forty yards of flat, and ending at the base of the wreck's stern.

Coming. Not going.

She pulled up hard, heart suddenly loud, and climbed to five hundred feet to look at the whole picture again. No boat visible in the channel. No camp on Gull Island—she swept it twice, reading the sparse spruce for any break in its silhouette, any glint of canvas or metal. Nothing. The prints led from the water's edge to the wreck and stopped there, as if whoever made them had gone *into* the ship and not come out—or had waded back down the channel on the ebb, their return erased by the same water that would soon erase everything else.

The tide would come back in four hours. Whatever those prints meant would be gone by afternoon, smoothed away like a sentence rubbed off a slate.

Hazel flew north along the channel, following it the way the prints implied someone had come, looking for an anchor buoy, a skiff pulled into the grass, a cache barrel. The channel bent east around a bar called the Spine, narrowed, shallowed—she could see the current lines writhing over it even at this distance, white water standing up in little combs against the ebb—and there, wedged in the lee of the Spine where the current couldn't reach it, sat a rubber Zodiac, deflated at the bow, camouflaged under a spread of netting that from two hundred feet read as nothing more than a patch of dead eelgrass.

Someone had come by water in the dark. Someone had walked to the wreck. And someone was either still aboard the Meridian Star or had left with the tide and meant to come back.

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. She took one more photograph—the Zodiac, close, unmistakable—and then climbed away from the flats in a long spiraling turn, putting altitude between herself and the channel, and let the fear arrive all at once, the way cold water arrives when a seal breaks the surface beside your float.

Brase's scouts. It had to be. Ruthie had warned her—*they're asking questions at the fuel dock, girl, asking about a medevac flight out of the Vane claim*—and Hazel had told herself she had time, that the permits wouldn't expire for six tides, that a company the size of Brase Salvage moved like a freighter, slow to turn. But this wasn't a freighter's work. This was a skiff in the dark, a man on the mud at first light. This was someone who already knew where to look.

Which meant Amos hadn't kept the coordinates after all. Or he'd given them up before the end, feverish, frightened, wanting to make sure the wreck was found by somebody. Or—

Or the chart wasn't the only copy. Or Brase had known about the Meridian Star for years and had simply been waiting for the concession window, and the dying ramblings of an old prospector had changed nothing except to start a clock.

Hazel didn't know which truth it was. She only knew that the flats, which had felt like hers alone for one bright hour, now felt watched.

* * *

She couldn't land. That was the arithmetic of it, plain and merciless.

The channel beside the wreck was too narrow for her approach line—she'd have to come in over the Spine, and the Spine's standing water at low tide ran barely a foot deep over gravel that would take her floats apart like a can opener. The nearest usable bar was two miles south, and even that was marginal, soft mud under an inch of water, the kind of landing that ended careers and sometimes pilots. She could see it all clearly from the air, could run the approach in her head three times and get the same answer each time: *no.* Not today. Not with the tide turning in four hours and no anchor, no winch, no second pair of hands to hold a line while she waded the flats.

Amos had warned her about that too, in one of his last lucid stretches, propped up in the hospital bed with his hands folded on the blanket like a man at prayer. *You can't do it alone,* he'd said. *Nobody works the flats alone. The tide doesn't care how good you are.*

She had smiled and told him she'd managed alone for ten years.

He had looked at her with eyes gone yellow and ancient, and he'd said, *That's why you're lonely, girl. Not why you're safe.*

Now, circling at a thousand feet with the camera cool against the window frame and the wreck's ribs gleaming below her like a promise half-kept, Hazel understood that he'd been right in a way she wasn't ready to name. She had found the Meridian Star. She had photographed her father's last ship from stem to stern. And she could not touch it—not today, maybe not ever alone—because the flats demanded partnership the way the sea demands respect, absolutely and without negotiation.

She turned south toward Pelican Narrows, and the dread came with her, sitting in the empty right-hand seat like a passenger.

Because it wasn't just the boot prints. It was what the boot prints meant about time. If Brase's people had found the wreck already—if they were walking her ribs while Hazel circled overhead taking pictures—then the six tides weren't six tides of advantage. They were six tides of pretense. The fleet didn't need her chart. They needed only to wait for the permit window and lower their cranes onto a site they'd already surveyed, and everything Amos had given her—everything he'd traded his last flight for—would amount to photographs of a thing taken from her before she ever reached it.

Unless the prints belonged to someone else entirely.

The thought arrived quietly, the way important thoughts do, and Hazel turned it over as the coastline scrolled beneath her. A prospector's old claim partner. A fisherman who remembered the wreck from his grandfather's stories. A kid. Anyone. The flats drew wanderers the way a lamp draws moths, and not all of them worked for Corwin Brase.

But hope, she reminded herself, was a fuel with a very narrow flash point. She'd spent ten years learning not to burn it.

* * *

Pelican Narrows resolved out of the haze at midmorning, its harbor a gray crescent crowded with fishing boats and, at the far end, the new steel barge and crane barge that had arrived with Brase's advance crew—vessels too big and too clean for the harbor, wearing their corporate paint like uniforms. She overflew the strip rather than landing immediately, making one slow circuit to read the field the way she always did: Ruthie's hangar open, the yellow Cub outside with its cowling off, the fuel drums ranked and orderly. Normal. Quiet.

Except for the truck.

It was parked at the edge of the apron, a gray pickup with government plates and two men standing beside it, drinking coffee from paper cups, watching the sky with the patient insolence of men being paid to wait. As Hazel banked past, one of them raised his cup to her—an easy, friendly gesture, nothing in it—and she felt the gesture land somewhere beneath her ribs like a hook finding flesh.

They knew her tail number. They'd have it written down. By tonight it would be in a file in Brase's office, next to a photograph of a medevac flight plan and a note about a prospector named Vane, and the thread would run backward from there, stitch by stitch, toward a woman who had spent a decade leaving no thread to find.

*Not yet,* she told the men in the truck, though they couldn't hear her. *You don't have me yet.*

She landed long and smooth on the crosswind strip, taxied to the far corner of the apron away from the truck, and shut down in the smell of salt grass and avgas. Her legs were stiff from four hours of flying and her hands, now that the yoke was out of them, had begun to shake—the delayed tremor that always came after, the body presenting its bill to the mind. She sat with it for a moment, breathing, watching the tide-flattened harbor light shimmer on the water.

Then she gathered the film canisters and the chart, wrapped the oilskin tight around it, and walked toward Ruthie's hangar with her collar up and her face arranged into the blankness she wore like a second coat.

Ruthie Okafor looked up from the Cub's engine when Hazel ducked under the wing, took one long look at her, and set down her wrench.

"You're back early," Ruthie said. "And you've got the face of somebody who found what she was looking for."

"I found it." Hazel glanced toward the apron, toward the gray truck, and lowered her voice. "I also found boot prints on the flats, Ruthie. Fresh ones. Somebody got to the wreck before I did."

Ruthie went very still. Somewhere across the harbor, a crane barge sounded its horn, one long note rolling flat across the water like a warning that had lost its meaning through repetition.

"Then," Ruthie said quietly, "you'd better tell me everything. And then you'd better decide fast who you're going to trust—because honey, from where I'm standing, you don't have six tides anymore. Looks to me like you've got five."
