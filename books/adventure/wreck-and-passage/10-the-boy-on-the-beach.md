## 10. The Boy on the Beach

The tide had gone out so far it seemed the sea had reneged on some ancient promise, and Hazel walked a mile of seabed to reach the wreck's ribs again.

She had told herself she wouldn't come back until the third tide, when the light would be better and the channels settled. But sleep hadn't come in the motel at Pelican Narrows, and the chart lay on the nightstand like something breathing, and by four in the morning she was standing in Ruthie's hangar warming up the engine just to have something to do with her hands. By six she had set down on the long gravel spit she'd found two days ago—wheels kissing stone, no damage this time—and now here she was, boots sinking in gray mud that smelled of sulfur and old iron, walking out to meet her father's grave for the second time in her life.

The Meridian Star's skeleton rose from the flats like the carcass of something enormous that had lain down to die a century ago. Ribs of blackened steel, plates peeled back like orange rind, weed hanging off everything in ropes and tatters. At low water you could walk right up into what had been her belly. Hazel had done exactly that on the first tide, and had come away with photographs, boot prints that weren't hers, and a hollowness behind her sternum that hadn't left since.

She checked the sand around the wreck before anything else. Habit now. The boot prints from last time had been washed clean by two high waters, and for one foolish moment she let herself believe whoever had walked here before her was gone for good.

Then she saw the smoke.

It wasn't much—a thread of gray rising from behind the wreck's collapsed stern section, thin as fishing line, the kind of smoke you'd miss if you weren't looking for it or if you wanted very badly not to be seen. Someone had built a fire small enough to hide under the hull's shadow, burning driftwood, maybe kelp. Someone who knew how to make a fire that didn't announce itself.

Hazel stood very still in the mud. The wind came off the water carrying salt and the faint sweetness of rotting eelgrass, and somewhere out on the flats a gull screamed once and gave up. Her hand went to the bear spray on her hip—not a gun, she'd never carried a gun, guns left paperwork and paperwork left trails—but her heart was going like she'd run the whole mile instead of walked it.

Whoever it was had been living here. The smoke thread had the steadiness of a fire tended through the night, banked and fed and banked again. And the boot prints from the first tide—

*Small,* she thought suddenly. *They were small prints. Narrow heels.*

She should go back to the plane. That was the smart move, the move ten years of careful living demanded: get airborne, radio nothing to nobody, come back armed with knowledge instead of curiosity. Curiosity was how people got found. Curiosity was how *she* had almost gotten found, standing in Amos Vane's cabin listening to a dead man's name come out of a dying man's mouth.

But the smoke kept rising, thin and patient, and Hazel Joubert—who had spent a decade perfecting the art of looking away—found she couldn't.

She came around the stern section with her boots quiet on wet sand and stopped like she'd hit glass.

A boy sat cross-legged by a fire no bigger than a hat, turning a tin cup over the flames. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Dark hair cut short and growing out unevenly, the way hair grows when nobody cuts it. A canvas coat three sizes too big, sleeves rolled and re-rolled. He was thin in the way that isn't lean but *used up*, cheekbones standing out like they were trying to leave his face. Beside him, laid out with a neatness that seemed borrowed from another life: a coil of line, a gutting knife, four mussels already opened on a flat rock, and a battered pair of binoculars wrapped in a plastic bag against the damp.

He looked up and froze. The cup stopped mid-turn.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The fire popped. Water dripped somewhere inside the wreck's ribs, counting seconds.

"You're the pilot," he said finally. His voice cracked in the middle of it, half boy, half something older. "The little yellow Cub. You landed on the spit."

Hazel said nothing. Saying nothing was her first language.

"I'm not stealing anything." He said it fast, the words of someone who'd rehearsed them. "I know how it looks. But I'm not—I don't take anything that's yours."

"Whose is the wreck?"

His eyes flicked toward the ribs, then back to her, and something in his face closed like a door. "Nobody's. Yet."

That was answer enough. She crouched down slowly, keeping her weight low the way you do with a spooked animal—or a spooked kid, which amounted to the same arithmetic. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and salt and beneath it something sour, the smell of a body running on too little food for too long.

"When did you last eat something that didn't come off these flats?" she asked.

"I eat fine."

"Mussels every day will put you in the hospital by winter. There's a reason they call it paralytic shellfish poisoning and not a mild inconvenience."

He looked at the mussels on the rock, and his jaw worked, pride and hunger fighting visibly behind his face. Hunger won, the way it always does, but he made it look like a draw. "I've been careful," he said. "I check the closures. I only take from the clean beds past the point."

*Closures.* He knew about closures. He knew about the clean beds past the point, which meant he'd mapped them, which meant he'd been here weeks at least, alone, fifteen years old, mapping shellfish beds on a tidal flat while a salvage fleet assembled at the harbor mouth.

"Teo," he said abruptly, as if deciding something. "My name's Teo."

"I didn't ask."

"No," he agreed. "But you were going to stand there all day not asking, and I figured I'd save us both the time."

Despite herself—despite everything, despite ten years of discipline—something in Hazel's chest loosened half a degree. She looked at the binoculars in their plastic bag. "Those are good glasses. Zeiss. Salvage company issue."

The door in his face slammed shut again. He picked up the binoculars and held them against his chest like a shield, knuckles white. "Found them," he said.

"You stole them."

"I *took* them." His chin came up, and for a second there was fury in him, bright and total, the kind that burns everything nearby including the person carrying it. "You want to know who I took them from? Corwin Brase. I took them off the *Iron Meridian*, his flagship, the night I jumped ship at Anchor Point. So yeah. They're stolen. Everything I own is stolen, because everything I earned, I earned working for a man who—" He stopped himself. Swallowed it. The fury went out as fast as it had flared, doused like the fire would be if the tide came early, and what was left underneath was worse: fear, plain and cold. "You're going to turn me in."

It wasn't a question. It was a forecast. He'd clearly run this scene in his head a hundred times, lying awake under the wreck's ribs listening to the tide turn, and in every version it ended the same way: an adult, a radio, the authorities, and whatever waited behind him that was bad enough to make tidal-flat exile look like freedom.

Hazel looked at him for a long time. The fire ticked. Out past the wreck, the water had begun its slow, silent return, sliding up the flats the way it always did, patient as a debt collector.

"What did Brase have you doing?" she asked.

* * *

Teo had watched the yellow Cub come in that morning the way he watched everything now—from cover, through glass, with his heart going hard.

He'd been on the flats for thirty-one days. He knew because he scratched a mark into the handle of the gutting knife each evening, and there were thirty-one marks, and some nights he counted them twice because counting was the only thing that made the dark pass faster. Thirty-one days of mussels and rainwater caught in the wreck's peeled-back plates, of sleeping in a nest of netting and salvage foam wedged between two ribs where the wind couldn't find him, of watching the horizon for boats with the Zeiss glasses and telling himself *one more tide, one more tide*, without ever saying what came after the one more tide.

He'd seen her land on the gravel spit the first time and known immediately she wasn't Brase's. Brase's pilots flew heavy machines and flew them loud, and they never landed on a bare spit when a harbor was forty minutes south. This woman had set down on gravel like she was parking a bicycle, walked a mile of mud alone, photographed the wreck, and flown away without telling a soul—which Teo knew because he'd listened to the whole district band that night and heard nothing but fishermen complaining about fuel prices.

So when she came around the stern this morning, he'd decided—somewhere between seeing her boots and hearing her voice—that she was either the best thing that had happened to him in a month or the worst, and there was no way to know except to let it happen.

Now she was crouched across the fire from him asking questions, and he understood, with the clarity that hunger gives you, that this was the hinge. Whatever he said next would decide the next thirty-one days, or decide that there weren't any more.

"What did Brase have you doing?"

Teo turned the cup in his hands. It was a condensed-milk tin with a wire bail, and he'd drunk from it every day for a month, and somehow explaining it to her felt harder than explaining anything else.

"Cabin boy," he said. "Officially. Unofficially I ran numbers. Brase doesn't trust radios for his scheduling—he says the band is full of ears, which, you know, is funny given what he does on it—so he keeps the fleet movements on paper. Paper schedules, paper manifests, paper everything. And the paper lives in a locker outside his cabin, and the cabin boy cleans the deck outside that locker twice a day." He shrugged. "I read fast."

"And?"

"And I memorized it. All of it. Which ships, which days, which survey grids. The *Iron Meridian* comes up from Anchor Point on the seventh, the dive tender *Kestrel* on the eighth, the barge after that once they've confirmed the site." He recited it flat, the way you recite a thing you've repeated to yourself so many times it's worn smooth. "Except they don't have the site. That's the thing. Brase has a search grid, forty square miles of tidal flat, and four boats to sweep it, and the permits expire in—" He stopped. Looked at her sharply. "How many tides left?"

"Three after today."

"Then they're going to be sweeping blind and desperate, and desperate men sweep sloppy." Something almost like a smile touched his mouth and died. "I could have sold that schedule. I thought about it. There are harbors where a man would pay real money to know where Brase's boats will be and when."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because the last person who sold Brase something he didn't buy ended up—" Teo shut his mouth. Looked at the fire. The fire offered nothing back.

"Ended up what?" Hazel asked quietly.

"My brother worked Brase's barge crew two seasons ago. Found a crate in the hold that wasn't on any manifest, opened it, asked questions." Teo's voice had gone very level, the levelness of someone holding something down. "He got paid off and blacklisted from every licensed boat on the coast, all in the same week. Nobody blacklists a deckhand coast-wide in a week unless somebody makes phone calls. My brother went inland after that. I haven't seen him since." He looked up. "I stole the glasses and jumped ship because I figured if that's what happens when you *ask questions*, I didn't want to find out what happens when you *know answers*. And then I sat on this flat for a month realizing I'd made myself the only person on this coast who knows Brase's whole schedule, which means I didn't run from knowing answers. I just ran somewhere quiet to do it."

The tide was coming in. He could see it past her shoulder, a shine of silver light moving up the flats, unhurried, absolute. In twenty minutes the fire would be under a foot of water. In forty, the wreck's ribs would stand in three feet of sea, and the place where he'd slept for a month would belong to the fish again.

"You're coming with me," Hazel said.

It wasn't a question either, and Teo bristled before he could stop himself, the reflex of a month of solitude. "I didn't ask for—"

"No. You didn't." She stood, mud sucking at her boots, and looked down at him with an expression he couldn't read—something between irritation and something else, something that might have been recognition. "Here's how it is. You're fifteen years old, you're eating poison mussels, you're sleeping in a shipwreck, and in three tides this flat is going to be crawling with Brase's divers, at which point your excellent hiding spot becomes a cage. Staying isn't an option. Going back to Pelican Narrows alone isn't an option, because Ruthie Okafor tells me Brase's scouts are in every bar in town asking about a yellow Cub, and a lone teenager with stolen Zeiss glasses is going to draw exactly the wrong kind of attention." She paused. "And I need to know Brase's schedule, and you're the only person on this coast who has it. So yes, you're coming with me. Not because you asked. Because it's the least stupid available plan, and I fly on least stupid plans."

Teo stared at her. The tide whispered closer. Somewhere in the wreck above them, water dripped onto water.

"What's in it for you?" he asked. It was the question he'd learned to ask first, the one Brase's world had ground into him: everyone wants something, find out what it is before it takes you.

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Out on the flats, the returning water reached the lowest rib of the wreck and began, gently, to lift the weed hanging from it, so that the whole dead ship seemed to breathe.

"Somebody's on that manifest," she said at last. "Somebody whose death certificate was signed by Corwin Brase." She turned and started walking toward the spit, not checking whether he followed. "Bring your tin. You can wash it at my camp."

* * *

Teo followed her because there was nowhere else to follow, and because—for the first time in thirty-one days—following felt less like surrender than like movement.

He packed in four minutes. He'd practiced packing in four minutes; the first week he'd packed in ninety seconds, every night, ready to run, before deciding that running every night was its own way of being caught. Now he rolled the netting, pocketed the knife with its thirty-one marks, bagged the glasses, kicked sand over the fire, and trotted after her across the shining mud with his bundle under his arm like the world's smallest refugee.

The Cub sat on the gravel spit looking impossibly small and impossibly free, and when Hazel waved him aboard without ceremony—just a jerk of her chin, the way you'd wave a workmate into a truck—Teo climbed in and put his hands in his lap and tried not to let his face show what his face was showing.

He'd ridden in Brase's floatplane once, in the co-pilot's seat, and Brase had spent the whole flight talking on the radio about money. This was different. This was a machine held together partly with fresh fabric and new paint, patched over the wingtip like a scar, and the woman flying it ran up the engine with the tenderness other people reserved for horses, and when they lifted off the spit the whole flat came up to meet them—the wreck shrinking to a brown splinter, the channels braiding silver, the sea arriving all at once like a held breath released—and Teo forgot, for eleven entire seconds, to be afraid.

"You've flown before," Hazel said. It wasn't a compliment. It was an observation, delivered over the engine noise without turning her head.

"Once. Floatplane."

"You didn't grab the seat or scream, so I figured." A pause. "Don't touch anything. If you're going to vomit, the bag's on the floor."

"I'm not going to vomit."

"They all say that."

He didn't vomit. He watched the coast unroll beneath them—forest to mudflat to open gray water—and thought about how a month ago he'd have given anything to be in the air, any air, going anywhere, and how now that he was here he mostly felt like cargo. Useful cargo, maybe. The girl-woman-pilot needed his schedule. That was something. Being needed was a kind of anchor, even if it was also a kind of leash, and Teo had learned to tell the difference, and he wasn't entirely sure yet which one this was.

She put down at a cove he didn't know, tucked behind a headland, in a grass strip that was really just the flattest part of a meadow. There was a tent, a tarp over supplies, a camp stove, and a plane parked with its wheels chocked and its tail tied down with the thoroughness of someone who expected to be gone before anyone came looking.

"Camp rules," Hazel said, crouching at the stove. "One: nobody hears about this place from you. Not names, not directions, not weather. Two: you eat what I eat, which tonight is beans, and tomorrow is beans. Three—" She handed him a tin plate, and their eyes met over it, and something passed between them that Teo would think about later, though he couldn't have named it. "Three: if Brase's people find us, you swim for the tree line and you don't look back and you don't come back for me. I'm not responsible for you. Understood?"

"Understood," Teo said, and took the plate, and understood completely that she was lying.

He ate beans in the doorway of the tent while the light went long and gold over the water, and Hazel sat apart with the oilskin chart spread on her knees, studying it the way you study a face in a crowd that you're afraid you recognize. Twice he caught her lips moving, tracing letters. Once he was almost sure he heard a name—not hers, not his, something older.

When it was full dark she banked the stove and said, "First light, you're going to draw me Brase's search grid. Every grid square, every scheduled position, every date. And then we're going to figure out how to be in the one place his boats aren't."

"And after that?" Teo asked, from the dark side of the fire.

Hazel was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer.

"After that," she said finally, "we see what the sixth tide brings."

Out on the flats, invisible in the darkness, the sea was already turning—coming in over the wreck, over the ribs and the rust and the letters that had crossed an ocean to reach a daughter who'd spent ten years making sure no one could find her. The tide didn't care about hiding. The tide came for everything eventually.

Two miles south, past the headland, a running light moved along the black water where no running light should be.

Neither of them saw it. Yet.
