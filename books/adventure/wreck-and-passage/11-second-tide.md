## 11. Second Tide

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Teo woke her by throwing a pebble at the tent fly.

"Water's going out," he said through the canvas. "You wanted to know."

Hazel had slept in her clothes, boots laced, chart folded under her hip like a second spine. She crawled out into a dawn the color of pewter, breath smoking, and looked down the long throat of the channel where the sea was already peeling back from itself—dark water sliding off mudflats in sheets, exposing the ribs of the world a few inches at a time. Somewhere out there, under forty years of silt and tide, the *Meridian Star* waited with its hold full of sealed crates and its manifest full of lies.

"Gravel bar's dry enough to take weight by six," Teo said. He was crouched at the waterline, reading the flats the way she read weather—not with instruments but with his whole body, head tilted, eyes moving. "We go up the north channel while it's still got a foot of water, then cut across the spit when it drains. If we're not off by the turn, the water comes back faster than you'd believe. I've seen it chase a man half a mile."

"You've seen a lot for someone who claims to be fifteen."

"I've seen everything except a way out." He said it flatly, without self-pity, which was somehow worse.

She checked the plane over by feel in the grey light—tailwheel still true after Ruthie's welding, fuel topped, the patched wingtip flexing no more than it should. The Beaver sat on the shingle like something patient and half-wild, and Hazel put her palm flat against the cowling the way she might touch a horse she was about to ask too much of.

"One pass," she told Teo. "One landing. We load what matters and we leave before the water thinks about coming home."

"And what matters?"

She didn't answer that. She didn't have an answer yet.

* * *

The north channel ran narrow as a river between walls of standing water, and flying it low was like threading a needle made of fog and reflected sky. Hazel kept the wheels a wingspan above the surface, the propeller chewing spray, Teo leaning forward in the right seat calling the bends before they came—"hard left past the dead spruce, then straight until you see the iron post"—his voice calm in a way that made her trust him more than any credential would have.

The channel opened onto the great bowl of the tidal flats, and there it was.

Not much of it, from the air. A dark suggestion beneath the drained sand: a long ridge where the silt lay thinner, and rising from it, here and there, curved shapes like the bones of some enormous animal that had lain down four decades ago and never gotten up. The *Meridian Star*. Two hundred feet of coastal freighter reduced to a spine and a suggestion of shoulders, its superstructure long since collapsed into a mound the sea had been slowly swallowing and spitting out with every tide, patient as a jeweler tumbling stones.

"There," Teo said, pointing. "The gravel bar east of the stern section. It drains first and floods last. Fishermen use it when they run aground."

"How would you know what fishermen do?"

He almost smiled. "I watched them do it wrong, mostly."

She brought the Beaver around wide, studying the bar—the way the wind combed the sand in long ripples, the color of the wet gravel, the absence of anything soft-looking. It wasn't a landing so much as an argument she intended to win once. She flared over the last fifty yards, held the tailwheel off, and set the mains down on gravel hard enough to feel it in her teeth. The plane bucked, slewed a half-length, caught, and rolled out trailing a rooster tail of grit.

Silence afterward, except the tick of cooling metal and the enormous quiet of the flats—a silence so complete she could hear the water retreating in the far channels, a sound like the whole ocean exhaling.

Teo was out of his door before she'd killed the mags, standing on the bar with his arms slightly out, turning in a slow circle. "It's bigger than I thought," he said. "They talk about it like it's small. Like it's nothing."

"To Brase it's nothing. Cargo on a schedule." Hazel stepped down onto ground that had been seafloor an hour ago and would be again by afternoon, and felt the strangeness of it move up through her boots. "To somebody else it was a ship with people on it."

They walked out along the drying ridge toward the exposed bones. Up close the wreck resolved into detail that hurt to look at: hull plating furred with barnacle and weed, a windlass lying on its side like a dropped toy, chain running out into the sand and vanishing. The stern section stood highest, listing hard to port, and it was there the tide had done its kindest work—scouring out the after hold, laying bare a cavern of ribbed steel floored with grey mud and forty years of whatever the sea had decided to give back.

Hazel stood at the edge of it and did not move.

She had imagined this moment as triumph. Standing here it felt like trespass. The hold smelled of salt and rot and old iron, and underneath all of it, faintly, impossibly, of engine oil—the same smell that had lived in her father's coveralls, in the cab of his truck, in the hair of a man who used to lift her onto his shoulders so she could see over the crowd at the regatta. Her eyes stung and she blamed the wind off the water.

"Miss Joubert." Teo's voice, careful. "The crates. Over there."

Against the port side, half-buried but unmistakable, a row of sealed crates still lashed to their dunnage—teak-bound, tarred, stamped with a shipping mark she recognized from Amos's chart. Registered cargo. The kind of freight that could buy a new life three times over. Teo was already wading toward them, practical as a crow, and part of her wanted to let him—wanted to fill her pockets with the price of anonymity and walk away from all of this.

Instead she found herself walking the other way.

Toward the forward section. Toward the crew spaces.

* * *

She told herself she was looking for the manifest locker, because the ledger mattered, because proof mattered, because anger was easier to carry than grief. But her boots took her where they wanted, up a collapsed ladderway and along a catwalk the tide had stripped to bare steel, into the wreck's upper works where the cabins had been—small rooms, each one a coffin now, filled with compacted silt and the pale shells of things that had moved in after the drowning.

Her father's name had been on the crew list. Not the passenger list. The crew list. Which meant he'd been working this ship, and working ships meant a bunk, and a bunk meant belongings, and men who planned to testify against Corwin Brase kept their evidence close.

The third door on the starboard side hung open on a room the size of a closet. Bunk frame rusted to lace. Locker tipped against the bulkhead, its door sprung. And scattered across the tilted floor, half-sunk in mud, the ordinary archaeology of a life: a tin cup. A belt buckle. A deck of cards fused into one solid block. A wristwatch, stopped, its crystal clouded to milk.

Hazel knelt in the muck of a dead man's cabin and understood, suddenly and completely, why people built graves. Not for the dead—for the living. So there would be one place to stand and grieve instead of having to carry it everywhere, leaking.

She dug with her hands. The cold went through her gloves fast, then through her skin, until her fingers ached in a way that felt honest. Under the bunk frame, wedged where a man would wedge the thing that mattered most, she found an oilskin pouch, smaller than the one Amos had given her, tied with wire and corroded nearly shut. She worked the wire loose with her teeth and the pouch fell open into her lap.

Letters. A dozen of them, rubber-banded, the band rotted to threads. Water-stained, salt-bleached, but intact—written in pencil on thin paper, and on the top envelope, in handwriting she knew better than her own, her own name.

*Hazel.* Just that. No surname. As if the name were a place he expected to find her, or a door he hoped stayed unlocked.

She did not open them. Not there. Some part of her understood that if she read her father's voice in a drowned cabin with the tide already thinking about turning, she would sit down among the rust and the shells and never get up, and the water would come back and be neither cruel nor kind about it.

So she put the letters inside her jacket, against her chest, where her heartbeat could argue with them, and she took the watch too, and the photographs she found folded behind the locker door—one of a woman laughing on a dock, one of a small girl on a man's shoulders, both bleached to ghosts—and she climbed back down into the daylight with her jaw set so hard it hurt.

* * *

Teo had the first crate open. He looked up as she came along the ridge, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop talking mid-sentence.

"Crates are sealed good," he said instead, quieter. "Tarred canvas under the slats. Whatever's inside stayed dry. There's twelve visible, maybe more buried. This one's machine parts, I think. Brass. Heavy."

"We're not taking the cargo."

He blinked. "Then what are we—"

"The ledger. The manifest. Paper." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "Brase can have every crate on this wreck. What I need is the paper that says what he is."

Teo studied her for a long moment, this girl-woman who'd pulled him out of the surf-line two days ago and hadn't yet decided what he was to her. Then he nodded, once, and pointed aft. "Ship's papers would be in the master's quarters. Above the after hold. But Miss Joubert—the tide. We've got maybe ninety minutes before the channels start filling. Less if the swell picks up."

"Then we don't waste them standing here."

They worked. The master's quarters had mostly collapsed, but Teo, who had spent a year learning wrecks and salvage camps from the inside of Brase's operation, knew where ships hid their paper—in the chart table, bolted down, built to survive. They pried it open together and found it flooded to the lid, a soup of pulp and brass fittings, and Hazel's heart went down with it until Teo, elbow-deep, made a small sound and lifted out a slim metal tube, screwed tight, wax-sealed, buoyant even now after forty years.

"A canister," he said. "Masters used them for the official documents. Registration, crew lists, manifests. Waterproof." He turned it in his hands. "This one never got opened."

Hazel took it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

They loaded the canister, the letters, the watch, the photographs. They left the crates sitting in their rows like sleeping soldiers, and Hazel found she felt no grief about that at all—only a clean, cold clarity, the same feeling she got in the air when a decision became obvious. Let Brase have the brass. Let him spend a fortune dredging up the price of his own ruin. The paper was the wreck. The paper had always been the wreck.

They were halfway back to the plane when Teo stopped walking.

"Listen."

At first there was nothing—just wind and the whisper of draining water. Then, beneath it, an engine. Not hers. Deeper, slower, diesel-heavy, and coming from seaward.

Hazel turned. Far out beyond the flats, where the water still held, a shape was moving through the morning haze—a boat, running dark-gray and purposeful up the outer channel, a bow wave folding away from it in a long white V. Too big for a fishing skiff. Too fast for a survey launch.

Behind it, fainter, two more shapes.

"They weren't supposed to find the channel till tomorrow," Teo breathed. "That's the *Kestrel*. That's Brase's lead survey boat—I'd know her anywhere, I scrubbed her decks for eight months. But how—" He stopped. Hazel watched him arrive at it, watched the color change in his face. "Ruthie's strip. Somebody talked to Ruthie."

"Or watched her." Hazel was already moving, breaking into a run across the gravel, the letters pressing cold against her chest like a hand. "Get in. Get strapped. Now."

The engine note swelled behind them as the *Kestrel* came on, patient and certain, following a channel that only a handful of living people knew—and Hazel understood, with the terrible clarity of the final approach, that Amos Vane had never given up the coordinates to anyone.

But someone had given up hers.
