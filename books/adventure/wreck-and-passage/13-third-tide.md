## 13. Third Tide

The fog came in off the water like something alive, the way it always did on the flats—first a gray suggestion at the horizon, then a wall, then a world. Hazel watched it build from the cockpit of the Cub, parked nose-out on the gravel spit she'd chosen three days ago for exactly this reason: high ground behind her, open channel ahead, and a quarter mile of tidal sand between her tailwheel and the sea.

"Radio," Teo said from the passenger seat, where he'd been sitting for an hour with his boots drawn up and his chin on his knees, listening the way he listened to everything—with his whole body, like a deer deciding whether to bolt.

She'd already heard it. A voice, thin and smeared by distance and moisture, working through a channel scan. Not words yet. Just the fact of a voice, out there in the gray, where yesterday there had been nothing but gulls and the long suck of the tide going out.

"They're early," she said.

"You said they wouldn't come before the fourth."

"I said I *hoped*." She keyed the radio down to receive-only and turned the volume to a whisper. "Hope's not a flight plan."

Teo uncurled and pressed his face to the window. The fog had swallowed the far edge of the flats entirely; the wreck lay somewhere out there under all that milk, the ribs of the *Meridian Star* standing in the sand like the skeleton of something that had once been proud. Somewhere beyond it, past the bar, past the channel markers nobody maintained anymore, was the lead vessel of Brase Salvage and Concessions—the one Ruthie had called *the Iron Matron*, the one the scouts' boat had been running supplies to since Tuesday.

Hazel had planned to have two more tides before this. Two more tides to finish mapping the channels Teo knew by heart, to mark the soft spots in the gravel, to decide—finally, honestly—what she was going to do when the moment came. Now the moment was coming to her, wrapped in fog, and all she could do was sit in a cold cockpit and listen to a stranger's voice getting slowly louder.

"What do we do?" Teo asked.

Hazel looked at him. Sixteen years old, maybe seventeen, all elbows and watchfulness, a boy who'd run from Brase's employ and learned to live off a tidal flat like a heron—patient, still, striking only when the water gave him something. He'd saved her chart from going overboard with both hands bleeding. She trusted him more than she'd trusted anyone in ten years, which was to say: not much, but more than anyone.

"We do what the fog is doing," she said. "We disappear."

* * *

Teo had never seen a fleet come in before. He'd seen pieces of it—tenders, skiffs, the supply runs he'd worked as a boy hauling chain and diesel up gangplanks with hands too small for the work—but never the whole animal at once, never the way it arrived like weather.

He heard it before he saw it, because everything out here you heard first: a low thrumming through the fog, deeper than any engine he knew, and under it the clang of a buoy bell that shouldn't have been ringing, because no buoy sat on that stretch of channel. Which meant the ship was dragging its own gear in blind, feeling its way along the flats the way a man feels along a wall in a dark house.

Then the fog tore for a moment—a seam of wind, a breath—and Teo saw it.

The vessel was big. Bigger than anything that had any business being inside the bar. Gray-white superstructure, a crane folded against its deck like a sleeping insect's leg, floodlights burning pale halos in the murk even though it was barely midmorning. Along its hull, in letters tall as Teo himself: **BRASE SALVAGE & CONCESSIONS — LICENSED TIDAL WORKS**.

Licensed. The word sat wrong in him. He'd been licensed too, once—licensed as labor, licensed as a minor in possession of nothing, licensed to be worked fourteen hours a day on a dredge barge until the night he'd filled a duffel with someone else's tools and walked off into the marsh. Licenses were just paper that said who was allowed to own things. And paper, he'd learned, could be written by anyone with enough money.

Beside him, Hazel had gone very still. That was the thing about her he was still learning—that stillness wasn't calm. It was a loaded spring. Her eyes moved across the vessel the way Amos Vane's feverish eyes had moved across the chart, cataloguing, measuring, filing away.

"She's drawing too much water for the inner channel," Hazel murmured. Almost to herself. "She'll anchor outside the bar and work tenders." A pause. "Which means she doesn't know where to go."

Teo frowned. "They found the cabin. They traced your flight. Ruthie said—"

"They know the *wreck* is here. They've probably known for years that something's on these flats—Brase has been bidding on this concession since before I ever heard of it." Hazel's breath fogged the glass; she wiped it with the heel of her hand, impatient. "But knowing a wreck is somewhere in forty square miles of tidal flat isn't the same as knowing where it lies. Amos had the coordinates. Amos never gave them up." Her jaw tightened. "That's why they want the chart. That's why they're willing to offer me money and immunity for a piece of oilskin. Because without it, they're doing what that ship is doing right now—feeling along the wall in the dark."

Through the seam in the fog, Teo watched a tender launch off the vessel's flank, a low black shape with two men aboard and a coil of cable between them. It turned north. The wreck lay south.

"They're surveying wrong," he said, and something loosened in his chest—not hope exactly, but the cousin of it. "The old channel shifted after the storm year. If they're following the charts from before—" He stopped, caught himself, glanced at her. "I used to run those lines. For them. Before."

Hazel looked at him then, fully, and Teo braced for the question he couldn't answer cleanly—*how long were you theirs, what did you see, what did you do*—but it didn't come. Instead she said, "Then you know their habits better than they know these waters," and turned back to the window, and Teo understood that this was how she trusted people: not with questions, but with jobs.

The fog closed again. The vessel vanished. But its sound stayed, that deep industrial heartbeat, and under it now the whine of a smaller engine—the tender, working its wrong channel north, sounding the bottom with a lead line or a sonar puck, finding nothing but sand and starfish and the slow grave of a ship that wasn't there.

"They'll figure it out," Hazel said quietly. "Brase didn't get where he is by being stupid. Give them a tide, maybe two, and they'll start pulling records, talking to old-timers, buying information the way they buy everything else." She reached over and touched the oilskin roll where it rode inside her jacket, against her ribs, like a second heartbeat. "Which means our advantage isn't a place. It's a clock."

* * *

They hid the plane the way Teo suggested, because it was his idea and it was a good one, and Hazel was honest enough with herself to admit that a month ago she would never have taken navigation advice from a teenager she'd fished off a beach.

There was an old slough two miles east, a drowned finger of spruce swamp where the fog pooled thickest and the canopy came down almost to the water. Teo had wintered within sight of it. Hazel flew the Cub in on a wing and a prayer at fifty feet, wheels kissing the black water of a flooded muskeg shelf hard enough to throw spray across the windshield, and rolled to a stop beneath trees so close the wingtips brushed needles. From the air, from the channel, from anywhere a man might look—you saw swamp. You did not see a plane.

Then they walked back to the overlook, a bald granite knob above the flats where Teo had built himself a shelter of driftwood and salvage tarps in another life, and they waited, and the fog did most of the work for them.

By afternoon the fog had thinned to rags, and through the rags they watched the fleet assemble.

Two more tenders had launched. A helicopter came in low off the sea, a mosquito-whine that made Hazel's hands curl into fists on the granite—it was a Bell, civilian-marked, but she tracked it with the instinct of a woman who had spent a decade knowing every aircraft in her sky—and began flying grid patterns over the southern flats, slow and methodical, a magnetometer fish hanging beneath it on a cable.

"It's looking for metal," Teo said.

"It's looking for a lot of metal," Hazel corrected. "A steel-hulled freighter buried in sand reads like a mountain. But so does every sunken barge, every dumped engine block, every wrecked fishing boat these flats have eaten in eighty years. There's enough iron under that mud to keep that pilot busy till Christmas." She said it steadily, but Teo heard the strain underneath, the same strain he heard whenever she talked about time. "And every pass that helicopter flies, it gets closer to the truth by accident."

They lay on the cold granite with binoculars passed back and forth, and Teo narrated what he knew, because Hazel asked him to and because saying it out loud made it real in a way that steadied him instead of frightening him. The tender crews rotated on six-hour watches. The helicopter refueled on the vessel's aft pad every ninety minutes. The galley smoke came from the port-side stack, which meant the mess was amidships, which meant if it ever mattered—if it ever came to running, hiding, sabotage—he knew which end of the ship was asleep at any given hour.

"You think like a thief," Hazel said at one point. It wasn't an insult. Her tone made it something closer to gratitude.

"I think like somebody who used to be property," Teo said, and she didn't answer, but she looked at him for a long moment, and he held the look, and neither of them blinked, and that felt like some kind of treaty being signed.

Toward dusk the helicopter made a pass that stopped Teo's heart. It swung wide of its grid, drifted south, hung for thirty seconds over the bar directly above the channel that led—in the old charts—to the deep hole where the *Meridian Star* had gone down. The magnetometer fish swung beneath it like a pendulum. Teo's mouth went dry. Beside him Hazel had stopped breathing altogether.

Then the pilot corrected, banked north, and resumed the grid, and the moment passed, and air came back into the world.

"He was just avoiding the wind off the point," Hazel said finally. Her voice was rough. "Or he was checking his fuel. Or a hundred boring things." She lowered the binoculars. "But that's what our advantage looks like, Teo. That. Thirty seconds of maybe. We are one bored pilot and one lucky instrument reading away from losing everything, and the only thing between us and that is that Amos Vane kept his mouth shut while he was dying."

The light failed. Floodlights bloomed on the vessel, turning the fog into a ceiling of dirty orange. Out on the flats, the tide had turned and was coming back in, patient and total, erasing the day's survey tracks, filling the channels, covering everything the fleet thought it had learned—which, Hazel had realized, watching them work the wrong water all afternoon, was nothing at all.

* * *

They ate cold beans in the driftwood shelter by the light of a candle stub, because a fire was a beacon and a flashlight was a signal flag, and Teo told himself the shaking in his hands was the cold.

It wasn't entirely the cold.

All day he had watched the machine he'd run from sit out there in the fog, patient as a spider, and all day he had waited for it to reach into the gray and pull him back. Every tender engine sounded like the dredge barge. Every shouted order carried across the water in a voice he half-recognized. He had told Hazel he thought like a former piece of property, and he'd meant it as armor, but lying here in the dark with the fleet's floodlights glowing through the tarp walls like eyes, the armor felt thin.

"Hazel." His voice came out lower than he intended. "If they find me—if they take me back—"

"They won't."

"You don't know what they—"

"I know exactly what men like Brase do," she said, and there was something in her voice he hadn't heard before, something old and banked and terrible, a fire that had been burning underground for ten years. "I've spent my whole adult life making sure I'd never be in a room with one again. And in two weeks I've flown into his weather, landed on his flats, and let his helicopter fly over my head." She turned the candle stub in her fingers, and the flame threw her shadow huge against the tarp. "So believe me when I tell you I understand wanting to disappear. I invented it. But I also know what it costs, because I paid it for ten years, and the bill just came due." She looked at him across the candle. "Nobody's going back to anybody. Not you. Not me. That's not a promise I can guarantee with guns—I don't have any. It's a promise I'm going to keep by being smarter than they are for six more tides. Starting tonight."

Teo nodded, because there wasn't anything else to do with a promise like that except carry it.

She spread the chart on the floorboards between them, weighting the corners with stones, and lit it with the candle, and for an hour they worked—her pencil, his memory, the tide tables scratched on birchbark. Where the fleet would search tomorrow. Where they'd already searched. How many hours of low water the third tide would give them, and whether the sandbar gravel would hold the Cub's weight, and how long it would take to load what needed loading if—when—it came to that.

Because that was the decision Hazel had finally made, there on the granite with the enemy's lights burning offshore: the fleet didn't have the coordinates. Amos had died keeping them. And that meant the *Meridian Star*'s hold—her father's letters, the sealed crates, whatever else the sand had kept for twenty years—was still hers to reach, if she was fast enough, if the fog held, if the tide obeyed the tables instead of the storm building somewhere out over the Pacific that Ruthie had warned her about on the evening radio band.

"The third tide comes in at 0440 tomorrow," Hazel said, tapping the table. "Low water gives us about three hours on the bar. The fog should hold till midmorning." She looked up, and in the candlelight her eyes were the color of the sea in bad weather, and Teo thought: *she's not afraid anymore. She's past it, into something else.* "We go at first light. You navigate. I fly. And we're gone before that helicopter finishes its coffee."

Outside, the floodlights burned. The tide crept up the flats, swallowing the day. And somewhere in the dark between the shelter and the water, Teo heard—for just a moment, maybe imagined, maybe not—the thin crackle of a radio transmission from the anchored vessel, a voice reading numbers into the night.

Coordinates, or something like them.

He didn't tell Hazel. Not yet. Some advantages, he'd learned, you checked twice before you spent them—and dawn was only five hours away.
