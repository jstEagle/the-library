## 14. The Offer

The fog came in off the water that evening like a thing with intentions, rolling up the channel in gray shoulders, swallowing the masts at Pelican Narrows one by one until the harbor was only sound—halyards ticking, water slapping pilings, the far-off clank of the cannery hoist. Hazel sat in the plane's cockpit with the door cracked to the cold and the radio tuned low, because the fog made the airwaves strange. Voices carried farther in fog. Everyone knew that. Everyone listened harder.

Teo was in the hangar loft with Ruthie's dog-eared tide tables, tracing channels with a pencil stub, and she could hear him murmuring the soundings to himself like a prayer he'd learned from someone who no longer said prayers. *Twelve feet at the cut, eight over the bar, four on the flats at low.* The boy had a memory for water the way other people had a memory for faces.

She'd been about to shut down for the night when the set crackled and a voice she didn't know said her father's name.

Not *Hazel Joubert*. Not the name on her license, her insurance, the faded registration in the plane's paperwork. The other name. The one that belonged to a girl who'd stood in a government office at nineteen with a death certificate in her hands and walked out of it a different person. The name hadn't crossed a radio wave in ten years, and hearing it now was like hearing ice shift underfoot—sudden, structural, the whole surface of her life suddenly untrustworthy.

The voice was calm. Cultivated. The kind of voice that came out of offices with brass fittings and never out of a cockpit.

"Hazel. I know you're monitoring. Your set's warm—I can hear the carrier. Let's not make this difficult."

Her thumb hovered over the transmit switch. Every instinct she had, ten years of instinct, said *don't answer, don't confirm, pull the breaker and sit in the dark.* But he already knew. The name had told her that much. The only question left was what knowing had cost him, and what he thought it could buy.

She keyed the mic. "Who is this?"

"You know who this is." A pause, unhurried, the pause of a man who had never once been made to wait. "Corwin Brase. I'd say it's a pleasure, but we've never actually met. Though I did meet your father. Twice. Once at a hearing, once at a funeral that turned out to be premature."

The fog pressed against the windshield. Somewhere down the channel a boat engine idled, low and patient, and she couldn't tell if it was moving.

"What do you want, Brase?"

"To talk. That's all. I'm told you're a reasonable woman. A careful one. You've built a life the way a man builds a wall in a flood—stone by stone, nothing showing, nothing to grab hold of. I respect that. It took me years to find you, and I'm good at finding people." The line hissed. "I'd rather not have to find you again."

* * *

She made him wait while she thought about it, and she suspected he knew she was thinking, and suspected he didn't mind. Thinking was a kind of answer too.

The fog had thickened until the hangar lights were only smears. Teo's pencil had gone quiet in the loft. Ruthie's shop was dark. The whole town had pulled its shutters in against the weather, and she was alone with a voice that knew her, which was, she realized, a thing she had not been in a decade: known. It should have felt like drowning. It felt, shamefully, briefly, like warmth—like someone had called her name across a crowded room—and she hated herself for the half-second of it, and then she was cold again, and careful.

"Talk," she said.

"Here's what I know, so we can skip the part where we pretend otherwise." Papers shuffled somewhere on his end, or he wanted her to think they did. "Amos Vane flew a charter with a pilot out of the interior ten days ago. That pilot filed no flight plan, which narrows the field to perhaps a dozen people in this territory, and I've spent the intervening time eliminating them. The aircraft that landed on the logging road had a wingtip repaired in Pelican Narrows with fabric I can trace to a single supplier. And the pilot who made that flight has a face I've had in a file since before she was flying under her current name."

He let that sit.

"I'm not telling you this to frighten you. I'm telling you so you understand there's no version of this where you go back to being nobody. That door closed the night you carried Amos Vane out of the hills. I'm sorry for it. Truly. I know what that life was worth to you."

And the terrible thing, the thing she would lie awake examining later, was that he *did* know. He knew what it was worth because he was the reason it had been worth anything—because a nineteen-year-old girl had read a falsified death record and understood that names were not protection but exposure, that her father's name had gotten him erased and hers could get her the same. Brase had built the wall she'd been hiding behind. He was the flood.

"The chart," he said. "Amos Vane's chart. I want it."

"And if I don't have it?"

"Then this conversation is shorter and less pleasant for both of us. But I don't think you'd have spent three days in Pelican Narrows trading favors for a tailwheel if you didn't intend to use it."

* * *

He made the offer the way a tide makes its offer—steadily, without malice, certain of the ground it would cover.

"Here's what I can do for you. A new identity—not the paper-thin kind you're living on, the real kind. History, references, a passport that opens doors instead of raising eyebrows. Money enough that you never have to haul freight and rumors into the bush again unless it amuses you. And immunity." The word came out smooth as varnish. "Whatever Amos Vane showed you, whatever you think the Meridian Star was carrying—none of it ever touches you. You hand over the chart, you fly out of Pelican Narrows tomorrow, and by next month Hazel Joubert doesn't exist and neither does the girl before her. You'd be free. Actually free, for the first time since you were nineteen. Not hiding. Free."

The fog beaded on the windshield and ran in slow tracks, like the glass was weeping on her behalf.

She thought about it. She let herself think about it, honestly, because she'd learned that the only way to survive temptation was to look at it straight. She thought about waking up without listening for engines. About signing her name—*any* name—on a lease, a license, a hospital form, without the small cold calculation she performed every time, weighing exposure against necessity. Ten years of not being found, and she had told herself it was safety, but sitting in the fog with Brase's voice in her ears she could finally admit what it had actually been: a long, slow exhale that never ended. A life held in reserve. She had kept herself the way you keep money in a mattress—available, but never spent, never risked, never *used*.

And he was offering to buy the mattress.

"That's generous," she said.

"It's fair. I pay fair. Ask anyone."

"Ask anyone whose permits you hold, you mean."

A pause, the first crack in the varnish. "Careful."

"No," Hazel said. "I don't think I will be."

She keyed the mic and let the silence work for her, the way it worked on the radio, the way it worked in negotiations, the way it worked on frightened passengers. "You said you met my father twice. Then you know he wasn't a careful man. He signed things. He wrote things down. He believed paper was a promise." Her voice was steady and she was distantly proud of that, the way you're proud of a wing holding in turbulence. "So here's what I know, Corwin Brase. I know Amos Vane didn't give me the chart because he wanted me rich. I know the Meridian Star's manifest has a name on it that you signed a death certificate over, and I know why a man falsifies a death record—only one reason. Because the man in question was about to say something true in a room where truth was expensive."

The radio hissed. Down the channel, that idle engine changed pitch—dropped, held, dropped again. Listening.

"I'm not going to sell you my father for a passport," Hazel said. "He's the only thing I've got that you can't print."

* * *

The warmth went out of Brase's voice then, and what was underneath was worse, because it was reasonable.

"You misunderstand the situation. You think this is a negotiation because I offered terms. It isn't. It's a courtesy. The concession permits expire in—" she could hear him checking, or performing the checking, "—two tides. After that, the flats fall under the next license, which will be mine, and everything on them, and everything *said* about them, becomes a matter for my lawyers and the courts, and courts are slow, and I am not. You have a chart and a patched airplane and a boy who used to steal from me, and I have eleven vessels, two aircraft, and the patience of a man who has already waited twenty years. Hand me the chart and I will make you rich and invisible. Keep it, and I will make you visible. You have no idea, Hazel, what I can do with a person who's visible. I did it to your father."

There it was. Not a threat dressed as a promise—the plain thing itself, laid on the table between them like a tool.

And still, in the fog, with her hand cold on the mic, she found she wasn't afraid. Not the way she expected to be. There was a clarity in it that frightened her more than fear would have: the same clarity she got at the moment an engine failed, when the ground stopped being an abstraction and became a fact, and every choice narrowed down to the ones that mattered. He had shown her the shape of her life. Ten years of fog, and one phone call had burned it off, and what she saw was small—a woman in a cockpit, hiding—and she was done with it.

"Two tides," she said.

"Two tides."

"Then you'd better hope your divers are better than your arithmetic. Because if you find that wreck before I do, you'll pull up cargo. And I'll still have the manifest." She let that land, let him do the arithmetic she'd been doing for three days—the ledger Amos had hinted at, the letters, the paper her father had believed was a promise. "You can buy a name, Brase. You can't buy back what my father wrote down. And it's not in the hold where you're looking."

A long hiss. Then, very quietly, almost gently, which was the most frightening tone of all: "Your father said something like that, once. In the hearing room. He was holding a piece of paper too." A breath. "Goodnight, Hazel. Mind the fog."

The carrier dropped. The channel went to static, then to the low murmur of the marine band, then to nothing.

* * *

She sat in the cold cockpit for a long time after, watching the fog crawl across the glass, and let herself shake—just once, briefly, the way a plane shudders through gust shear and then smooths. Teo's footsteps came down the hangar ladder. He didn't ask; he'd heard enough through the open door to know better than to ask. He climbed onto the wing root and crouched there with his forearms on his knees, a thin silhouette against the hangar lights, and looked at her the way he'd looked at the tide tables—like she was a channel he was learning the soundings of.

"He knows about me," Teo said. Not a question.

"He knows everything."

"Then we've got maybe a day before his boats stop looking for a chart and start looking for a plane." The boy's voice was flat and quick, the voice of someone who had run before and knew the mechanics of running. "We should go tonight. Fog cover's good. I can take you out the back channel past the narrows, deep water all the way, they won't—"

"Teo." She said it quietly and he stopped. "We're not running."

He blinked at her. "He said he'd—"

"I heard what he said." She looked out at the fog, at the harbor she could no longer see, at the invisible town full of people who'd traded her favors without asking her name. Ruthie's shop. The supplier who'd sold her fabric. Every one of them visible now, every one of them leverage, and Brase would use them, and that was the price of what she was about to do, and she paid it in her chest like a debt coming due. "He offered me a new name tonight. Clean papers, money, a whole life with nothing buried under it." She almost laughed. "Ten years ago I'd have taken it. Ten years ago I took a worse deal than that and called it survival."

"What are you going to do?"

Hazel reached under the seat and drew out the oilskin packet—Amos's chart, soft as cloth from handling, the pencil lines of a dead man's certainty—and laid it on her knee. Through the fog, far down the channel, the patient engine finally throttled up and faded, heading out to report.

"Tomorrow's the third tide," she said. "Low water at 0440. The fog lifts off the flats around five." She looked at Teo, and found that her hands had gone steady. "I'm going to need you to show me the deep channel in the dark. And Teo—we're going to need to be fast. He's done waiting."

The boy looked at the chart, then at her, and something in his face settled, the way water settles when the wind drops.

"0440," he said. "I'll wake you."
