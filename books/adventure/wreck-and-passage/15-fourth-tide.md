## 15. Fourth Tide

The radio crackled for a long time after Brase's voice left it, the way a room stays warm where a man has been sitting. Hazel let it hiss. She kept her hands on the yoke and her eyes on the channel markers sliding beneath her, and she did not answer, because an answer would have been a gift—her voiceprint, her location, her confirmation that she had heard.

Beside her, Teo had gone very still. He was fourteen and he had learned stillness the way other children learned to whistle: early, out of necessity, in a house where noise drew attention. Now he sat with the chart case strapped across his chest like a harness, both hands flat on it, watching her profile the way he watched weather build over water.

"He knows your name," Teo said finally.

"He knows a name." Hazel banked gently around a mudflat the color of old pewter, the tide already peeling back from it in long silver sheets. "Names are cheap. He's spent ten years proving that."

"What did he offer?"

"Everything I don't have." She checked her fuel gauge, then the sky to the west, where the storm's leading edge had begun stacking itself against the horizon like slate being loaded onto a cart. "And everything I do."

Teo nodded slowly. He didn't ask which one she'd chosen. He had been living off these flats long enough to know that when a woman flew this low, this deliberately, with a chart case buckled to a boy's chest, she had already chosen.

* * *

The fourth tide came early. The storm offshore was dragging the water ahead of it, sucking the flats bare two hours before the almanac said they should be dry, and Hazel meant to use that stolen time. She had spent the night at the island camp with the plane tied down under spruce boughs, listening to the wind walk through the trees and rehearsing the run in her head until rehearsal became prayer became something harder than either.

Now, at first light the color of dishwater, she lifted off the gravel bar with Teo beside her and the whole gray world tilting toward rain.

The Meridian Star lay eleven miles up-channel, its ribs exposed at low water like the carcass of some iron whale that had beached itself a century ago and never finished dying. Amos Vane's chart put it precisely; Brase's fleet, as far as they knew, was still dragging the wrong arm of the delta. But Brase had patrol skiffs, and patrol skiffs were fast, and a man who falsified a death record did not forgive being refused.

"They'll come from the seaward channel," Teo said. He had the chart open across his knees now, tracing channels with a blunt pencil stub. "If I were them."

"You're not them."

"I'm better than them. I lived out here eight months without anyone finding me." There was no boast in it. Just fact, laid flat as the flats themselves. "They run the deep water because their boats draw too much. If you stay in the shallows—here, and here—" his pencil tapped twice, "—they can't follow. They can only shoot."

Hazel looked at him. Fourteen years old, saying *they can only shoot* the way another boy might say *they can only honk.*

"That's the plan, then," she said.

* * *

She saw the skiff at 7:40 in the morning, or rather she saw what the skiff left behind—a wake cutting white across the black water of the seaward channel, running flat-out, bow spray standing up like a mane. It was one of Brase's aluminum patrol boats, twin outboards, and it was not searching. It was coming straight for the wreck site, which meant someone aboard knew the coordinates, which meant—

"Amos," Hazel said quietly, and hated herself for it.

"No." Teo leaned forward, squinting into the light. "Look at the heading. They're going to cut you off at the Narrows bend. That's not a wreck course—that's an intercept." His jaw tightened. "Ruthie."

Hazel felt the word land somewhere below her ribs and stay there. Ruthie Okafor, who owed her favors, who had patched her wingtip with her own hands, who had warned her about scouts. Ruthie, whose daughter lived in Pelican Narrows, whose shop could be burned down by a phone call, whose silences had always been bought cheaper than her words.

It didn't matter. Not now. Later there would be time to grieve people while they were still alive; Hazel had learned that trick young.

She pushed the nose down and went to the deck.

* * *

Flying the tidal flats at low water is not flying so much as arguing with the ground at speed. The mudflats scrolled beneath her at eighty feet, then sixty, ribbed with tide channels that gleamed like veins of mercury, studded with glacial erratics that had been dropped there by ice ten thousand years ago and had killed pilots ever since. Her tires' shadows ran along beneath her, twin dark fish keeping pace. The wind came off the storm front in long shoves, quartering from the southwest, trying to walk her tail around.

Behind and to starboard, the skiff matched her course along the deeper channel, holding a half mile back. Waiting. A rifle barrel glinted once in the bow—not aimed, just present, the way a knife is present in a man's pocket at a negotiation.

"He's herding you," Teo said. "Toward the open water past Sable Point. Once you're over the bay he can shoot freely. No witnesses, no cover, and if you go down out there—" He didn't finish. Out there the water was forty feet deep and cold enough to stop a heart in four minutes, and everyone on these coasts knew the arithmetic.

"Then we don't go past Sable Point." Hazel's voice was calm in her own ears, which surprised her. Underneath the calm, her pulse ticked like a cooling engine. "Hold on to something."

She hauled the plane into a turn so steep the wingtip pointed at the mud, pulling Gs that pressed Teo into his seat and made the old airframe groan like a ship in a swell. The skiff overshot, its wake swinging wide, and for ninety seconds Hazel had nothing behind her but gray water and gray sky.

Then the first shot cracked.

It wasn't loud so much as it was *present*—a flat snap that arrived through the airframe before it arrived through the air, a sound she felt in the soles of her feet. The bullet went somewhere aft. She didn't look. Looking cost altitude and altitude was life.

"Second boat!" Teo shouted, pointing. It had come out of nowhere—or out of the fog bank lying along the point, which amounted to the same thing—and it was cutting across the flats on the one channel deep enough to hold it, angling to box her in against the rising water.

Two skiffs. One plane. A storm building behind her like a debt coming due.

Hazel did the thing that made mechanics wince and old bush pilots nod: she went lower. Forty feet. Thirty. Low enough that the rifleman on the second skiff had to shoot upward through his own bow spray, low enough that every swell was a mountain range, low enough that the erratics rose up at her like teeth. The mud came up close enough to read—worm casts, shell hash, the tracks of birds written in a script older than any language she'd been refusing to speak.

The second shot hit her.

Not her—the plane, but the distinction felt academic. The round came through the fuselage fabric behind the cabin with a sound like a fist through paper, and something in the tail let go. The rudder pedals went soft under her boots, mushy, vague, a handshake from a stranger. She kicked them and the plane answered a half-second late, sloppily, the way a drunk man answers his own name.

"Tailwheel," she said through her teeth. "They got the tailwheel assembly, or the cables, or both."

"Can you fly it?"

"I can fly anything. Landing is the question." She glanced at the fuel. Twenty minutes, maybe less with the headwind. She glanced at the water, which was coming back onto the flats with the patience of something that had drowned better planes than hers. She glanced at the boy beside her, who had gone white but had not made a sound, and who had both arms wrapped around the chart case like it was a sibling.

Ahead, the flats ended. Beyond them lay the open reach of the bay—deep, cold, fatal—and beyond that, the storm wall, purple-black and boiling, walking toward her at the speed of bad news.

Between the flats and the bay lay one thing: a long, low sandbar called the Graveyard Tongue, a scythe of gravel and shell that surfaced at low water and vanished at high, three miles long and in places barely wider than her wingspan. No chart recommended landing there. No chart mentioned it at all, except Amos Vane's, penciled in the margin in his crabbed hand: *G. Tongue — hard pack at low — done it twice, lived twice.*

Amos had landed on it twice and lived twice. Amos was currently dying in a hospital bed on the coast, which Hazel chose not to read as an omen.

"Hold the chart," she said. "Whatever happens, hold the chart."

* * *

The third shot took the tailwheel off entirely.

She felt it go—a shudder through the airframe, a sudden wrongness in the way the tail sat in the air, like a spine slipping a vertebra. Pieces of it tumbled away into the gray and were swallowed. The plane yawed, wallowed, and Hazel fought it level with muscle and rudder cable and pure refusal, her shoulders burning, the stick heavy as a fence post.

The skiffs fell behind. They couldn't follow her into the shallows, exactly as Teo had promised, and shooting at a plane flying thirty feet over mud was a coin-flip even for a good rifleman, and she was presenting them with a target that jinked like a wounded bird. But they didn't need to hit her again. They had hit her enough. They had turned a functioning aircraft into a dying one, and dying aircraft choose their own graveyards.

The Graveyard Tongue rose out of the water ahead, long and pale and streaming with foam at its seaward end, and Hazel brought the plane down onto it the way you set down something breakable that you cannot afford to break.

There was no flare worth the name with the tail damaged, no gentle mains-first settle. She came in flat and fast, mains touching first with a bang that jarred her teeth, then the tail coming down hard because there was no wheel to catch it—steel and fabric plowing a furrow through wet gravel, the plane slewing left, right, left, Teo bracing against the panel, Hazel hauling the stick full back and standing on the rudders like a woman dancing on a floor that was trying to leave.

They stopped forty feet from the water's edge, listing slightly, steaming faintly in the cold air, trailing a scar of churned gravel behind them like a confession.

For a moment neither of them moved. The engine ticked. Somewhere a gull complained about the disturbance. The storm stood on the horizon, patient as a creditor.

"Are you hit?" Hazel asked. Her voice came out scraped.

"No. Are you?" Teo was looking at her hand, and she followed his gaze and discovered blood running down her wrist from a cut across her knuckles she had no memory of receiving. The yoke had bitten her during the landing. She flexed her fingers, winced, and wrapped them in a rag from the door pocket without ceremony.

"Metal," she said. "Cheap at the price."

* * *

They had perhaps four hours before the tide reclaimed the Graveyard Tongue entirely, and the storm would shorten that. Hazel walked the plane's length twice, cataloguing damage with her palms as much as her eyes. Tailwheel assembly gone—sheared clean, probably by the round itself or the violence of the touchdown. A hole in the aft fuselage you could put a fist through. Fabric torn in three places. The tailwheel spring bent like a wishbone. The propeller, miraculously, unmarked.

"We can't fix this here," Teo said. It wasn't despair. It was inventory.

"We can fix enough of it here." Hazel was already dragging her kit from the baggage compartment. "I don't need a tailwheel. I need a skid. Bush pilots have landed on skids since before your father was born—if you had a father, which I'm beginning to suspect is a whole conversation." She said it lightly, and saw him almost smile, and counted the almost-smile as salvage.

The plan assembled itself out of necessity and spruce: there was driftwood on the bar, hard and salt-cured, and Hazel had a hatchet, wire, and the particular stubbornness of a woman who had spent a decade solving problems alone in places where help was a rumor. By noon they had lashed a curved piece of driftwood where the tailwheel had been, wired and safety-wired and double-lashed, ugly as sin and possibly strong enough. She tested the rudder cables by hand, found one frayed to a handful of strands, and spliced it with safety wire and prayer.

Her knuckles bled through the rag twice. She rewrapped them twice.

Teo worked beside her without being asked, holding wood, tensioning wire, learning with the quick silent competence of a boy who had survived by being useful. At one point he paused, looking down the length of the bar at the water creeping back in from both ends, narrowing it to a causeway, then to a spit, then to nothing.

"When the tide comes all the way in," he said, "this island goes away."

"Then we're gone before it does." Hazel stood, wiped her hands, and looked south along the coast, where the fog was lifting just enough to show her the shape of the day: the storm behind, the fleet ahead, five miles of open water between her and the sheltered inlet where she intended to hide the plane until the fifth tide. "We fly out on this skid, we cross the bay low, and we put down in Miller Inlet before the front hits. Then we wait out the weather and come back for the ledger on the next ebb."

"And Brase?"

"Brase thinks he broke me today." She smiled, and it was not a warm expression. "Let him think it."

* * *

They took off from the Graveyard Tongue with twenty minutes of bar left under them, the driftwood skid shrieking against the gravel for a hundred yards before the plane staggered into the air, tail hanging crooked, flying like a bird with a broken feather that had decided to be furious about it. Hazel held it low over the water, in ground effect, nursing every knot, while the first spit of the storm came rattling against the windshield like thrown gravel.

The crossing took nine minutes and cost her ten years. Halfway across the bay the wind shifted without warning, a gust from the northeast that shoved the tail sideways, and for one long second the plane was traveling in two directions at once, and Hazel's whole life narrowed to her hands and the horizon and the small fierce animal fact of staying airborne. Then the gust passed, the tail obeyed, and the far shore rose up gray and welcoming as forgiveness.

Miller Inlet received them like a cupped hand. She put it down on the sheltered water at the head of the inlet—open water, because there was no choice, because the tail skid would not survive gravel again—flaring at the last possible moment and letting the hull slap down hard, once, twice, a third smaller slap, and then they were planing, decelerating, coasting to a stop in the rain with their hearts going like trapped birds.

Silence, except the rain on the fabric and the water lapping the floats'—the wheels'—hulls. Silence, except breathing.

Teo moved first. He unbuckled the chart case, checked it with the thoroughness of a customs officer, and then, apparently satisfied that it had survived, sat back and began, very quietly, to shake. Hazel reached over without a word and put her bleeding hand on the back of his neck, the way you steady something valuable in rough water, and let him shake. Outside, the storm arrived in earnest, and the inlet went white, and the plane rocked at its mooring like a cradle.

"It tried to go overboard," Teo said at last, when his voice worked. "In the landing. The chart. It nearly went out the door."

"But it didn't."

"I caught it." He said it simply, without pride, and then, after a moment, with a flicker of something that might have been pride: "I caught it."

"Yes," Hazel said. "You did."

She looked out through the rain-streaked window at the drowning world, at the flats she could no longer see, where the Meridian Star waited under six feet of returning tide with its sealed crates and its unsent letters and its proof. Two tides left. One ledger. One man ashore with a falsified signature and a fleet at his command, believing he had grounded her.

Hazel tore a strip from the rag, bound her knuckles properly, and began, in the rocking lamplight, to plan the fifth tide.
