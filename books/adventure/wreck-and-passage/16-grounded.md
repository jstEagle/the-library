## 16. Grounded

The island had no name on any chart Hazel owned, which was fitting. It was a thumb of granite and wind-bent spruce rising out of the flats like something the sea had tried to swallow and given up on. She had put the Cessna down on its shingle beach with one working brake and a tailwheel that dragged like a broken ankle, and now the plane sat tilted at the tideline, listing toward the water as if it were considering swimming out and ending it all.

Hazel stood in the shallows up to her knees, hands braced against the fuselage, and pushed. The plane did not move. The water around her ankles was the color of weak tea and cold enough to ache in her bones within a minute.

"It's not going to happen by wishing," Teo said from the beach. He had gathered driftwood without being asked and was building a fire ring out of stones, arranging them with the fussy precision of someone who needed a task or he'd start thinking.

"Nothing ever happens by wishing," Hazel said. "That's why I fly."

She waded out, wrung the hem of her jacket, and looked at the damage properly for the first time since the skiff's rounds had torn through them. The tailwheel assembly was the worst of it—bent fork, sheared bolt, the wheel itself cracked through the hub. The port wingtip carried a long silver scar where a round had kissed it, but the skin held. The real problem was the landing gear strut on the left side, dented where she'd ground-looped onto the shingle. Flyable, maybe. Landable, only once, and only if she never had to brake hard again.

Behind them, across four miles of gray water, the salvage fleet's lights would come on when dark fell. Here there was nothing but spruce, gulls, and a midden of old shells left by people who had eaten here a thousand years ago and never written their names down either.

"We can't stay," Teo said.

"We can't leave." Hazel crouched by the gear strut, running her thumb along the dent. "Not tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. Ruthie keeps spares at Pelican Narrows, but I'm not flying this thing anywhere until that wheel's solid, and I don't have a bolt that size."

"I could make one."

She looked at him. He was fifteen, maybe sixteen, all elbows and salt-cracked lips, and he said it the way he said everything—flat, watchful, ready to be laughed at.

"You know machining?"

"My uncle ran a shop in Ketchikan before—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Before. I grew up sweeping filings. You'd be surprised what I absorbed."

*You'd be surprised what I absorbed.* The boy was a walking inventory of other people's lives. Hazel wiped her hands on her trousers and made herself say the true thing: "Then we're grounded until you're a machinist and I'm a fool brave enough to test your work."

"That shouldn't take long," Teo said, and for the first time since she'd pulled him off the flats, he almost smiled.

* * *

The fire caught as the light died, throwing orange up the trunks of the spruce so they glowed like pillars in a burned church. They ate smoked salmon from Hazel's emergency kit and hardtack softened in melted snow, and Teo fed the fire small sticks one at a time, as if each one required his full attention.

Hazel watched him and felt the confession rise in her like water in a lock. She had been carrying it alone for ten years, and alone was a country she knew every road in. But the boy across the fire was a citizen of the same country, and tonight, with the fleet's distant lights not yet visible and the tide groaning somewhere out in the dark, she found she didn't want to be a citizen anymore.

"Teo." He looked up. "You told me you ran from Brase. You never told me why."

His shoulders did the thing they always did—drew in, closed like a gate. "Does it matter?"

"It matters because I'm about to tell you something I've never told anyone. And it isn't fair unless you get to decide if you want to trade."

He turned a stick in his fingers. Out on the flats, the tide made a sound like slow breathing.

"He kept books," Teo said finally. "Brase. Real ones and fake ones. The fake ones went to the permit office. The real ones stayed in a safe on the *Corvid*. I cleaned cabins on the flagship—kids do, they pay nothing—and one night the safe was open and nobody was watching, and I took a page. Just one. A cargo manifest. I thought—I don't know what I thought. That I could sell it. That somebody would pay me enough to get off the boats and go somewhere with trees that weren't all leaning the same way."

"What was on the page?"

"Crates listed as salvage equipment that were marked *received* twice. Same serial numbers, two different ports, three weeks apart." He shrugged, too casual by half. "I didn't understand it then. I understand it now. Brase was selling the same cargo twice—once to the permit holders, once to whoever pays cash and doesn't ask. And the page proved it."

"And he found out."

"He found out." The stick snapped in his fingers. He hadn't meant to break it. "They questioned the whole crew. I ran before they got to me. But they'll have my description, and my name is in their wage book, and if they catch me—" He looked at the fire. "There's a reformatory ship. The *Marlow*. Kids who steal from Brase go aboard and come back different, or don't come back. My cousin went aboard when I was nine. He came back two years later and didn't remember our mother's face until she showed him a photograph."

The fire popped. Hazel let the silence sit between them, because some things deserved silence first.

* * *

Teo watched the woman across the flames and waited. She had said *trade*, and in his experience nobody offered a trade unless what they held was worth more than what they asked for. He had learned to wait out adults the way you waited out weather: patiently, without hope, watching for the change.

Hazel Joubert—he still didn't believe that was her name, though he'd stopped saying so—stared into the fire for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was different. Lower. Unarmored.

"My name isn't Hazel Joubert."

He kept his face still. Inside, something that wasn't quite surprise settled comfortably into place, like a key finding its old cut.

"It was, once. Hazel Joubert. But before that—" She laughed, short and without humor. "No. Before and after are the same. Let me start over." She drew a breath. "My father was Elias Vane's brother. No—that's not how Amos would tell it. My father was Elias Marchand. First mate, then acting captain, of a supply steamer called the *Meridian Star*. And I was born Hazel Marchand, in a house above the chandlery in Pelican Narrows, and I lived under that name until I was nineteen years old."

Teo's mouth went dry. The *Meridian Star*. The wreck. The chart she kept rolled in oilskin like a relic. "Your father went down with her."

"The record says he drowned when she broke up on the Sable Flats. Eleven years ago. Corwin Brase signed the death certificate himself—witnessed, filed, stamped. Except Amos says my father was alive after the wreck. Alive, and asking questions about the cargo. And Brase needed a dead man asking no questions more than he needed a live one asking the wrong ones." Her hands were flat on her knees, pressing down, as if holding herself to the earth. "So I did what any sensible person does when the man who erased your father runs half the concessions in the territory. I disappeared. New name, no address, cash wages, flights logged under three different call signs. Ten years of being no one. I got very good at it. I could teach a course."

"Why?" Teo asked, and then hated the question, but she answered it anyway.

"Because being found was the dangerous thing. If Brase ever learned Elias Marchand had a daughter—a witness, a claimant, a loose thread—he'd cut it. So I cut myself first. On my own terms." She looked up at him across the fire, and her eyes were wet and furious and steady all at once. "And then a dying prospector said my father's name out loud in a cabin that smelled of kerosene, and ten years of careful nothing came apart in about four seconds."

Teo turned this over. It explained things—the way she flew like someone with nothing to lose and everything; the way she flinched from towns, from registries, from anyone holding a clipboard; the way she'd gone white in the motel room over a manifest. He had spent two years reading people for survival, and he had missed the largest fact about her because he'd assumed she was simply what she appeared: a bush pilot with a chip on her shoulder and a talent for vanishing.

"Hazel Marchand," he said, testing it.

"Don't." Sharp, then softer. "Not yet. Not out loud, anyway. Old habit."

"Okay." He poked the fire. Then, because fairness was fairness: "I'm not actually named Teo, either. Well—I am. But it's just Teo. No surname anybody wrote down. My mother died when I was six and the boats took me after that. On Brase's rolls I'm 'the Okafor boy's nephew'—Ruthie's cousin took me in for a winter once, and the paperwork stuck even though the family didn't." He hesitated. "I liked having a surname attached to me, even a borrowed one. Even fake."

Hazel made a sound that might have been a laugh. "We're a pair. You're a name with no family. I'm a family with no name."

"Between us we've almost got one whole person."

This time she did laugh, and it cracked something open in the air between them—not trust exactly, not yet, but the ground trust gets built on.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, which on the flats was a gift and an omen both. Hazel was up at first light with the tool roll spread on a tarp, and Teo emerged from the tent they'd rigged from the survival shelter half an hour later, hair flattened, eyes already on the damaged gear.

They worked through the morning. She showed him how to straighten the bent fork—heat, bend, check, curse, repeat—using a camp stove and a rock that served as an anvil. He turned out to have good hands, patient ones, and an eye for tolerance that surprised her. For the sheared bolt, he filed down a spare from her kit, cutting threads with a die she'd forgotten she owned, checking the fit against the yoke again and again until it seated smooth as a watch part.

"Where'd you learn patience?" she asked, watching him work.

"From waiting," he said. "It's most of what the boats taught me."

By afternoon the wheel spun true and the strut held weight. Hazel rocked the tail up and down, listening, and heard nothing worse than the ordinary complaints of an old airframe. It might hold. It might hold all the way to Pelican Narrows and back, twice.

They ate sitting against the fuselage, facing the water. Far out, right at the edge of seeing, the salvage fleet lay at anchor—a scatter of lights even in daylight, mastheads and crane arms, patient as vultures on a fence.

"They haven't moved," Teo said.

"They're waiting for the survey launch to report coordinates. Which it won't, because Amos never gave them up." She chewed, thinking. "Which means they're blind, and blind men get careless."

"Is that what we're counting on?"

"That, and Ruthie." Hazel reached into her jacket and brought out the letters—the bundle she'd recovered from the hold, tied with cord gone soft as cloth. She had read them all, some of them many times, but she carried them anyway, the way you carried a coal from a fire you refused to let die. "There's a letter here addressed to the harbormaster at Pelican Narrows. Dated three days before the *Star* sailed. My father writing to a friend, telling him if anything happens, look in the hold manifest—look at the duplicate serials. He knew, Teo. He was going to testify when they made landfall, and Brase knew he was going to testify, and that's why the *Meridian Star* is on the bottom of the Sable Flats instead of tied up at a dock."

Teo was quiet a moment. "The page I stole. Same serials."

"Same fraud. Eleven years apart. Brase has been running the same trick since before you were born, and my father found it, and now a boy who cleans cabins found it too." She looked at him sideways. "You see why they can't catch you. You're not a thief to them. You're a witness."

"I don't want to be a witness. Witnesses drown."

"Not this one." She said it lightly, but her hand found his shoulder and stayed there a moment, and neither of them pretended it was nothing.

* * *

They spent the evening preparing, because preparation was the only prayer Hazel believed in. Fuel calculated. Weight and balance scratched on the back of the chart. The route in her head: low along the unnamed island's lee shore, out over the channel at an altitude that would read on radar as a fishing boat's shadow, then north to Pelican Narrows while the light was still bad enough to hide them and good enough to land by.

Teo checked the tie-downs a third time and came back to the fire. Hazel was writing—actually writing, pen on paper, in a small notebook he'd never seen her use. He sat down across from her and, deliberately, did not look at what she wrote. Some courtesies cost nothing and buy everything.

"What happens if we make it to Ruthie's?" he asked.

"She fixes the plane proper. We resupply. And then we have two tides left, and a ledger to finish recovering, and a broadcast to make that Brase can't buy or bury." She capped the pen. "But first we get off this rock."

"And after? After the sixth tide. After Brase." He picked at a thread on his sleeve. "People like us don't get an after. We get a next thing. There's a difference."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"When I was little," she said, "my father used to tell me the difference between a wreck and a passage. A wreck is something the sea takes. A passage is something you sail through. Same water, same storm—what it becomes depends on whether you're still steering at the end of it." She tucked the notebook away. "I stopped steering for ten years, Teo. I just drifted under a different name. This week is the first time since I was nineteen that I've had a heading."

"And what is it?"

"Truth first. Cargo second. And after—" She glanced at him, and something in her expression was almost shy, which on Hazel Joubert—or Hazel Marchand, or whichever woman this was—was so unexpected that Teo forgot to breathe. "After, I'm going to need a mechanic's apprentice who knows his way around a machine shop and doesn't scare easy. If you want it. With a surname attached, if you want that too. Mine works fine. Nobody's using it."

Teo stared at the fire. His eyes were burning and he blamed the smoke, thoroughly and permanently.

"Yeah," he managed. "Okay."

"Good." She stood, brisk again, armor back on but buckled differently now, looser, like something worn by choice. "Sleep. We lift at first light, before the fleet's coffee's cold. And Teo—"

He looked up.

"If anyone asks who you are between here and Pelican Narrows, you're my crew. Hazel Joubert's crew." A pause, and the ghost of a smile. "For now, that name will keep you safer than the truth. One more tide. Then we stop hiding."

Out on the black water, far off, a searchlight swept the channel once, twice, and went dark. Neither of them saw it. But the fleet had begun to move, and the night was counting down.
