## 17. Fifth Tide

The barometer fell all afternoon like a stone dropped down a well.

Hazel watched it happen on the brass instrument bolted to the hangar wall in Pelican Narrows—watched the needle slide through twenty-nine and keep going—and felt the tide schedule shift under her feet the way she'd once felt river ice begin to move. The storm wasn't coming anymore. It was here, arriving early out of the southeast with a smell of iodine and cold iron, dragging the sea ahead of it like a dog hauling a sled. Which meant the water would come higher than the tables promised, and sooner, and the flats that should have lain bare for six hours of darkness would offer her perhaps four.

Four hours of night. One window. And somewhere out there, Brase's flagship riding at anchor off the Sable Tidal Flats with its lights burning like a carnival nobody had been invited to.

She stood in the hangar doorway while the first gusts came up the channel, flattening the water to hammered tin, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn. Fuel: enough for three hours' flying plus reserve if she leaned the mixture and didn't fight the wind more than she had to. Airframe: the tailwheel Ruthie had trued up after the fourth tide held true but sang a little at speed, a thin wire-whine she could feel through her boots on the rudder pedals. Chart: Amos's oilskin original, soft as cloth now from handling, folded against her ribs inside her jacket where it had lived so long it had taken the shape of her body. Tide tables: wrong by an hour, maybe ninety minutes, thanks to the storm surge stacking water into every bay along the coast.

And Teo, sitting on an overturned crate behind her with his elbows on his knees, watching her do the math without asking what the answer was.

"The divers," he said. "They'll run at slack water same as you. Maybe earlier. Brase doesn't wait for conditions to be perfect—he waits for them to be possible."

"You know this from working for him."

"I know it from being fifteen and carrying coffee to men who complained about waiting." He picked at the crate's splintered edge. "He'll have two boats on the site tonight minimum. Floodlights on poles stuck in the mud. They'll work the wreck until the water pushes them off, then pull back to the ship and start again next tide."

"Which is why I go tonight." She turned to face him. "Because tonight they're looking in the wrong place, and tomorrow they might not be."

Teo had shown her the channels twice now, drawing them in the dirt with a stick, correcting himself, arguing with his own memory the way boys argue with everything. The Meridian Star lay in a bowl of deeper silt between two sandbars that only exposed themselves below half tide—a pocket the survey boats kept skirting because their sounders read it as featureless bottom. Amos's chart said otherwise. Amos's chart, drawn in pencil lines gone silver with age, said the stern section had settled into that bowl upright, hold hatches still dogged, and that a man could walk to it across the flats if he knew which ribbons of gravel stayed firm and which were soup that swallowed boots whole.

Amos had walked it once, thirty years ago, and never told a soul until he was dying and out of options and had nothing left to trade except the truth.

* * *

She flew out at dusk, before full dark, because she wanted the last grey light to find the sandbars by. The front met her ten miles offshore—a wall of cloud the color of wet slate, lit from within by a bruised flicker that wasn't quite lightning yet but was thinking about it. Wind out of the southeast at thirty-five and gusting fifty, quartering across her course so the old plane crabbed sideways through the air like a crab crossing a deck. Rain came in sheets, then stopped, then came again harder, each cell a curtain she flew through with her jaw clenched and both hands white on the yoke.

Below, the sea was chaos. Whitecaps everywhere, streaks of foam torn loose and flying horizontal, and beneath all of it the surge piling water onto shores that weren't expecting it for hours yet.

She found the flats by the change in the water's color even through the rain—the green-grey of open sea giving way to the brown-cream of shallows churned to milk. The sandbars themselves were drowned; she could see that immediately, long dark swells breaking where the charts promised dry gravel. No landing there. Not yet. Not for hours.

But the storm worked both directions. If the water came early and high, it also went out early and hard when the wind swung, and the wind was swinging—she could feel it veering in the controls, degree by degree, the way a man turns in his sleep. By midnight the southeast gale would back around to east-northeast and begin dragging the water off the flats like a hand pulling a tablecloth.

She climbed above the weather instead of fighting it, into air so cold and clean it hurt her teeth, and set a circling pattern over the black emptiness of the Sable Flats with the storm flashing silently below her like something dreaming. From up here she could see Brase's operation laid out plain as a diagram: the flagship at anchor, riding lights doubled against the weather, and two smaller vessels working the shallows to the north with searchlights sweeping the water in slow arcs. Looking. They were still looking. Their lights crawled over square mile after square mile of brown water, methodical as a man reading a page line by line, and none of those lines touched the bowl where the Meridian Star lay sleeping under six feet of sea.

Amos had never given them up. Even dying, even bargaining, even begging her for a flight that nearly killed them both—he had kept the coordinates locked behind his teeth and paid for his passage with paper instead of words.

"Good man," she said aloud, to no one, to him, wherever dying prospectors went.

Then she checked her watch, saw the moonless dark coming down, and began letting herself down through the weather in slow spirals, feeling for the wind's new direction with her wingtips the way you test bathwater with one toe.

* * *

By eleven the wind had backed fully northeast and hardened, and the flats began to drain.

It happened fast—faster than any tide she'd ever watched. The water didn't recede so much as get pulled, hauled off the bars in long racing rips that hissed and foamed, and within forty minutes of the turn the first gravel shoulders broke surface, black and gleaming, streaming like the backs of surfacing animals. The rain had eased to a fine driven mist. There was no moon. There was no star. There was only the distant glow of Brase's floodlights to the north, painting the low clouds orange, and the sound of the wind, and the sound of the sea leaving.

Hazel brought the plane down over the southern bar with her landing lights off, navigating by memory and by the pale seam of foam that marked the channel edge, and touched down on wet gravel at the exact moment Teo had called from the beach below—the moment when the bar was firm enough to hold a wheel and soft enough to forgive one. The tires hit, skipped, hit again, and rolled out through ankle-deep sheet water that sprayed up past the struts and froze on contact with the cold metal. She taxied to the high shoulder of the bar where the gravel rose in a low ridge, turned the plane into the wind, and shut her down into a silence so complete after the engine that her ears rang.

For a moment she just sat there, hands on the yoke, listening to the wind rock the wings and feeling the gravel settle under the wheels.

Then she got out, and the cold took her breath like a slap.

Teo was already moving. He'd insisted on coming—had argued for it, actually, with a stubbornness that surprised her, and she'd let him win because he knew the ground and she didn't, not really, not at night, not in this. He carried the coil of rope and the pry bar and the big waterproof flashlight with the red lens, and he moved across the shining flats with a low crouched grace, testing each step before committing his weight, reading the surface the way she read sky. She followed in his footsteps exactly, placing her boots where his had pressed, and the gravel held firm and treacherous by turns, and the wind tried to take her hat and her balance and her resolve, one after another.

The bowl lay a half mile north. They walked it in twenty minutes that felt like two hours, bent double against the wind, and then the ground fell away beneath them and there it was.

Even in the dark, even stripped by thirty years of tide, the Meridian Star announced itself. The stern section rose from the drained bowl like the ribcage of something enormous—a wilderness of black frames and broken plating, weed-hung, barnacled white, standing perhaps twelve feet above the wet sand at its highest. The hull plating had rusted through in lace patterns, and the wind played through the holes in it, making a low moaning chord that changed with every gust. It sounded almost like singing. It sounded almost like a name being called.

Hazel stood at the edge of the bowl with the rain beading on her face and could not move.

She had stood here at low tide twice before. She had photographed the ribs, counted the frames, mapped the debris field. But this was different. This was the last time. Whatever she didn't carry out of that hold tonight would belong to Brase's divers by morning, or to the sea, or to the lawyers, and the knowledge sat on her chest like ballast.

"Hazel." Teo's voice, close, urgent, pitched under the wind. "Lights. North."

She turned. Far off across the flooded northern flats, two points of white light were moving—not sweeping now, but steady, converging, coming toward the bowl. Boats running the channels in the dark. Running them well, which meant local pilots, which meant Brase had bought or coerced someone who knew these waters the way Teo did.

"How long?"

"Fifteen minutes. Maybe less if they push." He was already scrambling down into the bowl, rope coiled over his shoulder. "The hold hatch is aft of the mainmast stump. Amos's chart showed it dogged. Come on."

She came.

* * *

The hatch fought them.

Thirty years of salt had welded the dogs solid, and the pry bar skated twice before Teo thought to wedge it and lean with his whole slight weight while Hazel struck the handle with the flat of the wrench. On the third blow something gave with a crack that echoed inside the hull like a rifle shot, and rust flakes rained down through the red glow of the flashlight, and the hatch cover lifted an inch with a sigh of trapped air that smelled of brine and tar and something older—something dry and papery and human, the smell of a sealed room, of belongings left behind in a hurry or left behind forever.

They got the cover back on its hinges and looked down into blackness.

The ladder was there, just as the chart promised, iron rungs bolted to the bulkhead, crusted but intact. Hazel went first. The rungs rang faintly under her boots, a small bell-note swallowed by the wind outside, and the darkness closed over her head like water. At the bottom her boots found decking buried under a foot of drifted silt, soft as flour, and her light found—

Crates.

Dozens of them, stacked in the shadows, some collapsed into pulp, some still squared and proud with stenciled lettering she couldn't quite read through the crust. Registered cargo. The salvage value Brase had been chasing across six tides and however many thousands of dollars, lying right here in the dark where it had waited out three decades and everyone's greed.

She stepped over it. All of it. Because Amos's chart had marked one thing besides the hatch, one small notation in that silver pencil, circled twice: *master's cabin, fwd, strongbox under bunk.*

The forward cabin was a squeeze of collapsed bulkheads and hanging wire, and the bunk was a rusted frame furred with weed, and when Hazel knelt in the silt and reached underneath it her fingers found cold metal almost immediately. A box the size of a bread loaf, latched, heavy, its handle wrapped in leather gone stiff as bark. She dragged it out into the light, and her heart was slamming now, and her hands shook—not from cold, though the cold was savage, but from the specific trembling of a person about to learn whether the last month of her life meant anything.

The latch gave. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth just as Amos had wrapped his chart: a ledger. Bound in canvas, corner-stamped with the Meridian Star's name and voyage number, its pages swollen with damp but legible, the ink faded to brown but the columns crisp. Cargo manifests. Two sets of them, side by side—one in the tidy hand of a shipping clerk, one in a different hand entirely, corrections scrawled in the margins, entries crossed out and rewritten. And on the flyleaf, in the second hand, a signature she recognized from ten years of nightmares:

*C. Brase, Salvage Master.*

Under it, itemized in his own writing: crates logged as dredging equipment outbound, logged as machine parts inbound, weights that matched nothing, ports of entry that didn't exist on any route the Meridian Star had ever sailed. Contraband, documented in the fraudster's own hand, carried aboard a ship whose crew had included a man named Elias Joubert who had seen too much and said so, and who had therefore needed to die on paper before the ship ever sailed.

Her father hadn't drowned. Her father had been erased. And here, in her numb hands, was the arithmetic of the erasure, page after page, in ink.

"Hazel!" Teo's voice cracked down through the hatch opening, raw with wind. "Lights in the channel! They're coming fast—they've got the entrance to the bowl!"

She stuffed the ledger inside her jacket, zipped it, and took the ladder in four jumps.

* * *

They ran.

The flats that had carried them firm and friendly an hour ago had begun to soften as the far tide turned, water seeping up through the gravel in dark lenses that gleamed in the floodlight spill now washing across the eastern bars. Behind them, in the bowl, lights bloomed—divers' lamps, torch beams stabbing down through the open hatch they'd left gaping, voices carrying thin and angry across the wind. Someone had found the disturbed silt. Someone had found the empty space under the bunk.

A shout went up, wordless, carrying.

Teo led them south on a line she wouldn't have chosen, cutting wide of the direct route, and she understood why when the ground ahead of them shivered and sucked at his boot and he veered hard left without breaking stride. Soft ground. Soup ground. The kind that took a man to his knees and kept him. They ran the firm ribbon between the traps with the wind screaming and the floodlights sweeping behind them, and once a torch beam caught them full-on for two seconds—two seconds of blinding white, of shadow thrown huge across the shining flats—and then Teo hauled her down behind a ridge of stranded kelp-wrack and the beam slid away, hunting elsewhere, and they ran again.

The plane sat on its high shoulder of gravel exactly where she'd left it, streaming rain, wings rocking, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. She got the door open and shoved Teo up and in, threw herself after him, and the engine caught on the third swing of the prop—third swing, in that cold, in that wet, a mercy she would think about later—and she hauled the tailwheel out of the softening gravel with full power and full forward stick and the old plane lurched free and began to roll.

Behind them, in the mirror of her rearview, the floodlights converged on the empty bar where she'd landed, and a voice was shouting into a radio, and somewhere out in the dark a boat engine opened up with intent.

Hazel pulled the wheels off the gravel and into the storm-dark and turned south for Pelican Narrows with the ledger hard against her ribs and her father's name written in a dead man's evidence, and behind her the sixth tide began to rise across the Sable Flats, covering everything—wreck, tracks, footprints, truth—with moving water.

She had until it crested. Then Brase would know exactly what she carried, and exactly who she was, and exactly where she was going.

She pushed the throttle forward into the rain.
