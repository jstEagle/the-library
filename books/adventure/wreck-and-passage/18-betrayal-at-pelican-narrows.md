## 18. Betrayal at Pelican Narrows

The radio crackled to life forty miles out, and Hazel knew before Ruthie spoke.

"Ox-alpha strip is clear," Ruthie said, her voice flat as slate. "Wind's down. Come on in."

Clear. Ruthie never said clear. Ruthie said *come look at this sky yourself, I don't trust it*, Ruthie said *the crosswind will eat your tires*, Ruthie said *you sure about this, girl* in a voice that made you check everything twice. Ruthie spoke in warnings the way other people spoke in sentences. *Clear* was a word from a brochure.

Hazel's thumb hovered over the transmit switch. Below her, the coast unspooled in the last gray light—black spruce, black water, the channel a ribbon of pewter where Brase's survey boats had been anchored two days ago. The ledger rode in a waterproof bag wedged under her seat, forty pages of Brase's own handwriting proving what he'd done to her father, to the Meridian Star, to every permit he'd ever signed. The chart was in her jacket, against her ribs, where it had lived since the third tide.

"Ruthie," she said. "Who's standing on my strip?"

A pause. Long enough to measure in heartbeats. "Nobody."

"Ruthie."

"Land the plane, Hazel." And then, quieter, almost breaking: "There's a man here with a paper that says he can take my license and my shop and put Teo on a boat south. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I called them an hour ago. I couldn't—"

Hazel keyed off the radio.

The engine droned. The propeller bit the cold air with its small faithful sound. She had ten minutes of fuel, forty miles of coastline, and a decision that split down the middle of her like a seam in ice.

She could run. North, west, anywhere the fuel would carry her, and become the woman without a name again—the Hazel who kept no address, filed no flight plans, existed in the territory's memory the way a bird exists in the memory of the sky. She had done it for ten years. She knew how. It was the one skill her father's death had taught her, and she had sharpened it until it was all she was.

But the ledger was in the bag under her seat. And Teo was on that strip.

* * *

Teo heard the plane before he saw it—the particular stutter of her engine, the one Ruthie said sounded like a man clearing his throat before telling a lie. He was sitting in the hangar office with his wrists zip-tied to the arm of a chair, which was, he had to admit, a better arrangement than he'd expected. The man from Brase Salvage—a wide, unhurried man named Dree with a face like a closed door—hadn't hit him. Hadn't needed to. Dree had simply shown him a photograph of a boy in a harbor town four hundred miles south, a boy with Teo's face and a woman's arm around his shoulder, and said, *Your mother still lives there. Boats go south every week. Some of them are ours.*

So Teo sat and watched the light die through the hangar's dirty windows and listened to Ruthie scrub a wrench across a workbench that didn't need cleaning, over and over, like penance.

"He'll understand," Ruthie said, for the fourth time. It might have been to Teo. It might have been to the wrench. "She's flown through worse than this. She'll read the strip and she'll—"

"She'll land," Teo said. "Because I'm here. That's what she does now." He flexed his wrists against the plastic. It bit. "She didn't used to do that. Land for people. I made her."

Dree looked up from his paperwork with something that was almost interest. "You made her."

"Nobody makes Hazel Joubert do anything," Teo said. "But she decided I was hers. That's worse than making. That's choosing."

Dree went back to his papers. But he shifted his chair a little, so he could see the window.

* * *

Hazel brought the plane in low over the tree line with the transponder dark and her lights off, running on the strip's edge lights and twenty years of knowing this coast the way other people knew their own kitchens. The tide was going out. The mudflats below the strip lay exposed and gleaming, acres of gray-brown muck veined with channels, and an idea came to her the way weather comes—whole, and with its own wind.

She flared short, touched down on the wet sand at the flats' margin where the gravel held, and rolled to a stop in the shadow of the bluff, a quarter mile from the hangar, engine ticking into silence.

Then she sat and watched.

The hangar doors stood open. Two figures in the doorway, one wide, one narrow. A truck with a light bar parked beside the fuel tanks. And Ruthie, out by the pumps under the single sodium lamp, arms wrapped around herself, looking not at the strip but at the flats—looking, Hazel realized, at exactly the place a plane would go if it didn't want to be seen.

Ruthie had left her a door. It was a small door and it might be a trap, and Hazel opened it anyway, because the ledger was under her seat and Teo was in the light and some things you carry into a burning building because they are the only things that were ever yours.

She waded in. The mud took her to the knee, then the thigh, cold as a grave and sucking like a debt. The chart pressed against her ribs. The ledger bag she slung across her back. She moved channel to channel, following the drainage the way Amos had taught her to read it, and the sodium lamp shrank behind her until it was a star with a town's name.

* * *

Teo saw her first—a shape in the last light, low along the flats, moving the way an animal moves when it knows it's hunted: fast, silent, committed. His heart did something complicated. He tipped his head, one degree, toward the window, and Dree followed the motion and was on his feet and out the door in three strides, and Teo was alone in the office with a zip tie and a decision.

The workbench had Ruthie's tools on it. Ruthie's tools were always sharp, always oiled, always where a hand could find them in the dark. Teo slid his chair sideways, hooked the tin snips off the pegboard with two fingers, and went to work on the plastic, sawing with the patience of someone who had spent a winter alone on tidal flats learning that panic was just time spent twice.

Outside, a flashlight beam swept the mud. Voices. Dree's voice, flat and carrying: *Miss Joubert. We know you're out there. We have the boy. We have the ledger's worth of trouble either way—come in and we talk like businesspeople.*

The zip tie parted. Teo rubbed his wrists, took the chart's location out of his memory and put it nowhere, took everything Ruthie had told him about the back road to the ridge and folded it flat, and went out through the hangar's side door into the dark that had raised him.

* * *

Hazel heard them coming—two men on the dike road, boots in gravel, lights bouncing—and went flat into a drainage channel with the cold water at her chin and the ledger bag sealed above the surface. The beams crossed over her. A voice said, *She went north along the bluff,* and the boots moved on, and Hazel lay in the channel of a tidal flat in the dark and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *They can take the ledger. They can take the plane. They cannot take the chart, and they cannot take what the letters say, because that lives in me now.*

She had read them all, on the island, by candlelight. She knew her father's handwriting better than her own. If the paper burned, the words would still be true.

She moved north along the bluff, and the hills took her.

* * *

They found Teo at the tree line because he let them—or because Dree was better in the dark than a boy who'd learned his woods on the flats, where the hiding places were all horizontal. A hand closed on his collar and turned him, and Dree looked down at him with no anger at all, which was the worst part.

"Where's the chart, Teo?"

"I don't have it."

"Where is it?"

"On her."

Dree nodded slowly, like a man confirming a figure in a ledger. "Then here's what happens. You come with us tonight. Tomorrow you're on a boat south, and the woman who runs the harbor office there gets a call, and your mother learns what you've been doing all winter. Unless—" he crouched, brought his voice down to the register men use for offers they're ashamed of— "unless when they find Hazel, you're standing next to me, and you tell her you asked us to come. That you were scared. That you wanted to go home. She'll believe it. People believe what they're afraid of."

Teo looked at him for a long moment. The flashlight made a white coin on the ground between them.

"You don't know her at all," Teo said. "She'd believe it and she'd come for me anyway. That's the whole problem with her."

Dree stood. "Put him in the truck."

* * *

Ruthie found Hazel at the treeline, because Ruthie had left the second door—the one that mattered. She came up the overgrown skid trail behind the hangar with a rucksack on her back and no flashlight, moving by memory, and when she spoke she didn't say *I'm sorry* again, because she'd spent it on the radio.

"Fuel truck's gassed. I told Dree it wouldn't start till morning. He believes me because he's never turned a wrench in his life." She set the rucksack down. Inside: the chart tube's spare, a first-aid kit, chocolate, a .303 rifle wrapped in an oilcloth, and the spare tailwheel assembly Hazel had left in the hangar in August. "Your plane's on the flats where you left her. Tide'll float her off at 0300 if you push her to the channel. Dree's men are watching the strip, not the water. Men like that never watch the water."

Hazel took the rucksack. Her hands were shaking—not from cold, not anymore, from the other thing. "Ruthie. The ledger."

"Gone. Dree has it in a document case, locked, and he's proud of it." Ruthie's voice cracked and she let it. "I gave it up, Hazel. They showed me a paper and I gave it up in under a minute. Ten years I've known you and I lasted under a minute."

Hazel looked at her—really looked, for the first time since the radio call—at the woman who had patched her wingtip with salvaged fabric and never asked what the flights were for, who had noticed the chart and warned her about the scouts, who had called Brase's men an hour ahead because they held her whole life in a folder.

"You lasted exactly as long as they designed you to last," Hazel said. "They didn't beat you, Ruthie. They built a trap with your shop in the middle of it. That's not betrayal. That's what it costs to be findable." She shouldered the rucksack. "I spent ten years being unfindable and I had nothing. You spent ten years being findable and you had a town. We were both just paying our rates."

Ruthie made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Teo's in the truck. They're holding him till morning."

"Then I have until morning." Hazel turned toward the flats, where the tide was already beginning its long slow turn, the channels filling from the sea end first, the way they always did, the way Amos had taught her, the way her father must have known them once, standing on this same coast with a testimony in his pocket and a daughter in a house he'd never see again. "Ruthie. When this is over—whatever this is—don't apologize to me again. Just be at the strip."

"Where else," Ruthie said, and went down the skid trail into the dark.

* * *

They kept Teo in the truck cab with the windows up, which was a mistake, because Teo had spent a winter reading water and the truck was parked facing the flats, and at 0230 he saw the channel fill and a shape come off the sand on the flood tide—her plane, engine at idle, running lights dark, sliding down the black water like something the sea was handing back.

He didn't make a sound. Dree was asleep against the far door. The driver had his radio on low.

The plane lifted off the channel with the tide under it and turned north, and Teo pressed his forehead to the cold glass and watched the running lights blink on at last, two small red stars climbing, and he thought: *She didn't leave. She's going where they're going. She's going to the sixth tide.*

And he thought: *I have until morning to be worth coming back for.*

The truck would leave at dawn for the coast road, and the coast road passed within a mile of the old survey camp at Miller's Bluff, and Teo knew every culvert and cutbank on that stretch the way he knew the channels—knew the one place the road narrowed between rock walls where a truck had to slow to walking pace.

He closed his eyes and started counting hours.
