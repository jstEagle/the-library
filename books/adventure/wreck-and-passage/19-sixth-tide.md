## 19. Sixth Tide

The storm did not break so much as fray. All night it had come down the coast like something dragged behind a truck, and by dawn it was coming apart at the seams, ragged curtains of rain torn loose from the main body, gaps of bruised gold light opening and closing over the water like eyes that couldn't decide whether to stay shut. Hazel stood at the window of the trapper's cabin in the hills and watched the flats appear and disappear beneath the scud, silver one moment, gunmetal the next, and thought: six hours. Maybe five.

The permits died at high water. After that, everything under the Sable Tidal Flats—the ribs of the *Meridian Star*, the crates in her hold, the truth packed between them like ballast—belonged to whoever held the next license. And everyone in the district knew whose name was already typed on that license, waiting only for the ink.

Hazel turned back to the table. The original chart lay there, soft as cloth now from handling, its oilskin wrapper long since sacrificed to keep the letters dry. Beside it, folded into eighths, were four pages she had copied by lantern light two nights ago, kneeling on the floor of this cabin while the wind tried to peel the roof off—four pages transcribed from Amos Vane's ledger before Brase's men ever knew it existed. She had done it out of instinct, the same instinct that kept her name off registries for ten years, the instinct that said *never let the only copy live in someone else's hands.* She had hated herself a little while she wrote, hunched over the paper like a thief in her own story. Now she kissed her own handwriting, almost.

*Meridian Star, outward manifest, 14 March.* Cargo entered as dredging equipment. Cargo actually loaded, per Vane's tally: forty-one crates marked D.E., contents unlisted. Three entries initialed C.B. Three payments routed through a company name she had traced to a post office box in Pelican Narrows. And in the margin, in Amos's crabbed hand, a line she had read so many times she could recite it against the wind: *Brase signs the permits, Brase signs the manifests, Brase signs the death certificates. Same pen.*

Her father's letters sat beneath the copies, weighted with a stone. She didn't open them again. She knew them now the way she knew the channels—by heart, by weight, by the way they pulled at her. He had been going to testify. He had written to her because he thought he might not get the chance to say anything else. The tide had taken him before the mail could.

The radio crackled on the shelf. District channel, mostly static, but underneath it she could hear the fleet talking—Brase's vessels checking in, positions called in clipped numbers, the *Corwin's Pride* leading them, the flagship riding low with divers and cranes and men who had spent five tides dragging the wrong half of the flats. They still didn't have the coordinates. Amos had kept them in his skull until his skull gave out, and what he'd given Hazel he'd given in trust.

But they had something else. They had Ruthie's report. They had the strip at Pelican Narrows watched, they had Teo in a locked room somewhere on the *Pride*, and they had a patrol skiff that had already put holes in her tailwheel once and would happily put holes in worse.

Hazel looked at the plane through the trees—her patched, scarred, resurrected Bird Dog, tied down on the mossy clearing Ruthie had shown her a lifetime ago, five days ago. The tailwheel was lashed with wire and hope. The wingtip patch was holding. The engine had started on the third crank this morning and run rough for thirty seconds before settling into its familiar gravel-voiced idle, and she had stood there listening to it the way you listen to an old dog's breathing.

She gathered the papers, sealed them in the oilskin, and went out into the wind.

* * *

The front's ragged edge was exactly where the forecast said it wouldn't be.

She climbed out of the hills into weather that hit the plane like a thrown blanket—rain in horizontal ropes, then a gap of clean gray air, then another wall. The Bird Dog bucked, wallowed, bit back. Hazel flew it with her whole body, feet dancing on the rudders, hands light on a yoke that wanted to be wrestled and couldn't afford to be. Below her the coastline scrolled past in glimpses: black spruce, white water, the long scar of the logging road where she had landed six nights ago with Amos dying beside her and no idea yet what he was handing her.

"Come on," she told the engine. "Come on, old girl. One more."

The district channel hissed in her headset. She had been monitoring it since takeoff, letting the fleet's voices wash over her, building the picture piece by piece the way she built weather pictures—from fragments, from instinct, from the shape of what wasn't said.

*—Pride, survey skiff three, we've got mud showing on the east bar, visibility maybe half a mile—*

*—Copy three. Hold position. Divers stand down till this squall line passes—*

And then a voice she hadn't heard except through a speaker, but knew instantly anyway, smooth as varnish: *All units, this is Brase. Tide turns in four hours. I want that wreck found before it turns. Spread east along the channel markers. She'll be shallow—she broke up shallow, every account says so.*

Every account except the right one.

Hazel checked her watch against the tide tables she'd drawn from memory onto the back of a fuel receipt. Low water had passed at 0640. The flats were filling now, the channels drowning first, the bars going under one by one like lights switched off in a house. In four hours the *Meridian Star* would sleep under nine feet of cold water, and the law would hand her to Corwin Brase with a bow on top.

Unless the law heard something else first.

She keyed the mic, and her voice came out steadier than her hands. "Any station, any station, this is Bushhawk Seven-Seven. I have documents pertaining to the Meridian Star wreck and to salvage concessions held by Brase Salvage and Concessions. Requesting all stations monitor one-four-two. I say again—all stations monitor one-four-two." She released the key. Static. Somewhere out there, she knew, every radio in the district had just gone quiet—fishermen, float operators, the Mountie detachment at Fort Alder, the concession office itself—and every one of them was now leaning toward their set.

Good. Let them lean.

* * *

She found the *Corwin's Pride* where she knew she would—not because anyone told her, but because the flats themselves told her. The deep channel ran along the north shore; the sandbars fanned south of it like fingers; and any man who didn't grow up reading these waters would hug the channel markers, especially in half-mile visibility, especially with a fortune waiting at the end of the run. The *Pride* was a converted trawler, steel-hulled, crane-armed, drawing eleven feet if she drew an inch. Eleven feet. Hazel had grown up on these flats. She knew a bar off Marker Nine that showed eight feet at mean low water and lied about it on every chart ever printed, because the river kept moving it and nobody official had bothered to follow.

The squall opened like a curtain and there she was—a quarter mile off, gray on gray, her running lights smeared in the rain, her wake a pale wound across the flooding shallows. Two skiff escorts fanned ahead of her, poking along the markers. Behind her, further out in the channel, the rest of the fleet strung out like beads.

Hazel's fury arrived all at once, and it arrived clean.

It wasn't the hot kind. It was the kind that comes after grief has burned everything flammable away and leaves you standing in ash with perfect vision. Ten years of silence. A falsified certificate with her father's name on it, signed by the same pen that signed the contraband manifests. A boy named Teo locked in a cabin for the crime of stealing bread from a monster. An old man dying alone in a freezing cabin because the system this man owned had left him nothing but a wreck and a secret. All of it—every piece of it—was steering toward Marker Nine at eight knots, trusting the water to hold it.

The water wouldn't. Hazel would make sure of it.

She dropped low. Fifty feet, forty, skimming under the cloud base with rain hammering the windshield and the flats blurring past below—mudflats the color of old pewter, ribbed with current, stitched with the white lace of shoal water. Ahead, the *Pride*'s masthead light swung gently, confidently, from side to side. A ship at ease. A ship whose captain believed the charts.

Hazel keyed the mic one more time, and this time she didn't ask anyone to monitor anything.

"Corwin Brase. This is Hazel Joubert. My father was Elias Joubert, deckhand on the Meridian Star, and your signature killed him twice—once on paper, once in fact. You're heading for a bar off Marker Nine that isn't where your charts say it is. Hard to starboard now, or you're aground inside twenty minutes. Your choice. I'm done choosing for you."

She banked hard, pulled up out of the spray, and circled wide, watching.

For a long moment the *Pride* held her course. Then, faintly, through the static, she heard the bridge voice—confused, angry, calling to the escort skiffs, asking who was flying, asking what the hell a Joubert was. Asking Brase. And Brase's answer came back calm and poisonous: *Ignore her. She's bluffing. Maintain course.*

"You always did think the paper was the world," Hazel said quietly.

The *Pride* drove on. The rain thickened. And then, with a slowness that made it almost gentle, the great ship began to die. First a shudder Hazel could see even from the air—a tremor running aft along the hull, a stutter in the wake. Then the sound, arriving seconds later across the water: a long grinding roar, metal on sand, the voice of eleven feet of draft meeting seven feet of bar. The *Pride* listed to port, wallowed, ground another fifty yards as her momentum carried her, and stopped. Her crane arm swung drunkenly. Her lights flickered and steadied. She sat there canted in the rising tide like a whale that had misread the beach, and around her the escort skiffs scattered like startled birds.

Hazel circled once above the wreck of Brase's ambition, and felt—nothing she'd expected. Not triumph. Something colder and better. Clarity, sharp as the air after lightning.

Now the second half.

* * *

She climbed to a thousand feet, above the worst of the rain, where the world was a rolling gray ceiling and the district spread out below her in patches: the channel, the islands, the far smudge of Pelican Narrows, the thin threads of antenna wire reaching up from every shack and wheelhouse and detachment office within eighty miles. She unfolded the copied pages on her knee, weighted them against the yoke, and keyed the transmit switch.

"This is Hazel Joubert, broadcasting on the district emergency frequency. What follows is read from the personal ledger of Amos Vane, purser's mate aboard the supply vessel Meridian Star, recovered from the wreck site and copied verbatim. Every station is welcome to record it. Mr. Brase—I know you're listening. Consider this my testimony. My father never got to give his."

And she read.

She read the manifest entries—forty-one crates entered as dredging equipment, contents unlisted, initialed C.B. She read the payment routing, the post office box, the shell company's name spelled out letter by letter so no one could miss it. She read Amos's marginal note about the same pen signing permits, manifests, and death certificates. She read the date of the wreck and the date of Elias Joubert's death certificate and pointed out, in a voice that did not shake, that the certificate was filed eleven days before any survivor list reached shore—which meant someone had known he was dead before anyone knew who had drowned. She read until her throat rawed against the wind noise, until the pages grew damp at the edges, until the static on the channel had changed texture—no longer empty, but crowded, full of the silent attention of a hundred listeners.

Then she read her father's words. Just one passage. The one where he wrote to her: *If anything happens, little bird, it will be because I saw too much and said so. Don't let them make you small enough to hide. You come from loud water and louder love.*

When she finished, she keyed the mic one last time.

"The original ledger and supporting documents are being delivered this morning to the detachment at Fort Alder and to the concession review board. Copies exist in more than one pair of hands. The permits expire at high water, Mr. Brase. So does everything else."

She released the switch. The channel erupted—not with Brase's voice, but with everyone else's, all at once, fishermen demanding confirmation, the detachment ordering all units to stand by, a woman's voice from Pelican Narrows saying *that's Ruthie Okafor's friend, that's the pilot, I knew it, I knew it* —and underneath it all, briefly, one smooth voice trying to cut through, saying *this is unauthorized, this is slander, disregard—* and being drowned, utterly, by the crowd.

Hazel pushed the nose over and dove back into the weather, laughing, or crying, or both, it didn't matter which.

* * *

Fort Alder strip appeared out of the murk at 1030, a gravel slash between black timber, and she put the Bird Dog down with the tailwheel chirping once on touchdown and holding. Two constables were already walking out to meet her, coats streaming, one of them carrying a waterproof evidence bag like it was a life preserver. She taxied to them, shut down, and climbed out into the rain with the oilskin bundle hugged to her chest.

"Hazel Joubert," she said, loud enough to be heard over the weather, loud enough to be heard by whatever ghosts were listening. "I have documents to file. And I'm told you have a boy on one of Brase's vessels who needs collecting before the tide turns."

The older constable took the bundle with both hands, the way you take something that weighs more than paper. "We've been listening to the radio all morning, ma'am. Half the district has." He paused, and something almost like a smile moved under his dripping hat brim. "The *Pride*'s master has been on the emergency channel for an hour demanding a tow. Nobody's answered him."

"He knows how the flats work," Hazel said. "He'll figure it out. Eventually."

She stood in the rain beside her battered plane and watched the constables carry her father's name—and hers—into the station, and the tide began to turn below the flats, filling the channels, covering the bars, erasing the morning's battlefield inch by inch. High water was coming. The permits were dying. And somewhere out on a sandbar off Marker Nine, Corwin Brase was learning what her father had learned at the end: that the water keeps its own records, and it does not care whose signature is on the paper.

Hazel looked south, toward the gray line where the fleet lay stranded and a fifteen-year-old boy was waiting in a locked cabin, counting the same tide she was.

"Hold on, Teo," she said to the wind. "I'm coming back for you."
