## 20. Concession

The tide turned at 11:47, and Hazel knew it the way she knew weather—by the smell of it, by the way the mudflats exhaled their long grey breath and began to drink the sea back in. She stood on the government dock at Pelican Narrows with her hands in her pockets and watched the water come for the Meridian Star again, covering the ribs she had bled to reach, and felt nothing like triumph.

She felt light. That was the strange part. Lighter than she had felt in ten years, as if the storm of the last six tides had scoured something off her that she hadn't known she was carrying.

The authorities had come at dawn. Not the way Brase's people arrived—three black-hulled vessels running dark through the fog—but properly, with paperwork and a coast guard cutter riding high on its own importance, its white paint scrubbed bright against the grey morning. There were four of them: two fisheries officers, an inspector from the Concessions Bureau whose name was Marsh and whose handshake was damp, and a young constable who kept looking at Hazel's plane—the patched wingtip, the scarred fuselage, the tailwheel lashed with wire—with an expression somewhere between horror and reverence.

They had taken her statement twice. Once on the dock, once in the harbour office with a tape recorder turning between them like a small patient insect. She told it all: Amos Vane's chart, the letters, the ledger, the falsified death record, the six tides, the patrol skiff's gunfire, the night run through the storm's ragged edge, the moment she had put her wheels down on the channel shelf and forced the *Corvina*—Brase's flagship, forty years new and worth more than everything Hazel had ever owned—onto the sandbar where the flats would hold it until the sea finished arguing with it.

"You understand," Inspector Marsh said, not unkindly, "that what you did to that vessel could constitute—"

"Could constitute," Hazel said, "the only reason your bureau has anything to investigate."

Marsh had looked at her for a long moment over his damp handshake of a mouth, and then he had nodded, slowly, the way men nod when they concede a point they'd rather not have lost.

* * *

By noon the news had come apart across the district like wet paper.

Hazel heard it in fragments, standing in Ruthie's hangar while the radio crackled through the frequencies she had flown alone for a decade. The Concessions Bureau had suspended every permit Brase Salvage held, pending investigation. The *Corvina* sat hard aground on the channel shelf, listing fifteen degrees, its survey gear drowned in salt water, its crew evacuated by its own tender in the kind of panic that makes men leave their boots behind. A fisheries launch stood off the wreck site now, buoys going in around the Meridian Star's ribs—not salvage buoys, yellow and greedy, but white ones. Protected custody. Evidence.

"Protected custody," Ruthie repeated, wiping her hands on a rag that had been black so long it might have been born that way. She leaned against the workbench under the hanging lamps, arms crossed, studying Hazel with the flat, measuring look she gave engines. "You know what that means? It means nobody gets it. Not you, not Brase, not the next outfit with a fast boat and a lawyer. It sits there under guard until the lawyers finish eating each other, and then it goes to a museum or the bottom, whichever's cheaper."

"I know what it means."

"Cargo's worth a fortune. Registered cargo alone. You flew through gunfire for that site three times."

Hazel turned a bolt between her fingers, feeling its cold weight. Through the hangar's open doors the tide was coming in, silver-grey, indifferent, beautiful. "I know," she said. And found, testing the thought like ice underfoot, that it held. "I don't want it, Ruthie."

Ruthie snorted. "Nobody doesn't want a fortune."

"Then I'm nobody." She set the bolt down. "It was never about the cargo. Amos tried to make it about the cargo because he thought that was the only coin I'd take. But he gave me the manifest too, and the manifest had my father's name on it, and everything after that—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Everything after that was just me trying to find out why a man signed a paper saying my father died when he didn't. I've got the answer now. It's written in a ledger sitting in a coast guard evidence locker. I don't need the ship. I never needed the ship."

Ruthie looked at her a while longer, then threw the rag into the corner where all dead rags went. "Well," she said. "That's either the sanest thing I ever heard or the stupidest, and knowing you it's both." She jerked her chin toward the door. "Constable's looking for you anyway. Something about the boy."

* * *

They held the interview in the harbour office again, the same room, the same recorder, but this time Teo sat beside her on the bench, and this time Hazel did the talking first.

He was fourteen, maybe—he wasn't sure himself—and he sat the way he had done everything since she'd pulled him half-frozen off the beach: watchfully, coiled, ready to be sent somewhere worse. He wore clothes Ruthie had found him, a flannel shirt too big in the shoulders, and he had washed the flats' mud out of his hair but couldn't wash the wariness out of his eyes. When the constable asked him to state his name he said "Teo," and when asked for a surname he looked at Hazel, just one quick glance, and said nothing.

"He worked for Brase Salvage," the constable said, reading from a folder. "Cabin boy, deckhand, general labour. Ran away eight months ago. His employer has filed a request for his return."

"His employer," Hazel said, "is currently under investigation for fraud, falsification of records, and firing on a civilian aircraft. I'd read that file again before you hand a child back to it."

"The request is standard procedure—"

"So is guardianship." She had spent the night before thinking about this, lying awake in the motel room with the chart spread on the bed like a map of her own chest, and she had come down to the dock this morning with the words already built, load-bearing, tested. "I'm filing for temporary guardianship. I have no criminal record—I have almost no record at all, which you're welcome to verify, though it'll take you a while. I have a fixed address as of this week, which is more than I've had in ten years. I have employment lined up with Miss Okafor at the airstrip. And I have—" she paused, and made herself say it plainly, "—a demonstrated willingness to fly through storms and gunfire rather than let this boy go back to the man who worked him and lied to him. I'd suggest you weigh which of us the district would rather see raising him."

The recorder turned. The constable looked at Teo. Teo looked at his hands.

"There's also this," Hazel said, and slid the photograph across the desk—one of the few things she'd recovered, printed at the drugstore that morning from the camera she'd carried through all six tides. It showed Teo on the beach at low tide, pointing at the Meridian Star's ribs, his face lit with the particular fierce joy of someone showing another person a secret. "He identified the salvage fleet's schedule for us. He testified about conditions aboard Brase's vessels. He's in that folder too, if you read past the request form."

The constable was quiet for a long time. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"The guardian application will need references," he said finally. "And the boy's own statement, on record, that he consents."

Teo's head came up. He looked at Hazel—not the quick sideways glance this time but straight on, the way he'd learned to look at her during the nights on the island, when they'd patched the tailwheel by lamplight and traded truths like rations. Then he turned to the constable.

"I consent," he said. His voice cracked in the middle of it and he pushed through the crack without slowing down. "I consent. I want to stay with her. She landed a plane on open water to keep a chart dry. You think Brase ever did anything like that for anybody?" He swallowed. "Please. Write it down."

The constable wrote it down.

* * *

They walked out of the harbour office in the late afternoon, and the light had gone gold and long across the water, the way it does in northern places when the season starts considering turning. The tide was fully in now. Out past the breakwater, the white buoys around the Meridian Star bobbed gently on the flooded flats, marking nothing anyone could take.

Teo stopped at the rail and looked at them for a while.

"It's still down there," he said. "All of it. The crates, the stuff worth more than—we could've—"

"We could've," Hazel agreed.

"And you're okay with that?"

She considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty; she'd promised him that on the island and meant it. She thought about the sealed crates in the hold, the registered cargo worth enough to buy a new life, the life she'd once imagined buying with it before she understood what she actually wanted. She thought about her father's letters, safe in her jacket pocket, going nowhere near any evidence locker. She thought about the word *concession*, which the whole district used for permits and rights and claims, and which had always sounded to her like something you surrendered.

"I spent ten years making sure I owned nothing," she said, "because anything you own can be taken, and anything taken can be used to find you. And it worked. Nobody found me. But nobody found me, Teo. That's not the same as being somewhere." She nodded at the buoys, the water, the wreck sleeping under the flood tide with its secrets finally spoken. "Let the lawyers have the crates. Let the museum have the ribs, if it comes to that. I got what was down there that mattered, and it fits in a jacket pocket, and nobody can buoy it off and guard it. It's mine."

Teo nodded slowly, the way boys nod when they are memorizing something to use later, in some year she couldn't yet imagine. Then: "Does it feel weird? Winning and not getting anything?"

Hazel laughed—a real laugh, surprising her own throat with it. "Ask me tomorrow. Today it feels like putting down something heavy and finding out my arms still work."

* * *

Ruthie met them at the airstrip at dusk with two things: a bill for repairs that she tore up in front of Hazel without explanation, and a piece of plywood on which she had painted, in careful block letters she clearly hadn't let anyone else touch:

**ASHGROVE CHARTER — PELICAN NARROWS**

"That's a working title," she said gruffly, leaning it against the hangar wall. "We can argue about fonts later. Point is, the bureau called. Your permits came through this afternoon—charter class, medevac endorsement, the works. Signed by somebody named Marsh, who apparently owes you his career." She squinted at Hazel. "You put your actual name on the application. I checked. Twice. Thought maybe you'd made a mistake."

"No mistake."

"Huh." Ruthie looked at the plywood sign, then at the plane in the failing light—patched wingtip, wired tailwheel, engine cowl still bearing the scratch from the sandbar gravel—and shook her head slowly. "Ten years invisible, and now there's going to be a sign with your name on it where every pilot and customs officer and bored teenager in the district can see it. You sure?"

Hazel looked at the sign a long time. ASHGROVE. Her father's name. Her name. The name on the manifest, on the falsified record, in the letters she hadn't finished reading yet because some of them deserved better than a motel room. For ten years she had treated the name like a wound; now it sat on a piece of plywood in Ruthie's blocky handwriting and looked less like a wound than like a door.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm sure. Mount it in the morning."

* * *

That night she sat in the motel room—her last night in it; the lease on the little house above Ruthie's hangar started Monday—and read her father's letters again, all of them this time, even the last one, the one she'd been saving. It was short. Most of it was instructions about tide tables, which made her smile through her eyes stinging. At the bottom, in his cramped, patient hand:

*If you're reading this, then someone kept a promise for me, and you got brave somewhere along the way, which I always figured you would. Don't waste time being angry at the sea, Haze. It only ever did what it does. Be angry at men who lie on paper, and then go write your name on something true.*

Outside, the tide was turning again. She could hear it, faintly, the great slow breath of the flats, and tomorrow it would go out and expose the ribs of the Meridian Star to whatever light the law allowed, and the day after that, and the day after that, guarded now, claimed by no one, belonging to the story instead of to any purse.

Hazel closed the letters, put them in the drawer she now thought of as hers, and lay back on the bed with her boots off for the first time in six tides.

In the morning there would be paperwork, and a sign to mount, and a boy to feed breakfast, and a district full of radios already buzzing with her name—her real name—spoken aloud like something newly discovered that had been there all along. In the morning she would start the life she'd been flying toward without knowing it, one delivery, one rumor, one impossible weather call at a time.

But tonight there was just the sound of the tide, coming in faithful as a promise kept, and Hazel Joubert—Ashgrove, both, all of it—slept without locking the door.
