## 21. Her Father's Name

The government office in Fort Meridian smelled of toner and cold coffee and the particular staleness of buildings where important things happen slowly. Hazel stood in the doorway for a long moment before she went in, one hand still on the strap of her pack, the way she'd stood in a hundred doorways over ten years—assessing exits, counting faces, deciding whether this was a room she could afford to be seen in.

There were four people inside. A clerk behind the counter with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair. An old man asleep in a plastic chair beneath a poster about workplace safety. Two women filling out forms at the side counter, murmuring to each other about ferry schedules. Nobody looked up. Nobody would remember her. And that, she realized, was the whole problem—the thing she had come to end.

Her boots left wet prints on the linoleum. Outside, October rain was coming down in long silver threads, drumming on the awning, running off the eaves in ropes. The storm from the sixth tide had blown itself out two days ago, but its aftermath hung around the coast like a debt not yet settled: skies the color of dishwater, wind that kept changing its mind.

"Help you?" the clerk asked, when Hazel finally reached the counter.

"I need to file some paperwork." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "A name restoration. And a correction to a death record."

The clerk pulled a laminated sheet toward herself without any particular interest. "Forms are on the rack. Name changes go through Vital Statistics, third window. Death record amendments—" She paused, looked at Hazel properly for the first time. "Those take longer. There's an adjudication process."

"I know," Hazel said. "I've had ten years to think about it."

* * *

She filled out the forms at the side counter, standing, because sitting down felt like something you did after, not before. The pen they gave her had a chewed cap and wrote in blue ink that smeared if you hurried. She didn't hurry.

**Current name:** Hazel Joubert. The name Amos Vane had used on her, the name she'd bought from a man in Whitehorse who dealt in clean papers and didn't ask questions, the name she'd worn so long it had grown into her like a splinter the body decides to keep. She wrote it carefully, letter by letter, the way you'd write the name of someone you loved but were about to leave.

**Name at birth:** Hazel Meridian Ashgrove.

Her hand stopped above the paper for a full breath. Ten years since she'd written it. Ten years since anyone had spoken it aloud in her hearing—except Brase, over the radio, saying it like he was laying it on a scale to weigh what it might buy him. *Ashgrove.* Her father's name. Elias Ashgrove, deckhand and engineer, second mate's ticket, a man who could fix anything with wire and patience and who sang off-key in the galley and who had walked up a gangplank in March of a year that felt like another century and never walked back down.

She wrote it. The ink took. The paper accepted it without ceremony, which seemed wrong somehow, and right.

Under **reason for change**, there was a line with maybe three inches of space. Hazel thought about what to put there. *Because I was hiding. Because hiding worked. Because it stopped working the day a dying man said my father's name in a cabin that smelled of woodsmoke and fever, and I couldn't pretend I hadn't heard it.*

She wrote: *Restoring legal identity previously relinquished under duress.*

It was true enough. It would do.

* * *

The death record amendment was harder.

The clerk—a different one now, a young man with careful hands and a sympathy he was trying to keep professional—took her through it twice. The original record listed cause of death as *drowned, presumed, body unrecovered*, filed eleven days after the Meridian Star went down on the Sable Flats. Filed by C. Brase, Salvage & Concessions Ltd., acting as reporting party per shipping company authorization.

"You're disputing the cause of death," the young man said. "But you're also disputing the death itself."

"No." Hazel set the photograph on the counter between them—the one she'd taken on the first tide, the ribs of the Meridian Star rising black from the sand, and beyond them, half-buried, the outline of the crew quarters where she'd found the letters. "He died there. I don't dispute that anymore. What I dispute is everything else on this form." She tapped the original record. "My father wasn't lost at sea. He was aboard a ship that was deliberately run aground with contraband in her hold, by a man who then falsified the passenger list, falsified the cargo manifest, and filed this—" she slid the certified copy of the death record across the counter— "to make sure no one ever came looking. My father was going to testify. That's why he died. That's what the record should say, or as close to it as your forms will allow."

The young man looked at the ledger pages she'd recovered from the hold—the ones Brase's men had seized at Pelican Narrows, and then returned, grudgingly and incomplete, once the investigation started pulling threads. He looked at the affidavit from the maritime board. He looked at Hazel.

"There'll be a hearing," he said. "Probably several. The other party has lawyers."

"I know," Hazel said. "So do I, now. The territory assigned one when the permits were voided." She almost smiled. "Turns out when you broadcast a man's fraud to every radio in the district, people start taking your phone calls."

* * *

She signed her new old name seven times that afternoon. On the eighth signature her hand finally shook—not from fear, not from grief, but from something closer to vertigo, the feeling of a long descent ending, wheels touching ground, the sudden strange weight of being somewhere instead of between places.

Hazel Meridian Ashgrove. It fit worse than Joubert had, at first. Like boots resoled after years of going barefoot.

She walked out into the rain and stood under the awning and let it fall around her, and for the first time in ten years she did not check the street for faces she knew.

* * *

Amos Vane lasted nine more days.

They moved him from the coast hospital to a room in the boarding house above the chandlery, because he'd made it plain he intended to die looking at water and not at ceiling tiles, and Dr. Ferreira, who had fought this kind of fight before and knew which ones couldn't be won, signed the discharge herself. Hazel flew him the twelve miles up the inlet herself, slow and low, the Cessna's patched wingtip catching the light. He slept most of the way. When they came down over Pelican Narrows he opened his eyes and watched the water slide beneath them, brown-green and patient, and said, "That's the softest landing you've ever given me," and closed his eyes again.

Hazel came every day. She told herself it was duty—the man had saved her father's chart, had carried a dead man's secret through forty years of tidal flats and bad lungs—and some days it was. But mostly it wasn't duty. Mostly she sat in the wicker chair by his window while the tide went in and out below, and he talked, when he had the breath for it, and she listened.

He talked about the Meridian Star. Not the wreck—he'd already given her everything the chart held, every sounding and channel and sandbar—but the ship alive. How she rode heavy in the water that last run, listing a degree to port that nobody would log. How Elias Ashgrove had found the false bulkhead in the number two hold and come to him in the engine room, white-faced, holding a rivet he'd pried out like evidence. How they'd agreed, the two of them, in the noise and heat below decks, that it had to be reported, that Elias would carry the papers ashore at the next port, that Amos would stand witness.

"And then we hit the bar," Amos said, on the fifth day, in a voice scraped thin. "Or we were made to hit the bar. I've had forty years to decide which, and I still don't know, and it doesn't matter anymore. What matters is your father got the papers into the oilskin and got me into the boat, and the boat didn't make it, and I washed up on the flats with the chart against my chest and him gone, and I told myself I'd carry it until someone came who should have it." He turned his head on the pillow. His eyes were bright and terribly clear, the fever burned down to coals. "Took you long enough."

"I didn't know to come."

"No," he agreed. "But you came anyway. That's the blood in you. Your father was the same—walked into rooms he shouldn't have, because leaving things wrong ate at him worse than danger did." He coughed, long and hard, and Hazel held the cup of water to his lips and hated how little her hands could do. "You want to know the last thing he said? Before the boat?"

Hazel shook her head. She had imagined it a thousand ways across ten years and six tides. Drowning. Cold. Alone.

"He said, 'Tell my girl the stars are the same from the water.'" Amos smiled, a small cracked thing. "He used to say you'd lie on the deck of whatever tub he was working and name constellations until he fell asleep. He figured wherever you ended up, you'd both be under the same ones." His hand found hers on the blanket, dry and light as driftwood. "He was right, girl. They are."

Hazel bowed her head over their hands and let the tears come, quietly, the way water finds its level, and Amos Vane watched the tide turn below the window and did not look away from her, because he understood that being witnessed was the last thing he had left to give.

* * *

He died on a morning of high clear weather, the kind pilots call severe clear, when you can see forever and the air is glass.

Ruthie Okafor found him. She'd been bringing him coffee he wasn't supposed to have, and she came down the stairs of the boarding house with the untouched cup in her hand and her face composed into the particular stillness mechanics wear, and she said only, "He went in his sleep. Window was open. You can hear the water from the bed." Then she set the coffee down on the chandlery counter and put her arms around Hazel, grease and diesel and iron, and held on while Hazel's knees argued with the floor.

Teo stood in the doorway, awkward, seventeen and furious at death the way only the young can be, until Ruthie beckoned him in too, and then it was the three of them in the smell of coffee and brine, holding each other up.

They buried him on the point above the Narrows, facing the flats. The minister was a retired fisherman who'd known Amos forty years and kept the service short, which everyone agreed Amos would have wanted. Hazel spoke. She hadn't planned to, but when the minister looked at her she heard herself say:

"He carried something heavy for a long time so that someone else wouldn't have to carry it alone. He kept his promise." Her voice held. "That's all any of us get to do, and he did it."

Then she stepped back, and Teo put a flat stone on the cairn, and Ruthie poured a measure of good rye into the grass, and the tide came in below them, faithful as ever, covering the flats where the Meridian Star slept under protected custody, her secrets finally told, her crew finally named.

* * *

The hearing on the death record amendment was scheduled for spring. Hazel's lawyer—a sharp, tired woman named Okonkwo who'd taken the case partly for justice and partly, she admitted, because the story was already all over the district radio—said it would be ugly. Brase's lawyers would contest everything. The man himself was under investigation for the permit fraud, the falsified manifests, the intimidation of witnesses; he'd retained counsel and gone quiet, but quiet, Okonkwo warned, was not the same as finished.

"He'll fight the amendment hardest," she said, in the office above the hardware store that served as her practice. "Cause of death, circumstances, the works. A corrected record makes him a killer on paper, not just a thief. Expect him to spend real money on it."

"Let him," Hazel said. "I spent ten years learning how to wait. He spent ten years learning how to spend. We'll see whose education holds up."

Okonkwo laughed despite herself, then sobered. "There's one more thing. The name restoration is straightforward—it'll process in weeks. But once it does, Hazel Ashgrove becomes a matter of public record. Property rolls, pilot registries, all of it. Anyone will be able to find you." She folded her hands. "I want to be sure you understand what you're asking for. You built a life on not being findable. This ends that. Permanently."

Hazel looked out the window. Below, the strip stretched along the tideline, and her Cessna sat in Ruthie's hangar with its new tailwheel and its scarred wingtip, and Teo was washing the windows of the office they'd leased together, whistling, careless, belonging. The charter outfit didn't even have a sign yet. They'd argued about the name for a week.

"Yes," Hazel said. "That's exactly what I'm asking for."

* * *

The certificate came in November, in a government envelope with a window cut in it, and Hazel opened it standing in the doorway of the charter office while Teo pretended not to watch and Ruthie absolutely did not pretend at all.

**Name:** Hazel Meridian Ashgrove.
**Status:** Restored pursuant to order of the Territorial Registrar.

It was just paper. It changed nothing about who she was, nothing about what she could fly or fix or survive. And it changed everything, the way a keel changes a hull—same boat, same water, but suddenly there was a shape underneath her that kept her upright in weather.

"Well?" Ruthie said.

Hazel pinned it to the wall behind the desk, crooked, next to the tide table. Then she took the marker from the cup on the counter, walked outside into the cold bright afternoon, and wrote on the fresh-painted board above the door, in letters big enough to read from the runway:

**ASHGROVE CHARTER — PELICAN NARROWS**
*Meds, mail, freight & rumors*

Teo whooped. Ruthie said, "About damn time," in the voice she used when she'd been right for weeks and wanted credit deferred.

And Hazel stood back in the wind off the water, under the same stars her father had promised her, and read her own name on her own door, and found that it fit after all.

The phone inside began to ring. First booking of the new outfit. Hazel went in to answer it.
