## 22. Wreck and Passage

The hangar door at Pelican Narrows was the first thing anyone saw coming off the water, and Hazel had painted it herself—two coats of weather-blue over the old gray, then the letters in white, tall as a man: **ASHGROVE CHARTER**. Not Joubert. Not the name she'd worn like a coat with the collar turned up for ten years. Her real one, her father's one, stenciled where the tide could read it twice a day.

Ruthie had stood on the ramp with her arms crossed while Hazel worked, shaking her head in that way she had that meant approval delivered as complaint.

"You understand what you're doing," Ruthie had said. "You're putting your name on a door. Doors get knocked on."

"That's the idea."

"Hm." Ruthie had spat thoughtfully into the channel, which was how she said *good for you* without the embarrassment of saying it.

That had been in March, when the ice was still going out of the narrows in plates the size of barn roofs, grinding and singing against each other all night. Now it was late summer, and the door had been knocked on plenty—a trapper needing insulin run to his brother's camp, a geologist with too much gear and not enough sense, a fisheries officer who wanted a look at the flats from the air and paid cash and asked no questions Hazel didn't want to answer. Medicine and mail and rumors, same as always. The only difference was that now, when people said *who flies you out here?*, they said *Ashgrove girl*, and Hazel didn't flinch when they said it.

She was learning, slowly, what a name weighed when you set it down instead of carrying it.

* * *

Teo had grown an inch since spring, which he announced every morning by standing against the pencil marks Ruthie made on the hangar's doorframe, scowling at the new line as if it were someone else's doing. He was fifteen now by his own best guess—he'd never known exactly—and he'd stopped flinching at loud voices somewhere around June, though Hazel noticed he still sat facing doors, still ate like the plate might be taken away mid-meal. Some things took longer than a season. She knew that better than anyone.

He ran the radio most mornings, because he was good at it—patient, careful, with a memory for voices and schedules that bordered on eerie. He kept the log book in handwriting so neat it looked printed. And he had a way with the old-timers on the coast, who'd decided within a month that the quiet boy at Ashgrove Charter was worth telling things to: where the fish were running, whose boat had engine trouble, which government man was coming up the coast and why.

This morning he was on the radio when Hazel came in with two coffees, and she caught the tail of a conversation—the Pelican Narrows store asking if she could bring out flour and diesel filters on the afternoon run, and something about a wedding in Klawock, and a rumor about a bear that had learned to open coolers.

"Flour and filters," Teo repeated, writing it down. "And Mrs. Aldous wants to know if you can take her sister's cat to the vet in town because her sister won't fly with it anymore after last time."

"What happened last time?"

"She didn't say. She laughed, though. For a long time."

Hazel looked at the manifest pinned by the radio: flour, filters, insulin, a crate of seed potatoes, veterinary cat. It was, she thought, the smallest and largest cargo list of her career. Ten years ago she'd have told you a life was built out of big things—one great score, one clean break, one new name written fresh on empty paper. She'd built hers that way, out of absence, and it had held about as well as a raft made of fog.

It turned out a life was built out of flour runs. Out of a boy marking his height on a doorframe. Out of a mechanic who owed you nothing and gave you everything anyway, and a hangar with your father's name on it, and mornings that arrived whether or not you deserved them.

"I'll take the cat," she said. "Somebody has to find out what happened last time."

* * *

Ruthie had the Cessna's number-two cylinder apart on a bench when Hazel wandered back to the hangar after lunch, hands black to the wrist, forearms greased to the elbow, humming something tuneless and old.

"How's she looking?" Hazel asked.

"Like an airplane somebody actually maintains." Ruthie didn't look up. "Imagine that. A pilot who brings the plane in *before* something falls off it." She set the cylinder down and finally glanced over, and her eyes went past Hazel to the window, to the light on the water, and something in her face shifted. "Tide's low at four-forty."

Hazel felt it land in her chest before her head caught up. "I know."

"You've been watching the tide tables all week. You think I don't see the calendar?" Ruthie wiped her hands on a rag that was more rag than cloth now. "You want to take him out there."

"He's never seen it. Not really. He lived off those flats for a year and he never once saw the wreck from above. He knows the channels better than the charts do, but—" Hazel stopped, started again. "He gave up everything to help me get there. He hid in the fog with me while Brase's divers were in the water. He never got to just *look* at it."

"And you?"

Hazel was quiet a moment. Outside, a raven walked along the ridge of the hangar like a small dark landlord inspecting the property.

"I've flown over it eleven times since the permits closed," she said. "Every time I'm out that way. I tell myself it's to check the channel markers, or it's on the way to Klawock, or whatever. But I go look at it." She turned her coffee cup in her hands. "It doesn't hurt anymore. That's the strange part. The first few times it did—like pressing on a bruise to see if it was still there. Now it's just... a place. Where my father was. Where everything started ending and started beginning, both at once."

Ruthie studied her for a long moment, the way she studied an engine—listening for the sound underneath the sound.

"Take the good plane," she said. "Not the trainer. Take the one with the new cylinders." And then, because sentiment physically pained her: "And check the fuel yourself. Don't trust my line boys. I have no line boys. Trust nobody."

* * *

They went up at three-thirty, into an afternoon so clear it seemed the whole coast had been washed and hung out to dry. The Cessna climbed away from Pelican Narrows with the water flashing below—channels the color of hammered pewter, islands dark green and dripping light, the snow still holding on in the high cirques even in August.

Teo rode in the right seat, and Hazel let him fly the first leg, his hands light on the yoke, corrections small and sure. He'd logged forty hours since spring. Ruthie said he'd make a better pilot than Hazel inside five years, and said it loudly enough for Hazel to hear, which was its own kind of compliment.

"Left rudder a touch," Hazel said. "We're drifting toward the ridge."

He corrected. Then, after a while: "Is it true what Ruthie says? That you used to land on roads at night with no lights?"

"On logging roads, sometimes. Once on a beach that turned out to be softer than it looked." She smiled at the memory—at the version of herself who'd done it, alone, unnamed, running from nothing toward nothing. "Don't try it."

"No promises."

The flats opened ahead of them the way they always did—all at once, a shock of space after the confining channels. Miles of tideland laid bare, braided silver and brown and gold, the channels writhing through them like veins under skin. Somewhere under all that shining mud lay the ribs of the Meridian Star, and under the ribs, sealed in the hold the government divers had catalogued and crated and carried away, the registered cargo and the unregistered secrets, the ledger that had ended Corwin Brase's empire and the letters that had ended Hazel's silence.

"There," Teo said quietly. He'd spotted it before she pointed—the dark lattice of the hull showing at this low tide, exposed along its starboard side where the sand scoured away each spring and buried it again each winter. From the air it looked small. It always looked small. It was hard to believe something so broken could have held so much.

Hazel brought them down to eight hundred feet and throttled back, and they circled, slow, the way you circle a grave or a church.

"It's smaller than I thought," Teo said.

"Everything is, from up here. That's the trick of flying. It makes the world honest."

He was quiet for a long time, forehead nearly touching the glass. When he spoke again his voice had changed—lower, older.

"I used to walk out there at night. When I was hiding. I'd walk the flats because nobody follows you onto the flats—they're too scared of the tide." He traced the outline of the wreck against the window with one finger. "I found it my second week. I didn't know what it was. I thought it was just an old ship. I slept inside it once, in a storm. There was a part of the hull that made a roof." He paused. "I stole a brass fitting off it. Before I knew it mattered. I still have it. Is that—"

"Keep it," Hazel said. "It's yours. You earned it the hard way—you slept there when it was just a cold old ship and nobody was watching." She banked gently, bringing the wreck around into the center of his window again. "Everything else went to the investigators. The ledger, the manifest, the letters. Protected custody, they called it. But that fitting was yours before any of us knew there was anything to protect."

Below them the tide had begun its long turn. She could see it already in the lower channels—the water creeping back up the flats, silent and total, reclaiming a foot of ground a minute. In an hour the ribs would be awash. By evening the whole thing would be sea again, and the Meridian Star would keep its counsel until the next low water, and the next, patient as it had been for years.

Her father had been aboard when it went down. That was the sentence she'd spent a decade not knowing, and half a year learning to carry. He hadn't died the way the falsified record said. He'd died trying to testify, trying to do the right thing in a crooked place, and the sea had hidden him and Brase had hidden him further, and for ten years Hazel had hidden herself so thoroughly that between the three of them there was almost nothing left of Elias Ashgrove at all.

Almost. There'd been letters in a flooded hold, addressed to a daughter, unsent. There'd been a dying prospector with a chart and a debt. There'd been enough.

"Can we stay a minute?" Teo asked. "Just one more circle."

"We can stay as long as you want. Fuel's cheap and the tide isn't going anywhere we need to be."

But that wasn't quite true, and she caught herself smiling at the untruth. The tide was going somewhere—it was always going somewhere—and so were they. Back to Pelican Narrows, where Ruthie would pretend she hadn't been listening to the radio the whole flight, and the cat would be waiting for its vet trip, and the flour would need loading before five, and the pencil marks on the doorframe would need measuring again tomorrow because the boy grew overnight, apparently, out of spite.

A life. Ordinary, scheduled, full of other people. She'd spent ten years building the opposite of this, and it had taken a wreck, a storm, six tides, and a dead man's promise to teach her that the opposite of hiding wasn't freedom. It was loneliness with better scenery.

"One more circle," she said, and rolled into it, wings tipping toward the sun, and for a moment the wreck and the flats and the whole shining coast swung together across the windshield like the world turning on a hinge.

* * *

They came home down the narrows with the evening light behind them, gold going to copper going to rose, and Pelican Narrows appeared the way it always did—sudden, modest, inevitable. The dock. The store with its hand-painted sign. The hangar with the blue door and the white letters catching the last of the sun: ASHGROVE CHARTER.

Ruthie was on the ramp, arms crossed, pretending to inspect the tie-downs.

"Fuel's cheap, huh," she said as Hazel shut down. "Eleven circles over the flats. I counted."

"You counted?"

"I count everything. It's my whole personality." But she was smiling, barely, the way she did—sideways, quickly, like something escaping. She jerked her chin at Teo. "Store called. Cat situation has escalated. Apparently there are now *two* cats."

Teo groaned and headed for the office to sort it out, and Ruthie followed him, already arguing about crates and sedatives and whose fault all cats ultimately were, their voices fading into the hangar's warm oil-and-cedar smell.

Hazel stayed on the ramp a moment longer.

The tide was coming in below the dock, filling the narrows, lifting the boats on their moorings one by one—she could hear the lines creak, the water slap the pilings, the whole coast settling into night. Far south, invisible beyond the headlands, the flats were drowning themselves again, and the Meridian Star was going under its blanket of cold silver water, keeping what was left to keep.

She thought about her father's name on the hangar door, and her own name on the license in her pocket, and the boy's height penciled on the frame, and the mechanic's voice rising inside on the subject of cats, and she understood—for the first time completely, the way you understand a landing once your wheels are actually rolling—that she had not come home. She had built it. Out of flour runs and favor-trades and one impossible flight with a dying man, plank by plank, tide by tide, the way anything worth having gets built.

Somewhere out in the darkening channel a floatplane was coming up the coast, lights blinking, calling the strip on the radio in a voice Teo would answer, calm and sure, from the desk by the window.

Hazel picked up the chocks, turned, and walked into the light of her own hangar, and the door stood open, and she went in.
