## 1. The Navy Won't Discuss It

The wreck lay at thirty-eight meters, which was deep enough to make the light go the color of old bruises and shallow enough that Ansel Yarrow could work it without a mixed-gas rig and the headaches that came with one. He'd been down four times in two days, and each time he dropped through the thermocline he felt the temperature take him by the throat like a hand he half-recognized—the cold of the Rivergate shelf in late spring, the cold his body had learned to read as a second language.

*Hull's intact on the port side,* he thought, running a gloved palm along the plating. *Starboard's a mess.*

The ship had gone down twenty years ago with a cargo manifest nobody would show him and a name nobody would say out loud. The navy called it a restricted site. Restricted meant no salvage rights, no survey permits, no questions answered—except they'd hired him anyway, through a broker in Rivergate who paid in crisp bills and asked for discretion the way other men asked for whiskey. Ansel didn't ask why a navy-sealed wreck needed a civilian diver with a leaky forty-foot boat and a crew of four. He'd learned a long time ago that questions were like ballast: useful only if you knew when to drop them.

He checked his gauge. Fourteen minutes of bottom time left, and he wanted all of them.

The wreck had been picked over before—cleanly, professionally, by divers who knew what they were doing and had orders about what to leave. He could read their work the way another man might read handwriting. They'd stripped the bridge. They'd cut the safe out of the purser's office with a plasma torch and taken the whole thing, bolts and all. They'd even swept the crew quarters, though whoever did it had worked fast, moving bow to stern, and fast work always leaves seams.

Ansel liked seams.

He swam aft along the port corridor, fins kicking up silt that hung in the water like smoke in a closed room. His lamp carved a cone through it. The corridor ended at a bulkhead door, warped half-open by the settling of the hull, and beyond it lay what had been the second officer's cabin—a coffin-sized room with a bunk frame furred white with coral and a locker sprung open like a mouth.

The navy's sweep had gone through here. He could see where a gloved hand had dragged across the bunk frame, leaving a clean stripe in the growth. But the sweep had moved fast, and the locker's false bottom—a sheet of steel welded over the real floor of the compartment, a trick as old as smuggling itself—had been left alone, because from above, filled with twenty years of silt, it looked exactly like a floor.

Ansel had found it on his third dive, purely because he'd run out of things to look at and started looking at nothing. That was the whole secret of salvage, if anyone ever asked him, which nobody did: you didn't find things by searching for them. You found them by getting bored in the right place.

He wedged himself into the cabin, knees against the bunk frame, and worked the pry bar into the seam of the false bottom. The welds were small, amateur, done in a hurry by someone who'd never expected the navy to come knocking—or maybe by someone who'd expected exactly that. The bar bit. Metal groaned somewhere below the threshold of hearing, a sound he felt in his teeth more than his ears.

Come on, he thought. Come *on*.

On the third pull, the plate gave with a shudder, and a cloud of silt bloomed up around his faceplate, erasing the world. He waited, breathing slow, counting his heartbeats the way his mother had taught him to count when the storms came off the water—*one for the wind, two for the rain, three for the waiting.* Under the plate, in a cavity lined with tarred canvas, sat a strongbox.

It was smaller than a sea chest, bigger than a breadbox, iron-bound, and sealed—not locked, *sealed*, with a lead stamp pressed into wax over the hasp, the kind of seal a government uses when it wants you to know that opening a thing is a crime and not merely an inconvenience. The stamp showed an anchor and a crown. Silt had crusted over it, but the shape was unmistakable.

Ansel crouched there in the brown water and felt something turn over in his chest, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting on a seabed.

Navy seals meant navy property. Navy property on a wreck this old meant the navy had sealed it *before* the ship went down, or shortly after—and either way, it meant the thing inside mattered enough to stamp and not enough to retrieve honestly. His broker hadn't mentioned any strongbox. His broker hadn't mentioned much of anything, which was its own kind of message.

He got his hands under the box. It weighed maybe thirty pounds, dense and stubborn, and the tarred canvas came away in sheets that drifted upward like the ghosts of moths. He clipped the lifting line to the box's handle, checked the knot twice—knots were prayers, his first captain used to say, the only ones that get answered—and gave the line three sharp tugs.

Above him, forty meters of green water away, the winch on the *Kestrel's* deck took up the slack.

* * *

Surfacing was like being born badly. The pressure peeled off him in stages, his ears popping, the world reassembling itself from green murmur to gray shimmer to the hard gray light of an overcast morning and the smell of diesel and salt that clung to everything in Rivergate waters. Hands grabbed his harness—Dess, the big Tongan deckhand, hauling him over the gunwale with the easy strength of a man who lifted engines for fun.

"Anything?" Dess asked.

"Box," Ansel said, pulling off his mask. "Iron-bound. Sealed."

Priya was already crouched over it on the deck, her dark braid dripping seawater, turning the lead stamp toward the light with fingers quick as a card sharp's. She was twenty-four and the best line-handler Ansel had ever employed, and she had the particular stillness of someone who noticed everything and said perhaps a tenth of it. "Anchor and crown," she said. "That's a naval authority seal. Captain-level or higher."

"I can read," Ansel said.

"You can read, but you don't." She looked up at him. "We're under contract to a broker we've never met, working a wreck the charts mark with a red band, and we just pulled a sealed naval strongbox off it. I'm allowed one question per day, boss. This is mine: are we keeping it?"

Ansel looked at the box. Water ran off it in threads, pooling around the lead stamp. The anchor and crown stared up at the sky like an eye that refused to blink.

"We're holding it," he said. "Until I decide."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

Priya held his gaze a moment longer—she was one of maybe three people alive who could do that without him looking away first—then shrugged and went forward to coil the lift line. Dess hauled the box to the wheelhouse and set it down in the corner like it might bite, which, Ansel thought, it probably would, eventually. Everything on the water eventually bit.

Old Ferro was at the helm, chewing the unlit pipe he'd chewed since before Ansel was born, watching the horizon with eyes the color of skimmed milk. "Patrol boat," he said, without turning. "Two points off the starboard bow, coming down the channel. Gray hull, running lights off."

Ansel felt the day change shape.

* * *

The patrol boat was the *Corvus*, sixty feet of naval iron with a deck gun that hadn't been fired in anger in a decade but didn't need to be. It came alongside the *Kestrel* with the smooth arrogance of a vessel that had never once worried about right of way, and a lieutenant with a sunburned neck leaned over the rail and informed them, pleasantly, that they were in restricted waters and would please identify themselves and state their business.

"Ferro," said Ferro, who handled these conversations because Ansel's talent for them was roughly equivalent to his talent for needlepoint. "Salvage survey. Contracted and permitted."

"Permitted by whom?"

"The harbor office."

The lieutenant smiled the smile of a man about to enjoy himself. "The harbor office doesn't issue permits for this grid, Mr. Ferro. This is a naval exclusion zone. You'll need to come about and return to port, where someone will want to have a look at your paperwork."

Behind the lieutenant, at the rail of the *Corvus*, stood a man in a captain's uniform who wasn't smiling at all. He was older, silver at the temples, with a face built for boardrooms rather than bridges—long, composed, the kind of face that gave away nothing because it had spent decades learning how little it needed to. He was looking at the *Kestrel*, at the deck, at the wheelhouse window behind which the strongbox sat in the corner.

And then he was looking at Ansel.

Ansel stood just inside the wheelhouse, half-shadowed, and let the glass do its work. But the captain's gaze found him anyway, or seemed to—held for two seconds, three, with an attention that had nothing casual in it, the way a man looks at a photograph he's trying to memorize. Then the captain said something quietly to the lieutenant, and the lieutenant's manner changed, softened, became almost apologetic.

"Actually," the lieutenant called, "you're free to proceed. Survey activity logged and approved. Apologies for the interruption. Carry on."

Ferro blinked. "Carry on."

"Carry on, Mr. Ferro."

The *Corvus* sheared away, its wake slapping against the *Kestrel's* hull, and stood off—half a mile, then a quarter mile back, then settled into a lazy station-keeping drift two hundred yards east, close enough to watch, far enough to pretend otherwise.

"Well," said Ferro, around his pipe. "That was strange."

"That was very strange," Priya agreed, appearing at the wheelhouse door. "They boarded to inspect and then didn't inspect. And the captain—" she glanced at Ansel, "—he was looking at you, boss. Not the boat. You."

"He was looking at the wheelhouse."

"He was looking at your face."

Ansel said nothing. He was thinking about the way the captain's eyes had moved, patient and deliberate, cataloguing. He was thinking that men who paid brokers in crisp bills to hire anonymous divers did not usually send captains to check on the diving. Unless the diving had turned up something they already feared existed.

Or unless the captain recognized something else entirely. Something about the set of Ansel's jaw, maybe, or the black hair going gray early at the temples. Ansel pushed the thought away before it could grow teeth. There was no reason on earth for a navy captain to recognize his face.

There was no reason, and yet the look had lasted three seconds, and three seconds was a long time to look at a stranger.

"Log it," he said finally. "Survey complete, site released, we're heading home tonight. Ferro, make for Rivergate before the weather turns. Priya—" he hesitated, and hated himself for hesitating, because hesitation was a tell and tells were for amateurs. "Stow the box. Somewhere it doesn't rattle."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Where it doesn't rattle," she repeated, in a voice that suggested she'd stow it wherever he said and also that she intended to know everything about it regardless. Then she went aft.

* * *

They made port at dusk, the sky over Rivergate burning orange and violet behind the cranes of the commercial quay, gulls screaming their evening arguments over the fish market. The town smelled of tar and frying fish and the sweet rot of the tidal flats, and Ansel had lived here long enough that the smell had stopped registering years ago—which was, he supposed, what living anywhere meant.

The *Kestrel* took her berth at the end of the long pier, third slip from the breakwater, and the crew dispersed into the evening with pay envelopes thin enough that Dess muttered something in Tongan that Ansel chose not to translate. Broker's fees, engine repairs, the survey permit that had cost triple what it should have—all of it ate the margin, and the margin was the difference between a crew that stayed and a crew that scattered to better boats. Ansel stood on the pier after they'd gone and watched the lights come on along the waterfront, one by one, like a slow infection.

The strongbox sat in the wheelhouse. He'd told Priya to stow it; she'd stowed it under the chart table, wrapped in a tarpaulin, exactly where he'd have put it, and exactly where he'd be able to feel it every time he sat down to plot a course. It itched at him through the wood and the canvas, the way a hook itches at a palm—small, sharp, patient. A hook doesn't do anything. It just waits for the fish to decide.

He went aboard. He told himself he was checking the bilge, which was true, and checking the mooring lines, which was also true, and then he was standing over the chart table with the tarpaulin folded back and the lead stamp gleaming dully under the wheelhouse lamp, and neither of those truths explained why he was holding a pry bar.

The wax seal cracked like ice on a puddle.

Inside, packed in oiled paper gone stiff with age: documents. A ledger bound in oilcloth. A bundle of letters tied with wire. And on top, a single typed page, its ink faded to the color of dried blood, bearing a letterhead he recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror—

and a name, typed in the witness line, that stopped his breath in his chest like a fist.

*Elias Yarrow.*

His father's name. On a naval document. In a sealed box, on a scuttled ship, guarded by a captain who had looked at Ansel's face like he was reading a dead man's handwriting on it.

Outside, the tide turned, and the *Kestrel* shifted against her lines, groaning, and somewhere across the dark water the lights of the naval station burned steady and white, and Ansel Yarrow stood alone in his wheelhouse holding twenty years of silence in both hands.
