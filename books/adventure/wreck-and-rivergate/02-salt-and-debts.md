## 2. Salt and Debts

Rivergate smelled the way it always smelled in late summer: diesel and brine and the sweet rot of fruit going soft in the market awnings. Ansel Yarrow stood at the rail of the *Corvina* as she wallowed into her berth, and he let the town's smell wash over him the way a man lets a familiar hand rest on his shoulder—without comfort, but without complaint.

Three days at the restricted site. Three days of running the hook and the lift bags down through water the color of old bottle glass, while a navy patrol boat sat a half-mile off with its engines idling like a held breath. Three days of the crew asking nothing, which was its own kind of noise.

He had the strongbox in a canvas duffel under his bunk, wrapped in a dive skin that smelled of other people's sweat, and he had not told a soul aboard that it existed.

The harbormaster's boy caught the lines. Ansel tossed them down without looking, watched the fenders take the weight, watched the *Corvina* settle into the harbor's brown-green water with a groan he felt in his teeth. She was tired. He was tired. Neither of them would say so.

"Payroll," Priya said, appearing at his elbow with the ledger hugged to her chest like a shield. She was twenty-three and had hands like a rigger and the unblinking patience of a woman who counted things for a living. "You want to do the talking, or should I?"

"I'll do it."

"You always say that."

"And it's always true."

She didn't move. Down on the dock, the crew had gathered the way they always did at the end of a run—Dov with his cap in his hands, Ferro leaning against a bollard like he'd been poured there, young Sef already counting in his head, lips moving. They were good men and one good woman, and they had spent three days diving a wreck the navy didn't want discussed, and they deserved to be paid in full.

Ansel went down the ladder and told them the number.

It was short. Everyone knew it would be short before he said it; you could read a shortfall in a captain's walk before he opened his mouth. The navy's contract paid well—that was the trouble with it, the part that itched—but the navy also paid in navy time, through navy clerks, and half the sum would sit in some requisition queue until the monsoon was a memory.

"Half now," Ansel said. "Half when the transfer clears. I'll cover food and fuel out of my own end."

Ferro looked at his boots. Dov put his cap back on, which was worse. Sef, who had a mother in the hill district and a sister who needed a tooth pulled, said nothing at all, which was loudest of all.

"You said this one was clean money," Ferro said, to the bollard, to the dock, to nobody.

"It is clean money. It's just slow money."

"Slow money's how boats die, Ansel. Slow money's how crews scatter." Ferro pushed off the bollard and straightened, and for a moment his eyes came up and were old and flat as coins. "We've been running on your name all season. Your name's still good. But names wear thin, same as hulls."

Nobody argued with him. That was the thing about Ferro—he only spoke when the truth needed saying, and when he said it, the air went still around it like water around a dropped stone.

"I'll have the rest inside two weeks," Ansel said. "You have my word."

Ferro nodded once, the way a man nods at weather, and walked off toward the taverns. Dov followed. Sef lingered, looked at Priya, looked at Ansel, and left without a word, and his leaving was a small cold draft through the whole day.

* * *

The marine surveyor came at four, a dry stick of a man named Okonjo with a rubber mallet and a listening posture, and he spent two hours tapping his way along the *Corvina*'s hull in the slatted light of the careenage, and the longer he worked the quieter he got, and by the end he was quiet the way a doctor is quiet.

Ansel stood in the yard with the survey report in his hands and read it twice.

Plating thin at the turn of the bilge. Frame corrosion at frames nine through fourteen, consistent with electrolytic attack—stray current, probably, some neighbor's badly grounded welder bleeding the boat's bones away season by season. The garboard seam weeping at two points. The rudder stock scored. *Vessel is serviceable in coastal conditions in fair weather,* the report said, in Okonjo's careful clerk's hand, and then, underneath, the sentence that mattered: *I would not take her offshore in the monsoon, Mr. Yarrow. I would not take her offshore in anything but a flat calm, and I would not sleep on her even then.*

The monsoon was six weeks out. Maybe five.

Ansel folded the paper into quarters and put it in his jacket, and he stood there in the yard while the light went long and amber across the mud, and he did the arithmetic he always did and always hated: the *Corvina* needed three thousand in steel and welding and another eight hundred in caulking, and he had, after payroll, four hundred and sixty, and a navy contract that would clear when it cleared, and no other work lined up because the good work had gone to boats with better bottoms and captains who hadn't spent the spring diving restricted sites for a service that paid slow.

The boat had been his mother's before it was his, and her father's before that, and it had never once been a good boat, only a boat that kept floating, which in the Yarrow family had always counted for the same thing.

* * *

He waited until the yard was empty. He waited until the harbormaster's boy had gone home and the tavern noise had risen to its evening pitch across the water, the sound that meant every eye in Rivergate was aimed at a glass and not at a moored salvage vessel. He waited because waiting was free and because he had spent his whole life learning that the difference between a careful man and a careless one was mostly a matter of patience.

Then he went below, alone, and shut the cabin door, and took the duffel out from under his bunk.

The strongbox sat in the middle of the chart table under the lamp, and it was a small ugly thing—navy gray, thirty centimeters by twenty, heavier than its size, the paint scarred where the reef had worried at it for twenty years. The lock was a naval pattern, brass, and the keyhole was furred with calcium, and Ansel looked at it for a long time before he touched it.

He had found it wedged under the collapsed deck of the aft compartment, in a void the navy's own survey had marked *silted—no access*, and the navy's divers had missed it because the navy's divers had trusted the survey, and Ansel had not trusted the survey because Ansel did not trust anything he hadn't put his own hands on. That was all. That was the whole story he told himself: he was thorough, and thorough men find things.

But he had pocketed it. He had felt the patrol boat's wake rock the *Corvina* a half-mile off, and he had wrapped the box in a dive skin and stowed it under his bunk, and at no point in three days had he mentioned it to his crew or logged it in the salvage record, and a man who did that was not being thorough. A man who did that was keeping something.

He worked the lock with a drift pin and a small hammer, the way his father had taught him to open frozen padlocks on derelict hulls—*persuade, don't fight, the metal will tell you where it wants to go*—and the thought of his father arrived and passed through him like cold water through a wetsuit, and he kept working.

The lock gave with a click that sounded enormous in the cabin's silence.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: paper.

Three bundles. He unwrapped the first with hands that were steady because he made them be steady, and it was a cargo manifest, typed, the ink faded to the color of dried blood, the header stamped with a shipping line's name—*Meridian Coastal Freight*—and a vessel name that stopped Ansel's breath in his chest the way a fist stops it.

*MV Aldernsea.*

He knew the name. Every kid in Rivergate knew the name the way kids in other towns know the name of the mine that collapsed or the bridge that fell. The *Aldernsea* had gone down twenty-two years ago, eight miles off the cape, with eleven men aboard, in weather the papers later called *squally but manageable*. The navy had run the inquiry. The navy had called it a grounding error compounded by cargo shift. The navy had paid out the widows at a rate the widows called shameful and the navy called generous, and then the navy had restricted the site, and Rivergate had done what Rivergate always did with its grief, which was to fold it up small and put it in a drawer and stop opening the drawer.

His father had been one of the eleven. Elias Yarrow, deck officer, thirty-four years old, drowned twenty-two years ago, when Ansel was six, in a sea his mother never once spoke about and never once let him ask about, in a house where the *Aldernsea* was a word that made his mother go into the other room.

Ansel set the manifest down. His hands were not steady now, and he let them not be, because there was no one to see.

He read the manifest the way he read anything—top to bottom, twice, slowly. Cargo listed as machine parts and timber. Weights that didn't reconcile; the declared tonnage was light against the draft figures penciled in the margin in a hand that wasn't the typist's. And in the margin, next to the discrepancy, someone had written a single line in pencil, small and hard-pressed, the letters gouged deep enough to survive the water:

*E.Y. — you were right. It's not on the books. Get clear of this.*

*E.Y.* Elias Yarrow. *You were right.*

Ansel read the line four times. The lamp hummed. Somewhere across the water a tavern crowd laughed at something, a sound from another country.

The second bundle was correspondence—copies, carbons, the gray flimsy kind offices kept for their own protection. Letters between the *Aldernsea*'s master and Meridian's port office, and the letters were polite the way knives are polished, each one a little tighter than the last, the master asking about cargo that wasn't on his manifest and Meridian's office answering questions he hadn't asked. The last carbon was dated nine days before the sinking. Ansel read it standing up, because sitting felt suddenly like a thing he couldn't do.

The third bundle was one page. Navy stationery. A typed order, a classification stamp half-faded, a signature he didn't recognize and a counter-signature he did, because the counter-signature belonged to a man whose name was now on the harbor commission, whose name was on the maritime college's front gate, whose name Rivergate said the way it said the weather—*Reeve*. Captain Aldous Reeve, then a lieutenant, junior, twenty-two years ago, signing as witness to a document titled, in flat bureaucratic type:

*Order for Controlled Disposal of Vessel — MV Aldernsea — Ref: Inquiry File 4-118.*

Controlled disposal. The navy's phrase. Ansel had grown up on the water and he knew every phrase the sea had for killing men—foundered, swamped, broached, lost with all hands—and the navy had its own phrase, and its phrase meant the ship had been sunk on purpose, with paperwork.

His father's name appeared on that page too. *Witness to be silenced per attached*—no. Ansel made himself read it again, the whole line, because his eyes had skipped it once and he did not allow himself to skip things, not even now, not even this. It said: *Witness statements to be collected from: E. Yarrow (deck), T. Ilic (able seaman), prior to disposal.*

Collected. Prior to disposal.

Eleven men had drowned. The inquiry had called it an accident. And here was a page, signed and stamped, that used the word *disposal* about the ship his father had died on, nine days after a letter in the master's hand said *you were right, it's not on the books, get clear of this*—a letter written to his father, Elias Yarrow, who had been going to say something to somebody, and had drowned before he could say it.

Ansel became aware, distantly, that he was standing with both hands flat on the chart table, bearing his weight on them, the way a man does when the deck has started to move under him and the deck is not moving.

* * *

He didn't remember lighting the stove, but there was coffee, eventually, going cold at his elbow. The lamp burned. The harbor breathed against the hull, that slow slap-and-hold, slap-and-hold, and Ansel sat in the cabin with the papers spread out in front of him like a hand of cards he'd been dealt by a cheat.

Twenty-two years. His mother raising him alone in a rented room over the chandlery, working double shifts at the cannery, never crying where he could see, never saying the ship's name, never saying the navy's name, taking the payout with a face like a closed door. He had thought, all his life, that her silence was grief. He had built his whole self on top of that belief the way you build a house on a foundation you never inspect.

Grief kept secrets. But grief didn't sign receipts.

He thought about the payout. He thought about how a cannery wage didn't cover a boy's school shoes and winter oil both, and how the money had always been there anyway, exactly enough, never more, arriving in envelopes his mother took from the post office with her head down. He was thirty-two years old and he was only now, sitting in his own cabin with a dead man's papers, doing that arithmetic, and the answer was so simple and so ugly that he put the thought down the way you put down something with teeth.

The navy had called it an accident. The navy had paid his mother's silence. And his father had died knowing the ship was going down—*get clear of this*—and had drowned anyway, with ten other men, in water eight miles off a cape his son had spent his whole life diving.

Ansel looked at the strongbox, empty now, its gray paint holding the lamplight. A hook he hadn't set. That's what he'd told himself it was, that first night, when he'd wrapped it in the dive skin. But a hook only itches when there's something on the other end pulling.

There was a name in the margin of the manifest that wasn't his father's. *T. Ilic.* Able seaman. A witness the order said should be *collected*—and the order hadn't said what happened to witnesses who couldn't be collected, or who ran. Ansel had never heard the name. Twenty-two years in Rivergate, half of them on the water, and he had never heard of a Tomas Ilic, which meant the man was either dead, or gone, or very good at being neither.

Outside, the tide had turned; he could feel it in the way the *Corvina* leaned her lines, gentle as a sleeping animal shifting its weight. The tavern noise had died down to a last stubborn few voices. Somewhere down the pier a hull knocked against its fenders, patient and repetitive, like a question being asked over and over.

Ansel gathered the papers, wrapped them back in the oilcloth, and did not put them back in the strongbox. He put them in the chart drawer instead, under the coastal pilots, where a man would look only if he already knew what he was looking for. The strongbox he stowed in the bilge space under the engine room plates, where the navy would never think to search and no honest thief would ever crawl.

Then he sat back down at the table, and he did the thing he had been not-doing for three hours: he thought about who else knew.

The navy knew the site was restricted. The navy's divers had worked that wreck and missed the box, and someone above them had decided the wreck was closed, and the patrol boat had sat out there all three days like a dog tied to a grave. And tomorrow morning, or next week, or whenever the navy's clerks finished their slow clean money, someone was going to reconcile the salvage record against the survey, and someone was going to ask why Ansel Yarrow's crew had spent nine extra dives on a void marked *no access*—and whether the son of Elias Yarrow had found anything down there that the inquiry, twenty-two years ago, had needed to stay lost.

He heard boots on the dock. Measured, unhurried, the boots of a man who had somewhere to be and all the time in the world to be there. They came down the pier past the *Corvina*'s lines and stopped.

Ansel didn't move. Through the cabin's salt-hazed glass he watched a figure stand at the head of the gangway—a navy greatcoat, a cap, and beneath the brim, in the dark, the stillness of a man reading a boat the way Okonjo read a hull, tap by tap, listening for the hollow places.

The figure didn't call out. Didn't knock. It stood there a long moment, and then it turned, and the boots went back up the pier, unhurried, and faded into the town.

Ansel sat in the lamplight with his father's name in a drawer beside him and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own depth gauge, that the water above him had already begun to close.

Two days later, at the end of the pier where the harbor light didn't reach, a woman he hadn't seen in six years was waiting for him, and she was smiling like a knife that remembered his hand.
