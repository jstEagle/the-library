## 3. The Rival at the End of the Pier

Mara Voss had learned to read harbors the way other people read faces, and the harbor at Rivergate was lying to her.

It lay in the way good harbors always lied—generously, with light. The morning came off the water in long gold bars that made the moored boats look richer than they were, gilded the rust on the crane rails, turned the oil sheen on the shallows into something almost beautiful. The fish market was shouting its prices. Gulls argued over the gutting tables. Somewhere behind her, a welder's torch hissed and spat its small blue lightning, and the smell of hot metal mixed with brine and diesel until the air itself seemed industrious, honest, busy with legitimate work.

But Mara had spent eleven years learning what lay under a harbor's face, and she knew that Rivergate's charm was a coat of paint on old rot. She could see it if she looked past the light: the pilings gone soft at the waterline, furred with weed, holding up piers they had no business holding. The dredge silt creeping into the channel because nobody paid for dredging anymore. The salvage yard on the north spit, shuttered these six years, its gate chained, its sign bleached to a ghost of itself—"RIVERGATE MARINE SALVAGE, EST. 1946"—the letters fading like a name on a grave marker left untended.

Her father's name wasn't on that sign. It didn't need to be. Everyone in Rivergate knew whose yard it had been, and everyone knew why it had died, and everyone knew the family that had killed it. They said *Voss* the way you'd say a word you'd stepped in.

Mara stood at the end of the public pier with her hands in the pockets of a coat too good for this harbor, and watched the *Kestrel* come in.

She'd been watching for an hour. That was the thing about waiting for Ansel Yarrow—you did it in full view or not at all, because he smelled ambush the way sharks smelled blood, and any whiff of arrangement would send him back out to sea on principle. So she'd bought coffee from the cart by the chandlery, tipped the woman too much, laughed at a deckhand's joke she hadn't heard properly, and let the morning believe she was a woman with nowhere particular to be. Only her eyes worked. Her eyes tracked the channel mouth the whole time.

The *Kestrel* came around the breakwater at half tide, riding low and wrong. Mara saw it before she could stop herself from seeing it—the list to port, subtle as a lie, the way her wake sat unevenly against the swell. A boat telling anyone who knew how to listen that she was taking water somewhere she shouldn't be, or carrying weight badly distributed, or both. Ansel Yarrow running his hull the way he ran everything: one season past the point where caution would have said stop.

He'd changed since she'd last seen him at anything closer than across an auction room. Thinner through the face. The black of his beard shot through now at the jaw, though he couldn't have been more than thirty-eight. He stood at the wheel the way he always had—not leaning on it, not relaxed, but *braced*, as if the wheel might try something. His crew moved around him with the economy of people who'd learned his silences: a big Samoan-featured man on the bow line, a slight dark-haired girl coiling rope with the quick precision of someone who'd been taught by someone impatient, two others aft whose names Mara had gathered from harbor gossip and filed away like everything else.

The *Kestrel* took the wall berth three down from the fuel dock, and even the berthing was a performance of competence—lines thrown and caught, engine killed on the exact beat, no second attempt, no scramble. Mara felt the old thing move in her chest, the thing she hated most about him, which was not hatred at all but recognition. He was good. He had always been good. That was the wound of him: not that he'd beaten her, but that he'd beaten her fairly, and she'd never once been able to make herself believe otherwise.

She gave them ten minutes. Long enough for lines to be doubled and the first argument about fuel money to start—she could see it starting, the big man gesturing at the gauge, Yarrow shaking his head once. Then she walked down the pier with her coffee cooling in her hand and her heart doing something childish and arrhythmic against her ribs.

"Captain Yarrow."

He turned from the transom with the unhurried rotation of a man who'd already known she was there. Of course he had. He'd probably marked her the moment she'd come within two hundred meters of his boat; he had that kind of attention, total and quiet and slightly insulting.

"Mara Voss." He said her name flatly, testing its weight. "I heard you were working out of Corvane now."

"I heard you were working anywhere that would have you."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of a shared language.

"You've got a list," she said, nodding at the waterline. "Port side, just aft of midships. You're weeping below the boot stripe."

"We're all weeping below the boot stripe."

"The survey I had done on this harbor's fleet says yours is the worst."

"Then your surveyor's been drinking with my engineer." He came down the transom step onto the pier, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last month. Up close he smelled of diesel and cold salt and something older, something like iron. "You didn't walk all the way down this pier to discuss my hull."

"No," Mara said. "I walked down this pier to offer you a contract."

His stillness was immediate and complete. She'd forgotten how completely he could stop—the way a heron stops, everything going watchful at once. Around them the harbor went on with its noise, but she had the sense that for Yarrow the world had narrowed to the space between them and the water under the pier.

"A contract," he repeated.

"Salvage work. One job. Deep recovery, document priority, wreck site already surveyed." She reached into her coat and produced the envelope she'd been carrying for three days like a stone in her pocket, and held it out between them without letting go of it yet. "Terms inside. Payment on delivery. Half up front, because I know your cash position better than you'd like me to."

"And what is it I'm raising?"

"Documents. From a wreck."

"Which wreck?"

There it was. The question she'd rehearsed eleven answers to, standing on the ferry, walking the coast road, lying awake in the room above the chandlery listening to the halyards slap. Eleven answers and every one of them a different size of lie.

"The *Meridian Star*," she said.

The name went off between them like a depth charge. She watched it land. Watched his face do nothing—which was itself something, because men who could keep their faces still when their father's death ship was named in front of them were men who already knew things.

Because that was the part Mara had not expected, the part that had kept her up half the night since the harbor gossip reached her two days ago: *Yarrow had been diving restricted water.* Her contact at the harbormaster's office—a man who owed her more than money, who owed her the kind of debt that came from a favor done years ago that could never be repaid, only serviced—had told her that Ansel Yarrow's crew had pulled a navy-sealed wreck job off the *Meridian Star*'s grid square, and that they'd come back with cargo, and that a patrol boat had met them at the edge of the exclusion zone and turned them away with unusual courtesy. Unusual courtesy. In Mara's experience, the navy's courtesy was like its honesty: you noticed it most when it was hiding something.

And so here was the arithmetic she'd been doing since: either Ansel Yarrow had found something on that wreck, or he was about to. And either way, she needed to be standing next to him when it surfaced, because whatever came up off the *Meridian Star* would have her family's name attached to it—had her family's name attached to it, had for twenty years, growing like coral over everything the Vosses had tried to build since.

"You know the story," she said, when he didn't answer.

"Everyone knows the story."

"Everyone knows the version that survived." She finally let go of the envelope, and he took it the way he took everything—withheld, suspicious, weighing. "My grandfather's shipping line. Fraud allegations. A ship lost with all hands in bad weather twenty-two years ago, including a man named Elias Yarrow. Your father. The lawsuit your mother filed afterward broke my family. Took the yard, took the ships that were left, took my father's health and then his life. I was nineteen years old watching auctioneers carry our furniture out of our house while your mother's lawyers stood on the lawn."

"I know," Ansel said quietly. "I was twelve. I remember the lawyers too."

"Do you." Something in her wanted to strike at him and something in her wanted to warn him, and the two impulses had been fighting each other for eleven years and had never once declared a winner. "Then you know why both families swore off that water. My father forbade me ever to approach that wreck. I imagine your mother gave you similar instruction."

"She did."

"And yet here you are, offering to take my contract."

"I haven't offered anything."

"You're still holding the envelope."

He looked down at it as if surprised to find it in his hand. Then he did the thing she remembered most bitterly about him: he opened it right there, in front of her, refusing even the privacy of refusal. His eyes moved down the page. She watched him read the figure she'd written and saw the number hit him—not greed, she noted, not exactly; more like relief wearing greed's clothing. A man with a leaking hull and a short payroll looking at a number that could fix both.

"This is document recovery," he said. "Not salvage. There's a difference."

"There is."

"Document recovery means someone knows specific papers are on that wreck. Means someone told you what to look for and where." His eyes came up. They were gray-green, the color of the sea before weather, and they did not blink enough. "Who hired you, Mara?"

"A client."

"What kind of client?"

"The paying kind."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting on a public pier." She stepped closer, close enough to lower her voice beneath the harbor's noise, close enough to smell the cold coming off his jacket. "You want to stand there and pretend you don't understand why I'm here? Fine. Let's pretend together. You dove sealed water last week. You came back with cargo the navy's own divers missed. And now a stranger offers you a fortune to raise documents from the same hull, and you're going to tell me those two facts aren't connected? You're going to tell me you haven't already opened whatever you pulled off that wreck?"

The silence stretched. A gull screamed somewhere above the masts. On the *Kestrel*, the girl with the dark hair had stopped coiling rope and was watching them with open, unembarrassed attention.

"You talk like a woman who's read my mail," Ansel said at last.

"I talk like a woman who's read *everyone's* mail. It's how I stayed alive after your family finished with mine." She held his gaze. "Here's what I know, Captain. Here's what I'll give you for free, because I want you to understand the shape of the hole you're standing in. The *Meridian Star* did not sink in bad weather. The weather that night was dirty but survivable—I've read the meteorological record, I've read it more times than I've read my own letters. Ships like her survived worse every season. She went down because someone wanted her down, and the fraud my grandfather was accused of—the fraud your mother built a lawsuit on—was never proven because the proof went to the bottom with the witnesses. All of them. Every man who could have testified drowned in the same night, on the same ship, in the same coordinates. Does that sound like weather to you?"

Ansel's hand had gone very still on the envelope. Still in the way water goes still over something enormous moving underneath.

"Why would you want that truth?" he said. "It burned your family once. Digging it up burns the name again—or clears it. Which is it?"

And there it was, the question she'd hoped he wouldn't ask on the pier, in daylight, where she couldn't control her own face well enough. Because the answer was complicated, and complicated answers were how liars talked, and she was so tired of talking like a liar.

"My family's name has been underwater for twenty years," she said. "I'm a salvage diver, Captain. Raising drowned things is the only trade I know."

It wasn't the whole truth. It wasn't even most of the truth, and she watched him register that—watched him file it away in whatever ledger he kept behind those unblinking eyes, the ledger where he recorded debts and weights and lists in hulls. But it was true enough to stand on, and she'd learned that you built with what you had.

"The terms are in the envelope," she said, stepping back. "Half on signature. Full crew rates, hazard pay, and I dive with you. Those are non-negotiable."

"You dive with us."

"I don't trust anyone else to bring up what matters. I never have." She said it before she could soften it, and hated how it sounded, and meant every word of it anyway. "You have until the monsoon window closes to decide. After that, the site's undivable for four months, and whatever's down there stays down there through another winter."

She turned to go. Made it three steps.

"Voss."

She stopped. Didn't turn. The harbor noise rushed back in around her like water filling a footprint.

"If you're lying to me," Ansel Yarrow said, and his voice had gone quiet in the way she remembered from the auction floor, from the day the lawsuit turned, from every moment their histories had touched and sparked, "about who hired you, about what's really down there, about what you already know—it'll be the second time a Voss cost me something I can't get back."

Mara stood with her back to him and let that land, because he deserved to land it, and because turning around would have shown him her face, and her face had never once been able to lie to Ansel Yarrow.

"Then we're even," she said. "Because a Yarrow cost me everything I couldn't get back first."

She walked up the pier with her spine straight and her coffee gone cold in her hand, and behind her she could feel him watching, and she could feel the envelope in his grip like a hook set deep, and she thought: *now we find out which of us drowns first.*

What she did not let herself think about—what she folded away into the same locked compartment where she kept her father's funeral and the auction receipts and the letter she'd received three weeks ago from a journalist named Oren Castellanos—was how easily Ansel had believed her. How little he'd asked. As if some part of him had already opened the strongbox. As if some part of him had already read a name on a manifest that changed everything, and was now deciding whether to tell her.

The monsoon was nine days out. The barometer at the chandlery had begun its slow fall.

Mara Voss walked into the crowd at the head of the pier and started counting hours instead.

* * *

That night, from the window of her room above the chandlery, Mara watched the lights of the *Kestrel* burn late at her berth. Not the working lights of a crew stowing gear—those would have been white, harsh, moving. These were the low amber lights of men sitting around a table, deciding something.

She poured herself the harbor's terrible whiskey and drank it standing up, and let herself remember, just for a moment, the last time she and Ansel Yarrow had been on the same water. Eleven years ago. The *Corvane* shelf, a wreck both their crews had bid on, and the storm that came early, and the way he'd put his own boat between hers and the worst of the swell for forty minutes without ever saying a word about it afterward—and the lawsuit her family filed anyway, because her father needed someone to blame and the Yarrows' claim paperwork had a flaw in it, one flaw, and her father had driven a wedge into that flaw and split two families apart like cordwood.

She had signed the deposition her father put in front of her. She was nineteen. She had signed it and watched it become a weapon, and she had spent eleven years telling herself she'd had no choice.

The lights on the *Kestrel* went out—all but one, at the stern, where the captain's bunk would be. Burning alone. Burning late.

"He's reading it again," she said to the empty room. "Whatever he pulled up. He's reading it again tonight."

And tomorrow, she thought, setting down the glass, tomorrow he would come to her with questions she couldn't answer honestly and answers she couldn't afford to give, and the wary electricity between them would begin its long discharge toward the only place such current ever ends: the bottom of the sea, or the top of the truth, and usually both at once.

Outside, the first fat drops of an early rain began ticking against the window, nine days ahead of schedule, like a clock starting without permission.

Mara lay down on the narrow bed with her boots on, the way she'd slept her whole adult life—ready to move—and listened to the rain come off the sea, and waited to find out what kind of man Ansel Yarrow had become in the dark.
