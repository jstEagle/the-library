## 4. Sworn-Off Water

The rain came in off the harbor that evening like it had a grudge, and the *Kestrel's* cabin smelled of diesel, wet wool, and the particular staleness of men who'd been paid short and were pretending not to mind.

Ansel spread the contract on the chart table and weighted its corners with a coffee mug, a wrench, a coil of copper wire, and the strongbox key he still hadn't decided what to do with. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, letterhead embossed with a name he hadn't seen in six years: **VOSS MARITIME RECOVERY**. Beneath it, Mara Voss's signature sat like a knife laid flat on a table.

"Read it," she said. She stood at the far end of the table, arms crossed, hair still beaded from the walk down the pier. "All of it. Then vote."

The crew was assembled the way they always were for big decisions—squeezed into the cabin that comfortably held four and barely held five. Priya sat on the icebox with her boots hooked on the bench, a notebook open on her knee because she wrote everything down when she was nervous. Deke leaned in the doorway with his arms folded tight, jaw working. Old Sam had taken the chair by the stove, which meant he intended to stay until this was settled or until the stove went cold, whichever came last. Renn perched on the edge of the bunk, elbows on knees, twenty-two years old and trying to look like a man who'd voted on things before.

"The job," Ansel said, because someone had to say it out loud. "Is the *Corvane*."

Nobody moved. The name hung in the cabin like smoke.

Twenty years she'd lain out there past the outer shoals, in water deep enough that the navy had sealed the site and then mostly forgotten it, forty meters down on a shelf of gray mud. Twenty years since she'd gone down with all hands on a clear night in the dry season, no storm, no distress call, just a navy corvette that foundered so completely and so quietly that the inquiry had closed in eleven days and called it an act of God.

And both families—the Yarrows and the Vosses—had sworn off her. Ansel's mother had made him promise at fourteen, hand on his shoulder, her voice shaking: *We don't touch that wreck, Ansel. Not for money. Not for anything.* He'd assumed it was grief. Grief made people superstitious. He'd signed the oath along with everyone else when he took his first salvage license, the one clause every diver from Rivergate carried like a scar: *the Corvane is dead water.*

"You're asking us to break an oath," Deke said. "Not just ours. Every oath on this coast."

"I'm offering you triple rates," Mara said. "And hazard pay."

"Triple rates for what?" Priya said quietly. She didn't look up from her notebook. "You haven't told us what's down there worth triple rates."

Mara's eyes went to Ansel. Just for a second—a flicker, quick as a fish turning—and Ansel felt the hook set deeper. She knew something. She'd known something the moment she'd stepped onto his pier three days ago with that smile that wasn't a smile, saying *I have a job nobody else will take, and I thought of you first.*

Which should have been flattering. Wasn't.

"Why me?" he'd asked her then, and she'd said, "Because you're the best diver on this coast and you don't scare," and both of those things were true and neither of them was the reason.

He turned back to the crew now. "Before anyone votes, I want to say my piece." He kept his voice level. "I'm against this job."

Deke's eyebrows went up. Renn actually stopped breathing for a moment.

"I'm against it," Ansel said again, firmer, testing the words, finding they held. "The *Corvane* is a grave. Forty meters of cold black water and twenty men who never came home. We don't know why Voss Maritime wants documents raised from her hull, we don't know who's paying, and I've been in this business long enough to know that when a client won't tell you what you're recovering, what you're really recovering is trouble." He tapped the contract. "This says *personal effects, documentary materials, one item unspecified*. One item unspecified. That's not a salvage job. That's a message in a bottle somebody wants us to open for them."

"And yet," Sam said, from the stove, without opening his eyes.

Ansel looked at him. The old man's face was seamed like driftwood, and his eyes, when they opened, were the pale gray of weathered rope.

"And yet you're going to sign it anyway," Sam finished. "Aren't you."

The cabin went very quiet. Rain ticked against the deck above them. Somewhere down the pier a halyard slapped a mast, keeping time.

"That's not—" Renn started.

"It is," Sam said. "Look at him. Boy's been walking around for three days like a man with a splinter under his skin. You don't argue a man out of a splinter. You either cut it out or you let it fester." He nodded at the contract. "Vote'll go however it goes. But he's already down there. We're just deciding whether we go with him."

Ansel wanted to tell him he was wrong. He opened his mouth to do it, and found nothing there but the truth, so he closed his mouth again.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told any of them—not Priya, who noticed everything; not Deke, who'd dive behind him anywhere; not even himself, if he could have avoided it.

Three nights ago, alone in the cabin with the lamp turned low, he'd finally broken the seal on the navy strongbox he'd stolen off a dead ship. Inside: a cargo manifest, typed on navy stock, listing stores loaded at Rivergate the week before the *Corvane* sailed. Routine. Boring. And stapled behind it, a single sheet on heavier paper, stamped twice with the Admiralty crest, marked **RESTRICTED — DO NOT DUPLICATE**.

A scuttle order.

Not an accident report. Not an inquiry summary. An order, dated two days before the sinking, authorizing the deliberate destruction of H.M.S. *Corvane* and the classification of all surviving documentation. And in the column of names—witnesses to be silenced, informants to be neutralized, the bureaucratic language doing its obscene dance around what it actually meant—one line had stopped his heart the way cold water stops it, one hard fist-clench at a time:

**E. YARROW — CIV. CONTRACTOR — WITNESS — STATUS: RESOLVED.**

His father. Elias Yarrow, civilian contractor, drowned twenty years ago when the *Corvane* went down, listed in the inquiry as a passenger lost in an accident. Listed here as a witness whose status had been *resolved*.

Resolved. Like a debt. Like a loose end.

Ansel had sat with that page until the lamp burned low and the harbor sounds changed around him, and he had felt something in his chest crack along a seam he hadn't known was there. His father hadn't drowned. His father had been *drowned*—and the navy had known, had ordered it, had filed it away in a box their own divers missed, and Ansel had pulled that box out of the dark with his own hands and carried it home like a man carrying a live shell.

He hadn't told Mara about the scuttle order. He hadn't told anyone. But Mara Voss had come to him with a contract to raise documents from that same hull, and either she knew what was in the navy's sealed records—which made her dangerous—or she knew more than the navy did—which made her worse.

So now he stood in his own cabin and argued against the only thing in the world he wanted, because that was what a captain did, and because some stubborn honest part of him needed the crew to hear the case against it before he led them into it.

"Here's what happens if we take this," he said. "We break a coast-wide oath. Word gets out, half the harbors between here and Cape Marren close to us. The navy notices us working a sealed site, and the navy does not notice things gently. And whatever's down there that's worth triple rates, it's worth triple rates to someone who'd rather it stayed down. That's the shape of it. That's the honest math." He looked around the cabin, meeting each of their eyes. "I can't recommend this job. I want to be recorded saying that."

Priya clicked her pen. "Recorded," she said softly, and wrote it down.

"But," Deke said.

Ansel exhaled. "But I'm signing. If the crew votes yes, I'm signing, and I'll be first down the line." He heard how it sounded—how it *was*—a man arguing himself into a trap with both hands. "There's something down there I need. Something that isn't in the contract. And I'd rather owe you all the truth after than lie to you now."

Sam snorted. "After. Divers always say after."

"What's down there, Captain?" Priya asked. Her pen had stopped moving. She was watching him with those dark, patient eyes that missed nothing, and he understood that she already knew he was lying by omission, and was giving him room to do it, and would remember later exactly how much room she'd given.

"A name," Ansel said. It wasn't even a lie. "A name I need to see with my own eyes."

* * *

They voted with chips. Old custom, older than any of them—Deke had carved the cup from a piece of teak thirty years ago, and each man dropped a sliver of wood into it: dark wood for yes, pale for no. No talking during. No looking.

Ansel went last, as captain, though the custom said the captain's chip counted same as anyone's.

While the others passed the cup, he stood at the chart table with the contract in front of him and read it through one final time, the way you check your gear before a descent—straps, valves, buckles, everything that keeps you alive when the world turns hostile. Triple rates. Hazard clause. A confidentiality rider stiff enough to bend: *all materials recovered remain the property of Voss Maritime Recovery; the crew agrees to neither copy, retain, nor disclose the contents of any recovered document.*

Neither copy, retain, nor disclose.

His hand drifted, without his willing it, to his jacket pocket, where the folded scuttle order lay against his ribs like a second heartbeat. He was already in violation of that clause and hadn't signed yet. Whatever Mara was playing at, whatever game had brought her to his pier, she was about to hire a man who was robbing her blind—and if she ever found the strongbox's contents, the partnership would die at birth.

Unless she already knew. Unless that was the point. Unless the whole contract was a net drawn slow around him, and the triple rates were the glitter on the mesh.

He thought of his mother, then—unexpectedly, painfully. The way she'd gone quiet every time the *Corvane* came up on the radio news, year after year, anniversary pieces, memorial dives. The way she'd refused, once, flatly and with white knuckles, to attend the twentieth-anniversary service, and had never explained, and had died the following winter with her affairs in perfect order and one drawer in her bureau locked, the key never found.

*We don't touch that wreck.*

He'd thought it was grief. Standing here, with the scuttle order warm against his chest, he understood it might have been fear. Or guilt. Or knowledge. His mother had known something about the *Corvane*, and had spent twenty years and her son's inheritance making sure it stayed underwater.

And Mara Voss knew something too. And Captain whoever-signed-that-order knew. And somewhere out past the shoals, forty meters down in the cold, his father waited inside a hull full of answers, wearing the navy's word for himself: *resolved*.

Ansel picked up the pen.

The ink felt cold. He signed where the X was drawn—A. YARROW, master, salvage vessel *Kestrel*—and the letters came out steady, which surprised him. Below his signature, in the space marked *crew assent*, he left room for the chips to speak.

The cup came around to him last. He tipped it, and the chips spilled across the chart table among the coffee rings and pencil shavings, and he counted them the way you count anything that matters: twice.

Four dark. One pale.

Renn. Of course. The boy had gone pale-chip, and now he wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, ashamed of his caution or his honesty, and Ansel put a hand on his shoulder and squeezed and said nothing, because the vote was done and shame was a private country.

"Well," Sam said, rising from the stove-side chair with the groan of old rope under load. "Somebody put the kettle on. Looks like we're diving a grave."

"Not a grave," Ansel heard himself say. He gathered the chips back into their cup, all five, dark and pale together, the way a crew ought to be. "Graves are where they put you on purpose. The *Corvane*'s a crime scene. We're just the first ones who've come to read the scene properly."

Mara, waiting by the door with the rain streaming behind her, smiled her knife-flat smile and held out her hand for the signed contract.

"Pleasure doing business, Yarrow," she said. "We sail on the next window. Before the season turns."

He shook her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and she held it one beat longer than the deal required, and in that beat her eyes flicked—just once—to his jacket pocket. To the place over his heart where the folded paper lay.

She couldn't have known. Couldn't possibly.

But Ansel Yarrow walked her up the pier into the rain feeling the hook set true behind his sternum, dragging him out toward the shoals and the dark water and the resolved man waiting there, and he knew—with the certainty of a diver who has felt the current change before the surface shows it—that Mara Voss had not come to hire his boat.

She had come to see what he'd pull up.
