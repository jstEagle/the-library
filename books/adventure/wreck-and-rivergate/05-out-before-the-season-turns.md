## 5. Out Before the Season Turns

The barometer had been falling for two days, and the gulls had gone quiet over the fish market, and Priya Chandrasekaran knew what both of those things meant better than she knew her own mother's face: the season was coming early this year, and if the *Kestrel's Grace* was going to make the run to the wreck and back, it would be now, in the narrow window before the monsoon closed the coast like a fist.

She'd been loading boats since she was nine years old, first her uncle's crab skiff out of the eastern shallows, then anything that would take her, and she had learned to read a departure the way other people read weather. This one was wrong. She felt it in the way the crates came down the pier—not the usual lazy rhythm of a crew that trusted its captain to have told them everything, but a quicker, tighter cadence, men and women moving with their eyes down, the way you moved when you didn't want to be asked questions you couldn't answer.

Ansel had told them at dawn. *Navy contract, deep wreck, three days out, back before the season turns.* That was all. No charts spread on the table. No discussion of the site. When Dov had asked which wreck, Ansel had said *restricted water* and turned to the manifest, and the manifest had said nothing either, and the crew had looked at each other the way families look at each other when the father has decided something and the deciding is over.

Priya had said nothing too. That was the arrangement, the one nobody had ever spoken aloud. Ansel Yarrow ran the *Kestrel's Grace* the way a lighthouse keeper runs his light—alone, at all hours, answerable to nobody—and the crew stayed because the pay was honest and the work was real and because there was a kind of shelter in a boat that never asked you where you'd come from. Priya had come from a fishing family that lost everything to a debt collector with a smile, and she had learned early that some silences are load-bearing. You don't pull at them. You stack your weight on them and hope they hold.

But she watched. Watching was her job, though it had never once appeared on a manifest. She watched the deckhands' hands and the rigging's voice and the water's color over the reef, and for eleven years on Ansel's crew she had watched him too, and she had never seen him carry a secret badly before.

He was carrying one now.

* * *

She saw it at the second hour of loading, when he went below with a canvas bag that rode wrong on his shoulder. Too heavy for clothes. Too light for tools. He came up without it, and his shoulders came up with him, set high and square, the way a man carries himself when there's a stone in his boot and he's decided to walk on it rather than stop and shake it out.

Priya coiled line and thought about it.

The strongbox. She'd been on deck when they'd brought it up off the wreck, navy-sealed, green with twenty years of sea, and she'd seen the look that crossed Ansel's face when the patrol boat hailed them and turned them away—seen it only because she'd been looking for it, the way you see a fish's shadow in dark water, a flicker of shape where shape shouldn't be. Ansel had handed the box to the navy men with both hands and a straight face, and Priya had thought at the time: *good, it's done, it's theirs.*

Except the box had come aboard weighing more than a box that size should weigh, and Ansel had carried it below himself, and the navy divers had already gone through the wreck twice by their own admission, and Ansel Yarrow did not carry things below deck himself. Not ever. He had hands for the work and eyes for the crew and a back that bent to the sea, but he did not carry cargo. Cargo was for deckhands.

She turned it over while she worked, the way you turn a shell over in your hand. A copy, then. Or the original, and the navy got a shell. Either way, he'd taken something off that wreck that wasn't on the contract, and he'd hidden it below, and now he was loading his crew onto a leaking boat for a run into monsoon water over a site the navy had sealed, and he was doing it all without one true sentence in his mouth.

Dov came up the gangway with a crate of compressed air on his shoulder and set it down hard. "Three days," he said. "He said three days, out and back. You believe that?"

Priya kept her eyes on the coil. "I believe the captain said three days."

"That's not what I asked."

She looked at him then. Dov was forty, thick through the shoulders, a man who had run crab boats in the northern water until the northern water took his partner, and he had the particular honesty of men who had learned that lying to the sea gets you drowned. He was looking at her with his jaw set, waiting for her to say the thing he was already thinking.

"Dov." She said it low. "You want to ask him, ask him. You want to walk, the gangway's right there. But don't stand on my deck asking me to say it for you."

"Your deck," he said, and almost smiled. "It's his deck."

"It's the boat's deck," Priya said. "The boat doesn't lie. That's why I like it better than most people."

He grunted and shouldered the crate and carried it below, and Priya stood a moment with the wind coming off the harbor smelling of diesel and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-yet, and she asked herself the question she'd been avoiding since dawn: *why didn't you ask him?*

Because she knew the answer, and the answer was the same as it had been for eleven years. Because the crew was a family, and families run on trust the way boats run on ballast—you never see it, you only feel the list when it's gone. And because Ansel Yarrow had pulled her out of a harbor town that was eating her alive, had taught her to dive, had paid for her mother's doctor when the doctor's bill was bigger than the boat, and had never once, in eleven years, asked her for a lie.

He was asking for one now. Not in words. In the set of his shoulders, in the manifest that said nothing, in the canvas bag that rode wrong. He was asking her to load the boat and keep her eyes down and let him carry the stone in his boot all the way out to the wreck and back, and the terrible thing, the thing she turned over and over in her chest like a stone of her own, was that she was going to do it.

Not because she owed him. Debts between family don't work like that. Because she trusted him—had trusted him for eleven years—and trust, once you've spent it on a man, doesn't come back when you want a refund. It stays spent. And a man like Ansel, who had never asked for a lie, must have a reason that would hold weight, or he wouldn't be asking.

She just wished, standing there in the rising wind with the gulls gone silent and the barometer falling, that the reason didn't feel like a hook she could feel set in her own chest, line running back to his hand, waiting for whatever came next to pull it tight.

* * *

She caught him at it near dusk, and she was never sure afterward whether he'd meant her to.

The crew had gone ashore for the last hot meal before the run—Dov and the twins to the noodle stall, the engineer to say goodbye to a woman who never came to the pier—and the *Kestrel's Grace* had gone quiet in the way boats go quiet, hull ticking against the fenders, rigging humming a half-tone above the wind. Priya had stayed aboard to finish the fuel log, because someone had to, and because she wanted the boat to herself for an hour. It was the only place in the world where her thoughts arranged themselves properly.

She was in the galley when she heard the forepeak hatch.

It was a small sound. A hinge, eased rather than opened. But the forepeak was supposed to be empty—stripped for the run, ballast forward—and there was no reason on any honest boat for a man to open that hatch at dusk and ease it shut behind him.

She came up the companionway without hurry and stood in the main cabin, and through the open door of Ansel's bunk she watched him kneel on the deck with the canvas bag open and the strongbox half out of it, and he was doing something to the hull liner—loosening it, cutting a pocket into the space between the liner and the planking, the space where a boat keeps nothing but the sea's cold breath. He worked fast and quiet, the way he worked underwater, and he did not know she was there, or he had decided she wasn't, and either way she stood in the doorway and watched a man she loved like a brother hide a lie inside the bones of the boat that kept them all alive.

The box was navy-sealed. She could see the seal from the doorway, dull green in the lamplight, the stenciled numbers she'd read on deck the day they raised it. The same box. The same box the navy had taken from his hands with both his hands giving it.

*He kept it,* she thought, and the thought went through her like cold water through a seam. *He gave them a shell and kept the true thing, and now it's in our hull, and we're sailing it into a storm.*

Ansel finished the pocket, pressed the liner back over the box, and sat back on his heels. And then, without turning, without moving anything but his voice, he said:

"How long have you been standing there?"

Priya didn't flinch. "Long enough."

He turned then. He didn't stand up. He stayed kneeling on the deck with the lamp behind him, and his face was in shadow, but she could read it anyway—she'd been reading that face for eleven years—and what she read was not guilt and not shame but something wearier and more dangerous: a man past the point where guilt would have done him any good.

"Priya—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it's not what it looks like. Don't tell me it's contract material, salvage rights, any of it. I loaded this boat for eleven years, Ansel. I know what a lie weighs, and I know what that box weighs, and I know the difference."

The wind had come around with the dusk, and it was pushing rain-smell through the open hatch, and somewhere out in the harbor a ship's horn sounded, long and low, the sound the sea makes when it clears its throat before it speaks.

"You want to ask me something," Ansel said. "Ask it."

She had a hundred questions. She had *what's in the box* and *whose seal is that* and *why are we really going out there* and *what did the navy take and what did they leave,* and every one of them was a fair question, and every one of them would make him lie to her out loud, and she understood—with the clarity that comes to people who have spent their lives watching instead of speaking—that once he lied to her out loud, something would break that the boat could not carry and the crew could not repair.

So she asked the only question that mattered.

"Does it put my crew in danger?"

Ansel was quiet a long time. The lamp flickered. Rain started on the deck above, the first fat drops of the season, hitting the planks like slow applause.

"Yes," he said.

It was the first true sentence he'd spoken since dawn, and Priya felt it land in her chest like an anchor finding bottom. Not *no.* Not *I don't know.* Not any of the soft shapes a lie takes. He had looked at her and given her the truth, and the truth was *yes,* and she stood in the doorway of the family she'd chosen and understood that she was being asked, without being asked, to carry it.

"Why are we going, Ansel?"

He looked down at the hull liner, at the pocket he'd cut into his own boat's bones, and when he looked up his eyes were wet in the lamplight, or maybe that was the lamp, or maybe it was twenty years of something she didn't yet have a name for.

"Because I have to know," he said. "That's the whole of it. I have to know, and I can't know alone, and you're the only people I—" He stopped. Started again. "You're my crew."

*My crew.* As if the boat were his to spend and the crew were his to risk, and the worst of it, the part that made her throat tight, was that he wasn't wrong. That was what a captain was. That was the bargain none of them had ever signed and all of them had made.

Priya looked at him for a long moment. Then she stepped into the bunk, knelt down across from him, and took the edge of the hull liner in both hands, and pressed it flat over the pocket, and smoothed it the way her mother had smoothed sheets over sleeping bodies, sealing the lie into the boat's bones with her own hands.

"Then we do it clean," she said. "We do it fast, we do it smart, and we come back with the whole crew breathing. And when it's done, Ansel—when we're tied up and paid and standing on solid dock—you tell me everything. Not the captain's version. Everything. That's the price. That's what it costs to spend this crew's trust, and you're paying it, do you hear me?"

"I hear you."

"And Ansel." She stood, and her knees cracked, and she was suddenly, enormously tired. "The others. Dov will figure it out—he's already chewing on it. The twins don't ask, but they count, and they've counted the air crates and the fuel and the days, and the numbers don't sit right with them either. You've got maybe two days of their trust left. Spend it wisely."

She went up on deck then, into the first real rain of the season, and stood at the rail while it came down warm and heavy and washed the harbor gray, and she did not tell Dov what she'd seen, and she did not tell the twins, and she did not tell the engineer, and the weight of that silence settled onto her shoulders like wet canvas.

* * *

They cast off before first light, in the gray hour when the harbor lights still burned and the rain had thinned to a drifting mist that beaded on the rigging and ran down the stays in slow lines. The crew moved without talking, hands finding their work the way hands do when the body knows the job better than the mind wants to admit, and the *Kestrel's Grace* went out past the breakwater with her patched hull groaning at every swell, and Priya stood at the wheel while Ansel stood at the bow with his back to all of them, watching the open water where the wreck waited.

She watched him for a long time. The set of his shoulders. The canvas jacket dark with mist. The way he did not look back at the harbor, not once, the way a man doesn't look back at a door he's not sure he'll be allowed through again.

Behind her, Dov came up the companionway and stood at her shoulder, and for a while neither of them said anything. The engine turned over below them, steady and honest, the only thing on the boat that never lied.

"He's carrying something," Dov said at last. It wasn't a question.

"Yes," Priya said.

"You know what it is."

"I know it's heavy."

Dov looked at her, and she looked at the water, and the mist thickened around them until the harbor was gone and there was only the boat and the crew and the gray sea running out ahead toward the sealed wreck and the storm behind it.

"Two days," Dov said quietly. "That's what I'd give him. Two days before I start asking questions he has to answer."

Priya turned the wheel a point to starboard, lining them up on the wreck site, and the compass needle swung and settled, and somewhere below her feet, in the bones of the boat, a navy-sealed strongbox rode in a pocket cut into the hull, going out to sea with the family that trusted the man who'd hidden it there.

"Then let's hope he spends them well," she said.

Ahead, the weather was building along the horizon like a wall going up, dark and patient, and the *Kestrel's Grace* ran straight for it.
