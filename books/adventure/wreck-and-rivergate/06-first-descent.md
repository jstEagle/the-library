## 6. First Descent

The sea off the Corren Shoals was the color of old bottle glass, green-black and patient, and Ansel Yarrow had been staring at it for two hours before anyone spoke.

"Bottom's reading forty-two meters," Priya said, hunched over the sonar with her headphones half off one ear, the way she always wore them, as if the ocean might say something worth hearing and she didn't want to miss it. "Wreck's lying on its port side. Big shadow. Bigger than the charts say she should be."

"Charts lie," said Dov from the stern, coiling line with the mechanical grace of a man who'd done nothing else for thirty years. "Charts are drawn by people who've never been down."

Ansel said nothing. He was watching the water the way he watched everything—sideways, without commitment—and thinking about the strongbox locked in the compartment beneath his bunk, and about the name Elias Yarrow typed on a manifest that had spent twenty years in a navy-sealed hull, and about how a man could carry a question in his chest for two days until it stopped feeling like a question and started feeling like a stone.

The *Merrow* rode the swell with the tired complaint of a boat held together by habit and epoxy. Her hull survey had come back worse than grim; the surveyor had used the word *marginal* three times, and Ansel had paid him extra not to write it a fourth. They had two days of weather window before the season's first real system swung up from the southeast, and one job to run in it, and a crew that had voted—four to one, Ansel the one—to be here.

"You're sure about this," Priya said. Not a question. She'd stopped making her observations sound like questions weeks ago, when she'd realized he answered them the same either way.

"No," Ansel said. "But the wreck's not going to get shallower."

He checked his kit the way he always did, by feel and by order, hands moving through the ritual: regulator, octopus, gauge, knife, light, reel. The suit was a cold-water rig, thick neoprene that smelled of rubber and old salt. Dov would stay topside on the line. Priya would run the winch and watch the gauge. Ansel would go down alone, because the job was his and because going down alone was the only part of his life he'd never had to share.

The water took him at 0700, and it was cold enough to make a fist of his chest.

* * *

Forty meters is a country with its own laws.

The light down there came down in gray-green shafts that bent and broke against the hull as Ansel descended, and the wreck of the *Corren Star* rose out of the murk like a cathedral that had fallen over in its sleep. She was a coastal freighter, or had been—ninety meters of riveted steel on her beam ends in the sand, her starboard rail buried, her port side furred with twenty years of coral and anemone, the whole of her wearing the sea's slow forgiveness like a shroud.

Ansel hung in the water above her and let his heart slow, because a diver's first look at a wreck is the most dangerous moment of any dive. The temptation is to go in. The temptation is always to go in.

He checked his gauge. Thirty-eight meters. He checked his line. Taut, running up to the *Merrow*'s shadow far above, a silver thread through the green. He checked the wreck, and this time he made himself see it the way a salvager sees it and not the way a man with a stone in his chest sees it.

The hull breach was aft, on the port side near the waterline—a tear in the plating wide enough to drive a cart through, ragged at the edges, bent *outward*.

He'd read the navy's report. He'd read it three times in the two days since the strongbox, sitting in the *Merrow*'s cabin with a lamp and a bottle he didn't drink from. *Hull failure due to grounding, progressive flooding, loss of all hands.* Grounding. But groundings tear a hull inward, where the rock comes through. This steel had been pushed from the inside out.

Or from an explosion inside, which leaves the same signature and tells a different story.

Ansel hung there in the cold and the current and felt the story shift beneath him like sand under a hull. Then he swam down to the breach and went in.

* * *

Inside, the *Corren Star* was a throat.

His light carved a tunnel through the black. The corridor ran forward along what had been the deck, now a wall, and the silt lay over everything in a soft gray snow that had been settling, undisturbed, for two decades. Ansel moved with the fin discipline his father had taught him before his father had become a name on a manifest—short kicks, no silt, no hurry. *The water remembers every mistake you make,* Elias Yarrow had told a boy of nine in a swimming hole upriver, *and it keeps better books than any harbormaster.* Ansel had forgotten the man. He had never forgotten the sentence.

The cargo hold came first. He worked it methodically because the method was all he had: crates burst and empty, bollard eyes furred white, a cargo hook hanging from a chain like a hanged man. The manifest in his bunk had listed machine parts, timber, and general freight. The hold said timber, all right—enough of it—and the rest of the space was empty in a way that empty space shouldn't be, the deck rings bare where heavy cargo should have been chained. A ship that went down carrying half of what she'd signed for.

He marked it on his slate without letting himself read what he'd written.

Forward, the corridor narrowed toward the crew quarters, and the silt got deeper, and the light got smaller, and Ansel became aware—gradually, the way cold gets into a suit—that he was no longer diving a wreck. He was diving a grave, and he knew whose.

The first body he'd been prepared for. A wreck listed as *loss of all hands* keeps its dead, usually in the places they ran to. He'd found drowned men before, in three oceans, and he had a salvager's discipline about them: you note the position, you note the depth, you do not disturb, and you say a word in whatever language you have left. He'd expected the crew quarters to hold two or three of them, men who'd come aft looking for a way out that the sea had already closed.

He found the first one in the passageway, curled against a bulkhead door, and gave him his word, and moved on.

The second one was in the chief's cabin, and he had not drowned.

Ansel's light found the shape first—a man seated against the outboard bulkhead, as if he'd sat down to rest, as if he'd been waiting. Then the details assembled themselves out of the murk with terrible patience: the uniform, merchant marine, rotted to lace. The boots. And the hole.

It was in the man's chest, just left of center, and it had been made from the inside out, and around it the bone of the rib had been pushed *outward*, the way steel bends outward when the explosion comes from within.

Ansel hung in the black water of the chief's cabin of the *Corren Star* and felt forty meters of ocean press down on him and understood, with the flat clarity that comes sometimes to men underwater, that the navy's report was not merely wrong. It was a document. Someone had written it the way you write a hull survey when the buyer has already decided the answer.

The man had been shot. On a ship that was about to be scuttled. And then the ship had been scuttled over him, and the sea had been asked to keep the secret, and the sea—for twenty years—had.

Ansel's breathing was too fast. He knew it by the gauge, the needle trembling down through the numbers, and he made himself stop, made himself hang in the cold and breathe slow the way the water demanded, because panic forty meters down in a wreck is just a slower way of joining the dead man in the chair. *The water remembers.* He breathed. He counted. He got his heart down from its gallop to a walk.

And then, because he could not make himself leave, he looked again at the dead man's hands.

One hand was closed. Rigor had come and gone and left the fingers set around something, and twenty years of water had done its work on the flesh but the grip had been tight enough, or the thing small enough, that a shape remained. Ansel knew he should not take it. He knew the discipline: you do not disturb. But the discipline had been written for salvagers, and he was no longer sure, hanging there in the dark with his father's ship around him like a rib cage, that he was a salvager on this dive. He might be a son. He pried the fingers open as gently as he could, the way you'd open a hand you loved, and the water took what the hand had kept—a key. Brass, small, the kind that fits a locker. Tarnished green, but whole.

The dead man had held onto a key while he died. Ansel put it in his suit pocket and did not let himself think about why.

* * *

The locker was in the next cabin down, bolted to the bulkhead the way lockers are, and it was not remarkable except that it was the only one in the row that had ever been opened and shut again. The others hung open, mouths full of silt. This one's door sat flush. And on its face, where the crew of a working ship marked their gear, someone had painted initials in white that the sea had not entirely eaten.

*E.Y.*

Ansel's light held on the letters for a long time. Long enough that his gauge told him what his body wouldn't: he was burning air like a man on fire, and the depth, and the cold, and the dark, and the dead man in the chair one compartment back, all of it was waiting for him to do something stupid.

He thought about the manifest. *E. YARROW — WITNESS.* He thought about the scuttle order folded beneath it, the navy stamp, the date. He thought about a boy of nine in a swimming hole being told that water keeps better books than any harbormaster, and about a mother who had never once, in all the years after, said the word *ship* in his hearing without turning her face toward a window.

He thought: *he was here. He stood in this corridor. He breathed this air when it was air.*

The key fit the locker. Of course it fit. The dead man in the chief's cabin had died holding the key to Elias Yarrow's locker, and Ansel did not have the imagination left to wonder at the shape of that, only the strength to turn it.

Inside: a seabag, rotted soft. A tin cup. A Bible gone to pulp. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth that twenty years of cold water had only just begun to find its way through, a bundle. Ansel drew it out with both hands, the way you lift a thing that might be alive. It was heavy for its size. Letters, by the shape of them. A sheaf of letters bound with string, and the oilcloth had kept the sea off most of it, and the topmost envelope, water-stained but legible, was addressed in a hand Ansel recognized the way you recognize a face in a dream—

He recognized it because he'd seen it every day of his childhood, on grocery lists and chalkboard sums and the flyleaf of the swimming-hole book, and then not at all for twenty years, and the hand came back to him whole: *Elias Yarrow, writing to his wife, from a port he never reached.*

His gauge was screaming at him now, or he was screaming at himself through the gauge. He was deep, alone, inside a wreck, holding his father's last letters, and the air in his tank was a number falling toward zero, and none of that mattered as much as it should have, which was how he knew he was in trouble.

He wrapped the bundle back in its oilcloth, zipped it into the mesh bag clipped at his chest, and turned for the breach.

That was when his light swept the bulkhead one last time and caught what he'd missed coming in: a chart, bolted beside the cabin door, the kind crews mark up in grease pencil. The *Corren Star*'s last plotted course, twenty years old, the pencil lines still sharp under the film of silt. And beside the final fix, in the same hand as the initials on the locker, in the same hand as the address on the envelope now pressed against Ansel's chest, three words that the sea had not been able to eat:

*REeve knows. Run.*

Ansel Yarrow read his father's last written words by the light of his own lamp, forty meters down in the dark that had kept them, and the cold water did what cold water does—it carried the grief up out of him in a silver string of bubbles, rising and rising toward a surface that had lied to him his whole life.

Then the needle on his gauge touched reserve, and the diver in him took over from the son, and he swam.

* * *

He broke the surface into gray morning light and the smell of diesel and Priya's hand on his arm, and he heard her say his name twice before he answered.

"Air," he managed. "I cut it close."

"You cut it stupid." But her voice shook, and her hand didn't leave his arm, and Dov was already hauling on the line, and the *Merrow* rocked around him like a thing alive. Ansel let them pull him aboard because his body was done pretending, and he lay on the deck with the mesh bag under his back and the oilcloth bundle hard against his spine, and he watched the clouds go by and thought: *the accident story died down there. Forty meters down. It's not coming up.*

Priya crouched over him, checking his eyes, counting his breathing, doing all the things a deckhand does when the diver comes up wrong. Then her gaze dropped to the mesh bag, and to the corner of oilcloth showing at the zipper, and to the brass key glinting in the pocket of his suit.

She looked at him for a long moment. Ansel looked back, and did not explain, because he had spent two days learning how to lie to the people on this boat and was discovering he was better at it than he'd wanted to be.

"Anything down there?" Priya asked quietly.

"Timber," Ansel said. "Empty hold. Old bones."

Priya nodded slowly, and stood, and turned back to the winch—and Ansel, lying on the deck of a leaking boat with his father's letters against his spine, understood that she knew he was lying, and had decided, for reasons he hadn't earned, to let him.

Then the radio crackled, and Dov's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat and careful in the way that made Ansel sit up.

"Skipper. Navy patrol boat, two points off the port bow. Running without lights. And she's coming straight for us."
