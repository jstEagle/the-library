## 7. The Captain's Wake

Mara Voss had learned to read water before she learned to read words, and the wake behind them was wrong.

She stood at the stern rail of the *Kestrel's Fortune* with her third cup of coffee going cold in her hand, watching the gray shape hold its distance two miles back. It had been there since dawn. Not closing. Not falling away. Just there, patient as a debt, riding the long swell with the particular steadiness of a vessel that had nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to be there.

"Coastal patrol," Priya had said that morning, squinting through the binoculars. "Hull number puts her out of Rivergate station."

Mara hadn't needed the binoculars. She knew that hull. She'd grown up on a shipping line, and navy hulls were the first alphabet she ever learned—the way they sat in the water, the way their wakes broke, the difference between a boat going somewhere and a boat waiting for you to go somewhere. This one was waiting.

The monsoon clouds were stacking up over the western horizon like unloaded cargo, dense and bruised-purple, and the air had gone thick enough to chew. Three days out from the wreck site, two days from Rivergate harbor if the weather held, which it would not. The barometer on the bulkhead had been falling since noon, slow and steady, like something sinking.

Ansel came up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and didn't look at the stern once. That was its own kind of information. A man who checked a shadow twice was worried; a man who refused to check it at all was pretending, and Ansel Yarrow had been pretending since the day Mara had walked down that pier with a contract in her hand.

"He's been holding position for six hours," she said, coming to stand beside him at the rail—not too close, never too close with Ansel, the distance between them calibrated like a dive table.

"I know."

"You haven't looked."

"Don't need to." He wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "If it were going to board us, it would have done it by now. Patrols don't shadow for six hours unless they're deciding something."

"Or unless someone told them what to look for," Mara said, "and they're waiting for us to show them."

That got him. A flicker, quick as a fish under a hull—there and gone, but she'd spent years learning to read him across auction rooms and salvage docks, back when they'd been rivals instead of whatever this careful, armed truce was. He looked at her then, really looked, and she watched him decide not to say what he was thinking.

"What's our position?" he asked instead.

"Fifty miles off the shelf edge. We'll raise the lightship buoy by midnight if the wind holds." She paused. "It won't hold."

He nodded and went below, and Mara stayed at the rail with her cold coffee and watched the gray shape ride its patient wake, and thought: *somebody called it in.* The question that mattered wasn't who. The question was how much they knew.

* * *

The hail came at dusk, when the light had gone the color of tarnished brass and the first spit of rain was ticking against the deck plates.

"*Motor vessel Kestrel's Fortune, this is Navy patrol vessel Corvane. Prepare to be boarded for routine customs inspection. Do not alter course. Acknowledge.*"

The voice was calm, clipped, almost gentle. Mara had heard a hundred boarding calls in her life, most of them barked by junior officers drunk on their own authority. This one was different. This one sounded like a man reading from notes he'd written himself.

Ansel killed the engines and the boat rolled in the swell, corkscrewing lazily, and the crew gathered in the wheelhouse without being called—Priya with her arms crossed, the Ferro brothers flanking the door like furniture that happened to have opinions. Nobody spoke. In the last light they watched the Corvane's launch come across the water, running lights doubled, four sailors and an officer in the bow.

Mara made herself useful. She put the kettle on.

It was an old trick of her mother's, from the days when Voss ships got boarded in foreign ports and the difference between a fine and a confiscation could be a cup of tea served at the right temperature by a woman who looked unbothered. Authority expected either fear or defiance. What it never expected was hospitality, because hospitality implied you had nothing to hide and nothing to lose, and men who had come expecting one or the other always hesitated a half-second too long trying to reclassify you. Half a second was a currency. Mara had built a career on smaller margins.

She heard boots on the deck ladder and turned with the tray just as the officer stepped into the wheelhouse's overhead light.

Captain Aldous Reeve was not what the voice had prepared her for. Boarding officers ran young and hungry; this man was neither. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the lean, weathered build of someone who'd started on decks and ended up in command, and he moved through the wheelhouse doorway the way a man moves through his own house. His uniform was immaculate without being new. His eyes did a full circuit of the space—radar, chart table, the crew, the sealed hatch to the forward hold—and came back to rest on Ansel Yarrow's face.

And stayed there.

It was only three seconds. Maybe four. Mara counted them the way she counted everything, and in those seconds she saw something pass over the captain's expression that had no business being there during a customs inspection. Not recognition exactly. Something older and more private than recognition. Grief, maybe. Or guilt. The look of a man seeing a face he'd hoped never to see again, wearing the features of a man he'd buried.

Then it was gone, folded away so smoothly that anyone who wasn't watching for it would have sworn it never happened, and Aldous Reeve smiled and said, "Captain Yarrow. I understand we've met before—or rather, I've read your name. Salvage license 4471, is it? You've been busy this season."

"Captain." Ansel's voice was level. His hands, hanging loose at his sides, were not quite loose. "Routine inspection, is it?"

"Routine." Reeve accepted the tea Mara offered—milk, no sugar, taken with a small courteous nod that acknowledged her presence and dismissed it in the same gesture—and walked the wheelhouse slowly, unhurried, trailing one finger along the chart table's edge. "You'll forgive the inconvenience. Storm season makes everyone nervous. We like to know what's moving on the water before the water decides for us."

"We're a salvage boat," Ansel said. "We move wrecks, not contraband."

"Mm." Reeve stopped at the chart and studied it with genuine professional interest, and Mara watched his finger hover—almost imperceptibly—over the coordinates of the site they'd worked three days ago. He didn't touch the spot. He didn't need to. The hovering was the message. "Salvage. Yes. There's been good money in it lately, I hear. Enough that boats are working water they used to leave alone." He turned. "You've been out past the shelf. Deep work."

"Survey job," Ansel said. "Client wants a hull assessment. Nothing raised yet."

Something shifted behind the captain's eyes—a small, precise disappointment, like a man turning a key and finding the lock already changed. "Nothing raised yet," he repeated softly. "Well. Best of luck with the survey." He finished his tea in two unhurried swallows and set the cup down with a faint ceramic click that somehow sounded like a period at the end of a sentence. "Weather's turning. I'd make for harbor, Captain. The Corvane will be running these waters tonight—we'd hate for anything to happen to you out here."

It was courtesy. Every word of it was courtesy, laid out like a formal dinner service, and every word of it was also a blade lying flat on the plate. *We'd hate for anything to happen to you* was not concern. It was a man telling you he knew where you slept.

Reeve paused at the wheelhouse door. He looked back once more at Ansel—that same fractional pause, that same unreadable weight—and said, quietly, almost kindly: "Your father worked these waters too, I believe. Before the accident."

Then he was gone, down the ladder, across the dark water, and the launch's running lights shrank back toward the gray shape waiting beyond the swell.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a full minute after the engines of the launch faded. The rain had thickened to a steady drumming on the deck above, and the barometer needle had given up pretending and dropped another notch.

Priya broke the silence first. "He knew your name before he boarded. And your license number."

"Public record," Ansel said.

"He knew the site." Mara moved to the chart and tapped the coordinates with one fingernail. "He hovered right here. He wanted you to see him do it. And then he asked whether anything had been raised *yet*—" she stressed the word deliberately, "—which means he already believes something has been. You don't ask a question you don't already think you know the answer to, Captain. You ask it to watch the answer land."

Ansel said nothing. He was staring at the door Reeve had left through, and Mara recognized the look on his face because she'd worn it herself once, years ago, in a courtroom, listening to a lawyer read out the lawsuit that would take everything her family had built. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"The manifest," she said quietly, and Priya's head snapped toward her, and Mara realized too late what she'd said and in front of whom.

"The what?" Priya asked.

"Nothing," Ansel said, at the same moment, and the two words collided in the middle of the wheelhouse and lay there, obvious as a oil slick.

The Ferro brothers exchanged a glance and withdrew aft with the practiced discretion of crew who understood that some conversations weren't for them. Priya did not withdraw. She stood by the helm with her arms still crossed, looking between the two of them with the flat, patient expression of someone adding figures, and finally said, "I'll be in the galley," in a tone that made clear she would be listening through the bulkhead, and left.

The rain filled the silence. Mara leaned both hands on the chart table and looked at Ansel across the spread of paper and instruments, at this man she had hated for a decade and hired for reasons she was only beginning to admit to herself.

"That captain boarded this boat to look at your face," she said. "Not your holds. Not your papers. Your face. He looked at you like a man looking at a ghost, Ansel, and then he said your father's name like he was laying a flower on a grave. That isn't customs work. Customs officers don't grieve."

"You don't know that he—"

"I grew up in shipping," she cut in. "I've been boarded eleven times in my life, and I can tell you the difference between an inspection and a hunt, and that was a hunt. He's not working smugglers' water. He's working *your* water. Someone gave him the coordinates of that wreck, and someone told him what might be coming off it, and when he couldn't find it in ten minutes of tea and courtesy, he told you the storm was coming and his boat would be out here tonight." She straightened. "He's not trying to catch you carrying it, Ansel. He's trying to make sure you never get it home."

Ansel's jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and rough, like something dragged over coral. "The scuttle order lists witnesses. My father was one of them. If Reeve's hunting the manifest, then twenty years ago—" He stopped. Started again. "My mother took money. After the accident. I never asked from whom."

The admission hung between them, and Mara felt something turn over in her chest—not pity, nothing so clean as pity, but the grim kinship of one liar recognizing another. Her family had taken money too. Different sums, different sins, same architecture: a name kept clean by keeping it dirty.

"Then we run for harbor," she said. "Tonight, before his patrol settles onto station. Through the outer passage, ahead of the front."

"In this?" Ansel gestured at the deckhead, where the rain was drumming like fingers. "The hull survey was grim, Mara. You read it. One hard sea through the outer passage and we're testing every patch on that plating at once."

"And staying here is testing Reeve's patience instead, and his patience has guns on it." She pulled the chart toward her, found the lightship buoy, traced the channel east with one finger. "He expects us to shelter or to creep home on the coastal route where he can stop us legally, politely, with tea. So we don't. We go now, we go fast, and we go where he isn't."

Ansel looked at the chart for a long time. Rain hammered the wheelhouse windows. Somewhere beyond the darkening water, the Corvane was settling into its patient wake, waiting for morning, waiting for them to do the predictable thing.

"Get Priya," he said at last. "Both of you. If we're running the outer passage, everyone votes."

Mara went to the door, and paused there with her hand on the frame, and looked back at him—at the grease still on his forearms, at the ghost of his father sitting behind his eyes.

"For what it's worth," she said, "when he said your father's name? He wasn't threatening you. He was warning me. He knows whose daughter is standing on this boat, Ansel. He knows what my family did, and he wanted me to know he knows." She let that settle. "Whatever he's covering up, it's big enough that he thinks my name is a lever. Which means it's big enough that he can't afford to let us reach harbor."

She pushed open the door into the rain, and behind her she heard Ansel say quietly, to himself or to the chart or to a drowned man twenty years gone:

"Then we'd better be faster than he is."
