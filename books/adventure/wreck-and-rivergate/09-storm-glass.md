## 9. Storm Glass

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and Priya Chandra felt it in her teeth before she read it in the glass.

She'd grown up in her grandmother's house above the chandlery in Rivergate, in a room where three generations of storm glasses hung by the window like quiet old men, and she had learned early that weather did not arrive so much as seep—into the mortar of walls, into the joints of doors, into the small hollow behind the eyes where dread kept its office. The glass on the *Kestrel's* bulkhead was a cheap navy-surplus thing, its liquid gone cloudy as bad broth, and when Priya wiped it clean that morning and held it to the grey light, the crystals inside had begun to feather downward like ferns dying in fast motion.

"Early," she said to the empty galley.

The monsoon wasn't due for another nine days. The almanac said so; the harbor master's bulletin said so; twenty years of Rivergate seasons said so. But the glass didn't care what anyone said, and neither, apparently, did the sky. Through the porthole above the chart table the clouds had organized themselves overnight from scattered cotton into something with intent—a low, bruised ceiling pressing down on the water, the color of wet slate, moving without wind in the way that meant the wind hadn't started yet but was thinking about it.

Priya set the glass down and went topside to look at the sea.

The *Kestrel* rode at anchor in the lee of Cutter Rock, forty miles out from Rivergate and thirty from the wreck they'd been diving—the hull that had no name on any chart, the one Ansel wouldn't talk about except in clipped fragments between dives. Three days they'd been on station. Three days of Ansel going down and coming up with his face shut like a hatch, and Mara Voss standing at the rail with her arms crossed, watching him surface the way a person watches a fuse burn toward something they can't decide whether to stamp out.

The swell had come up in the night. Not big—not yet—but long, the kind of swell that traveled ahead of weather the way rumors travel ahead of trouble, rolling in from the southeast under a sea that still looked almost calm. Almost. Priya stood at the bow rail and watched the water move beneath them and thought: it's coming early, and we're forty miles from home, and this boat is held together with prayer and epoxy.

She went below and woke Ansel.

He slept badly these days—she'd heard him turning in the forward bunk at all hours, the creak of the frame, the long silences that weren't sleep—and he came awake the way he always did now, instantly and completely, like a man surfacing from depth with his regulator already out. His eyes found hers before his body finished sitting up.

"Barometer," she said. "Down twelve points since midnight and falling faster. Swell's out of the southeast. Cloud deck's lowering."

He was already swinging his legs off the bunk. "How fast falling?"

"Faster than I've seen outside of a named system." She hesitated. "Ansel. It's not supposed to be here for over a week."

"I know." He pulled on his shirt. "Get the others up. We run for home now, while there's still light and the sea's only angry instead of furious."

"And if Reeve's boat is sitting between us and the harbor?"

Ansel paused with one boot half on. In the lamplight his face did something complicated—too quick to call fear, too slow to call nothing.

"Then we'll deal with that," he said, "when we see it."

---

They raised steam—or diesel, which amounted to the same thing on the *Kestrel*, a throaty complaint from an engine older than any of them—and weighed anchor by seven. By eight the first rain arrived, not as a squall line but as a fine, needling drizzle that seemed to come from everywhere at once, beading on the rails, darkening the deck, turning the world beyond fifty yards into a soft grey rumor. By nine the drizzle had opinions. By ten it was rain in earnest, slanting, cold, and the barometer had fallen another six points.

Priya took the wheel for the mid-morning watch because Ravi's hands were needed in the engine room, where the port engine had developed a knock that everyone pretended was normal. The wheel spoke to her through her palms—the *Kestrel* was honest that way, transmitting every shift of load and sea through the spokes like a nervous animal leaning into your hand—and what the wheel told her was that the boat was riding heavy and wrong. Listless to starboard. Sluggish coming off the crests.

At eleven she sent Devan forward to check the forepeak, and at quarter past he came aft at a jog, rain streaming off him, his face wearing the expression of a man about to deliver news he'd rehearsed three ways and hated all of them.

"We're taking water," he said. "Forward bilge. Slow, but it's steady. Patched seams—weeping. All of them, looks like. Wherever we put the patches during the survey work, the compound's letting go."

"How much?"

"Bilge pump's keeping up. Barely. But the pump wasn't built to run continuous, and the intake strainer's going to clog with debris sooner or later, and then—" He made a small gesture with one hand, opening fingers, a flower blooming, a hull flooding.

Priya looked at the compass, then at the rain-hammered water ahead, then back at Devan. "How far to Rivergate?"

"Thirty-one miles direct. Forty-six if we go around the outer shoals the long way."

"Then we pray the pump holds for thirty-one."

Devan didn't laugh. Nobody laughed anymore, Priya noticed. There had been a time, two seasons ago, when the *Kestrel*'s crew joked through everything—through engine failures, through the near-grounding at Sallow Point, through the week they lived on rice and tinned fish because the pay hadn't come. Joking was how a crew breathed. But somewhere between the sealed strongbox and the second dive on the nameless wreck, the jokes had stopped, and the boat had gotten quieter than the sea around it, and Priya had started counting things she used to take for granted: the number of hours the bilge pump ran, the number of times Ansel lied to their faces, the number of days since anyone had said anything true out loud.

She knew about the strongbox. She'd known since the night in port when she'd come aboard early and seen Ansel kneeling at his bunk with the box open, reading papers by lamplight, his face lit from below like something in a nightmare. She'd withdrawn quietly and never said a word, and the silence had sat in her chest ever since, heavy as ballast. A crew runs on trust the way an engine runs on fuel, her father used to say—you can run dirty for a while, but you'll feel it, and eventually you'll seize. Ansel was running them dirty. And the terrible thing, the thing she turned over at night in her hammock, was that she understood why. She'd seen the name on the manifest edge when he'd shuffled the papers. *Elias Yarrow.* Everyone on the coast knew that story, or thought they did—the diver lost in an accident twenty years back, the widow who never remarried, the boy who grew up on the pier and became the best salvage man in Rivergate. If the truth about Elias Yarrow was inside that box, then Ansel wasn't lying to protect himself.

He was lying because the truth was a drowning, and he was deciding who to pull under with it.

Priya spun the wheel two degrees to port, correcting a drift she'd felt rather than seen, and watched the rain march across the water toward them like an army that had all the time in the world.

---

At noon the wind arrived.

It came out of the southeast exactly where the swell had promised, building from nothing to twenty knots in under an hour, and the sea followed it up the way water follows a breach in a dam. The *Kestrel* began to work. Not dangerously, not yet—but working, pitching into short steep seas that the new wind was stacking on top of the old long swell, so that the rhythm of the ride kept changing underneath them like a heartbeat skipping. Every third wave hit the bow flat and threw spray the height of the mast. The patched seams wept a little harder each hour. Priya could tell without looking at the bilge, just from the way the boat sat, heavier, more reluctant, a swimmer taking on water by degrees.

Ravi surfaced from the engine room at one o'clock, wiping grease onto a rag that was past saving, and delivered his report in the flat tone of a man who has stopped being surprised.

"Port engine's knocking worse. Could be a bearing. Could be nothing. Can't tell without pulling it apart and I'm not pulling an engine apart in this." He braced against the doorframe as the boat rolled. "Also the bilge pump's cycling more often. Whatever Devan told you, add twenty percent."

"I'll add forty," Priya said. "I don't trust optimistic numbers today."

Ravi almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the boat had seen in days, and it lasted until he glanced through the wheelhouse windows at the water astern of them, and stopped.

"Priya."

"What."

"You should look at this."

She looked. Two miles back, half-veiled in rain and spray, a shape was following them. Grey hull, low superstructure, the unmistakable aggressive silhouette of a navy patrol boat—and not just any patrol boat. Priya had spent enough years on this coast to know the vessels of the Rivergate squadron the way farmers know their neighbors' dogs, and this one, cutting the grey sea with its white wake, riding with the particular confidence of a ship that has nowhere else to be, was Captain Aldous Reeve's command. The *Vigilant*. It had been shadowing them, off and on, since the second day at the wreck. Now it had closed the distance to two miles and settled there, matching their speed, patient as a shark pacing a wounded thing.

"He's been back there since the squall line," Ravi said. "I saw him on the last pass. He's not closing. He's just... staying."

"Why would he stay?" Devan asked from the companionway, though his voice suggested he already knew.

Nobody answered him. The answer was obvious and unsayable: because he doesn't have to catch us. Because the sea is doing his work. Because a leaking boat in a rising monsoon either turns back—which puts her in his reach—or pushes on, and drowns honestly, and becomes nobody's problem but her own crew's.

Mara Voss appeared in the wheelhouse doorway then, rain-wet, hair plastered flat, and Priya watched her study the patrol boat astern with an expression Priya couldn't fully read—something between recognition and reckoning, the face of a woman doing arithmetic with numbers she'd been carrying a long time.

"He's herding us," Mara said.

"Or escorting," Ansel said from behind her, and even Priya, who had spent weeks cataloguing the ways Ansel Yarrow could lie, heard the hollowness in it.

"There's a difference," Mara said, "and it's measured in whether we're alive at the end of it." She stepped into the wheelhouse, dripping, and planted herself at the chart table. "Direct route home takes us through the channel past Point Merrow. That's where he'll be waiting if he's waiting anywhere—it's the only deep-water approach to Rivergate from this heading, and a patrol boat can sit on it all day. Or—" she tapped the chart, "—we divert south around the outer shoals. Adds fifteen miles. Puts us in open water longer, in worsening weather, on a hull that's already drinking."

"And the direct route," Priya said slowly, "puts us in front of whatever he's decided to do with us."

"Yes."

The rain drummed on the wheelhouse roof. Somewhere below, the bilge pump cycled, paused, cycled again—counting, Priya thought, like a heart that had learned to worry. On the bulkhead the storm glass hung in its bracket, and the crystals inside had fallen completely now, a white sediment of storm, the glass full of winter in the middle of the warm season.

Ansel stood at the window with his back to all of them, watching the *Vigilant* hold its distance in the grey, and Priya saw his hands flex once at his sides—open, closed—as if testing the weight of something invisible. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet enough that the wind nearly took it.

"We go direct," he said. "Through the channel."

Mara turned. "You're certain."

"No," Ansel said. "But I'm done running the long way around things."

Priya held the wheel and said nothing, and kept her own counsel the way she had for weeks now, the secret sitting in her chest like a second bilge, filling slowly. But as she brought the *Kestrel* two degrees to starboard, onto the heading that would carry them straight down the throat of Point Merrow channel, she caught Ansel's eye in the reflection of the rain-streaked glass, and she made a decision of her own, small and private and irreversible.

Tonight, she thought. Tonight, whatever happens with the channel and the captain and the storm—I'm telling him I know about the box. Because a crew runs on trust, and the trust is nearly out, and someone aboard has to start telling the truth before the sea does it for all of them.

Ahead, through the driving rain, the headland of Point Merrow rose out of the sea like a closed fist—and anchored squarely in the mouth of the channel, exactly where Mara had said he'd be, sat the *Vigilant*, her searchlight sweeping the water even in daylight, sweeping, sweeping, like something looking for a specific face.
