## 10. Mara's Price

The storm had been sitting on the horizon all day like a debt collector who knew you were home.

Mara Voss felt it in the way the crew moved—quicker, quieter, no one meeting anyone's eyes for longer than a courtesy required. The *Corvid's Rest* rode at anchor in the lee of a nameless islet, forty miles of open water between them and Rivergate, and every soul aboard knew that Captain Aldous Reeve's patrol boat was out there somewhere beyond the rain curtain, patient as rust.

She found Ansel in the aft hold, where he'd gone to be alone with the locker, because of course he had. He sat on an upturned crate with the thing between his knees, its brass fittings green with twenty years of sea, and he wasn't opening it. He was just holding it, the way a man holds a door he isn't sure he wants to walk through.

"You should eat," she said.

"I'll eat."

"You said that six hours ago."

He looked up then, and she saw what the water had done to his face down there at forty meters—the salt-cracked lips, the eyes still red-rimmed from a grief that had nowhere to go because it was two decades old and the man it belonged to had been dead since Mara was learning to tie bowlines. She had watched him surface with that locker. She had watched him read the letters by lamplight while the rest of them pretended not to see his hands shake. And she had known, with the cold certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant, that this was the moment she'd have to tell him.

Not because he deserved it. Because the storm was coming, and Reeve was coming, and lies kept in a flooding hold will drown you faster than the water.

"Ansel." She lowered herself onto the crate opposite him. The hold smelled of bilge and diesel and old rope, and beneath it all the faint mineral breath of the sea coming through seams that were never meant to hold this long. "There's something you need to hear before the weather makes liars of us all."

His jaw tightened. "More than my mother taking blood money? More than my father being murdered by a captain who's currently circling us like a shark that learned table manners?"

"Yes," she said. "Because I'm about to make it worse."

* * *

She told it plainly, because she had learned long ago that plainness was the only mercy left to people like them.

"A journalist came to me eleven months ago. Name of Corin Hale. Writes for the *Coastal Ledger*, does investigative work nobody else has the stomach for. He'd spent three years pulling threads on the *Meridian Star* sinking—old shipping fraud, insurance claims, a paper trail that led straight into the navy's procurement office and stopped dead against a wall of sealed records." She watched Ansel's face as she laid it out, watched each word land like a stone dropped down a well. "He couldn't get near the wreck. It's restricted water. Navy divers went through it twice and reported nothing recoverable—which we both now know was a lie, because they were sent to destroy, not recover."

"And he hired you," Ansel said. His voice was very flat. "To raise evidence from a wreck my family swore off. From a hull your family helped sink."

"From a hull my family was blamed for sinking," she corrected, and hated how quickly the old wound spoke for her. "The Voss Shipping Company took the fall for the Meridian fraud. My grandfather lost everything—the fleet, the yards, the name. My father drank himself to death over it. I grew up on stories about how the Yarrows' lawsuit finished what the fraud started. That's the version I was raised on, Ansel. That's the version *you* were raised on too, just pointed the other direction."

"Don't you dare—" He stood, and the crate scraped, and the locker shifted on his knees and he caught it with both hands like it might bolt. "Don't you dare put my family's lawsuit next to what happened here. My father is *dead*. He's been dead since I was nine years old, and I found out this week that he didn't drown—he was *shot*—and you're standing there telling me you've known pieces of this for a year?"

"I've suspected pieces of it for a year," she said. "I confirmed pieces of it when I opened your strongbox in your cabin while you slept, which yes, I did, and yes, I'm telling you now because you deserve the whole ledger, not the parts I choose."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them, Priya's boots crossed the deck, quick and light, checking lashings. The hull ticked and groaned around them, the ship talking to itself in the language of stress.

"You went through my things," Ansel said finally.

"I went through the evidence," Mara said. "There's a difference, and if you don't believe me, ask yourself why I'm telling you this instead of letting Hale publish whatever I chose to give him."

"Which is?"

"Which was going to be everything except the parts with Voss fingerprints on them." She made herself say it cleanly, no varnish, because she could feel the varnish wanting to creep in—the smooth explanations, the reasonable framing, all the tools her mother had taught her for surviving rooms full of men deciding her family's worth. "The manifest names a Voss shell company as the fraud's instrument. The scuttle order was signed off through channels my grandfather built. Hale wanted the full story. I intended to sell him ninety percent of it and bury the ten percent that would drag my name through the mud a second time. Scrubbed clean. That was the plan from the first day I walked down your pier."

Ansel stared at her. And Mara watched something move across his face—not the rage she'd braced for, but something worse: recognition.

"Ninety percent," he repeated softly. "You were going to sell the truth at a discount."

"The truth doesn't come free for people like us. You know that better than anyone—you just found out how much your mother paid for her version of it."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same." She stood too, now, and the hold felt suddenly small, two people and a dead man's locker and twenty years of inherited lies pressing in from all sides. "Your mother took hush money from Reeve's people and raised you on a story that kept you safe and kept you blind. I was raised on a story that kept *me* safe and kept *me* blind. Neither of us chose our lies, Ansel. We just got good at living inside them. And when the chance came to trade one lie for the truth, we both reached for the version that protected what was ours."

"My father is in that locker."

"Yes."

"He wrote letters. To me. From every port, and I never got one of them."

"Yes."

"And you were going to decide which ones I deserved to read."

That landed. She let it land. She had spent her whole life deflecting blows, and something in her—something tired, something that had been tired since the lawsuit took the last Voss ship off the water—decided she was done deflecting.

"Yes," she said. "I was. Because I'm a coward in the ways that matter most, and I learned it from people who loved me, and I am so far past pretending otherwise that you could cut me open and find apology notes filed by year."

* * *

He didn't speak for a long time. The lamp swung gently overhead with the swell, throwing their shadows up the bulkhead in slow arcs, two shapes leaning toward and away from each other like a argument the ship itself was having.

Mara used the silence to look at him properly—at the Yarrow jaw, the Yarrow stubbornness set in a face that had aged harder than thirty-odd years should allow. She had hated this man once, or told herself she had, back when hatred was cheaper than understanding. Their families had spent a generation suing and countersuing over the corpse of the Meridian fraud, two houses burning each other's fields while the man who lit the fire wore a captain's uniform and collected his pension. And here they both were, in a leaking hull in monsoon waters, holding the same evidence, having arrived by different roads at the same terrible arithmetic: *what is the truth worth, and who pays?*

"Hale," Ansel said at last. "Where is he now?"

"Waiting. Rivergate, probably, or wherever I told him to wait. He thinks I'm delivering the full archive within the week." She paused. "He doesn't know about the locker. He doesn't know about Tomas Ilic. As far as Hale knows, I'm recovering documents, and the story runs with whatever framing I hand him."

"And the framing was going to be: Voss innocent, Yarrow witness, navy corrupt."

"The framing was going to be: Voss *absent*," she said. "Innocent is more than I planned to claim. Absent. Uninvolved. A family that lost everything to someone else's crime and asked no questions afterward because asking questions gets people killed—I know that now, don't I? Your father asked questions."

Ansel's hand closed on the locker's handle. Not opening it. Anchoring himself to it.

"Here's what I can't get past," he said, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that scared her more than shouting, because she'd heard that quiet before, from her own father, in the last year of his drinking, when the truth got too large for volume. "Every person in this story lied to protect themselves. Reeve lied to protect his career. My mother lied to protect me, maybe, or her money, I honestly don't know anymore. You lied to protect your name. And my father—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, jaw working, "my father told the truth, and they put a bullet in him and sank him with the proof. So tell me, Mara. Tell me why any of us should do it differently. Tell me why I shouldn't take Reeve's offer tomorrow morning, because he's the only one making sense. He's offering to pay me to lie, and everyone else in my life did it for free."

The words hung in the diesel-smelling dark, and Mara felt them land in her own chest like ballast dropped into shallow water.

"Because lying works," she said. "That's the honest answer. Lying works beautifully, right up until it doesn't, and then it takes everything. My family's lie cost us a century of reputation and my father's life, slowly, bottle by bottle. Your mother's lie cost you a father you thought you'd mourned correctly. Reeve's lie is costing him right now—that patrol boat isn't out there because he enjoys the weather. Lies are cheap until the interest comes due, and the interest always comes due, and it compounds, Ansel. God, how it compounds."

"So the truth is just the smarter investment."

"No." She shook her head. "The truth is the dumber investment. It pays late, it pays badly, and half the time the people who deserve the return are dead before it clears. I'm not selling you the truth because it's profitable. I'm telling you I was going to trim it for market because I'm my mother's daughter, and then I'm telling you I changed my mind, and I'm not even sure I can tell you when."

"When did you change it?"

She thought about it. Really thought, because he deserved the actual answer and not the polished one.

"On deck," she said. "When you surfaced with the locker and couldn't get your gloves off. Your hands were shaking so hard Priya had to help you, and you wouldn't look at any of us, and I thought—there's a man finding out his whole life was a receipt someone else signed. And I realized that if I handed Hale a scrubbed story, I'd be doing to the record exactly what Reeve did to your father. Editing. Deciding what history gets to keep." Her mouth twisted. "Twenty years ago someone edited my family out of being victims and into being villains. I know what editing costs. I just never thought I'd pick up the pen myself."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind had begun to find its voice—a low moan in the rigging, the first real announcement of the front that had been waiting offshore all day.

"You're still going to sell it to Hale," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going to sell it to Hale, yes. The *whole* archive, Voss fingerprints included, because a story with the dirt left in is a story that survives cross-examination, and Reeve has friends in every office from here to the capital. If we're going to burn him, we burn him with dry wood." She held his gaze. "But the story goes to the port authority first. Official channels, sworn statements, Tomas Ilic's testimony witnessed and logged—before Hale prints a word. Because Hale's story can be discredited. A file in the port authority's safe cannot."

"And your name goes in the file."

"My name goes in the file." She said it steadily, though saying it felt like paying out her last length of anchor line into black water. "Mara Voss, associated with the recovery of evidence pertaining to the Meridian Star fraud. Let them read it however they want. I spent fifteen years hiding from that name and it bought me nothing but smaller and smaller boats."

* * *

Above them, the hatch slid open and Priya's voice came down the ladder, tight with weather-report economy: "Barometer's falling fast. Skipper—we've got maybe four hours before this gets ugly, and the reef passage closes with it."

Ansel closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, something had settled in his face—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but a working truce, the kind sailors sign with weather they can't beat.

"We're not running yet," he said. "Reeve's boat is watching the direct channel. We run now, we run into his guns. We wait for the front to cover us and thread the outer reef at night."

"That's suicide in this hull," Mara said.

"That's why I'm telling Priya and not asking her." He lifted the locker, tucked it under his arm like a man carrying a sleeping child, and moved toward the ladder. At the bottom rung he stopped, without turning around.

"Mara. The ninety-percent plan. If I hadn't found the second body—if my father's initials hadn't been on that locker—would you have told me any of this?"

She considered lying. It would have been easy; it was practically her native language, inherited like the jawline and the temper. Instead she said, "No. Probably not. Probably I'd have sold you a cleaned story and called it partnership, and hated myself in installments for the rest of my life."

He nodded once, slowly, absorbing it the way a hull absorbs a grounding—with damage that wouldn't show until later.

"Well," he said, climbing. "At least we're honest about the lying now."

And then he was up through the hatch into the rising wind, and Mara stood alone in the hold with the swinging lamp and the sound of the sea getting hungry against the hull, and she understood that the confession had cost her exactly what she'd feared and bought her exactly what she hadn't dared hope: not his trust, but his continued presence. A truce. A shared deck.

It would have to be enough, because through the hull plating she could hear the barometer's promise arriving early—the deep, rhythmic concussion of swell against the outer reef, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Four hours. And somewhere out in that building dark, a patrol boat with a patient captain, and a channel home that was about to become the most dangerous water on the coast.
