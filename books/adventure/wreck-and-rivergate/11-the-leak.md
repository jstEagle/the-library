## 11. The Leak

The barometer had been falling all evening, and by midnight the *Kestrel's Wake* was riding a sea that had no interest in being ridden.

Ansel stood his watch in the wheelhouse with the rain hammering the glass so hard the world beyond it was a smear of black and white—the black of open water, the white of foam tearing off wave crests and flung sideways like thrown salt. The wind came out of the northeast in long, screaming gusts that made the rigging sing a note he'd never heard her sing before, high and thin, like wire about to part.

She was handling it. That was the thing he kept telling himself. She was an old boat with a patched hull and a survey he'd stopped reading halfway through because reading it felt like reading a diagnosis, but she was handling it, the way old boats did—stubbornly, gracelessly, with a kind of furious competence that no modern hull could match. She rolled through each swell and shook herself and came up for more.

Below him, in the engine room, Dev Okonkwo was keeping the watch, and every twenty minutes he climbed the ladder to report the same three words: *"Still dry, skipper."*

At 0140, the words changed.

"Skipper." Dev's voice came up the companionway before he did, and there was something in it that put ice down Ansel's spine—not panic, nothing so useful as panic, but a careful flatness, the voice of a man who has decided to be calm and is spending everything he has on the decision. "You need to come see this."

* * *

The engine room at night was a cathedral of noise and shadow—the diesel thudding its steady heartbeat, the shaft turning, the bilge pump cycling, everything lit in the amber glow of the work lamps they'd rigged after the main fixtures started shorting in the spray. Ansel dropped down the ladder and stood on the grate plates and listened.

He heard it before Dev pointed it out. Under the diesel, under the wind transmitted through the hull as a low groan, there was a new sound. A hiss. Thin and constant, like air escaping a tire, except it wasn't air.

"There." Dev aimed his lamp at the forward bulkhead, low, near where the frame met the plate. A seam of water was coming through the caulking in a fine silver thread, arcing maybe half a meter across the compartment before it broke apart into droplets. It looked almost delicate. It looked like nothing.

It looked like the first sentence of a very short story.

"That seam was weeping two days ago," Ansel said. "Weeping, not spraying."

"It's been getting worse since the last set of swells. I didn't want to say anything until I was sure."

"How sure are you?"

Dev turned the lamp along the length of the bulkhead, and the light found what the dark had been hiding: not one thread of water but four, spaced along the seam like stitches coming undone, and below them a shallow lake already spreading across the lowest plates, ankle-deep and rising.

The bilge pump kicked on. Ansel watched the water level and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since Rivergate, the arithmetic he'd hoped never to do in earnest. Pump capacity against ingress rate. Freeboard against weight of water. Distance home against time.

"We're taking ground," he said. "Slow, but we're taking it."

"The patch from the reef scrape is holding. It's the original seams around it that are letting go. She's working too hard in these seas—every swell flexes her, and she's twenty years past the age where flexing is free."

Ansel crouched and put his hand flat against the hull plating. Cold. He could feel the sea through the steel, feel the whole ocean leaning on the other side of six millimeters of metal, patient as debt. Somewhere aft, thunder rolled across the water like furniture being dragged across heaven.

"Get Priya," he said. "Wake everyone. And get me the soft wood plugs and the marlin spikes—we're going to have a caulking party."

* * *

By two in the morning all five of them were in the engine room, and the boat was teaching them humility.

They worked in shifts of two on the seam while the others bailed and monitored. The procedure was old as wooden ships and translated badly to steel: drive oakum into the leaking seams with spikes and mauls, pack it tight, smear it with sealant compound, hold a timber plug against the worst of it and wedge it in place. It was surgery performed on a moving patient, in the dark, in a room that tilted fifteen degrees one way and then twenty the other without warning, throwing tools across the deck plates like dice.

Priya worked beside him without a word for the first hour, which was how he knew she was scared. Priya talked when she was comfortable and went silent when she was afraid, and right now she was driving oakum into a seam with the concentration of a woman defusing something.

"Hold the light steady," she said.

"I am holding it steady."

"You're breathing it around like a lantern in a gale."

"I'm breathing at all, which is more than the sea planned for us tonight."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her, which was the point. Fear on a boat was contagious, and so was the refusal of it, and Ansel had learned years ago that a skipper's job during a crisis was half engineering and half theater.

Sef and Mara took the bail-and-monitor shift, hauling water out of the bilge in buckets when the pump couldn't keep pace, passing them hand over hand up to the cockpit to dump over the side—a human chain, ancient and absurd, five people fighting the ocean with oakum and buckets while forty miles of open water tried to come aboard.

And beyond the reef line, visible through the engine room's vent grating only as a suggestion, Captain Aldous Reeve's patrol boat sat on the far side of the shoal water where she couldn't follow, her running lights burning steady through the rain, her searchlight sweeping the sea at intervals like a lighthouse with opinions.

"She's still there," Mara said, on one of her trips past him with a bucket. "Third sweep in ten minutes. He's not looking for survivors, Ansel. He's watching to see if we sink."

"I know."

"He could come around through the northern channel if he wanted. Deep enough for his draft. He's choosing not to."

"I know that too."

"So what does that make this?" She jerked her chin at the leaking seam, at the buckets, at all of it. "Because from where I stand, the navy doesn't need to board us. They just need to wait. We're doing his work for him."

Ansel drove a spike into the seam and packed oakum behind it, and didn't answer, because the answer was true and ugly: yes. Every hour they spent fighting this leak was an hour Reeve didn't have to spend lifting a finger. The storm was his weapon now. All he had to do was keep station and let gravity and pressure and twenty years of corrosion do what warrants couldn't.

Which meant, Ansel thought, driving the next spike harder than he needed to, that saving the boat wasn't just survival anymore. It was defiance. Every plank they kept above the waterline was a fact Reeve couldn't erase.

"Then we don't sink," he said finally. "Not tonight. Not for him."

* * *

At 0330 the sea found their weak point, because the sea always finds your weak point; it has nothing else to do with its eternity.

A swell came under them unlike the others—longer, steeper, the kind of rogue that comes out of a storm's heart without invitation—and lifted the stern and dropped it into the trough with a slam that Ansel felt in his teeth. Somewhere below, steel gave a groan like a living thing in pain, and the hiss they'd been fighting became a rush.

He was down the ladder before the boat finished rolling.

The forward seam had opened. Not a thread now—a finger-width gap, flexing with each roll, and through it the sea came in pulses, gallons with every swell, in gray-green surges that hit the plates and fanned out across the deck. The water was knee-deep and climbing. The bilge pump ran continuously and lost ground anyway.

"Priya! Wedges! Now!"

They worked by feel as much as sight, the lamps swinging wild shadows as the boat rolled. Ansel jammed the biggest soft-wood wedge they had into the gap and hammered it home, and the sea squeezed around it anyway, thinner but not stopped. He hammered a second wedge beside it. A third. Oakum packed into every remaining void, sealant smeared over everything, and then—because the wedges alone wouldn't hold against the flex—he braced them with a timber shore cut to length and driven vertical between the deck plates and the hull stringer, turning the whole repair into compression structure. Let the sea push. The boat would push back.

For ninety seconds after the last blow of the maul, nobody moved. The engine room dripped and shuddered and roared around them, and the seam held.

"Level's dropping," Sef called from the bilge well, where he'd been reading the depth against a marked stick. "Pump's winning again. Barely."

"Barely counts," Ansel said. His arms were shaking. Everyone's were. "Barely is how most things survive."

Priya sat back on her heels against the bulkhead, soaked through, hair plastered to her face, and looked at him across the lamplit chaos. There was sawdust and diesel and seawater on all of them, and in the amber light they looked less like a crew than like survivors of something, which, Ansel supposed, was what they were becoming.

"You knew the hull was bad," she said. Not accusing. Just saying it, the way you say a fact you've been carrying a while. "You read the survey. You signed the contract anyway. You've known since Rivergate we might not make it home, and you didn't tell us."

The engine thudded. The rain drummed. Beyond the vents, Reeve's searchlight swept its patient arc.

"No," Ansel said. "I didn't."

"Why?"

He could have given her the easy answer—the money, the season, the window closing. She would have accepted it, the way crews accept the official version of things, and something between them would have quietly died, some small load-bearing trust, and the boat would have sailed on carrying the loss without anyone naming it.

He was done with official versions. His whole life had been raised on one.

"Because my father's name is in that strongbox," he said. "And because the man who put it there is sitting outside the reef waiting for us to drown, and I will not give him the satisfaction. That's why. It's a bad reason and a good reason wearing the same coat, and I'm sorry I made the choice for you instead of with you."

Priya held his eyes for a long moment. Rain hammered the hull. Water sloshed at their knees, slowly losing its argument with the pump.

"When we're home," she said, "you owe the crew the whole story. All of it. No edits."

"All of it. No edits."

"Then get back to work, skipper. Barely counts."

* * *

Dawn, when it came, was not a sunrise so much as a rumor—a bruised gray lightening in the east, filtered through rain that had settled from screaming to a hard, steady fall. The wind backed a few points and eased from murderous to merely hostile. The *Kestrel's Wake* rode on, wounded, wallowing lower than she should, but riding.

Ansel stood in the wheelhouse with a chart spread on the table and coffee he was holding mostly for the warmth, and did the arithmetic again with fresh numbers. Ingress rate, down. Pump capacity, degraded—they'd burned the spare impeller overnight keeping the primary alive. Fuel, sufficient. Sea state, improving on a curve that might or might not outrun their hull.

Direct route home: through the channel where Reeve waited.

Northern route: around the outer shoals, adding six hours they might not have, through water the charts marked with the cheerful indifference of men who had never been there—*unsurveyed, local knowledge advised.*

Local knowledge. Ansel stared at the chart and thought about the coastline north of the reef, the stretch where the fishing boats never went and the charts went vague, and about a name he hadn't thought of in days: Tomas Ilic. The recluse. The man Mara had mentioned once, half-dismissively—a fisherman with a hidden anchorage somewhere along that coast, a man who wanted nothing from anyone.

There was no reason to trust a stranger. There was even less reason to trust the sea. But the sea, at least, made no promises, and strangers sometimes did.

Behind him, boots on the companionway ladder. Priya emerged, wrung out, and stood beside him at the chart table.

"Well?" she said.

Ansel laid his finger on the coast north of the reef, on the blank stretch where the soundings ran out and the pencil marks of some long-dead surveyor trailed away into guesswork.

"We can't run the channel," he said. "Reeve owns it. And we can't take six hours of open water on this hull—she'll finish opening up somewhere with no help in reach. So we go here." His finger tapped the blankness. "North along the inside of the reef, and we find a cove I'm not sure exists, belonging to a man I've never met."

Priya studied the chart, then him. "And if the cove isn't there?"

Ansel looked out at the gray morning, at the rain, at the distant steady lights of the patrol boat still holding its station beyond the reef like a creditor at a funeral.

"Then we'll be having this conversation again," he said, "with worse numbers."

He rang for half ahead, and the *Kestrel's Wake* turned her bow north, into the unsurveyed water, leaking the whole way.
