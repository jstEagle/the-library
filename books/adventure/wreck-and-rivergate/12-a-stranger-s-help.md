## 12. A Stranger's Help

The storm found them before the cove did.

Tomas Ilic had watched it build all morning from the mouth of his cave, the way he watched everything—patiently, from a distance, with a mug of chicory going cold in his hands. The sky over the outer reef had gone the color of wet slate, and the swell was coming in long and wrong, out of season, out of rhythm. Forty years on this water had taught him that storms were like men: you could read their intentions in how they carried themselves. This one meant business.

He'd also been watching the boat.

It had appeared an hour past dawn, limping along the reef line like a wounded animal looking for tall grass. A salvage trawler, old-fashioned, hard-used, riding low at the stern as if she were already half full of apology. He knew her lines, or thought he did—a Rivergate workboat, the kind that used to crowd these waters before the navy bought up half the coast and posted signs no one could read from the sea. She was flying no colors. Her running lights were dead. And she was taking water; even at this distance he could see her sitting wrong, see the crew at the pumps working in shifts.

Tomas had not spoken to another living soul in eleven days. He had not spoken to anyone who wasn't selling him fuel or fish bait in four years. He told himself, as he told himself every time a boat came too close to his cove, that it was none of his affair. The world had made its arrangements with him long ago, and the arrangement was simple: he left it alone, and it returned the favor.

But then the wind shifted, and the boat turned toward the rocks—the only stretch of the reef where the coral rose close enough to the surface to open a hull like a tin of sardines—and Tomas set down his cold chicory, swore in three languages, and went to start the winch.

* * *

Ansel Yarrow felt the keel shudder and knew they had seconds, not minutes.

"Hard to port!" he shouted over the wind, one hand on the wheel, the other braced against the console as the *Maren Lee* wallowed through a trough deep enough to swallow her whole. Rain came sideways now, needling his face like thrown gravel. Ahead, through the gray curtain, he could make out the black teeth of the reef—and then, impossibly, a gap in them. A channel, narrow as a keyhole, marked by nothing but a single weathered pole with a scrap of red cloth tied to its top.

"There's a marker," Priya shouted from beside him, her hair plastered flat, both hands white-knuckled on the rail. "Somebody put a marker there."

"Somebody lives here." Ansel didn't know how he knew it, only that he did. No chart showed a passage here. No commercial fisherman would waste a marker on water nobody fished. But someone had stood on those rocks in better weather and tied that rag with care, the way you mark a path for people you expect to come someday—or people you're willing to help if they do.

The engine coughed. Both of them heard it.

"No," Priya said, to the engine, to the storm, to God or whoever ran the accounting of such things. "No, no, not now—"

"Hold her together," Ansel said, and threw the wheel over.

The channel was barely two boat-lengths wide. He took it at speed because slowing down meant losing steerage, and the *Maren Lee* threaded the needle with a scream of coral somewhere beneath her hull that Ansel would hear in his sleep for years. Then the rocks fell away on either side, the wind dropped as though a door had closed behind them, and there it was: a cove, small and round as a cupped hand, ringed by black volcanic stone and overhung with wind-bent pines. Calm water. A wooden jetty, silvered with age. And above the jetty, built into the cliff itself, a low stone house with smoke rising from its chimney.

A man stood on the jetty. Old, broad-shouldered, weathered down to leather and gristle, with a coil of rope already over his shoulder and a winch cable running down into the water beside him. He didn't wave. He just pointed—at the jetty, then at the waterline amidships, then held up three fingers.

Three feet of water in the bilge, maybe. Ansel didn't argue.

They came alongside rough, the way you do when your boat is dying faster than your pride, and hands caught the lines—old hands, strong ones, hauling them in against the surge while Priya and Devic scrambled ashore to take turns on the cleats. The moment the *Maren Lee*'s hull kissed the jetty, the old man was down the ladder and into the shallows without hesitation, dragging the winch cable with him, feeding it under the hull by feel while the waves tried to knock him into the rocks. Ansel watched him work and understood, with a strange lurch, that this stranger was saving his boat the way a surgeon saves a patient: quickly, expertly, without sentiment.

It took two hours to get her stable enough to pump out properly. By the time they'd rigged the old man's spare pump to the bilge and gotten the flooding down to a manageable trickle, the storm had settled into a steady roar outside the cove, and the five of them—the crew, plus the stranger—sat in the stone house drinking chicory that was, miraculously, hot.

He hadn't given them his name yet. He hadn't asked theirs. He'd simply cooked—fish and rice and something pickled in a jar that made Devic's eyes water—and pointed at chairs until everyone sat. Now he sat across the fire from Ansel, studying him with pale gray eyes that seemed to have been bleached by decades of glare off the water.

"You're Yarrow," the old man said.

Ansel went still. Beside him, Priya's head came up sharply.

"How—" she started.

"Your father's face," the old man said simply. "You have your father's face. I'd know it anywhere. I saw it every day for six years." He set down his mug. His hands, Ansel noticed, were perfectly steady. "Elias Yarrow. Second mate on the *Corvane Star*. My shipmate. My friend." A pause, and something moved behind the gray eyes like a shadow crossing water. "And the man whose death I've spent forty years trying to forget."

The fire popped. Outside, the storm hammered the cliff like something demanding entry.

"I'm Tomas Ilic," the old man said. "And I think you've come about the scuttling."

* * *

Later—much later, after the plates were cleared and Devic and the others had bedded down in the loft, exhausted beyond curiosity—Tomas led Ansel out onto the ledge above the cove, where the wind couldn't reach and the rain fell soft as mist. Below them, the *Maren Lee* rode her mooring lines, listing slightly, alive.

"I saw them do it," Tomas said, without preamble. He was looking out at the dark water, not at Ansel. "The night the *Corvane Star* went down. I was supposed to be aboard. I'd drawn the midnight watch, but I'd been drinking in town—Ilos Kade's tavern, back when Ilos Kade still had a tavern—and I missed the launch. So I rowed out to catch up, and I was maybe two hundred meters off when I saw the lights go wrong." His voice stayed level, reciting, the way men recite things they've rehearsed against forgetting. "Not distress lights. Not flares. Interior lights, going out deck by deck, top to bottom. Like someone walking through a ship turning off lamps. And then she started settling, bow first, and there was no radio call, no boats launched, nothing."

Ansel's throat had gone tight. "The manifest said—"

"The manifest said boiler failure. Twenty-two dead. Your father among them." Tomas finally turned to look at him. "I got close enough to see men in the water. And I saw a navy launch standing off, watching. Not searching. Watching. It stayed until the ship went under, then it turned and went home." He looked back out at the sea. "I rowed away. That's what I did. I rowed away, and I told myself I'd imagined it, and when the inquiry came around asking questions, I told them I'd seen nothing. Because a man named Aldous Reeve—young then, hungry, already wearing his captain's ambition like a borrowed coat—came to my door and told me what would happen to my family if I remembered otherwise."

The name landed between them like a dropped anchor.

"He's the one who signed the salvage order," Ansel said quietly. "On the wreck I dove last month. The restricted site."

"I know why you're out here, boy. I've known since I saw your boat come round the point." Tomas's voice softened—not much, but enough. "Forty years I've lived in this hole, diving my own reefs, keeping my own counsel, telling myself that staying silent kept me safe and safe was enough. And then tonight a boat comes limping into my cove carrying Elias Yarrow's face on a younger man's body, and I find out that safe was never enough. Safe was just slow drowning."

He reached inside his oilskin and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine. He didn't offer it yet. He held it in both hands, weighing it.

"There are letters," he said. "Elias wrote to me from every port. I answered maybe half. When he started talking about testifying—about the cargo that didn't exist, the insurance money, the names—I begged him to keep quiet. The last letter came six weeks before the ship went down. I never answered it." His jaw worked once. "I've read it every year since. On the anniversary. Like penance."

"What does it say?"

Tomas looked at him for a long moment, and in the dim light from the house, his eyes were bright and terrible.

"It says he knew he might not come home," he said. "And it says where he hid the proof."

* * *

Below them, a light came on in the *Maren Lee*'s cabin—Priya, keeping watch, the way she always did when the world felt unsteady. Ansel watched her silhouette move behind the glass and thought about trust: how he'd hidden a strongbox from these people, lied to them by omission, spent their faith like a man spending money he hadn't earned. And how a stranger who owed him nothing had just handed him more truth in one night than twenty years of silence ever had.

"Tomas," he said. "Why now? Why help us?"

The old man was quiet so long that Ansel thought he wouldn't answer. Then:

"Because I ran once," Tomas said. "And running has a cost, boy. You pay it in installments, every single day, for the rest of your life." He pressed the oilcloth bundle into Ansel's hands. It was heavier than it looked. "Elias deserved better than a friend who rowed away. Maybe this is the only payment I'll ever get to make."

Ansel untied the twine with fingers that had suddenly gone clumsy. Inside the oilcloth: a bundle of letters, bound with string gone soft with age, and beneath them, folded into quarters, a single sheet of heavy paper covered in Elias Yarrow's handwriting—a map, or part of one, annotated in a careful sailor's script.

Ansel read the heading twice before his eyes would accept it.

**For the locker in the crew quarters — E.Y.**

His own breath stopped. The locker. The locker they'd found on the dive—the one with his father's initials, the one they'd had to leave behind when Reeve's patrol boat appeared on the horizon. They'd marked its position. They'd sworn they'd come back for it.

"They're still down there," Ansel said. "The papers. In the locker. We know exactly where."

"I know," Tomas said quietly. "That's why I'm coming with you."

Ansel looked up sharply. "It's forty meters down on a restricted wreck with a navy captain hunting us. You're—"

"—seventy-one years old," Tomas finished, "and the last living witness to murder. Boy, I've been waiting forty years for a reason to stop hiding. Don't you dare take this one from me."

Outside the cove, the storm raged on—but Ansel, holding his father's handwriting in his hands for the first time since childhood, felt something shift in his chest like ballast settling. The next chapter of this was written already. All that remained was to sail into it.

"We leave when the weather breaks," he said.

Tomas nodded once, and for the first time all night, the old man smiled.
