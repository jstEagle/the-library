## 13. Twenty Years Under

Tomas Ilic had not spoken this many words in a single night since his wife died, and his throat knew it. It rasped like a rusted winch cable paying out. But the boy—no, not a boy, a man of thirty with Elias's jaw and a stranger's eyes—sat across the oilcloth table with his father's last letter held flat between both hands, careful as a man holding a bird with a broken wing, and Tomas found that twenty years of silence had only been waiting for this exact listener.

The cove was quiet now. The storm had spent its worst on the outer reef and come through here as rain and mutter, drumming the tarp over the boat shed where Ansel's crew slept off their watch. Inside Tomas's hut, a single lamp burned fish oil and gave everything a color like old brass. The letters lay spread on the table between them—the ones from the locker, water-stained but legible, and beside them a bundle of Tomas's own, tied with tarred twine, kept dry for two decades in a tin that had once held rifle cartridges.

"You were there," Ansel said. Not a question. He'd been saying it in different ways all evening, circling it, the way a diver circles a wreck before committing to the descent.

"I was there." Tomas poured tea neither of them would drink. "Third engineer's mate on the *Corvina Star*. Twenty-two years old and certain I'd die an old man aboard her."

* * *

He told it the way he'd told it to himself a thousand times in the dark years, in the cove, mending nets by feel because his hands remembered work even when his mind was elsewhere. He told it slowly, because the details were the only thing he owned outright.

The *Corvina Star* had left Rivergate on the fourteenth of October, twenty-two years ago, carrying general cargo and eleven passengers who were not on any manifest. One of them was a shipping clerk named Elias Yarrow, who had booked passage under a false name because he'd been told to—told by men who said it was safer until the hearing, told by the same firm whose ledgers he'd been copying for three months in secret.

"He wasn't running," Tomas said. "You have to understand that. Everyone always assumed he ran. He was being *moved*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole story."

Elias had evidence. Copies of manifests showing the Voss line had been insuring phantom cargo—ships declared loaded that sailed half-empty, hulls scuttled in deep water, claims paid on losses that never existed. Elias had spent a year as a junior clerk noticing numbers that didn't breathe right, and then another year gathering proof, and when the fraud case finally cracked open in the port courts, his name was on the witness list. The hearing was set for November. He wrote to his wife about it in letters full of careful optimism—he never told her the danger, only that soon they'd have the yard, the house above it, the life he'd sketched in pencil on the backs of envelopes.

Tomas had known him eight months by then. They'd played cards in the engine room during long watches. Elias taught him a card game from the northern ports and lost at it cheerfully and always, always asked after Tomas's mother's health in a way that made Tomas feel like the younger brother instead of the older man. "Your father," Tomas said, watching Ansel's face do something complicated in the lamplight, "had a habit of making people feel looked after. Even the ones looking after him."

The night of the fourteenth, the weather was fair. That was the first wrong thing. A ship doesn't founder in fair weather. But at two in the morning the order came down to secure hatches and stand by, and then came the sound Tomas had spent twenty years failing to describe—a sound below the waterline, forward of the engine room, like a door closing in a cathedral. Charges. Small ones, placed against the sea cocks, placed by men who came aboard at the last port wearing stevedore's clothes and never signed off the articles.

"There were six of us who understood what was happening," Tomas said. "Six who got to the boats before the list took them. The rest—" His hands flattened on the table. "The rest were told it was routine. Most of them went down believing a lie, which is the only mercy in it. Eleven passengers. Nineteen crew. Your father was in his cabin, aft, because he'd been locked there—not roughly, they brought him food, they were polite, politeness costs nothing—but locked, because he was the one man aboard who could not be allowed to reach shore."

Ansel's voice came out low and level, which was worse than shouting. "And you swam away."

"And we swam away." Tomas didn't flinch from it. "Four of us made the liferaft. We watched her go down stern-first with her lights still burning, and we heard—" He stopped. Started again. "We heard nothing. That was the terrible part. No screaming. She just settled into the water like a woman lying down to sleep, and the sea closed over the lamps one by one, and then there was nothing to hear at all. And we knew. We knew what we were watching, and we let it happen, because the alternative was going back aboard a sinking ship toward men with guns."

"Who were the men?" Ansel asked. "Names."

"One name I'll give you, and you already carry it. Reeve." Tomas watched the boy absorb that. "He was a lieutenant then, running harbor security for the firm's insurers. He didn't place the charges. He arranged the patrol schedule so no navy vessel would come within ten miles of the *Corvina Star* that night, and he interviewed us survivors afterward, and he wrote down our statements, and every statement that mentioned charges or locked cabins was rewritten before it reached the inquiry. I saw my own words come back to me with the truth amputated out of them. I signed anyway." His voice cracked on the admission, twenty years late. "I signed, because they knew where my mother lived. Because I was twenty-two. Because signing seemed survivable and not signing seemed like drowning, and I chose drowning later, slower, every day since."

* * *

The rain thickened against the roof. Ansel hadn't moved except to turn the letter over once, still unopened, his thumb resting on the fold like a man testing whether a stove is hot.

"The other three on the raft," Ansel said. "What happened to them?"

"One drowned himself within the year. Drink, mostly, and the drink couldn't hold what he knew. One took the money—they offered it quietly, through lawyers, a settlement for 'distress'—and moved inland and never touched salt water again. Last I heard he was dead of his heart at fifty-one, fat and safe and silent." Tomas untied the twine on his own bundle with fingers that shook only a little. "And one built a hut in a hidden cove and fished a reef for twenty years and told himself that staying alive and staying out of reach was its own kind of testimony. I'm not proud of which one I became. But I'm the one still breathing, and breathing men can sign things."

"Sign things?"

"Statements. Depositions. Whatever your journalist wants written down." He pushed the bundle across the table. "But those aren't mine to open first. Those are yours. I've carried them since the week after the funeral I wasn't allowed to attend."

Ansel looked at the bundle and didn't touch it. "You knew my mother took the money."

"I guessed. When she stopped answering my letters, I guessed. Reeve's people would have gone to her within the month—grieving widow, no income, a boy to feed. They'd have offered her exactly enough to survive and exactly enough to be complicit. It's how it's done. Nobody signs away the truth for luxury. They sign it away for rent and school shoes." Tomas's voice gentled. "She wasn't weak, lad. She was cornered, and the corner was built by professionals."

"She let me grow up thinking my father drowned drunk on a bad crossing." Ansel's jaw worked. "Twenty years. She let me hate him a little, maybe. For leaving. For the accident."

"She let you stay alive. Some mornings those are the same choice." Tomas tapped the bundle. "Open it. Grief needs a shape or it eats whatever shape you give it instead."

* * *

Ansel opened the letter with a diver's care, slitting the fold along the old crease. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, but the handwriting was strong, slanting, a workingman's hand that had learned penmanship from a book. Tomas watched him read and marked the exact moment the words landed—the small collapse of the shoulders, the breath taken in through the nose and held, the way his eyes stopped moving and just fixed on one line and stayed there.

Tomas knew that line. He'd read it a hundred times in twenty years, though he'd never earned the right to answer it.

*If you are reading this, then I did not make it home, and I need you to know it was not the sea that took me.*

The letter ran three pages. Elias Yarrow had written it in his cabin on the last night, by lamplight, knowing—or suspecting enough to act on—and had given it to Tomas at cards that evening with instructions: *if I'm ashore in Rivergate, burn it. If I'm not, get it to my wife.* Tomas had gotten it to the house, and had been turned away at the door by a woman with red-rimmed eyes and a man in a good coat standing behind her, and had left the letter with a neighbor who swore it was delivered, and had spent two decades wondering if it ever arrived.

It hadn't. Ansel's face said it hadn't.

"He wrote about you," Tomas said quietly. "Every port, a letter. I've got nine of them here that he never sent—drafts, things he wanted to say when you were older. He talked about the yard. He'd drawn plans. He wanted a crane on the north wall and a slipway facing east so the morning light hit the hulls while they dried. He said—" Tomas had to stop, clear his throat, start again. "He said a man who salvages things gives the sea back its stolen goods. He thought that was holy work. He thought you'd understand that without being told."

Ansel made a sound that might have been a laugh in a kinder register. "I salvage. I've salvaged for fifteen years. I never knew why it felt like—I never knew why I couldn't stop moving. Like if I stayed anywhere, something would catch up." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious at once, catharsis and rage sharing the same small space behind them. "Something did catch up. It was in a box at the bottom of the sea the whole time."

"That's grief for you," Tomas said. "It keeps better underwater than most things. Comes up clean."

* * *

They sat until the lamp burned low. Outside, the storm had passed entirely; the cove breathed with the small honest sounds of a night after weather—water dripping from the tarp, the crew's boat shifting on its lines, a night bird working the mangroves.

Ansel read all nine drafts. He read them the way he dove—thoroughly, methodically, missing nothing—and twice he had to put them down and walk to the doorway and stand looking at the black water until he could come back and sit. Tomas kept the tea warm and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the letters weren't saying better.

Near dawn, Ansel folded the last page and squared the bundle and set it on the table with the precision of a man racking a tool after use.

"He named Reeve in the letter," Ansel said. "Not as the man who sank the ship. As the man who *let* it sink—who cleared the patrol lanes and buried the survivors' words. My father called him 'a small man climbing a large ladder.'" He looked at Tomas across the guttering lamp. "Reeve boarded our boat two days ago. Looked at me too long. Offered me tea and courtesy. And yesterday, through channels I won't pretend I don't know about, he offered me silence priced like a fortune."

"I know what he offered. He'll have offered you the same thing he offered me at twenty-two. Survival in exchange for the truth, amortized monthly." Tomas leaned forward. "So the question isn't whether he's a monster. Monsters are simple. The question is what you do with a man who genuinely believes he's reasonable."

Ansel stood. In the grey first light coming through the doorway, he looked suddenly very much like the man in the photographs Tomas had kept—Elias at thirty, squinting into harbor glare, a pencil behind his ear.

"I'm going to wake my crew," Ansel said. "All of them. And I'm going to put it on the table—the letters, the manifest, the scuttle order, you, everything—and I'm going to ask them to choose. Because Reeve will come for this cove within a day, and after that nobody chooses anything freely ever again."

Tomas rose too, joints cracking like kindling, and picked up the cartridge tin and pressed it into Ansel's hands along with the unsent drafts.

"Then take these where they're going," he said. "Twenty years I kept them dry. Don't let them drown twice."

Ansel weighed the tin in his palm like ballast, like ballast finally lightened, and turned toward the boat shed where his crew slept—and stopped, because down at the waterline, where the cove opened onto the grey morning sea, a white hull with no markings was standing in slow, patient circles beyond the point.

Waiting for the tide to bring them out.
