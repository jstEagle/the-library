## 14. The Offer

Captain Aldous Reeve had spent thirty years learning that the sea did not forgive, and that men who forgot this drowned in it. He had also learned—more slowly, more painfully—that institutions were simply larger seas, and they drowned men just as thoroughly. The trick was to know which water you were standing in.

He stood now on the deck of the *Corvus*, his patrol boat, watching the storm build along the western horizon like a debt coming due. The monsoon was early this year. The barometer aboard had been falling all morning in small, stubborn increments, and Reeve trusted instruments the way other men trusted scripture: completely, and with a certain grim comfort.

The salvage boat—the *Kestrel*, according to the harbor registry he'd pulled twice—lay at anchor beyond the reef line, riding the swell with the slightly drunken roll of a vessel whose owner hadn't looked at her hull plates recently enough. Reeve knew her condition better than her captain did. He'd read the survey. He'd read everything.

That was, after all, the whole of his profession. Reading things other people believed were private.

"Signal's ready, sir," said Ensign Okafor beside him. "Flag hoist for parley, and we've got the launch fueled."

"Thank you, Ensign." Reeve did not move yet. He watched the storm instead, letting it arrange itself in his mind the way he let all threats arrange themselves: by weight, by speed, by how much room they left him to maneuver. This one would arrive tomorrow night, perhaps the night after. Which meant he had perhaps forty hours of usable weather, and a man named Ansel Yarrow sitting on a locker full of letters that could unmake twenty years of careful, patient, necessary work.

Reeve had made his decision somewhere around three that morning, lying awake in his cabin while the *Corvus* ticked and groaned against her mooring lines. It was not a difficult decision. It was only an uncomfortable one, and he had long ago learned to tell the difference.

"You'll come with me," he told Okafor. "Bring the case."

"The gift case, sir?"

"The gift case."

* * *

The launch crossed the two miles of open water between the patrol boat and the anchored salvage vessel in fifteen minutes of gray chop. Reeve sat in the stern with the case across his knees—a lacquered wooden box, brass-cornered, the kind of thing a shipping company might send to a valued client at Christmas. Inside it, packed in straw like something fragile and alive, were the things he intended to trade: a letter of commission on navy letterhead, already signed; a certified copy of a clean inspection record for the *Kestrel*, backdated and stamped; and beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth, a brick of banknotes that represented roughly four years of a salvage diver's honest earnings.

He had considered bringing whiskey instead. He'd decided against it. Whiskey was for men you wanted to feel like friends. This was not friendship. This was arithmetic.

The *Kestrel*'s rail came up out of the water ahead of them, streaked with salt bloom, and a young woman with dark skin and watchful eyes leaned over it—Priya, the deckhand, the one who kept the crew's accounts and, if Reeve's information held, its conscience. She looked at the launch, then at Reeve's uniform, and her face did the thing faces always did when they saw the navy arrive without being summoned: it closed like a shop shutter.

"Captain Yarrow's below," she said. "You can wait."

"I'd rather not wait, if it's all the same." Reeve climbed the ladder himself—he was fifty-eight and still climbed ladders himself, because a captain who couldn't climb his own ladders deserved what happened to him—and stepped onto the deck with the case under one arm. "Tell him Captain Reeve is here to discuss salvage terms. He'll want to hear that."

Something moved behind the girl's eyes. Not fear. Calculation. She was deciding whether to warn her captain or let him walk into whatever this was blind. Reeve found he respected either choice; both had their logic.

"He's in the aft cabin," she said finally, and turned away, and did not call down through the hatch. That was a warning of a kind, too. Reeve noted it and filed it.

* * *

The aft cabin of the *Kestrel* smelled of diesel, wet canvas, and old coffee—the honest smell of working boats everywhere, the smell Reeve privately preferred to the polished-teak formality of the officers' wardroom. Ansel Yarrow sat at a bolted-down table with charts spread before him and a pencil behind his ear, and when he looked up, Reeve watched the recognition land.

It landed the way he'd known it would. Not surprise—Yarrow had seen him board once before, during the inspection, and had felt the weight of that long look even if he hadn't understood it—but a kind of settling, the way a man settles when a bill he'd hoped was lost arrives anyway.

"Captain." Yarrow didn't stand. Good. Standing was deference, and deference would have made this harder. "Come about my boat again? Find anything else needs inspecting?"

"No." Reeve set the case on the table, on top of the charts, and took the opposite bench. The cabin was small enough that their knees nearly touched. He let the silence sit for a moment, because silences were tools too, and this one needed to do its work. "I've come to make you an offer, Mr. Yarrow. I'd take it as a courtesy if you'd hear all of it before you answer. You're free to throw me off your boat afterward. I won't resist."

Yarrow's jaw tightened. He had his mother's face—Reeve had checked the photographs, the few that existed, the ones in the file he'd maintained for two decades with the discipline of a man tending a wound—and he had Elias's hands. Reeve recognized the hands. Big knuckles, a scar across the left thumb where a winch cable had bitten. Elias Yarrow had shown him that scar once, laughing about it, the week before everything went wrong.

"Say your piece," Ansel said.

Reeve opened the case.

He did it slowly, deliberately, laying each item on the chart table like a man laying out surgical instruments. The commission first. Then the inspection record. Then the money, unwrapped so the notes showed their faces.

"A salvage commission," he said. "Navy-contracted, exclusive rights to the eastern grounds for five years. There are eleven wrecks on those grounds worth raising, conservatively, and no competition. The paperwork is signed. It requires only your name added to it."

Yarrow didn't look at the paper. He looked at Reeve, which was correct, and which was also going to be a problem.

"An inspection record certifying your hull as seaworthy—which, between us, she is not. I've read the survey you've been hiding from your own crew. Your frames are corroded past the point where patching is honesty. In monsoon seas, that boat will come apart, and I don't say that as a threat. I say it as the man who will have to write the report when she does." He tapped the second document. "This makes her legal, insurable, and saleable. You could sell her tomorrow at a fair price instead of losing her and possibly your crew next week."

"And that?" Yarrow's eyes had gone to the money despite himself. Reeve noted it without judgment. Men who didn't look at money were usually lying about something else.

"That's nothing," Reeve said. "Consider it a signing consideration. Consider it my personal regard. Call it whatever lets you sleep."

"And what does it buy?"

"It buys silence." Reeve folded his hands on the table. "Mr. Yarrow, I'm going to speak plainly, because I believe you're a plain man, and because I think you've already guessed most of what I'm about to say, and guessing is worse than knowing. The wreck you've been diving—the one you pulled documents from—is a closed matter. It was closed twenty years ago. The ship was lost due to structural failure in heavy weather. The crew was lost with her. The manifest you recovered is a commercial document of no legal significance, and the locker you've taken from the crew quarters is private property of a deceased man's estate, which makes taking it theft, technically, though I'm not interested in pressing that."

"You know about the locker."

"I know about everything on your boat, Mr. Yarrow. That's not a boast. It's my job, and I'm good at it, and I'd think a professional such as yourself would respect that rather than resent it." Reeve let a breath out through his nose. "Here is what I'm offering. Take the commission. Take the certification. Take the money, or leave it, whichever sits easier in your stomach. Sign nothing against me, publish nothing, sell nothing to any journalist who may have approached you—I'm aware one has approached someone in your circle, and I'd be disappointed but not surprised to learn it was Voss. And in exchange, your father's name rests."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere above them, Priya's boots crossed the deck, unhurried, deliberate—walking a perimeter, Reeve thought, keeping herself between this conversation and the rest of the crew.

"My father's name," Ansel said carefully, "is already resting. That's the official story, isn't it? Accident. Weather. Structural failure."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me that's what it stays."

"I'm telling you that's what it *is*." And here Reeve leaned forward, because this was the part that mattered, the part he had rehearsed in the dark of his cabin not because he needed rehearsal but because he wanted to get it exactly right. "Mr. Yarrow, you've read whatever you pulled out of that hull. Letters, testimony, some account of events the night she went down. I won't insult you by denying it exists. But I want you to understand what it actually is, because I don't think you do yet. It is the word of dead men against the record of the living. It is a story told by a man who was drowning, about men who were drowning with him, and every word of it is unfalsifiable and unusable. No court will hear it. No newspaper can print it responsibly. If you bring it forward, here is what happens: an inquiry opens. It runs eighteen months. It interviews me, and I answer every question truthfully within the limits of what I am permitted to disclose, which is considerable. It interviews the Voss family lawyers, who are very good and very expensive. And at the end of it, the finding is weather, and structural failure, and your father's name appears in a government report as a witness of unsound mind who imagined conspiracies in his last weeks—which, I'll note, is what certain parties said about him while he was alive, and is a slander I have personally worked to keep out of print for twenty years."

"That's generous of you."

"It is not generosity. It is the truth, and I'd thank you to hear it as such." Reeve's voice stayed level. He was genuinely, sincerely calm; that was the thing people never understood about him. They expected the uniform to be armor over anger, and it wasn't. It was armor over exhaustion. "Elias Yarrow was my friend. Before your time, before any of this. I drank with him in this port. I knew the scar on his hand. When the order came down—the actual order, the one you imagine you've found—I was a lieutenant with a mortgage and a pregnant wife, and I executed it, and I have regretted it every day since in the specific, useless way a man regrets a thing he would nevertheless do again, because the alternative was his ruin and mine and the ruin of eleven families whose bread depended on that shipping line staying solvent. You want justice. I understand wanting justice. But justice is not a thing you can dive for and haul up. It's a thing institutions decide, and the institution decided, and the decision kept the port running and kept two hundred men employed and kept your mother fed, which I can prove, if it comes to that, because I'm the one who arranged it."

There it was. He watched it strike—watched Ansel Yarrow's face go through surprise, confirmation, and something colder underneath, all in the space of a breath. Yes. The hush money. The mother. Reeve had held that card for twenty years hoping never to play it, and playing it felt exactly as foul as he'd expected, and he played it anyway, because the alternative was the collapse of everything, and a man had to choose which rot he could live inside.

"You paid my mother," Ansel said. His voice was very quiet.

"I kept your mother from destitution after the lawsuit stripped her husband's estate. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and I know it won't feel like it matters right now." Reeve straightened. "So. The offer stands. Commission, certification, cash, and silence. Your father rests with honor in the record, because I will personally ensure the inquiry—if there ever is one—never prints a word against him. Or you keep your letters, and you spend them, and you learn what they cost. I've watched men make that trade before, Mr. Yarrow. I've never once seen it come out the way they hoped."

He rose. The low overhead forced him to stoop, and he hated that, hated stooping in front of this boy, this diver, this son of a dead friend. He pushed the case's handle toward Ansel.

"Keep the documents either way. The commission is real regardless of your answer—it's yours if you want it next month, next year, whenever the dust settles. I'm not a man who punishes caution." He moved toward the hatch, then stopped, one hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around. "One more thing, and then I'll go. The storm arriving tomorrow is going to be worse than the forecasts say. I've seen the pressure trace. Whatever you decide about me—decide it fast, and get that hull of yours into shelter, because the sea doesn't care whose son you are. Elias would have told you the same."

He dropped through the hatch into the gray light.

* * *

The launch pulled away from the *Kestrel* at a quarter past four, and Reeve sat in the stern and did not look back, because looking back was weakness and also because he already knew what he'd see: Ansel Yarrow standing at the rail, the case untouched on the table behind him, or maybe not untouched—maybe held, weighed, the way a man weighs a hook he hasn't quite swallowed.

Okafor steered them home through the building chop. Halfway back, the ensign spoke without being asked to, which was unlike him.

"Sir—do you think he'll take it?"

Reeve considered the question honestly, because the ensign deserved honesty and because the answer mattered to what came next.

"No," he said. "He's Elias's son. Elias never once took the sensible door when there was a locked one nearby." He watched the western sky, where the clouds had organized themselves into a single dark thought. "Signal the *Corvus*. Have them ready the boarding party and double the watch tonight. And get me the harbor authority on the wire—I want the records office closed for 'storm precautions' by morning."

Okafor hesitated. "Closed, sir?"

"Closed, Ensign. Weather happens. Paperwork drowns." Reeve settled back against the gunwale and let the spray hit his face, cold and honest, the only thing left in this affair that was either. "If the boy won't take the door I offered him, then he'll find the next one locked. And when he comes back to the first one—because he will, they always do—we'll renegotiate. Everyone negotiates eventually. The sea teaches you that, if nothing else."

Behind them, the *Kestrel* rode her anchor in the rising swell, small and stubborn and doomed-looking, and Aldous Reeve told himself—as he had told himself for twenty years—that everything he did next was for the best, and almost believed it.
