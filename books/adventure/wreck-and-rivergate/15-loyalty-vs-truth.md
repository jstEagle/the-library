## 15. Loyalty vs. Truth

The storm had passed through in the night and left the cove rinsed clean, the water flat as poured pewter, the air smelling of wet rope and crushed kelp. Tomas Ilic's boat sat at anchor beside ours like two old dogs leaning against each other, and I stood at the rail of the *Marrow's Grace* watching the light come up over the reef and thinking about how a man decides what he is willing to lose.

I hadn't slept. The letters were under my shirt, against my chest, where they'd been since dawn. My father's handwriting. Twenty years of ports and weather and small hopes, folded into paper gone soft as cloth from its years in the locker, and every time I breathed I could feel them shift against my skin like something alive. Like something waiting.

Below decks, the crew was waking. I could hear Priya coaxing the stove, hear Okafor coughing in his bunk, hear the particular groan of the hull as it settled—still weeping a little at the seams despite everything we'd done to her, a thin salt trickle that never quite stopped now, the way an old wound never quite closes.

I went down.

* * *

They gathered around the table because I asked them to, and that alone told them something was wrong. In six years running this boat I had never once asked for a meeting. Decisions on the *Grace* happened the way weather happens—in the open, argued over, voted with shrugs and curses until the shape of them was plain. A formal table meant a captain who didn't trust the open air anymore.

Priya set out coffee without being asked. She knew me well enough to know I'd need something to do with my hands if my mouth failed, and she set the pot down within reach of my elbow like a woman arming a soldier.

Okafor sat across from me, big shoulders filling his corner of the bench, his hands wrapped around his mug. Beside him, Ferro leaned back against the bulkhead with his arms crossed, already wearing the expression of a man who has read the last page first. Young Dev perched on the edge of the chart table, legs swinging, too nervous to sit still. And Priya took the seat nearest mine, close enough that her knee touched mine under the table, and stayed there.

"Reeve came to see me yesterday," I said. "While you were ashore."

Ferro's eyebrows went up. "The captain. Here. On Tomas's beach."

"On his skiff, off the point. He sent a boat." I turned my mug a quarter turn on the table. "He made an offer."

"And you're telling us because?"

"Because it wasn't just for me."

That got them all quiet. Even Dev stopped swinging his legs.

I laid it out the way Reeve had laid it out to me, because there was no honest way to soften it: a salvage commission, exclusive rights to half the restricted wrecks on the coast, our record wiped clean of the harbor violations that had been accruing like barnacles for three years. The strongbox and the letters left resting. The dead left dead. His father's name—my father's name—left sleeping under twenty years of navy paperwork that said *accident* in tidy type.

"He wants us to walk away," Okafor said slowly. "And he'll pay us like princes for it."

"He wants us to forget," I said. "There's a difference. Princes don't get watched for the rest of their lives. Princes don't find out what happens the next time they know something the navy doesn't want known."

"You don't know that," Ferro said. "You're guessing at the fine print."

"I'm guessing at the man. He looked at me across that skiff and he thought he was being generous. That's what frightens me. Not the threat underneath it—the sincerity on top of it. He believes he's offering us mercy."

Priya's hand found my knee under the table and pressed once, then withdrew. She said nothing. She'd been saying nothing for days, holding the weight of what she'd seen me hide, and I understood now that her silence hadn't been agreement. It had been patience. There was a difference there too, and I was only learning to tell them apart.

"There's more," I said. "Isn't there always." I reached into my shirt and brought out the letters, and set them on the table between the coffee pot and the salt-stained chart of the outer reef.

Nobody moved. The cabin was so quiet I could hear the hull weep—that thin salt trickle finding its way down into the bilge, patient as anything.

"These are my father's," I said. "Elias Yarrow. Written over four years, from every port the ship ever called at. The last one was sealed the week before she went down." I touched the topmost page and felt the softness of the paper, the ghost of a pen pressed hard enough to nearly tear. "Tomas Ilic was his shipmate. He saw the scuttling. He ran, and he's been hiding in that cove for twenty years carrying it, and he gave me these because he's too old and too frightened to carry them to the port authority himself."

Dev was staring at the letters like they might bite. "Your father. The diver. The one the yard's named after—the one your mother always—"

"Yes."

"The navy said it was a grounding. Weather."

"The navy said a lot of things." I kept my voice level. It cost something. "Reeve's mentor ordered that ship sunk with witnesses aboard. My father was going to testify. Instead he went down with her, and my mother took money to raise me on the lie, and I've spent my whole life diving wrecks for the same service that drowned him."

Okafor set his mug down very carefully, the way a man does when he isn't sure what his hands will do otherwise. "So the choice is real," he said. "Take his money, or take these papers to the port authority and let whatever comes, come."

"That's the choice."

"And what comes?" Ferro again, sharp as a gaff hook. "You've thought about that? Because I have. Reeve gets suspended, maybe. Maybe. Investigations drown in their own ink. But before that happens, every man on this boat becomes a name on a list. You think the harbor master forgets? You think the shipping families do? We work these waters. Our names are on every manifest from here to Corren Bay. You want to spend the next ten years getting inspected twice as often, contracted half as much, quietly starved by men who never have to say why?"

"I've thought about it," I said.

"And?"

"And I keep coming back to the fact that Reeve offered us this because he thinks we can be bought. Not because he fears us. Because he prices us." I looked around the table, at each face in turn, and made myself say the rest. "I won't pretend I came to this clean. Three days ago I hid the strongbox in my bunk and lied to all of you about it, and Priya caught me at it and covered for me anyway, which is more than I deserved. So I'll say it plain: I've been spending this crew's trust like it was mine alone to spend. That ends tonight. Whatever we decide, we decide together, and I'll live with it. Even if the decision is to take his money."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, a gull cried out over the cove, and the sound came down the companionway thin and far away, like a voice from another world.

"You'd take it?" Dev said. "If we voted that way?"

"If you voted that way, I'd take it, and I'd hate myself in a way no commission would fix, and I'd dive for that man until the day I couldn't look at myself in the water. But it would be your call, not mine. That's what I owe you for the bunk. For the lies."

Priya spoke then, for the first time, and her voice was quiet and absolutely steady. "Read the last letter."

"What?"

"Out loud. If we're choosing, we choose knowing what's in them. Not the idea of them. The thing itself." Her eyes held mine, dark and unblinking. "You've had them against your heart for two days. We've had nothing but your face. Read it."

My hands weren't steady. I noticed that from a great distance, the way you notice weather happening to someone else. I unfolded the last letter, the one sealed the week before the ship sailed for the last time, and I read it by the light of the swinging lamp while four people I would drown for listened to a dead man speak.

* * *

*Dear Ansel —*

*You'll be seven by the time I'm home, or near enough. Your mother writes that you've taken to the water like it owes you money, which sounds right, because it owes our whole family money and has never paid up. I've been looking at a boatyard on the Rivergate flats. Derelict, but the pilings are sound and the slipway faces east, which matters more than people think. I've drawn it out — I'll show you when I'm home. A man should leave something standing that isn't a grave.*

*I have to tell some men some true things when I make port. It's past time, and I'm tired of carrying it, and your mother deserves better than the silence I've asked of her. It may cost us. I've made peace with that. What I haven't made peace with is you growing up thinking your father was afraid.*

*I'm not afraid. I want that written down somewhere that lasts. Whatever they tell you after — and men like this always arrange for things to be told after — know that I walked toward it with my eyes open.*

*Keep to the deep water, son. The shallows are where everything runs aground.*

*Your father,*

*Elias*

* * *

When I finished, nobody spoke for a long moment. Dev had gone white. Okafor's jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing on something too large to swallow. Ferro stared at the table, and I watched the fight move behind his eyes—because Ferro was the pragmatist, Ferro was the one who counted the fuel and the beans and the odds, and here was a dead man asking him to bet all three on a word like *true*.

"A man should leave something standing that isn't a grave," Priya repeated softly. She looked up at me. "He wrote that twenty years ago. Before any of this. Before the yard, before the wreck, before—" She stopped. Started again. "Ansel. He wrote your ending into that letter without knowing it. You're sitting in it."

"I know."

"Then you know what I'm going to vote."

"I know," I said again. "But say it anyway. All of you. Out loud. Because whatever we do, we do it together, and I want to hear it in your voices, not guess at it in your faces."

Ferro laughed, short and bitter, without any humor in it at all. "You always did have a taste for ceremony, Yarrow."

"It's not ceremony. It's the last fair thing any of us gets."

He pushed back from the table then, and stood, and rolled his shoulders like a man preparing to lift something heavy—or put it down forever. "All right. Let's have it done. I'll go first, since I'm the coward and everyone knows it." He planted his fists on the table and leaned into the lamplight. "I say we take the captain's money. Every coin of it. I say we buy the new hull plating and the good winch and pay off my sister's debts and Dev's mother's roof, and I say we do it knowing full well what we are afterward, because I've been what we are now for six years and poor besides, and I am tired, Ansel. I am tired down to the keel. Truth doesn't plate a hull. Truth doesn't keep the monsoon out. I've got nothing against your father, and God forgive me for saying it, but he's twenty years dead and comfortable, and we're the ones who have to float."

The words landed on the table and lay there like fish dying in air. Nobody rushed to answer them, because they were true, and everyone knew it, and truth cuts both ways on this boat or it cuts nothing.

"Dev?" I said.

The boy—man, really, though none of us treated him like it—took his feet off the chart table and sat up straight. He looked at the letter. He looked at Ferro. When he spoke, his voice cracked in the middle and he pushed through it. "My mother says the navy buried my grandfather with a flag and a lie both, and she's never decided which one she resents more." He swallowed. "I vote the papers. The authority. All of it. I'm sorry, Ferro. I want to be the kind of man who reads a letter like that and does nothing, and I've met him, and I don't like him."

Ferro closed his eyes. "Okafor."

Okafor took his time. He was a man who believed silence was also a kind of speech, and he made everyone wait through all of it. Then he said, "I've served on boats where the captain decided and the crew bled for it. Never again. This one goes to a vote, and my vote is this: I didn't sign on to salvage paper. I signed on to salvage what's worth keeping." He looked at me. "Your father's name is worth keeping. Vote's for the truth."

"Ferro," I said. "You've heard them."

"I've heard them." He straightened. He looked older suddenly, or maybe just finally his real age, worn where the wear actually was. "Then here's mine, and here's the rest of it: I'm walking. Not angry. Don't follow me to the dock and don't write. I'll take the mail boat to Rivergate tomorrow and find work on a freighter, and in ten years I'll read about it in the papers and buy the round when it's over—if it's over and you're alive to drink it." His voice roughened, and he hated that, I could see him hating it. "But I won't be on this boat when she runs that reef, and I won't be in the room when Reeve's friends start collecting names. I've got a sister. You all have somebody. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic."

"Ferro—" Dev started.

"Don't." He held up one hand. "Don't make it small. Six years, and I love every scar on this tub, and that's exactly why I get to say no." He looked at me last, longest. "You were always going to end up here, Yarrow. The day I met you, you were diving something everybody else had sworn off, and you came up grinning with blood in your ears. Some men are built for the deep water. I'm built for the shallows, and the shallows are where everything runs aground—his words, not mine, and he was right, and I intend to stay aground and alive."

He picked up his mug, drained it standing, and set it upside down on the table—an old deckhand's signal, the cup turned over, the watch ended, no more pouring after. Then he went up the companionway into the grey morning, and we heard his boots on the deck, and then we didn't.

* * *

Three remained. Priya, Okafor, Dev. And me.

"Well," Okafor said into the quiet. "Two for the papers, one gone, and the captain hasn't voted."

"I don't need to," I said. "You all know."

"We know," Priya said. "Say it anyway. Fair's fair—you made us."

So I said it. "We go to the port authority. Tomorrow night, through the outer reef, with Tomas and the letters aboard. Reeve watches the harbor, so we don't use the harbor. And I won't lie to you about the odds. She's weeping at the seams, the season's turning early, and the man who offered to buy us will be looking for us the moment he realizes we didn't sell. Some of us might not come back from this. I'd be lying if I promised otherwise, and I'm done lying on this boat."

Dev nodded, pale but set. Okafor nodded once, slow, like a door closing on solid hinges.

Priya reached across the table and turned Ferro's overturned cup back upright. Set it down gently, in its old place.

"He'll be back," she said. "After. When it's over. Men like Ferro always come back for the drinking."

"And if it doesn't end?" Dev asked.

"Then we'll have been right," Priya said, "in company worth being right with."

I gathered the letters, folded them soft against my chest again, and felt them settle like a hand pressing flat over my heart. Above us, boots crossed the deck—Tomas, keeping his silent rounds—and beyond the companionway the cove lay bright and windless, holding its breath.

Tomorrow night we'd run the reef. Tonight, we had a boat to patch and a course to plot, and somewhere out in the grey water, a patrol boat was already turning toward us, though none of us knew it yet.
