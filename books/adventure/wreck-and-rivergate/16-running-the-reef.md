## 16. Running the Reef

The harbor lights of Rivergate came on at dusk, and Priya counted them the way other people counted sheep—one for every reason they shouldn't be here.

Twelve lights on the customs pier. Three on the navy tender moored off the breakwater, her running lamps burning that flat, patient white that meant *we are not going anywhere*. A fourth light, higher up, in the signal tower: someone awake up there, someone with binoculars and a thermos and nothing to do all night but watch a leaking salvage boat sit at anchor like an honest vessel with honest intentions.

"They know," Dev said beside her. He'd been saying it since sundown, in different words, each one more resigned than the last.

"Of course they know." Priya kept her voice low and her eyes on the water. "Knowing isn't stopping us."

Below her feet, through the deck plates, she could feel the engine idling—*Kestrel's* old heart beating slower than it used to, the way Ansel had tuned it, quiet as a sleeping animal. The bilge pumps had cycled four times in the last hour. She'd stopped logging it. Some numbers you didn't want written down where the crew could see them.

Ansel came up from below without a sound, which was his particular gift—six feet of silence in rubber boots—and stood at the rail beside them. He didn't look at the navy tender. He looked at the channel mouth, the gap between the breakwater arms where the harbor opened onto black water, and beyond it, faint even in the dark, the long white stutter of surf breaking on the outer reef.

"The harbor channel's watched," he said. "So we don't use the harbor channel."

Dev made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer. "The reef passage. At night. In this swell."

"The reef passage is unwatched because it's suicide." Ansel turned then, and Priya saw his face in the binnacle light—the calm of it, the terrible settled quality, like a man who had already drowned once and found the bottom agreeable. "Tomas ran it forty times before the navy closed it. He says there's a window at slack water, two hours wide, when the surge drops enough to clear the teeth. After that we wait a month, or we hand Reeve the letters ourselves and save him the trouble."

"And if Tomas is wrong?"

"Then we're a story fishermen tell." Ansel looked at her. "Priya. The helm."

It wasn't a question, and that was its own kind of trust. There were three people aboard who could take *Kestrel* through coral at night, and Ansel had chosen her—not because she was the best, though she was, and not because he loved her, though he did, in the wordless way of crews—but because her hands didn't shake. Not in weather, not in fire, not now, with everything they had left folded in oilcloth below decks and a dead man's son standing at her shoulder asking her to drive them into the one place the sea kept for itself.

"Aye," she said, and took the wheel.

* * *

They slipped anchor at 2340, no lights, no radio, the engine breathing so low it was more felt than heard. The tide tables said slack water began at midnight. The moon was a fingernail paring behind cloud, which was either luck or providence or simply the monsoon being generous with its darkness before it took everything else.

Tomas Ilic stood at the bow like a figurehead that had been left out in the weather too long—seventy years of salt cured into leather, eyes that caught whatever light existed and gave none back. He'd refused the wheel, refused the chart table, refused everything but the bow, where he said a man could smell the water change over the coral. Priya believed him. She'd seen him taste the wind that afternoon and name the swell height within a hand's width.

"Hard to starboard," he called, soft as a secret. "Mind the moaning buoy."

There was no moaning buoy. There hadn't been one in twenty years, Tomas said; the navy pulled it when they closed the passage. But he steered by its ghost anyway, and Priya learned fast that ghosts were better charts than paper, because paper didn't remember.

The breakwater fell away behind them. For eleven minutes they were in open dark, and Priya let herself breathe—let herself think, absurdly, *maybe it'll be easy*—which was exactly the kind of thought the sea punished.

"Sound changes," Tomas called. "Listen."

She listened. Under the engine's murmur, under the hull's small complaints, there it was: a long, low exhale from ahead, rhythmic as something breathing in its sleep. Surf on the outer barrier, a mile of it, a white wall they could hear but not yet see.

And beneath that, closer, a second sound—a hiss, a spit, the sea chewing on stone.

"Reef's ahead," she called back. "I hear it eating."

"Good girl." Tomas didn't turn around. "Now find the quiet place inside the noise. There's a lane through the middle where the water goes silent between the breaks. When you hear the silence, you're in. When you lose it, you're dead. Simple."

Simple. Priya gripped the wheel and thought about her mother, who had told her once that courage wasn't the absence of fear but the decision that some things mattered more than it. Her mother had been talking about leaving her father. Priya was thinking about coral heads the size of trucks, lying in wait just under a black surface, patient as creditors.

The first swell lifted *Kestrel* and set her down gently, almost kindly, and Priya understood they were inside the reef's reach now. The water had gone the color of wet slate. Somewhere off the port bow, white water bloomed and died, bloomed and died—a breaker marking a coral head like a candle on a grave.

"Port," Tomas said. "Ten degrees. Follow the dying water."

She followed the dying water. The breaker slid past close enough that spray reached them, cold fingers down her neck, and beyond it the night opened into a corridor of black glass with walls of white noise on either side. The silence Tomas had promised lived in the middle of it, narrow as a hallway, and *Kestrel* crept into it at six knots with her engine muttering prayers.

Behind her, she heard Ansel at the depth sounder, calling numbers in a voice gone flat and careful. "Four fathoms. Three and a half. Steady." Then, after a minute: "Two and a half."

Two and a half fathoms. Fifteen feet of water under a keel that drew eight. Seven feet of margin, and every foot of it full of coral that would open their hull like a letter.

Dev was on the bow with a boathook and a flashlight he wasn't allowed to turn on, leaning out into the dark like a man reading braille. "Shoal water port side! White sand—I can see it, it's right there—" His voice cracked upward and he wrestled it back down. "Starboard. Come starboard, come starboard—"

"Starboard ten," Priya said, and the wheel answered, and the boat answered the wheel, and for three seconds the whole world was the slow swing of the compass card and the sound of her own blood.

Then the sand fell away beneath them and the depth went to four fathoms again, and Dev laughed—one sharp bark, quickly strangled—and said, "Never doing that again," in the tone of a man who absolutely would.

* * *

Halfway through, the sea tried to kill them properly, because it always did.

It came without warning, the way the worst things do—a set of swells out of the southeast, bigger than anything before it, rolling into the passage and stacking against the outgoing tide until the slack water wasn't slack anymore. The corridor of black glass became a river of moving hills, and the silence Tomas steered by filled up with roar.

"Tide's turning early!" Tomas shouted, and for the first time all night his voice had edges on it. "The storm's pushing the water ahead of it! We have minutes, not hours!"

Priya did the arithmetic without wanting to. Minutes to cover the last half mile of reef, at speed, in the dark, with the passage narrowing ahead where the charts showed a bend around a formation the old fishermen called the Teeth—jagged spires that broke the surface at low water and hid just beneath it at high.

"Ansel." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I need more engine."

He was already there, hand on her shoulder, looking past her at the white chaos ahead. Whatever he saw in it, he must have found acceptable, because he nodded once and went below, and thirty seconds later *Kestrel's* old engine woke up angry, the hull coming alive under Priya's hands like a horse feeling the spur.

"Eight knots," she said. "Nine. Ten."

At ten knots in fifteen feet of water, a mistake stopped being a grounding and became a sinking. Priya knew it. Everyone aboard knew it. Nobody said it, because saying it would have spent breath they needed for steering.

The Teeth announced themselves as a solid wall of breaking white directly across their path, and somewhere inside it, if Tomas was right, was the gap—a slot maybe twenty meters wide where the spires leaned apart and the water funneled through smooth.

"Where?" Priya shouted.

Tomas was soaked through, clinging to the shrouds, hair plastered to his skull, and he was smiling. She'd remember that afterward—how the old man smiled into the worst water he'd seen in forty years, like a man greeting an enemy he'd finally decided to forgive.

"Left of the big break!" he roared. "Aim for the loudest part and hold your nerve!"

Which was, Priya thought, the most insane piece of seamanship ever handed down, and also, she realized as she squared the wheel, exactly right. The biggest break marked the deepest slot—that was why the water piled highest there, all that ocean squeezing through one throat. Aim for the calm and you'd die on the spires. Aim for the roar and you might live.

"Hold on!" she screamed aft, and drove *Kestrel* at the wall of white.

The world dissolved. Spray hit like thrown gravel, the bow punched into a wave face and buried, and for one endless second Priya was steering blind, trusting the compass, trusting Tomas, trusting seven feet of margin and a dead man's memory of a passage he'd run in daylight with a young man's eyes. The engine screamed. Something heavy broke loose below and crashed against a bulkhead. The stern lifted, dropped, lifted—

—and then the roar split around them, fell away on both sides, and *Kestrel* shot through the Teeth into deep black water smooth as poured oil, still carrying the sea's fingerprints in foam along her rails.

Behind them, the reef went back to eating itself.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then Dev, dripping at the bow, started to laugh—really laugh, helpless, gasping—and Priya found she was laughing too, and so was old Tomas, and the sound carried across the flat water like something alive escaping.

* * *

They cleared the outer shoals at 0110 and stood toward the mainland lights, and that was when Priya let herself look aft, back along the way they'd come, and saw the thing that took the laughter out of her chest.

A light was moving along the line of the reef. Low, steady, sweeping side to side—a searchlight, mounted on something with a wake behind it, working the passage mouth with the methodical patience of a man checking a trap he'd baited weeks ago.

The navy tender. Coming around the outside of the harbor to close the reef route, arriving just late enough to watch them go.

"He knew," Dev said quietly, beside her. He'd come aft without her hearing. "Reeve knew about the passage. He was watching it anyway."

"Then he knows something else too." Priya turned back to the wheel and found Ansel standing at her shoulder, looking at the same distant light, his face unreadable in the dark. "He knows we didn't run blind. He knows we had a guide. And there's only one man alive who could thread that passage from memory."

Ansel was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and even. "Then we make sure it doesn't matter. By sunrise those letters are in the records office, witnessed and sealed, and no warrant on earth pulls them back out. Let him watch. Let him follow. He's out of time and he knows it."

Ahead, the mainland rose out of the sea—a long dark shoulder with a scatter of lights at its hip, and among them, somewhere, the squat stone building where Rivergate kept its records, its deeds, its judgments, its truths. Twenty minutes at this heading. Fifteen, if the tide helped.

Priya put the wheel over and held the course, and *Kestrel* ran for the lights with her patched hull groaning and her pumps whispering and everything anyone had ever died for lashed down below.

The barometer on the bulkhead had fallen another two points since midnight. Behind them, far out over the reef, the sky had begun to flicker—dry lightning, heatless, silent, the storm's opening argument.

Fifteen minutes, she thought. Just give us fifteen.

Then the engine coughed.
