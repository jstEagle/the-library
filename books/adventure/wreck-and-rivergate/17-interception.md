## 17. Interception

Mara Voss had spent her whole life learning to read water, and the channel ahead of them was reading like a confession.

The rain had thinned to a fine gray hiss, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air, and through it she could see the patrol boat sitting square across the mouth of the channel like a hand laid flat over a page. *Vigilant*. She knew her hulls, and she knew this one—Reeve's command for six years now, white above the waterline and black below, its running lights burning even in daylight because a man like Aldous Reeve never wanted there to be any doubt about who was watching.

"Two hundred meters to the narrows," Priya called from the wheelhouse, her voice stripped down to function. "Current's against us. If we try to run past him on the north side, we're in the breakers inside of four minutes."

"And if we try the south side," Ansel said from beside her, "we're in the shallows where his draft won't follow but neither will ours."

They were standing at the rail of the *Kestrel*, which wasn't really theirs anymore—it was Tomas Ilic's cove behind them, and the letters were aboard in an oilskin wrap against Ansel's chest, and everything that mattered in the world was riding on forty-year-old planking and an engine that had been fighting floodwater two nights ago. Mara had her own skiff trailing on a tow line, the little aluminum runner she'd kept moored in Rivergate since before the lawsuit, since before Voss Shipping had been carved up and sold off in pieces. She'd brought it along without explaining why. Some preparations you don't explain, because explaining them means admitting what you already know is coming.

She'd known since the cove. Since Reeve's boat had appeared on the horizon like a sentence being handed down.

"He'll want to board," she said. "He won't fire first—not with a journalist waiting in Rivergate and the port authority already sniffing around. He needs this quiet. Quiet is the whole point of twenty years."

Ansel turned to look at her. Rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away, and in the gray light he looked less like the boy who'd beaten her to the *Corvane* wreck a decade ago and more like his father in the one photograph Tomas had shown them—young Elias Yarrow, squinting into the same weather, wearing the same expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"You have a plan," Ansel said. It wasn't a question. He'd learned that much about her, these last weeks, if nothing else.

"I have a family inheritance," Mara said. "It's just not the one people expect."

* * *

The patrol boat came about to meet them with the unhurried grace of something that has never once needed to hurry. Through the rain Mara watched the figures on its deck resolve: two sailors at the rail, a coxswain at the helm, and Captain Aldous Reeve standing forward of the bridge wing with his hands clasped behind his back, as though the monsoon were a garden party and they were all honored guests.

They hailed from fifty meters, a loudhailer flattening Reeve's voice into something courteous and dead.

"*Fishing vessel, identify yourself and heave to. This is a navigation safety inspection. Comply and be boarded.*"

Navigation safety. In the middle of a monsoon, in a channel no commercial traffic had used since the war. Mara almost admired it. Reeve lied the way her father had signed contracts—with beautiful penmanship and no conscience whatsoever.

"Take us in slow," she told Priya. "Throttles at bare steerage. Let him think we're deciding."

Priya hesitated—one heartbeat, her eyes flicking to Ansel—and then the *Kestrel*'s engine note dropped, and the old hull wallowed into the swell, coasting toward the white shape of the law.

Ansel stepped close to Mara, close enough that his voice wouldn't carry past the rail. "Whatever you're going to do—"

"You'll know when I do it."

"Mara." His hand caught her sleeve. Under the rain his grip was careful, the way he handled anything he thought might break. "I've watched you calculate your whole way through this job. Every kindness priced, every risk hedged. So tell me honestly: is whatever you're about to do for the evidence, or for yourself?"

The question should have stung. Weeks ago it would have; back at the end of the pier, when she'd offered his crew a contract built on half-truths, she'd have answered it with a smile and a deflection and gone on lying beautifully, because that was the Voss inheritance too. But she'd stood in Tomas's cove and heard a dying man describe her grandfather's signature on a scuttle order, and something in her had cracked along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing.

"For everyone my name ever drowned," she said. "That's honest enough for open water."

Ansel held her eyes a moment longer, then let go of her sleeve. "Then I'm with you," he said, "whatever it is. But don't die cleverly. Die stupidly if you have to, but don't die cleverly. Clever people die alone."

"Noted," she said, and went aft to her skiff.

* * *

They came alongside under fenders, and Reeve's boarding party made a show of procedure: lines thrown, gangway rigged, a young lieutenant with a clipboard reciting regulations into the rain. Reeve himself crossed last, stepping onto the *Kestrel*'s deck with the lightness of a man who had done this a hundred times and enjoyed it perhaps more than he'd admit.

"Captain Voss." He said her name like a chess move. "Or is it Ms. Ferren now? Forgive me—the registry files are so inconsistent since your family's restructuring."

"Ferren," Mara said pleasantly. "But you knew that when you cornered us, Captain, so let's not spend the storm pretending. You've been shadowing us since the outer reef. You blocked the harbor approach. And you've put your boarding party on a salvage boat carrying nothing worth inspecting except—" she paused, letting the rain fill the silence, "—paper."

Something moved behind Reeve's eyes, quick and cold as a fish turning. "Paper," he repeated.

"Documents. Correspondence. The sort of thing that gets wet and ruined and stops mattering entirely, if only someone responsible were to take custody of it." She smiled the smile her father had taught her, the one that opened doors and closed ledgers. "I'm told you're very responsible, Captain."

Behind him, his sailors were moving through the deck with practiced hands—checking hatches, lifting tarps, their eyes cataloguing everything. One of them glanced into the wheelhouse where Priya stood rigid at the wheel, and another toward the hold where Tomas sat wrapped in blankets, an old man trying to look like cargo. Ansel leaned against the rail near the stern, arms folded, radiating the boredom of a man with nothing in his pockets. He was good. He'd always been good. But Reeve wasn't looking at him yet, and when he did—

"The manifest," Reeve said quietly, for her ears alone. The courtesy dropped away like a coat. "You have it. And the letters."

"I might."

"You do." He turned fully to face her, positioning his body between her and the others, a courtesy of executioners. "Ms. Ferren—Mara. Let me save us both the theater. That paper is classified naval material recovered illegally from a restricted wreck. Everyone aboard this vessel is facing ten years minimum, and that's before we discuss the deaths that will inevitably occur when untrained civilians handle military ordnance in monsoon conditions. Accidents happen at sea. You of all people know how easily a Voss ends up at the bottom of one."

The threat was silk over steel, and beneath both was something almost gentle. That was the worst part of Aldous Reeve—he believed himself. Twenty years ago he'd signed or countersigned or simply looked away while a ship full of men went into the water, and he had built a life on the foundation that it had been necessary, regrettable, clean. Men like him didn't lie to themselves. They just never checked the math twice.

"I want a deal," Mara said.

Reeve's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "Now we're being sensible."

"My name stays out of it. My record, my family's history—all of it sealed. You get the evidence, complete, and I walk away with my crew and a pardon for the salvage."

"All of it," Reeve said slowly, tasting it. "The manifest and the letters."

"Everything. I'll bring it up from the hold myself."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him decide that greed and fear were sufficient explanations for her—that a woman would trade a nation's scandal for her own skin, because that was what he would do, and men like Reeve assumed the whole world was built to their own blueprint. She let him believe it. Belief was a rope, and rope could be paid out.

"The hold," Reeve said, gesturing to his lieutenant. "Open it. Slowly."

* * *

The hold of the *Kestrel* smelled of bilgewater and diesel and the ghost of the flood they'd fought two nights ago. Mara went down the ladder first with a lantern, feeling Reeve's presence above her like weather, and knelt beside the false ballast panel Ansel had welded shut in Tomas's cove.

Her heart was doing something strange—not racing but slowing, each beat deliberate, the way time moves in the last seconds of a dive when you're watching your gauge and your air and the distance to the surface and knowing that only one of them can be wrong.

Under the panel, in a waterproof case, lay the decoy: a bundle of aged paper Tomas had prepared from his own stores, tea-stained and lettered in a shaking hand, convincing enough at arm's length in lantern light. The real letters rode against Ansel's chest in the oilskin wrap, and the real manifest was sewn into Priya's jacket, because they had agreed in the cove that no single person would carry the truth. She had insisted on that arrangement herself. She'd told herself it was tactics.

It hadn't been tactics. It had been this. Some part of her had been writing this scene since the pier, and had simply declined to inform the rest of her.

She lifted the case and climbed back into the gray daylight, and Reeve took it from her hands with the reverence of a man receiving communion.

"Captain," she said, "a word about the channel."

He was already opening the case, already scanning the first tea-browned page, his lips moving slightly. His sailors crowded near, and for thirty seconds—she counted them, thirty seconds of a man's attention given wholly to paper—every eye on the patrol boat and every eye on the *Kestrel* was on Aldous Reeve and his prize.

That was the window. Windows don't stay open.

"The channel," she said again, louder, and pointed south, toward the narrows where the current funneled between the reef arms. "There's a hazard. You'll want to log it before you take your prize home."

Reeve looked up, irritated. "A hazard."

"A derelict," Mara said. "Adrift in the narrows. Small craft, aluminum, no lights. It came off our tow line in the last squall." She turned and gestured aft, and every head followed her gesture—to the empty spot at the stern where her skiff had been riding on its tow line since dawn.

Because it wasn't there anymore.

She had cut it loose herself, twenty minutes ago, in the worst of the squall, when the rain was thick enough to hide a swimmer and the sound of a small engine. She had motored it into the narrows alone, in seas that would have frightened her at twenty, and set it athwart the channel with its sea anchor out and its bilge plug pulled, and then she had swum back to the *Kestrel* through water cold enough to stop a heart, and climbed aboard by the stern ladder while Ansel covered her with a blanket and asked no questions, because he understood, the way salvage men understand, that some jobs you do alone.

The skiff sat in the throat of the channel now, shipping water, settling by the bow, a small aluminum obstruction directly in the path of the only deep water for six miles. Not big enough to sink a patrol boat. Big enough to force one to stand off, to circle, to send boats and lines and men and minutes—minutes measured against a rising sea and a falling barometer and a records office in Rivergate that closed its doors at nightfall.

"You lost your skiff," Reeve said flatly.

"Squalls are treacherous." Mara spread her hands. "You'll want to clear it before you run the narrows. Standard procedure, I'd imagine. Marking hazards, protecting navigation." She let the smile come back, and this time there was nothing sold in it, nothing performed. It was just true, and it felt like setting down weight she'd carried since she was nine years old and first learned what her family's ships carried besides cargo. "My family put a lot of things in the water that didn't belong there, Captain. Consider that a Voss, taking one back out."

Reeve stared at her, and she watched understanding arrive in him slowly, the way a wreck settles into mud—not all at once, but finally, completely. His gaze went from her face to the empty stern to the distant narrows, where the skiff's mast tip was just visible above the chop, tilting.

"You swam that channel," he said. "In this."

"I grew up on these waters. So did you. We just learned different things from them."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—she thought he might order his men to seize them all anyway, consequences and witnesses be damned, because the mask had slipped and underneath it he was simply a man watching twenty years of careful work dissolve in rainwater. His hand actually moved toward the loudhailer.

And then the sea decided. A swell came through the channel mouth, larger than the others, born of the storm far offshore, and the *Kestrel* rolled hard enough to throw the lieutenant against the rail, and somewhere beyond the reef the wind found its second voice, the deep one, the one that meant the monsoon's leading edge had arrived. Barometers all along the coast were falling together, and every professional instinct Aldous Reeve possessed—the instincts that had kept him alive and promoted for thirty years—told him the same thing: get your boat to shelter, clear your channel, survive the next six hours, and settle accounts after.

"After the storm," he said, and it sounded like a promise and a sentence at once. He thrust the decoy case at the lieutenant. "Back to the *Vigilant*. All of you, move." Then, to Mara, as his sailors scrambled for the gangway: "You understand I'll find you. There's nowhere on this coast—"

"There's exactly one place," Mara said, "and you'll be reading about it in the papers."

He left. The gangway came across, the lines were cast off, and the white hull of the patrol boat swung away toward the narrows and its new hazard, its searchlight already sweeping the rain for a piece of aluminum that had once belonged to a woman who no longer existed.

* * *

They ran north.

Priya gave the *Kestrel* everything the old engine had, and the hull slammed and staggered through building seas while the storm came down on them like a lid closing. Ansel appeared at Mara's side at the rail, soaked through, holding a blanket he didn't offer and she didn't take.

"That skiff," he said. "How long did you know?"

"Since the pier," she said. "Since I read the contract terms and realized I was going to need an exit that cost me something. People trust a sacrifice, Ansel. It's the only currency that never bounces." She watched the narrows fall astern, the little mast tip vanishing under the waves, taking the last of the Ferren alias and the last of her mother's money and the last of her escape routes down with it. "My grandfather bought silence with a scuttled ship. I just paid the debt back in the same coin. Smaller ship. Better reasons."

Ahead, through the ragged curtain of rain, the mainland showed itself—a gray smear of hills, and somewhere inside it, a records office with its lights coming on against the early dark, and a journalist who had been waiting twenty years for a story, and a port authority that would open at dawn.

The *Kestrel* groaned beneath them, a long metallic complaint from somewhere near the keel, and Priya's voice came back from the wheelhouse, tight and clear:

"We're taking water again. Forward compartment. And the harbor's still got navy eyes on it—we can't come in the front door."

Ansel looked at Mara, and Mara looked at the sea, and the sea looked back the way it always had: indifferent, enormous, and full of things that had gone down carrying the truth.

"Then we go in the way my family always did," she said. "Through the wreck grounds, in the dark, at the height of the storm. Hold together, *Kestrel*. Just once more."

The old boat answered with a shudder that ran from stem to stern, and drove on into the dark.
