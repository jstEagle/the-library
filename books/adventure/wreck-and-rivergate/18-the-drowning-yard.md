## 18. The Drowning Yard

The boat died the way old boats die—not all at once, but with a kind of tired finality, a groan in the plates that Ansel felt through his boots before he heard it.

They had cleared the reef by a boat's length and a prayer. Priya had the wheel, white-knuckled, running them down the channel with the storm's first real wind flattening the rain sideways. Behind them, Mara's burning skiff was a smear of orange light in the murk, and beyond that, invisible but present as a hand on the neck, Reeve's patrol boat was coming through the gap she'd made and then closed.

Then the engine note changed. Not sputtered—*changed*, dropped half a tone, and every salvage diver alive knows that sound. Ansel was moving before the thought finished forming.

"Engine room," he said. "She's making water fast."

The engine room was a nightmare of spray and shadow. The bilge pump was running, but the water was coming in faster than it could be thrown out—a seam had opened along the port side, low, where the old patch job from the cove had finally surrendered to the storm's pounding. The leak wasn't a leak anymore. It was a wound.

"Plug kit," Ansel shouted over the engine. "Priya, keep her headed into the chop—we slow down, she dies faster."

He worked with the plug kit and a canvas collision mat, kneeling in rising water that was already over his shins and cold as a grave. The seam had opened along three feet of plating. He got the mat over it, braced it with a spreader, and bought them minutes. Minutes, not hours. He could feel the hull flexing under him with each wave, the whole boat working like a jaw with a broken tooth.

He surfaced from the engine room into chaos. Rain like nails. Priya at the wheel, shouting something he couldn't hear. The mast groaning. And ahead, through the gray curtain, the pale line of the shallows off Cutter's Point—sand and coral rising fast, no channel through.

"She won't make the harbor," Priya screamed at him. "She's sitting lower every minute. We've got maybe ten minutes before she goes under us."

Ansel looked at the wheelhouse, at the chart table where the strongbox sat lashed down under oilskin, at the letters inside it—his father's handwriting, Tomas's testimony, twenty years of drowned truth—and felt the arithmetic settle in his chest with terrible clarity.

The boat was lost. It had been lost since the cove, maybe since the day he'd signed Mara's contract. But the evidence was not lost, and he was not lost, and there was one thing a salvage diver knew how to do better than anyone alive.

He could work a sinking wreck.

"Get her as close to the sand as you can," he said. "Then everyone off. I'm going down with her."

Priya stared at him. "You're insane."

"I'm a salvage diver," he said, and heard his father's ghost in the words. "This is the job."

* * *

The shallows off Cutter's Point came up fast—three fathoms, then two, then the sickening grind of the keel kissing sand. Priya ran her aground as gently as a boat can be run aground in a monsoon, which is to say not gently at all, and the impact threw them all against the wheelhouse bulkhead.

"Off, all of you," Ansel said. "Dinghy. Take the flares. Get to the fishing camp on the point—Tomas knows the way. I'll be right behind you."

"You'd better be," Priya said, and there were tears in her eyes that the rain made impossible to see, and then she was gone, over the side and into the dinghy with the others, and Ansel was alone on a sinking ship with the storm and the strongbox.

He got his gear on in the wheelhouse, working by feel and memory—tank, harness, mask, the dive knife at his belt. The strongbox came free of its lashings. It was heavier than sin and twice as stubborn, a navy-sealed box with a hasp that had defeated three attempts to open it cleanly. He'd need the cutting gear, and the cutting gear was in the engine room, which was already under water to the workbench.

Of course it was.

He slung the box across his back with the harness, cinched it down, and went over the side into the black churning water.

The storm had turned the sea into a fist. Visibility was nothing—two feet, maybe three, in the churned-up shallows. He swam for the engine room hatch, found it by counting: the boat was lying on a list to port, so the hatch was down and to his left, and he pulled himself along the hull by feel until his glove found the frame.

Inside was worse. The engine room was a cathedral of chaos—water surging with the boat's motion, tools drifting like dead fish, the workbench where the cutting torch lived already half-submerged and swinging loose from its brackets. He caught it, dragged it to the strongbox, and worked in the dark with his hands and his rage.

The torch wouldn't light. The striker was fouled. He cleared it, tried again, and got a stuttering flame that the water kept trying to drown. He cut the hasp by feel, sparks swirling away in the black water like dying stars, and when the hasp parted the box fell open and the oilskin packet inside was intact.

Dry. His father's letters, dry.

He got the packet inside his suit, against his chest, and that was when the boat moved.

Not a groan this time. A shift—the whole hull settling deeper onto the sand, rolling with a swell that came through even here in the shallows, and the engine room hatch, which had been above him, was suddenly not. The boat was going down, and it was going down *now*, and Ansel was inside it.

He kicked for the hatch. The hull rolled again, and something heavy—a spare tank, the tool rack, he never knew—came off the bulkhead and caught him across the shoulder, spinning him, driving the air from his lungs in a burst of bubbles. He grabbed for the hatch frame and missed. Grabbed again. Caught it.

The hatch was closing. Not closing—*sinking*, the frame rising past him as the boat settled, the opening that had been an exit becoming a ceiling becoming a memory. He pulled himself through it with the last of his strength, and then he was out, into the churn, into the storm, with the boat rolling over above him like a whale dying.

Something caught his ankle.

He looked down. A line—the dinghy's painter, or a halyard, something from the rigging, whipping in the current, and it had wrapped him twice around the boot and was pulling him back toward the wreck. The boat was settling onto the sand in ten feet of water, and the line was anchored to something aboard, and the storm was pushing him and pulling him and he had maybe a minute of air and no knife free hand and—

The knife. His belt. He drew it and cut, once, twice, blind, sawing at line he couldn't see, and the third stroke parted it and he shot upward into the rain.

He broke the surface gasping, and the first thing he saw was his boat going down.

* * *

She went slowly, in the way that boats do when they die in shallow water—settling rather than sinking, the wheelhouse going under last, the mast leaning, leaning, and then the water closing over the top of it with a sound like a held breath released. The storm hammered the spot where she'd been. Rain on water, nothing more.

Ansel treaded water and watched, and something in his chest that wasn't grief and wasn't relief and wasn't anything with a clean name turned over and went still.

He'd lived on that boat for eleven years. He'd learned to dive off her deck. He'd patched her hull with his own hands a dozen times, cursed her engines, slept in her bunk with the strongbox hidden beneath the mattress. She had carried him through every season of his adult life, and now she was a shape under the gray water, and he was treading water above her with his father's letters against his heart.

"Right behind you," he said to Priya, to the boat, to nobody. "Told you."

Then he swam.

The fishing camp on the point was a quarter mile of the worst swimming of his life—through chop that came at him like fists, through rain that made the air itself hard to breathe. Twice a wave drove him under and he came up coughing, and both times he thought about the line around his ankle, about how close the arithmetic had come to not working, and both times he put his head down and swam anyway.

The letters were dry. That was the thought he swam on. Whatever else the storm took, whatever else he lost—eleven years of boat, the last of his money, maybe the last of his luck—the letters were dry, and his father's voice was still in the world, and he was going to carry it the last quarter mile if it killed him.

The beach, when it came, was a mercy. Sand under his hands, then his knees, then his whole body, and he lay there in the rain with the packet clutched to his chest and breathed.

"Ansel."

Tomas's voice. Hands under his arms, hauling him up the beach, and then Priya's voice, and the others', and the small orange glow of the camp's storm lantern under its shelter. They got him under the lean-to and someone put a blanket around his shoulders and someone else—Priya—pressed a tin cup of something hot into his hands.

"You're bleeding," she said. "Your shoulder."

"It'll keep."

"Ansel—"

"It'll *keep*, Priya." He looked at the packet, still inside his suit, and began the slow work of peeling the suit off without letting the rain touch the letters. "We need to move. Reeve's boat went through the channel. He'll be at the harbor by now, and he'll have the port authority sealed up tight by morning if we're not there first."

"The harbor's twenty miles," Tomas said. "In this?"

"The records office is on the mainland side. There's the coast road." Ansel looked up, and found Tomas's eyes in the lantern light—old eyes, calm eyes, eyes that had seen a ship scuttled twenty years ago and had run from it ever since. "You said you had a truck."

Tomas's mouth twitched. "I said I had a *tractor*."

"Will it start?"

"It's a tractor," Tomas said. "It always starts. That's the whole point of tractors." He was already moving, pulling a tarp from under the lean-to. "But the coast road floods at the culvert in this rain. We'd be driving into water over the axles."

"Then we drive into water over the axles." Ansel got the packet into a waterproof bag, got the bag inside his jacket, and stood. His legs shook. His shoulder screamed. He ignored both. "Reeve has had twenty years to bury this. I'm not giving him one more night."

Priya stepped in front of him. Rain ran off her hood. Behind her, the two crewmen who'd stayed—Rafe and Osei—looked at each other, and Rafe shook his head slowly, and Osei looked at the ground.

"Ansel," Priya said quietly. "The boat's gone. The storm's still building. Reeve's men will be watching the coast road—we saw the patrol truck at the last checkpoint, they'll have it covered. And you're half-drowned with a bad shoulder." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I'm not saying don't go. I'm saying *how*."

It was Tomas who answered. The old fisherman had come back from the shed with the tarp over his arm and something else in his hand—a battered oilskin pouch, tied with cord.

"There's a way," he said. "The old oyster beds. My father worked them, and his father before him. There's a track through the marsh that doesn't show on any chart, and it comes out on the mainland side behind the fish market—two hundred yards from the records office." He held out the pouch. "But the marsh track floods at the culvert too. And there's a man at the fish market who owes me a favor, and the favor is twenty years old, and I've never called it in."

"Call it in," Ansel said.

Tomas looked at the pouch in his hand, and then at Ansel, and something moved across his face like weather.

"It's not a favor I can call," he said. "It's a favor I have to *give*. The man is Aldous Reeve's harbor master. And twenty years ago, when the ship went down, he was the one who rowed me out of the cove and told me to run and never come back." He tied the pouch to his belt. "He doesn't know I'm alive. Tonight, he finds out."

The rain came down harder. Somewhere out in the dark, the storm found its full voice, and Ansel stood in it with his father's letters against his chest and his boat going soft under the water behind him, and felt the night pivot around him like a keel coming about.

"Then we go now," he said. "All of us. Before the culvert floods."

* * *

They went.

The marsh track was exactly as bad as Tomas had promised—water over the axles, the tractor's headlights carving yellow tunnels through the rain, Priya and the others bailing with buckets when the cab leaked. Ansel rode in the back with the packet inside his jacket and his hand on it the whole way, and watched the lightning walk along the horizon toward the harbor, and counted the miles by the thunder's delay.

Two miles out. One. The culvert came and went, water boiling over the track, the tractor wallowing through with its engine screaming, and on the far side the land rose and the lights of Rivergate harbor appeared through the rain like a scatter of coins at the bottom of a gray sea.

The fish market was dark. The harbor master's office was not—a single light burning in the window, and a figure in it, and when Tomas knocked, the door opened on a man Ansel had never seen but recognized instantly from the set of his shoulders: a man who had been carrying something for twenty years.

"You," the harbor master said to Tomas. His voice cracked on the single syllable.

"Me," Tomas said. "And I've brought you the end of it, Deniz. The letters. The manifest. Everything. And a boy who's been carrying his father's name like a stone." He stepped aside, and Ansel stood in the doorway with the rain streaming off him, and held out the waterproof bag.

"I need to get into the records office," Ansel said. "Tonight. Before Captain Reeve seals it."

The harbor master—Deniz—looked at the bag for a long moment. Then at Ansel's face, and Ansel watched him see it: the resemblance, the jaw, the eyes, twenty years arriving all at once.

"Elias's boy," Deniz said quietly.

"Yes."

Deniz stepped back from the door. Behind him, the records office corridor stretched away, lit by a single emergency lamp, and at the far end of it a door stood ajar, and through the door's gap Ansel could see the records office itself—shelves, filing cabinets, the port authority's seal on the wall.

And standing at the filing cabinets, coat still dripping, warrant folder under his arm, was Captain Aldous Reeve.

Reeve turned. Their eyes met through the gap in the door, across twenty years and forty meters of black water, and Ansel's hand went to the bag at his chest.

"Too late," Reeve said, almost gently. "Or perhaps," and his eyes went from Ansel to the bag to the old man in the doorway whose face had gone the color of ash, "perhaps exactly on time."

He stepped into the corridor. Behind him, through the office window, the first gray light of dawn was breaking over the harbor—over the shallows where Ansel's boat lay drowned, over the channel where Mara's skiff still burned, over the whole drowning yard of a night that had cost everything and was not yet finished costing.

Ansel did not step back.

"Captain," he said, and his voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "You're going to want to read these letters."
