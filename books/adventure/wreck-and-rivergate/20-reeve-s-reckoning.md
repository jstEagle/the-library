## 20. Reeve's Reckoning

The tribunal room smelled of old varnish and newer fear.

Priya had never been inside the port authority building before—she'd spent her whole career on the water side of its windows, watching officials in pressed uniforms decide things about boats they'd never sailed—and now she sat on a bench of polished hardwood with salt still crusted in the seams of her jacket, and she understood that the room was built to make people feel exactly the way she felt: small, damp, and temporary. The ceiling was high enough to hold echoes. The windows were tall enough to show the harbor, and the harbor was gray under a sky that couldn't decide whether the monsoon was finished with them or not.

Captain Aldous Reeve sat at the long table below the dais, and he did not look like a man who had lost anything yet.

That was the thing that unsettled her most, in those first minutes. She had expected him diminished—expected the uniform to hang wrong on him, expected his hands to be unquiet, expected some visible seam where the life he'd built had split open. Instead he sat straight-backed, cap squared on the table before him like an exhibit, and listened to the investigating officer read the charges in a voice like someone reciting tide tables. Dereliction of duty. Obstruction of a civilian investigation. Abuse of naval authority in the matter of the vessel *Corvane*, sunk twenty-two years ago with eleven souls aboard.

Eleven souls. Priya counted them silently against what Tomas had told them in the cove, that night of lamplight and fish-smoke, when the old man's voice had cracked for the first time in her hearing. Eleven. He'd named every one. He'd been naming them for twenty years, alone, to no one.

Reeve's face did not change as the charges were read. He watched the investigating officer the way Ansel watched weather coming over open water—not afraid, just measuring.

Priya looked away from him and found Mara Voss three rows down, sitting alone, spine straight as a mast step. Mara wore a charcoal coat Priya had never seen before, something bought for this, something that cost more than the *Kestrel's* engine rebuild, and her hair was pinned back off her face in a way that made her look older and, strangely, more like her brother—the Voss jaw, the Voss eyes, the family written plainly on her for the first time since Priya had known her. For six years, Mara Voss had been "Mara Chen" on every manifest and mooring contract in Rivergate. Priya had learned the truth in a storm-lit cabin, along with everyone else, and had said nothing then either. It seemed to be her function in this story: to notice, and to hold it.

Today the name on the witness roster read *M. VOSS*, in full, in ink.

* * *

They had come back through the reef channel nine days ago, half-drowned, boatless, carrying a strongbox and a dead man's letters in oilcloth, and Priya remembered thinking that the hardest part was over. That was a diver's mistake, and she wasn't even a diver. The hardest part was the waiting afterward—the days when the evidence went into port authority custody and disappeared into the same machinery that had buried it once already, and Ansel Yarrow sat on the dock at the salvage yard with his hands empty and his eyes fixed on the water, saying nothing, doing nothing, which for Ansel was louder than shouting.

It was Priya who had gone to the journalist. Not because anyone asked her to. Because she had stood in the records office lobby while Ansel handed over the letters, and she had seen the way the clerk's face went carefully blank—the face of a man deciding whether to put something in a drawer or send it up the chain—and Priya had spent her whole life around men who put things in drawers. So she found the journalist's card in Ansel's jacket, the one from Mara's arrangement, and she walked to the print office on Cordwainer Street in her salt-stiff clothes and said, "You should know there's a witness. He's alive. He saw it happen."

Tomas Ilic had refused, at first. Had stood in the doorway of his cove hut with his arms crossed like a man guarding a door that had kept him safe for two decades, and said, "I ran. Running men don't get to testify." It had taken Ansel three visits and a bottle of the cheap plum brandy Tomas liked, and finally it hadn't been argument at all—it had been Ansel saying quietly, "My father wrote to you from every port. You're the last person he trusted. If you stay quiet, Reeve wins twice." And Tomas had looked at the sea for a long time and then gone inside and come out with a seabag full of letters he'd kept for twenty-two years, every one of them, and said, "All right. But I'm not wearing shoes in your courtroom."

He wasn't wearing shoes today either. Priya could see them—his bare feet, broad and scarred, tucked under the bench in front of him—and somehow they were the most honest thing in the room.

* * *

The first witness was a retired harbormaster who testified about the *Corvane's* insurance filings with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own eulogy. The second was a naval records clerk whose hands shook so badly she dropped her notes twice, and who confirmed, in a voice barely above the sound of the rain starting against the high windows, that the scuttle order in the recovered manifest bore an authorization signature matching Captain Reeve's mentor, Vice-Admiral Corliss Hale—dead four years now, honored in bronze on the naval college wall, and posthumously about to become the most discussed corpse in Rivergate.

Reeve watched all of it without moving.

Then the presiding officer called, "Mara Voss," and the room changed temperature.

Priya felt it happen—a shift in the benches, a rustle, heads turning not toward the witness table but toward each other, the name passing mouth to mouth like contraband. *Voss.* Everyone in Rivergate over forty knew what the name meant: the shipping family that had built half the waterfront and lost it in a fraud case so old it had softened into rumor, the lawsuit that had broken them, the daughter who had vanished into another name and another trade. Priya watched a man in the third row lean toward his neighbor and whisper, and watched the neighbor's eyebrows climb, and understood that half the room had come for the navy scandal and was getting a second story free.

Mara walked to the table like she was walking a deck in a swell—balanced, unhurried, giving nothing to the motion.

"State your name for the record."

"Mara Ellery Voss."

"And you previously conducted business under—" the officer checked his papers, and something in his tone suggested he disapproved of aliases on principle, "—the name Chen."

"I did." Her voice carried clean to the back benches. "For six years. After my family's name became worth less than the paper it was sued on. I'd ask the record to reflect that I hid from a reputation, not from a charge. There's a difference, and this inquiry ought to know it better than most."

Someone near Priya exhaled a laugh and smothered it. The presiding officer did not smile.

They took her through it slowly. The contract with the journalist, Ovid Renner—the one she confessed to Ansel in the cabin while the storm leaned on the hull. The original commission: recover documents from the *Corvane* wreck, deliver them for publication, with the Voss family's complicity in the old shipping fraud scrubbed from the story. Priya watched her say it out loud, in public, under oath, and marveled at what it cost her—not in her face, which gave nothing, but in her hands, which lay flat on the table and did not move at all, the stillness of a woman holding herself down.

"You intended to suppress material relevant to a criminal inquiry," the officer said. "Material concerning your own family."

"Yes."

"Why should this panel believe anything else you say?"

And here Mara paused, and Priya saw her look—not at the panel, not at Reeve, but back toward the benches, one heartbeat, toward where Ansel sat with his arms folded and his jaw set—and when she turned back her chin had come up a degree.

"Because I came here under my real name to testify against a captain who offered me money to disappear, and I turned him down. Because everything I planned to hide is going to be in tomorrow's paper anyway, with my name attached, because I stopped running. And because"—and here her voice did something Priya had never heard it do, something almost gentle—"a man died on that ship trying to bring the truth in, and his son pulled it out of the water with his bare hands. I'm not going to sit in this room and be the second lie he has to survive."

The rain thickened against the windows. Nobody whispered after that.

* * *

Reeve testified in the afternoon.

Priya had imagined it a hundred ways during the sleepless nights—him raging, him denying, him cracking open like the *Kestrel's* hull—but none of her versions matched the man who rose, buttoned his jacket, and answered every question with the patience of a tutor explaining arithmetic to slow children. Yes, he had boarded the salvage vessel. Yes, he had questioned the crew. No, he had not ordered them followed; he had "maintained a presence" during a period of concern over restricted wreck sites. When the officer read aloud the offer he'd made Ansel—salvage commission, clean record, the father's name left resting—Reeve didn't flinch.

"I made a reasonable proposal to a distressed young man," he said. "Grief makes people reckless. I've seen it sink crews. I offered him a future."

"You offered him silence about a murder."

"I offered him context." And then Reeve did the thing Priya would remember for the rest of her life, the thing that made her understand the beat was deeper than any of them had sounded: he turned, deliberately, and looked at the benches—at Ansel, at Tomas's bare feet, at her—and said, with something that in a kinder man might have been sorrow, "Elias Yarrow was going to destroy a fleet, a hundred jobs, and the stability of this coast, for a principle. The men who stopped him believed they were saving Rivergate. I have protected their decision for twenty-two years because someone has to carry the ugly work quietly, or nothing holds. You all sail on what we bury. Every one of you. You're welcome."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water. Priya realized her fists were clenched and made herself let go, finger by finger.

Because that was the horror of it—that he meant it. That somewhere in twenty-two years of quiet ugliness, Aldous Reeve had built himself a story where he was the load-bearing wall, and he had never once doubted the architecture. Justice outside the law, he'd have called it, if he'd called it anything. Priya thought of Tomas in his cove, twenty years of self-exile, calling himself a coward; and Reeve in his whites, calling himself a pillar; and understood that men could drown in opposite directions.

The presiding officer's gavel came down like a hatch closing. "Captain Reeve, effective immediately you are suspended from duty pending the findings of this inquiry. Your command is transferred. You will surrender your sidearm and your access credentials at the close of these proceedings."

For the first time, something moved behind Reeve's face. Not fear. Calculation, finishing, arriving at a number he didn't like. He picked up his cap, and as he turned to go his eyes swept the room one last time—an officer taking a final count of his deck—and they rested on Ansel for a moment, and whatever passed between them was private, and cold, and unfinished.

Priya wrote in her notebook, without quite deciding to: *he doesn't think he's done.*

* * *

Outside, the rain had settled into the steady monsoon rhythm that meant it intended to stay for hours. The crowd spilled down the authority steps under umbrellas and newspapers, and the journalists were already working—Renner among them, sleeves rolled, dictating to a boy with a pencil, and Priya caught the shape of tomorrow's headline forming in his mouth: *Hale, Reeve, and the Ship They Sank.*

Ansel stood at the bottom of the steps in the rain without an umbrella, letting it hit him, and Priya went and stood beside him because someone should and because she'd learned, this season, that Ansel Yarrow accepted care best when it pretended to be coincidence.

"Tomas testified?" he asked.

"For an hour. Named all eleven again. The stenographer couldn't keep up." She hesitated. "He cried at the end. Just once. Said, 'Elias, they're listening.' Then he apologized to the panel and finished the list."

Ansel nodded slowly. Rain ran off his chin. Down the steps, Mara was surrounded by reporters, and Priya watched her handle them—watched her give them exactly enough, the Voss name spoken evenly, the confession about Renner's original terms owned outright, no spin, no scrubbing—and saw the moment one of the older reporters recognized her, really recognized her, and said something too low to hear, and Mara smiled like a woman setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"The case reopens Monday," Ansel said. "The fraud. Hale's estate, the insurance syndicate, all of it. Twenty years of settlements built on a scuttled ship—they'll be unwinding it for years. Lawyers are already circling the harbor like gulls."

"Does it feel like winning?"

He considered it honestly, which was the thing she'd always liked best in him, underneath all the armor. "No. It feels like surfacing. Everything's too bright and my ears won't clear and I can't tell yet if I'm alive or not." A pause. "But the truth's topside. That part's done. Whatever they build out of it now is theirs."

Then Mara detached from the reporters and came down the steps to them, rain spotting her expensive coat, and she was wearing an expression Priya had never seen on her—unguarded, almost young.

"They've reopened it," Mara said. "The Voss case. Which means the estate seizure reverses, eventually, if the findings go our way—which means—" She stopped herself, laughed shortly at herself. "Twenty years ago my family's name was worth a fleet. This morning I testified under it in front of the whole coast, and I keep waiting to feel ruined, and instead I feel—" She shook her head. "Lighter than I have any right to."

"You sold yourself pretty hard in there," Ansel said. "Owed us nothing."

"I owed your father everything, actually. I just found that out this season." She glanced between them, and the old wariness flickered back for a moment, the rival's reflex, and then she mastered it. "There's something else. The yard on Cutter's Row—the derelict one, Harrow's old place. It's coming up for sale. Insurance settlement money's going to be moving through this town for months, and I've been doing sums." She held Ansel's eyes. "Your father sketched that yard in his letters. Tomas told me. I want in, Yarrow. Partners. Don't answer now—answer when you've slept."

She walked off into the rain before he could refuse, which Priya suspected was the entire strategy.

Ansel stared after her. Then he looked down at his own hands, spread open at his sides, palms up, catching rain—as if checking whether they still belonged to a man who moved, or could belong to one who stayed.

"Priya," he said at last, without turning. "The Kestrel's gone. The crew's scattered—Dov and Rens walked, and I don't blame them. I've got a settlement check, a dead father's letters, and a rival offering me a home." His voice was quiet under the rain. "Tell me honestly. If I stop—if I actually stop moving—what do I do with all this?"

Priya looked at him—this man she'd followed through storms and wrecks and a season that had nearly killed them all—and she grinned, because the answer had been sitting in plain sight for weeks.

"Same thing you do underwater," she said. "Salvage."

Down the row, past the dripping eaves of the authority building, the derelict yard on Cutter's Row stood gray in the rain, its cranes rusting, its slipways silted—and in the doorway of the old office, faint through the weather, someone had already hung a lamp.
