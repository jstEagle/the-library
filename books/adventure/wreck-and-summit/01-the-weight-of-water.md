## 1. The Weight of Water

The quarry gave up its dead slowly, and Oscar Vane had learned to work at the pace of the water.

Forty feet down, the flooded pit was a cathedral of green murk, its walls terraced where the blasting machines had cut them decades ago, now furred with algae that swayed like the hair of drowned things. Her lamp cut a cone through it all, and in the cone floated silt, diesel sheen, the occasional startled perch. Below her, on the quarry floor, lay the reason anyone paid her to be here: a dump truck, nose-down, half-swallowed by the soft grey clay that every body of water in this country eventually became.

She'd been on it for two hours. The winch cable from the crane barge above hung slack beside her, waiting for her to set the chokers under the chassis, and the truck's cab window — a black square of glass gone opaque with growth — stared up at her like an eye that had stopped believing anyone would come.

Oscar worked the wrench with gloved hands, unhurried, precise. That was the thing people never understood about salvage diving: it wasn't bravery. Bravery was for emergencies. This was bookkeeping. You went down, you did the arithmetic of weight and current and air, you came back up, and if you were good, nobody ever had to think about what you'd done. The best dives were the ones that left no story behind.

Her gauge read 1,400 psi. Plenty. She checked it anyway, because checking was the discipline, and discipline was the only inheritance from her mother she'd kept.

*Don't trust the number. Trust the check.*

Nine years since she'd heard that voice out loud, and it still ran the checklist in her head like a foreman who never slept.

The second choker seated with a clunk she felt more than heard, transmitted up through her bones and the water and the steel. She gave the line three tugs — *ready* — and felt the answer come back down through the cable before she saw it move: the slow, patient tension of the crane taking up the load. The truck groaned against the clay, sucking free with a sound like a mouth opening, and a bloom of silt swallowed everything, white-out conditions, zero visibility, the moment when amateurs panicked and professionals simply hung in the dark and counted.

Oscar counted. She'd gotten very good, over ten years, at hanging in the dark and counting.

When the cloud settled enough to see her gauges again, the truck was rising above her, trailing clay like a shed skin, and she rode the line up behind it into the light.

* * *

The surface was November cold and the kind of grey sky that made noon look like dusk. The salvage outfit — Kessler Marine Recovery, four barges, one rusting crane, a payroll that arrived late but arrived — worked the quarry under a contract to clear submerged equipment before the county turned the pit into a reservoir. Nobody's treasure. Nobody's history. Just iron and mud and invoices, which suited Oscar fine.

"Vane." Kessler himself stood on the barge deck, a squat man built like something meant to be moored permanently, holding two thermos cups. He handed her one. "Truck came up clean."

"They usually do."

"You always say that."

"It's always true."

He grunted, which from Kessler was applause. She drank the coffee — terrible, scalding, perfect — and watched the truck swing over the water and settle onto the flatbed with a clang that shivered up through the barge plates and into her boots. Around them the crew moved with the loose efficiency of men who'd worked together long enough to stop talking. They didn't ask her questions anymore. In her first week they'd tried — where she'd learned, who she'd dived for, whether it was true she'd once pulled a locomotive out of a river gorge — and she'd answered each question with exactly enough truth to end the conversation. *Here and there. Various outfits. It was smaller than the stories.* After a while they'd learned that Oscar Vane was a woman who surfaced with the work done and no story attached, and they'd stopped asking, and she had made herself comfortable inside that silence the way other people made themselves comfortable inside a house.

It hadn't started as a policy. It had started as grief, and then it had become a craft.

That evening, in the bunkhouse — a converted shipping container with a space heater that sounded like a man clearing his throat forever — she sat at the little bolted-down table with a cup of tea going cold beside her and did the other work. The quiet work. The work nobody paid for.

A folder, thin cardboard, soft at the corners from handling. Inside it, her life reduced to paper: a certified diver's card, a first-aid certificate, a letter of reference from a marine contractor in a port town she'd left in a hurry, a photograph of her father she allowed herself to keep because it contained no information anyone could use.

One by one, she went through the documents the way she'd gone through the truck's chokers. Methodical. Unhurried.

The reference letter mentioned her full name — *Oscar Iris Vane* — and she took her pen and drew a single line through the middle name, then thought about it, and blotted the ink so thoroughly the word became a smudge, then nothing. Her mother's name lived in enough databases already. There was no reason to hand strangers the connection. Dr. Iris Vane, whose name appeared in journals, in conference programs, in the acknowledgments of books Oscar refused to read, sitting on shelves in houses she refused to enter. A famous woman. An important woman. A woman whose daughter had spent a decade becoming unfindable, one crossed-out record at a time.

The dive certification listed her address as a post office box in a town she'd departed eight months ago. She wrote to the certifying agency twice a year, paying small fees, requesting corrections, keeping herself a moving target on paper. It was astonishing, she'd found, how much of a person could be dissolved with patience. Not destroyed — dissolved. Like sugar in water. Still there, technically. Just nowhere you could put a finger on it.

Teo would have said that was sad. No — Teo Reyes didn't exist yet; he was nine years in the future, a botanist she hadn't met, asking kind questions she couldn't imagine wanting to answer. But someone like him would have said it was sad, and Oscar would have told him what she told everyone:

It's not hiding. It's tidying.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out somewhere around year four, floating in some other green water in some other country: you could not outrun a shadow by moving fast. Shadows moved with you. The only way was to shrink the light. Fewer names, fewer addresses, fewer photographs, fewer threads connecting you to the world — until the day the shadow had nothing left to hold onto.

Except it still held. That was the joke she didn't laugh at. Every quarry, every wreck, every black-water dive, she carried the shadow down with her, forty feet or eighty or a hundred, and it fit perfectly, because it had been tailored to her at birth.

Her father had understood. Her father, who had been a lockkeeper on a canal nobody important used, who had fixed gates and logged water levels and died of his heart in a season when his wife was opening a tomb on another continent. Oscar had been twenty-three. She had sent the telegram — *COME HOME, DAD IS DYING* — and waited eleven days, and her mother had sent back a reply that Oscar could still recite in the dark, which she sometimes did, as a form of self-harm: *Site is at critical phase. Cannot leave. Will explain later. Tell him I love him.* Later had never come. The explanation had never come. The funeral had happened with one empty chair in the front row, and Oscar had looked at that chair and understood that her mother would choose the dig every time, in every possible life, and that the only way to survive being the daughter of such a woman was to become a woman who needed nothing from anyone.

So: the folder. The crossed-out names. The moving target. Ten years of it, and she was good at it now, genuinely good, the way she was good at setting chokers blind in silt. Restless competence, Kessler called it, when he called it anything. She called it the only prayer she knew.

The space heater cleared its throat. Outside, wind moved across the quarry water, and somewhere far off a train horn sounded, low and lonesome, heading somewhere without her.

She was re-inking the blotter over the last trace of her mother's name when the knock came.

* * *

Nobody knocked on the bunkhouse door after dark except Kessler, and Kessler didn't knock, he kicked. So the knock itself was the first wrong thing, and Oscar registered it the way she registered a shifted current underwater — instantly, silently, her whole body reclassifying the situation.

She opened the door with her foot ready to shove it shut again.

The kid from the office — Danny, seventeen, acne and enthusiasm — stood on the gravel path holding an envelope the way you'd hold a live fish. "Telegram," he said. "Operator tried the phone all afternoon. Said this one's marked urgent-urgent, so." He shrugged apologetically, as though the urgency were somehow his fault, and held it out.

Urgent-urgent. The double marking. It had a specific smell, that phrase. It belonged to hospitals and coroners and lawyers. Oscar took the envelope and her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady, that was the whole point of her, but underneath the steadiness something old and cold turned over, the way the truck had turned over in the clay.

"Dad's dead," she said, before she could stop herself saying it aloud to a seventeen-year-old. "Sorry. Old reflex. Thanks, Danny."

He fled, relieved.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, envelope in hand, letting the cold have her shoulders. Then she went inside, sat down, and opened it with the same care she'd use on a fouled line — because a telegram, like a line, could be worse than it looked, and you wanted to know exactly how much of it was true before you committed your weight to it.

The paper was thin, official, printed in the flat capitals of the telegraph office:

OSCAR VANE STOP REQUIRE SERVICES ONE SEASON STOP ARCHAEOLOGICAL EXPEDITION UPRIVER STOP DIGGING DIVING SALVAGE EXPERIENCE REQUIRED STOP TERMS GENEROUS STOP NO QUESTIONS ASKED STOP REPLY RIVER STATION BOX 44 STOP

No signature. But signatures were for people who might be refused, and there was no person alive who needed to sign a telegram to Oscar Vane, because there was exactly one person in the world who knew she did this work, who knew the trade magazines she occasionally appeared in under initials, who knew the river stations and the salvage outfits and the exact grammar of a hire notice designed to be answered by a stranger.

*Iris Vane*, the telegram said, in the language of a stranger. *Iris Vane needs a digger.*

Oscar read it four times. The heater cleared its throat. The tea had gone fully cold, and she drank it anyway, because wasting things was another inheritance, the good kind.

No questions asked. That was the part that snagged her, the hook under the gill. Because her mother was not a no-questions person. Her mother was a woman who had interrogated a customs officer for forty minutes over a mislabeled crate of trowels. Her mother believed questions were the highest form of respect. *You ask questions of what matters,* she used to say, kneeling in some trench, holding a potsherd up to the light like a priest with a relic. *What you don't question, you don't love.*

And now: NO QUESTIONS ASKED. Which meant one of two things. Either Iris Vane was so desperate she'd abandoned her own religion — or she was afraid, and fear was a thing Oscar had never once seen her mother wear, not at funerals, not at collapses, not at the tribunal where a government had tried to strip her permits and she'd dismantled them argument by argument like a woman taking apart a clock.

Oscar laid the telegram flat on the table, next to the folder with its dissolved names, next to the cold tea, next to the photograph of her father smiling on the lock gate with his sleeves rolled up.

One season. One dig. One chance to stand in the same mud as the woman who'd missed the funeral, and ask — no. Not ask. Answer. Answer with competence, with a set choker, with a clean dive, with the only language they'd ever shared, which was work. Go upriver, do the job, take the generous terms, and leave before anything as dangerous as honesty could happen.

Or burn the telegram, finish the contract, request a transfer to some colder water, and keep dissolving.

Outside, the train horn sounded again, farther now, going where she wasn't.

Oscar Vane sat in the throat-clearing dark of the bunkhouse and realized, with the particular horror of a professional recognizing her own error, that she had already decided. Somewhere between the second reading and the fourth, her hands had decided — the hands that tied knots, that crossed out names, that had held her father's at the end while her mother held a trowel. The hands had said yes.

All that remained was the paperwork.

She took out a fresh sheet, and in the flat capitals the telegraph office would understand, she began her reply — and paused, pen hovering, over the one line that mattered, the line that would tell her mother, in the language of strangers, that the stranger was coming:

CONFIRM ENGAGEMENT STOP DIGGER EN ROUTE STOP

She signed it with initials only. O.V.

Some shadows, she thought, folding the paper, you don't outrun. You just find out, sooner or later, whether you've been carrying them — or whether they've been carrying you.

The morning steamer left at six. She'd be on it.
