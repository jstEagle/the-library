## 2. Nine Years of Silence

The steamer left Puerto Halcón at dawn, and Oscar spent the first hour of the voyage learning the deck.

Not the ship — the deck crew, the purser, the two missionaries going upriver to a station that had burned down twice and been rebuilt both times by the same stubborn congregation. She learned them the way her mother had taught her to learn a site before touching it: names first, then habits, then the soft places where a person could be moved. The purser, Delgado, took a cut on every crate he didn't weigh properly. The first mate drank his coffee with three sugars and talked to the engine like it was a horse he was trying not to spook. The missionaries were harmless. Nobody was entirely harmless, but the missionaries came closest.

It was useful work, this cataloguing, and she did it because the alternative was standing at the rail watching the river unspool toward the one conversation she had rehearsed for nine years and never once gotten right.

The telegram sat in her breast pocket, folded to the size of a playing card. *NEED DIGGER ONE SEASON STOP NO QUESTIONS ASKED STOP IRIS.* No *dear*, no *Oscar*, no acknowledgment that the last time they'd stood in the same room, Oscar had told her mother she hoped the ground swallowed her. Fourteen words. Iris Vane had published papers with more tenderness in their footnotes.

Oscar took it out anyway, for the hundredth time since the quarry office, and read it again as if the missing words might have grown in overnight.

*No questions asked.* That was the part that had gotten her. Not the money, though the money was real and the salvage outfit's winter contract was not. It was the offer itself — Iris Vane, who had built a career on asking questions until artifacts confessed, offering a season of silence. It was either an apology in the only dialect she spoke, or it was a trap, or it was simply that her mother needed hands and Oscar's were the cheapest ones within telegram range. With Iris you could never rule out the third option. You could rarely rule out anything; you could only stand at the edge of her silences and guess at their depth, the way you'd guess at a flooded shaft by dropping a stone and counting.

She'd counted for nine years.

"First time up?" asked one of the missionaries, a round woman with sunburned forearms and the relentless cheer of someone who had chosen her disappointment in advance.

"No," Oscar said, and went back to the rail.

* * *

The river widened through the morning, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past the boat with their roots combed out like hair. By ten they'd passed the last of the fish farms, by noon the last telegraph pole, and after that the world went green and pre-verbal — forest to the waterline, mist sitting in the valleys like something waiting its turn. A kingfisher detonated off a branch close enough to startle her. Somewhere behind the treeline, unseen, birds argued in voices like rusted hinges.

Oscar leaned on the rail and let the motion of the boat do what motion always did: loosen the sealed rooms in her head.

Her father had died in March, nine years ago. A heart, sudden, between one breath and the next, in the kitchen of the house in Caldera with the radio on and the kettle not yet boiled. She had found him. That fact lived in her body somewhere below language — in the way she still checked kettles, in the way she could not hear a radio staticking between stations without her chest tightening. She had made the calls. She had signed the forms. And then she had called her mother, who was four hundred kilometers away in a tent at a place called Quebrada Honda, where a burial ground was coming out of the earth in layers like the pages of a book, and where Dr. Iris Vane was, at forty-nine, doing the best work of her life.

*Come home,* Oscar had said. Or maybe she'd said *Dad's dead* and nothing else. She honestly couldn't remember anymore which words she'd used. What she remembered was the pause on the line — long enough to hold a whole life in it — and then her mother's voice, level as a surveyor's transit: *The monsoon window closes in six days. If I leave now, the site is lost. Tell me about the funeral when I can hear it.*

That was the sentence. Nine years, and Oscar could still recite it like scripture, every clause load-bearing. *Tell me about the funeral when I can hear it.* As if grief were data. As if her father — Tomás, who had repaired diving regulators at the kitchen table and taught his daughter to hold her breath in the bathtub until the tiles went soft-edged, who had ironed Iris's field shirts because she never learned — as if he were a report she could summarize later.

Iris had come back eleven days after the burial. She had stood at the grave with rain running off her hat brim, said something quiet Oscar hadn't caught, and then gone inside and started inventorying Tomás's tools, because leaving them unsorted was, in her mother's cosmology, a kind of disorder the universe would punish. Oscar had watched her wrap each wrench in newspaper and something in her chest had torn along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"You could have come," Oscar had said.

"I explained why."

"You chose dirt over Dad."

"I chose the only thing I could save." Her mother hadn't raised her voice. She never raised her voice; she lowered everything else around it instead. "You think grief keeps? It doesn't keep, Oscar. Sites don't keep either. I'm sorry about your father. I am. But I will not apologize for arithmetic."

And Oscar — twenty-three, hollowed out, still smelling of the church lilies — had said the thing she'd meant to save for a better moment, or never: "I hope the ground swallows you someday. Then you'll know how it feels to be left."

Then she'd taken the regulator bag, the one with her father's tools, and walked out, and she had been walking out ever since. Quarry to wreck to dredge barge to quarry. Ten years of salvage work under other people's flags, digging things up for men who wanted them sold, keeping her name off manifests, letting the paperwork call her *V., O.* or nothing at all. Erasing herself methodically, room by room, record by record — because if no one could find Oscar Vane, then no one could find the girl who'd said that to her mother, and the girl who'd said it was the only version of herself she couldn't live beside.

The steamer's horn sounded, two notes, and a flock of egrets lifted off a sandbar like a sheet shaken out.

* * *

They tied up that night at a fueling stop, and Oscar climbed to the hurricane deck with a tin cup of coffee she wasn't drinking and watched lightning walk along the horizon over the headwaters, silent, too far for thunder. The same direction she was going.

She tried the apologies again. It was a discipline, almost — she ran them like drills, the way she ran dive checks before entering black water.

*I'm sorry I said what I said.* True but useless; Iris would accept it in four seconds and change nothing, because Iris believed words were receipts for transactions already completed.

*I've thought about what you said, about arithmetic, and you were right.* Also true, some days, on the days when she could look at her own decade of flight and admit that her mother's coldness and her own were cut from the same ore. But saying it would hand Iris the win, and Oscar had discovered she'd rather carry the wound than lose the argument. That was the ugly honest center of it, and she turned it over in the lamplight and put it away again.

*I found him. I want you to know what that was.* This one stopped her breath every time. Because underneath all of it — under the fight, under the silence, under the ten years of unlisted addresses — was the plain fact that her mother did not know. Did not know that Oscar had been the one to find him, that she'd done compressions on the kitchen floor until her arms gave out, that the kettle had boiled dry and filled the house with that smell, that she had sat with him for two hours because she didn't know who you called first when the person you'd call was the person lying on the floor. Iris knew the date. She did not know the kitchen.

Maybe that was the apology that mattered. Not sorry for the words — sorry for the silence that had kept her mother outside that room for nine years, punishing her with ignorance, which was, Oscar could admit at midnight on a moving river, exactly the cruelty she'd accused Iris of committing.

The lightning stepped closer. Thunder arrived late, a long gravelly roll, and the first fat drops hit the hurricane deck.

Oscar tipped the coffee over the rail and went below.

* * *

On the third day the steamer turned into the tributary, and the world narrowed. The banks closed in; the light came down green and underwater-colored; the air thickened until breathing felt like drinking. They passed a floating logging camp, a canoe with four children who shouted at the boat and were answered by the mate's whistle, a dead tapir bloating at the waterline with a wake of butterflies. Twice the pilot slowed where the chart showed nothing at all, studying the water's skin for the submerged snags that could open a hull like a can.

Oscar watched the water and thought about her mother's hands. That was the strange thing memory kept — not the fights, not even the funeral, but the hands. Iris's hands sorting seed potatoes in the garden while explaining sedimentary layers to a seven-year-old. Iris's hands wrapping a splint on Oscar's broken wrist so competently it felt like being loved by a machine. Iris's hands, the night before the steamer to Quebrada Honda, packing instruments into felt rolls, each tool in its loop, everything in order, while the phone rang and rang in the other room and neither of them picked it up because they both knew who it would be and what it would ask.

Her mother had known, Oscar realized suddenly, watching the banks slide past. Standing in that hallway with the felt rolls under her arm, Iris had known she was choosing. It hadn't been obliviousness. It had been a decision made in advance and executed without flinching, and somehow that was worse than carelessness and also, in a way she refused to examine, more forgivable — because her mother had never pretended to be anything other than what she was. A woman who saved what could be saved and grieved on schedule, later, alone, in ink.

The steamer's whistle announced the landing at Kilometer 214, a shelf of mud and planking with a fuel drum, a shack, and a hand-painted sign: *HALCÓN RIVER EXPEDITION — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL.* Authorized by whom, the sign did not say.

Two people waited on the dock. One was a young man in a canvas hat with a clipboard clamped under his arm, vibrating slightly with the effort of appearing official. The other stood at the end of the planks with her hands in the pockets of a field coat gone grey at the elbows, and even from thirty meters, even after nine years, Oscar knew the set of those shoulders the way she knew the pull of a current.

Dr. Iris Vane had gone white at the temples. Otherwise the river had failed to touch her.

Oscar came down the gangway with her duffel on her shoulder and her heartbeat doing something embarrassing, and the two women closed the distance at the same unhurried pace, each refusing to be the one who hurried.

"You're late," Iris said. "Boat was due yesterday."

"River was high. Pilot wouldn't run the narrows at night." Oscar set the duffel down. Up close she could see the new lines around her mother's eyes, the deepened grooves beside her mouth, and the eyes themselves — grey, exact, and carefully not searching Oscar's face, the way you don't stare at a structure you're afraid of collapsing.

"Vane," the young man said brightly, extending a hand. "Teo Reyes, botany. We weren't sure the telegram reached you — Dr. Vane said it would, but the relay at—" He stopped, because both women had looked at him, and whatever they looked like when they did it made him retract the hand and study his clipboard.

"Quarters are the second tent from the latrine," Iris said. "Gear check at six tomorrow. Radio schedule is posted inside the mess. Breakfast is at five-thirty; if you miss it, you wait until lunch." She paused, and for one naked half-second something moved behind the grey eyes — the kitchen, maybe, the kettle, the felt rolls — and then it was gone, shuttered, surveyed, recorded, buried. "Your father's regulator bag. Is that—"

"It's mine now."

"Yes." Her mother nodded slowly, as if confirming a measurement. "Of course it is." She turned to Teo. "Show her the supply manifest. I want the dive inventory reconciled by dark."

And she walked back up the trail toward the camp, boots finding the mud like she'd invented it, coat swinging, spine straight as a ranging rod — and did not look back. Of course she didn't. Looking back was for people who believed the past could be renegotiated. Iris Vane traveled forward only, dragging her dead behind her like ballast she'd weighed and accepted.

Oscar stood on the dock a moment longer, watching her go, the telegram a small stiff rectangle against her heart.

"So," Teo Reyes said, with the doomed cheerfulness of a man stepping between weather fronts. "Tents?"

"Tents," Oscar agreed, and picked up her duffel.

Behind them, far up the darkening tributary, thunder rolled again — closer now, and lower, and it did not move away.
