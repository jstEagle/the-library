## 3. Basecamp Rules

The rules were posted on the mess tent's center pole, typed on a portable typewriter with a failing ribbon so that the letters faded halfway down the page like a voice losing conviction. There were fourteen of them. Oscar read them twice over her first cup of camp coffee, which tasted of river water and scorched tin, and decided that her mother had not softened in nine years.

*Radio check at 0600 and 1800, no exceptions.* *All personnel accounted for at lights-out.* *No artifact leaves the site. No fragment, no shard, no soil sample beyond the survey grid. If it is found here, it stays here.*

That last one was underlined twice, the second stroke tearing slightly through the paper.

Oscar set down her cup. Around her, the camp was waking in the gray half-light — canvas snapping, the generator coughing itself awake somewhere behind the equipment tent, a young man with a clipboard moving between supply crates with the solemnity of a customs officer. The river ran brown and patient thirty meters below the bluff, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets toward the sea without any interest in what people did on its banks.

"You're the replacement."

The voice belonged to a woman in her twenties, freckled sunburn across her nose, holding a laminated card like a badge. "Priya Chandrasekaran. Site surveyor. Dr. Vane wants you at the grid by seven. She said—" Priya hesitated, clearly deciding whether to soften it, then didn't. "She said to tell you she doesn't repeat herself."

"Wouldn't expect her to," Oscar said.

She'd been at basecamp for eighteen hours now, and in that time she and Iris Vane had exchanged perhaps forty words, all of them logistical: where to stow gear, when meals were served, which tent had the bad zipper. It was, Oscar had to admit, an efficient arrangement. Forty words was forty fewer than there were opportunities to say something that couldn't be taken back.

She rinsed her cup in the basin, signed the manifest Priya held out — *O. Vane, salvage diver, temporary* — and went to work.

* * *

By noon she understood why they'd needed a replacement.

The original digger, a man named Okafor, had gone down with a burst appendix two weeks into the season, evacuated by floatplane to a hospital six hundred kilometers away. What he'd left behind was a diving operation cobbled together from optimism: two tanks, one compressor older than most of the crew, a lift bag patched with bicycle-tire rubber, and a site plan drawn in pencil that assumed the floodplain would behave like a swimming pool instead of what it actually was — a drowned landscape, terraces and walls and doorways swallowed whole, current threading through it like breath through lungs.

Oscar stood at the edge of the cutbank and looked down through three meters of tea-colored water at the shadow of a wall. Not rubble. A wall. Dressed stone, courses laid true, running off into the murk farther than her eyes could follow.

"Dr. Vane says you're to map the north face today," said the young man with the clipboard. He'd introduced himself that morning as Teo Reyes, botanist, though he seemed to function as everything else too — radio operator, cook's assistant, general runner. He had a way of standing slightly on his toes, as if the ground might offer something interesting if he leaned toward it. "She says no penetration. No entry through any opening. Survey only."

"I heard the rules."

"Everyone hears the rules." He smiled, quick and unguarded. "Not everyone follows them. Okafor used to say the rules were more like suggestions with consequences."

"And what happened to him?"

"Appendix." Teo shrugged. "But he also once brought up a pot shard in his pocket because he wanted to show his girlfriend, and Dr. Vane made him write a five-page report on chain of custody. In the rain. So." He handed her the depth gauge, its strap cracked with age. "Survey only. I'll be topside."

Oscar pulled on the wetsuit, checked her regulator twice the way she always did — once with her hands, once with her memory of her hands — and went down into the green dark.

The water closed over her like a held breath released. Below the surface the river changed character entirely; the current dropped away, replaced by a cold stillness that pressed against her ears and turned every movement into a slow negotiation. She descended along the line Okafor had anchored, fins barely kicking, and let the wall rise around her.

It was beautiful. That was the thing nobody had warned her about, or maybe everybody had warned her about it and she hadn't believed them. Ten years of salvage work had taught her to see underwater as a ledger — what was recoverable, what was worth the air, what would pay. This wasn't a ledger. This was a city, or the bones of one, lying in the floodplain the way a body lies down when it has finally stopped fighting. Terraces stepped down into the deep. A stairway emerged from the silt, each step furred with algae, ascending toward a doorway that opened onto nothing but packed earth and darkness.

She ran her light along the north face, calling out depths and features to Teo through the comm line, marking measurements on the slate strapped to her wrist. Her hands knew this work. That was the strange mercy of it — that after nine years of avoiding everything her mother touched, her mother's discipline lived in her fingers anyway. Measure twice. Touch nothing you can't account for. Leave the site better documented than you found it. Iris Vane's voice, coming out of Oscar Vane's body, forty meters from Iris Vane herself.

At the doorway she stopped.

The rules said no penetration. The rules were typed on fading paper and posted on a pole. But the doorway breathed — she could feel it, a faint pulse of cooler water pushing out from the dark, rhythmic, like the structure was exhaling on some tide she couldn't see. Somewhere behind that threshold, the floodplain connected to something larger. Caverns, maybe. Channels. A whole hidden architecture of water and stone.

Her hand drifted toward the opening. Stopped.

*No exceptions,* the paper said, in her mother's handwriting-adjacent typeface, and Oscar Vane, who had broken contracts, outrun creditors, and once walked out of a salvage job mid-dive because the outfit lied about the wreck's history, backed away from a doorway because of a piece of paper.

She surfaced twenty minutes later with a full survey of the north face and a feeling in her chest she refused to name.

* * *

Iris met her at the cutbank, kneeling over the slate before Oscar had even gotten her fins off.

"The terrace steps are offset," she said. It wasn't praise. It also wasn't not-praise; with Iris, the absence of criticism had its own temperature. "Okafor never caught the offset. It means the construction phases don't align — someone built over someone else's work." She looked up, and for a moment her eyes were the same gray-brown they'd always been, sharp as a trowel edge. "Where did you learn to read masonry?"

"Same place anyone learns anything useless."

Something moved behind Iris's face — not quite a flinch, more like a door closing quietly in a distant room. She stood, brushed silt from her knees, and became again the expedition leader, the fortress commander. "Lunch is at 1230. Radio check at 1800. Don't be late for either."

She walked back toward the main tent, and Oscar watched her go, hating how easily she could still catalog her mother's movements — the slight favoring of the left knee, old injury, the habit of touching the journal pocket on her hip like a man checking for a wallet in a crowd. Nine years, and her body still kept the inventory.

"You made her stop walking."

Teo had materialized beside her with a towel and two cups of something that claimed to be tea. He held both out, offering her the choice, which struck Oscar as either politeness or a test.

"She stopped herself."

"No." He shook his head, earnest, infuriatingly kind. "People who work for her don't make her stop. I've watched her walk through hailstorms, through a wasp nest, through Marta crying — Marta's our medic, she cries at everything, it's fine — but she does not stop walking when someone's talking to her unless the talking matters." He pushed the cups forward until she had to take one. "So. Where did you learn to read masonry?"

"My mother taught me." Oscar drank the terrible tea. "When I was eleven. Before I decided I hated her."

Teo blinked. Then, to his credit, he laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and Oscar realized with some irritation that she'd made him laugh on purpose, that some part of her had already started performing for this crew's approval, and that the performance had felt good.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said all season," he said. "Come on. Marta wants to look at your hands. She looks at everyone's hands. Don't fight it, it's faster if you don't."

* * *

The days took on a shape, and the shape was her mother's.

0600 radio check, the generator-fed set crackling out its scheduled call to the downriver station, confirmation received, log signed. Supply counts every third day — every crate weighed, every fuel drum dipped and measured, every item entered in a ledger in Iris's small, ruthless handwriting. Work parties out at dawn, back before dusk, no exceptions after the evening storm rolled in off the headwaters and nearly took Priya's tent with it. Lights-out at 2200, and at 2205 Iris walked the perimeter with a flashlight, counting tents the way other people counted sheep.

"It's not paranoia," Teo said one night, sitting beside Oscar on the compressor housing while the river muttered below. "Okay, it's a little paranoia. But she has reasons. Ask her about the 2011 season sometime."

"She won't tell me."

"No," Teo agreed comfortably. "Nobody asks anymore. We just respect the walls." He glanced sideways at her. "You don't, though. You keep looking at them like you're measuring for a door."

Oscar said nothing. Below them, the floodplain lay flat and silvered under a rising moon, hiding its city.

The no-artifacts rule held, and it cost her. On the fourth day she brought up a ceramic fragment the size of her palm — incised, deliberate, a pattern of interlocking spirals that made her think of water thinking about itself. Protocol said photograph in situ, log coordinates, return to the grid. She photographed it. She logged it. And then she sat on the cutbank with it in her hand for a long minute, feeling its weight, its coolness, the way ten centuries fit inside something smaller than a saucer, before she carried it back down and set it exactly where it had lain.

Iris was watching from the survey tent. Oscar felt the gaze between her shoulder blades the whole descent, and neither of them mentioned it, and somehow that felt more honest than anything they'd said aloud.

* * *

On the sixth day, the speed came out.

They were raising the compressor hose when the coupling failed — a corroded fitting, sixty kilos of equipment dropping toward the terrace steps, dragging the air line with it, and Marta on the surface screaming that the line was fouling on something below. Oscar went down after it without planning to, without tanks, on a single breath, following the rope down through the green murk while the hose snaked and whipped above her like something alive.

She found the coupling wedged in a gap between two courses of stone, the air line wrapped twice around a fallen lintel. Working blind, working fast, she unwound it hand over hand, freed the coupling, clipped it to the recovery line, and gave the signal. Sixty kilos rose past her toward the light. She followed it up more slowly, lungs burning, and broke the surface to find the entire crew lined along the cutbank staring at her like she'd grown gills.

Marta reached down and hauled her out by the arm, then immediately began checking her pulse, her eyes, her reflexes, muttering in two languages. Priya said something in another language entirely that made Teo cover his mouth. And Iris —

Iris stood apart, arms crossed, and looked at her daughter for a long moment.

"Free ascent from eight meters," she said finally. "Without a tank. Without a safety diver."

"There wasn't time to—"

"Don't." Iris held up a hand, and Oscar braced for the lecture, the fortress rules recited like scripture, the *this is exactly why we have protocols*. Instead her mother said, quietly: "You waited for the line to clear before you came up. You didn't rush the decompression." A pause. "Who taught you that?"

Oscar wiped river water from her face. "You did. Summer I was twelve. Lake Champlain. You made me sit on the bottom for a full minute before every ascent because you said panic kills more divers than pressure does."

Iris's jaw worked once, twice. Whatever she was going to say, she folded it away, unspent, and turned to the crew. "Back to work. Hose gets inspected daily now. Daily," she repeated, silencing the groan before it formed, and walked toward the survey tent with her measured, knee-favoring stride.

Teo appeared at Oscar's elbow, holding a towel she was beginning to suspect he kept on his person specifically for her.

"Lake Champlain," he said.

"Drop it."

"I'm not dropping anything. I'm collecting. There's a difference." He draped the towel over her shoulders, and his voice gentled in a way that should have been annoying and wasn't, entirely. "For what it's worth — the crew's been waiting to see if you were real. Okafor talked you up before he left. 'Vane's kid, best hands on the river.' Now they've seen it." He smiled, that quick unguarded smile. "You're real."

*I'm not her kid,* Oscar almost said. *I'm just a diver she hired late.* But the lie wouldn't come, and Teo was already walking away, and she stood dripping on the cutbank with a borrowed towel around her shoulders and the uncomfortable recognition that she had spent six days trying not to belong here, and had belonged since roughly the second morning.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep in the heat, she walked the perimeter herself — not checking tents, just moving, the restlessness that had driven her across three continents humming in her legs like a second pulse. At the survey tent, the lamp still burned. Through the canvas she saw her mother bent over the field journal, writing, stopping, writing again, and beside the journal a map she'd never shown the crew: the floodplain, yes, but extending far upriver, past the survey grid, past the mapped tributaries, into terrain marked with a tight, obsessive crosshatching and a single word in Iris's hand.

Oscar couldn't read it from outside. She told herself she didn't try.

She was almost back to her own tent when the radio crackled to life — hours off schedule, past midnight, a voice downriver relaying a message for Dr. Vane. Oscar paused in the dark between tents and heard Iris emerge, heard the low exchange, heard her mother say, very clearly:

"Say that again. Slowly."

Then silence, long enough to have weight, and when Iris spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, controlled, the voice of someone closing shutters before a storm.

"Understood. Log the transmission. Wake me if anything else comes through."

The lamp in the survey tent burned for another hour. Oscar lay in her bunk listening to the river, and to the generator, and to the sound of her mother not sleeping, and told herself it was none of her business.

It was, of course. It had been her business since a telegram arrived in a rented room four hundred kilometers downriver. But she was tired, and the work was good, and belonging — grudging, wary, hard-won — was a currency she'd forgotten how to spend.

In the morning, she learned, there would be a new boat on the river.
