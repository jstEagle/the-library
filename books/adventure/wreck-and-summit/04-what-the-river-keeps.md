## 4. What the River Keeps

The anomaly showed up on the third pass, a smudge of hard return where the floodplain should have read as soft mud all the way down to bedrock.

Oscar saw it before anyone else did, which was a small satisfaction she didn't share. She'd spent the morning running the sonar sled off the skiff in slow transects while Teo Reyes sat cross-legged in the bow with his plant press and his endless questions, and she'd learned the river's voice by then — the way it murmured silt, the way it grumbled over clay, the clean flat silence of stone. This was stone. But not the scattered boulders the flood had dumped over centuries. This was patterned. Regular. Repeating at intervals too even for geology.

"Come look at this," she said.

Teo set down his ferns with the care of a man who treated everything living as fragile, including her, apparently, because he'd been asking since Tuesday whether she was sleeping well, eating enough, "settling in." She'd answered none of it. Now he crowded into the stern beside her and squinted at the screen, and Oscar watched his face do what every field scientist's face did when the data stopped making polite sense: first doubt, then hunger.

"That's not natural," he said.

"Could be."

"It's terraced." He traced the contour lines with one finger, hovering above the glass without touching it, the way you'd hover near something holy or explosive. "Look. Step after step. That's architecture, Oscar. That's *built*."

She said nothing. She was already turning the skiff, already marking coordinates, already feeling the old pull in her chest — the same pull that had put her underwater at nineteen, twenty-two, twenty-five, chasing wrecks and wells and drowned towns for men who paid by the day. Find the thing. Name the thing. She knew this feeling like she knew her own pulse, and she distrusted it accordingly.

The steamer run back to basecamp took forty minutes. By the time they tied off, Teo had told everyone within earshot, and half the crew came down to the bank to look at the printout Oscar carried under her arm like contraband.

Iris Vane was in the survey tent when they brought it to her.

Oscar hadn't spoken more than two hundred words to her mother in nine days. The words had been functional: tide tables, ration counts, radio schedules. Iris received them the way a lock receives a key — precisely, without warmth, the mechanism turning because that was its purpose. Oscar had rehearsed other words on the steamer, whole speeches, and had burned through all of them somewhere around the second set of rapids. What remained was logistics. Logistics were safe. Logistics kept nine years of silence intact while technically breaking it.

Now she laid the printout on the folding table between the lamp and the theodolite case, and watched her mother read it.

Iris read the way she did everything: completely, once, no wasted motion. Her eyes went down the contour lines, across the depth readings, back up. And then Oscar saw it — saw it so clearly she would remember it later, in worse moments, as the first thread coming loose.

Her mother's hand moved toward the paper and stopped.

It was a small thing. A hesitation of maybe half a second. Iris Vane did not hesitate. Iris Vane had once signed off on dynamiting a rockfall with a pencil stub and a shrug, had waded into a flooded shaft in Chiapas because the pump failed and someone had to check the air line. Iris Vane touched things. That was the whole argument, nine years ago, distilled: her mother touched everything, opened everything, and if there was anything left in the world her hands couldn't reach, she'd build longer hands.

But now her fingers hovered over the terraced lines and drew back, and she said, in a voice pitched exactly level:

"Where is this?"

"Fifteen hundred meters off the confluence," Oscar said. "Under four meters of silt and about six of water."

"And the extent?"

"We only ran three passes. It could go on for kilometers. The returns are consistent."

Iris looked up. For an instant — less than an instant, a flicker, gone before Teo could have caught it but Oscar wasn't Teo, Oscar had spent her childhood learning this woman's face the way other children learned prayers — for that flicker, her mother looked afraid.

Then it was gone, filed away behind the professional mask, and Iris Vane said: "Map it. Full grid survey, every pass, every angle. Photograph the sonar records, log the coordinates in triplicate." She rolled the printout and held it out, and her eyes when they met Oscar's were steady and cold and absolutely wrong. "And nobody touches it. No probes, no test pits, no cores. Nothing goes in the water except instruments. Is that understood?"

There was a silence in the tent. Teo shifted his weight. Somewhere outside, the cook was shouting cheerfully about rice.

"Ma'am," said Duarte, the expedition's senior surveyor, easing in from the flap with his hat in his hand the way he did when he wanted to disagree respectfully, "begging pardon, but that's a site down there. If that's what it looks like—"

"If it's what it looks like, it will still be there next season." Iris's voice didn't rise. It never rose; it compressed, like water going deep. "We map. We document. We do not open it. That's an order, and it applies to everyone on this expedition, myself included."

*Myself included.* Oscar turned the phrase over later, lying in her bunk with the river talking to itself outside the netting. Her mother had never once in her life included herself in a restriction she intended to obey. Restrictions were for other people, for governments, for daughters. Iris Vane's own name always appeared at the top of any exception list, in her own handwriting.

Something was wrong. Something was very specifically, personally wrong, and it wore her mother's face and used her mother's voice and ordered the expedition to leave a discovery buried.

*

The crew talked about it for three days.

They talked in the low register people use when the subject of conversation might be listening — over dishes, over card games, on the smoke break behind the fuel drums. Oscar heard most of it without trying, because she'd spent a decade being nobody anywhere, and nobodies hear everything.

Duarte thought the boss had gone soft, or religious, or both. The young techs thought it was some permitting issue — maybe the concession papers weren't clean, maybe the ministry hadn't signed off, maybe Dr. Vane was protecting the funding. One of the boat drivers, a local man named Esteban who rarely spoke, said only: "The river keeps what it keeps," and wouldn't elaborate, and the techs laughed, and he didn't.

Teo didn't laugh. Teo found Oscar on the fourth evening sitting on the supply crates at the camp's edge, watching the water go bronze and then black, and sat down beside her without asking, which was either rudeness or trust, and she'd decided by now it was trust, and she hadn't decided how she felt about that.

"You saw it too," he said.

"Saw what."

"At the table. When she read your printout." He was quiet a moment. "You flinched. Both of you, actually. Like the paper bit you."

"I don't flinch."

"No," Teo agreed mildly. "You don't. That's what made it interesting."

Oscar turned a pebble over in her fingers. Downriver, the last light was failing in the trees, and the first insects were tuning up in the reeds, and somewhere out on the floodplain, under four meters of silt and six of moving water, something built by human hands lay waiting to be named, and the one person alive who had never in her life refused to name a thing had ordered it left alone.

"Why do you think she won't touch it?" Teo asked.

"Ask her."

"I did. She gave me a lecture on survey ethics that lasted eleven minutes and contained zero information." He smiled, briefly, without much humor. "She's good at that. You'd know better than anyone."

That landed closer than he could know. Oscar let it sit. Out on the water, a fish broke surface and folded back under, leaving rings that widened and widened until the dark took them.

"She's afraid," Oscar said finally. It was the first time she'd said it aloud, and saying it made it heavier, realer, a fact with weight instead of a suspicion. "Not careful. Not cautious. Afraid. I've seen her scared twice in my life. Once when my father's boat went down and she thought I was on it. Once—" She stopped.

"Once?"

"Once when I was twelve, and we got a letter from a government office about a valley she'd surveyed." Oscar threw the pebble into the water. "I don't remember what the letter said. I remember she burned it in the cook fire and we moved camps the next morning, and she never explained, and I learned not to ask. That was the deal growing up. Don't ask about the letters, don't ask about the closed sites, don't ask why we're leaving. You dig, you keep your mouth shut, you move on."

Teo was watching her with an expression she couldn't fully read in the dying light — sympathy, maybe, or the particular sadness of a man realizing that the story he'd been curious about was made of worse material than he'd hoped.

"And now?" he said gently. "Are you still keeping the deal?"

Oscar didn't answer. The question hung there between them, patient as the river.

*

On the fifth night, unable to sleep, Oscar took the survey tent's spare lamp and went through the site files.

She told herself it was routine — a diver checking the charts she'd be working under, verifying depths, hazards, access routes. Professional diligence. It was even mostly true. But her hands moved faster than diligence required, and she skipped past the bathymetry and the sediment logs and went straight for the folder marked CONFLUENCE ANOMALY, in her mother's block capitals, and inside it she found the grid plan, the photographic record of the sonar prints, the triplicate coordinate logs —

— and a single sheet of older paper, typed on a machine with a failing ribbon, decades out of date, that had no business being in a current expedition file at all.

It was a partial report. A survey summary. The header had been torn away, but the body text survived, and Oscar read it standing under the hissing lamp with her heart going slow and strange, like a diver's heartbeat in deep cold water.

*A submerged structural complex extends approximately 2.1 km along the paleochannel. Construction appears pre-colonial; masonry techniques are consistent with no known regional tradition. Recommend immediate protective designation. NOTE: Any announcement of this find must account for the presence of established communities along the tributary corridor, whose tenure—*

The rest was torn away. But the date was legible. Twenty-two years old. And in the margin, in younger handwriting than the typewriter's, in ink gone brown, a single annotation in her mother's hand:

*Do not let them have it.*

Oscar stood for a long time.

Twenty-two years ago, her mother had found this place. Had mapped it, measured it, written the recommendation, and then — what? Filed it where? Buried it under what? And now, two decades later, Iris Vane had assembled an expedition, hired a crew, hauled tons of equipment upriver to this exact bend of this exact river, and ordered everyone — *myself included* — to leave it untouched.

You don't mount an expedition to a site you intend never to open. Unless the expedition isn't really about opening it. Unless the expedition is about getting there first — about standing between the thing and everyone else who might come for it.

*Do not let them have it.*

Them. Who was *them*? A ministry? A bureau? Men like the ones whose letters got burned in cook fires? Oscar thought of the unmarked launch Esteban swore he'd seen holding station downriver two nights ago, too far out to flag, too close to be innocent. She thought of her mother burning pages, moving camps, compressing her voice like deep water.

Outside, the river ran on over its secret, dark and patient, keeping what it kept. Oscar folded the old page along its existing creases, slid it back beneath the grid plans exactly as she'd found it, and killed the lamp.

In the darkness she stood a moment longer, listening to her mother's tent — silent, as it had been silent every night, though Oscar had begun to suspect the silence was manufactured, that Iris Vane slept as badly as her daughter did, on the far side of a canvas wall, guarding a secret twenty-two years old.

Three days later, the unmarked launch came upriver with the morning fog, flying no flag at all.
