## 5. The Other Boat

The launch appeared on the fourth morning after the sonar anomaly, and Oscar saw it before anyone else because she was the only one who still looked at the river like a diver looks at water — not as scenery but as a surface, something that could be broken from either side.

She was scrubbing the skiff's hull at the landing when the sound reached her: an engine note too even for a fishing boat, too patient for a trader. She straightened, rag in hand, and watched it come around the far bend where the current ran green over drowned cane. No flag. No name painted on the bow. Just a long white hull riding low in the stern, the way boats ride low when they're carrying weight they don't want you to count.

It slowed half a kilometer out. Held there. Then came on again, unhurried, and dropped anchor two hundred meters downstream of basecamp with the precision of a man who had measured the distance and chosen it.

Oscar dried her hands and went to find her mother.

Iris Vane was already standing at the edge of the camp's rise, binoculars up, her field hat pulled low. She didn't lower them when Oscar arrived beside her.

"Surveyor," Iris said.

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell what he wants us to think." She passed the binoculars over. "Look at the deck."

Oscar raised them. The man at the rail was broad and sunburned, wearing a canvas shirt with the sleeves rolled square, and he waved — actually waved, a big friendly sweep of the arm, the wave of a neighbor greeting neighbors across a lake. But behind him, lashed under tarps along the starboard side, were shapes Oscar knew the way she knew her own hands. A-frame davits. Cable drums. The blunt steel heads of salvage winches.

"Those aren't survey tools," she said.

"No," said Iris. "They are not."

* * *

He came ashore at midmorning in a rubber dinghy, alone, carrying a leather document case and a cooler of cold beer as if the beer were his credentials. He introduced himself to the whole camp at once, loudly, cheerfully, shaking hands down the line of them like a politician working a wharf.

"Corliss Hale. Hydrographic survey, contracted out of the capital." His grip was dry and firm and lasted exactly one beat longer than comfortable. When he reached Oscar, his eyes did a quick, professional inventory — calluses, forearms, the salt-cracked boots of someone who worked wet — and filed it away. "And you'd be the dive specialist. Dr. Vane mentioned she'd brought on talent."

"She mentioned that?" Oscar said.

"Mentioned needing one." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "Word travels on a river."

It didn't. That was the thing. Rivers carried fish, silt, rumors among the fishing villages maybe, but not names, not contracts, not the specific fact that a salvage diver had joined an archaeological expedition three weeks ago. Someone had told him. Someone upstream, with access to a radio.

Teo Reyes drifted close to Oscar's shoulder, holding his sample notebook against his chest like a shield. He was twenty-four and earnest and had asked her twice already whether she missed anything about the coast, which was the kind of question nobody who wanted nothing ever asked. Now he murmured, "Why does a surveyor need winches?"

"Why does a surveyor need beer?" Oscar said. "To make friends. Watch the other hand."

Hale spread his documents on the mess table under the awning: permits, letterhead, a government seal stamped in violet ink. Iris sat opposite him with her arms folded, and Oscar stood at her mother's shoulder and read upside down, fast, the way she'd learned to read manifests and customs forms in ports where honesty was optional.

The letters were real. That was the first unsettling thing. The bureau crest, the reference numbers, the signature of a deputy director whose name Iris went very still at. But the body of the principal letter — the one authorizing "hydrographic assessment of the upper valley in advance of infrastructure review" — had been redacted. Whole paragraphs blacked out with heavy bars of ink, and beneath the seal, where the authorization's scope should have been itemized, a single line:

*[REDACTED — INTERNAL]*

"Infrastructure," Iris said. Her voice was level. Oscar had grown up hearing that voice across dinner tables and dig sites both, and she knew it was level the way ice is level. "What kind?"

"The boring kind." Hale uncapped a beer and set it in front of her like a peace offering. She didn't touch it. "Dam siteworks, eventually. Right now just measurement. Depth soundings, flow rates, sediment cores. Your terraces under the floodplain are of great interest to us, Doctor — purely as substrate, I assure you. We wouldn't dream of disturbing archaeology."

"You have winches on your deck."

"For the cores." He spread his hands, all reasonableness. "Heavy equipment, heavy work. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Iris said.

There was a silence with a lot of history in it. Hale let it run for exactly as long as served him, then laughed, warm as a stove, and turned the charm on the rest of the camp — asking Teo about orchids, complimenting the cook's rice, learning everyone's name and using each one once, deliberately, so it would stick. By the time he pushed back from the table, half the crew liked him. That was the second unsettling thing. He was good at this. Whatever *this* was, he had done it before, on other rivers, to other camps.

At the landing, while his crewman rowed him back, he paused beside Oscar and lowered his voice into something almost confidential.

"Your mother's a legend, you know. In certain circles." He looked out at the water, at the drowned terraces sleeping somewhere under all that green current. "In those circles, we've always wondered why she stopped publishing. Fifteen years ago she was the most exciting field archaeologist in the hemisphere. Then — nothing. A woman like that doesn't go quiet. She goes *careful*." He tapped the side of his nose. "Careful people usually have something worth being careful about."

"Enjoy the beer," Oscar said.

He laughed all the way back to his boat.

* * *

That evening the storm built itself over the headwaters — not yet rain, just heat and pressure and a sky bruising purple above the ridgeline, the air gone thick enough to chew. The crew ate early and turned in early, the way people do when weather is coming and there's nothing to do but wait for it.

Oscar didn't sleep. She lay in her tent listening to the river and, around midnight, heard what she'd been waiting for: the soft creak of the mess tent's guy-line, footsteps on packed earth, the strike of a match.

She gave it five minutes. Then she got up, barefoot, and moved through the dark the way she moved underwater — slow, no splash, no wake.

Her mother was crouched at the camp's iron stove, feeding pages into it one at a time. Not crumpled, not torn in haste. Each page torn cleanly from its binding, held a moment in the firelight as if rereading, then laid flat on the coals and pressed down with a stick until it caught. There was a discipline to it that made Oscar's skin crawl. This wasn't panic. This was procedure.

Iris must have heard her — of course she had; the woman could hear a heartbeat at forty meters — but she didn't turn around.

"Go back to bed, Oscar."

"What are you burning?"

"Field notes."

"From when? Today?"

"From fifteen years ago." Iris fed another page to the flames. In the orange light her face was unreadable, carved and old in a way it never looked by daylight. "Transcriptions. My own handwriting. Copies I kept because I told myself keeping copies was different from publishing them."

Oscar came closer despite herself. The page burning now was dense with figures — depths, strata codes, a sketch of stepped stone rendered in quick confident lines that she recognized, painfully, as her mother's drafting hand. At the top, a site designation she'd never seen: a valley name, a year, and beneath it the words *DO NOT SURVEY — SEE APPENDIX C.*

"Mom." The word came out before she could decide whether to use it. Nine years of *Dr. Vane*, and here it was anyway, dragged out of her by firelight. "What is Appendix C?"

Iris watched the page curl and blacken. For a long moment Oscar thought she wouldn't answer, and braced herself for the old wall, the old silence, the decade of closed doors between them.

Then her mother said, quietly, "The last time a government found a valley like this one, they called it protection. Drew boundaries. Moved eleven thousand people off land their families had farmed for four hundred years, to build a visitor center and a parking lot." Another page onto the coals. "I was twenty-nine. I wrote the report that made it happen. My name is in the citation."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the black water, faintly, came the idle burble of a generator — Hale's launch, running lights low, awake the way its pilot was awake.

"So I found this place," Iris went on, "fifteen years ago. Before the floodplain drowned. And I wrote the report. And then I read my own report the way I'd taught a hundred students to read reports — imagining every hand it would pass through — and I put it in a box, and I lied to my colleagues, and I lied to my funders, and I have been lying ever since." She finally looked up at Oscar, and her eyes in the firelight were the same gray as the river before a storm. "Because some things are safer lost than found. And I decided which kind this was. Alone. Without asking anyone who lives here, which is its own crime, I know that now. But I made the decision, and I've paid for it, and I will keep paying—"

She stopped. Looked past Oscar, toward the river.

Oscar turned.

Far out on the water, a shape was moving without lights — a dinghy, low in the water, sliding along the basecamp shoreline at drift speed. Too slow for fishing. Too steady for drifting. It paused opposite the landing, hung there for the length of ten heartbeats, and then continued on toward the tributary mouth, silent as a log.

When Oscar looked back, her mother had fed the last page to the stove and was closing the iron door on the embers, on the ashes of fifteen years of careful lies.

"Hale's boat runs its generator all night," Iris said, in the flat professional voice again, the door swinging shut over everything she'd just shown. "Every night since he anchored. I want you to listen to it tomorrow. Tell me if the pitch changes."

"Why?"

"Because generators change pitch when they're powering something new." She stood, brushed ash from her knees, and for a moment her hand hovered near Oscar's shoulder — an inch away, trembling slightly, not quite landing. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we start double shifts on the mapping. If he wants to watch us work, he can watch us work fast."

She walked back toward her tent, straight-backed, and Oscar stood alone at the cooling stove with the smell of burnt paper and river damp, understanding two things at once.

The first was that her mother had spent nine years being afraid of exactly this — of the day the careful lie met the outside world.

The second was that Corliss Hale hadn't come up this river for substrate.

He'd come because somewhere, somehow, the box had opened — and whatever was left of Appendix C was still hidden somewhere in this valley, and by morning, Oscar intended to know where.
