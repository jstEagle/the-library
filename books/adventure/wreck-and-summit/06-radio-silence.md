## 6. Radio Silence

The tributary party left on the fourth morning after the sonar anomaly, and Oscar watched them go from the water's edge with the particular stillness of a woman counting exits.

Iris took four: Teo Reyes with his plant presses and his endless questions; Sefa Iuliano, the surveyor, who carried the theodolite like a sleeping child; Marta Okonkwo, the expedition medic and strongest swimmer among them; and Dov Harlan, the geologist, quiet as sediment. Five people in a longboat with two weeks of provisions, climbing into the green throat of the western tributary where the floodplain narrowed to a gorge and the maps went vague.

"Ten days," Iris said. She stood in the bow, one hand on the gunwale, wearing the canvas hat that had been old when Oscar was born. "Check-in at 0800 and 1800, channel four. If we miss one, don't panic. If we miss three—"

"I know the protocol," Oscar said. "I wrote half of it."

Something moved behind her mother's eyes — not warmth, exactly, but a recognition of the shape of things, the way a diver recognizes current by the behavior of silt. Iris had taught her that. Read the water before you trust it.

"We'll be back inside the window," Iris said, and then, because she was Iris Vane and could not leave anything unqualified: "The gorge narrows at the third bend. If the headwaters come up, we'll be above the flood line. We chose the route for that reason."

"You're telling me it's safe."

"I'm telling you we planned for it not to be."

That was more honesty than they'd exchanged in nine years, and it sat between them on the morning air like something dropped. Iris touched the brim of her hat — an old gesture, half salute, half apology — and turned away, and the longboat slid out into the brown water, and Teo raised one hand from the stern until the current took them around the first bluff and the river closed over the place where they'd been.

Oscar stood there a while longer than she needed to.

Then she went back to work, because work was the only language she'd ever been fluent in.

* * *

Basecamp without Iris was a different animal. The woman ran the camp like a fortress — radio schedules logged to the minute, supply counts signed in triplicate, the strict no-artifacts-out policy enforced with a ferocity that had made more than one visiting academic laugh and then stop laughing when they understood she meant it. With her gone, the camp exhaled into a looser shape, and Oscar found herself, without deciding to, tightening it again. She checked the generator fuel lines. She re-lashed the supply tarps against wind. She posted the radio schedule on the mess tent pole in block letters because the young crew kept forgetting, or pretending to forget.

"You know she'd have done it differently," said Priya Nair, the comms tech, watching Oscar chalk the times onto the board. Priya was twenty-four and treated the radio set like a lover — she polished its dials, knew its moods, slept within earshot of its hum.

"Differently how?"

"She'd have made us feel bad about forgetting."

Oscar almost smiled. "I'm saving that for later. Building anticipation."

The crew had warmed to her in the way people warm to weather that turns out kinder than forecast. She'd arrived as the replacement digger, the last-minute name on the manifest, and for the first week they'd watched her the way you watch a stray dog near your food. But she dove the flooded cutbank on her third day and recovered the lost sounding line in forty minutes flat, and she'd done it without theatrics, without even surfacing to explain herself, just handed the line up and gone back to checking the anchor points. After that they stopped asking why Iris Vane had hired someone with no academic credentials and started asking her things instead. How deep could she free-dive. Whether the story about the sunken ferry at Puerto Oscuro was true. (It was, mostly.) Whether she was really Dr. Vane's daughter.

That last one she never answered directly. Neither did Iris. The camp had learned to read the silence as confirmation.

Now, with five of their nine gone upriver, the remaining four orbited Oscar the way they'd previously orbited Iris, and she felt the weight of that transfer settle on her shoulders like wet rope. She wasn't good at being looked to. Being looked *for* — that she understood. That was salvage work: someone lost, someone paid to find. But this was different. This was people trusting her to simply keep the lights on and the schedule kept, and every hour of it felt like a rehearsal for something she didn't want to name.

On the second evening she cleaned her dive mask at the water's edge and let herself think about the site.

Terraced stone under the floodplain. Sonar had caught it by accident — a regularity in the riverbed too geometric to be geology, steps descending in ordered courses beneath six meters of moving water. Iris had ordered it mapped, photographed, buoyed, and left untouched, and the order had landed on the camp like a stone in still water. Everyone knew Iris Vane's reputation. She opened sites the way other people opened letters. There were entire chapters of regional archaeology that existed because Iris Vane had refused to leave a stone unturned, sometimes literally, sometimes against considerable objection. A site left sealed was not in her nature.

Unless her nature had changed. Unless she was afraid.

Oscar had seen fear on her mother exactly twice in her life. Once the night the telegram came about her father — though that had been grief wearing fear's clothing, grief and guilt braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was which. And once, years earlier, in a museum basement in the capital, when a man from the antiquities bureau had come to look at Iris's preliminary surveys and Iris had smiled at him with her teeth and then, alone, standing over her own drawings, had looked like a woman reading her own death warrant.

Oscar had been nineteen. She hadn't understood it then. Standing at the river now, mask in hand, watching the water carry the light away in long bronze ribbons, she thought she was beginning to.

* * *

The check-ins came like clockwork, and then they didn't.

Day three, 0800: Iris's voice through the static, clipped and clear. "Position past the second bend. Gorge walls rising. All well." Nineteen seconds. Priya logged it.

Day three, 1800: "Camp established above the third bend. Water's up maybe half a meter since noon. Nothing concerning yet." Thirty-one seconds. At the end of it, a pause — a breath held a half-second too long — and then the carrier dropped. Oscar, who happened to be in the comms tent when it came through, asked Priya to play it back. She listened to the pause four times.

"What do you hear?" she asked.

Priya frowned. "Static?"

"A person deciding what not to say." Oscar tapped the table. "She says the water's up half a meter and nothing's concerning. Then she stops talking. If it were truly nothing, she'd have signed off clean. My mother doesn't pad her transmissions. Every word costs her something."

"Maybe the battery was low."

"Maybe." It wasn't, but saying so would only frighten Priya, and there was nothing yet to act on. Oscar filed the pause where she filed everything — in the cold ledger at the back of her skull, next to tide tables and decompression limits and the exact depth at which a human voice stops carrying through water.

Day four, 0800: nothing.

"Not unusual," Priya said, though her hand hovered over the transmit key like it might bite. "Gorge walls block line-of-sight propagation. They said themselves the walls are rising. Could be a shadow zone. Could be the antenna took a knock in transit."

"Log it as missed," Oscar said. "One missed is weather. Two is information."

1800 came. Priya called on schedule, gave the basecamp identifier, waited through the standard ninety seconds, called again. Static answered, thick and grainy, the sound of atmosphere minding its own business. She tried the backup frequency. She tried the emergency channel, which was supposed to be monitored continuously. She swept across the band slowly, the way you'd sweep a torch across a dark room, looking for any shape at all.

Nothing. Not silence exactly — silence would have been cleaner. What came through was the river of noise itself, all the world's unclaimed signal hissing and crackling, and somewhere in it, presumably, five people who weren't transmitting.

At 1900 the sky over the headwaters changed.

Oscar stepped out of the comms tent and saw it building in the northwest, where the tributary came down from country none of them had surveyed. Clouds were stacking. Not drifting — stacking, the way cargo gets stacked, deliberate and load-bearing, tower upon tower going blue-black at their bases, lit from within by a bruised silver light. The air had gone still in the wrong way, the way it goes still before a quarry wall lets go. Ten kilometers off, lightning moved inside the cloud mass like eels under ice, silent at this distance, patient.

"The headwaters are getting it," Dov said quietly. He'd come up beside her, and he was looking at the clouds with a geologist's arithmetic running behind his eyes. "That system's parked over the upper catchment. Everything it drops has one place to go."

"How fast?"

"Depends on the gorge. Narrow channel, steep gradient—" He shook his head. "Could be a day. Could be less. Flash floods in confined valleys don't send invitations."

Oscar looked at the sky a moment longer. Then she went back inside and picked up the logbook.

* * *

By midnight they'd logged eleven failed contact attempts. Priya ran the set until her eyes burned, sweeping frequencies, sending scheduled calls into the dark, keeping the discipline of it even as the discipline curdled into ritual. Oscar sat across from her and made herself useful the only way she could: she did the math.

The tributary catchment above the third bend: roughly two hundred square kilometers, if the old survey maps were honest, and they were the only maps anyone had. Rainfall under that cloud mass — she'd seen storms like it drop eighty millimeters in six hours, and this one looked hungrier than that. The gorge below the third bend narrowed, per Iris's own transmission, per the maps, to a channel perhaps thirty meters wide between walls of soft shale. Two hundred square kilometers of runoff funneled into thirty meters of width.

She wrote the numbers down. Crossed them out. Did them again. The answer stayed ugly.

Three days. Maybe four, if the storm broke early and the ground drank some of it. Call it three. In three days, a flood pulse would come down that gorge with enough force to scour the banks, take out the lower campsites, and turn the lower chambers of whatever lay behind the waterfall — if there was a waterfall, if there were chambers, if the thing her mother wouldn't say aloud was real — into a drowning trap.

And the authorities? She'd already sent the message, priority routing through the relay station at Puerto Oscuro. Response time for a government patrol boat to this stretch of river, in the wet season, with the bureaucracy involved: seven days if they cared, ten if they didn't, fourteen if someone in the capital decided the paperwork needed a signature. She'd gotten an acknowledgment receipt. An acknowledgment receipt. Somewhere a clerk had stamped a form confirming that someday, possibly, people with rifles and clipboards might think about coming.

"So we wait," said Jonas Ferreira, the cook, who had joined the vigil uninvited and unhelpful. He was a decent man and a nervous one, and he filled silences the way other men filled pipes. "Protocol says wait. Dr. Vane's own protocol. Three missed check-ins, we hold position and notify authorities. That's the rule. She wrote it."

"She wrote it for a camp that can't dive," Oscar said.

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is." Oscar kept her voice level, which took effort, because the effort was the point. Shouting would make it about her. Level would make it about the numbers. "Your protocol assumes rescue comes from outside. Outside is ten days out. The water is three days out. The protocol has a hole in it the size of my mother's campsite, and everyone sitting in this tent knows it."

Priya looked up from the radio. Her face in the lamplight was young and scared and trying hard to be neither. "What are you saying, Oscar?"

Oscar looked at the set. At the static. Out the tent flap, where the clouds had finished stacking and now simply stood, monumental, holding their cargo.

She thought about nine years of not speaking to the woman up that gorge. About the funeral she'd missed, the dig she'd chosen instead, the conversation they'd never had because both of them would rather bleed than ask for it. She thought about how easy it would be to sit here, keep the schedule, honor the protocol, and lose her mother to a river with a clear conscience — how the river would even be kind enough to take the blame, so that Oscar Vane could spend the rest of her life grieving instead of choosing.

Her whole adult life she'd been fast. Fast in the water, fast out of towns, fast past anything that looked like roots. Running was the family trade; she'd inherited it honestly. And now the trade offered her its favorite bargain: stay put, stay safe, stay blameless.

"I'm saying I'm going up there," she said.

Jonas started to object. She talked over him, not rudely, just completely, the way water talks over a stone.

"Alone, in the skiff, at first light. The longboat's gone, so it's the skiff or nothing, and the skiff can run the gorge in low water if the pilot knows what she's doing. I know what I'm doing. I'll follow their marked route — my mother marks everything, she's marked routes since before I was born, there will be blazes. I'll find them, assess, and get word back. If the radio's dead because of terrain, I fix it or I carry the message myself. If it's dead because something else is wrong—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "Then they need a diver more than they need a committee."

"And if the flood comes down while you're in the gorge?" Dov asked. He wasn't objecting. He was asking the question a friend asks, the one that makes the plan better or kills it honestly.

"Then I'll be in the same water they are," Oscar said, "and at least nobody will be waiting ten days for help that's already too late."

She stood, and the others stood with her, and for a moment the tent held all four of them in the lamp's small circle — the comms tech, the geologist, the cook, the diver — bound together by nothing sturdier than a decision.

"Priya. Keep calling on schedule. Log everything. If you get so much as a click of carrier wave from channel four, you note the time to the second. Dov — weather watch, every two hours, and I want the river stage measured at dawn and dusk. Jonas, fuel the skiff, charge the batteries, pack me four days of food and the medical kit. Nobody touches the artifact crates. Nobody leaves camp. If I'm not back in six days—"

She paused. Six days would put the flood behind her and the authorities still ahead of her, and everyone in the tent could do that arithmetic too.

"If I'm not back in six days," she said, "you tell the authorities exactly where I went, and you tell them my mother would want the site documented before anyone else touches it. Word for word. She'll understand why."

Outside, the first far thunder finally arrived, rolling down the valley like furniture dragged across a floor above. The clouds over the headwaters had begun, very slowly, to move.

Oscar packed her dive knife, her spare mask, and — after a moment, from the bottom of her kit, where she kept the things she didn't look at — her mother's old field compass, the one Iris had pressed into her hands at sixteen and taken back at twenty-two, and which had somehow, impossibly, ended up in her gear bag on the steamer north, wrapped in a page torn from a journal, in handwriting Oscar recognized and had chosen not to read.

*Not yet*, the page had said. Or maybe *not here*. She'd stuffed it away unread and told herself it was sentiment.

Tonight, wrapping it in oilcloth beside the flare gun, she admitted what it actually was. It was a message. Her mother had known, before leaving, that the radio might go quiet — had known it the way she'd known to choose the high route, the way she'd paused half a second too long on an open channel. Iris Vane planned for the flood. She always had.

Which meant the silence wasn't an accident.

Which meant whatever was waiting up that gorge, her mother had walked toward it with her eyes open — and had left her daughter a compass, and a choice, and three days of river to make it in.

Oscar doused the lamp and went to check the skiff one last time, alone, under a sky stacked black to the ridge lines, while the first fat drops of the long rain began to fall.
