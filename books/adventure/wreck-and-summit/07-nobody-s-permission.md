## 7. Nobody's Permission

The radio hissed all night.

Oscar sat in the comms tent with her boots on the crate that served as a table, watching the needle of the signal meter twitch like something dying. Every fifteen minutes she keyed the mic and called the tributary party in the clear, then in code, then in the old field alphabet Iris had drilled into her when she was nine years old — *dash-dot-dash, Oscar, again, you're dragging the tail* — and every fifteen minutes the river answered with nothing but static and the far-off grumble of thunder rolling down from the headwaters like furniture being dragged across an upstairs floor.

At 0300 she stopped calling. Not because she'd given up, but because she'd started to hear the water.

That was the thing about rivers that people who hadn't grown up on them never understood: they talked before they acted. A river about to rise had a voice — a low, wet consonant under the ordinary rush, the sound of current shouldering into new channels, testing the banks, deciding what it was going to take. The Río Blanco had been talking since sundown. By midnight it was arguing with itself. By 0300 it had made up its mind.

Oscar stepped out of the tent into rain so fine it seemed suspended rather than falling, a gray gauze over everything, and looked upriver toward the black wall of the cordillera where lightning flickered without sound, sheet after sheet, like a lamp behind a curtain. The storm wasn't passing over them. It was sitting on the headwaters and feeding.

She did the arithmetic standing there in the dark, because arithmetic was the only prayer she trusted.

The tributary camp sat at roughly 340 meters elevation above basecamp, up a gorge whose walls she'd studied on Iris's maps for two days now, tracing the contour lines with her finger until they were printed on the inside of her eyelids. The gorge narrowed twice — once at the narrows Teo had nicknamed the Throat, once higher up where the survey notes marked a landslide scar from some previous century's flood. Water coming down that gorge wouldn't spread. It would stack. It would come through the Throat like a fist through a paper bag.

Rainfall this heavy in this kind of catchment: thirty-six hours of concentrated runoff before the main pulse hit the lower gorge. Add the saturated ground, add whatever feeder streams were already running brown. Call it three days before the flood reached the tributary camp's elevation, if the party was still at the camp. Call it less if they'd gone deeper into the caverns, which — knowing why Iris had gone, knowing what she'd found — they almost certainly had.

Three days.

She went back inside, pulled the government hydrology folio out of the supply chest — the one Corliss Hale had left behind like a calling card, all those confident blue contour maps — and checked her numbers against theirs. They matched. That was somehow worse. Even the bureau's own engineers knew the valley would flood; they just intended to do it deliberately, with concrete, in five years' time.

Then she calculated the other number, the one that mattered more.

Basecamp's radio could reach the regional station in Puerto Andes in good conditions. In these conditions, with atmospheric interference stacking up like wet wool, maybe one transmission in ten got through clean. She'd sent four priority messages since the missed check-in yesterday afternoon. She'd received one acknowledgment: *message received, relayed to authorities.* Which meant the request was now in a queue. Which meant it would be read by a functionary who would determine whether a missing archaeological party constituted an emergency or merely a concern. Which meant a patrol launch, if one was authorized, if one was fueled, if its captain felt like navigating flood-stage water at night.

Ten days. Eleven, if the paperwork disagreed with itself.

Oscar set down the folio and looked at her hands. Steady. They were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook.

* * *

By first light the whole camp knew, the way camps always know, and they gathered under the mess fly in a loose, unhappy semicircle while the rain came down harder and the river below them ran the color of milk chocolate, carrying branches whole, carrying a dead capybara spinning slowly past like a thought nobody wanted to finish.

"—not saying we do nothing," Amaya Duarte was saying. She was the expedition's logistics officer, twenty-six years old, competent as a Swiss watch and visibly running on no sleep. "I'm saying we don't do *this*. One boat, one diver, up a flooding gorge, alone? That's not a rescue plan, Vane, that's a second funeral."

"The authorities—"

"Are ten days out," Oscar said. "I've done the math. You can check my math."

"I believe your math." Amaya pressed her knuckles against her eyes. "That's what scares me."

There were six of them left at basecamp. Amaya. Old Ferro, the boatman, who'd said perhaps forty words since Oscar arrived and made each one feel rationed. The two student assistants, Paloma and Ruiz, who kept looking at each other the way young people do when they're waiting for someone older to tell them what's true. Teo Reyes would have been the seventh, except Teo was up the gorge with Iris, pinned somewhere behind rising water, and the thought of it moved through Oscar's chest like a swallowed fishhook every time she let herself think it directly, so she didn't; she kept it turned sideways, kept working around it.

And there was Marta Quispe, the site conservator, sitting slightly apart on an ammo box with her arms folded, watching Oscar with an expression Oscar couldn't parse yet. Marta was fifty-something, built like a door, and had spent thirty years keeping artifacts alive in places that wanted them dead. She hadn't spoken since the gathering began.

"Radio's not getting through clean," Oscar said. "You all heard last night's relay attempt. Half our traffic is coming back garbled. We have a skiff, we have fuel for two runs, and I have—" she stopped herself from saying *nine years of diving flooded ground*, because it sounded like bragging, and anyway it wasn't the point. "I know that gorge. I've been studying the charts since the day they left. I can be at the tributary camp in under two days if the water lets me, and I can bring them down ahead of the pulse."

"If," said Ferro, from the edge of the group. First words of the morning. Everyone turned to him as if a stone had spoken. He spat a thread of tobacco into the mud. "Water doesn't let. Water takes."

"Then I'll take it back."

Nobody laughed. It hadn't been a joke, and they all knew it.

Paloma raised her hand halfway, like she was still in a classroom. "Shouldn't we wait for Dr. Vane's instructions? There might be a protocol—"

"There is no protocol," Oscar said, and heard her mother's cadence come out of her mouth, flat and final, and hated it. She softened it by force. "There's no protocol for this. That's the whole problem. My mother wrote half the safety standards these expeditions run on, and even she'd tell you—" She stopped. Because that was exactly it, wasn't it. Iris Vane had written the book. Chapter one, page one, underlined twice in every edition since Oscar was a child: *No one goes in alone. No one goes in blind. The dead do not need company.*

Every rule her mother ever taught her, stacked up between Oscar and the skiff like a fence.

"She'd tell me to stay," Oscar finished quietly. "I know. I'm going anyway."

"Why?" Ruiz asked. Not hostile — genuinely asking, a boy who wanted to understand the shape of courage before he tried any on himself. "I mean — she's your mother, obviously, but. Why you?"

Oscar looked at him for a long moment. Rain drummed on the fly overhead. Down at the landing, the skiff tugged at its lines like a dog that smelled the road.

"Because I'm the best digger here," she said. "And because I'm the only one here who's ever swum a sump. And because—" and here the fishhook turned in her chest, and she let it, let it speak for her, "—because nine years ago I decided my mother wasn't worth the trouble, and I have regretted it every single day since, and I am not going to spend the next nine learning what missing her feels like when it's permanent. That's why. Any more questions?"

Silence. Then Marta Quispe stood up off the ammo box, unhurried, joints cracking like green wood, and walked over to the supply chest, and began loading gear into a waterproof duffel with the economy of a woman who had packed for disasters before. Rope. Flares. The medical kit. A coil of signal wire.

"What are you doing?" Amaya asked.

"Packing," Marta said. "The girl's going. I can see it in her face. I've seen that face before, on better people than me, and arguing with it wastes daylight." She glanced at Oscar, and something passed across her features — not quite approval, not quite grief. "Iris took four people up that gorge because she believed the site had to be verified before anyone else could find it. She believed it enough to break her own rules. So." She zipped the duffel. "Her daughter breaking a few seems fair inheritance."

"I'm not taking passengers," Oscar said.

"No," Marta agreed. "You're taking supplies. I'm staying here, holding the fort, keeping the radio hot, and making sure that when the authorities finally drag themselves upriver, somebody with sense tells them where you went." She pushed the duffel into Oscar's arms. Its weight was honest and grounding, forty pounds of other people's care. "But you'll carry my kit to the top of the Throat, because if you get past it and I'm wrong about you, you'll want everything in there. And if I'm right about you—" her jaw worked, "—you'll want the flares most of all. Water takes. But light carries."

* * *

They loaded the skiff in the gray half-light, and the work itself steadied everyone, gave their fear something to hold that wasn't each other. Fuel drums lashed amidships. Spare prop. The chart case sealed in oilcloth. Oscar checked the bilge twice, checked the shear pins, checked the kill-switch lanyard, and caught herself performing each task with her mother's exact motions — thumb-testing the fuel line fitting the way Iris did, tapping the hull below the waterline to hear for delamination the way Iris had taught her at eleven years old on Lake Titicaca, both of them laughing then, before everything — and she let her hands remember without letting her face show anything at all.

Ferro walked the skiff's lines with her in silence, checking knots, and when he finished he stood looking downriver for a while.

"My grandfather ran this water," he said at last. "Before the dam talk, before any of it. He said the Blanco forgives nothing but respects preparation." He looked at her sideways, one eye, weathered as driftwood. "You prepared?"

"Since 0300."

He nodded slowly, and untied the bowline himself, and put it in her hands like handing over something heavier than rope. "Then go. And girl—" he waited until she met his eye. "The Throat. If the water's white all the way across, you portage. You hear me? Portage. Pride floats downstream. Divers don't."

"I hear you."

Amaya hugged her briefly, hard, the way people hug when they think it might be the last time and are embarrassed about it. Paloma pressed a tin of biscuits into the chart case. Ruiz stood at the end of the landing with his fists in his pockets and said, "Bring them home," in a voice that cracked on *home*, and Oscar nodded and didn't trust her own voice enough to answer.

Marta stood apart, arms folded, and as Oscar cast off and the current took the bow, Marta raised one hand — not waving, just open, palm out, the way you'd greet someone arriving rather than leaving. Oscar held that image all the way around the first bend.

* * *

The gorge swallowed the sky within the hour.

Walls of wet granite rose on either side, furred with moss and hanging gardens, dripping threads of water that joined the brown flood below with a sound like whispering crowds. The skiff fought upstream at half throttle, engine screaming against current that wanted everything back that the mountain had lent it. Oscar ran the throttle in pulses, reading the water the way she'd learned to read faces — where the surface smoothed, depth; where it wrinkled in long diagonal seams, a submerged shelf; where it boiled white and silent-looking, rocks with murder in them. Twice she ferried across the current's shoulder to ride the eddy lines, the skiff corkscrewing through slack water behind midstream boulders, gaining ground in increments bought with concentration so total it erased everything else — the fight, the silence, the fishhook — until there was only the next eddy, the next seam, the next hundred meters.

She made the lower pools by late afternoon and camped on a gravel bar forty feet above the waterline, hauling the skiff up on rollers of dead bamboo because she did not trust the river overnight and the river would not have disappointed her. From dusk to dawn the flood rose four feet, groaning, pushing whole trees past her camp like battering rams, and she lay in the lee of the overturned hull listening to them go by and doing the arithmetic again, and the answer was worse every time.

Dawn came the color of dishwater. She launched into rain.

It was near noon on the second day, soaked through, hands blistered raw on the tiller, when she rounded the bend below the Throat and saw what the river had become.

The narrows had gone from a rapid to a roar — a single unbroken line of white from wall to wall, thirty feet of standing waves stacked on each other like collapsed architecture, the whole gorge filled with a sound that wasn't noise so much as presence, a pressure she felt in her sternum through the hull. Spray hung in the air above it like smoke. Somewhere beyond that chaos, two days' climb further up, were her mother and Teo and two others, listening to the same sound growing louder.

Ferro's voice in her head: *portage.* Her mother's voice underneath it, from a lifetime of field manuals: *no one goes in alone, no one goes in blind.*

Oscar cut the engine and let the skiff drift in the eddy below the Throat, and looked up at the portage route — a goat track along the left wall, visible in switchbacks, maybe six hours' carry with the boat and fuel, doable alone if her back held, and then another day and a half of water beyond it, and the flood clock ticking down to hours instead of days.

She thought about turning around. Honestly, she thought about it — pictured the look on Amaya's face, the warm dry mess tent, the word *reasonable* like a blanket she could pull over all of it. Nobody would blame her. Everybody would understand.

That was precisely the problem. Understanding was cheap. Her mother had chosen a dig over a funeral because she'd judged the find worth the cost, and the world had called it ambition, and Oscar had called it betrayal, and neither of them had ever said aloud the simpler truth: that some things are worth the cost, and the only question is whether you're the person willing to pay it.

Nine years she'd paid in distance instead. She was done paying in distance.

Oscar nosed the skiff toward the gravel bank below the portage trail, killed the engine, and started hauling.

* * *

The trail climbed through leech country and cloud forest, and she carried the skiff in sections — hull on her shoulders, fuel on a tumpline across her forehead, making three trips per segment because she refused to leave anything unguarded where Hale's people might pass. That thought surfaced more than once as she climbed: the sabotage she'd done to Hale's fuel line had bought a day, maybe two, but a man like Corliss Hale didn't lose a day, he rescheduled it. Somewhere behind her, or ahead of her, or already above her, his launch was working its way upriver with winches and a redacted letter and patience like a noose. She scanned the water whenever the trees opened, and once, near dusk, she saw far downstream a smudge of engine smoke hanging in the gorge air, and stood very still among the ferns until it faded, and told herself it might be anything, and knew it wasn't.

On the third morning, wrung out and running on biscuits and will, she launched above the Throat into water that had risen another six feet overnight, and the upper gorge received her with a new character entirely — slower here, deeper, the flood backing up behind some obstruction downstream, the current gentler but the surface wrong, swirling in slow dead rotations around floating debris. She wove the skiff through a mat of logs and flowering branches torn from some drowned garden upslope, and the smell of the water changed too: cold mineral breath, and beneath it something else, faint and strange, like wet stone and old air.

Like a cave breathing.

She rounded the final bend at midday and saw the tributary camp, and her heart performed a maneuver she felt in her throat.

The tents were slashed. All of them — not wind-torn, *cut*, long deliberate strokes through canvas, gear strewn across the clearing, a lantern shattered, a boot lying sole-up in the mud. No bodies. No blood she could see. The survey equipment was gone, which meant whoever had left had taken tools, which meant planned departure or capture, not slaughter. But the cuts in the tents were not the work of her mother's careful crew.

Oscar grounded the skiff, drew the knife from her belt, and stood in the ruined camp letting her eyes do what they were trained to do. Drag marks led away from the tents toward the cliff face at the gorge's head — toward the waterfall she could hear now, a steady white noise through the trees, bigger than it should be in this season, swollen with the storm. At the treeline, half-hidden by fronds, she found what she was looking for: a blaze cut into bark at shoulder height. Fresh. And beside it, small and precise, a second mark.

A dash, a dot, a dash.

The letter K. *Kilo.* In her mother's field code, the letter that meant one thing and one thing only, the word Iris had used exactly once in Oscar's childhood, the night a flash flood nearly took their first camp together and Iris had gotten everyone out by marching them into the limestone caves behind the falls:

*K — cavern. Come in. Safe.*

Oscar stood in the slashed camp with the rain coming down and the flood climbing the banks behind her, and looked at the waterfall veiling the cliff, and behind it, invisible, a staircase her mother had found and feared and followed — and somewhere inside it, if the mark told true, living people waiting in the dark.

She picked up the duffel. She walked toward the water's roar.
