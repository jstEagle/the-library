## 8. Upstream

The tributary did not want her there.

Oscar understood this within the first hour, when the river that had looked like brown glass from the skiff's mooring turned out to be a liar with teeth. The charts Iris kept in the basecamp strongroom showed the Serrado as a lazy ribbon, two meters deep at the dry season's lowest, meandering through floodplain forest toward the highlands. What Oscar got instead was a channel braided into three, then five, then a dozen silver threads that split and rejoined around sandbars like veins in a lung, each one shallow enough to ground her and deep enough to drown her, and no way to know which was which until she was committed.

She ran the first set of rapids by instinct and the second by prayer she didn't believe in, and after the third she pulled into an eddy behind a fallen kapok tree the size of a cathedral and let her hands shake where nothing could see them.

*Three days.* That was the arithmetic she'd built her whole mad plan on. The flood gauge at basecamp had risen eleven centimeters overnight; the headwaters were taking the storm first and passing it down like a debt being collected. Three days before the water climbed past the lower chambers of whatever cave system her mother had walked into, if the chambers existed, if the Morse code she'd heard — or thought she'd heard — on the dead radio band hadn't been wishful thinking dressed up as signal.

Ten days for the authorities. Three days for the water. She'd chosen the smaller number because it was the only one that left anything to do besides sit and count.

She checked the skiff's hull out of habit. The aluminum had taken the rapids better than she deserved; her patch job on the old stress fracture was holding, the epoxy still tacky at the edges. Her gear was lashed down under oilcloth: rope, the dive kit she'd refused to leave behind even though it weighed as much as a sin, tinned food, the machete, Iris's field maps copied onto waterproof paper in her own careful hand. She'd stolen the copies fair and square — photographed them at 3 a.m. while her mother slept thirty meters away, and felt nothing about it, or told herself she felt nothing, which amounted to the same thing in practice.

Nine years of silence, and now she was running her mother's rivers alone, following her mother's blazes, reading her mother's handwriting by flashlight. There was a joke in there somewhere. She couldn't find it.

She pushed back into the current.

* * *

The leech country began where the rapids ended, which was the river's idea of mercy: it stopped trying to kill her quickly and settled for wearing her down instead.

The portage was marked on Iris's map with a terse annotation — *carry trail, 2.4 km, mud* — and *mud* turned out to be generous. The trail was a memory of a trail, a corridor of bootprints gone green with algae, hemmed in by ferns that grew taller than she was and wept water down her collar every time she brushed them. Underfoot the ground was not ground so much as a rumor of ground, a brown soup over root-lace that grabbed her boots and held them with a wet sucking sound, like the forest kissing her goodbye with every step.

She carried the skiff on her back.

It was a twelve-foot aluminum hull, forty kilos dry, and she'd flipped it overhead in the eddy with her thighs screaming and her spine finding the keel like an old enemy. She'd carried boats before — quarry salvage meant hauling gear up cliff faces, and the flooded slate pits outside Vellamora had taught her what her body was worth per hour — but never for two and a half kilometers through country designed by something with a grudge.

The leeches found her inside the first hundred meters.

They came off the ferns in ribbons, sensing heat and carbon through the steam-thick air, and dropped onto her arms, her neck, the backs of her hands where they gripped the hull's gunwales. She couldn't stop to burn them off — stopping meant setting the boat down, and setting the boat down meant the mud would take it, and taking it back would cost twenty minutes she didn't have. So she let them feed. She felt them attach along her forearms like small cold coins, felt the faint wrong pressure of them working, and kept walking, counting her steps in hundreds, breathing through her nose, doing the only thing she had ever been genuinely excellent at, which was enduring.

Her mother would have stopped. That was the difference between them, Oscar thought, and then corrected it, because it wasn't true — Iris Vane had once spent six weeks in a tent with a broken wrist rather than abandon a dig, and had set the bone herself with a field splint and a bottle of aguardiente. No. Her mother would have stopped, assessed, made the efficient choice. Oscar was simply too stubborn to put the boat down. It wasn't competence. It was spite with a work ethic.

Somewhere around step nine hundred she started talking to the forest, low and continuous, the way divers do on long bottom times when the silence gets structural.

"Two point four kilometers," she said. "You're lying. You're a lying map in a lying notebook written by a woman who burned her own journal, so forgive me if I don't trust your surveying."

The ferns said nothing. A bird somewhere screamed like a hinge.

At step fourteen hundred she found the blaze.

It was cut into the buttress root of a fig tree at shoulder height — three horizontal lines with a vertical stroke through them, the bark still pale and fresh at the wound. Iris's mark. Oscar knew it from childhood, from hiking trips that were supposed to be vacations and were always secretly surveys, from the summer her mother had taught her to read contour lines by drawing them in the dirt of their campsite until the girl could see the shape of a valley in her sleep. Three lines and a stroke. *Follow the water. Trust the cut, not the path.*

There were more blazes after that, spaced at intervals that told Oscar her mother had come this way recently, moving fast, marking as she went — which meant Iris had expected someone might need to follow. Which meant some part of Dr. Iris Vane, the woman who communicated exclusively in supply manifests and radio schedules, had sat down with a machete and carved a breadcrumb trail into the wilderness for the daughter she hadn't spoken to in nine years.

Oscar stood under the fig tree with forty kilos of boat on her spine and let that fact sit in her chest like swallowed stone.

Then she kept walking, because the water was rising whether she wept about it or not.

* * *

She launched again at midafternoon, having picked eleven leeches off her body with the machete tip and salted the wounds with river water and profanity. The channel above the portage was kinder — deeper, slower, canopied so completely that the light came down green and underwater-colored, and she moved through it feeling like a diver who'd forgotten to get out. Fish rose to inspect the hull. Somewhere ahead, thunder worked the highlands over, patient as a debt collector.

That was when she smelled diesel.

Not a whiff — a presence. Faint, threaded through the green smell of the water, but unmistakable to a woman who'd spent a decade around engines: raw fuel, unburned, sitting on slow water. She killed the outboard and drifted, paddle out, ears open.

Around the next bend, tied up under a stand of bamboo, rode Corliss Hale's launch.

It was bigger than her skiff the way a knife is bigger than a scalpel — eighteen feet of gray-painted steel with a cabin, a winch mount on the bow, and the particular clean emptiness of a vessel whose owner keeps his tools stowed. No flag. No registration numbers. The name on the transom had been painted over so recently the ghost of the old letters still showed through: *something-something Survey*, the word dying under gray like a man under snow.

Hale himself was not aboard.

Oscar held position in the shadow of the far bank for a full ten minutes, paddle locked, letting the current talk to her. She catalogued what she could see: a coil of mooring line, fresh-cut ends. A camp stove on the deck, cold. Two fuel drums lashed amidships, one nearly empty, one full — he'd been here long enough to burn through most of a drum, which meant either he'd run hard to get this far or he'd idled, waiting, watching. Boot prints in the mud of the landing, size eleven, going up the carry trail. Going *her* direction. Going, she would have bet her remaining tinned peaches, toward the blazes.

He was following the same trail. Of course he was. He had the same map — or enough of one — and the same arithmetic, and men like Hale didn't wait for authorities either. They just had better boats and worse reasons.

She should go around him. That was the sensible play: hug the far bank, engine at idle, slip past in the green dark under the canopy and put a kilometer between them before he came back down to the water. She had a day's lead, maybe less, and burning it on a confrontation with a man who traveled with armed friends was the kind of decision that got solo travelers buried in leech country with their throats opened.

She looked at the launch. She looked at the fuel drums. She looked upstream, where the thunder was stacking itself against the sky like masonry.

Here was the thing about Oscar Vane that nobody who'd ever hired her had fully understood, though her mother had known it all along: she was not brave. Bravery implied a relationship with fear that involved overcoming it. What Oscar had was colder and more useful — a kind of bookkeeping. She could look at a bad situation and see it as a ledger, entries and balances, and act on the math without letting the fear sign anything. Divers who felt fear died of it, usually by rushing. Divers who did arithmetic came home.

So she did the arithmetic.

Entry one: Hale was ahead of her on the trail, headed for her mother. If he reached the caverns first, everything after that was his game — hostages, bargains, bodies. Entry two: she could not outrun him indefinitely; his launch was faster than her skiff and he knew these waters at least as well. Entry three: but a launch burns fuel, and his reserve was visible, finite, and currently moored unattended in a green tunnel with no witnesses except the fish.

The conclusion assembled itself in her mind with the smooth, ugly ease of a knot tying itself, and she examined it honestly, the way you're supposed to examine a thing like that, looking for a flaw, looking for a reason not to.

She didn't find one. She found, instead, a memory: her mother at the basecamp fire, feeding a page of her own field journal into the flames, page by page, face lit orange and absolutely unreadable. Whatever Iris was afraid of, it wore a smile and carried a government letter with a redacted seal, and it was currently tied to a bamboo stand fifty meters away, waiting for her with its boots pointed upstream.

Oscar grounded the skiff on a sandbar sixty meters short of the launch, in the lee of a deadfall, and stripped her kit down to the essentials: machete, knife, the waterproof pouch with the maps. She waded the last stretch, chest-deep, moving slow so the water wouldn't speak for her, and came up the muddy landing on the downstream side of the bamboo, out of the sightline of anyone walking back down the trail.

The launch's stern sat low in the water. She went under the transom.

Up close the boat smelled of diesel and cigarettes and, faintly, of machine oil and money. The fuel line ran from the drum through a filter to the outboard's intake — standard rig, well maintained, the fittings bright with use. Hale took care of his equipment. Men like him always did; it was the people they treated as equipment who got the neglect.

Oscar drew her knife and considered the line for a long moment.

Cutting it outright was amateur. A severed line announced itself the moment the engine coughed, and any competent operator would trace the fault in minutes and splice it in ten. What she wanted was not a breakdown. What she wanted was a *delay* — something that would run fine for a while and then fail slowly, confusingly, at the worst possible hour, costing him a day of diagnosis in country where every hour of daylight was rationed by the storm.

She worked the fitting loose a quarter turn — just enough to seep, not enough to spray. Then she took her knife and scored the rubber hose along its length, deep enough to weaken, shallow enough to hold, and packed the score line with river mud, which would stay damp and keep the weep invisible until vibration and heat finished the job. Finally she backed the drum's shutoff valve to half-open, so the flow would stutter under load in a way that would read as a clogged filter, a phantom fault, a ghost in the machine that would send a careful man checking spark plugs while his day burned down around him.

Total time: four minutes. Total noise: none. Total guilt: she checked, thoroughly, and found the account empty.

She was re-wading toward the sandbar when the forest spoke.

A single sound, upstream and upslope — not an animal, not wind. Metal on metal. A tool, being used, with the unhurried rhythm of a man who believed himself alone. Then, closer to the water, a second sound that stopped her blood mid-current: a voice, low, talking to someone. Not Hale's cadence. Younger. And answering it, fainter, another voice — and under both of them, threading up the carry trail from wherever they'd come from, a smell she knew from a decade of salvage sites and had hoped never to smell again in the wild.

Gun oil.

Two of them, at least. Maybe more up the trail, maybe standing between her and her mother right now, and she was chest-deep in a river with a knife and a machete and a plan that suddenly looked very small.

Oscar sank to her chin in the tea-brown water and became, in the old quarry phrase, part of the substrate. The voices moved — downslope now, toward the landing, casual, arguing about something in a language she half caught and didn't place. Boots squelched in the mud thirty meters away. Through the screen of bamboo she watched a young man in a gray shirt step into view, rifle slung backward, and stop, frowning at the water.

He was looking at the ripples. Her ripples. Expanding outward from a spot where nothing visible stood.

"Hey," he called up the trail, and his hand drifted toward the sling. "Boat's here, but there's something—"

The thunder arrived.

It came off the highlands all at once, a wall of sound that shook silt off the roots and sent every bird in the canopy up screaming, and under its cover Oscar moved — not away, but forward, a long silent glide through the murk toward the skiff's mooring on the sandbar, because the boy had seen the ripples but the rain had already begun to fall, fat warm drops dimpling the whole river at once, erasing everything, giving the water back its privacy.

By the time the thunder rolled off downstream, the sandbar was empty, the skiff was gone, and the young man in the gray shirt was standing in the rain telling himself he'd seen fish.

* * *

Oscar ran the last hours of light hard, engine wide open where the channel allowed, poling where it didn't, the storm chasing her down the valley like something with her name in its mouth. Rain hammered the oilcloth over her gear. Lightning turned the river white, then black, then white again, and in each flash she checked the banks for blazes — three lines and a stroke, trust the cut — and for gray steel moored in the shadows, and for the particular silhouette of trouble waiting upstream.

Behind her, she knew, a fuel line was beginning to weep.

Ahead of her, somewhere past the last bend the daylight would allow, her mother was pinned in the dark under a waterfall with the water rising, and the only thing standing between Corliss Hale and the find of the century was a woman in a leaking skiff who had just discovered she was capable of sabotage without losing any sleep over it.

She'd have thought about what that said about her. Later. If there was a later.

The blazes appeared on the left bank as the light failed — fresh cuts, pale in the lightning, marching upstream into the black throat of the valley — and beside the third one, hacked deep into a fig trunk at chest height, was a mark she didn't recognize.

Not her mother's. A circle with a bar through it, cut hard enough to scar the heartwood.

Someone else was marking the trail too. And they were ahead of her.
