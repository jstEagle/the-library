## 9. The Drowned Staircase

The blazes appeared an hour after the tributary narrowed, and once Oscar knew what to look for, she couldn't unsee them.

Machete cuts in the bark of the riverside trees, each one a shallow chevron angled toward the water, low enough that a passing boat would hide them from anyone not actively searching. Iris had taught her the trick when she was eleven, on a survey in the chalk hills — *never mark a trail at eye level, mija, eye level is where strangers look*. Oscar had spent nine years trying to forget everything her mother taught her, and here she was reading the woman's handwriting in tree bark like a letter she'd been waiting her whole life to open.

She counted them as the skiff pushed upstream against the brown current. Fourteen chevrons. Then a gap of nearly two kilometers where the river braided through drowned grassland and the blazes would have been pointless — the water itself was the road. Then fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, each cut fresher than the last, the sap still glossy at the edges.

The tributary bent west around a shoulder of black rock, and the sound reached her before the sight did: a low, continuous thunder, felt in the hull more than heard. The river was running high. Running higher than it should have been this early in the season, and every kilometer she climbed was a kilometer of water that would come back down looking for her.

The camp appeared around the next bend, tucked into a shelf of gravel at the base of the cliff, and Oscar's stomach dropped through the bottom of the skiff.

Four tents. She knew it was four because Iris had requisitioned four — she'd seen the manifest, had helped load them at basecamp in the days before the silence, back when she still believed the worst thing between her and her mother was nine years of unsaid things. All four tents were down. Not collapsed — *down*, flattened, the poles snapped and the canvas slashed in long deliberate strokes, the kind of cuts made by someone standing over a thing rather than fighting it. Gear was scattered across the gravel in a wide arc, as if someone had gone through it with a rake. A boot lay on its side at the waterline, laces still tied.

Oscar cut the engine and let the skiff drift the last fifty meters, paddle out, moving slow.

No bodies. No blood that she could see. That was something. That was maybe everything.

She grounded the skiff on the gravel and sat for a moment with her hand on the gunwale, listening. The waterfall thundered somewhere ahead, around the cliff's shoulder. Birds — the wrong birds, the common ones, the ones that came back after a place had been empty for a while. Nothing human. Nothing moving except the water and the light.

She got out.

*Methodical*, she told herself. *Be methodical. It's the only prayer you have.*

She worked the camp the way her mother had taught her to work a site — grid by grid, nothing touched before it was understood, everything read before it was moved. The tents had been cut from the windward side, all four, same height, same stroke. Someone had wanted the camp opened up, its contents exposed. Not destroyed — exposed. A message, or a search.

The gear told the rest. Rucksacks turned out and rifled, but carelessly, the way a man searches when he doesn't know what he's looking for. A theodolite case, empty. A food crate with its tins punched in, all of them, every single one — that was spite, or discipline, or both. Someone had wanted this party unable to stay and unable to leave.

But here was the thing, and Oscar crouched over it in the gravel with her heart going hard: the scientific equipment that mattered was gone. Not stolen — *carried*. There were drag marks in the gravel, two sets, heading away from the camp toward the cliff. Heavy cases, hauled by people in a hurry. And beside them, boot prints. At least six. Maybe eight. Some of them Iris's — she'd know that tread anywhere, the old field boots with the cracked soles her mother refused to replace because *broken-in leather has saved more expeditions than new leather ever lost*.

They'd walked out. Under guard, or under escort, but walking. And they'd taken their instruments, which meant someone had convinced their captors the instruments were worth carrying, which meant someone had been thinking fast in the worst hour of their life, and Oscar would have bet the skiff and both her hands that the thinker had been a small gray-haired woman with a cracked-sole walk and a lifetime of practice making hard bargains.

The drag marks led to the cliff. Of course they did. Everything here led to the cliff.

* * *

The waterfall came around the rock shoulder all at once — a broad white sheet falling maybe thirty meters off a shelf of black basalt, feeding a churning pool that threw spray fifty feet into the air. The noise was enormous, total, a sound that erased thought. The pool's edge was a chaos of foam and half-submerged boulders, and behind the falling water, visible only as a darker darkness, a seam in the cliff face. A hollow. A mouth.

Oscar stood at the pool's rim and let the spray soak her shirt and understood, with the flat certainty of a diver reading water, that this was where the river went in. Somewhere behind that curtain, the tributary dropped into the mountain, and the mountain had been hollowed out over ten thousand years the way limestone hollows, patiently, in the dark.

And her mother had walked into it, dragging instrument cases, with men who cut tents.

The blazes changed here. The last chevron — she found it on a tree at the pool's edge, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots — wasn't a chevron at all. It was a cut she'd never seen, a vertical stroke with a crossbar, and below it, smaller, a second mark: two short strokes, angled, like a pair of legs walking. Field code. Her mother's field code, the private shorthand the two of them had invented one bored summer when Oscar was thirteen, a whole language of ticks and strokes for *safe*, for *water*, for *turn back*, for *wait for me*.

The mark meant *down*. The second meant *follow*.

Oscar laughed — one short, ugly sound, swallowed instantly by the waterfall. Nine years of silence and the first conversation they'd had was carved into a tree at the edge of a drowning river: *down, follow*.

She checked her gear with the ritual attention of someone who had once nearly died for skipping a step. Air — two tanks lashed to a frame, because whatever was behind that water, she wasn't betting her life on there being an easy way back out. Light — primary, backup, backup to the backup, because her mother had drilled into her that darkness is a country with no rescue. Knife, line, chalk, flares, the little brass compass that had been in her kit since she was sixteen and that she'd never once admitted was a keepsake. She left the skiff tied high, above the obvious flood line, and marked the tree beside it with a blaze of her own: a single horizontal stroke. *Here. Return.*

Then she waded into the pool.

The cold hit her like a hand. She'd dived quarries in November; she knew cold. But this was mountain cold, snowmelt cold, the kind that took the breath and folded it small. She went under the boulder shelf where the seam opened, and the waterfall came down on her like the sky collapsing, a pressure and a roar that shoved her sideways against the rock, and then she was through — through the white chaos and into the black — and the sound cut off as if a door had closed.

Silence. Total. The kind of silence that has weight.

She stood in water to her chest on a shelf of wet stone, in a darkness so complete it felt like a substance, and gave herself ten seconds to breathe before she touched the light.

* * *

The cavern opened around her like a held breath.

Her beam found wet stone first, then walls rising beyond the reach of the light, then a ceiling somewhere impossibly high, lost in drip-stone and shadow. She was standing on a natural shelf above an underground channel — the river's swallowed throat — and the water here ran black and slow and deep, sliding away into the dark with a sound like something breathing.

She played the light along the wall and stopped breathing.

The carvings covered the stone from the waterline to twenty feet above her head, and they went on. Not glyphs — she'd seen glyphs, had catalogued them for a salvage outfit that sold them to men who wore them on cufflinks — but figures. Whole processions of them, cut shallow and confident into the living rock: lines of people walking, carrying things, bending toward the water, arms raised. Boats. Terraces. A city rendered in profile, stacked in tiers, with a stairway running down its center into a band of carved water. And at the top of the composition, larger than all the others, a figure with its arms spread wide, holding up — or holding down — a circle.

Oscar had spent a decade in flooded places. She'd pulled locomotives and automobiles and one entire chapel out of drowned valleys, and she'd seen the marks people leave: dates scratched in concrete, names in dock timber, the small stubborn graffiti of the displaced. She knew what human presence looked like, in stone, in water, in the record.

This was older than any of it. Older than it had any right to be. The style belonged to no tradition she'd ever seen in a book, and she'd read the books — she'd read them to spite her mother, and read them again to spite her more thoroughly. There was nothing in the literature like the tiered city with the stairway running into water. Nothing like the figure holding the circle.

And the waterline of the carvings — that was the thing that made her skin crawl, made her check the black channel twice — the lowest figures stood *in* the carved water. The artists had carved the flood into their own history. This city had drowned before, or been built knowing it would drown, or had been built *as* an answer to the drowning. The people walking in the lowest band weren't fleeing. Their arms were raised. The same gesture as the figure with the circle.

*Reaching up*, she thought. Or *holding it down*.

She worked the wall slowly, the way she'd worked the camp, reading instead of touching. There were places where the carving changed hands — literally, where the cut of the chisel shifted, deeper or shallower, generations of makers visible in the stone like handwriting in a family letter. The work had been maintained. Revisited. For how long? Centuries? A thousand years of people coming back to this wall in the dark behind the water, adding their figures to the procession?

The last section, near the seam where she'd entered, was different. The cuts were cruder. Recent-looking, though she knew better than to trust that instinct — patina lied in wet caves. But the figure there was unmistakable even in the crude style: a small procession of six, walking toward a stairway, and above them, scratched with a different tool entirely, in a hand she'd have known blindfolded —

Two short strokes, angled. Legs, walking. *Follow.*

Iris had stood here. Iris had read this wall — had read it years ago, Oscar realized, because there were older marks further in, faded survey chalk, a datum point scratched and numbered in her mother's cramped field shorthand, a number that matched nothing in any published report because there *was* no report. Iris had found this place before. Had mapped it, or started to, and then walked away and told no one and burned whatever she wrote down, and Oscar was only now, standing in a cathedral behind a waterfall, beginning to understand what her mother had been so afraid of.

The stairway was real. That was the last thing the light found before Oscar turned it toward the back of the cavern: a flight of steps cut into the living rock, descending from the shelf where she stood, wide enough for four abreast, worn in the center into smooth shallow bowls by centuries — millennia — of feet. The steps went down alongside the black channel, into the dark, and the dark took them after eleven steps, and the air moving up out of that darkness was cold and mineral and faintly, impossibly, carried something else.

Smoke. Old smoke. And under it, so faint she might have invented it, the smell of lamp oil.

People had gone down those stairs recently. Many people. Some of them willing.

Oscar stood at the top of the drowned staircase with her light pointed down into the breathing dark, and the awe that had filled her chest since the first figure came off the wall curdled slowly into something with more edges. Because she could read the story in the stone now, and the story was simple: a city that had learned to live with the water, that had carved its drowning into its own walls and kept coming back, generation after generation, to add its people to the procession. A city whose descendants were probably, at this moment, living in a mission settlement downriver without knowing their ancestors had built a cathedral behind a waterfall.

And men with machetes had dragged her mother's team down these stairs.

She checked the compass out of habit more than need, marked the seam of the waterfall entrance with chalk so she couldn't lose it, and started down.

Eleven steps into the dark, the stairs turned, and the light found the next thing: a second blaze, scratched into the wall at knee height, fresh, the sap of the stone still pale in the cut. And beside it — her breath stopped — a third mark. Not her mother's. Not the old code at all.

A single horizontal stroke. *Here. Return.*

The mark she'd carved into the tree above her skiff, an hour ago, alone, in a language of one.

Someone had seen her come.
