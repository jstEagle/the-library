## 10. Voices in the Dark

The dark in the cavern was not like night. Night had edges — a horizon, a star, the grey suggestion of where the world might resume if you walked long enough. This dark had no edges at all. It pressed against Oscar's open eyes with the same weight as her closed lids, so that after an hour she could no longer be certain they were open, and she stopped trying to know.

She kept one hand on the guide line and one on the wall, and she counted her breathing the way her mother had taught her when she was nine years old and afraid of the quarry water: four counts in, six counts out, the body fooled into believing it was calm until it actually was. The trick had never failed her. She resented that it was Iris Vane's trick, and used it anyway.

The staircase had gone down farther than the carvings had suggested — forty steps, sixty, then the stone gave way to a sloping gallery where the waterfall's roar faded to a pressure she felt in her back teeth rather than heard. Her carbide lamp threw a coin of light three meters wide and no farther, and within that coin the rock was wet, ribbed, ancient, veined with mineral stain the color of dried blood. Somewhere ahead, water moved. She could hear it the way you hear your own pulse when everything else goes quiet: patiently, without malice, rising.

She had left a marker at the top of the stairs — a strip of orange survey tape knotted to a root, visible from the entrance chamber if the light hit it right. That was for herself, on the way out. She had not let herself think about what would happen if there was no way out.

* * *

She found the first blaze forty minutes in.

A smear of Iris's chalk on the wall, low, at knee height, arrowed left. Below it, scratched in the same hurried hand: **WATER RISING — 3 CHAMBERS — M.**

Marta. So Marta was hurt, or worse, and Iris had left the message for whoever came behind them, which meant Iris had believed someone would come behind them, which meant —

Oscar stood very still in the coin of light and made herself not feel the thing that rose in her chest, because hope in a cave was a liability. Hope made people hurry. Hurrying killed divers. She had pulled two bodies out of flooded workings in her decade of salvage, and both times the report said the same thing: victim descended rapidly, became disoriented, line entanglement, air exhaustion. Hope, wearing a wetsuit.

She marked the chalk arrow with tape of her own, checked her gauge — a third of her lamp fuel gone, half her air, if she turned around now she'd make the surface with margin — and went left.

* * *

The second chamber opened around her like the inside of a throat. Her lamp couldn't find its ceiling or its far wall; the beam simply gave up, swallowed by distance and hanging moisture. The floor tilted toward a black channel where the tributary had found its way underground, and the sound of it filled everything — not roaring now but muttering, conversational, a river talking to itself about what it intended to do.

That was when she heard the hammering.

Three beats. Pause. Three beats. Then a longer sequence, crisp, deliberate, spaced like a person who knew exactly what they were doing:

**V-A-N-E.**

Oscar's whole body went cold and then hot. Not Morse — not exactly. It was the field code, the one Iris had invented for radio-silent survey work decades ago, the one Oscar had learned at eleven because there was nothing else to do on digs but learn things, tapped out on tent poles and cook pots across a dozen countries. Most of it mapped onto standard Morse anyway. Iris had been lazy that way, or efficient. But the letters were signed, and the signature was unmistakable.

Her mother was alive. Her mother was down here, somewhere past the muttering water, tapping her name into the dark.

Oscar fumbled for the steel carabiner clipped to her harness, the one she'd brought for exactly this without admitting to herself why, and struck it against the wall. Once. Twice. Hard enough to sting her hand through her glove.

Then, deliberately, spacing each letter the way Iris had taught her, spacing it *correctly*, which was itself a message — *I am your daughter, I learned it from you, I remember*:

**O-S-C-A-R.**

Silence. Long enough that she counted ten breaths, twenty. The river muttered. A drop fell somewhere high above her and struck the water with a sound like a struck bell.

Then the hammering resumed, fast now, almost ragged at the edges, and Oscar translated as it came, her lips moving in the dark:

**ALIVE. SIX. PINNED. WATER TWO CHAMBERS IN. MARTA LEG BROKEN. AIR BAD. COME.**

Six. Iris, Teo, and the two others whose names Oscar knew from the manifest — Okonkwo and Lindqvist — plus Marta made five. The sixth took her a moment, and then she remembered: Dr. Amara Sefu, the epigrapher who'd joined at the last port, whom Oscar had met once for ninety seconds on the dock. Six alive. One broken leg. Air bad.

Air bad. In a cave system sealed by floodwater, with a storm stacked over the headwaters feeding the rise, "air bad" was not a status update. It was a countdown.

Oscar keyed her reply against the stone, each strike precise:

**COMING. HOW MUCH AIR.**

The answer came back after a pause long enough to frighten her:

**CANDLES AND LUNG. HOURS NOT DAYS. FLOOD BLOCKS RETURN PASSAGE BY TOMORROW NIGHT. IF YOU TURN BACK NOW YOU LIVE.**

Iris was giving her an out. Of course she was. Nine years of silence and the first real conversation her mother offered her was permission to leave.

Oscar struck the answer before she could think better of it, before the part of her that had spent a decade erasing herself from records could intervene:

**NOT LEAVING. ROUTE TO ME.**

And then, because the dark was enormous and her hand was shaking and some words are easier hammered into stone than spoken:

**IT'S OSCAR. I CAME FOR YOU.**

The pause on the other side lasted so long she thought the line had been cut, that something had happened, that the water had taken them while she stood there translating. Then the hammering started again, and it was slower now, and the rhythm of it was wrong in a way she recognized with a jolt — uneven, the intervals stretching, the way a person taps when their hands are unsteady for reasons that have nothing to cold.

**THANK GOD,**

three letters said, and then,

**— FOLLOW THE CHALK. LEFT AT EVERY BRANCH UNTIL THE NARROWS. MOVE FAST BUT DO NOT RUN. YOUR RULES TOO.**

Your rules too. As if the rules had ever belonged to both of them. As if the decade hadn't happened. Oscar pressed her forehead against the wet stone for exactly four seconds — she allowed herself four, timed against her breathing — and then she lifted her lamp and went.

* * *

The narrows nearly ended her.

The passage Iris had named was a fissure barely wider than her shoulders, floored with loose scree that shifted under every step and slicked with a film of water that made the chalk marks the only trustworthy thing in the world. Twice her lamp guttered and she had to stand motionless in absolute blackness, thumb on the carbide valve, listening to her own heart argue with the river, before the flame caught again. Both times, in the seconds of darkness, she heard the hammering continue far ahead — patient, steady, keeping time for her like a lighthouse that had learned to knock.

At the third branch the chalk changed character. The arrows were lower, faster, dragged. And beside one of them, in handwriting compressed by urgency, a full sentence:

**IF THIS IS TEO READING — GOOD BOY. IF IT'S ANYONE ELSE — ALSO GOOD.**

Oscar laughed. It came out of her as a single bark, absurd in the stone throat, and it echoed away into chambers she couldn't see, and somewhere ahead the hammering stopped, and then resumed in a new rhythm — quick, doubled strikes that she needed a moment to decode because she'd never heard anyone use it before.

It wasn't a letter. It was the field code's oldest signal, the one reserved for emergencies within emergencies, the one Iris had told her meant only: *someone is crying, keep coming.*

Oscar kept coming.

* * *

She smelled them before she saw the chamber — candle wax and wet wool and the sourness of human fear sustained too long. Then her lamp rounded the final bend and the light found faces.

Six of them, clustered on a shelf of rock perhaps four meters square, above a pool of water that had climbed the chamber walls to within arm's reach of the shelf's edge. The pool was black and utterly still, which was worse than movement — still water was water that was waiting. Two candles burned in tin cups, their flames bent sideways by the draft from the passage, throwing shadows that swung across the walls like slow birds.

Teo Reyes saw her first. He made a sound — not a word, just a sound, the noise a person makes when a thing they've stopped believing in walks into the room — and then he was up, crossing the shelf, gripping her arms hard enough to bruise through her jacket, saying her name over and over as though checking whether it still worked.

"You're alone," he said finally. His face was hollowed, filthy, a cut crusted along his jaw. "Oscar. Tell me you're not alone."

"I'm alone," she said. "But I'm the right kind of alone. Where's my mother?"

Iris Vane was sitting against the far wall with a steel hammer in her lap and her hands flat on her knees, and she looked smaller than Oscar remembered, and older, and exactly the same. Her headlamp was dead, its strap hanging loose around her neck. In the candlelight her eyes found Oscar's and held them, and nine years passed through the space between them like water through a sieve — all of it moving, none of it staying.

"You got my code," Iris said. Her voice was rough from disuse, or smoke, or something else. "You answered in proper form."

"You taught me proper form."

"I taught you when you were eleven." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, not quite a smile, not survivable as one. "I didn't know if it stayed."

"It stayed," Oscar said. "Everything stayed. That was the problem."

Okonkwo made a low sound — grief or relief, hard to tell — and Lindqvist was already moving, pressing a tin cup of water into Oscar's hands, and Dr. Sefu sat wrapped in a groundsheet with her arms around her knees, whispering what might have been prayer or inventory. And at the shelf's edge, propped with her ruined leg bound in splinted survey staves and stripped bark, lay Marta Ferreira, gray-lipped, fever-bright, watching Oscar with the level, unsentimental gaze of a woman doing arithmetic of her own.

"Two chambers," Marta said, before anyone else could speak. "Between us and the staircase. The water took the first one yesterday afternoon. It's chest-high and moving now, and it's cold enough to stop your heart if you stay in it too long. We measured." She patted a notebook beside her, absurdly intact, pencil-marked columns marching down its pages. "I'm good at measuring."

"How long," Oscar said, "before it takes the second chamber?"

Marta and Iris exchanged a look, and it was Teo who answered, his voice carefully level, the voice of a man who has rehearsed a sentence until it stopped being true. "By tomorrow night. Maybe dawn after. The storm's feeding it. We can hear the headwater changing pitch."

Oscar set down the cup. She looked at the black pool, at the candles, at the six faces arranged around her in the small trembling light, and she did the arithmetic she had crossed a hundred miles of river to do, and the number it produced was the worst number she had ever held.

One route out. Two chambers of floodwater between here and the staircase. Six people, one of them unable to walk. Her own gear: one spare regulator, fifty meters of line, two lamps' worth of fuel, and a skill set built for one body, not seven.

"Okay," she said aloud, to all of them, and to the dark beyond the pool, and to whatever version of herself had once promised never to come back to any of this. "Here's what we know. Here's what we have. Here's what we're going to do."

And behind her, at the edge of the lamplight, the black water rose another centimeter, silent as a held breath, patient as a debt.
