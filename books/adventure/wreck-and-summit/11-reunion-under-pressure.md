## 11. Reunion Under Pressure

The water was at her thighs when she heard the hammering stop.

Oscar stood in the second chamber with her carbide lamp turned low, one hand on the guide line she'd strung from the sump mouth, and listened to the silence that followed nine years of silence and three days of floodwater and a hundred meters of black stone. Her heart was going like a winch motor. She made herself count — four seconds, five — because panic was just arithmetic done badly, and if there was one thing Oscar Vane could do, it was arithmetic.

Then the hammering resumed. Three strikes, pause, three strikes. Pause. One.

*Alive. All alive. Come.*

Her mother's field code. Iris had invented it in the seventies, back when radios were too heavy for jungle work, and had taught it to exactly two people: her graduate students, and her daughter, on a kitchen table in Callao, tapping out letters on the wood grain while rain came down outside like it would never stop. Oscar had been eleven. She'd thought it was a game.

She fumbled for her own hammer, clipped to her harness beside the dive knife, and answered against the wall where the sound would carry through the water-filled passage: *Coming. Hold.*

Then she went into the flooded gallery without giving herself time to think about it, because thinking about it was how people died down here.

* * *

The third chamber was an air pocket shaped like a lung — low and long and narrowing toward its far end, where the ceiling dipped to meet the rising water and left perhaps half a meter of breathing space above a floor of submerged rubble. The water in it was warm as bathwater and moved with a slow, tidal patience, seeping in through cracks no map would ever show. When Oscar surfaced into it, her lamp found four faces.

Iris Vane sat hip-deep against the far wall with her knees drawn up, gray-streaked hair plastered flat, a headlamp with a dying battery strapped over her forehead so that the light wobbled every time she breathed. Beside her, Teo Reyes held a bandaged arm against his chest and looked at Oscar the way men look at rescue boats — with hope they're afraid to say out loud in case saying it jinxes it. Behind them, pressed into the last dry seam of rock, were the two others: Dr. Amara Osei, the surveyor, shivering violently; and young Pilar Quispe, the expedition's field assistant, who couldn't have been more than twenty-three and was holding a waterproof notebook against her chest like a child holds a doll.

"Oscar." Teo's voice cracked on her name. "Christ. *Christ.*"

"Don't use up the air on my name," Oscar said, and got a broken laugh out of him anyway.

She pulled herself onto the rubble shelf, unclipped her pack, and started inventorying aloud because inventories kept people sane: two lamps plus hers, call it six hours of burn between them. Half a liter of water. Four ration bars gone soft. A flare — one. Her spare dive bottle, still slung on her back, good for maybe forty minutes of bottom time if someone needed to go back through the sump.

"How much airspace is left?" she asked.

Nobody answered immediately, which was an answer.

"Since this morning," Amara said finally, teeth chattering, "the water's come up—" She held her hand at her sternum, then dropped it ten centimeters. "It slows when the river pauses. It doesn't stop."

Oscar did the math she didn't want to do. The chamber was maybe thirty meters long. At ten centimeters an hour, give or take what the storm upstream decided to send them, they had somewhere between thirty and fifty hours before the pocket closed. The sump behind her was ninety meters of flooded passage, and she was the only certified diver in the room.

"I can get one person out per run," she said. "Maybe two on the last one, if I rig them right. That's four runs. Six hours each, round trip, with decompression I can't afford to skip." She let them hear the number land. "So we have a problem."

"We have several," Iris said.

Oscar turned. It was the first full sentence her mother had spoken since the lamp found her face, and it was delivered in exactly the tone Iris used for correcting a student's sloppy trench profiles — dry, level, infuriatingly calm. Nine years, and the woman opened her mouth and it was like no time had passed at all, which made Oscar furious, which was easier than being afraid.

"You're welcome," Oscar said.

"Yes," Iris said. "Thank you. You shouldn't have come."

"Yeah, well. Somebody raised me stubborn."

"That was your father."

The name dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well, and everyone heard how deep it went. Teo shifted. Pilar stared very hard at her notebook. Oscar crouched by the waterline and let the silence sit there, because she'd rehearsed this conversation on a steamer deck for six days and now that it was here she found she had nothing prepared that wasn't either a knife or a wound.

The water lapped. Somewhere above them, muffled through a hundred meters of limestone, the river made a sound like slow applause.

"The blazes," Oscar said at last. "On the trees. You marked the trail yourself, didn't you. Fresh cuts."

"I marked them the day we went up," Iris said. "Standard practice."

"You marked them *for someone following.*" Oscar looked at her. "You cut them high and you cut them deep and you put a double blaze at the waterfall turnoff, which is not standard anything. You wanted whoever came after us to find the cave. You wanted it to be me."

Iris's headlamp wobbled once, twice, and went still. In its guttering light her face looked carved, all planes and shadow, older than nine years should have made anyone.

"Yes," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I knew Hale would follow us, and I knew he'd find the site whether I led him to it or not, and I needed—" Iris stopped. Started again, slower, the way she talked when she was excavating herself instead of a trench. "I needed the person who found it next to be someone who couldn't be bought."

"That's a hell of a job description to write for somebody you haven't called in nine years."

"I know."

"Do you?" Oscar heard her own voice go up and made herself bring it down, because sound carried in here and panic was contagious and there were two people in this chamber who hadn't signed up for a family excavation. "Do you actually know what nine years is? Because I've been doing it, Mom. Every day of it. You picked a dig over Dad's funeral. Not a conference. Not funding season. A *hole in the ground.* And then you sent a telegram like it was a supply requisition — *need digger, one season, no questions asked* — and I came, God help me, I came, and you handed me a supply manifest and a radio schedule and you never once—"

"Iris," Amara said quietly. "The air."

Oscar shut her mouth. Her pulse banged in her ears. She counted four seconds, five, and made herself look away from her mother's face, at the water, at the ceiling coming down to meet it inch by patient inch.

When she looked back, Iris had unzipped the inner pocket of her field jacket — the one Oscar knew, the one her mother had carried on every dig since before Oscar was born, the one that always smelled of tobacco Iris didn't smoke — and taken out a slim bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord.

"I need to tell you something," Iris said, "and I need you to listen to all of it before you decide what you think of me. We may not get another chance to talk honestly, and I've spent nine years treating honesty like something I could schedule for later." She set the bundle on her knee. "This is why I left a dig over your father's funeral. And it's why I'll die in this cave before I let certain people walk out of this valley with what's under our feet."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way she told everything, in the flat competent voice of a woman reading stratigraphy.

Nineteen sixty-eight. A different river, a different country, but the same story: a valley, a proposed hydroelectric project, and a site — pre-colonial, significant, *extraordinary*, Iris said, and for the first time her voice lost its levelness — extraordinary beyond anything in the literature. Iris had been thirty-one. She'd filed a report recommending protection. The government had been delighted to protect it. They'd declared a reserve, drawn lines on maps, brought in soldiers, and moved eleven villages off land their families had worked for four hundred years, because you cannot have an ancestral landscape with living ancestors on it; it complicates the signage. Two thousand people. Resettled to a road town with bad water and no fields. By the time the dam was cancelled — budget, politics, nothing noble — the villages were gone, dispersed, and the valley stood empty and preserved like a museum with the people scraped off it.

"I stood on the bluff," Iris said, "and watched the trucks leave, and I understood that I had done that. My report. My signature. My *protection.*" She looked at her hands. "Your father wrote to me while it was happening. He said, *you found something beautiful and you gave it to the people with guns.* I didn't answer him. I should have. That letter was the beginning of the end of us, and I let it be, because answering it meant admitting I'd chosen the work over everything the work was supposed to be for."

"And then Dad died," Oscar said, "and you chose the work again."

"And then your father died," Iris agreed, "and I chose the work again, because choosing the work was the only thing I knew how to do with grief, and I told myself the dig was a monument to him, which was a lie, it was a hiding place, and I let you believe you'd lost to a hole in the ground because the truth was worse. The truth was that I couldn't stand in front of you and be the person who'd failed him and failed those two thousand people and failed you, all with the same signature." She lifted her eyes. "There. That's the whole trench profile. No questions asked — that was the telegram's other meaning. I didn't think I deserved yours."

The chamber was very quiet. The water had come up another finger's width; Oscar could see it without looking, the way you can feel weather coming.

"So the site here," Oscar said slowly. "The terraces under the floodplain. The city. You found it years ago."

"Fourteen years ago. A fisherman showed me a pot his grandfather had pulled from a snag. I traced it. I spent two seasons mapping the floodplain with a magnetometer and a prayer, and I found it, and I wrote the report, and then I sat with it for a month remembering nineteen sixty-eight." Iris patted the oilcloth bundle. "That's the report. Never filed. Field notes, survey data, photographs, correspondence — everything anyone would need to claim this valley's history belongs to it. Including the names of the descendant communities. Rosa Anco's people, though I didn't know her name then. I knew the mission settlement existed. I knew who lived there. I buried the report because filing it meant drawing the same map I drew in sixty-eight, and I swore I'd rather the city stay drowned than watch its people get moved off it again."

"But Hale knows."

"Hale suspects. His backers commissioned a survey of this whole watershed eighteen months ago — ostensibly for fisheries. Their permit application includes a dam feasibility study, and the study's archaeological section says, quote, *no significant pre-colonial deposits identified.* They surveyed right over the top of my terraces and declared them empty, because an empty valley is easy to drown and a full one requires paperwork they don't want." Iris's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "My silence has been convenient for them. Which means my silence has been complicity. Again."

"Mom—"

"Let me finish, because the air won't wait and neither will the water." Iris leaned forward, headlamp swinging, and for a moment the guttering light steadied as if the battery had found one last argument to make. "I brought you here because you are the best salvage diver working these rivers and the only person I trust to get people out of flooded stone. But also because if I die in this chamber, or if Hale takes that bundle off my body, I needed someone else to know it exists and where it goes. It doesn't go to a ministry. It doesn't go to a university. It goes to the people downstream whose grandmothers still sing about this place. Do you understand me, Oscar? Whatever happens — whatever *I* let happen — that paper travels. Promise me."

Oscar looked at the bundle. Oilcloth and cord, smaller than a brick, heavier than any artifact she'd ever raised off a riverbed. Ten days ago she'd have said yes to anything her mother asked just to hear her ask. Five days ago, after the manifest and the radio schedule and the cold logistics, she'd have said no out of spite.

Now she looked at her mother — hip-deep in rising water, battery dying, having finally said aloud the thing nine years of silence had been built to protect — and understood that the silence had never been absence. It had been shame, wearing absence as a coat.

"I promise," Oscar said. "But you're carrying it out yourself. Nobody's dying in here holding anybody's legacy. I didn't fight rapids for three days to deliver a eulogy."

"Oscar—"

"No. That's the deal. You talk, I dive, everybody leaves. That's the only plan I'm accepting." She turned to the others before Iris could argue, because arguing cost air. "Teo. How's the arm?"

"Clean break, I think. Splinted. I can swim if I have to."

"You won't have to swim. You'll breathe off my backup regulator and let me pull you. Ninety meters, gentle current, one constriction at the sixty-meter mark — I've been through it twice, it's passable single file." She looked around the chamber, meeting each face. "Order of extraction: Pilar first, smallest lungs, least calm. Then Amara. Then Teo. Iris last, because Iris is going to help me rig the others and because if I put her first she'll spend the whole time worrying about the rest of you and forget to breathe, and I know her."

Something crossed Iris's face at that — *I know her*, said with the certainty of a daughter who'd catalogued her mother's failures the way Iris catalogued sherds — and it might have been pain, or pride, or both wearing the same expression.

"It'll take me most of tomorrow to rig the sump with guide lines and stage bottles," Oscar went on. "Which means we hold this chamber tonight. Lamps low, talking minimal, bodies still. Anybody claustrophobic, tell me now, because I'd rather know than find out at meter sixty."

Pilar raised her hand halfway, like a student. "I'm scared," she said. "But I'm okay."

"Scared's fine. Scared keeps you sharp." Oscar dug out the ration bars and dealt them out like cards. "Eat. Drink small sips. And somebody tell me something boring — Teo, tell me about your mosses, I don't care, talk about substrate — because we're going to be here a long night and quiet minds start writing horror stories."

Teo, bless him, actually started talking about bryophytes, his voice thin but steady, and the chamber filled with the soft nonsense of liverworts and drainage gradients while the water rose its patient centimeters.

* * *

Later — hours later, when the others had wedged themselves into the driest seams and dropped into exhausted half-sleep — Oscar sat beside her mother at the waterline, shoulder to shoulder, both of them watching the black water breathe.

"You could have told me," Oscar said quietly. "Any year. One letter. *I'm sorry, I was drowning in my own mistake.*"

"I drafted it," Iris said. "Every birthday. Every anniversary of the funeral. I have a drawer full of unsent letters that would embarrass a novelist." She paused. "Your father used to say I could talk to dead civilizations more easily than live relatives. He was right. Stone doesn't leave. Stone forgives you by not caring."

"Dad cared."

"Dad cared enough to keep writing even when I didn't answer." Iris's headlamp flickered, steadied. "He was the better of us, and I married him anyway, and then I ran from both of you because being loved by good people requires a version of myself I'd already buried in sixty-eight." She turned her head, and in the failing light her eyes found Oscar's with an exactness that bypassed nine years entirely. "You dove through a flooded mountain to reach me. After everything. I want you to know I noticed the size of that."

Oscar's throat did something complicated. She watched the water instead.

"It's not forgiveness," she said. "Not yet. But it's a down payment."

"I'll take installments," Iris said. "I'm good at long projects."

Despite everything — despite the rising water and the man with the redacted letter somewhere upstream and the ninety meters of black sump waiting for her in the morning — Oscar laughed, short and real, and felt something in her chest shift that had been locked since a kitchen table in Callao.

Then the sound came.

Faint, upstream, through the flooded galleries — a rhythmic knocking, wrong for water, wrong for stone settling. Metal on rock. Deliberate. From the direction of the fourth chamber, the one that connected to the dry upper passages, the ones that reached the surface behind the waterfall.

Where the trail led. Where the double blaze pointed. Where anyone following Iris's marks would come in.

Oscar was on her feet before she'd finished counting. She killed her lamp to a coal-red glow and put her hand over Pilar's mouth before the girl could make a sound, and in the dark she felt her mother go rigid beside her, felt the oilcloth bundle slide silently from Iris's knee into Iris's jacket, zipped, hidden.

The knocking stopped. Then, clear across the water, unhurried, almost friendly, came a new sound: a man's voice, echoing down the flooded passage, pitched to carry.

"Dr. Vane," Corliss Hale called, cheerful as a census taker. "I know you can hear me. The water's rising faster than your daughter dives. Let's talk business."
