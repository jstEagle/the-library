## 12. Hale's Bargain

The water told them before Hale did.

Oscar heard it first as a change in the drumming — the flood pulse in the lower chambers had a rhythm she'd been tracking for hours, marking its rise against a notch she'd cut in a stalactite with her dive knife. The water was climbing faster now. Half a meter since dawn, maybe more, and the air pocket they'd retreated into was shrinking by degrees she could feel in her chest, a tightness that wasn't entirely fear.

Then, over the hammering of the falls beyond the sump passage, came another sound: an engine. Small. Outboard. Idling somewhere above them, on the river that fed the waterfall.

Iris's head came up like a bird dog's.

"That's not an echo," Teo whispered. He was sitting against the wall with Marta's bandaged leg propped across his knees, his hands stained brown with old blood and cave mud. "There's no echo in here. The rock eats everything."

"Everyone quiet." Iris moved to the mouth of the chamber where the daylight came through in a green-gray shaft, thin as wire. She pressed herself flat against the stone and listened the way she used to listen to Oscar breathing when she was small and sick — total, absolute attention, the rest of the world deleted.

Oscar joined her at the gap. Below, through the falling water, she could see the pool at the base of the staircase, the one she'd swum through two days ago with her lungs burning and her mother's blazes guiding her in. The surface of it shivered. A shadow crossed it — long, low, deliberate — and then Corliss Hale's voice floated up through the roar of the falls, amplified by the stone amphitheater of the gorge, pitched to carry without shouting.

"Dr. Vane! I know you can hear me. The acoustics down there are marvelous."

Oscar's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

* * *

He didn't come in. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. Hale stood on the natural ledge beside the staircase where it emerged from the curtain of water, close enough to be soaked in spray, far enough back that the falls made a wall between him and anything coming up the drowned steps. He'd brought two men with him — big, weathered, standing with the loose patience of people paid by the day — and they'd posted themselves at the top of the staircase, cutting off the dry route out entirely.

So he knew about both exits. Or he suspected, and he was covering both anyway, because covering both was cheap.

"Dr. Vane," he called again, pleasantly. "Or should I say Dr. Vane and family? I have to say, when your radio went dark, I assumed the worst. Rivers are so unforgiving. But then I found your blazes on the trees, and I thought — no. Iris Vane doesn't get lost. Iris Vane *goes*."

Behind Oscar, in the dark of the chamber, she heard Marta shift, a wet drag of breath. The injured woman had been fading in and out for hours, fever climbing, and every hour of Oscar's arithmetic said the same thing: Marta couldn't make the sump. Not today, maybe not ever. Which meant they needed another way out, which meant —

Which meant they might need Hale. And Hale, she was almost certain, had done this same arithmetic from the other side.

"Iris." His voice again, warm as brandy. "You've had time to think. We've all had time to think. I'm going to leave some things at the top of these stairs — a letter, and a pen, because I know how you feel about signing things in your own hand. And I'm going to walk back down to my boat and give you until the water reaches the second gallery. My hydrologist says that's tomorrow night, maybe the morning after. You're a careful woman. You'll want to verify my figures yourself."

A pause. Water thundered.

"You have four people down there," Hale said, gentler now, which was worse. "One of them is bleeding. I have a launch, a stretcher, plasma, and a doctor in Puerto Rojas who owes me a favor. All of that is yours. Everyone walks out. Everyone lives. The price is a signature."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed.

"And if you're wondering what happens if you don't sign," Hale continued, and now the warmth drained out of his voice like water through gravel, leaving something flat and administrative underneath, "then nothing happens. That's the beauty of it. I don't have to do anything at all. The river does it for me. I'll file a report saying the expedition was lost in the flood — tragic, the whole valley's flooding, everyone knows it — and in ten years, when the reservoir goes in, nobody will ever look twice at a missing archaeologist from a decade ago. People vanish into water, Dr. Vane. It's practically traditional."

Oscar felt Iris go very still beside her. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of someone loading a weapon inside themselves.

* * *

They waited until the engine sound faded upstream — Hale keeping his word about withdrawing, or performing withdrawal, and after nine days of him Oscar couldn't tell the difference anymore — and then they gathered around the bundle he'd left at the top of the stairs. Teo fetched it, moving fast and low, and brought it back into the chamber where the last of their three candles made a puddle of light.

It was a oilcloth packet, tied with waxed cord. Inside: a fountain pen. A folded letter on heavy paper. And beneath the letter, a document — official, creased once, stamped.

Iris read the letter first. Her face did something Oscar had never seen it do in thirty-one years of watching it: it aged. Not gradually. All at once, like a cliff face calving.

"What is it?" Teo asked. "What does he want?"

"He wants me to sign over all rights, title, and interest in the discovery to a consortium called Meridian Development Partners." Iris's voice was level, but Oscar could hear the effort holding it level, the way you hear the effort in a rope under load. "Site, artifacts, documentation, survey data, and — quote — 'all associated intellectual property including but not limited to publications, lectures, and derivative works.' In exchange, extraction for five persons, medical care, and — " her mouth twisted — "'non-interference assurances.'"

"Five persons," Oscar repeated. "Marta makes four of us. Who's the fifth?"

Iris didn't answer. She held out the second document instead, the stamped one, and Oscar took it because her hands were already reaching.

It was a government letterhead. Bureau of Antiquities and Hydraulic Resources — a name that tried to be two things at once and succeeded at being neither. Most of the body text was blacked out, thick redaction bars running across the page like the censor had dragged a comb through it. But the redactions were sloppy, or arrogant, or both, and enough survived between the bars to assemble the skeleton of the thing:

*...survey conducted under authority of... ...reservoir project designated Upper Valley Phase II... ...cultural resources within the projected inundation zone shall be deemed legally salvaged upon completion of transfer documentation... ...no further claims, including claims of descent communities, shall be recognized subsequent to...*

"Drowning it," Oscar said. Her own voice sounded far away. "They're going to dam the valley."

"Not just dam it." Iris sat down slowly on a ledge of stone, the letter still in her hand, and for a moment she looked less like the fortress Oscar had been bracing against for nine years and more like what she actually was: a sixty-year-old woman with a bad heart in a wet cave. "Drowning it *legally*. That's the elegance. Once the site is transferred and the reservoir permit is issued, every artifact in the inundation zone becomes state property by statute. The villages downstream — Rosa Anco's people, the families who've been telling stories about this city for four hundred years — they lose any legal standing to claim it. Their ancestors get flooded, and the flood gets called preservation."

Teo made a small sound. Oscar looked over and saw him staring at the letterhead with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the particular nausea of a young man discovering that the institutions he'd been trained to trust had teeth.

"The redacted seal," he said. "On the letter Hale showed us at basecamp. This is what it was."

"This is what it was." Iris folded her hands. "Meridian isn't a salvage outfit. It's a front — a way to move the site into private hands before the public review process can catch it. Hale's backers get the collection, the bureau gets its reservoir without a heritage fight, and the paper trail shows a voluntary transfer from a reputable archaeologist." She looked up, and her eyes found Oscar's in the candlelight. "From me. They need my signature because without it, the provenance is dirty. A stolen site is worth a fraction of a documented one. I am, apparently, the documentation."

"So we refuse," Teo said. "We wait them out. The authorities—"

"Are ten days away," Oscar said, "and the water is two." She'd run the numbers so many times they'd worn grooves in her. "And even if we could hold out — even if we could keep Marta alive that long — the moment we surface anywhere Hale can reach, we're negotiating from a stretcher. He doesn't need to kill us. He just needs us slow."

The candle guttered. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water moved through stone with a patience that made their deadline look hasty.

* * *

They argued for an hour. It was the first real argument the group had ever had, and it had the raw, unpracticed quality of people who hadn't learned each other's rhythms yet — Teo pushing for the authorities, the two younger crew members, Sefu and Dana, splitting down the middle, Marta drifting in and out of fever and offering, once, lucidly, "Don't sign anything while I'm like this, I'm not a good witness."

Through all of it, Oscar watched her mother.

Iris Vane had taught her to read water, stone, weather, and men, in roughly that order, and everything the old lessons said about Corliss Hale was screaming. He had covered both exits because he was thorough. He had left a pen because he was confident. He had given them a deadline measured in floodwater because he was patient — patient the way a man is patient when the outcome is already arranged and only the paperwork remains. And he had offered five extractions, not four.

That number kept snagging in Oscar's mind like a hook in a net.

When the argument burned down to embers, she took her mother aside, back into the deeper dark where the carvings were — the procession of figures descending the wall toward the buried city, their hands open, their faces calm. Four hundred years the Anco stories had kept this place alive without a single document. Now a bureau wanted to erase the story and a consortium wanted to buy the stones, and the only person whose signature could unlock either fate was leaning against the carved wall with her eyes closed.

"The fifth person," Oscar said quietly. "Who is it?"

Iris opened her eyes. For a long moment she studied her daughter's face as though memorizing it, and Oscar understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that she was looking at a woman doing arithmetic of her own.

"You," Iris said.

The word landed like a depth charge.

"Hale knows you're down here. He watched you sabotage his fuel line — don't look surprised, Oscar, I raised you, I know what your handiwork looks like, and he's not a fool either. He knows you're the one who found this place, who got past his people, who's been running circles around his schedule since basecamp went dark. He also knows you'll never let the rest of us drown while you swim free." Iris's voice was very soft. "So the fifth extraction is leverage. It's not for Marta. It's for you. Sign, and everyone lives — and he says it in front of witnesses because he wants you to believe it. He needs you to believe it, because you're the one who'll hold a knife to the rest of us if it turns out to be a lie."

"Then we don't sign."

"If we don't sign, Marta dies of this fever in a wet hole in the ground, and the four of us take our chances with a sump I'm not sure I can swim, and Hale gets the site anyway the moment the flood does his work for him." Iris pushed off the wall, straightened, and something in her spine reassembled itself — the fortress coming back online, brick by brick. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the signature is worthless." Iris reached into the inner pocket of her shirt, where she'd carried things for as long as Oscar could remember — compass, matches, the small tin of emergency ration tablets she never ate. What she drew out now was a fold of waterproof paper, soft as cloth from handling. "Hale needs my signature on a transfer of rights. Rights to a discovery. But a discovery has to be *documented* to be transferred — coordinates, surveys, the photographic record, my field journals. That's what gives the find its value. Its provable, defensible existence."

Oscar stared at her. "Where is it?"

"Where it's been since the day we opened the terrace trench. Where I put it the night I burned the journal page, because I already knew what was coming and I wanted one copy that no search would find." Iris's mouth curved, and it was almost — almost — the ghost of a smile, the first one Oscar had seen from her in nine years. "I hid it in the one place a salvage man would never think to look, because to him it's not a place at all. It's inventory."

"The artifacts," Oscar breathed. "You hid the documentation *with the artifacts.*"

"In the burial chamber, under the lead coffin plate, wrapped in oilcloth and wax. The one room in this mountain Hale's men won't disturb, because disturbing it destroys the very provenance they're paying for." Iris tucked the paper away again. "So. Here is the bargain I propose we make instead of his. We let him believe the signature matters. We let him bring his boat, his stretcher, his doctor. And while he's watching me sign — while every eye he has is on that pen — someone goes into the chamber and takes the documentation out from under him."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"It has to be someone who can hold their breath through the low gallery," Iris continued, "who can move silent in the dark, who can lie to a man like Hale with a straight face and steal from under his nose without breaking stride. Someone who's spent ten years erasing herself from every record she touches." She looked at her daughter, and in the candlelight her eyes were wet and fierce and terribly proud. "Someone like you."

Oscar's heart was slamming. Partly fear. Partly something older and stranger — the feeling, unfamiliar as a foreign language, of being *seen* by her mother, accurately, completely, for the first time in nine years.

"There's a problem with your plan," she said.

"Name it."

"When Hale finds the chamber empty, he's going to know exactly who took it. And I'll be standing right next to you when he does."

Iris nodded slowly, as if Oscar had confirmed something she'd already counted.

"Yes," she said. "That's why the signature will be real. I'll sign his paper, Oscar. Every line of it. Because the moment I do, the transfer becomes a matter of record — and a matter of record can be contested, challenged, voided. A signed document in the hands of thieves is evidence. An unsigned document is just ambition." She held out the fountain pen, and her hand, for the first time Oscar could remember, was shaking. "Let him have his paper. We'll keep the truth. Truth travels lighter."

Outside, far off, the engine sound returned — idling, waiting, patient as water finding its level. The flood had risen another finger-width past Oscar's notch.

Tomorrow night, the second gallery would drown. Tomorrow night, Hale would come back for his signature.

Oscar took the pen.

* * *

They spent the remaining hours preparing, and it was strange, Oscar thought, how much preparation could happen in silence — Sefu rigging a litter for Marta from pack straps and tent poles, Dana counting their remaining candles down to the stub, Teo copying the key passages of the redacted letter onto the back of a field map in case the original was lost. Iris sat apart, composing, of all things, a gracious acceptance letter to Corliss Hale, formal and dignified and full of the language of surrender.

Oscar checked her dive gear by feel. Mask. Knife. The spare line coiled at her hip. The low gallery between this chamber and the burial rooms ran chest-deep at current levels; by tomorrow it would be a sump, which meant tonight was the only window, and tonight Hale's men were watching the stairs.

She'd go in with the last of the light, when the falls' noise was at its daily peak. She'd be back before full dark, or she wouldn't be back at all.

At the chamber mouth, pausing, she looked back once. Candlelight on wet stone. Her mother bent over a letter that was really a weapon. Teo asleep sitting up with his hand still resting on Marta's ankle, as if touch alone could anchor her to the world.

Nine years of silence, and it had taken a flood, a villain, and a drowning city to teach Oscar the simplest lesson of her life: that her mother had been fighting this war alone for a decade, and had never once asked for help, because asking was the one thing prouder people couldn't do.

Well. She wasn't proud anymore. None of them could afford it.

Oscar slipped into the dark, and the mountain closed over her like a mouth.
