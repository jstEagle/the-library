## 13. The Long Way Down

The lantern light made the water look like poured glass, black and patient, and Oscar stood at its edge doing the arithmetic she had been trying not to do since the moment Hale's boots faded up the passage.

One sump. Maybe thirty meters, maybe forty — she couldn't know, because the far side was a guess and the near side was a lie of stillness that would end the moment the flood found this chamber too. Four swimmers. One set of gear that amounted to a mask with a cracked strap, two dive reels, half a tank of compressed air she'd hauled upriver on her back for three days because she was the kind of paranoid who carried salvage equipment on a rescue, and God, God, she had never loved her own paranoia more.

And one man who could not swim it at all.

"Say it out loud," Iris said behind her. "Whatever you're calculating. Say it so we're all in the same equation."

Oscar didn't turn around. Her mother's voice had gone hoarse over nine years and one flood season, but it still had that edge to it, the field voice, the voice that meant *stop feeling and start working*. It used to make Oscar furious. Right now it was the only rope she had.

"The sump runs under the rock spine," Oscar said. "I spotted it yesterday when I went looking for air movement. There's a current pulling through the crack at the waterline — cold, steady. That means an outlet. The question is whether the outlet is big enough to breathe through or small enough to drown in."

"And if it's small?"

"Then we come back and try the chimney over the falls, and by then the water will have taken the lower gallery and we'll be climbing a waterfall in spate." She crouched, touched the surface. Cold bit her fingertips like a warning. "So I go first. Alone. If there's air on the other side, I come back, and we run it one at a time on my line."

"Oscar." Teo Reyes's voice cracked on her name. He was sitting against the wall with his knees drawn up, his glasses fogged, his hands shaking in a way he clearly hated and couldn't stop. He'd nearly drowned once before — he'd told her that on the steamer dock, back when they were strangers, back when kindness was still cheap. "The line. If it snags—"

"It'll snag. Lines always snag. That's why you don't panic, you feel your way back along it, and you trust the person holding the other end." She looked at him then, and made herself smile, because fear was contagious and someone in this room had to be immune. "That's me. I'm very trustworthy. Ask anyone who's ever paid me."

Nobody laughed. Fine. She hadn't expected them to.

* * *

They spent the next hour turning the chamber into a classroom, and it was the strangest seminar of Oscar Vane's life: four adults kneeling on wet stone by lantern light while she taught them how not to die in the dark.

She started with breathing, because everything started with breathing.

"You will want to hold your breath. Your body is going to beg you to hold your breath the second your face goes under, because every animal you descend from knows that water means death. You are going to override several million years of instinct, and you're going to do it calmly, or you're going to die. So we practice." She demonstrated: slow inhale through the nose, longer exhale through pursed lips, the rhythm divers called *four count down, six count up*. "Breathe like this the whole way. Panic breaths are short and fast and they use your air twice as quick and they fog your brain until you do something stupid, like tearing off your mask. People do that. Drowned people do that."

Marta Okafor sat propped against the supply crates with her leg splinted from knee to ankle, a broken femur wrapped in a torn tent fly and two tent poles, and she watched Oscar with the flat, unblinking attention of someone taking inventory. She was the expedition's conservator — the woman who kept ten-thousand-year-old textiles from crumbling into dust — and she had taken the fall in the rockslide two days ago without making a sound, which told Oscar everything about what kind of person she was.

"Marta," Oscar said gently. "You don't have to practice."

"I know." Marta flexed the toes of her splinted foot, testing, the way she'd probably tested a hundred fragile things. "I'm watching anyway. In case."

In case of what, neither of them said.

Teo practiced his breathing until his hands steadied. Iris practiced like she did everything else — precisely, silently, as though the technique were something she'd read in a paper twenty years ago and simply never needed until now. The fourth survivor, the surveyor named Dunmore, practiced badly and knew it, and kept glancing at Marta, and kept not saying anything either.

Oscar rigged the line. She tied one end to the iron mooring ring some forgotten prospector had driven into the rock a century ago — the ring was sound; she'd hung her whole weight from it twice — and paid out reel after reel, knotting markers every five meters so that a hand in total darkness could count its progress. Then she laid out the rest of the arithmetic, out loud, because Iris had been right about that much: say it so everyone stands in the same equation.

"One diver at a time. Ten minutes between. If I'm not back in forty minutes from my first descent, Dunmore takes the second reel and follows my line anyway, because the alternative is waiting here for the water." She checked her watch by lantern light. "Flood crests tomorrow night, maybe dawn the day after. This chamber floods last — it's high, it's dry, it buys us time. But time is the only thing in this cave that isn't running out."

"And me?" Marta asked.

The lantern hissed. Somewhere deep in the rock, water moved with a sound like slow applause.

"We'll get to me," Oscar said.

* * *

She dove the sump alone first, because that was the deal and because no one could stop her anyway.

The crack at the waterline swallowed her like a throat. Cold closed over her head and the world became pressure and blackness and the thin silver thread of her own line running through her gloved fist. She breathed her own rhythm — four down, six up — and let the current take her sideways and down, counting knots, feeling the ceiling press close enough that her tank scraped stone with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard.

This was the part people never understood about salvage diving, she thought, in the place where thinking still worked. It wasn't bravery. Bravery was for moments. This was the opposite of a moment — it was a long gray country where nothing happened except your own heartbeat and your own discipline, minute after minute, and the only thing between you and death was the agreement you'd made with yourself beforehand to keep being boring. Keep the rhythm. Count the knots. Trust the line.

Nine knots. The passage bent upward. Her chest began to ache — not from the breathing, from the cold, from the squeeze of rock overhead, from nine years of things she hadn't said — and she made herself exhale longer, six counts, seven, and rise.

Her hand broke the surface first. Then her head. Air — real air, moving air, tasting of wet stone and green growing things — and beyond her, impossibly, gray daylight.

She'd come up inside the gorge below the falls, in a slot canyon the flood hadn't reached yet, a corridor of churning white visible fifty meters downstream where the tributary threw itself over rocks. Above her, the walls rose sheer and dripping. But there was a gravel bank. There was a route along it, narrow but walkable, angling down toward the treeline. And there was sky.

Oscar floated on her back and laughed, once, a raw sound that bounced off the canyon walls, and then she cried for exactly eleven seconds — she counted, because discipline — and then she went back down the way she had come, because four people were waiting in the dark and the clock was the clock.

* * *

"There's air," she said, hauling herself out onto the stone. "There's a canyon. There's a walkable route down to the forest. We can do this."

The relief in the chamber was a physical thing, a loosening, and Oscar watched it move through the others like wind through grass — Teo's shoulders dropping, Dunmore exhaling a prayer in Portuguese, even Iris closing her eyes for one long moment. Only Marta didn't move.

"How many can swim it?" Marta asked.

Oscar knelt and started checking the regulator, the spare mask, the line. "All of you. With instruction. It's a straight passage, well-marked, and I'll be shuttling. Teo first — you've got the steadiest nerves and I want my best student out early in case something goes wrong behind him."

"I'm not—" Teo started.

"You are. Don't argue with the dive instructor." She looked up. "Dunmore next. Then my mother. I go last, sweeping the line."

"And me." Marta said it plainly, the way she might read an inventory number. "You've done the sum, Oscar. I've watched you do sums all day. A woman with a broken femur cannot do a forty-meter sump. Even if she could hold her breath, even if you dragged her — the passage bends. There's a section where the ceiling comes down. You showed us on the map you drew. A rigid body doesn't bend."

"We build a litter. We float you—"

"On half a tank of air, through a bend, in water that's rising?" Marta shook her head slowly. "I'm a conservator. My entire career is knowing what can be saved and what saving would destroy. You save three people and lose four, or you save four and lose one, and everyone in this room already knows which sum you're doing. I'm just refusing to let you pretend otherwise."

"Then I refuse to let you finish the sentence," Oscar said, and her voice came out harder than she intended, harder than the situation deserved, and she hated herself for it. "Nobody gets left. That's not — I didn't come up this river to leave somebody in a hole."

"No," Marta said. "You came up this river for her." She nodded at Iris. "And she came up this river for the valley, and the valley is full of people who are going to need to know what's down here, and someone has to stay with it. Someone who understands what these things are." Her hand rested on the crate beside her — the field documentation, the photographs, the sealed sample tubes, the record of a city older than any nation that would claim it. "Let me finish the sentence, Oscar. Please. I've had two days to write it."

* * *

Iris took her turn in the sump at dusk, or what passed for dusk forty meters underground, which was nothing at all — just the lantern burning lower and everyone's watches agreeing it should be dark somewhere.

Before she went, mother and daughter stood together at the waterline, and the old silence rose up between them like groundwater, familiar, almost comfortable, and Oscar broke it because she was tired of carrying it.

"When you surface out there," she said, "don't wait. Start down the canyon. Teo will be with you. I'll bring Dunmore, and I'll be right behind you. All right?"

"All right." Iris adjusted the mask strap with steady hands. Then, quietly, without looking at her: "Your grandfather taught you to dive. On the coast. You were eight."

Oscar stared at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because I was there. I was the one who bought you the first mask." Iris's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You think I forgot everything, Oscar. I forgot nothing. I just chose wrong, and then I was too proud to say so, and pride is a very expensive habit." She stepped to the edge of the water. "Come find me on the other side. Both of us owe Marta better than tears right now."

Then she was gone, a shadow sliding under black glass, and Oscar stood at the line's anchor with her fist around the reel and her heart in her fist too, counting knots she couldn't see, trusting her mother to keep being boring, to keep the rhythm, to come back.

Eleven knots. Twelve. Thirteen. The line went slack, then taut, then slack — moving, moving, alive —

Fourteen. Fifteen. And then the reel spun free in her hand, feeding out, and stopped, and the pull from the far side came steady and strong and purposeful, and Oscar let herself breathe.

* * *

Dunmore went through at midnight, panicking halfway, snagging his own mask on a knob of rock exactly the way Oscar had warned, and coming up the far side alive anyway because the line held and the rhythm held and because Oscar talked him through it by tug-code, two pulls for *breathe*, three for *keep going*, one long pull for *almost*. When he surfaced in the canyon he vomited river water and laughed and cried all at once, and Oscar, listening to the line signals relay his survival back to her, put her forehead against the cold stone and gave thanks to whatever god ran salvage operations.

Which left the chamber with two people in it, and one sum left to do.

Marta had spent the hours arranging things. Of course she had. The documentation was packed in waterproof sleeves and lashed into a bundle sized for one diver to carry. The artifacts that could be moved were crated and stacked above the projected floodline, catalogued in her precise hand, each label a small act of faith that someone would come back for them. The lantern sat beside her, turned low to save fuel, throwing her shadow huge against the carvings on the wall — the terraced city, the river serpent, the figures with raised arms that might have been greeting the water or begging it to stop.

"Sit down," Marta said. "You look terrible."

"I've been told I always look like this."

"Sit down anyway." Oscar sat. Marta studied her for a moment with those inventory eyes. "Tell me about the arithmetic again. Out loud. I want to hear you defend it."

"There's no version where you swim."

"Why not a litter? Half-measures. Tell me why they fail."

"Because the passage narrows at knot nine and bends at knot twelve, and a rigid body jams in a bend, and a jammed body blocks the channel for everyone behind it, and the water is rising through all of it." Oscar's jaw ached. She realized she was clenching it. "Because I've pulled bodies out of sumps before. Two of them. Both times someone loved the math more than the person."

"So you love the person more than the math." Marta smiled faintly. "Good. That's the correct order. Now hear mine." She shifted against the crates, wincing, settling. "The flood takes the lower galleries tonight. Tomorrow it takes this one. Everything in these crates goes under — and with it, the only complete record of a city that Rosa Anco's people have carried in songs for four hundred years. Unless someone stays. Someone stays, keeps the record dry, keeps the light, marks the water level, and when the rescuers come — Hale's men, the bureau, whoever wins the argument upstream — they find a witness. Not a corpse. A witness, with a catalogue, and a name, and a story about whose city this actually is." She tapped the crate. "I'm not staying to die, Oscar. I'm staying to testify. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"The rescuers might not come."

"They came for you," Marta said. "A telegram, a steamer, three days upriver, alone. Someone came for you. Someone will come for me." She paused. "Besides. My grandmother was a schoolteacher in a flooded valley too — different river, different government, same story. They drowned her town for a reservoir and called it progress, and the thing she never forgave was that nobody stayed to write down what was lost." Her voice didn't waver. "I stay. Write it in your report however you need to. But we both know it's the only sum that balances."

Oscar looked at the water, black and patient. At the carvings. At the woman who had chosen this the way she did everything — carefully, completely, without flinching.

"If I ever tell this story," Oscar said finally, "you don't die in it. I need you to know that. Whatever happens, in the version I carry out of here, you live."

"Then you'd better come back and check your facts," Marta said.

* * *

Oscar made the crossing twice more that night — once to escort the final bundle of records, once alone, sweeping the line clear. Each time she passed through the throat of the sump she thought of Marta's voice keeping its steady rhythm in a chamber filling with the river's patience, and each time the thought hurt worse, and she let it, because pain was proof she was still the kind of person who didn't leave people in holes, and she needed the proof.

On the far side, in the gray slot of the canyon, the survivors waited among the wet stones. Iris stood when Oscar surfaced, and pulled her up by the arm with both hands, and for a moment neither of them managed any words at all.

Then Teo pointed downstream, where the canyon opened toward the forest — and froze.

Downriver, where the white water thundered, a light was moving. Not daylight. A beam, sweeping the canyon walls, methodical, patient, working upstream against the current.

Hale's people. Or the bureau's. It didn't matter which; the letter had only ever had one seal.

"Off the bank," Oscar breathed. "Into the trees. Now. Quietly."

Behind them, muffled by forty meters of rock and rising water, the flood began to take the lower chambers — and somewhere in the dark, a single lantern burned low beside a woman who had decided to be the record.

They slipped into the rainforest as the beam swept past the waterfall, close enough to touch, and did not look back.
