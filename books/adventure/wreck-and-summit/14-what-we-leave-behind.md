## 14. What We Leave Behind

The water in the third chamber had risen past the carved lintel while they argued, and it kept rising, patient as arithmetic.

Oscar stood with her boots in it, feeling the cold climb her shins, and watched Marta Okafor do the same math she was doing. The surveyor sat on a dry shelf of stone with her left leg propped on a coil of rope, the splint Teo had built from a tent pole and duct tape dark with seepage. Above the knee, the gash had closed ugly. She couldn't swim a sump. Everyone in the chamber knew it, and no one had said it yet, because saying it out loud would make it a decision instead of a fact.

The facts were these: one route out, underwater, forty meters of flooded passage that Oscar had found by lamplight two hours ago and not yet told anyone about. Four swimmers who could maybe be taught to make it. One who couldn't. A flood cresting somewhere above them, pushing water down through the mountain's throat at a rate that would fill this chamber — all of it, every gallery, every dry shelf — within a day, perhaps less.

Iris stood apart from the group with her back to the wall, arms crossed over her chest like a woman holding her own ribs together. Her face in the lamplight was gray. Oscar had catalogued every small wrongness since the reunion: the way her mother leaned when she thought no one was watching, the way her breath went shallow after any exertion, the way she'd pressed two fingers under her jaw twice in the last hour and counted something private behind her eyes. Whatever it was, it wasn't for now. Nothing in this room was for now except the water.

"Say it," Marta said.

No one moved. The lamp hissed. Somewhere deeper in the cavern system, water fell through stone with a sound like slow applause.

"Someone has to say it," Marta said, "or we'll stand here being polite until the polite kills us." She looked at Oscar. "You're the diver. You've been doing the numbers since you got here. Say it."

Oscar crouched so they were level. Up close, Marta's skin had gone the color of old candle wax, but her eyes were steady, and there was something almost amused in them, the way there always was — Marta was the one who'd named the camp mule *Bureaucracy* because it carried everything and complained constantly, and she'd laughed hardest at her own joke even though no one else had.

"One way out," Oscar said. "A sump behind the lower gallery. Forty meters, maybe fifty. It comes up outside the waterfall curtain. I can get four people through it if I teach them fast and nothing goes wrong."

"And me?"

"You can't kick with that leg. You'd panic at the far end, or halfway, or you'd snag the line and take someone else down with you. That's not an insult. It's physics."

"I know it's physics." Marta smiled. "I did the surveying on this expedition. I know exactly how much water is between us and the sky."

"So we wait," Teo said. His voice cracked on it. He was twenty-six and he had dirt on his cheekbone he didn't know about and he was looking at Marta the way people look at a fire they can't put out. "Rescuers come. We hold out. Someone brings gear, a stretcher, a—"

"The rescuers are ten days out," Iris said quietly. "The water is one day away. Maybe less." She hadn't moved from the wall. Her voice had the flatness she used for dates and site coordinates, and Oscar recognized it now for what it was: the sound of a woman spending everything she had to keep her hands from shaking. "Teo. There isn't a version of this where help arrives in time. You know that. You've known it since the check-in failed."

"There has to be—"

"Marta." Iris pushed off the wall and crossed the chamber, and Oscar saw the effort it cost her, the careful distribution of weight, and hated herself for noticing, for filing it away, for already planning around it. Iris knelt — slowly, badly — beside the shelf. "Marta, listen to me. If you stay, you will not be abandoned. I am telling you that as the person responsible for putting you in this hole, and I am telling it to you as a promise, and I have broken very few promises in my life and none of them were this kind."

"Iris." Marta reached out and took her mother's-age hand off her knee and held it, briefly, like closing a ledger. "Stop performing. I'm not staying because of a promise. I'm staying because of what's in the next chamber."

* * *

They went to look at it together — those who could walk. The passage narrowed, then opened into the gallery where the team had sheltered during the first rise, and the lamplight caught it all again: the burial niches cut into the walls in rows, hundreds of them, each sealed with a fitted stone; the painted band running the length of the ceiling, ochre and charcoal, figures walking in procession along a river rendered in white; the offering bench where the artifacts lay wrapped in the team's spare clothing because there had been nowhere else and no one could bear to leave them on bare stone.

Ceramic vessels with incised serpents. A copper pectoral worked into the shape of a bird mid-dive. Textiles gone to lace but still, unmistakably, textiles — patterns no loom in any museum held. And the stelae, three of them, leaning against the wall where the team had winched them clear of the waterline days ago, their glyphs crisp as yesterday's carving.

"This is why," Marta said.

She moved down the gallery slowly, hopping, one hand on the wall, touching nothing. "Hale wants these. The bureau wants these drowned. Every government and every collector between here and the coast wants these in a glass case with a placard written by someone who's never stood in this valley." She turned. In the lamp light her face was fierce and exhausted and completely clear. "And the only reason they don't have them yet is that they're down here, behind a hundred meters of flooded stone, guarded by nobody."

"They're not yours to guard," Iris said. "That was the whole point. They belong to the Anco. To Rosa Anco's people. We agreed—"

"We agreed they stay *here*. In their city. Until their descendants come for them." Marta lowered herself onto the offering bench, carefully, easing the leg. "So somebody stays with them. Somebody keeps the records dry, keeps the lamps, keeps watch, and when the rescuers finally come — ten days, twelve, whatever it is — somebody walks them out through the front door in daylight, documented, witnessed, accounted for. Not smuggled. Not drowned. Handed over."

"That's suicide dressed up as curating," Oscar said.

"It's my job dressed up as curating." Marta looked at her, and there it was again, the amusement, thin but real. "Oscar Vane. I've read your file. Iris talks about you more than she thinks she does — don't look at her like that, she knows it's true. You spent ten years becoming the best salvage diver on this river so you could pull things out of the water before the water takes them. Well." She gestured at the rising black water visible through the passage mouth, already lapping at the threshold of the gallery. "Here's the last job. Everything in this room goes into the water in a day unless someone stays and moves it up to the high gallery and holds it there. I can't walk out. But I can haul pots up a slope on my arms, and I can keep a logbook, and I am extremely good at sitting still."

"No," Teo said.

"Teo."

"No. We draw lots. We split the supplies. Someone stays *with* you—"

"And halves everyone's air and doubles everyone's risk and makes the swim four people instead of five, which changes nothing for me and might kill one of you." Marta's voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled once, like a struck string, and was silenced. "Teo Reyes. You brought me orchid samples every morning for six weeks because you noticed I liked them. Don't ruin it now."

Teo stood there with his fists at his sides and his eyes shining, twenty-six years old and learning in real time that some arithmetic has no kind answer. He looked at Iris. Iris looked at the floor.

"He stays with you until the water forces him out," Oscar heard herself say. "That's the deal or there's no deal."

"Oscar—" Iris started.

"Not negotiable." Oscar turned to Marta. "He helps you move everything to the high gallery tonight, all of it, everything above the projected crest line — I'll mark it. Then he swims out with us. You'll have the lamps, the food cache, the radio — the transmitter's dead but the receiver might catch weather bands, and knowing what the river's doing is worth more than talking right now. Rescuers reach the tributary camp in eight to ten days. The high gallery stays dry even at full crest — I checked the flow marks twice. You hold this place for ten days, Marta. That's all. Ten days, and then this room fills with flashlights and stretchers and men with ropes, and you walk out of here on someone's shoulder with a logbook full of provenance records that no lawyer on earth can argue with."

Marta studied her for a long moment. "You've thought about the lawyers."

"I've thought about Hale. Same thing, worse suit."

Something moved through the gallery then — not laughter exactly, but its shadow, passing across all four of them at once, and for a moment the water noise receded and it was just people in a beautiful terrible place agreeing to something none of them wanted.

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night.

Oscar marked the high gallery's safe line with chalk and lamp soot — *this stroke and below, the water reaches; above, you live* — and then she and Teo ferried the collection upward while Marta directed from the bench like a general with a map made of memory. Every artifact got logged before it moved: photographed by lamplight against a scale bar improvised from a ruler, described in Marta's tight surveyor's hand, assigned a number. Vessel 1 through 34. Pectoral, copper, 1. Textile fragments, 11 lots. Stelae 1 through 3, weights estimated, positions recorded relative to the niche wall. She wrote the location of the high gallery itself into the record in three forms — compass bearing, pace count, and a sketch — so that even if every map burned, the collection could be found by anyone who could read a page.

"Why three ways?" Teo asked, hauling the second stele up the slope on a sling, sweating, shaking, refusing to stop.

"Because paper burns and memories drown," Marta said, "but redundancy is love, Teo. Write that down."

At some point Iris appeared at the top of the slope with a thermos of the terrible camp coffee and four tin cups, and they stopped, all of them, and drank it in the lamplight among the niches, and nobody said anything about the water, which had climbed another hand's width and now breathed against the base of the stairs with a sound like a sleeping animal.

It was Marta who broke the quiet, the way she always did.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my grandmother kept a shop in Lagos. Fabric. Rows and rows of it, colors you couldn't name. When she died, the family fought over the shop for two years — who owned it, who deserved it, whose name went on the deed. And the whole time, the fabric sat there. Moths got into the indigo. Rain came through the roof and ruined the adire, the ones she'd dyed herself." She turned her cup slowly. "And I understood, standing at the funeral, that the fighting was never about the cloth. The cloth was just the excuse. What they were really fighting over was the right to say *she was mine.*" She looked around the gallery, at the niches, at the procession marching along the ceiling toward a river that had swallowed their city a thousand years ago. "Hale and the bureau and every thief on this river — they're fighting over the right to say this place was theirs. Found it, claimed it, saved it, own it. And the only way to beat them is for the people who actually get to say *mine* — the ones with the grandmothers — to say it first. Out loud. On paper. With witnesses." She raised her cup an inch. "So that's the mission now. Not rescue. Witness. Rescue is just how we get the witnesses home."

Nobody spoke. Teo wiped his face with his wrist, pretending it was sweat.

Then Marta set down her cup and said, briskly, in the voice she used for closing a day's survey: "Right. Stelae three still needs logging, and I want the textile lots repacked with the silica gel before you go, and Oscar — I need you to show me how the receiver works, and where you've cached the spare batteries, and I need you to do it now, because I intend to spend my last hour with you lot complaining about the coffee, and I can't do that properly with unfinished business."

* * *

The goodbye happened at the water's edge, and it was small, which was the only mercy available.

Oscar checked the sump line one final time — anchored, tensioned, run through the flooded passage and up through the waterfall curtain to air — and then stood in the third chamber with the others while the water licked at their knees and the lamp guttered low.

Iris went first. She took Marta's face in both hands, and whatever she said was spoken against Marta's forehead, too quietly for the rest of them, and Marta's eyes closed, and when Iris stepped back her composure had a crack straight down the middle of it and she let everyone see it, which for Iris Vane was the loudest thing she'd ever done.

Teo hugged her hard enough to lift her, gently, off the good leg, and said something into her shoulder that made her laugh — actually laugh — and then he couldn't stop holding on, and she patted his back twice, firmly, like landing a plane, until he let go.

Then it was Oscar.

"You'll take care of her," Marta said. Nodding past Oscar's shoulder, at Iris. Not a question.

"She won't let me."

"Even better. Keep her arguing. Arguing keeps the heart going." Marta gripped Oscar's forearm, diver to diver, hard. "Listen. The file I read — the quarry work, the erased names, the woman who deletes herself from every record she touches. I understand it. I spent years making myself useful enough that nobody would ask where I came from." Her grip tightened. "But you can't witness anything if you're not in the picture, Oscar. That's the whole trick of it. Somebody has to stand in the frame and say *I was here, I saw this, it mattered.*" She released her arm and stepped back, balancing on the shelf, the lamplight catching the wet stone and the wrapped vessels and the painted procession overhead, all of it hers to keep for ten days. "So get in the picture. All the way upriver and all the way back. Promise me that instead of promising me you'll come back for me — because I already know you'll come back for me. You're predictable that way. It's your best quality."

Oscar's throat had closed entirely. She managed a nod.

"Go," Marta said. "The water's not getting shallower and neither is my patience."

They went. Oscar went last through the sump, as she had to, towing the line, counting kicks in the black, feeling the mountain close over her and then open — and then they were out, all four, coughing and gasping in the roar behind the waterfall curtain, hauling themselves onto wet stone in gray dawn light while the flood behind them swallowed the lower chambers chamber by chamber, exactly on schedule.

From inside the rock, faint through thirty meters of stone and falling water, came three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

Marta's all-clear. Her daily signal, starting today, starting now.

Oscar raised her fist and knocked it against the stone three times, and felt Teo and Iris do the same beside her, and then there was only the waterfall and the river, risen brown and furious below them, and the long downriver country waiting — and somewhere on it, a launch with a redacted letter and armed men, coming upstream toward everything they had left behind to protect.
