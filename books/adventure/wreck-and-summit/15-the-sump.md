## 15. The Sump

The water in the third chamber was black and patient.

Oscar knelt at its edge with her lamp turned low, watching the surface for the tell she'd been taught to look for before she was old enough to spell her own name: the slow lateral drift of debris, the way a single leaf spun once and slid left instead of right. Current. Not much of it — a whisper of it — but current meant the sump connected to something moving, and moving meant out.

"Say it again," Iris said behind her. "Slowly."

"The passage runs under the ridge for maybe forty meters. It narrows to a crawl about halfway — I saw it when I came in from below, before I found your blazes. On the far side it opens into the plunge pool under the falls." Oscar kept her eyes on the water. "It's diveable. Barely. No visibility once we stir up the silt, so it's all line work and touch. One at a time, hands on the rope, follow my tug signals."

"And Marta."

"Marta stays."

Iris said nothing. In the lamplight her face had gone the color of the limestone around them, gray shot through with white veins, and Oscar understood that her mother was doing the same arithmetic she'd been doing since the water started rising — subtracting one name from the list of survivors and trying to find a way to make the sum come out honest. There wasn't one. They both knew it. Marta had known it first, which was somehow worse.

"I'll go last," Teo said.

Oscar turned. He stood at the chamber's edge with his pack already stripped down to nothing — no notebook, no specimen tubes, nothing but the clothes he wore and the waterproof bag holding Rosa Anco's name written in his careful hand alongside everything they'd memorized about the site. His lips were pale. His voice wasn't.

"You're the strongest swimmer after me," Oscar said. "You go second-to-last so I can be behind you."

"That's exactly why I should go last." He managed something that wanted to be a smile. "If I panic, you'll be there. If anyone panics, better it's me, because I'm the only one who's terrified enough to admit it out loud, and admitting it is half the battle. My grandmother taught me that. She also taught me never to swim in brown water, so this whole plan is an insult to her memory, but the other half stands."

Behind him, Serrano laughed — a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man who hadn't slept in two days and had stopped expecting to. He and Duarte were rigging the guideline now, unspooling the nylon from Iris's survey reels, tying it off to a fallen stela whose carved face watched them with eyes worn smooth by a thousand years of floodwater. The glyphs spiraled down its chest like water finding its way downhill. Oscar had caught herself looking at them too often. *They knew about the floods,* she thought. *They built their dead above the waterline and left instructions.*

"All right." Iris stepped forward, and something in her posture changed — the field commander assembling herself out of the exhausted woman, piece by piece, like someone dressing a wound. "Order of descent. Serrano first, then Duarte, then Teo, then myself. Oscar leads. Nobody lets go of the line. Nobody rushes. If you lose the line, you stop, you feel along the wall to your right, and you wait. She will come back for you." Her eyes found Oscar's across the black water. "Won't you."

It wasn't a question. It was nine years compressed into a single sentence, and every apology Oscar had rehearsed on the steamer and discarded at basecamp and swallowed in the dark of the caverns was suddenly unnecessary, because here it was, the plain truth underneath all of it: *she trusts me with their lives, and she has always trusted me with their lives, and neither of us knew how to say anything smaller than that.*

"Yeah," Oscar said. "I will."

* * *

They spent twenty minutes on drills in the shallows, because Iris Vane did not send people into the dark unprepared even when the dark was rising to meet them. Oscar made each of them practice the tugs twice with their eyes closed: one pull, *continue*; two pulls, *stop*; three, *come to me*; a long steady drag, *something's wrong, hold on*. She made them breathe from the spare regulator until the motion was reflex. She made Serrano take off his boots because the laces would snag, and she watched him do it with the expression of a man undressing in front of strangers, and she didn't soften it, because softness got people killed underwater.

Marta sat on the stela's base with her splinted leg propped on a block of masonry, supervising with a clipboard she'd insisted on keeping even though there was nothing left to record but the water level, which recorded itself.

"Dive watch says the main channel crests in four hours," she said. "When it does, this chamber goes under completely. So whatever happens in that sump—" she tapped the black water with the clipboard's edge, "—happens today."

"We know," Duarte said quietly.

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway because saying things out loud is how you keep from screaming them later." Marta looked at Oscar, and her voice dropped into something private, just for the two of them. "The high gallery behind the burial shelf — the one with the painted ceiling. There's a dry ledge, air space, probably good for days if the water doesn't find a chimney up. I can hold there. And if anyone comes looking — Hale's people, rescuers, whoever — I'm a witness, not a body. That matters. You taught me that without meaning to, back when you were inventorying supplies and counting everything twice. Nothing survives unless somebody writes it down."

Oscar crouched beside her. Up close, Marta's pain sat behind her eyes like a lamp turned low, banked but burning.

"I'll come back for you," Oscar said. "After the flood drops. Three days, maybe four."

"I know." Marta smiled. "That's why I'm not scared. Go drown yourself responsibly, Vane."

* * *

Serrano went first.

Oscar led him to the lip of the sump, where the water slid over a lip of stone and vanished into a throat barely wider than her shoulders, and pressed the regulator into his hands one last time. "Breathe. Don't think. The line does the thinking." Then she tipped forward and let the water close over her head, and the world became a lamp beam and a rope and the sound of her own heart.

Blackout dark was not like night. Night had edges, horizons, the faint gray suggestion of a world beyond. This was dark with the volume turned up — dark that pressed against her eyeballs, dark that made her own hands doubtful things at the end of her arms. Her lamp cut a cone of murk that filled instantly with stirred silt, turning the beam into a wall of drifting chalk. She could see perhaps half a meter. She could feel everything: the cold layering itself against her skin as she descended, the line running smooth through her gloved fist, the walls narrowing until her shoulders brushed stone on both sides and the passage became exactly what she'd feared — a crawl, a throat, a place built by water for water.

One pull. Serrano acknowledged. Good.

The narrow section came at what her watch said was six minutes in, though her body insisted it had been an hour. She'd remembered it tight; memory had flattered it. She turned onto her side, exhaling slowly to shrink her chest, and pulled herself through a gap that scraped her tank, her hip, the length of her spine. Behind her the line went taut, then slack, then taut — Serrano following, doing it right, breathing slow. Ahead the gap opened, widened, and the ceiling lifted, and for ten blessed meters she could almost see her own fins.

Then the line snagged.

She felt it before she understood it — a sudden resistance, the rope dragging against something rough, and when she backed up a body-length and swept her lamp she found the problem: the line had run across a shelf of broken flowstone, and the nylon had looped over a jagged spur, and somewhere past that spur Serrano had stopped pulling because the line wouldn't give and he was following protocol, stopped, waiting, in a flooded crawlway with a finite supply of air.

Two pulls. *Stop.* She worked her way to the snag, got her knife out, and then didn't use it — cutting meant a free end, and a free end in blackout dark meant a diver with nothing. Instead she fed slack, reached past the spur, and hauled the loop off with her fingers, tearing a glove in the process, feeling the stone open a hot line across her knuckles that the water immediately numbed. The line ran free. One pull. A pause that lasted a geological age. Then one pull back, and they moved.

* * *

Duarte went through clean. Teo did not.

Later, Oscar would reconstruct it in pieces, the way she reconstructed wrecks — from fragments, from what the damage told her. The pieces were these: Teo entering the water with his jaw set and his grandmother's advice folded away somewhere behind his ribs. Teo making it through the narrow section, because she heard the scrape of his tank on stone and felt the line shudder and continue. And then, eight meters past the snag, in the widest part of the whole passage, the line going violently wrong — jerking, whipping, yanked sideways in a rhythm that had nothing to do with signals and everything to do with a human body deciding, all at once and beyond any training, that it wanted out.

Three pulls. Then five. Then a long frantic series that meant nothing and everything.

Oscar reversed along the line hand over hand, lamp useless in the silt cloud he'd raised, swimming blind into a chaos of bubbles and thrashing limbs. Her shin cracked against rock. Her lamp clipped something and spun, painting the darkness with strobing ghosts. And then her free hand hit him — shoulder first, then the wild architecture of his arms, then the terrible discovery that his regulator was out of his mouth and his mouth was open and he was trying to breathe water.

She got the spare reg into him on instinct, jamming it past his teeth, and he clamped down on it so hard she felt the mouthpiece flex. For a moment he just hung there, hauling air, his hands locked on her forearm hard enough to bruise bone. Then the panic came back in a wave — she felt it arrive through his grip, the animal certainty that stillness meant death — and he started to claw upward, toward a ceiling that was stone, toward a surface that did not exist.

She did the thing her mother had taught her when she was fifteen and cocky and convinced she needed no one: she wrapped him in a full embrace, pinning his arms to his sides, her back to his chest, her mouth close to his ear where he couldn't hear words but might hear tone.

"*I've got you,*" she said, knowing he couldn't hear it. "*Nobody drowns on my line. Breathe. Breathe.*"

He fought her for ten seconds. It felt like a season. Then his body remembered the drill — eyes closed, hands on the rope, the line does the thinking — and he sagged against her, breathing, alive, and she turned him to face down-line, put his hand on the rope, closed his fingers around it, and pressed it twice. *Hold.* She felt him nod, or felt something that served as a nod, and she took his wrist and towed.

The rest of the passage she swam by feel and fury, dragging him through the last of the crawl, counting her own breaths because counting his was impossible, listening to the wet mechanical rhythm of his regulator and hating the water, hating the mountain, hating every meter of stone between her and sky. When the ceiling finally lifted and the water lightened from ink to brown to the green-gold blur of daylight filtered through falling water, she nearly wept into her mask.

They broke the surface together in the churning plunge pool, and Oscar dragged Teo to the rocky shelf at the pool's edge and shoved him up onto it, and he lay there on his back in the rain, coughing, laughing, crying, all three at once, while the waterfall thundered down beside them loud enough to shake the stones.

Iris surfaced thirty seconds later, calm as a rumor. Serrano and Duarte hauled her out. For a moment the four of them lay on the wet rock in the rain and noise, gasping, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing language could do that breathing wasn't already doing better.

Then the mountain exhaled.

It came up through the rock first — a deep subsonic groan, felt in the teeth and the sternum — and then the pool itself moved, bulging, surging, spilling over its rim in a brown sheet. Oscar scrambled up the slope and turned to look back at the cliff face behind the falls, at the seam in the rock where the sump breathed, and saw water hammering out of it in a thick pulsing jet, like blood from a wound.

"The chambers," Duarte shouted. "It's gone through—"

The lower gallery. The entrance passage. The stela hall where Marta had kissed her clipboard goodbye. All of it, in minutes, filling top to bottom with the river's cold indifferent weight.

Teo was on his knees at the waterline, staring at the drowned mountain, his lips moving. Oscar crouched next to him and put her hand on his shoulder, and felt him shaking, and let him shake.

"She's in the high gallery," Oscar said. Loudly. Over the falls. Saying it out loud because Marta had been right — saying things was how you kept from screaming them. "Dry ledge. Air space. She has food, light, the artifacts. She's the best-protected person on this entire river right now, and in three days I'm going back in after her, and you're going to help me, because you owe me a favor you haven't finished paying."

Teo looked up at her. His face was wrecked and washed and, underneath it, stubborn as bedrock.

"I'm never getting in that water again," he said.

"Sure."

"I mean it."

"I know." She hauled him to his feet. "Come on. We've got a river to cross and a day's head start to spend, and somewhere behind us there's a man with winches who's about to find his fuel line doesn't work."

Above them, through the rain, the ridge line stood black against the storm — and along it, small and unmistakable, a signal mirror flashed twice. Someone was watching from the heights. Someone who was not supposed to be there yet.
