## 16. Downriver, Running

The rain came before the gunfire did, which was the only reason any of them were still breathing.

Oscar heard it first as a wrongness in the forest — not the percussion of water on leaves but a rhythm underneath it, arrhythmic and deliberate, the sound of men who had learned to walk quietly in wet country and hadn't quite managed it. She held up her fist the way Iris had taught her when she was nine years old, on a dig in the northern hills, and the column behind her stopped: Teo with the map case strapped to his chest like a child, the two students — Priya and Okonkwo — soaked through and gray-lipped with exhaustion, and behind them Iris, leaning against a buttress root with one hand pressed flat to her sternum.

They had been moving for six hours since the sump. Six hours of torn hands and leeches and the constant roar of the flood behind them, which had swallowed the lower chambers whole while they stood dripping on the ledge outside the waterfall and watched the river take what it wanted. Marta's light had been the last thing visible down there, a small yellow star in the black, holding steady until the water closed over it.

Oscar had not let herself think about that star since. There would be time later, or there wouldn't, and either way thinking about it now would get them killed.

She counted the shots without wanting to. Three, spaced wide, upstream and to the east — warning rounds or bad ranging, she couldn't tell. Then a fourth that cracked past close enough to shave bark off the tree beside her head.

"Off the trail," she breathed. "Now."

They went left, into the green dark, Oscar cutting point with the machete she'd taken from the tributary camp, opening a path along a game trail only she could read. Behind her she heard Teo murmur something to Priya, heard Okonkwo's boots slip in the mud and his breath catch, heard — most worrying of all — nothing at all from her mother.

Iris Vane silent was a thing Oscar had spent nine years believing she understood. This silence was different. This silence had weight.

* * *

They moved until the light failed, and then they moved a little longer, because stopping meant making camp and making camp meant fire and fire meant Hale's men would find them by its glow the way moths find a porch lamp. When they finally halted, it was in the hollow beneath a fallen ceiba so large that its root plate had become a wall of woven earth three meters high, furred with moss and hung with the pale threads of fungal filaments that caught what little light remained.

Oscar got them settled with the economy of someone who had done this alone too many times: tarps strung low, packs arranged as windbreaks, no fire, cold rations passed hand to hand. Teo tried to give her his share of the cassava bread and she pushed it back at him without looking up.

"Eat it. You swam a sump today. You need it more than I do."

"I threw up half of it already," he admitted, and despite everything — despite the gunfire and the drowned city and the fact that his hands were shaking so badly he could barely hold the bread — he smiled. "Twice. You didn't need to know that."

"Good. Keep your secrets." She crouched beside him, checked the strap of the map case where it crossed his chest, tugged it straight. The case held everything: Iris's field journals, the survey plates, the photographs of the carvings, the copies of the falsified hydrological report that Oscar had lifted out from under Hale's nose two nights ago. Nine years of her mother's work and one week of Oscar's theft, all riding on the chest of a botanist who vomited when he was scared.

"You should carry it," Teo said quietly.

"No." She said it fast enough that it came out harsh, and she made herself soften it. "If we're split up, if they take me, you keep going downriver. Rosa Anco's settlement is on the mission maps — old ones, the ones the bureau doesn't bother updating. You can find it. I'm not sure I could explain why to anyone else, but you'd understand it."

He looked at her for a long moment, rain dripping off his chin. "You're different out here," he said. "At basecamp you were all edges. Out here you're—" He searched for the word.

"Tired," Oscar said. "Sleep."

But she didn't sleep. She sat with her back against the root wall and listened to the forest breathe and watched her mother across the hollow, a shape under a tarp, too still.

Around midnight, Iris began to breathe wrong.

Oscar knew the sound. She had heard it once before, years ago, through a phone line — her father, the last night, the wet rattle that meant the body was negotiating terms it couldn't honor. She was across the hollow before she'd decided to move, kneeling in the mud, peeling back the tarp.

Her mother's face was gray. Not tired-gray, not sixty-year-old-woman-in-the-rainforest gray, but the gray of poor circulation, of blood being sent elsewhere on urgent business. Her lips had a bluish cast. Her hand was still pressed over her sternum, fingers curled, as though holding shut a door.

"Mama." The word came out in their old private register, the one they hadn't used in nine years, and it cost her nothing, which frightened her more than the gunfire had. "Mama, look at me."

Iris's eyes opened. They found Oscar's face with an effort that was visible, physical, like a swimmer surfacing. "Don't shout," she whispered. "You'll wake the students."

"The students are asleep ten feet away and they're going to wake up anyway because I need light and water and your pills, if you have pills, which I am now certain you do." Oscar was already digging through the pack at Iris's side, past the journal, past the waterproofed matches, until her fingers closed on a small tin that rattled. She shook two tablets into her palm and read the label by the light of a shielded torch. Nitroglycerin. Of course. Of course her mother had been carrying nitroglycerin through rapids and leech country and a flooded cavern system like it was aspirin.

"How long," Oscar said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation wearing a question's clothes.

Iris was quiet long enough that the tablets dissolved under her tongue before she answered. "Two years," she said. "Diagnosed in Lima. The cardiologist used the word *manageable*, and I decided to hear *fine*."

"Two years." Oscar's voice cracked on the number. "You hired me. You sent me a telegram — *one season, no questions asked* — knowing this?"

"I needed a diver I trusted." Iris's eyes closed again. "There was exactly one."

Oscar sat back on her heels in the mud and let that land. Around them the forest went on with its enormous indifferent conversation — frogs, insects, the drip of the canopy — and somewhere far downstream, faint as memory, an engine.

Teo appeared at her shoulder with water, having slept lightly or not at all. He looked at the tin in her hand, at Iris's face, and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had done all day. He simply took the watch, and pressed his own dry shirt into Oscar's hands for her mother, and went back to the dark.

Oscar covered Iris, tucked the tarp around her shoulders, and did not cry, because crying was a noise and noise was death out here. But she stayed kneeling in the mud a long time, holding her mother's wrist between two fingers, counting a heartbeat that stumbled like a man walking downhill in the dark.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, and with it came the engine sound again, closer, unmistakable now: an outboard working the flooded margins of the main channel, patient as a vulture. Hale had boats. Hale had men with rifles and a payroll and a redacted letter, and all Oscar had was four exhausted people, a stolen map case, and a mother whose heart was running on borrowed time.

She gathered them under the ceiba and laid it out flat, no speeches.

"They'll sweep the banks. Boats can't follow us inland, but they can leapfrog us downriver and put men ashore ahead of us. So we don't take the riverbank. We cut southeast, high ground, through the palm swamp, and come down on the mission settlement from behind. Rosa Anco's people. Two days' walk if we're lucky. Three if we're honest."

"And if your mother can't walk three days?" Okonkwo asked. He was twenty-four and had the decency to look ashamed the moment he said it.

"She can hear you," Iris said from under the tarp, and pushed herself upright on one elbow, gray-faced and furious. "And she can walk."

"You're not walking," Oscar said.

"The hell I'm not—"

"You collapsed twelve hours ago. Your heart is failing and you've known for two years and you've been lying to everyone including yourself, which is the most Iris Vane thing you have ever done." Oscar heard her own voice rise and made herself stop, made herself breathe, because shouting in the rainforest was suicide. When she spoke again it was low. "We're building a litter. Teo and I will carry you in shifts. You will lie there and direct us, because God knows you can't stop directing us, and that way everybody gets to be useful. That's the deal. Take it or I sit on you."

For a moment Oscar thought her mother would fight it — every instinct in that woman was built for fighting, for choosing the dig over the funeral, the site over the safety, the work over the daughter, always, always — but then Iris looked at the students, at their blistered faces, and something in her folded.

"All right," she said quietly. "Build your litter."

It took an hour. They cut poles from standing deadwood, lashed them with vine and the last of the cordage, stretched a tarp between. Oscar wove a headrest from ferns because her mother's neck was bad, and hated herself for remembering that detail after nine years of trying to forget everything.

They moved at first light.

* * *

The palm swamp was the worst country Oscar had ever crossed, and she had crossed leech country twice and a mangrove delta once during a cholera season. The ground didn't exist so much as it was suggested — a brown soup over black muck, waist-deep in places, webbed with roots that grabbed ankles like deliberate malice. Insects rose from it in clouds. The heat was a physical weight, a hand pressing down on the back of the skull.

Iris directed from the litter, and to her credit she did it well — calling terrain features from memory of surveys thirty years old, correcting their bearing twice before Oscar's compass confirmed her right. But by midday her voice had gone thin, and by the second afternoon she had stopped narrating altogether, and Oscar found herself checking the litter every twenty minutes the way she'd check a dive gauge.

On the third morning, Hale's men found them.

Not all of them — a pair of scouts, moving along the swamp's eastern margin, and Oscar saw them first because she had been the one taught to look, all those years ago, by the woman now lying gray and silent on a stretcher of vines. She froze the column with her fist, sank them all into the root shadow, and watched the two figures pick their way along the margin fifty meters out. One carried a rifle slung loose. Neither wore uniforms. Hale's people dressed like fishermen and shot like soldiers.

The scouts passed. The column breathed again. And then Iris, who had been watching them too from her horizontal vantage, said in a whisper that was almost amused:

"That one on the left plants his heel first. Hale trained him himself. He always did hire cheap and train hard."

"You know his methods," Oscar murmured.

"I know his backers' methods. Hale is just the hand." Iris's eyes were closed. "The arm belongs to people who wear suits in capital cities and call displacement *development*. I've been reading their letters for thirty years, Oscar. I burned my own journal rather than let one page of it fall into their hands. Don't look at me like that. I'd burn the whole valley first."

Oscar adjusted the litter straps and said nothing, and thought about the star of Marta's light going under the water, and walked on.

* * *

Iris collapsed for the second time at dusk on the third day, at the top of a ridge where the swamp finally gave way to dry forest, and this time she did not get up.

One moment she was giving directions — *bear south of that granite dome, there's a stream cut, the Anco people marked it on the old mission maps* — and the next her voice simply stopped, mid-word, like a radio losing signal. Oscar turned and her mother was sliding off the litter and Oscar caught her, both of them going down into the leaf mold together, and the world contracted to the weight of a small woman in her arms and the terrible bird-quick flutter under her fingers.

"Priya — the tin. Now. Teo, water." Her voice came out level, drill-sergeant calm, and some distant part of her marveled at it, at how the panic just converted itself into procedure, the way fear converts to air in a tank when you've been trained right. She got the tablets under her mother's tongue. She raised her legs on a rolled tarp. She counted the pulse and hated the arithmetic of it.

Iris surfaced slowly. Her eyes wandered the canopy overhead as if reading something written there.

"I was going to tell you," she said. "In the cavern. I started to. About Lima. About why I buried the report." A breath, drawn carefully, like someone handling broken glass. "Nine years I rehearsed apologizing to you. Did you know that? Every expedition, every field season. I'd write it out in the journal and then tear the page out. Your father's funeral. I chose a trench over you and I told myself it was principle. It wasn't. It was cowardice. Grief was in that church, Oscar, and I couldn't—"

"Stop." Oscar's voice broke clean through her procedure. "Save it. Save all of it. You're going to tell me at the settlement, sitting down, with tea, in about six hours, and I'm going to pretend I don't want to hear it, and it's going to be awful, and we're both going to survive it. That's the plan. So don't spend it here on a hillside."

Iris almost smiled. "Still bossy."

"Learned from the best liar I know."

They rested two hours, which they could not afford, because staying still was dying. When they moved again, Oscar carried the front of the litter alone for the first shift, and the second, and would have taken the third except Teo physically pulled her hands off the poles and put them on his own shoulders, and Okonkwo took the rear, and Priya walked point with the compass, and none of them discussed it. Somewhere in the dark of that day's march Oscar understood that she had stopped being the hired digger, the replacement, the outsider. She was the one they followed. The realization sat in her chest strange and warm, like a stone warmed by sun.

* * *

They smelled woodsmoke an hour before they saw the settlement.

Then bells — a mission bell, cracked and sweet, ringing what might have been the hour or might have been an alarm, and then dogs, and then lights moving among trees, and then they came down the last slope through cassava fields silver with dew and a line of people was waiting for them at the edge of the clearing, and at its center stood a small broad woman in her seventies with a walking stick and eyes that went straight past Oscar to the map case on Teo's chest and then to Iris's gray face, and softened, and hardened, both at once.

"You are the Vane woman," the elder said. Not a question. "And you have brought the river's secret down to us on a stretcher."

Oscar set the litter down gently in the dirt. Her arms were shaking, her hands were shredded, and she was so tired the clearing seemed to breathe around her. "Dr. Iris Vane," she said. "And yes. We brought what the river keeps. All of it. For you — for whoever it belongs to. Which I'm starting to understand isn't me, or her, or the men with guns coming downriver behind us."

Rosa Anco looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, the settlement stirred — doors opening, lamps lit, children peering. Somewhere downriver, very faint, an engine turned over in the dark.

"Then you had better come inside," the elder said, "and tell me quickly how much time we have."

Oscar looked back the way they'd come, at the black wall of the forest, and heard the engine note climb.

"Less than you'd think," she said.
