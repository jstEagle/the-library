## 17. The Keepers

The mission settlement of San Rafael de las Nubes sat on a bluff above the river like a thing that had decided, long ago, not to be washed away. Oscar saw it first as a smudge of tin roofs through the rain, then as a church with a leaning bell tower, then as people — dozens of them, standing at the edge of the compound in the downpour, watching the five ragged figures come up the switchback trail from the water.

She had imagined every version of arrival except this one. In her head they'd stumbled into an empty clearing, or been met by a priest with a rifle. She had not imagined old women wading out into the mud in their Sunday clothes, or children being held back by their collars, or the way the whole settlement went silent when it saw Iris.

Iris was conscious again — barely — riding Teo's back in a sling made from a tent fly, her arms looped over his shoulders, her face gray as river clay. She had stopped arguing about being carried somewhere around the second hour of the flight through the forest, which frightened Oscar more than the collapse itself. Her mother did not stop arguing. Arguing was how Iris Vane breathed.

The oldest woman in the crowd came forward alone. She was small and straight-backed, wrapped in a wool shawl gone soft with decades of rain, and she walked through the mud without looking down at it, as if the ground were obligated to hold her. She stopped in front of Oscar, looked her over from boots to face, and said something in a language Oscar didn't know — quick, rhythmic, addressed to her as though she were expected.

"She's asking who you are," said a young man beside her, stepping forward. He wore a schoolteacher's shirt gone translucent with wet, and he translated with the careful diction of someone who'd learned his Spanish twice, once at home and once in books. "And why you bring the doctor's daughter to us."

Oscar blinked. "The what?"

But the old woman wasn't waiting for translation anymore. She had moved past Oscar entirely, to where Teo stood swaying under Iris's weight, and she reached up and touched Iris's cheek with two fingers, the way you check whether fruit is ripe, or whether a fever has broken. Then she began speaking again, low and continuous, and the crowd behind her answered as one voice — a sound like wind moving through a stand of bamboo, rising and falling, rising and falling.

"What is that?" Oscar asked. "What are they saying?"

"It's a welcome," the young man said. "A very old one." His name, he'd said when he introduced himself on the trail, was Emiliano Anco; he taught at the settlement school and had studied in the capital before coming home. "It's for someone returning from the drowned country."

* * *

They laid Iris in the mission's infirmary, a room with whitewashed walls and a crucifix and a cot that smelled of camphor, and a nun with forearms like a stevedore took one look at her and started issuing orders in a voice that brooked no discussion. Oscar tried to stay and was pushed out with the rest. Through the door she heard the nun say something about digitalis, and about fools who climb mountains with a bad heart, and then the door closed.

Teo slid down the wall outside until he was sitting in the dirt. Marta's absence hung around all of them like smoke. They hadn't spoken of her since the waterfall — there had been no time, only movement, only Hale's men and the rain and the miles — but Oscar could feel the whole group carrying her the way you carry a stone in your boot: unmentioned, always felt.

Emiliano brought them food — cassava, dried fish, sweet tea — and watched them eat with the patience of a man who understood that shock takes time to metabolize.

"You knew my mother," Oscar said finally. "You called her the doctor's daughter."

"Everyone in the valley knows Dr. Vane." Emiliano smiled faintly. "She came here nine years ago. Before the survey. She stayed three weeks. She interviewed the elders. She recorded songs." He paused. "Then she left, and we never heard from her again, and the government men came instead."

Oscar set down her cup. Nine years. The number landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Nine years of silence between them, and now she learned that the silence had a shape — that Iris had walked into this settlement, listened to these people, and then buried everything, including herself, including her own daughter, under nine years of fieldwork and formality and never once calling home.

"What did she ask you?" Oscar said. "Nine years ago. What did she want?"

Emiliano glanced toward the infirmary door. "That is not mine to tell," he said. "But my grandmother's is. And she has been waiting since dawn to talk to whoever came out of the drowned country alive."

* * *

Rosa Anco received them in her house at the center of the compound, a structure older than the church, built of dark timber and thatch, its interior lit by a single lamp and dense with the smell of woodsmoke and dried herbs. She sat on a low stool with her hands folded, and around the walls, in the shadows, hung things Oscar's professional eye catalogued before she could stop it: featherwork gone brown with age, a drum with a skin like parchment, strings of shells that clicked softly whenever the door moved the air.

Rosa spoke in her own language, and Emiliano translated, but after a while she stopped waiting for him. She switched to Spanish — accented, deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall — because, Oscar understood later, some things you do not want filtered through your grandson's book-learning.

"You went into the mountain behind the falls," Rosa said.

"Yes," Oscar said.

"And you saw the stairway going down into the water."

"Yes."

Rosa nodded slowly, as if confirming a delivery. "Then you have seen the top of our city."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Somewhere outside, rain kept up its work on the thatch.

"Our people did not always live on this bluff," Rosa continued. "We lived below. In the valley. There was a city there — my grandmother's grandmother called it by a name I will give you, but the word does not translate, so do not ask me to. It means roughly *the place where the river remembers*. It was built before the priests came, before the soldiers, before any of the names on your maps. We farmed the terraces. We buried our dead in the hill behind the falls, in chambers the river could not reach. And then the water came."

"Flood legend," Oscar said quietly. "Every valley has one."

"This is not a legend." Rosa's eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they did not blink enough. "This is accounting. The water rose in a single season — my grandmother counted seven generations back to it, and her grandmother counted seven before that. The terraces drowned. The city drowned. The dead in the hill did not drown, because the hill is high, and the chambers sealed themselves behind the falls. Our people climbed to this bluff and lived, and waited, and told the story so no one would forget where the doors were."

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. "The doors."

"The entrances. The stairways. There are four, in the old telling. One behind the falls. One under the fig tree at the river bend. Two more that the water took." Rosa unfolded her hands for the first time. "For seven generations we kept the story. And for seven generations, the story protected nothing, because the water did the protecting for us. No one can loot a drowned city. No one can dam a grave that is already flooded. We thought the river would keep them forever."

She looked at Oscar then, fully, and Oscar felt the look go through her like a probe through sediment.

"But rivers change their minds," Rosa said. "And so do governments. Two years ago, men came with instruments. They measured the water. They talked about a dam. My grandson wrote letters. Nobody answered. And then, nine years ago—" she paused, correcting herself, recalculating, and Oscar saw that time worked differently in this house— "no. Eleven years ago. Forgive me, grief makes my arithmetic sloppy. Eleven years ago, a woman came. A scientist. She had already found the stairway behind the falls. She came here and she sat where you are sitting, and she asked us to tell her the story. All of it. Every generation of it. And we did, because she asked the way no government man had ever asked — she asked as if the story belonged to us, not to her."

Oscar's heart was beating very hard. "And then?"

"And then she left. And we heard she had published nothing. Filed nothing. That the great Dr. Vane, whose name was in the newspapers for other valleys, other finds, had gone quiet as a closed mouth." Rosa smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of someone watching a debt get paid. "We did not know why. We suspected. Now you have come out of the mountain, half-drowned, hunted, carrying nothing — and I think you can tell me why."

* * *

Oscar told her. Not all of it — Marta's choice stayed private, a coal she carried in a closed hand — but the shape of it: the sonar anomaly, the mapped-but-unopened site, the burned journal page, Hale and his redacted letter, the dam permit resting on a falsified survey, the plan to drown the valley to a depth that would erase every claim, ancestral or otherwise, beneath a reservoir named for a politician.

Rosa listened without interrupting, which Oscar recognized, with a jolt, as her mother's listening posture — the stillness, the total attention, the sense that the person across from you was building a model of you inside themselves.

When Oscar finished, Rosa was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "The bureau thinks the city belongs to the state. The state thinks it belongs to history. The man Hale thinks it belongs to whoever pays him. And your mother thinks—" she tilted her head— "your mother thinks it belongs to the dead, and that the living should leave them be."

"That's not—" Oscar started, and stopped. Because it was, more or less, exactly what Iris thought. It was the no-artifacts-out rule. It was the site mapped but never opened. It was eleven years of silence. Iris hadn't been afraid of the site. She'd been afraid of what opening it would unleash — the permits, the papers, the machinery of heritage that in every other valley had ended the same way: a fence, a ticket booth, a displaced village, a museum case full of somebody's ancestors.

"But she is wrong too," Rosa said, gently, as if reading the thought off Oscar's face. "Leaving it buried protects it only while the water holds. The water is changing its mind. Your mother bought eleven years, not forever. And now the bureau knows where the doors are, and the man Hale knows, and the flood will open what no one opens." She rose, stiffly, one hand on the wall, and crossed to the strings of shells hanging in the shadow. She lifted one shell free and held it out to Oscar on her flat palm. It was small, spiral, worn smooth, drilled through with a hole for stringing.

"When the water took the city," Rosa said, "the people who climbed to this bluff carried one thing each. This is what my line carried. Not gold. Not stone. A shell, so we would remember the sound of the river we lost." She pressed it into Oscar's hand and closed Oscar's fingers over it. "Your mother understood that the city is not the stones. The stones are just where the city keeps itself. The city is this." She tapped Oscar's closed fist. "Story. Ownership of story. And that, child, cannot be drowned, dammed, or stolen — unless we let someone else write it down first."

Oscar stood there holding a shell worth nothing on any market on earth, and felt the whole architecture of her life shift on its foundations.

She had spent ten years believing artifacts were the treasure — that value lived in objects, that ownership followed possession, that the only question ever worth asking was who got to keep the thing. It was the salvager's catechism, and her mother's too, in her way: Iris had simply concluded that the safest keeper was no one, the deepest vault was mud. Both of them wrong, Oscar saw now, in mirror image. The bureau wanted to own the past by filing it. Hale wanted to own it by selling it. Iris wanted to protect it by erasing it. And none of them had thought to ask the people whose grandmothers' grandmothers had carried the memory uphill through the floodwater, one shell at a time.

"They filed a claim once," Emiliano said quietly, from the doorway. "My grandfather's generation. After the last displacement upstream, when the 'protected zone' cleared four villages for a reserve nobody local could enter. The lawyers in the capital laughed. We had no documents. No survey. No photographs. Just stories, and stories don't register with land offices." He met Oscar's eyes. "But your mother recorded ours. Eleven years ago. Songs, genealogies, the boundaries of the terraces as the elders recite them. If those recordings still exist—"

"They exist," Oscar said. "She hid them. In the caverns, with the documentation. With everything." Her mind was already moving downstream of the sentence, fast and cold, the way it moved when she planned a dive: depths, currents, margins of error. The caverns where Hale's men were camped. The records hidden in a chamber that would be underwater within days if the flood kept climbing. The documentation that could prove the falsified survey, and the recordings that could prove seven generations of continuous claim, and both of them sitting in the dark behind a waterfall guarded by armed men who thought they were guarding treasure.

Rosa watched her face change and nodded, unsurprised, as if she'd seen this exact expression on another woman's face eleven years ago.

"So," the old woman said. "Now you understand what your mother could not finish. The find does not belong to the bureau. It does not belong to the thief. It does not even belong to the dead, who are past wanting. It belongs to the keepers." She settled back onto her stool. "And the keepers will need someone who can go into the water and come back out."

Outside, the rain thickened. Downriver, unseen, the flood was doing its patient arithmetic against the doors behind the falls, and somewhere in the dark between here and the mountain, Hale's launch sat at anchor with its winches and its guns, waiting for the water to deliver what his divers couldn't reach.

Oscar closed her fist around the shell and felt its spiral press into her palm like a signature.

"I'll need a boat," she said.
