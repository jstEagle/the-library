## 18. Two Kinds of Thieves

The river gave her back the way she'd come, only faster, because now she knew its moods. Oscar ran the skiff upstream through the last grey hour before dawn, running the channels she'd memorized on the way down — the split rock with the deadfall snagged against it, the long shallow where the water braided and lied, the bend where the current shouldered you toward the cutbank like a drunk looking for a fight. Her hands knew all of it. Her hands had always known this kind of work better than she knew herself.

Behind her, three days downriver, Rosa Anco's settlement slept with Iris's field records sewn into a canvas satchel under a floorboard, and Marta's light still burned in the high gallery of a drowned city, and Hale's men were somewhere on this water ahead of her, armed and bored and certain that no one would be stupid enough to come back.

That was the whole of her plan, really. Be stupid enough. Be alone enough. Be the kind of nobody who had spent ten years making herself invisible in every ledger, every manifest, every employment roll from here to the coast. Corliss Hale could buy permits, fuel, men, and the silence of a government bureau with a redacted seal. What he couldn't buy was a woman who had practiced disappearing since she was nineteen years old.

The tributary mouth came up on her left, a dark seam in the black wall of the forest. She killed the engine a quarter mile out and let the current do the last of it, drifting in on the paddle, quiet as silt.

The camp where she'd found the slashed tents was occupied now. She could see the shapes of it before she could see the details: two new tents pitched where Iris's had been, a tarp strung between trees, a cook fire burned down to coals with a man's silhouette folded beside it. Hale's men had done what occupying soldiers always did — they'd built a small, comfortable version of the world they understood, and they'd assumed the world outside it was empty.

Oscar beached the skiff in the root tangle of a fallen ceiba two hundred meters downstream, under cover that smelled of rot and orchids and the mineral breath of the waterfall. She wrapped the engine in her spare tarp. She took the dive knife, the waterproof tube of matches she'd carried since her first salvage job, the coil of spare line, and nothing else. Weight was honesty. Everything you carried told the river what you intended.

She stood a moment at the waterline, letting the dark finish its work on her eyes, and she thought about her mother — three days' travel away, flat on a cot in the mission's back room, her heart stuttering like a bad engine, refusing with what strength she had left to be carried anywhere. *The documentation is in the third chamber,* Iris had said, the night before, her voice thin as tracing paper. *Behind the shelf of stone with the serpent glyph. There's a cache — a ammunition box, green, army surplus. Everything is in oilcloth. Everything that matters.* And then, because she was Iris Vane and could not leave a thing alone: *Don't be clever, Oscar. Be thorough.*

*You be thorough,* Oscar had said. *I'll be clever.*

Now she was clever alone in the dark with a knife and a box of matches, and the cleverness felt less like a plan than like a prayer.

* * *

She went up through the forest instead of along the trail, because the trail was a corridor and corridors were for people who wanted to be seen. The going was slow and vicious — vines that grabbed like hands, roots that turned ankle-deep mud into a trapfield, insects that found every gap in her clothing within the first hundred meters and filed formal complaints. She moved the way her mother had taught her, in a rhythm of stillness and motion, three steps then listen, three steps then listen, until listening became the whole of her and the forest's noise folded around her like a coat.

The waterfall announced the cavern long before she reached it, a white roar she felt in her teeth. She circled wide, came down through the fern brake on the north side, and lay in the wet green dark looking at what Hale's men had made of the approach.

They'd built a platform. Of course they had — a rough scaffold of cut saplings at the lip of the cavern entrance behind the falls, with a block and tackle rigged to it and two men on watch, one smoking, one not. A generator sat under the tarp, silent, its fuel drums stacked beside it in a neat row of five, red as wounds in the grey light. They were set up to work the site, not just hold it. Winch cable ran into the cavern's throat. Somewhere in the dark behind the waterfall, men were pulling her mother's discovery apart, piece by piece, and writing numbers on it in somebody else's ink.

Oscar lay in the ferns and did the arithmetic. Two on watch. Probably four more inside, working shifts — she'd counted six at the downriver camp before she'd slipped past them in the night, and Hale traveled with eight. The generator meant light inside, which meant they could see her coming. The drums meant fuel, which meant the generator, the winch, the launch, all of it, ran on five red steel hearts.

She looked at the drums for a long time.

Her mother had taught her two kinds of digging. The first kind was for things you wanted: careful, patient, documented, every layer read like a sentence before you turned the page. The second kind was for things that wanted to hurt you — how to collapse a trench wall behind you, how to cut a rope so it looked worn, how to make a site so unremarkable that no one ever came back. *The second kind is the one that keeps you alive,* Iris had said, in another life, over a camp table, in the only year they'd still been speaking easily. *Anyone can take. Knowing what to leave is the discipline.*

Oscar smiled in the dark, and it wasn't a kind expression.

She wasn't going to leave anything.

* * *

The rain came an hour after full light, which was the forest's mercy and her opportunity. It came down in sheets, hammering the canopy into white noise, drowning every small sound a sentry might strain for. The smoker took shelter under the tarp. His partner pulled his collar up and turned his back to the weather, staring out at the waterfall as if watching it were a job.

Oscar came down the fern slope on her belly, through mud, through water, through the kind of wet that stops being a condition and becomes a substance. The generator tarp was ten meters from the nearest man. She covered them in three moves that would have made her old salvage boss proud: flat to flat, pause, flat to flat. Her hands, when she lifted the tarp edge, were steady.

The fuel line was rubber and clamp, no challenge at all. She didn't cut it. Cutting was a message; loosening was an accident. She worked the clamp loose until it would fail under pressure, then did the same to the winch cable's anchor bolt, backing it off two turns and smearing the threads with mud. Then she put her shoulder to the nearest drum and rolled it.

It was heavier than it looked, the way everything is when it matters, but she'd moved engine blocks alone in flooded quarries with worse footing than this. She walked it — rolled it, walked it, caught it — down the slope behind the platform, into the ravine the rain was filling, and stood it upright against a deadfall where it couldn't be seen from camp. Then she went back for the second one.

The smoker almost caught her. She heard him come out from under the tarp — boots on planking, a curse about the weather — and she flattened into the ravine with her cheek in the running water and her body against the first drum and did not breathe for what felt like the length of a funeral. He stood at the edge of the platform. He spat. He went back in.

She rolled the second drum down beside the first, and then she did the thing she'd been turning over since the fern brake. She opened the drum's bung, tipped it, and laid a slow rope of fuel down the ravine floor, a dark thread of it running from the drums toward the deadfall, pooling in the low places, following the rain-water downhill and away from the cavern. Then she walked back up through the rain, took the matches from the tube inside her shirt, where they'd stayed dry against her skin, and did not light anything yet.

Yet was the whole discipline of it.

* * *

The cavern entrance behind the waterfall was guarded by one man, which told her they weren't worried. He sat on the platform with a rifle across his knees and a poncho over his hat, watching the water fall, and Oscar understood him in a way she didn't want to. He was the bored one. He was the one who'd been told this was easy.

She went in through the water.

It was the thing she knew how to do that no salvage man on Hale's payroll would think of, because they were river men and she was a quarry diver, and quarry divers know that water is a door. She slipped into the pool at the falls' base upstream of the platform, let the cascade's turbulence hide her, and swam the edge of the plunge pool with only her face out, riding the roar, until she was under the lip of the cavern mouth. Then a breath, a dive, and the dark took her.

Inside, the generator's light had made the cavern honest in a way it hadn't been when she'd first come through — and worse for it. Floodlamps on stands threw hard shadows across the terraces, and men were working the upper gallery with brushes and crates and the brisk, cheerful cruelty of people cataloguing a place that wasn't theirs. She could hear them talking. Spanish, two of them; one of the voices was complaining about the humidity and the other was agreeing in the toneless way of a man who wanted the complaint to be over.

She kept to the water channel, low, where the falls' noise still reached, and moved chamber to chamber by the route her body remembered. Past the carved lintel. Past the terrace where the serpent glyph ran the wall like a current. The men's voices thinned behind her. The light did too. By the third chamber she was in her mother's dark again, the true dark, and she stood dripping in it and let her eyes adjust and felt the old, complicated awe move through her — the city all around her, patient under stone, holding its dead and its names and its nine hundred years of nobody's permission needed.

The serpent shelf was where Iris said it would be. Oscar found the seam by touch, worked it the way her mother had described — lift, then slide, the stone wants to go sideways, not up — and there it was. The ammunition box, green, army surplus, its lock rusted shut until her knife persuaded it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: notebooks. Film canisters. The brass survey markers. A bundle of photographs in a waterproof sleeve, and beneath them a folded map with Iris's handwriting dense along every margin — the site's true extent, the chambers, the sump, the gallery where Marta had held the light. Everything that mattered. Everything Hale's redacted letter wanted to drown.

Oscar wrapped the box in the coil of spare line and slung it across her back, and it rode against her spine like a second heart.

She was three steps from the water channel when the floodlamp at her back went out.

Not all of them — one. A stand had tipped, or a wire had finally failed, and the chamber behind her went from hard light to a long, soft gloom, and one of the voices down the gallery stopped mid-complaint. She heard boots. She heard a man say something about the generator, about the fuel pressure, about how it had been running rough since yesterday.

The loosened clamp, doing its patient work.

Oscar stood in the dark with a stolen box on her back and listened to the machinery of the occupation begin to stutter, and she thought: *Mamá, you taught me the second kind of digging. Watch what I do with it.*

* * *

She went out the way she'd come in, through the water door, and the rain had thickened into a deluge that was doing her work for her — washing scent, washing sound, washing the memory of her out of the air. The two men on the platform had their backs to her, both staring down the slope now, because one of them had noticed the smell. Fuel, in the rain, running downhill. A man's voice, angry, tracing the dark thread of it through the ravine with a flashlight beam that shook.

Oscar lay in the fern brake with the box under her and the match tube in her hand and waited for the flashlight to find the drums.

It did. She heard the shout go up — *¡Aquí! ¡Los tambores!* — and heard boots come down the ravine, three men now, four, all of them gathered around the two red drums standing innocent against the deadfall, all of them looking at the fuel trail and the soaked ground and the rain coming down like judgment, and not one of them looking up.

She struck the match on the third try. The rain almost had it. The fourth caught.

She threw it low and short, into the pool of fuel at the ravine's lip, and the world turned orange.

The flame ran the thread of fuel like a lit fuse in a war film, hissing, jumping the low places, and the men scattered back from it with the particular panic of people who have just realized they are standing in the blast radius of their own expedition. The first drum went with a *whump* that she felt in her chest. The second caught a heartbeat later, and the fire stood up between the deadfall and the ravine wall, a wall of it, black smoke driven flat by the rain, and the heat came up the slope and pushed the men back and back and back.

Nobody was hurt. She'd built the arithmetic to make sure of that — the trail ran away from the men, away from the platform, away from the forest edge, into the stone throat of the ravine where there was nothing to burn but stone and water and Hale's ambitions. But the launch's spare fuel had been stacked with the drums — she'd counted it, she'd planned for it — and when that went, the sound was different, deeper, a *boom* that rolled up the valley and brought every man in the cavern running out into the rain with rifles they had no targets for.

Oscar was already moving.

She went down the fern slope with the box on her back and the fire painting everything orange behind her, through the mud, through the water, to the fallen ceiba where the skiff waited under its tarp. Her hands shook now, finally, doing the honest work of fear after the dishonest work of courage. The engine caught on the third pull. She ran it down the flooded ravine mouth with the firelight strobing off the water behind her and the shouts thinning into the rain, and she did not look back, because looking back was for people who hadn't planned the exit first.

* * *

Two miles downriver, in the grey-green murk where the tributary braided into shallows, she let the skiff drift and finally let herself breathe. The box rode against her spine. Inside it: her mother's nine years of silence, written down, measured, photographed, and marked with brass. The proof that the city existed. The proof that it belonged to someone. The proof that a bureau with a redacted seal had built its dam permit on a survey that never happened.

She'd gone upriver as a digger. She was coming down as a thief, and she found, turning it over in the rain, that she couldn't feel the difference in her hands. Both trades were the same trade, really: reading what the ground was hiding, and deciding who got to know.

Ahead of her, downriver, lay the settlement, and Rosa Anco, and the claim that had to be filed before Hale's backers could buy their way around the truth. Behind her, upstream, the fire would burn itself out by dark, and Hale would stand in the wreckage of his fuel cache and do his own arithmetic — and Corliss Hale did not seem, to Oscar, like a man who forgave arithmetic that came out against him. He had eight men, a government letter, and now nothing left to lose but the letter itself.

He would come downriver. He would come fast, and he would come angry, and the water between here and the settlement was narrow, and slow, and full of places to hide a boat.

Oscar opened the throttle. The rain hammered her shoulders, and the box pressed its weight against her spine like a hand between her shoulder blades, pushing her forward, and she ran for the confluence with the storm closing behind her like a door.
