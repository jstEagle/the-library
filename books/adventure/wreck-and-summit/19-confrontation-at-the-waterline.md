## 19. Confrontation at the Waterline

The rain had been falling for six hours without conviction — not the hammering deluge of the headwaters but a fine, needling drizzle that turned the whole world the color of wet slate. Oscar crouched at the edge of the cutbank where the tributary spilled into the main river and watched the water climb.

It climbed the way it always did here: not in waves but in inches, patient as an auditor. A brown stain crept up the exposed roots of a fig tree, then another hand's width, then another. Somewhere below the surface, she knew, the drowned staircase was already drinking. The lower chambers would be gone by morning. Whatever Marta was sheltering in that high gallery, the river had no interest in her survival or anyone else's. It only knew how to rise.

Behind her, forty meters back under the canopy, Iris sat propped against a buttress root with Teo holding a tarp over her head and a pulse he kept checking like a man counting money he didn't trust. She had argued, of course. She had argued with the particular ferocity of a woman whose heart had tried to quit on her twice since dawn and who considered this a scheduling inconvenience. *Go*, she'd said. *I'll be fine.* The second time she'd said it, her voice had cracked down the middle like old plaster, and Oscar had understood that her mother was terrified, and that the terror wasn't for herself.

*You're the only one of us he can't out-talk,* Iris had said. Which was either faith or an indictment. With Iris Vane it was usually both.

Oscar checked the compass — her mother's compass, brass-cased, the needle still true after thirty years of being carried through places that should have killed it — and then checked the river again.

Hale's launch would come from downriver. She'd counted on that. He had men, fuel, and a schedule, and schedules were the one thing Corliss Hale loved more than winning. He'd lost a day to the sabotaged fuel line upstream; he'd spent two more chasing them through the green maze toward the mission settlement; and now his quarry had doubled back along the waterline instead of running for the village, which meant they were cornered, which meant Hale would want to enjoy it.

Good. Men who wanted to enjoy things made mistakes. Men in a hurry just shot you.

She heard the engine before she saw the boat — a low, diesel-throated idle, throttled back to something almost polite. The launch slid around the downstream bend with its gunwales riding low, four men aboard besides Hale, two of them standing with the loose-legged ease of people who were paid to be comfortable holding rifles. Rain beaded on the canvas canopy. The hull bumped against the cutbank and a man threw a line around a root with a fisherman's economy.

Corliss Hale stepped ashore in rubber boots too clean for the mud, and smiled at her the way he'd smiled at basecamp nine days and one lifetime ago.

"Dr. Vane's daughter," he said. "Or do you go by Vane yourself? I've never been sure which family name you're hiding behind."

"Oscar." She stayed crouched, forearms on her knees, deliberately unhurried. "You're early. I gave your fuel cache until noon."

Something flickered behind the smile — quick, professional annoyance, there and banked. So the fire had reached him. Good. Every hour his men spent mourning their reserve drums was an hour Marta's gallery stayed above the waterline.

"That was a costly decision," Hale said mildly. He walked to the edge of the bank beside her, close enough to talk over the river, far enough that his riflemen could work if it came to that. He looked out at the brown flood the way other men looked at ledgers. "You know what's remarkable about this valley? From the air it looks like nothing. Green carpet, some water, no roads worth the name. And underneath—" he tapped his boot on the mud, once, twice, a little drumroll— "a city older than anything my backers have ever put their name on. Do you know what a discovery like that does to a permit portfolio? It's not treasure, Ms. Vane. It's leverage. Whole decades of it."

"I know what it is." She stood slowly, letting him see her hands, empty, palms out — a diver's habit, showing you carried nothing that could snag. "That's why I'm here."

"Here?" He gestured at the empty bank, the rain, the treeline where his men had already begun drifting outward with the unconscious geometry of a sweep pattern. "You're here because you're out of options. Your mother's dying — don't look surprised, I've seen heart failure before, it has a walk. Your botanist can barely carry her. You've got no radio, no boat, and the nearest settlement is a day's march through leech country with a woman who shouldn't climb stairs. And I—" he spread his hands, generous— "have a launch, a doctor on retainer in Puerto Rojas, and absolutely no reason to hurt any of you. That's not a threat. That's arithmetic. I'm offering you the same bargain I offered your mother in the caverns. Sign the transfer. All of you go home. The bureau gets its site, my backers get their compensation clause, and everyone lives to write memoirs."

"And the dam gets built."

"The dam," Hale said, "gets built regardless. That's the part your mother never understood, and I suspect it's the part you do understand, because you strike me as someone who's done the reading. The permit was approved in March. The funding closed in June. There are eleven signatures between that reservoir and this valley, and every one of them belongs to a man who will retire comfortably whether or not you drown in this river today. Signing the transfer doesn't build the dam. Not signing it doesn't stop it. All it changes is whether the artifacts come out first, catalogued and preserved, or sit under sixty meters of water for the next thousand years while lawyers argue about whose grief owns them." He shook his head with something that was almost tenderness. "Your mother thinks she's protecting the dead. She's drowning them."

Oscar let the silence stretch. Rain ticked on the leaves. The river rose another finger's width against the fig roots, and she watched Hale watch it, and she thought: *he believes every word he's saying*. That was the thing about Corliss Hale that made him dangerous and also made him beatable. He was honest with himself. He genuinely believed the future belonged to whoever filed the paperwork fastest, and everything else — the descendants, the oral histories, Rosa Anco's grandmothers singing the flood legend to children who'd never seen the city — was sentiment, and sentiment was weather, and weather was not a factor in permits.

Men like that could not imagine losing to anything they couldn't invoice.

"You're right about one thing," she said. "My mother buried her report nine years ago because the last time a government 'protected' a valley like this, they moved three hundred people at rifle point and called it resettlement. She's been carrying that ever since. She'd rather drown the site than hand it to men like your backers." She tilted her head. "But you already know that. You read her journal page when you burned half her camp looking for it. So let's skip to the part where you tell me what happens if I say no."

Hale sighed, and the sigh was real, and somehow that was worse than a threat. "Then my men take the documentation off you — and yes, we know you went back upriver for it, the smoke from our fuel cache was visible from the ridge and so were you, coming down with a dry bag — and we take your mother to a hospital anyway, because I'm not a monster, and we take the botanist because he'll sign anything if you put a gun near the girl, and you—" he considered her, frankly, the way he might consider a piece of equipment with a fault line in it— "you I'd prefer not to have an accident. But accidents are cheap out here, Ms. Vane. You know that better than most. How many bodies has the river taken this season alone? Nobody counts. That's the beauty of it."

"There it is," Oscar said.

"There what is?"

"The arithmetic." She took a step toward him, and one of the riflemen shifted, and Hale lifted two fingers without looking — *stand down, she's talking* — because he wanted to hear the end of whatever he assumed was her surrender. "You're very good at it. Eleven signatures. Sixty meters of water. A thousand years. You count everything except the one number that matters."

"Which is?"

"Which survey your backers' permit rests on."

For the first time since the launch had touched the bank, Corliss Hale stopped smiling. It wasn't much — a millimeter of slack around the eyes, a breath taken through the nose instead of the mouth — but Oscar had spent ten years reading faces across flooded quarries and auction-house back rooms, and she knew what a man looked like when a word landed somewhere he hadn't armored.

"Floodplain survey, 1978," she said. "Commissioned by the provincial water authority, signed by a Dr. E. Marchetti, hydrologist. It's the foundational document. Every subsequent study cites it. It establishes the valley floor elevation, the flow rates, the displacement figures — the numbers that prove this basin can hold a reservoir without back-flooding into the Anco lands upstream." She paused. "Marchetti never surveyed the valley floor."

The rain filled the silence. One of the riflemen coughed.

"In 1978," Oscar continued, conversationally, "the dry-season channel ran along the eastern bluff. You can still see the old course — the sediment banding, the scoured gravel. Marchetti surveyed the *channel*, because the channel was accessible, and then someone — I'd love to know who, I have theories, all of them expensive — transposed those measurements onto the floodplain and called it done. The elevations are wrong by four meters. Maybe five. At design capacity, the reservoir doesn't hold. It overtops the northern saddle and puts two meters of seasonal floodwater through every village the Anco community has left, including the mission settlement, including the school." She shrugged. "I didn't figure this out myself. My mother did, nine years ago. It's the other reason she buried her report. She found the falsification in the same archive box where she found the first references to the burial city. She called it, in her field notes, *the same lie wearing two coats* — the state pretending the valley was empty so it could take it, and the survey pretending the water would behave so it could drown it."

Hale had gone very still. Stillness, in a man like Hale, was the loudest thing he could do.

"You're lying," he said at last. "Or guessing. A salvage diver doesn't read hydrological surveys."

"A salvage diver reads *water*." She pointed at the river with her chin. "I've been watching this flood climb for three days. I know its rate within centimeters per hour, because my life has depended on that kind of number since I was nineteen years old. Two nights ago, at the confluence, I marked the stage height on that fig tree and compared it against the rainfall record Teo Reyes keeps in his field book — he's meticulous, it's a botanist thing. The discharge doesn't match the published basin model. It runs high. Consistently, structurally high, exactly the way a basin runs high when the modeled storage volume is inflated by four meters of fiction." She let that sit. "Your backers' engineers haven't caught it yet because they're working from the same 1978 numbers. They'll catch it during construction, when the coffer dams won't draw down. Or they'll catch it the year after completion, when the saddle overtops and drowns the school. Either way, Mr. Hale, the permit is built on a falsified survey, and I have the proof."

"You don't have anything," Hale said. "You have a dry bag and a story."

"I have Marchetti's original field sheets." She said it flatly, without triumph, because triumph was a smell and Hale would have smelled it and bolted. "Recovered from the provincial archive annex in Puerto Rojas eight months ago, by a researcher who owed my mother a favor. Marginalia in Marchetti's own hand: *channel data only — do not extrapolate*. Dated three weeks before the final report was issued with the extrapolated figures. Someone above him made the switch. His field sheets prove it. They're not in the dry bag." She watched his eyes drop, involuntary, to the waterproof roll slung across her shoulder, and she almost pitied him. Almost. "They're in three places. One copy goes to the national audit office the moment I reach the mission settlement — Rosa Anco's community has a lawyer now, did you know that? A good one, paid for by a church group that's been asking questions about this valley since before you were hired. One copy is with my mother, who has instructions. And one copy is somewhere you will never find it, held by someone who will send it if I don't send word every evening. That's not paranoia, Mr. Hale. That's how my mother taught me to handle evidence. You burn a page of your own journal, you learn habits."

Hale looked at the river. When he spoke again, the salesman's warmth was gone, and what remained was thinner and more human and more dangerous: a man doing sums he hated.

"What do you want."

"Passage," Oscar said. "Downriver, all of us — my mother, Teo, me. To Puerto Rojas, or to the mission landing, whichever comes first. No stops, no detours, no messages sent ahead. Your men stay on the boat and keep their rifles pointed at the scenery."

"And the documentation?"

"Goes where it goes. I'm not trading it. I'm trading my *silence*." She stepped closer, close enough that the rain between them seemed suddenly loud. "Here's the arithmetic you missed, Mr. Hale. If that permit fails — and it will fail, because falsified foundations always fail eventually, all you control is whether it fails quietly or catastrophically — your backers don't just lose the dam. They lose the compensation clause, the antiquities concession, the entire portfolio position they built on this valley. They will want to know who sold them a falsified survey as collateral. They will ask you. And the answer to *when* they find out depends entirely on how I feel about the next twenty-four hours. Bring us downriver safe, and the audit office receives an anonymous package next month, and the story is about a dead hydrologist and a careless ministry, and nobody's name is in it. Hurt us, chase us, put one hand on my mother — and the package goes out tomorrow with a cover letter naming your firm, your letterhead, and the redacted seal on that beautiful government letter you carry around like a rosary." She smiled at him, and it was not a kind smile, and she found she didn't mind. "You can't fake what I have. You can't buy it, because I don't want money. You can't steal it, because stealing it proves it. All you can do is decide whether I'm your enemy or your silence. Silence is cheaper. Silence is the best price you'll ever get."

A long moment. The river climbed. Downstream, gray on gray, the main current carried a broken branch past the bend like a slow hand waving goodbye.

Hale turned and looked at the launch, at his men, at the treeline where somewhere behind him a woman with a failing heart lay under a tarp trusting her daughter to do exactly this. Then he laughed — short, genuine, disgusted, the laugh of a gambler watching someone turn over the card he'd palmed.

"Your mother," he said, "must be an extraordinary woman."

"She is," Oscar said. "That's the whole problem."

He waved two fingers at the boat. "Get her aboard. Carefully. If she codes on my deck, I'm throwing the body overboard and telling the inquiry you kidnapped her." He was already turning away, barking orders in a flat river patois — *lines off, clear the bank, get the canvas up* — and then he stopped, half-turned, and said, without any smile at all:

"The fuel cache cost me eleven thousand dollars."

"Send me the invoice," Oscar said. "I'll frame it."

* * *

Getting Iris down the cutbank took four men and all of Oscar's patience. Her mother weighed nothing — that was the worst part, the lightness of her, this woman who had once hauled core samples up a mountainside like they were groceries — and she hated being carried with a passion that briefly restored her to full health. She cursed Hale in Latin, which impressed the riflemen despite themselves, and cursed Oscar in English, which was quieter and worse.

"You gave him the copies," Iris muttered as they slid her onto the padded bench under the canopy. Her skin was gray as the rain. "Three places. Evening check-ins. You planned this before you came down to the bank."

"I learned from a paranoid woman," Oscar said, tucking the blanket under her mother's elbows. "Best education I never thanked anyone for."

Iris's hand found hers — cold fingers, a grip that still had bedrock in it. "The Marchetti sheets. Tell me they're real."

"They're real. I bought them eight months ago and I've been waiting for a reason." Oscar held her mother's eyes. "I was always coming back, Mamá. I just needed the river to make it urgent."

Iris closed her eyes. Something moved across her face that was not quite a smile and not quite tears, and she said, so softly only Oscar could hear it under the engine's first throaty rev: "Your father would have bargained harder."

"He'd have set the boat on fire," Oscar said.

"Yes," her mother breathed. "Yes, he would have."

The launch pulled away from the cutbank and swung into the main current, and the valley began to slide past — the terraced shadows under the brown water, the drowned staircase, the high dark seam of the gallery where a woman named Marta was keeping a light burning for rescuers who were, at this moment, three hours behind them and closing. Oscar stood at the stern rail and watched it all go by, the city staying exactly where it belonged, hidden by choice, defended by paper, and felt the strangest sensation in her chest, which took her a long moment to identify because she had not felt it in ten years.

She was going downriver. And for the first time in her adult life, she was not running.

Then one of Hale's men touched her elbow and pointed upstream, to where the tributary met the main river — and where, riding down the flooded chute with the current, oars shipped, moving fast, came a long dugout canoe flying no flag at all, and in its bow a figure raising one arm in a signal Oscar recognized from Rosa Anco's stories: palm open, fingers spread.

*We see you. We are coming.*

The rescue hadn't waited for permission either.
