## 20. Marta's Light

The message came through at 04:12, crackling out of the settlement's ancient radio in a voice Oscar didn't recognize — a rescue coordinator from the regional bureau, reading coordinates off a card like scripture. *Cavern accessed. Lower chambers flooded as projected. One survivor located in upper gallery. Condition stable. Repeat: condition stable.*

Stable. The word went through Oscar like a fishhook through soft tissue, catching on everything.

She had spent nine days telling herself that Marta Quispe was dead. She'd done the arithmetic a hundred times in the dark of the mission settlement's guest room, lying on a cot that smelled of woodsmoke and dried corn: the water rising through the sump behind them, the air in the lower chambers finite, one injured woman with a shattered ankle left holding a carbide lamp and a promise no rational person should have made. Oscar had run the numbers until they ran her instead, and every calculation ended the same way — with Marta's light going out somewhere under two hundred meters of stone while the rest of them swam toward morning.

*Condition stable.*

Oscar stood in the radio room in her underwear and boots, the receiver pressed to her ear hard enough to hurt, and made the operator repeat it three times before she could make herself let go of the set.

Teo found her there twenty minutes later, still standing, still holding the dead frequency like a talisman. He didn't say anything. He just put his hand on the back of her neck, the way you'd steady someone on a ladder, and stood with her while the generator coughed and the rain came down on the tin roof like applause for something none of them deserved.

They flew out at first light in a bureau helicopter that smelled of kerosene and mildew, because the river was still running brown and murderous and the overland route would take four days they couldn't justify. Rosa Anco came with them — not as a passenger but as a presence, sitting straight-backed in the jump seat with her hands folded, watching the valley unroll beneath them with an expression Oscar couldn't read and eventually stopped trying to. From the air, the floodplain looked like a bruise. The tributary cut through it silver and swollen, and where the waterfall should have been there was only a white smear, the whole cliff face weeping.

The landing zone was a gravel bar half a kilometer downstream of the cavern mouth. The rescue team had already rigged lines; men in orange drysuits moved along them with the unhurried competence of people doing a job rather than performing a miracle. Oscar walked past all of it. Her body seemed to know the way even though she'd only made it once, in terror, in the dark, with Teo's weight dragging at the line and the flood chewing at their heels. Her feet found the wet stone, the ledge, the curtain of falling water now thinned by the receding peak, and then she was inside, headlamp beam swinging across walls that had held a city's dead for six hundred years and, for eleven days, one living woman.

The lower chambers were still flooded — black water standing chest-high in the passage where they'd nearly died, carrying a fine sediment that turned every surface slick. But the rescue team had strung a guide rope up through the chimney to the high gallery, and Oscar climbed it hand over hand with her heart going like a trapped bird, and came up over the lip of stone into light.

Not carbide light. Not the guttering, sulfur-smelling desperation she'd imagined for nine days. Daylight — real daylight, funneled down through a natural shaft in the gallery ceiling, falling in a column so clean and gold it looked painted. And in the middle of it, propped against a rolled sleeping pad with her leg splinted in aluminum and gauze, sat Marta Quispe, eating a ration biscuit.

She looked terrible. She was gray-faced and hollow-eyed, her lips cracked, her hands shaking slightly around the biscuit. There were carbide smears on her jaw where she'd been adjusting her lamp, and the lamp itself sat beside her, burned down to a stub, its job done. Behind her, arranged along the gallery wall with a care that made Oscar's throat close, were the artifacts — the ceramic vessels, the copper ornaments, the bundle of textiles wrapped in Iris's groundsheet — exactly where Marta had moved them, out of the flood line, dusted and ordered, guarded by a woman with a broken ankle and a dying lamp for eleven days.

Marta looked up. Her eyes found Oscar's headlamp and she squinted against it.

"Turn that off," she said, voice scraped raw. "You're ruining the light."

Oscar turned it off. And then she crossed the gallery — three steps, four — and knelt down in the gold column and took Marta's face in both her hands, and neither of them said anything at all, because there wasn't anything that fit. Marta's skin was cold and papery. Her pulse beat against Oscar's thumb, slow and stubborn, like something that had decided a long time ago not to stop.

"You did the math," Marta rasped finally. "Back at camp. I saw your face when you volunteered me for the gallery." A ghost of a grin. "You thought I was dead."

"I ran it," Oscar admitted. Her voice came out wrecked. "Every night. It never once came out this way."

"That's because you calculate like a diver." Marta patted the stone beside her. "You count what runs out. You don't count what comes in." She nodded upward, at the shaft, at the column of light. "Found that hole on day two. Air shaft, maybe old, maybe natural. Rain came down it sometimes, but mostly it just — gave. Kept the air moving. Kept a little light." She shrugged, wincing. "I figured whoever built this place knew more about living in stone than we do. Seemed rude to assume otherwise."

Behind Oscar, someone made a sound — Teo, coming up over the lip, and beyond him the medics, and beyond them, impossibly, Iris.

Oscar hadn't known if her mother would come up. The hospital in Puerto Rojo had kept her four days; the cardiologist's language had been firm and final, and Iris Vane had signed her own discharge against advice with the same pen she used for field notes. She'd flown up against stronger advice still. Now she stood at the edge of the light with her gray hair loose and her hands empty, and she looked at Marta the way archaeologists look at things they know they have no right to touch.

"Marta," Iris said. Just the name. Her voice failed after that.

"Dr. Vane." Marta's grin widened a fraction. "Told you I'd hold it."

Iris crossed the gallery slowly, favoring nothing, hiding everything, and lowered herself to her knees beside her student with a gracelessness Oscar had never seen in her — nine years of watching this woman move through dig sites like weather, and here she was folding down onto wet stone like any mortal, and taking Marta's dirty hand in both of hers, and bowing her head over it.

What she said was too quiet for Oscar to hear. That was all right. Some things weren't for witnesses.

* * *

They brought Marta down on a litter, slow and careful, the whole operation taking most of the afternoon because Marta insisted — insisted — on supervising the packing of the artifacts herself, calling out instructions from the litter in a voice that got stronger with each hour. *Textiles flat. Ceramics nested, straw between. Copper last, it scratches.* The rescue team exchanged glances over her head. Nobody argued.

At the gravel bar, waiting for the helicopter, Oscar sat with her back against a supply crate and watched the river. It had dropped a meter since the peak; the brown water had given way to a tired green, and along the margins the drowned vegetation lay flattened in rows like combed hair. Somewhere upstream, Hale's fuel cache was still smoking — or what remained of it. Somewhere downriver, Hale himself was sitting on the proof Oscar had traded him, keeping his end of a bargain that had cost him his backers and bought five lives. She wondered which of those facts he resented more.

Iris settled beside her. Not gracefully — Oscar watched her brace her arm against the crate, watched the small pause while her breath caught and resumed, and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite anger anymore. It had softened into something harder to carry: the specific fear of loving someone whose body is quietly failing.

"The cardiologist gave me a pamphlet," Iris said, without preamble. She was looking at the river too. "It has a diagram of a heart on it. Like a textbook illustration. I keep thinking about how many diagrams I've drawn of things I've taken apart, and now there's one of me." She exhaled. "I signed something else, too. In Puerto Rojo. Before I flew up."

Oscar turned. "Signed what?"

"A withdrawal. Formal. My name comes off everything — the site report, the survey data, the documentation you carried out. All of it." Iris's jaw worked. "Nine years ago I would have told you that was impossible. My name on a discovery is the only currency I ever had. I built my whole life on being the person who finds things first." She laughed, short and dry, no humor in it. "And then I spent thirty years learning what happens to the things we find. Every site I ever opened, I can tell you who profited and who paid. The ledger never balances, Oscar. It never once balanced in my favor."

"So you're erasing yourself," Oscar said. "Like I do."

Iris looked at her then, really looked, and something passed between them that had no words attached — recognition, maybe, or its gentler cousin. "I suppose I learned it from watching you," she said. "Or you from me. At this point I honestly can't tell which."

She reached into her jacket — carefully, the way she now did everything — and produced a folded paper, creased into eighths, soft at the folds from handling. She smoothed it on her knee and held it out.

"It's not official yet. The bureau wants signatures, affidavits, the whole theater. But this—" the paper, Oscar saw now, was a letter, handwritten, addressed to the regional heritage office. "This is mine. I wanted you to read it before I send it. Not for permission. For witness."

Oscar took it. The handwriting was her mother's — small, precise, unhurried — and the letter was short. It stated that Dr. Iris Vane was withdrawing her name from all claims, publications, and professional credit pertaining to the site designated in the enclosed documentation. It stated that the documentation itself had been compiled under conditions of emergency and belonged, in any moral sense, to the descendant communities of the valley. It ended with a single sentence that Oscar read four times:

*I have spent my career opening doors, and I am asking, finally, to be permitted to close one gently.*

Below it, the signature. Below the signature, a blank line, and beneath the blank line, smaller: *Witnessed.*

"They want a witness signature," Iris said. "Someone who can attest the withdrawal was voluntary and lucid." She held out a pen. "I could ask anyone on this river. But I'd rather it was you."

Oscar looked at the pen. Looked at the line. Nine years of silence, and here was her mother handing her the smallest possible act of trust — not an apology, not an explanation, just a line at the bottom of a page and the implicit confession that some things needed another person standing there to be real.

Her hand didn't shake. She'd expected it to.

She signed. The pen scratched loud in the river quiet, and when she finished, Iris took the letter back, folded it along its worn creases, and tucked it inside her jacket, over the heart the pamphlet had diagrammed, and neither of them said anything about it, and that was right too.

* * *

The helicopter lifted off the gravel bar at dusk, banking south toward Puerto Rojo, and the valley opened beneath them one last time — the tributary, the falls, the green dark of the forest closing over the cliff face where a staircase descended into stone. Marta slept against the bulkhead, sedated, her splinted leg propped on a crate, one hand resting on the artifact case beside her even in sleep, fingers curled around the handle like a child around a doorframe.

Rosa Anco sat across from Oscar. For a long time she said nothing, watching the valley sink away below, and when she finally spoke her voice was low enough that it stayed between the two of them, under the rotor noise.

"My grandmother told a story about that place," she said. "About a city the river took back. She said the ancestors didn't drown — they went inside the mountain and closed the door, and they would open it again when someone came who asked permission." She turned her head. Her eyes in the cabin gloom were unreadable and very old. "Eleven days that woman sat in the dark with our dead, and she asked permission for everything. Where to set the pots. Whether to unwrap the textiles. She talked to them, Oscar. Out loud. Like guests."

Oscar followed Rosa's gaze to Marta's sleeping face, to the hand on the case.

"She wasn't guarding treasure," Rosa said. "She was keeping house. There's a difference. My people have known the difference for five hundred years, and it's the difference everyone else keeps failing to learn." She leaned back. "The claim documents go before the review board in three weeks. After what happened to Hale's backers, and after your mother's letter, there are people in that bureau suddenly very interested in being on the right side of history." A pause. "There will be hearings. Testimony. They'll want the divers who went in. They'll want the woman who held the gallery."

The helicopter droned southward. Below, the last light caught the main river, turning it briefly to hammered copper, and then the forest took even that.

Oscar thought about the blank line she'd signed. About doors opened and closed. About a woman who'd done the arithmetic of survival with a broken ankle and a stub of carbide, and come out the other side having counted something no equation holds — what comes in, when you leave a light burning and talk to the dark like company.

Three weeks, Rosa had said. Hearings. Testimony. A room full of strangers deciding whether a buried city belonged to the living who remembered it or the powerful who'd found it.

Oscar realized she was already rehearsing what she'd say.
