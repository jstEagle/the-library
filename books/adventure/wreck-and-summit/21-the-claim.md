## 21. The Claim

The rain came back in October, and with it the lawyers.

Oscar watched them arrive at the mission settlement the way she'd once watched weather fronts stack over open water — with a diver's instinct for what a storm could do to a hull. There were four of them: two from the regional heritage office, one from a university that had spent three decades publishing papers about the valley without ever asking its people a single question, and one thin man in a linen suit who introduced himself as an "independent consultant" and whose hands smelled of ink and government corridors. They sat on plastic chairs under the corrugated awning of Rosa Anco's kitchen annex, sweating through their shirts while Rosa poured them coffee from a dented aluminum pot and said nothing at all.

Oscar stood at the edge of the awning, arms crossed, close enough to hear and far enough to be ignored. That was her position now. She had learned it over nine years of salvage work — the best place to stand was always one step outside the circle of attention, where you could see everyone's hands.

The records lay on the table between them. Iris's field journals — the ones Oscar had carried out of the caverns wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat — plus the photographic plates Teo had developed in a darkroom he'd improvised out of a storage crate, plus the survey documents Oscar had lifted from Hale's camp the night she burned his fuel cache. Everything was there. The terraced stone beneath the floodplain. The drowned staircase. The carvings that predated every date in the literature by eight hundred years. And underneath all of it, folded into the back of Iris's oldest notebook, the thing that mattered most: the falsified survey, the one Hale's backers had used to secure their dam permit, the one that claimed the valley was geologically stable and archaeologically empty.

It was neither. It was full of ancestors, and it was going to flood.

"Mrs. Anco," the university man said, leaning forward with the careful patience of someone addressing a child, "what we're proposing is a collaborative framework. The site would be designated a protected zone under state administration, with academic oversight—"

Rosa set down the coffee pot. The sound it made against the table was small and final, like a stone dropped into deep water.

"My grandmother," she said, "told me about the city under the river when I was six years old. She told me her grandmother told her. We have songs about it. We have a word for it that your language doesn't have a translation for." She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried. "You are offering to protect my family's house by putting a lock on the door and keeping the key yourselves."

The silence that followed had weight. Oscar felt it settle over the awning like humidity.

"We filed this morning," Rosa continued, turning to the heritage office men as if the university man had already stopped existing. "A heritage claim. Under the statute of 1974, section eleven. The documentation is complete. You may examine it." She slid a folder across the table — not the originals, Oscar noted; Rosa had insisted on copies, the originals locked in a footlocker under her bed along with a shotgun older than anyone present. "You will find that the land parcel described in the claim matches, exactly, the parcel your bureau intends to drown behind a dam wall. You will also find, on page forty-one, evidence that the geological survey supporting that permit was fabricated. I believe the gentleman in the linen suit can speak to how such a document comes to exist."

The consultant's face did something complicated. Oscar almost pitied him. Almost.

* * *

The review stalled within the week.

Oscar tracked it the way she tracked everything now — through the mission's single telephone line, through the radio schedule Iris had drilled into the crew back when basecamp still ran like a fortress, through the newspapers that arrived three days late on the mail boat, soft with river damp. The bureau announced a "procedural pause" pending verification of the claim's documentation. Then a second pause. Then, in November, a statement so carefully worded it read like a man backing away from a cliff edge: *the Antiquities Bureau has determined that the proposed reservoir project requires comprehensive reassessment in light of newly available cultural and geological information.*

Newly available. As if the city hadn't been under the water for a thousand years. As if Rosa's grandmother hadn't sung about it before any of these men were born. Oscar read the statement twice, then folded the paper and set it under the leg of the wobbly table in the mission kitchen, which was the only honest use she could find for it.

Teo found her there that evening, sitting in the blue half-light with a cup of tea gone cold beside her. He'd filled out since the caverns — or maybe he just stood differently now, she thought. People who had nearly drowned sometimes did. He pulled up the other chair without asking, the way he'd started doing sometime in September, and she'd stopped minding sometime after that.

"You're smiling," he said.

"I'm not."

"There is a very small smile happening at the corner of your mouth. I've been studying it for weeks. It appears when you think no one's looking."

Oscar turned the cold tea in her hands. Outside, rain worked the tin roof, and somewhere down the slope a generator coughed and caught, coughed again. "The dam's dead," she said. "That's all. It's not a smile, it's arithmetic."

"It's a smile." He leaned back until the chair balanced on two legs, a habit that had given Iris fits during the expedition and apparently survived near-death intact. "Do you know what Marta asked me yesterday? Down at the clinic? She said, 'Has anyone apologized to the river yet?' Because in her family you apologize to a place before you take something from it, and nobody ever apologized to this valley. Not the bureau, not the company, not us." He tipped his head toward the window, toward the black shape of the hills beyond the settlement, where the tributary ran down to meet the main river somewhere out of sight. "I think we're the first ones who ever asked permission instead."

"And the river said?"

"The river," Teo said, "is still deciding about you specifically."

Oscar laughed — a short, surprised sound, rusty from disuse. She covered it by drinking the cold tea.

* * *

Hale's backers imploded the way large things implode: quietly, from the inside, mostly invisible unless you knew where to look.

Oscar knew where to look. She'd spent a decade erasing herself from records, which meant she'd spent a decade learning how records worked — how money moved through holding companies like water through gravel, how a consortium could be six names deep and still answer to two men, how a falsified survey didn't just sink a dam but dragged everything anchored to it down into the silt. By December, the engineering firm that had signed off on the fake geology had dissolved its contract. In January, the holding company restructured. In February, a business journal that reached the mission two weeks late reported that Corliss Hale had resigned from Meridian Valley Resources "to pursue independent consulting opportunities," which was, Oscar reflected, the corporate equivalent of abandoning ship in a neat suit.

She never saw him again. But once, in late January, a package arrived at the mission with no return address — inside, wrapped in butcher paper, a single page: the redacted letter, unredacted now, every seal and signature exposed, along with a note in a precise draftsman's hand. *For completeness. Some surveys should never have been filed.* No apology. Hale wasn't built for apologies. But he was built, she suspected, for survival, and survival sometimes looked like cutting your losses and mailing them to the people who beat you.

Rosa burned the note. She kept the letter, filed with the rest.

"They'll try again," Oscar said one night, standing with Rosa on the mission steps while the settlement's children chased each other through the puddled yard below, shrieking in two languages. "Not this year. Maybe not this decade. But a valley like this, with water like that — somebody will always come back with a new survey and a better smile."

"Yes," Rosa agreed, placid as the flooded quarry Oscar had once worked, where the water looked calm over sixty feet of drowned machinery. "And we will be here. That is the difference. My grandmother's grandmother was here when the first surveyors came. I am here now. My granddaughter—" she nodded toward the yard, toward a small fierce girl who had just pushed a boy into a puddle with great precision— "will be here after. You cannot outlast a people who are not going anywhere. Your mother understood that eventually. Most outsiders never do."

Oscar thought about saying something — about how Iris had understood it nine years ago, actually, and buried the report rather than hand the valley to the last people who'd promised to protect one. About how that choice had cost her everything except the daughter standing on these steps. But some accounts weren't hers to reconcile aloud. She just nodded, and Rosa, who missed nothing, let her keep the silence.

* * *

The claim was granted in March.

There was no ceremony. That was the part Oscar kept turning over afterward, the way she'd turn a recovered artifact in her hands looking for the maker's mark. She had expected trumpets, or at least newspapermen — justice, in her experience, always arrived with an audience or not at all. Instead, a clerk from the regional office came up on the mail boat, handed Rosa a folder containing a certificate and a boundary map, drank one cup of coffee, and left. The whole transaction took forty minutes.

That evening the community held what Rosa called, dryly, "a meeting." Everyone gathered in the long room behind the church — the same room where, months earlier, they'd pored over Iris's photographs by lamplight, elders tracing the carved processions with fingers that trembled slightly, naming figures out of stories they'd been telling their children for generations. Tonight there were no photographs. There was food, and singing that went on past midnight, and at one point an old man pulled Oscar onto the floor to dance despite her protests, and she discovered she remembered how, her body recalling things her mind had surrendered years ago.

The city itself stayed buried. That had been decided early, decided by Rosa and the council of elders before any lawyer arrived, and defended against every academic who came sniffing afterward with trowels and grant applications. The terraces under the floodplain belonged where they were. The staircase behind the waterfall would remain sealed — monitored, documented, guarded by the people whose ancestors had built it, opened for no one, not yet, maybe not ever. The dead did not need excavating. They needed leaving alone.

Oscar understood this in her bones, in the way divers understand pressure. Some things held their shape because nothing disturbed them. She had spent ten years disturbing everything — her records, her name, her history, scraping herself clean of every trace until there was almost nothing left to find. And here was a people who had done the opposite: kept everything, kept it close, kept it whole, and been stronger for it.

On the night of the meeting, she stepped outside into the warm dark and found Teo on the steps, sketching by flashlight — botanical drawings, the valley's plants catalogued in his careful hand, a different kind of record than the kind she kept.

"They're going to ask you to stay," he said, not looking up. "Rosa. For the stewardship program. Site monitoring, oral history transcription, the training curriculum. They want someone who knows the water and knows how looters think."

"I'm a salvage diver, Teo. I dig things up for money."

"You dug things up for money because you were running." Now he looked up, and the flashlight caught the bottom of his face, leaving his eyes in shadow. "You're not running anymore. I've watched you unpack. Slowly. One bag at a time. Like you're testing whether the ground holds."

Oscar said nothing. Down the slope, the river spoke to itself in the dark, patient, unhurried, carrying everything it carried toward the sea.

In the morning, Rosa called her into the kitchen annex and set a folder on the table between them — the same table where the lawyers had sweated. Inside: a position description, handwritten, in Rosa's own script. Co-director, Valley Stewardship Initiative. A salary that was modest and real. A start date of whenever Oscar was ready.

"No hurry," Rosa said, pouring the coffee. "The valley has waited a thousand years. It can wait for you to finish thinking."

Oscar took the cup. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the light coming through the trees was the particular gold that meant the season was turning — that meant, though she didn't know it yet, that a telegram was already moving toward her downriver, written in her mother's careful hand, asking a question that would require her, at last, to stop packing and start staying.

But that morning, she only drank her coffee, and watched the water slow where the tributary met the main current, and found — to her genuine surprise — that she had nowhere else she wanted to be.
