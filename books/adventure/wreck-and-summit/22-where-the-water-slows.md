## 22. Where the Water Slows

The confluence was best at dawn, when the two rivers argued in colors — the tributary running green and cold off the high valleys, the main river brown and heavy with the season's silt. They met a hundred meters below the mission settlement's landing, braided for a stretch, then surrendered to each other and became one water going one way. Oscar had been standing there long enough now that the villagers had stopped asking what she was looking for.

She wasn't looking for anything. That was the strange part. For ten years she had stood on riverbanks all over the map with her eyes fixed downstream, calculating current, reading debris lines, thinking about what the water carried away and how fast. Now she stood with her hands in her pockets and watched the slow eddy where a fallen ceiba trunk turned in circles it would never escape, and she felt nothing like urgency at all.

In her right pocket, her thumb found the compass.

It was a brass surveyor's compass, older than she was, its needle housing clouded with hairline cracks, its bezel worn smooth by forty years of Iris Vane's fingers before it ever touched hers. The needle still swung true if you held it level and waited. It always needed waiting. Her mother used to say a compass was not an answer machine; it was a patience machine. Oscar had hated that as a girl. She understood it now the way you understand things only after they've nearly killed you — which is to say completely, and too late to matter, and exactly in time.

* * *

The dry season had come to the valley the way it always did: not as an ending but as a revelation. The flood that had nearly taken them all in the spring had retreated down the main river in stages, leaving the banks scoured and reorganized, sandbars moved, channels rewritten. The tributary dropped until the waterfall that hid the cavern mouth ran thin as lace, and Rosa Anco's people walked up through it in single file with lanterns wrapped in oilcloth, and came back down carrying nothing but photographs and measurements and, once, weeping.

The city stayed buried. That had been the whole point, though it took outsiders months to believe it.

Oscar had watched the review board's decision arrive by radio — the dam permit withdrawn pending "further hydrological study," which everyone understood to mean never — and she had expected to feel triumphant. She didn't. She felt the way she'd felt surfacing from the sump with Teo coughing river out of his lungs beside her: wrung out, blinking, alive in a way that made ordinary light seem loud. Justice, it turned out, did not feel like winning. It felt like putting something heavy down very carefully so it wouldn't break.

Hale's backers had imploded quietly, exactly as quietly as they'd operated. There were rumors of a falsified survey, of a bureau official reassigned, of a salvage company's assets frozen in three jurisdictions. Hale himself had vanished upriver or downriver or into some capital's back rooms; Oscar liked to imagine him wherever he was, explaining to men with redacted letters why a diver with no institutional backing had beaten them with a stolen fuel cache and a bluff about paperwork. She hadn't bluffed, actually. The falsified survey was real, and copies of it now lived in four places, and one of those places was a strongbox under the floor of the Anco community house. She'd learned that lesson from her mother, finally, at fifty-three years old: you don't hide the truth. You distribute it.

Marta had come out of the cavern on a stretcher in June, dehydrated and furious and clutching a field notebook she'd kept by lamplight for eleven days, cataloguing burial chambers she could see but not reach. The rescuers — Rosa's grandsons, mostly, with Teo translating between Spanish and the valley dialect — had followed the sound of her tapping. Morse. Iris's old field code, passed down like everything else in that family, through hands that never meant to pass it on.

Iris had watched the stretcher come down the waterfall trail from a chair someone had carried up for her, because her heart would not allow her to climb, and because Iris Vane had never in her life accepted a limitation without renegotiating its terms. She'd gripped the armrests. She'd said nothing. But when Marta raised the notebook above her head like a trophy, Iris had laughed — a short, cracked sound, startled out of her — and Oscar, standing behind the chair with her hand on her mother's shoulder, had felt the laugh go through both of them like current through water.

That had been five months ago. The hospital in the city had kept Iris for six weeks. She'd discharged herself against advice, obviously, and come back to the valley, obviously, and now she lived in the room behind the community house that Rosa had cleared for her, surrounded by boxes of records and a radio she'd rebuilt herself, teaching Rosa's grandchildren to read survey maps on the veranda in the evenings.

And Oscar — Oscar had stayed.

* * *

She heard footsteps on the bank path and knew them without turning. Iris walked differently since the hospital, slower, with a stick Rosa's son had carved for her, but she still walked like a woman arriving rather than passing through.

"You're up early," Iris said, coming to stand beside her at the waterline. "Or late. With you I've stopped being able to tell."

"Late," Oscar said. "Couldn't sleep."

"Your father used to say the river talks to people who can't sleep." Iris looked out at the confluence. "He said it mostly to annoy me."

"He said it to annoy you," Oscar agreed. "He told me it was true."

They stood together while the eddy turned its log. It had been nine years of silence, then six months of something else — not warmth exactly, not yet, but a kind of working honesty, two professionals sharing a site. They spoke in logistics most days. But logistics, Oscar had learned, could be a language of love if both parties agreed to pretend otherwise. *Did you eat. Did you take the digoxin. The radio mast needs re-guying before the rains.* I love you, translated.

"I have a proposal," Iris said.

"God."

"Listen first. Complain after. You get that from me."

Oscar almost smiled. "Go on, then."

"The Anco council has voted. They want a stewardship program — their people trained as site guardians, proper documentation, oral history recorded alongside the surveys, a watch over the valley that answers to the valley. Rosa wants it formal. Funded. Recognized by whatever ministries can be persuaded to recognize things." Iris paused, and Oscar heard her mother choosing words, which was rarer than rain in the dry season. "They need two co-directors. Someone who knows the bureaucracy and the science. And someone who knows rivers, and salvage, and how to move through country like this, and—" Iris exhaled. "And someone the community already trusts, which is not me. I burned my credibility in this valley thirty years ago with a report I buried. Rosa forgave me. Trust is slower than forgiveness."

Oscar said nothing. The compass needle turned in her pocket, patient as ever.

"Teo will take the botany and the training," Iris went on. "Rosa will hold the cultural authority, and no one will pretend otherwise. But the field program needs a lead who isn't afraid of water, or of men like Hale, or of hard arithmetic." She turned then, and looked at her daughter directly, and Oscar saw that her mother's hands were trembling slightly around the carved stick, and that she was letting them be seen. "Co-director, Oscar. Not assistant. Not digger. Not replacement hired last-minute, no questions asked. Co-director. Questions asked, all of them, answered whenever you like."

The river talked. Oscar let it talk for a while.

"You know what I was doing before your telegram," she said at last. "Ten years of erasing myself. Different names on different manifests. I got good at it. I could vanish from a company's books in an afternoon." She shook her head slowly. "You know why?"

"Because of me."

"Because every record was a rope tied to a dock I didn't want to be tied to. Every name, every contract, every fixed address — it was all just somewhere to be left from. I thought if I never unpacked, nothing could ever be taken away again." She looked down at the water. "Like Dad."

"Yes," Iris said quietly. "Like your father."

"And now you're offering me the opposite. A title. A program. A place on maps other people draw."

"I'm offering you work worth staying for. The staying part is yours to decide. It always was." Iris's voice roughened. "I chose a dig over his funeral, and I have spent thirty years telling myself it was principle, and I have spent six months knowing it was cowardice wearing principle's coat. I won't make that trade again. Not with you. So I'm asking, not assuming. And if the answer is no, I'll run the program alone and you'll still have a bed here whenever—"

"Yes."

Iris stopped.

"Yes," Oscar said again, and found that saying it twice made it more real, the way a second fix on a bearing confirms the first. "Yes, co-director. Yes, questions asked. Yes to—" She gestured at the whole valley, the terraces sleeping under the floodplain, the waterfall, the settlement with its smoke rising straight in the windless morning. "All of it."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment with an expression Oscar had never seen on her face — not pride, which she'd seen, cold and rare, but something softer and less defended. Then Iris did something even rarer. She reached out and put her hand on the side of Oscar's face, briefly, the way you touch something you're afraid might not be there.

"Well," Iris said, stepping back, voice restored to its usual dryness though her eyes hadn't gotten the message. "First order of business, Co-Director Vane. We'll need the skiff surveyed before the rains. Second, Rosa wants the eastern blazes remarked — your marks, apparently, are the only ones the grandsons trust. Third—"

"Third," Oscar said, "you take your morning medication, which I know you skipped, because the bottle was still shut when I came through the kitchen."

Iris glared at her. It was, Oscar thought, the warmest glare she'd ever received.

* * *

She went back to her room at midday, the small one at the end of the community house with the window facing the water, and she did the thing she had been circling for weeks the way the log circled in the eddy.

She unpacked.

It should not have been difficult. It was, in fact, the simplest physical act of her adult life: open the duffel, lift things out, put them in places. But her hands stalled at the zipper the way they'd stalled at the hatch of the sump, and she had to stand there breathing the way she'd taught Teo to breathe, slow and counted, before she could do it.

Everything she owned came out in twenty minutes. That was the whole inventory of ten rootless years: two shirts and a spare pair of trousers, a dive knife, a coil of line gone soft with use, a waterproof bag of documents that had changed a valley's future, a photograph of her father on a boat deck squinting into sun, and the compass.

She set the photograph on the windowsill, facing the river, so he could watch the water. She hung the shirts on nails. She coiled the line and hung it beside them, and put the knife in the drawer, and the documents in the strongbox under the floorboards that Rosa's son had built for her without being asked, because in this valley you built the strongbox before you were asked, the way you mended a neighbor's roof.

Then she sat on the edge of the narrow bed with the compass in both hands and opened it.

The needle swung, wobbled, settled. Northeast, toward the headwaters and the drowned staircase. She turned. South-southwest, downriver, toward the cities and the companies and the manifests with false names on them. The needle didn't care where she'd been. It only cared where north was, and it would tell her forever, patiently, as many times as she asked.

Her mother's handwriting was scratched inside the case lid, tiny, in ink gone brown: *True north is not where you are going. It is how you know where you are.*

Oscar had read that inscription a hundred times over the years and thought it sentimental. Sitting in her own room, in a place with her name on a program and her shirts on nails and her father watching the river, she finally understood it. All those years of motion hadn't been freedom. They'd been a person moving fast enough to avoid finding out where she was. And now she knew. She was here. At the confluence. Where the water slowed down enough to braid itself together and become one thing.

Outside, Teo's voice rose in argument with one of Rosa's grandsons about the correct angle for a blaze mark, and somewhere down the path Iris was presumably negotiating with the universe about taking her medication, and the river went on talking to anyone who couldn't sleep, and would talk tomorrow, and the day after.

Oscar closed the compass, set it on the windowsill beside the photograph — not packed, not pocketed, just placed, in the light — and went out to measure the skiff.

There was a season coming, and for the first time in her life, she would be there when it arrived.
