## 1. The Weight of Staying

The trick to leaving a place well, Cyrus Brightwater had learned over ten years and eleven seasonal contracts, was to start leaving the day you arrived.

You kept your gear packed in the same order every night. You learned the names of the locals only as far as politeness required—first names, never surnames, because surnames invited questions about whether you were related to anyone. You did your work cleanly enough to be remembered fondly but not so memorably that anyone got ideas. And on the last morning, you drank your coffee standing up, shouldered your pack before the sun cleared the ridge, and walked out while the town was still too sleepy to make leaving feel like anything more than weather.

She was three days from executing this maneuver on the town of Merrow's Bend, population four hundred and eleven according to the sign, which she suspected had not been updated since the Nixon administration, when the mule decided to complicate everything.

"Absolutely not," Cyrus told him.

The mule—a dun-colored animal with the moral compass of a raccoon and the name Huckleberry, which struck her as a slander against literature—stood with one hoof planted directly on her coil of climbing rope and an expression of profound satisfaction.

"Huck." She held out her hand. "We've talked about this."

Huckleberry blinked at her with the serene confidence of a creature who had never once been talked out of anything.

It was the fourth morning in a row he'd done this. Not biting, not kicking—Cyrus had worked with biters and kickers and had developed a professional respect for their straightforwardness. Huckleberry's vice was theft. He stole rope, tarps, gloves, once an entire canvas tool roll that turned up two days later wedged under the water trough like contraband. He stole things the way other animals chewed: constantly, compulsively, for reasons known only to himself.

"You know what I think it is?" said Deke Hollis, who owned the mule and half the trail crew and all of the opinions. Deke came around the supply shed wiping his hands on a rag that had started life white sometime in the previous decade. "I think he likes you."

"He likes my rope."

"Same thing." Deke squinted at the sky, which was doing its late-September thing—blue and hard and promising nothing good about October. "You're the only one who makes him work the switchbacks without hollering. Mule remembers kindness. It's his one flaw."

"It's going to be *my* one flaw if he eats my rope. That's forty dollars."

"I'll knock it off your invoice."

"You'll do no such thing. You'll add it to my invoice and then buy me a beer and call us even, which is how you've settled every debt since June."

Deke grinned, unrepentant, and Cyrus felt the familiar small ache she always felt around day sixty of a contract—the one where a place starts fitting itself into the grooves of you despite your best efforts. She'd gotten good at ignoring it. Ten years of practice. You didn't get good at things by enjoying them.

She wrestled the rope free of Huckleberry's hoof, coiled it, and slung it into the truck bed, and the morning resumed its ordinary shape: coffee from the thermos that tasted faintly of pine pitch no matter how many times she scrubbed it, the drive up the drainage road, six hours of cutting deadfall off the Copperline Trail with a crosscut saw while Huckleberry watched her with the adoring eyes of a stalker.

Ordinary was the whole point. Ordinary was the product.

* * *

The trouble with Merrow's Bend—and Cyrus had catalogued this trouble carefully, the way you catalogue the weaknesses of any place you intend to leave—was that it had exactly one bar, one diner, and one bulletin board, and all three of them were in the same building, so information moved through the town the way water moves through limestone: slowly, invisibly, and into everything.

She was in the diner at six-thirty, eating meatloaf she hadn't ordered but had accepted because Ruthie behind the counter operated on the principle that a thin woman eating alone needed feeding the way a cold engine needs priming, when the door banged open and Ranger Deputy Okafor came in shaking rain off a jacket that hadn't seen rain yet today and wouldn't see it again for weeks.

Okafor was new-ish—eight months in the district, which by Cyrus's standards made him practically a tourist—and he had the particular energy of a man who still believed the paperwork mattered. He scanned the diner, found her in the corner booth, and crossed to her with the deliberate speed of someone trying very hard not to look like they were hurrying.

"Brightwater."

"Deputy." She cut another bite of meatloaf. "If this is about the culvert on Copperline, the crew already flagged it. If it's about Huckleberry, the answer is no, I won't buy him."

"What?"

"Never mind. Sit down or don't, but stop hovering. You're blocking Ruthie's light and she gets superstitious."

Okafor sat. He put his hands flat on the table like a man about to pray or negotiate. Up close, Cyrus could see he hadn't slept much, and that the sleeplessness wasn't fresh—it had layers to it, days of it, worn down like a trail under boot traffic.

"The Kestrel survey team," he said. "Government outfit, contracted through the regional office. They went up the Vell approach nine days ago to run a boundary assessment above the tree line. They were due back Monday."

Cyrus ate her meatloaf. Outside, the light was going long and amber across the parking lot, catching the dust on her truck, and somewhere in the back of her mind a clock she'd wound three days ago ticked steadily toward Friday morning and the open road south.

"It's Thursday," she said.

"It's Thursday," Okafor agreed. "No radio contact since Saturday. A spot check flight yesterday couldn't see the upper camps through cloud. The agency is calling it a communications failure. Weather window, equipment problems, that kind of thing." His hands stayed flat on the table. "I'm calling it what it looks like, which is nine people overdue in country that kills experienced people in September."

"That's unfortunate," Cyrus said, and meant it, and reached for her coffee.

"They're asking for trackers."

"Mm."

"Local ones. Ones who know the Vell approaches." He paused, and she heard the pause for what it was—a man stepping carefully onto ice he'd been told would hold. "Ones who know it *really* well."

There it was.

Cyrus set the mug down. She did it slowly, because her hands wanted to do it fast, and fast hands told stories.

"Deputy," she said, "I'm a trail maintenance contractor. I cut logs and clear drains and argue with a mule. My contract ends Friday. I'd invite you to check my references, except my references are mostly mules."

"I know who you are."

"No," Cyrus said pleasantly, "you really don't."

Okafor looked at her. He was younger than her by five years, maybe six, with the kind of earnest face that aged badly in this work, and right now the earnestness was aimed at her like a lamp.

"Your mother mapped the western Vell Range," he said quietly. "Every approach. Every camp. The routes in the agency's own field manual are hers. There are maybe three people alive who can read that ground the way she could, and one of them eats meatloaf in Ruthie's diner every Thursday, and I have been sitting in my truck outside this building for two days working up the nerve to ask her."

Ruthie, materializing with the pot, refilled Okafor's cup without being asked and gave Cyrus a look over his shoulder that contained an entire editorial page. Cyrus ignored it with the skill of long practice.

"My mother's been dead ten years," she said. "Whatever she knew, the mountain kept. I cut brush, Deputy. That's the trade."

"The agency will pay. Per diem, hazard rate—"

"Money's not—" She stopped herself. Money was fine. Money was a perfectly good reason and she intended to keep it that way. "I have a contract to close out. Equipment to return. There's a process."

"There's a helicopter fueling at the county strip tomorrow morning," Okafor said. "And after that there's weather coming in behind it, real weather, the kind that closes the high country until spring. If those nine people are alive up there, the finding-of-them happens in the next four days or it doesn't happen at all."

He stood, left money on the table for a coffee he hadn't drunk, and paused beside her booth.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'm not asking you to be her. I'm asking you to be the person who can find them. I'm told those aren't the same thing."

Then he was gone, the door swinging shut behind him, and the diner settled back into its evening hum, and Cyrus sat with her cooling meatloaf and understood that something had cracked.

* * *

She packed the truck that night anyway. That was the discipline—that was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing and sound. Tent rolled tight. Stove bagged. Food box inventoried, the map case squared away on top where her hand could find it blind. Eleven contracts, eleven clean exits. She could break camp in twenty minutes in the dark, and often had, and there was a comfort in it she'd stopped examining years ago because examining it never went anywhere good.

The comfort was this: nowhere had ever managed to hold her long enough to lose her.

Her father used to say she slept like a sailor—one ear open, boots by the bed. Her mother used to say—

Well. Her mother used to say a lot of things, most of them true, all of them unbearable, and Cyrus had spent a decade making sure none of them applied to her anymore.

She sat in the cab of the truck with the dome light off and the gear packed behind her and the cabin dark across the yard, and she thought about nine people above the tree line in the Vells, nine strangers whose names she didn't know, running out of stove fuel and daylight while a government office called it a communications failure.

She thought about the Vell approaches. That was the treacherous part—not the guilt, she could carry guilt like spare change, but the ground itself, rising up unbidden in her memory. The Copper Stair. The shelf above Mirror Tarn where the scree ran in long grey tongues and you read the slope the way her mother had taught her to read it, before Cyrus was old enough to know she was learning something she'd spend her whole life refusing to use. *Rock doesn't lie, Cy. People lie, maps go stale, but rock tells you exactly what it's done and when.* She'd been nine. She'd been so proud. She remembered being proud, the way you remember a room you grew up in.

Ten years since the funeral she hadn't attended. Ten years of contracts that ended at first snow, of towns that learned only her first name, of a signature—C. Brightwater, trail crew—that was technically true and completely empty, like a mailbox with no house attached.

The moon came out from behind the ridge and laid a pale stripe down the truck hood, and Huckleberry, tied at the fence line, blew air through his lips at her with the tenderness of a creature who had stolen her rope four mornings running and would steal it again tomorrow, and would miss her when she left, and would forget her by Tuesday.

That was the arrangement. That was the deal she'd struck with the world, and the world had honored it faithfully for a decade: you can go anywhere, as long as you're willing to be missed briefly and forgotten quickly.

Nine people, though.

She got out of the truck, walked to the fence, and stood with Huckleberry breathing his hay-and-dust breath against her neck while the moon did its indifferent work on the mountains to the north. The Vells were just visible past the foothills' black shoulders—a jagged white-toothed silhouette, holding whatever it was holding.

"I'm leaving Friday," she told the mule.

Huckleberry leaned his considerable head against her shoulder, nearly knocking her over, which was his way of agreeing with everything and committing to nothing.

She went inside, lay down on the cot with her boots by the bed like a sailor, and did not sleep. At some hour past midnight she got up, dug the old topographic map of the western Vells out of the bottom of the map case—she'd told herself she kept it for reference, the way you tell yourself all your lies—and spread it on the floor under the lamp.

Her mother's handwriting was on it. Of course it was. Half the maps in the district carried her marginalia, corrections, little penciled warnings in a slanted, impatient script: *rockfall active here after thaw — do not trust the 1978 route.* Ten years dead and still annotating the world.

Cyrus knelt over the map for a long time, one finger resting on the upper basin where the survey team had gone, where the pencil lines of her childhood converged like threads pulled tight.

Friday, she'd told the mule. Contract ends Friday. Truck loaded, account settled, gone by dawn.

Outside, the wind shifted and came down off the Vells carrying the first cold edge of the season, and Cyrus Brightwater sat on the floor of a cabin she'd meant to leave in three days, looking at her mother's handwriting, and felt the one-foot-out-the-door comfort crack straight down the middle.

At dawn, she was still sitting there.
