## 2. Gear With No Owners

The ranger's name was Deputy Okafor, and he had the particular stubbornness of a man who had been told no eleven times and intended to be told no twelve.

"No," said Cyrus, and felt the small satisfaction of a prophecy fulfilled.

He stood in the doorway of her cabin at the maintenance station, dripping snowmelt onto the boards she'd swept that morning, holding his hat in both hands like a man in a church he didn't believe in. Behind him the foothills went up gold and gray into the Vells, and somewhere up in all that gray was a survey team of six people who had stopped answering their radio nine days ago.

"I'm not asking you to find them," Okafor said. "I'm asking you to look."

"Looking is how finding starts. It's the first step. I've seen it happen."

"Cyrus." He said her name the way people had started saying it again lately—carefully, the way you'd set down a full cup. "Six people."

"Six people who hired a guide and filed a flight plan and carry satellite phones. Six people the government is already looking for, with helicopters, which is a thing I do not have." She leaned against the doorframe and crossed her arms, which was a posture she'd practiced until it looked natural instead of defensive, and even she had to admit it mostly worked. "I fix trails, Deputy. I move rocks from one side of a path to the other side of a path. It's very satisfying and nobody dies."

"The helicopters turned back yesterday. Weather window's closing. First snow is—" He glanced at the sky as if it might show him a date. "Early this year. Everyone says so."

"Everyone always says so."

"Everyone's usually right eventually."

"That's the trick of it," Cyrus said. "Say a thing long enough and you get to be right for free."

Okafor put his hat on, which she understood to mean he was done pleading and was now simply informing. "I'm going up to their last known camp tomorrow morning with two volunteers and a mule. If you happened to be walking in the same direction—well. I can't stop you. It's public land."

"It is," Cyrus agreed. "I love public land. I'm going to enjoy it from right here."

He nodded, thanked her for her time in a voice that made it clear she hadn't given him any, and crunched away down the road. Cyrus watched him go, then shut the door, then stood with her hand on the latch for a long moment, listening to the cabin settle around her like a dog curling up for the night.

Her contract had eleven days left on it. Eleven days, and then the truck she kept parked behind the station with a quarter tank and a packed duffel would carry her south, the way it had carried her north two seasons ago, the way something had carried her somewhere every year since she was twenty-two. Ten years of one foot out the door. It was a way of living that got described in flattering terms—free, untethered, light on her feet—and it was all of those things, the way a man running from a dog is light on his feet.

She made coffee she didn't drink and sat with it while it went cold, which was its own small ritual, a way of proving to herself that she could let a thing sit still.

The problem—the actual problem, the one she was going to spend all night not thinking about—was the map. She had a copy of the survey team's route, because everything filed with the county crossed the maintenance station's desk eventually, and she had read it the way she read everything that came out of the Vells: automatically, hungrily, before she could stop herself. And their route went up Miller's Fork and over the shoulder of Cairn Hill and into the high basin where her mother's last dig had been abandoned mid-season, twenty years ago, when the season and the woman both ended early.

Six people were overdue in the one place on earth Cyrus had spent a decade arranging her life to avoid.

She looked at her cold coffee. The coffee looked back, smug about something.

"Fine," she told it.

* * *

She caught up with Okafor's party at first light, which surprised him so thoroughly he dropped the mule's lead rope.

"You said public land," Cyrus reminded him, falling into step. "I'm a member of the public."

"I said that as a tactic. You weren't supposed to call the bluff." But he was grinning as he said it, and the two volunteers—a retired schoolteacher named Pru and a kid of maybe nineteen called Fen who kept looking at Cyrus's boots like they were famous—shifted over to make room for her on the trail without being asked. Word traveled in the foothills. She'd spent years making sure the word about her was *nothing*, and apparently all it took was one overdue survey team for the old word to surface again: *Brightwater's girl. Reads ground like scripture.*

Her mother had hated that phrase. *Ground isn't scripture, Cyrus, it's gossip. Everyone's talking. You just have to listen.* Cyrus had been eleven and had rolled her eyes, and her mother had laughed, and that memory arrived now without permission and had to be shoved back down where it belonged.

The trail up Miller's Fork was hers, in the contractual sense—she'd rebuilt the switchbacks below the narrows two summers back, and she took a proprietary pleasure in how well they were holding. Above the narrows the country opened into scree and krummholz and long gray slabs of stone, and the party went quiet the way people do when the land gets big. Pru hummed. Fen asked questions until Okafor gently suggested that mountains and questions were best taken one at a time.

The camp sat in a shallow saddle below the basin, a shelf of flat ground by a stream that was more suggestion than water this late in the year.

Cyrus saw it from two hundred meters out and stopped walking.

"What," said Okafor.

"Nobody's touched it," she said. "Nine days, and nobody's touched it."

They came down into the saddle and she made them stop at the edge of the camp and stand there, all four of them, like mourners at a viewing.

Here was what she saw. Two dome tents, guyed out taut and square, staked with the even spacing of people who expected to come back and didn't want to re-learn their pitch. A cook tarp folded over the stove box, corners tucked, weights on top—stones chosen for size, placed with a hand that cared. The food barrels were lashed and the lids were on. A shovel stood leaned against a rock at the exact angle of a shovel set down by someone meaning to pick it up again in an hour.

Nothing had been scavenged. That was the thing that put ice down her spine. Nine days in country with ravens and marmots and foxes and at least one wolverine she knew of, and not a single barrel lid pried, not one boot chewed, not one tarp tugged loose. The animals hadn't touched this camp because the animals had already read it the way she was reading it: *nobody lives here anymore, and nobody's coming back, and there's nothing to be afraid of.*

"No panic," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "You don't fold a tarp in a panic. You don't stake your tent square."

"Maybe they left in a hurry anyway," said Fen. "Maybe they—"

"Then show me where they went."

He blinked at her. She gestured at the ground around the camp, the whole ring of it, the meager grass and the gravel and the streambank, and waited while he looked and looked and slowly understood that he was looking at nothing. No trail out. No scuffed path toward the basin, no track toward the descent, no trampled streambank where six people and a guide had crossed water. The ground around that camp said, in the flat clear voice of ground everywhere: *six people arrived, set up housekeeping, and evaporated.*

Except it didn't say evaporate. Cyrus walked the perimeter slowly, hands in her pockets, and let the ground talk, and what it said was stranger than vanishing.

"They were carried," she said.

Okafor came to stand beside her. "Carried."

"Look at the weight marks. Here—" she crouched at the edge of the tent area, where the gravel held the faint memory of compression, the way snow holds the memory of a boot an hour after the boot's gone. "Loads went out from the tents toward the basin trail. Heavy, even loads, spaced like people under packs. But no footprints behind the loads. You walk, you leave feet. They didn't leave feet because they weren't walking under their own—"

She stopped.

Because she'd found the case.

It sat at the edge of the camp where the ground began to rise toward the basin, set down neatly on a flat stone, out of the drip line of the tarp, handle facing out. A government transit case, the olive-drab kind with the folding latches and the stenciled inventory numbers. And on its lid, in white paint, in a hand she knew better than her own—knew it from the inside covers of forty field notebooks and the margins of her childhood homework and the title page of the book that had made her mother's name and ruined everything—

**E.M. BRIGHTWATER — VELL RANGE SURVEY — PROPERTY OF THE SERVICE**

Cyrus did not touch it for a long moment. The others were saying things—Okafor saying her name, Pru saying something gentle—and she heard it the way you hear weather from indoors.

Her mother had been dead twenty years. This case had been government property, signed out in her mother's name, and it had been sitting in a saddle in the Vells long enough for the paint to chalk and fade, and someone had recently carried it up here and set it down on a stone with the handle out, like a gift, or a message, or a dare.

She opened it. Her hands were steadier than she expected them to be.

Inside, packed in wool that had once been army blankets: survey instruments. A theodolite, brass and glass, older than she was. Aneroid barometers in foam. A leather roll of plotting tools. And notebooks—six of them, water-stained, their spines broken soft with use, and when she opened the first one her mother's handwriting marched up the page in that fast slanted script, numbers and elevations and a marginal note in the margin of the margin, because her mother had never met a margin she respected: *C. would love this light. Bring her next season.*

Cyrus had been nine when that was written. There had been no next season. There had been no next anything.

She closed the notebook. She was aware that she was crouched on the ground holding her mother's handwriting in a government case in the mountains she'd fled, and that four people were watching her do it, and that the professional thing—the smart thing, the thing with one foot already out the door—was to put it down and walk back down the trail and let helicopters and deputies and other people's wars have it.

"Deputy," she said, and was interested to hear that her voice came out level. "Who signed this equipment out?"

Okafor had a clipboard. Okafor always had a clipboard. He flipped pages with the grim energy of a man who had already looked. "The survey permit lists a Dr. Aldous Vess as team lead. Equipment requisitions are in his name." He paused. "But the case stencil's original. That's old Service stock. That kit would've been signed out twenty years ago and never returned. It should've been written off."

"So somebody found it," Cyrus said. "In a records room, or a storage depot, or—" or in her mother's last cache, in the basin, in the dig site where the season ended early, "—and brought it up here, and left it where anyone looking for the survey team would be sure to find it."

"Why would they do that?" asked Fen.

Nobody answered him. Pru, who had taught school for thirty years and knew a rhetorical question when it froze solid, put a hand on the girl's—no, the boy's—shoulder and said nothing.

Cyrus stood. She looked up the trail toward the basin, where the ground rose gray and enormous into the cloud ceiling, where her mother had stopped mid-sentence twenty years ago and where six people had apparently walked out of their own footprints. The wind came down off the high country and moved through the krummholz with a sound like a long exhale, like the mountain itself saying *well?*

The smart move was down. The smart move had always been down and away and south, and she had ten years of practice at it, and her truck had a quarter tank and a duffel and no reason at all to stay.

But the ground was talking. It had been talking since the saddle, and now it was saying something she couldn't not hear: that someone had erased a trail in her mother's mountains, with her mother's instruments sitting in the middle of the erasure like a signature. That whoever had done it had known exactly whose name was stenciled on that case, and exactly what it would do to the one person in the foothills who could read what had been done.

They hadn't just hidden a survey team. They'd baited a hook, and the bait was her.

"Deputy," Cyrus said. "How many days to first snow?"

"Everyone says—"

"How many days?"

Okafor looked at the sky, and then at her, and had the grace not to enjoy it. "Twelve. Maybe fifteen if we're lucky."

She looked at the case. She looked at the basin. She thought about her truck, warm and patient and pointed south, and about the marginal note in a dead woman's handwriting—*bring her next season*—and about how a person could run from a place for ten years and still, apparently, arrive.

"I'll need the mule," she said, "and someone who can keep a radio dry."
