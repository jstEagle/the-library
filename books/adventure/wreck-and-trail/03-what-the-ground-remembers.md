## 3. What the Ground Remembers

Deputy Okafor drove like a man who had learned to respect gravel the hard way, which meant Cyrus spent forty minutes watching the foothills slide past at a pace that would have insulted a mule. She didn't complain. It gave her time to rehearse the speech she'd prepared in the shower, the one that went *I'll look at the camp, I'll tell you what I see, and then I'm getting back on the road to nowhere in particular.*

She'd given versions of that speech before. It usually worked. People could smell reluctance, and reluctance, unlike blood or fear, was the one scent trackers couldn't follow.

"You can say no," Okafor said, for the fourth time. His hands were steady on the wheel but his jaw kept working, like he was chewing on something that wouldn't go down. "I want to be clear about that. You're a contractor. You're not obligated to—"

"I know what I am."

"I'm just saying, if it were my family out there, I'd want somebody looking who actually knew what they were looking at. And everybody else has already walked the site twice."

"Walked it," Cyrus said. "Or read it?"

Okafor glanced at her. "Is there a difference?"

"There's always a difference." She looked out the window at the Vell Range, still holding its early snow like a secret it wasn't ready to tell. Her mother had spent twenty years learning that range the way other people learned languages. Cyrus had spent ten trying to forget it, which turned out to be impossible, because you couldn't forget a language you'd learned before you learned your own name.

* * *

The camp sat in a saddle below the tree line, a shelf of ground between two granite shoulders, and from two hundred yards out Cyrus could already tell that something was wrong with it. Not wrong the way a camp is wrong when a bear has been through it, or when weather has shredded it, or when people have left in a hurry. Wrong the way a sentence is wrong when it's grammatically perfect and means nothing.

"Nobody's touched it?" she asked.

"Perimeter's taped. Two of my people did a walk-through the first morning. They photographed everything and then we pulled back." Okafor stopped at the tape and didn't cross it, which Cyrus noted and filed away. A man who respected a scene even when he didn't know how to read one. That was worth something. "They found nothing. That's the part that's been keeping me up. Six people, eleven days out, and there's *nothing*."

Cyrus ducked the tape.

The first thing she did was stand still. This was the discipline her mother had drilled into her before she was tall enough to see over a pack frame: *the ground talks, Cyrus-girl, but only if you shut up long enough to listen.* So she stood at the edge of the camp with her hands in her pockets and let her eyes go soft and wide, the way you do when you're trying to catch movement in peripheral vision, and she let the camp talk.

Six tents. Or what had been six tents, now collapsed and staked down flat—staked down, she noted, not just abandoned. Poles broken down and laid parallel. Guy lines coiled. The cook area was swept, literally swept, the ash raked and the fire pit cold and tidy, with a flat stone set over the top like a period at the end of a sentence. Food was cached in bear hangs, three of them, properly done, bags up high and away from the trunks. The latrine trench was filled and the sod replaced so neatly you'd have to know what you were looking for to find it.

"Okay," she said quietly.

"Okay good or okay bad?"

"Okay *weird*." She crouched at the edge of the cook area. "This is a camp that was closed down. Not abandoned—closed. Like the end of an expedition. Like everybody packed up, said their goodbyes, and walked out in good order." She stood and turned a slow circle. "Except they didn't walk out. Because there's no exit."

She made herself walk the perimeter twice, once fast and once slow, and the slow pass was where the camp finally started confessing. The ground around the site was a mix of pine duff, sedge, and broken granite, and it should have been loud with six people's traffic—scuffed duff, compressed sedge, the little honest messes that human beings can't help leaving. Instead the approaches were clean. Too clean. Along the eastern edge, where the ground narrowed toward the drainage, the duff had been disturbed and then disturbed again, brushed with a branch in long even strokes.

"They swept it," she said. "Here, and here, and along this line." She walked the line with her finger in the air. "Somebody took a pine bough and brushed out their own tracks going east. Not scattered—*swept.* You scatter a trail to make people think a group broke up, or to fake a direction. Sweeping is different. Sweeping says *we were never here, and neither was anybody else.*"

Okafor had gone very still. "You're saying they erased themselves."

"I'm saying somebody did." She crouched again, at the last place the brushing stopped, where the duff gave way to granite slabs and the slabs gave way to scree. There, at the transition, she found what she was looking for, because there was always something, there was always one thing, the ground never gave up everything: a single boot print in the soft seam between two slabs, half a heel, deep, weighted. Someone carrying a heavy pack, moving carefully, heading up.

"Six people don't sweep their own trail," Cyrus said. "Sweeping takes time and patience and it's miserable work. Six scared people leave a scattered, ugly trail. One calm person with a plan and a branch leaves this." She looked up at the scree field climbing toward the ridge, a grey river of broken stone frozen mid-pour. "And that person went up. Into the Vells. In October."

"Nobody goes up in October."

"Somebody did."

* * *

It was while she was working the eastern perimeter a second time, because the first time had bothered her and the second time was how you found out why, that she found the case.

It had been cached under a granite overhang, wrapped in a groundsheet, tucked with the same tidy deliberation that marked everything else. A hard-sided transit case, government grey, latches brass, the kind of box that carried instruments or documents or things that needed to survive being dropped out of a helicopter. Stenciled on the lid, in the faded block letters of a label that had been applied years ago and never removed:

**M. AMBERDALE — VELL RANGE SURVEY — PROPERTY OF THE PROVINCIAL ARCHIVE**

Cyrus stopped breathing for a second. It was a stupid, animal thing, the way the body betrays you, and she hated it.

M. Amberdale. Marguerite Amberdale. The name was on trailhead plaques and in three textbooks and on the little bronze marker in the cemetery in Millbrook that Cyrus had driven past exactly once, at night, with the windows up, eleven years ago. Her mother's initials were on half the old survey equipment in the province, because the province had never bothered to reissue any of it, because Marguerite Amberdale's name on a transit case was still worth something, still opened doors, still made bureaucrats sit up.

"Okafor." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "You want to explain this?"

He came over, and when he saw the stencil his face did something complicated. "That was in the inventory photos from the first walk-through," he said. "Listed as *miscellaneous equipment, provenance unclear.* I didn't—" He stopped. "I didn't make the connection until you told me your name yesterday."

"Whose case is it, officially?"

"Officially? The survey team's. It was on their manifest as a sample case." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Unofficially, I pulled the archive records last night after you went to bed. That case was signed out of provincial stores fourteen years ago and never returned. Signed out by M. Amberdale. It should have been sitting in a basement in the capital. Instead it's in a camp where six people vanished, and the only person who'd have a reason to have it—" He didn't finish.

Cyrus crouched and looked at the latches. They were brass, old style, and the locks had been drilled, cleanly, by someone who knew how. Inside—she worked them open with her multitool, feeling Okafor's breath on the back of her neck—there was nothing but foam padding, cut to hold something long and narrow, and the foam was empty. Whatever had lived in that case had left in someone's hands, going up the mountain.

"Her old theodolite would fit this," Cyrus said. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone else. "Or the sample cores. She kept cores from the last season in cases like this." She closed the lid. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "Why does a government survey team carry a dead woman's equipment case into the field?"

"Maybe they didn't know whose it was."

"Maybe." She stood. The wind came down the drainage and moved through the pines, and for a moment the whole forest sounded like it was whispering the same word over and over, and the word was her mother's name, and Cyrus told it to shut up. "Or maybe somebody on that team knew exactly whose it was. Maybe that was the point."

* * *

They walked back to the truck in silence, and Cyrus spent the silence losing an argument with herself.

The argument went like this. *You have a contract in the next valley. You have a truck that runs, mostly. You have a rule—ten years, one rule, never go back into the Vells—and rules are the only thing that's kept you upright. Okafor can call in search and rescue. There are other trackers. There are always other trackers.*

And the other side of the argument went like this. *Swept, not scattered. A trail erased by someone patient. A case with your mother's initials, drilled open, emptied, carried up. A route going up into the range in October, by somebody who moved like they knew the ground.*

Nobody swept a trail like that without knowing exactly what they were doing. And nobody knew this ground like that except people who had learned it from the same teacher Cyrus had. Which meant either the survey team had a guide who'd studied her mother's published maps—and fine, plenty of people had—or they had her mother's *unpublished* field notes, the ones that had never been filed, the ones that had gone into a box in a basement in Millbrook eleven years ago that Cyrus had never opened because opening it would have meant—

"Contractor rates," Cyrus said.

Okafor looked over. "What?"

"You want me to track them, it's contract rates. Per diem, equipment allowance, and a hazard multiplier, because it's October and you want me to go *up*." She heard her own voice doing what it always did, wrapping the decision in paperwork so it wouldn't look like what it was. "And I want copies of everything. The manifest, the personnel files, the satellite phone logs. All of it."

"Done." Okafor's shoulders came down half an inch, the first time they'd moved since the saddle. "I won't pretend I'm not relieved."

"Don't be relieved. I'm going to look at a trail. That's all I agreed to." She looked out the window at the range. The snow line had come down another hundred feet since morning, patient as anything. "I read the ground, I tell you what happened, and then search and rescue takes it from there. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," Okafor agreed, in the voice of a man who would have agreed to a deal involving a live wolverine in his truck if it meant somebody was finally reading the ground.

Cyrus turned back to the window and let herself admit, in the privacy of her own skull, the thing she'd been not-admitting since the saddle: her pulse was up. Not fear-pulse. The other kind. The one she'd felt at fifteen, crouched over a print in the snow while her mother watched, when the whole world narrowed to a seam between two stones and she *saw* it, the whole story, the weight and the direction and the hurry, and her mother had said *there it is, Cyrus-girl, there's the whole story, nobody else can see it but you.*

A swept trail. An erased camp. A puzzle with her mother's initials on the lid.

Nobody else could read this. That was the hook in her, she knew, the oldest hook there was, and she took the bait anyway, telling herself it was for the fee, telling herself it was for Okafor's sleepless nights, telling herself anything at all except the truth, which was that the ground was talking again, and after ten years of silence it was saying her name.
