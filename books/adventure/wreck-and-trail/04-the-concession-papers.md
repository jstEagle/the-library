## 4. The Concession Papers

Cyrus had been in the county records office exactly four minutes and had already counted three ways to leave.

The front door, obviously. The window beside the microfiche cabinet, which opened—she'd checked, casually, like a woman admiring the light. And the back hall marked STAFF ONLY, which was less a door than an invitation if you were the kind of person she'd spent ten years being.

She stayed anyway. She told herself it was because Deputy Okafor had promised coffee, and the coffee in Meridian Falls was famously terrible, and there was a certain pleasure in confirming a bad reputation. She told herself it was because her truck was still at the ranger station and Okafor had driven. She told herself a lot of things before breakfast these days.

The records office smelled of toner and old paper and someone's microwaved fish, a crime that had clearly happened hours ago and would never be solved. Fluorescent lights hummed overhead. A clerk with reading glasses on a beaded chain typed with two fingers, each keystroke landing like a small verdict.

"Meridian County keeps everything," Okafor said, shrugging off his uniform jacket. He was a big man, careful-moving, with the patient voice of someone who talked to lost hikers for a living. "Flood of '09 took out some boxes, but the land records survived. They always survive. Land records are cockroaches."

"That's a comforting thing to say about documents."

"It's a comforting thing to say about most things." He led her past the clerk with a nod—"Morning, Dot"—and down an aisle of gray filing cabinets to a table under the one window with actual daylight. On it sat two boxes, a legal pad, and a thermos.

Cyrus looked at the boxes. Gray cardboard, string-tied, labeled in fading marker: **VELL RANGE — SURVEY & CONCESSION, 1998–2011**.

Her mother's dates. Not on purpose—the range didn't know it belonged to anyone—but Cyrus felt the coincidence settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well, taking its time hitting bottom.

"Start anywhere," Okafor said.

* * *

Deputy Okafor had made eleven phone calls since the survey team went quiet, and every one of them had taught him the same lesson: nobody wanted to talk about the Vell Range survey.

The Forest Service referred him to the county. The county referred him to the state lands office. The state lands office referred him to a voicemail box that was full. The consortium—Halvard Minerals, or Halvard Resources, or whatever they were calling themselves this fiscal quarter—referred him to a press line staffed by a cheerful woman who said "we're cooperating fully with authorities" in the tone of a woman reading a hostage statement.

He wasn't a suspicious man by trade. Suspicion was inefficient; you saw a lot of it in this work and it was usually wrong. But he'd stood in that last camp with Cyrus Brightwater and watched her walk the perimeter twice without saying a word, and then she'd pointed at the ground and said, "Somebody swept this."

Not scattered. Not panicked. *Swept.* Like a floor.

And then she'd crouched over that transit case with the stenciled initials—**M.A.B.**—and gone very still, in the way of people who have just heard their own name called from a long way off.

Okafor had been a deputy long enough to know that when a tracker goes still, you pay attention.

So: the records office. Dot owed him a favor from the incident with the escaped goats two summers back, and favors in Meridian County were currency that appreciated. He'd asked her to pull everything touching the upper Vell drainage, and she'd produced these two boxes with the smugness of a woman who knew exactly what she was handing him.

Now he watched Cyrus Brightwater open the first box, and understood within thirty seconds that he'd brought a fire extinguisher to a house fire.

* * *

Cyrus read fast. It was one of the few inheritances she'd accepted without argument—her mother had read the same way, pages turned like shuffling cards, eyes snagging on the one number in a column that didn't belong.

The early files were boring, which meant they were honest. Timber cruise reports. A grazing lease. A 1999 hydrology study with a coffee ring on the cover page. Then, in 2004, the tone changed.

**MINERAL EXPLORATION PERMIT — HALVARD CONSOLIDATED MINING CO.**

Ten-year term. Expiration: **upon first measurable snowfall following the tenth anniversary**, a clause so strange Cyrus read it three times. Permits expired on dates. Dates were how lawyers kept the world from eating itself. This one expired on *weather*.

"Okafor." She held up the page. "Who writes an expiration clause like this?"

"A lawyer who wants flexibility," Okafor said, leaning over her shoulder. He smelled of coffee and wool. "Or a lawyer whose client expects the snows to come late."

"The snows came late nine years running." She flipped forward. "Look. Renewal applications. 2006, denied—insufficient environmental review. 2007, denied. 2008—" She stopped.

2008 was her mother's last field season. The file thinned after that, the way a trail thins when everyone stops using it. One renewal application, 2009, stamped **PENDING — FIELD VERIFICATION REQUIRED**. And then nothing. Fourteen years of *pending*, gathering dust in a gray box in a room that smelled of fish.

"They never got their verification," Cyrus said slowly. "Because the verification was supposed to come from an independent survey. And every independent survey they tried to buy—"

"—came back saying the ground shouldn't be mined," Okafor finished. He pulled a folder from the second box. "Including this one."

It was a survey report, 2009, authored by **Dr. M. A. Brightwater, Lead Field Archaeologist / Cultural Resources**. Cyrus's hands went cold around the paper.

She knew this report. Not its contents—she'd refused to read anything her mother published after the funeral she hadn't attended—but she knew the *shape* of it. The dense, unshowy prose. The maps drawn by hand, contour lines confident as handwriting. Her mother had believed a map should look like the person who walked it.

She read it anyway. There was no leaving now; even the window seemed farther away than it had.

The report documented the upper basin below the eastern ridge of the Vells: seasonal camps, lithic scatter, a game-drive system older than any nation that currently claimed the valley, all of it sitting directly atop the ore body Halvard's geologists had flagged. Her mother's conclusion occupied one paragraph and read like a door closing:

*"The cultural deposits of the upper Vell basin are extensive, intact, and irreplaceable. No mitigation strategy can preserve them in situ under open-pit development. Recommendation: permanent protective designation."*

Permanent. The opposite of pending.

"They used her report," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew she was angry; anger flattened her like a roller. "They attached it to the permit application as supporting documentation, and then they spent fourteen years making sure no follow-up survey ever happened. Because every real survey ends the same way hers did."

"And now their permits are about to lapse," Okafor said. "First snow. Which is why a survey team went up there in October with a schedule that made no sense and a route that made less."

Cyrus looked at him.

"You've read the route plan?"

"Dot found a copy filed with the county's special-use permits." He laid it on the table between them, next to her mother's report, and Cyrus felt something turn over in her chest—not quite recognition, more like a key testing a lock it remembered.

The survey team's planned route climbed the east drainage. It passed through the basin. Their stated objective, in bureaucratese so thick it was nearly a disguise: *"cultural resources reconnaissance in support of permit renewal determination."*

In support of renewal. A team sent to produce the paperwork that would finally let the consortium dig up the ground her mother had died trying to protect—or die trying to, depending on which version of the story Cyrus allowed herself on a given night.

"They weren't lost," Cyrus said. "They were *sent*. And then somebody swept their camp so clean that even I almost missed it, and now they're overdue, and the county is going to look very diligent if a search happens, and very tidy if it doesn't."

Okafor was quiet for a moment. Outside the window, a pickup went by hauling a boat, though the lake had been frozen for a week. Small towns.

"I don't like where your sentence was going," he said.

"Nobody does. That's why they hire trackers instead of detectives."

* * *

Deputy Okafor had grown up in a house where his mother settled disputes by saying, "We can be right or we can be family, choose fast." He recognized the dynamic immediately: Cyrus Brightwater was deciding whether this was her fight, and every part of her was voting no.

He couldn't blame her. That was the ugly arithmetic of it. The consortium had spent fourteen years and, presumably, a great deal of money arranging matters so that whatever happened in the upper Vells would happen to other people. The vanished team was other people. The swept camp was other people's mess. Even the transit case with the dead woman's initials was, in a sense, other people using Cyrus Brightwater's grief as free equipment storage.

But he'd watched her read her mother's report. He'd seen her thumb rest, without her seeming to notice, on the hand-drawn contour lines, the way you'd touch a scar to check it was still yours.

"Cyrus," he said. "I'm not going to pretend I can order you to do anything. You're a contractor. Your contract is culverts."

"My contract is culverts," she agreed.

"But I am going to tell you what I see, and you can do what you want with it." He ticked points off on thick fingers. "One: six people are missing, and the official search is waiting on weather and paperwork, and we may not have either. Two: the camp was erased by someone who knew tracking well enough to beat a casual look but not well enough to beat you. Three: the only person in five hundred miles with a professional reason to care about that specific basin is standing in front of me smelling faintly of culvert sealant."

"That's slander. I smell of woodsmoke and poor decisions."

"Four," Okafor said, "the case with your mother's initials on it was in that camp. Somebody on that team packed government property stenciled M-A-B, which means somebody accessed her archived field materials. Which means somebody has been in her files. All of them, maybe. Including whatever she was working on when she died."

Cyrus went still again. Second time in two days. He was starting to build a little catalog of her stillnesses.

"She had unfinished work," Cyrus said at last. "Everyone said so. The season ended early—she got sick fast—and the final report was never filed. The basin designation never went through. It just... stopped. Like a sentence with no period."

"And now a team carrying her equipment is missing somewhere along her old route, right before the permits that her unfinished work would have killed."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere in the building, Dot's typing resumed, each keystroke a small verdict.

"I'm not asking you to care about the mountain," Okafor said quietly. "I'm asking you to consider that somebody took your mother's name in a stencil kit up into country you know better than anyone alive, and now six people are unaccounted for, and the ground out there is the only witness left. And you're the only one who can take its statement."

Cyrus looked at the window. He watched her measure it—front door, window, staff-only hall, the whole escape architecture of a woman who'd been leaving places for a decade. He watched her decide, visibly, painfully, not to use any of them.

"For the fee," she said. "I want that on record. Daily rate, per diem, and hazard pay, because I read the weather forecast and it's insulting."

"On record," Okafor said, and wrote it on the legal pad with the gravity of a man signing a treaty.

* * *

Cyrus drove back toward the station with Okafor's thermos between her knees and her mother's report open on her lap, because apparently she was the kind of person who read and rode now, who had become, without noticing the transition, someone who stayed in rooms until the rooms were finished with her.

The anger hadn't faded. It had refined. That was the trouble with anger—it started as a bonfire and ended as a blade, and blades were useful, which meant you kept them.

Fourteen years of *pending*. Fourteen years of lawyers keeping a dead woman's recommendation in a drawer, waiting for the snows to come late enough, one more time, so the clause could expire and the ground could be theirs. And when the snows threatened to arrive on schedule, they'd sent up a team to manufacture the one document the drawer lacked—and the team had vanished, and the camp had been swept, and her mother's initials had traveled up the drainage like a flag planted in territory someone intended to take.

They'd handed her someone else's war. That was the part that burned. Not the danger—danger was just weather with intentions. But the presumption of it. Some man in an office she'd never see had decided that the Brightwater name was a tool, the way you'd pick up a pry bar, without wondering what hands had held it last or what it had cost those hands.

The truck crested the pass and the Vells came up out of the haze, white-capped already, indifferent, gorgeous. Her mother's mountains. Everyone said it that way, at the diner, at the gas station—*your mother's mountains*—as if terrain could be willed to a daughter, as if Cyrus had signed something.

She pulled over at the overlook, engine idling, and looked at the east drainage, where the survey route bent up toward the basin. Toward the sentence with no period.

Her mother had stood guard up there. That was the thing the report made undeniable, the thing Cyrus couldn't unread: not glory, not fame, not the legend the magazines had built and Cyrus had fled. Just a woman with cold hands writing *irreplaceable* in careful letters, knowing it would cost her, staying anyway.

Cyrus put the truck in gear.

The snowline was high still, but it was coming down the ridges like a tide going out in reverse, and somewhere above it were six people, a swept camp, and whatever her mother had left unfinished.

She'd track them. For the fee. For the daily rate and the per diem and the hazard pay.

She was already lying to herself, and she noticed, and she drove on anyway.
