## 5. Cold Camp

The light went out of the valley the way it always did up here—not fading so much as being taken, all at once, like a coin snatched from a table. Cyrus stopped where the trail bent around a granite shoulder and gave herself ten seconds to feel sorry about it.

Then she got to work.

Camp was a discipline, not a decision. Her mother had drilled that into her before she could spell *stratigraphy*: you didn't camp where you wanted to, you camped where the ground told you to. Wind direction. Drainage. The angle of the slope above you and what it might decide to do in the night. She walked the bench twice, found a shelf of flat stone tucked behind a windfall spruce, and started unpacking with the economy of someone who had done this alone so many times that company would have slowed her down.

Tent. Sleeping bag, unrolled but not yet climbed into. Stove hissing on a flat rock while she melted snow for water—snow she'd gathered from the lee side of a drift, because wind-packed snow carried less grit, and grit in your water meant grit in your teeth, and her mother's voice in her head saying *you can taste a lazy camper, Cyrus*.

She'd been hearing that voice all day.

That was the trouble with this route. She'd known it would be. Every mile of it was a sentence her mother had written first.

The stove flame went blue and steady, and Cyrus sat on her heels and let herself eat—jerky, hard cheese, the last good apple from Okafor's desk drawer, which the deputy had pressed on her at the trailhead with the solemnity of a man handing over state secrets. "For morale," he'd said. "Yours, specifically. You looked like a woman who hadn't eaten an apple in years."

She bit into it now, cold and sharp, and watched the dark come down the far wall of the range.

Below her, somewhere past two ridgelines, the survey team's erased trail waited for morning. Above her, the switchback through the slide chute—the one that shouldn't have existed, the one that lived only in a dead woman's field notes—waited too. And between them, in the thin cold air, waited everything else: the fight, the funeral, the ten years of putting distance between herself and both.

Cyrus finished the apple and set the core on a rock for the jays, because her mother had also taught her that, and she'd never managed to unlearn any of it. That was the joke of it, really. She'd spent a decade running from Marion Brightwater's legacy, and she couldn't even leave an apple core without hearing the woman's approval.

*Leave the ground better than the ground expected.*

She banked the stove, crawled into the tent, and lay in the dark listening to the mountain tick and settle around her.

Sleep came the way it did on the first night out—thinly, reluctantly, in shallow pieces—and in the gaps between the pieces, the past came through.

---

She was nine years old, and the scree field below Kestrel Pass was singing.

That was what her mother called it. "Listen," Marion said, crouching, one gloved hand flat on the slope of loose stone. "It's talking to us."

"It sounds like water," Cyrus said.

"It sounds like a lie." Her mother's eyes were bright, sun-wrinkled at the corners, absolutely delighted. "Water runs downhill in a line. Scree talks when something moves it. So we ask it questions. What moved? When? How heavy?" She picked up a flat shard of shale and held it out. "Your turn. Walk it. Tell me what it says."

Cyrus had walked it, small boots crunching, terrified of doing it wrong. Halfway across, the slope under her feet shifted—a soft slithering sigh, stones resettling—and she froze.

"Good," her mother said behind her. "Now tell me."

"Um. It moved because I'm heavy?"

"And?"

"And... it moved easy? Like it does this a lot?" She frowned, feeling for it. "Like it's already loose. Something big came through here recently and woke it all up."

Her mother laughed—that real laugh, rare even then, the one that made the whole mountain feel briefly like a warm room. "Ten years old and reading scree better than half my graduate students. Come on, monster. Let's go see who woke it up."

They'd found the elk herd an hour later, forty animals moving through the pass like weather. Cyrus remembered standing against her mother's side, watching them, feeling like the two of them were the only people in the world who knew how to look.

She hadn't felt that way since. She'd made very sure of it.

---

Cyrus woke once, deep in the night, heart going, hand already on the knife before her mind caught up. Wind in the spruce. Just wind. She lay back down and stared at the roof of the tent, and the second memory arrived without asking permission, the way the bad ones always did.

It was raining in the foothills the day they fought. Not mountain rain—foothill rain, gray and apologetic, the kind that made everything smell like wet dust. She was twenty-six. She'd come back from three seasons guiding in the northern ranges, tanned and capable and full of words she'd been rehearsing for months.

"You're coming back to finish the Vell survey," her mother said. Not asked. Said, the way she said *the sun will rise*.

"No, I'm not."

"The basin needs another season. One season. I'm not as young as I was, and there's no one else I trust to—"

"There's no one else at all, Mom. That's the problem. There's never anyone else. It's just you and the mountain and me, and I am done being the junior partner in your obsession."

Marion Brightwater stood very still in the doorway of the cabin, rain dripping off the eaves behind her, and Cyrus watched the hurt land and watched her mother do what she always did with hurt, which was convert it into certainty at speed.

"It's not an obsession. It's evidence. What's in that basin matters—what's *under* it matters—and if I don't document it properly, some consortium with a lawyer and a helicopter is going to—"

"Then document it! Alone! You're better at alone than anyone I've ever met!"

The silence after that was the loudest thing Cyrus had ever heard.

Her mother's face did something complicated and then went simple, and simple was worse. "All right," she said quietly. "Go on, then. You've clearly had practice."

"I didn't mean—"

"You did. And you're right, which is the insulting part." Marion turned away, started stacking papers on the table that didn't need stacking. "Go be free of me, Cyrus. Send a postcard sometime."

And Cyrus—twenty-six, furious, certain she was right in the way only the young and the running are ever certain—had gone.

She'd meant to go for a season. Then two. Then it was easier not to call than to explain why she hadn't called, and then it was easier not to write than to admit the postcard idea had died somewhere in a bus station, and then it was four years, and then it was a phone message from a stranger named Okafor saying *I'm sorry, Ms. Brightwater, your mother didn't make it down*, and then it was a funeral notice in a newspaper she read in a laundromat in another state, sitting with wet clothes in her lap, unable to move.

She hadn't gone to the funeral.

Everyone understood. That was the worst part. Okafor, gentle and careful, had said *of course, no pressure, everyone grieves differently*, and every person who said it had been kind, and every kindness had been a door closing on a room she could never enter again. There was no version of showing up late that wasn't worse than not showing up. So she hadn't shown up. She'd sent nothing. She'd taken a contract in the coastal ranges and worked until her hands bled and told herself that was grief enough, that was penance enough, that distance was its own kind of mourning.

Ten years of that. Ten years of one foot out the door, of leases by the month, of learning towns just well enough to leave them.

And now here she was, lying in a tent on her mother's own route, following her mother's own notes, chasing strangers who had stolen her mother's stolen map, and the grief—which she had outrun across four ranges and a decade—had apparently kept pace the whole time and simply chosen tonight to catch up.

Cyrus lay in the dark and let it.

That was the thing nobody told you about grief. You could outrun it for years if you were fast and mean about it. But it didn't get tired. It just walked, patient as winter, and eventually you camped, and it sat down across the fire from you, and there was nowhere left to be rude to it.

"I know," she said out loud, to the tent roof, to the mountain, to whoever was listening. "I know I should have gone."

The wind said nothing. Mountains were terrible conversationalists. Her mother had loved that about them.

---

Dawn came gray and honest, and Cyrus was up before it finished arriving, hands working on their own—stove, water, oatmeal with the raisins she always picked out and ate first anyway. Frost furred the outside of the tent. Her breath hung in the still air like a small ghost that had followed her up the trail and wanted to be paid.

She checked the sky while she ate. High cloud moving in from the northwest, thin but organized. Not today's problem. Maybe tomorrow's. She logged it the way her mother had taught her—*weather is a rumor until it has a wind behind it*—and broke camp with the same economy as the night before, leaving the ground exactly as she'd found it plus one apple core's worth of improvement.

The erased trail began at the base of the talus below the bench, and morning light was kind to it. Too kind. In raking light, the sweeping showed itself plainly—long arcs of disturbed fines, stone dragged in broad deliberate strokes, a broom-work of somebody with time and nerve. They'd spent hours here. Hours, on a mountainside, making sure no one could follow.

Except someone who'd learned to read ground from the woman who wrote the book on it. Literally. There was a textbook. Cyrus had grown up inside its acknowledgments.

She knelt and put her hand where the sweep ended, where the erasers had gotten confident or tired or both, and found what they'd missed: a single boot print pressed into the shadowed underside of a tilted slab, protected from their broom and from the weather. Deep heel. Heavy pack. Someone carrying more than camping weight.

And beside it, half-crushed, catching the early light—

A scrap of paper. Torn, weather-spotted, but legible. A corner of a page torn from a bound notebook, dense with her mother's unmistakable handwriting, and along the margin, in a different hand entirely, cramped and hurried:

*route confirmed — chute passable if dry — push for the basin before permits lapse*

Cyrus sat back on her heels.

The basin. Her mother's basin. The unfinished dig, the site at the end of every road this trail had, the place her mother had died trying to reach one more season.

Whoever had erased this trail wasn't running from searchers.

They were running *to* it. And they were using her mother's map to get there.

Cyrus folded the scrap into her breast pocket, against her heartbeat, next to the grief that had finally caught up—and started climbing.
