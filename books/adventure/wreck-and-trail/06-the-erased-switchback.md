## 6. The Erased Switchback

The morning came up gray and thin, the kind of light that made the whole Vell Range look like a pencil sketch someone had abandoned halfway through. Cyrus broke camp with the economy of long practice—stove packed before the water finished cooling, tent down in four minutes flat—and stood a moment at the tree line, chewing the last of her oatmeal and studying the slope above.

The trail she'd been following since dawn did something wrong about two hundred yards up.

She'd felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a stair that isn't there. For three days the erased trail had been consistent: swept clean at intervals, cairns toppled, blazes on the trees scored over with a knife in quick, efficient strokes. Whoever had done it was methodical and unhurried, and Cyrus had developed a grudging professional respect for them, the way you might respect a burglar who wiped their feet on the way out.

But here, where the drainage bent east toward the high country, the erasure stopped making sense. The natural route—the one any competent party would take, the one her mother's published maps showed—climbed the open shoulder to the west. Gentle grades, good footing, water along the way. The survey team should have gone west.

Instead, the faint sign she was reading—a scuffed stone here, a thread of trampled moss there, the particular way pine needles lie when they've been disturbed and then raked back—bent *east*. Toward the cliff bands. Toward the slide chutes.

"Nobody goes east," Cyrus told the mountain conversationally. She talked to terrain sometimes; it was either that or talk to herself, and the terrain was better company. "East is where mountains keep their teeth."

She went east.

* * *

It took her an hour to find it, and when she found it, she sat down on a rock and laughed—one short, unhappy bark of a laugh that startled a ptarmigan into flapping off through the krummholz.

There, half-hidden behind a curtain of alder, was a switchback. Not on any map. A cut trail, old but maintained once upon a time, zigging up through what looked from below like unbroken cliff. It threaded between two rock walls into the mouth of a slide chute—a steep gully of loose stone funneling down from the heights, the kind of feature that was passable in dry August and lethal in anything else.

And the chute had been swept. Recently. Someone had come down this gully—or gone up it—and then taken the trouble to erase their own passage, kicking fresh rockfall over their tracks, scattering the surface so it read as ordinary seasonal slough.

Cyrus knew this switchback.

Not because she'd ever been here. Because she'd *read* about it. She could see the page in her mind's eye: her mother's cramped field hand, the marginal sketch, the note in the corner—*unmapped route via chute, saves 4 hrs, DO NOT use after rain or before June thaw — M.A.B.* Margery Anne Brightwater, initialing everything like she expected her notes to be cited posthumously, which, knowing her mother, she probably had.

Cyrus hadn't looked at those field notes in ten years. Hadn't let herself. But they lived in her head anyway, all of them, every page she'd ever sneaked as a kid, every dinner-table recitation her mother had delivered with her hands shaping the mountains in the air. You didn't get raised by Margery Brightwater and come out without the whole archive installed whether you wanted it or not.

"So," Cyrus said to the chute. "Somebody up here has my mother's notebooks."

The thought landed in her stomach like a dropped piton.

Because think about who that meant. The government survey team had vanished—but the erasure wasn't government work. Government work left paperwork; this left nothing. This was somebody moving quiet and covering themselves, and moving by a route that existed nowhere except in a dead woman's unpublished field notes. Which meant either the survey team had acquired those notes somehow—which raised the question of how—or whoever had come through behind them, doing the sweeping, had them.

Or both. Or worse. Cyrus had spent three days telling herself a simple story: team lost, team overdue, maybe injured, maybe holed up waiting out weather, and a tracker paid by the day to go find them. Clean. Legible. The kind of job you took, finished, and walked away from.

This switchback just tore that story up and fed it to the ravens.

* * *

She climbed the chute anyway. Of course she climbed the chute. That was the whole disease of her, right there—she could stand at the bottom of a thing and know exactly how much trouble it was, and her feet would start moving regardless, some engine in her chest that only ran on unsolved problems.

Her mother used to say she had two speeds: gone and fascinated.

The chute was ugly going. Rock the size of dinner plates over rock the size of cars, everything angled and hungry for ankles. Cyrus moved the way she'd been taught—three points of contact, weight low, never trust the stone that's singing—and tried not to notice how naturally the lessons rose up, unbidden, in her mother's voice.

*Test it with your toe first, Cy. The mountain lies with its eyes but tells the truth with its sound.*

She'd been nine. They'd been somewhere in the southern Vells, and Cyrus had been showing off, taking a slope too fast, and her mother had caught her elbow—not angry, never angry about the climbing, only about the *not paying attention*—and made her do the next hundred yards slow, narrating every step. *What do you hear? What does that tell you? Good. Again.*

Ten years of running, and the woman was still teaching her. It was unbearable. It was also, Cyrus admitted, hauling herself over a chockstone the size of a bathtub, extremely useful.

Near the top of the chute she found the second thing.

A cairn. Small, knee-high, built in the old style—flat capstone, stones fitted tight, the kind of construction that said *this means something, come look closer*. Not a trail marker. Trail markers were casual piles. This was deliberate.

And it had been knocked over. Deliberately too—the capstone set aside rather than thrown, the rest scattered in a neat fan, the eraser's fingerprints all over it. But whoever knocked it down hadn't known what was under the capstone.

Cyrus lifted it.

Scratched into the underside, in a hurried hand that dug deep enough to last: **6 UP CHUTE — V.**

V. She turned the stone over twice, as if it might explain itself. Vess—that was the name Okafor had given her off the roster, the team leader, Elias Vess. So Vess had come up the chute, marked his passage under a cairn like a man leaving himself breadcrumbs, and then—what? Knocked his own marker down on the way back? Or had someone else found it and buried it?

Either way, the arithmetic was getting worse. A missing survey team was a rescue. A survey team moving by secret routes, leaving coded marks, being followed by someone with a broom—that was something else entirely. That was a story with a villain in it, and villains, in Cyrus's experience, were best appreciated from a very great distance, ideally through several intervening mountain ranges.

She should turn around. Take the western shoulder, sweep it for sign of the actual lost team, do the job she'd been hired for, collect the fee, and be in a new county by the time anyone asked follow-up questions. That was the play. That had always been the play. One foot out the door, both hands in her pockets, nothing held tightly enough to hurt when she dropped it.

She looked up the chute instead, to where it debouched onto a bench of tundra still holding patches of last year's snow, and beyond that to the high country rising gray and gold against the sky.

She knew what was up there. Of course she did. Everyone who'd grown up on her mother's legend knew: the final season, the unfinished dig, the basin in the high Vells where Margery Brightwater had worked the summer she died, the site she'd never gotten to write up. The place Cyrus had refused to visit for a decade, refused to discuss, refused to so much as look at on maps—turning the atlas pages fast whenever the range swung into view, a superstition dressed up as indifference.

And now here was a trail, erased by careful hands, running straight toward it like an arrow someone had drawn on her life without asking.

"Nope," Cyrus said firmly, to nobody. "Absolutely not. Coincidence."

The mountain declined to comment.

* * *

She ate lunch on the bench above the chute, sitting on her pack to keep it out of the wet, and made herself think it through properly, the way her mother would have—evidence first, conclusions after, feelings last if there was room.

Fact: the survey team's last camp had been abandoned neatly, no panic, no tracks leading out by any normal route. Fact: the transit case stenciled M.A.B.—her mother's initials, government property tags, the kind of case that meant official custody of official materials. Her mother's field notes had ended up in a government archive after she died; Cyrus had signed nothing, attended nothing, learned of it secondhand years later and felt nothing she'd admit to. If a survey team had pulled those notes for reference—which, fine, plausible, they were mapping the same range—

Then the team itself had been navigating by Margery Brightwater's private routes. Up a switchback that saved four hours and cost you your margin for error. Into country her mother had flagged with warnings in three colors of ink.

And fact: someone had come behind them and swept the trail.

Unless—"unless" being the word she kept circling like a bird over carrion—someone *in* the team had swept it. Split the party. Taken the notes and gone ahead, erasing the way for anyone following.

Cyrus sat with that one for a while. It fit too well. The neat abandonment at the camp, the absence of struggle, the single set of purposes written across the ground. Teams didn't vanish in the Vells in August. Teams got *led* places.

"You're speculating," she said, and unwrapped a strip of dried elk, and chewed on it and the problem together. "You're a person on a hill making up stories. Get evidence."

Fine. Evidence. There was more ground above—two days of it, if the weather held, which the bruised look of the western sky suggested it might not. Somewhere up there was a team of six, or five, or however many were left, and somewhere up there was the answer to who held her mother's notes and why they'd wanted the trail behind them to stop existing.

Somewhere up there, in other words, was her mother's unfinished business, wearing other people's boots.

Cyrus looked at the sky again. The gray in the west had organized itself into something with intentions. She had maybe a day and a half of decent weather, less if the mountain lied to her, which it would, because it always did.

She thought about turning back. Honestly, seriously, with her whole heart, she thought about it. She even got as far as standing up and facing downhill, pack settled, boots pointed toward the easy world—toward the fee, the bus depot in Kestrel Falls, the next contract, the next county, the long gray corridor of years she'd been walking since the funeral she hadn't attended.

Then she looked at the scratched letters in her memory. *6 UP CHUTE — V.* Six people. Maybe six people, up a chute, led by a man with a code mark and a broom, into ground her mother had died trying to finish mapping.

Her mother had spent her last season standing guard over something up there. Cyrus had spent ten years pretending she didn't know that, the same way she pretended she didn't have her mother's hands or her mother's stubbornness or her mother's habit of talking to rocks.

"For the fee," she told the mountain, with dignity. "That's all this is."

The mountain, having heard this line delivered many times over many years by many Brightwaters, said nothing.

Cyrus turned uphill and started walking.

Behind her, far below, the wind moved through the alder curtain hiding the switchback, and if you'd been standing there—nobody was—you might have noticed what she hadn't: a second set of older sign, days cold, climbing the chute after hers began, small-booted and steady, heading up into the high country ahead of her.

Someone else was already on the trail. And they weren't erasing it anymore.
