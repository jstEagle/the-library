## 7. A Man in the Talus

The boulder field announced itself an hour before Cyrus reached it, the way big fields always did—a low grinding note under the wind, like the mountain clearing its throat. She'd been following the erased trail since dawn, up through the slide chute her mother's notes had described, and the whole time she'd had the feeling of walking through a room where someone had just finished tidying. Nothing out of place. That was the problem.

Then, at the edge of the talus, something was out of place.

A boot. Just a boot, wedged between two granite slabs, sole facing her, laces still tied. Nobody walked out of their boots up here. Nobody took them off to sun their feet at nine thousand feet in October.

Cyrus crouched and listened. Wind in the scree. The tick of stone cooling in the shade. And underneath it—faint, arrhythmic, human—a voice. Not calling for help. Cursing. Methodically, creatively cursing, with the patience of a man who had been doing it for a long time and had gotten quite good at it.

She picked her way toward the sound, testing each block before trusting it, and found him on the third tier of the field: a broad-shouldered man in a government-issue shell jacket, propped against a boulder the size of a kitchen table, one leg straight out in front of him at an angle legs were not meant to hold. His face was two days of stubble and one day of genuine fear worn down to something more stubborn. A survey tripod lay beside him, dismantled, its case serving as a pillow. He'd organized his gear around himself with the tidy desperation of someone keeping his hands busy so his head wouldn't start screaming.

He saw her and went very still, the way a man does when he's decided that if this is a hallucination, he's not going to jinx it by moving.

"You're real," he said finally. His voice was gravel and rust.

"Last I checked."

"You're not with Vess."

It wasn't a question, exactly, but it had the weight of one. Cyrus kept her distance, hands visible, the way you'd approach a hurt dog that had been kicked enough to have opinions about hands. "I don't know anyone named Vess."

Something in his shoulders let go, half an inch. "Then God has a sense of humor after all." He tried to shift and thought better of it, breath hissing through his teeth. "Tomas Reyes. Survey team. The overdue one, I'm guessing, since you're up here looking like you expected to find somebody."

"Cyrus Brightwater." She said it flatly, without ceremony, the way you'd hand over a business card you didn't like. "Ranger station sent me."

"Brightwater." He blinked. Blinked again. "As in *the* Brightwater? As in the woman who mapped—" He stopped himself, which Cyrus appreciated. Most people didn't stop themselves. They said the name like it opened a door they were entitled to walk through. "Huh," he said instead. "Small range."

"Smaller every year." She knelt at last, close enough to see the leg properly without touching it. The boot was swollen tight around the ankle; above it, the shin sat wrong, a smooth wrongness under the fabric that her hands recognized before her eyes did. Simple fracture, probably. Probably. "How long?"

"Two days. Maybe three. I stopped counting nights because counting nights is how you start talking to rocks, and I refuse to be the guy who talks to rocks."

"You've been talking to rocks anyway. I heard you from the ridge."

"That wasn't talking. That was *negotiating*. There's a difference." He grinned, and even starved and frozen and broken, the grin had engine in it. Then it faded, and what was underneath was older and colder. "You should know I'm not the whole team. I'm the part that's left."

Cyrus sat back on her heels. "Start there."

* * *

He told it badly at first—the way people do when they've rehearsed a story in the dark until it wore grooves—and then better, once she got him water from her pack and made him drink slow. Six of them had come up the valley behind the official survey: Reyes, Vess the team lead, two techs, a cook who doubled as radio operator, and a guide hired out of the eastern valleys, a quiet man named Aldous Parr whom nobody had interviewed properly because the permit paperwork said he came recommended.

"The route was wrong from the start," Reyes said. "Vess took us off the mapped trail on day two. Said there was a shortcut through the chute." He nodded upward, toward the slide path Cyrus had climbed that morning. "Nobody questions the man holding the contract. But I'd been over those maps—I mean the *real* maps, the old ones—and that chute isn't a shortcut. It's a coffin with good views."

"So you said no."

"I said no." He looked at his leg as if it were a colleague who'd betrayed him. "Not heroically. I want that on the record. I said no because my knee's been bad since '19 and I know what scree does to bad knees, and Vess looked at me like I'd spat in the flag. We argued. Loud. In front of everyone, which I regret, because it split the group right down the middle. The two techs went with him—they were young and wanted to keep their jobs. Cook stayed neutral, then stayed with the majority, which is what cooks do. Parr just watched. Never said a word, that man. Just watched everybody like he was taking inventory."

"And then?"

"And then that night the slope moved." Reyes's voice flattened out, going careful, the way voices do around the worst part. "Not a slide. A *shift*. Half the chute let go below us and took the cook down with it. We got the techs back up but the camp was gone—tents, most of the food, the radio antenna snapped like celery. Vess stood there in the rubble and said the smart move was forward. Said we were closer to the high basin than to anywhere else, and the window was closing, and we'd come all this way." He laughed, short and ugly. "Closer to the high basin. Like it was a destination anybody sane would pick in October."

"But you didn't go forward."

"I couldn't go forward. I'd gone down into the talus to check on the cook's gear—somebody had to mark where it went in—and the slope shifted again and dropped me in here. Leg first." He tapped the ruined boot. "So understand me clearly, Ms. Brightwater: I didn't refuse to follow Vess. I refused, and *then* the mountain refused for me. I'm alive because I was too stubborn and too broken to be worth carrying."

Cyrus heard what he wasn't saying, the way the ground taught you to hear what it wasn't saying. "And the others who could walk?"

Reyes looked away, up the valley, toward the high country. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and level, stripped of the humor, and somehow that was worse than shouting.

"Vess took the notes and went ahead with Parr. Told the techs to come or get written up. They went." He swallowed. "That was four days ago. No word since. And here's the thing that keeps me up when I manage to sleep, which is not often, lying on granite with a leg full of weather." He turned back to her, and his eyes were sharp and frightened and honest. "Surveys don't need to reach the high basin. Our permits cover the lower valley. I ran the numbers myself. Whatever Vess is doing up there, it isn't the job we were paid for. And whatever's in those notes he's carrying—" he hesitated, "—he wouldn't tell us what they were. Kept them wrapped in oilcloth, slept with them under his head. I saw the corner of a page once. Handwritten. Old handwriting. Not government paper."

Cyrus felt the cold arrive in her chest before her mind caught up. Handwritten. Old. Carried up a deliberately erased trail, through a chute only one set of field notes in the world described.

Her mother's handwriting.

"You know something," Reyes said, watching her face. "Your face just did a thing."

"My face does lots of things."

"Yeah, well, that one looked expensive." He shifted, winced, and pressed on, gentler now. "Whoever wrote those notes, Vess got them from somewhere. And whoever they belong to, I'd bet my other leg they're dead, because men like Vess only carry the dead people's work when the dead can't object."

The wind came down the chute and pushed between them, and Cyrus looked up the valley—at the white shoulders of the range, at the basin hidden beyond them that she had spent ten years not thinking about—and felt the strange double pull she'd been feeling all week: the trail wanting her to turn downhill, toward warmth and paperwork and never having known; and the ground itself, patient as her mother had been patient, wanting her to keep reading.

"Here's where we are," she said, and heard her own voice go practical, tracker-flat, because practical was a place to stand. "I can't carry you out. Not alone, not over this talus, not fast enough to matter. But I can splint that leg, leave you shelter and supplies, and mark your position for the rangers. They'll have you out in two days."

"Or," Reyes said.

"There's no or."

"There's always an or. There's an or in your jaw right now, working its way loose." He grimaced—not at the leg, she noticed, but at her. "You're going up there. After him. After the notes." He studied her with the frank appraisal of a man who had spent weeks watching six strangers decide things. "I've got nothing to offer you. No money, no leverage, no reason you should risk your neck for a stranger with a busted shin. But I'll tell you this for free: whatever Vess is doing, he's scared. Scared men in mountains make choices that kill people. You already knew that, though. I can see it in how you're sitting."

"How am I sitting?"

"Like somebody who's counted the cost before." He held out his hand—steady, surprisingly, for a man two days into dying politely. "So. Splint me, cache me, mark me for rescue. And if you happen to catch up to Vess—" his grip, when she took it, was dry and strong, "—tell him Tomas Reyes says the mountain keeps books too."

* * *

The splint took twenty minutes and cost them both some dignity. Cyrus cut his boot open rather than try to pull it, padded the shin with her spare fleece, and bound it to the tripod's collapsed leg with tape and cordage, checking the pulse past the break twice until Reyes told her, with strained patience, that her bedside manner had a lot in common with a tax audit.

"I've carried people out of worse," she said.

"Sure. But you've never *left* one before. It's different. Leaving's harder." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on her hands, and Cyrus realized he'd read her the way she read ground—quickly, accurately, without permission. "Go on," he said. "Ask. You've been circling it since you found me."

"Ask what?"

"Why a surveyor who plays by the rules got left in a rock pile, and why you're about to chase the man who did it into weather that kills professionals." He leaned his head back against the boulder and closed his eyes, and for a moment he looked ten years older and very tired. "My daughter thinks I'm at a conference in Denver. If I don't make it out, she gets a phone call and a box of my effects, and somewhere in that box is a postcard I never mailed her from the last job, because I kept meaning to write something worth mailing." He opened his eyes. "I'm telling you this so you understand I'm not brave. I'm just out of alternatives, same as you probably are. So cache me your extra rope and your stove, mark my spot big, and go do whatever it is you're going to do. And Ms. Brightwater—"

"Yes."

"When you find whose handwriting those notes are in—" he said it carefully, kindly, the way you hand someone a heavy thing handle-first, "—don't decide what it means before you're standing where they stood. Ground lies less than people, but only if you ask it the right questions."

Cyrus went still. "Where did you hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"That phrase. Ask the ground the right questions."

Reyes frowned, working back through exhaustion. "Parr. The guide. First night out, sitting at the fire, he said it—word for word, like it was a rule he'd learned somewhere. I remember because it sounded like something a professor says, not a hired gun." He paused. "Why? Is it important?"

Above them the wind changed direction, coming down off the high basin now, carrying the mineral smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but had already made up its mind. Cyrus looked up the valley, at the route her mother had drawn and a stranger had followed, and understood that the man waiting for her up there had been carrying more than notes.

"He quoted my mother," she said quietly. "Word for word."
