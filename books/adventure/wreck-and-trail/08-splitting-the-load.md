## 8. Splitting the Load

### Cyrus

The litter took forty minutes to build and cost Cyrus two of her three good cordage loops, a ski pole she'd have liked to keep, and most of her patience.

"Is it supposed to look like that?" Tomas asked from where he lay propped against a boulder, his broken leg splinted with a tent stake and a rolled puffy jacket, his face the color of old candle wax.

"It's supposed to hold you," Cyrus said, testing the crossbar lashing with her boot. "Whether it looks like anything is between you and God."

"That's not reassuring."

"Nope." She knelt and checked the knots again anyway, third time, because a knot that fails at 9,000 feet in October isn't a knot problem, it's a body problem. Her mother's voice was in her head doing the arithmetic—*load weight, slope angle, anchor points, and how many hours of daylight, Cyrus, always count the daylight first*—and Cyrus let it run, because for once the voice was useful instead of just loud.

Tomas Reyes weighed maybe 170 pounds soaked, and he was going to be soaked if the clouds kept doing what they were doing. The litter frame was two cut poles and the tarp from her bivy kit, rigged as a drag-sled rather than carried: on this terrain, with one person hauling, carrying was a fantasy. Dragging meant friction, friction meant slow, slow meant—

She counted the daylight. Four hours, maybe five under the cloud deck.

"We're not making the station tonight," she said.

"I figured." Tomas had stopped arguing an hour ago, which she suspected was less about agreement and more about the fact that every time he moved his leg the world went white at the edges. "There's a bench of trees about—" he squinted up the valley, professional even through the pain, "—two miles down? South-facing, some cover. I mapped it yesterday morning before all this." He gestured vaguely at his leg, the talus field, the entire catastrophe.

"You mapped it."

"I'm a surveyor. I map things. It's the whole personality."

Despite herself, something in Cyrus's chest unclenched half a degree. She'd found him at midday wedged in the boulders like a coin in a couch, alive because he'd done the one thing nobody else on that team apparently did: he'd disobeyed. Vess said follow; Tomas looked at the slide chute, looked at the fresh fracture lines in the slope above it, and said no. The mountain had then spent the afternoon demonstrating he was right, taking the other four somewhere no one had come back from yet.

She got the litter beside him and they went through the ugly business of moving him into it, which involved more swearing than technique and ended with him gray-faced and shaking but aboard. She took the haul line over her shoulder, leaned into it, and the sled came free of the scree with a grinding whisper.

Two miles. At their speed, four hours. They'd make the tree bench at last light or not at all.

"So," Tomas said, after twenty minutes of nothing but breath and rock-grind, "are we calling it in?"

"The radio's dead. I told you."

"Not the radio. You. When we get to shelter, signal coverage, whatever—you're going to want to call the rangers, right? Get a helicopter, get a rescue team, go home, let professionals do the rest."

Cyrus hauled another yard of line. "That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether the professionals would find them." She didn't look back at him. "Your team didn't vanish, Reyes. They were erased. Swept trail, rerouted switchback, someone who knew my mother's field notes well enough to move eleven people off every documented route in this range. A search party flies in, walks the marked trails, finds nothing, files a report saying *no survivors located*, and the snow closes the range until June. Whoever did this knows exactly how that report reads. They're counting on it."

"And you think you can find them before the snow does."

"I think somebody has to try."

Tomas was quiet for a while. Then: "Vess will be past the ford by now. Maybe past the second one. He had the guide and he had two days' head start and he wasn't dragging a sled."

"Then I'd better stop wasting breath answering questions."

But the question stayed with her, the way questions do when they've hooked into something true. Every hour she spent hauling Tomas was an hour Vess gained. Every hour she abandoned Tomas was a man who died alone in a tree bench with a radio that might reach a repeater and might not, betting his life on her guilt being stronger than her arithmetic.

Her mother had died doing arithmetic like that. Everyone said so—*she pushed too far, she wouldn't turn back*—and Cyrus had built ten years of running on top of that sentence without once asking whether it was even accurate. Whether *turning back* had ever really been on the table for Marguerite Brightwater, or whether the story was just easier for everyone left behind.

The sled ground on. The light went long and coppery and then went out altogether into flat gray, and the first spits of sleet came ticking against the tarp over Tomas's legs.

"There," he said, pointing with his chin. "Trees."

A dark ragged line below them, south-facing, dense enough to promise firewood and windbreak. Cyrus adjusted her angle of pull and tried not to think about how much the next hour was going to cost her shoulders, because the answer was *everything*, and knowing wouldn't change the price.

### Tomas

Tomas Reyes had spent nine years learning to be comfortable being uncomfortable—cold camps, bad food, bosses who thought *field season* was Latin for *suffer competitively*—and none of it had prepared him for lying in a tarp sled watching a stranger drag him across a mountain while she pretended not to be exhausted.

She was exhausted. He could read it in her gait, the way her left step had gone half an inch shorter than her right over the last mile. He'd spent nine years reading terrain; people weren't so different. Slopes failed along their weakest seam.

"You should rest," he said.

"I'll rest when we're under the trees."

"You should rest *before* your legs stop negotiating with you. That's the whole trick. My dad was a long-haul trucker. He used to say the road doesn't care how tough you are, it only cares whether you planned your stops."

"Your dad sounds wise."

"He also died of a heart attack in a Denny's parking lot, so, mixed record."

That got a sound out of her—not quite a laugh, more like a laugh that had been locked in a drawer for years and opened with difficulty—but a sound. Progress. Tomas catalogued it and filed it under *assets*, because right now his life consisted entirely of what this woman decided to do with him, and he intended to be worth keeping.

The trees, when they reached them, were subalpine fir bent into wind-combed shapes, thick-trunked, close-packed. She dragged him into the lee of the biggest one and then, finally, mercifully, sat down. For a full minute she didn't move at all, just breathed, hands on knees, steam rising off her shoulders.

"Okay," she said eventually. "Fire, shelter, water, food. In that order."

"Shelter first. You're wetter than I am."

"I know what order it goes in."

"Clearly I struck a nerve."

"You're a man in a sled, Reyes. Pick your battles."

He picked them. While she got a fire going with a competence that bordered on show-off—he clocked the feather sticks, the way she built the flame small and fed it patient instead of smothering it big—he worked the zipper of his pack one-handed and got out the thing he'd been turning over for two miles: the folded page from his survey book, the one with the numbers on it that weren't numbers a government survey produces.

"Hey. Brightwater."

"Cyrus."

"Cyrus." He held the page out. "You should see this before you decide anything else."

She took it, wiped sleet off her face with her sleeve, and read by firelight. He watched her expression do something complicated—the kind of complicated he'd seen on landowners' faces when a survey line came back through their living room.

"These are mineral assay projections," she said slowly. "Concentration estimates. On the Kestrel Basin."

"Kestrel Basin is protected study ground. Has been since—" He stopped. "Since your mother proved it out. Yeah. I know whose name is on the designation. Everybody in the office knows."

"Then why does a government survey team have private assay data showing extractable concentrations that would justify a permit exception?"

"Because we weren't a government survey team." Tomas shifted against the log, and his leg sent a hot wire of pain up through him that he talked through, low, because she needed to hear the rest and he needed to say it. "Officially we're a Bureau seasonal assessment crew. Unofficially, Vess has been on retainer since spring with Halvard Minerals. Not a bribe, nothing so crude. A consulting contract. Legitimate-looking. Payable to a shell LLC. I only found out because I did the payroll reconciliation in August—I used to do side work for our admin, and the numbers didn't reconcile, and I'm the kind of boring man who cannot sleep until numbers reconcile."

"And the survey itself?"

"A fiction with a budget line. We were never mapping trails. We were mapping *claims*. Establishing that the basin's resource profile justified emergency permit renewal before the consortium's existing claims lapse at first snow. If we 'confirmed' high-value deposits inside protected ground, there's a legal mechanism—a hardship exemption—and Halvard's lawyers had it drafted in July. All Vess had to do was walk the basin, collect samples, and file the report before the snow closed access. After that it's fait accompli. Permits renewed, protection designation challenged, and your mother's work gets unwound by a paragraph in an appendix."

Cyrus stared into the fire. The sleet had thickened around them, ticking through the branches, and the temperature was falling in a way Tomas could feel in his teeth.

"She wasn't wrong, then," Cyrus said quietly. "In the fight. Before I left. She said the consortium would come back for the basin, that the protection was paper and paper burns. I told her she was paranoid. I told her she loved the mountain more than—" He watched her jaw set. "Doesn't matter what I told her."

"It matters that she was right."

"It matters that she *knew*, and she stood guard alone for six years, and then she died out here, and the first thing that happens is they send a fake survey to dig up her grave site and sell it." Cyrus folded the page with great care, the way you handle something fragile or explosive, and put it inside her jacket. "This comes with us."

"Us," Tomas repeated. "So we're still an us. Even though every hour with me costs you Vess."

She looked at him then, straight on, firelight in her eyes, and Tomas braced for the answer he deserved, which was yes.

"Here's my arithmetic," she said. "If I chase Vess tonight, alone, in the dark, in worsening weather, I catch nothing. I lose the trail, I lose sleep, and probably I lose me. If I get you to the ranger station tomorrow—signal reaches the repeater from the ridge above the station, I checked the maps—that's a helicopter for the basin search, that's Okafor freezing the permits process with a fraud complaint, which hurts Halvard worse than anything I could do to Vess personally. And it's a stretcher team for you, so I stop owing a debt I don't like carrying." She poked the fire. "After that, I go up. Alone, fast, light. Vess has the notes, but the notes are my mother's route descriptions, and I grew up on those routes. He's reading a map. I'm reading home."

"That's not arithmetic," Tomas said. "That's a plan with feelings in it."

"Everything worth planning has feelings in it. That's how you know it's yours." She glanced at him sideways, and there it was again, the almost-laugh, dry as tinder. "Besides. You're the only witness who can testify Vess falsified the survey. Dead men make terrible affidavits. So congratulations, Reyes. Your survival is now load-bearing."

"My favorite kind of survival." He settled deeper into the sled, leg throbbing, fire warm on his face, and let himself say the thing he'd been chewing since the boulder field. "For what it's worth—when Vess ordered us into the chute, and I refused, and he looked at me like I was the problem? That look. I've seen it before. Guys who've decided the mission matters more than the people, and then need everyone else to agree so it stays true." He nodded at her. "You don't have that look. You've got the opposite one. Like people are the mission and everything else is negotiable."

"Give it time. It's a long mountain."

"Yeah," Tomas said. "I've heard that from better men than Vess."

Above them the sleet turned, very briefly, to snow—small, dry, early—and Cyrus looked up at it the way people look at a bill arriving ahead of schedule.

"That's the snowline coming down to meet us," she said. "Sleep if you can. We move at first light, and tomorrow we stop losing."

### Cyrus

Cyrus took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep, and because the fire needed feeding, and because if she lay down she was afraid of what the dark would hand her—her mother's voice, the funeral she'd skipped, the chute full of people who'd followed orders off a documented route. The night had a full inventory ready and she wasn't buying.

Instead she inventoried gear. It steadied her. Rope: one loop plus the haul line. Food: five days if she ate like a monk, three if she shared honestly, and she'd been sharing honestly. Fuel, stove, first aid, the transceiver she'd pulled from the transit case—dead, but its batteries might trade into the radio at the station. And the field notes fragment she'd taken from the case, her mother's handwriting marching across it in tight, certain columns, describing a route Cyrus had walked at fourteen with cold fingers and her mother's gloved hand pulling hers over the bad step.

*Count the daylight first,* the notes said, in the margin, in her mother's hand. *Always.*

Four hours of hauling tomorrow to the ridge above the station. Half a day to brief Okafor and get Tomas handed off. Then up. Two days to the basin if the weather held, which the weather had already announced it would not.

It was insane arithmetic. She ran it again and it came out insane both times, and both times it also came out *necessary*, which was new. For ten years her plans had all ended in leaving—next contract, next range, next name on a sign-in sheet. This plan ended in staying, in a sense: ending at the place her mother stopped, finishing what she started, walking back down with proof in her jacket. Every version of the future she'd ever drawn had an exit door in it. This one had a door she'd have to build herself, on site, in the snow.

Across the fire, Tomas made a small hurt sound in his sleep and then quieted. Snow ticked through the firs. Somewhere north and uphill, a man was crossing rivers with her mother's notes in his coat, spending them like money, getting farther away with every hour she sat here being decent.

She fed the fire and let the arithmetic stand. Some debts you pay forward. Some you pay uphill.

At gray dawn she woke him gently, repacked the sled, and put the haul line over her shoulder—and that was when she saw it, down-valley, where the drainage bent toward the ford: a thread of smoke, thin and deliberate, rising off the far bank.

Somebody was signaling. Or somebody was burning evidence.

Cyrus counted the daylight—eleven hours, maybe—and started hauling.
