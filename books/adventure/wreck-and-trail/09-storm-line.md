## 9. Storm Line

The weather turned a day and a half early, which was the mountain's way of reminding Cyrus that schedules were a courtesy it had never agreed to.

She felt it first in her teeth. That was the joke she'd never told anyone—that after ten years of high country she could read a front coming the way other people read a letter, and the first line of it always arrived as an ache in her back molars. By midmorning the ache had become a conversation, and by noon the peaks to the west had put on their gray hoods and the wind had swung around out of the north, carrying that particular mineral smell of snow being manufactured somewhere just beyond sight.

Tomas Reyes lay propped against a boulder where she'd left him, one leg splinted from ankle to knee with a ski pole and two lengths of spruce, watching the sky with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"That's not a cloud," he said. "That's a *decision*."

"It's a decision," Cyrus agreed.

They'd made good time since the talus field—good time for a litter party of two, which meant roughly the speed of continental drift. She'd rigged the litter from her rope and his pack frame and a rain fly, and they'd come down off the shoulder through the morning, stopping every twenty minutes so she could check his circulation and every forty minutes so he could insist he was fine and she could ignore him. He'd stopped insisting around the second hour. Now he watched her face instead of the trail, and she could feel him reading it the way she read ground.

"You're going to leave me here," he said. Not accusing. Just getting there ahead of her, the way surveyors do.

Cyrus crouched at the edge of the bench they'd reached—a broad shelf of meadow and krummholz below the last real tree line, with a stream cutting through it and a wall of granite on the north side that would break the wind. It was, she thought grimly, almost perfect. The mountain had gone to the trouble of building them a campsite, which felt less like luck than like a trap being set politely.

"Talk me through your reasoning," Tomas said. "I want to hear it out loud. I find people lie less out loud."

"I don't lie."

"Everybody lies. I'm a surveyor. I've watched landowners describe their own property lines." He shifted, winced, kept going. "Out loud, Brightwater."

She sat down on a stone across from him. Below them the valley dropped away in long green folds; above, the route she needed climbed into cloud that was already eating the ridgeline bite by bite. Somewhere up there was Vess, with her mother's notes in his coat, moving toward a crossing he probably couldn't make and might die trying. Somewhere behind her, two days down, was the ranger station and help.

And between those two facts was her, sitting on a rock, discovering that the arithmetic she'd been doing all morning came out wrong no matter how she arranged it.

"The storm hits tonight," she said. "Early storm, which means heavy, which means it'll sit on this range for two days minimum, maybe three. If I stay with you, we descend together when it breaks—and by then Vess is either dead or through the ford, and if he's through, the permits lapse and everything he did gets laundered clean by paperwork. If I go now, ahead of the worst of it, I might catch him at the river."

"And if you go now," Tomas said, "I'm alone on a mountainside with a broken leg, a radio, and my own excellent company."

"You have a radio?"

He patted his jacket pocket with the smugness available only to injured men. "Emergency unit. Team issue. I've been saving it because I wanted to see what you'd do first."

Cyrus stared at him. "You've had a radio this whole time."

"I've had a radio this whole time," he confirmed, "and I haven't called anyone in, because if I call it in, they send a rescue party up this drainage, and a rescue party means helicopters and ground teams and every eye in the county on this range—which is exactly what Vess wants nobody to have until the paperwork closes. Also," he added, more quietly, "because I wanted to know whether you were going to finish this. You had the look. I used to have the look. I wanted to watch someone keep theirs."

"That is the single most irresponsible use of emergency equipment I've ever heard of."

"I once watched a man radio in a false grizzly sighting to close a trail so he could poach it solo," Tomas said. "I'm comfortable with my ranking."

* * *

They argued about it for another hour, which was its own kind of intimacy, though neither of them would have called it that.

The argument wasn't really about whether she should go. They both knew she was going. The argument was about the terms—about who got to feel bad about it and how much, and whether leaving a man alone under a storm line counted as abandonment or logistics, and whether Tomas got a vote. He insisted he got a vote. She let him cast it. She ignored the result. This, too, felt familiar in a way she didn't examine, because it was exactly how decisions had been made in her mother's camp, and she had sworn off inheriting anything, including habits.

What settled it was the stream. It froze at the edges while they talked—thin white lace creeping out from the stones—and Tomas watched it happen and finally blew out a long breath.

"All right," he said. "All right. Cache me. But you're going to do it properly, because I intend to survive this out of spite, and spite requires supplies."

"Spite is the most reliable fuel there is," Cyrus said. "I've run on it for a decade."

"What happens when it runs out?"

She didn't answer that, and he had the grace not to repeat it.

So they built the cache, and they built it well, because that was the language they shared—the grammar of people who work outdoors, where care is expressed entirely through knots and redundancy. She pitched her tent in the lee of the granite wall, staked it like she was anchoring a bridge, and doubled the guy lines. She moved the whole operation twice before she was satisfied, walking the wind patterns with her face tipped up like a woman tasting soup. Tomas supervised from the litter, offering commentary.

"Left corner's loose."

"It's not loose."

"It has a *vibe* of looseness."

"It has a vibe of being tied by someone who's done this longer than you've been alive."

"I'm forty-three."

"There you go."

But she retied it anyway, and he saw her retie it, and something passed between them that neither of them mentioned: the understanding that fussing over a tent knot was how these two particular people said *I don't want to leave you*.

She gave him the food she could spare without gutting her own margin—half the freeze-dried meals, all the cheese, the bag of trail mix she'd been hoarding because it had the good almonds. She filled both his water bottles and set them inside the tent where they wouldn't freeze solid. She took the foam pad from her own pack and slid it under his splinted leg, and when he started to object she said, "You'll be lying still for two days. I'll be moving. Moving keeps you warm. Lying still doesn't. This isn't generosity, it's thermodynamics," and he shut up, because it was true, and because they both knew it wasn't only thermodynamics.

Then she dug out the radio, and the moment got heavier in her hands than a plastic box had any right to be.

"Channel six, hourly check-in schedule," she said. "If you don't raise the station by tomorrow noon, you call it in yourself, consequences be damned. Rescue comes, rescue finds you, Vess does whatever Vess does—but you don't sit up here quiet to protect my mission. Your leg outranks my mission. Say it back."

"My leg outranks your mission."

"If the tent fails, get in the litter and pull the fly over you. There's a candle stub in the side pocket—one at a time, and only if the airflow's good. Don't be brave about carbon monoxide. Bravery is for things that can tell the difference."

"Anything else?" Tomas said, and his voice had gone gentle in a way that made her want to hand him the radio and walk into the trees immediately.

"Yes," she said. "Don't die. It would ruin my report."

* * *

The hard part wasn't the packing. The hard part was the standing there afterward, with her pack lighter than it had been in years and nothing left to arrange.

Tomas leaned in the tent mouth, propped on his elbows, and looked at her with the frank attention of a man who had spent three days watching her work and had drawn conclusions he was polite enough to mostly keep.

"You know," he said, "you could just say it."

"Say what?"

"Whatever it is you're not saying. The thing making you stand there like the tent's going to file a complaint."

Cyrus checked a guy line that did not need checking. "I don't know what you mean."

"Sure." He nodded slowly. "Ten years I've worked with people who run. Government crews attract them—every season, somebody new with a duffel bag full of exits. And they all have the same tell. When it's time to leave somewhere, they get *efficient*. Everything becomes procedure. Because procedure doesn't require looking at anybody." He tapped the ground beside him. "Sit down for five minutes, Brightwater. The storm's not here yet. Five minutes isn't going to kill the trail."

"I don't have five minutes."

"You have five minutes. What you don't have is the nerve." He said it without heat, almost kindly, and that was worse. "Five minutes. Then I'll personally throw you uphill."

She sat. The meadow grass was going gold at the tips, and the stream talked to itself over the stones, and above them the cloud came down the ridge in slow gray tongues, unhurried, enormous, indifferent to both of their plans. She found that her hands had gone still in her lap, which was unusual enough that she noticed it the way you notice silence in a house.

"When I was nineteen," she heard herself say, "I left in the middle of the night. No note. My mother was—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "There was a fight. It doesn't matter what it was about. What matters is I'd gotten very good at leaving, and I treated her like one more place I was overwintering. And then she died before I could—" The sentence ran out of road. It did that a lot, whenever she tried to drive it anywhere.

Tomas waited. He was good at waiting; it was probably in the job description.

"She stocked a cache for me," Cyrus said. "Up there. Dated years after we stopped speaking. She spent money and weight allowance on a daughter who'd walked out on her, on the chance I'd someday come up this drainage needing dry socks." Her jaw tightened. "I keep thinking about the weight allowance. You don't waste grams unless—"

She stopped again. Tomas finished it for her, quietly.

"Unless it matters."

"Unless it matters." She stood, abruptly, brushing off her knees as if the conversation were dirt. "Check-in schedule. Noon deadline. Candle protocol."

"Candle protocol," Tomas agreed. Then, as she turned: "Brightwater."

She paused. The wind had picked up, flattening the grass in long silver strokes, and the first flakes came down while she stood there—not real snow yet, just scouts, a few fat uncertain flecks that melted on her sleeve.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I've watched a lot of people leave. You're the first one who left anything behind."

She didn't turn around. "It's just gear."

"It's never just gear," Tomas said. "Gear is how people like us say things."

* * *

She climbed.

The scouts became a skirmish within the hour, and the skirmish became a war by midafternoon, snow coming in sideways off the north wall in sheets that erased the switchbacks and rewrote the world in white on white. She navigated by counting—paces between landmarks, her mother's habit, the old discipline of *the map is in your legs*—and by the time the light went she was moving through a featureless gray room with the wind shouting and her mother's route descriptions running in her head like a litany: *two hundred paces past the split slab, bear right at the rib, do not trust the chute in fresh snow.*

Do not trust the chute in fresh snow. As if her mother had known, all those years ago, exactly which patch of white Cyrus would someday stand in front of.

She made camp in the lee of the rib with the last of the light, dug in fast, ate half a meal with numb fingers, and lay in the dark listening to the storm lean on the nylon and thinking about a man alone on a bench below the tree line with a radio and a candle stub and her tent around him.

That was the part she hadn't budgeted for. Not the guilt—guilt was an old traveling companion, lightweight, well-broken-in. It was the *company*. Three days of arguing knots and thermodynamics and spite-as-fuel, and now the mountain was silent except for wind, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was missing somebody. She'd spent a decade paying for solitude in advance, saving up for it, and it had never once occurred to her that she might spend it and miss it, like a traveler who hoards coin and discovers at the border that the currency's changed.

*You pay in company,* she thought, staring at the black ceiling of the tent. *That's the exchange rate. Nobody posts it.*

Sleep came eventually, thin and full of weather. She dreamed of a cache with her name on it, and woke before dawn with frost on the inside of the fabric and one clear, cold certainty sitting in her chest where the dread used to live:

Two days of storm. Two days of Vess's head start becoming Vess's lead becoming, if the permits lapsed, Vess's clean escape. Unless the storm slowed him too. Unless the ford was already rising. Unless—

She was breaking camp in the dark, rolling her lighter pack, when the thought arrived whole, the way the important ones do:

*The notes are paper.* Paper and cold and a river crossing. And a man carrying them who had already shown her, twice, what he did when survival asked him a question.

Cyrus stepped out into the blue-black snow-light, turned uphill, and began to move—faster now, because she had finally understood that she wasn't only chasing Vess.

She was racing whatever he'd do when the mountain cornered him.
