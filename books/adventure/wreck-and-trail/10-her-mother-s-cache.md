## 10. Her Mother's Cache

The storm found her an hour after she'd stopped pretending it wouldn't.

It came off the western peaks the way her mother used to describe it—*like a door opening in the sky, Cy. You'll hear it before you see it. That low hush. That's the mountain clearing its throat.* Cyrus had been eleven when she'd heard that lecture, sitting on a granite slab above the tree line with a thermos of cocoa going cold in her hands, and she had thought: *I will never need to know this, because I am never coming back here.*

And yet here she was, thirty-one years old and one hundred percent needing to know it, listening to the hush roll down the valley like something enormous drawing breath.

She'd been watching the light go wrong all afternoon—the flat pewter sheen that meant the clouds had weight now, the wind swinging north in little nervous increments—and she'd known she wasn't making the pass before dark. Not in this. The smart move was shelter, and the only shelter the map promised was a shallow overhang in the limestone band above the creek, marked in her mother's field notes with a small, dry annotation: *adequate. barely.*

Her mother's handwriting had always been like that. Economical to the point of insult.

Cyrus reached the overhang as the first flakes came down, fat and slow and deliberate, like the storm was taking its time because it knew it had already won. The cave—if you were feeling generous; a rock lip if you weren't—ran back maybe fifteen feet into the cliff, floored with old gravel and the desiccated droppings of some long-departed packrat. It was dry. It was out of the wind. It was, by the standards of the last three days, practically a hotel.

She dropped her pack, shook the snow off her shoulders, and got a fire going in the lee of the entrance with the practiced economy of someone who had built ten thousand fires alone. Small flame, dead wood, no smoke worth seeing from below. Then she sat back against her pack, ate half a brick of pemmican without tasting it, and let herself do the thing she'd been not-doing since morning: think about what Tomas had said.

*Vess took the notes and went ahead.* Ahead meant up. Up meant the basin. Up meant—

Up meant her mother's last dig site, which was where this whole rotten story had apparently been heading since the day a government transit case showed up stenciled with initials that made Cyrus's chest hurt. E.A.B. Elena Amberdale Brightwater. The woman who mapped the Vell Range. The woman who died up here with her work unfinished and her daughter three provinces away, deliberately not answering letters.

Cyrus pulled the field notes out of their oilskin wrap—not Vess's stolen originals, but her own copy, transcribed in Okafor's office from the county archive microfilm—and flipped past the route descriptions to the pages she'd been avoiding. The survey grids. The basin itself.

Her mother's notes there were different. Everywhere else in the document the handwriting marched along, brisk and bored, cataloguing elevations and talus angles like a woman filling out forms. But at the basin, the writing slowed. Got denser. Marginal annotations crowded the edges in that tiny compressed script she'd used when she actually cared about something:

*Stratigraphy inconsistent with consortium's published core samples.*
*Coal seam shown in claim map does not exist at surveyed coordinates.*
*If I'm right about this, the whole eastern concession is built on paper mountains.*

And then, at the bottom of the final page, in handwriting that had gone shaky in a way that frightened Cyrus more than anything else in the document:

*Season ending early. Will resume spring. — EAB*

That was it. That was where her mother's life in these notes simply stopped. Somewhere between that line and the spring that never came for her, the mountain had collected its debt, and Cyrus had collected a legacy she'd spent ten years trying to mail back postage due.

The fire popped. Outside, the snow thickened until the world beyond the cave mouth was just gray static.

"Fine," Cyrus said aloud, to nobody, in the tone of someone conceding an argument they'd been losing for days. "Fine."

Sleep didn't come so much as ambush her, somewhere between the pemmican and the memory of a fight she'd had at nineteen that still lived in her body like shrapnel.

* * *

She woke to full storm and full dark, and lay still for a while doing the arithmetic of it. Wind against the rock face: steady, maybe forty miles an hour, gusting harder. Snow load: building. Visibility: none. Which meant she wasn't moving today either, and the day after that was questionable, and every hour she sat in this hole in the ground, Vess was getting further ahead with notes that could bury the truth—or sell it—for good.

There was a version of her, the old version, the one who'd gotten very good at leaving, that would have found this restful. Forced stillness. Nothing to decide except whether to eat the other half of the pemmican. She'd spent a decade constructing a life out of exactly these kinds of interruptions—contracts that ended, seasons that turned, towns that ran out of reasons to keep her—and she'd told herself it was freedom.

Lying in her mother's cave, in her mother's mountains, tracking a man through her mother's unfinished work, Cyrus was starting to suspect it had never once been freedom. It had just been motion with better marketing.

She fed the fire, melted snow, drank. And then, because there was nothing else to do and because avoiding it any longer felt less like caution and more like cowardice, she took out her headlamp and started really looking at the cave.

Not the cave. The walls.

Because here was the thing about people who worked in the field for decades: they left marks. Tent-pitch scars on gravel benches. Soot ghosts near old fire rings. And in caves—her mother had taught her this when Cyrus was small enough to still be learning instead of resenting—people who cached supplies almost always chose the same kind of spot. Back wall, above flood line, out of the drip. Dry stone holds scent differently than wet, holds dust differently, holds *time* differently. You could read a cave the way you read scree.

Cyrus swept her lamp beam along the back wall, above the old flood stain, and stopped.

There. A discoloration in the dust, roughly rectangular, about the size of an ammunition crate. Faint—but faint in the wrong way. Dust accumulated evenly unless something interrupted it, and this rectangle was *too clean*, its edges too sharp, for weather alone. Something had sat here. Recently, in geological terms. Recently, in human terms too.

Her heart did something complicated.

"No," she said to the cave, reasonably. "Absolutely not. There is no version of this where my mother hid a box in a random cave and I find it during a snowstorm like the protagonist of a bad novel."

The cave declined to argue. It just held its rectangle of clean stone, patient as everything else her mother had ever done.

Cyrus crawled back there anyway. Of course she did. Her knees complained; she ignored them. The rectangle was set behind a shelf of rock that looked natural until you put your hand on it and felt the deliberate fit of it—a stone seated into a gap, wedged with smaller stones, disguised by twenty years of dust. She worked it loose with numb fingers, taking longer than pride allowed, and behind it, wrapped in waxed canvas and tied with cord that had gone stiff with age, was a metal cache box.

An army-surplus ammunition can, painted green, the latch crusted with corrosion. And stenciled on the lid in white paint, faded but unmistakable, in her mother's blocky field lettering:

**E.A.B. — FOR C.**

Cyrus sat back on her heels and did not touch it for a long moment.

The storm hissed outside. The firelight reached this far back only as a faint orange suggestion, and in that half-dark the box seemed less like an object than a sentence—one written years ago, addressed to her, held in escrow by a mountain. *For C.* For Cyrus. Not for the archive, not for the university, not for whatever colleague or lawyer would have sorted through her effects. For C.

She pried the latch open with her knife tip.

Inside, packed with the obsessive neatness that had driven Cyrus insane her entire childhood—*Mother, it doesn't matter how the tent is folded*—was a small civilization.

A candle lantern and six spare candles. A tin of matches, wax-sealed, plus a second sealed tin, because her mother had never trusted a single point of failure in her life. A folding saw. A coil of good cord. A medical kit, complete, with a note taped inside the lid listing expiration dates in that merciless little script, updated twice—*checked 2009, replaced analgesics; checked 2012, replaced analgesics*—which meant her mother had come back here, more than once, years after the season ended, years after the fight, and maintained this box the way other people maintained graves.

At the bottom, wrapped separately in a wool sock, were the things that undid her.

A pair of wool gloves, men's size large. Not her mother's size. Not her mother's style—plain, unpatched, the kind sold at the outfitter in Millbrook where Cyrus had bought her first pair at fourteen and worn them until the thumbs went. Beside them, a paperback novel with a cracked spine: *The Long Patrol*, by Anselm Reyes, the adventure serial Cyrus had devoured at sixteen, the one her mother had called "pulp" and "a waste of your considerable mind" in front of Cyrus's entire eleventh-grade study group, thereby committing what teenage Cyrus had considered an unforgivable act of war. This copy was a later printing. Bought new. Bought sometime in the last decade, by a woman in her fifties walking into a bookstore and asking for a book she'd once dismissed, because her daughter had loved it.

And under the book, folded into a square gone soft at the creases, a single sheet of paper. Not a letter. A page torn from a trail register—the old kind, the heavy stock they used at the trailhead stations—with a date from nine years ago and one line in her mother's hand:

*Cabin at the ford needs a new roof ridge. If Cy ever comes through, tell her the cocoa tin above the stove is hers. — E.A.B.*

Cyrus read it four times. It wasn't a letter. It wasn't an apology. It was a maintenance log entry that happened to contain her name, and it was the most her mother had said to her in nine years, and it wrecked her completely.

Because she understood what she was looking at. She understood it with the part of her brain that read ground and trails and the marks people leave. This box wasn't hope. Hope was cheap; anyone could hope. This box was *logistics*. Her mother—who planned routes down to the water source, who carried two of every critical item, who had once made fourteen-year-old Cyrus repack a rucksack three times because the weight distribution was "an insult to the mule"—had looked at the shape of her life, looked at the estrangement she'd helped cause and couldn't figure out how to mend, and had done the only thing her nature allowed.

She couldn't say sorry. So she'd stocked a cache. Gloves in Cyrus's size. A book Cyrus loved. Matches sealed against damp. Checked and re-checked across the years, analgesics rotated, candles counted, a quiet argument maintained against time itself: *my daughter may come through here someday, and if she does, she will not be cold, and she will not be without light, and that is the part of loving her I still know how to do.*

Love arriving too late to be simple. Love arriving as a green ammo can in a limestone overhang, annotated like a supply manifest, because that was the only language the woman had ever been fluent in.

Cyrus became aware, distantly, that she was crying, which annoyed her enormously. She cried the way she did everything alone—efficiently, silently, furious about it—and then she wiped her face on her sleeve, laughed once at the absurdity of the gloves (*men's large, Mother, honestly, I was sixteen the last time I wore men's large*), and put the gloves on.

They fit perfectly. Of course they did. Her mother had probably measured a pair of Cyrus's old mittens against her hand at some point, memorized the dimensions, and bought accordingly. The woman had mapped an entire mountain range. She could map a glove size.

* * *

She spent the rest of the storm-day inventorying the box properly, because inventorying was easier than feeling, and because her mother would have risen from whatever plane the dead occupied to personally criticize a sloppy gear check.

The practical items went into her pack: matches, candles, cord, the medical kit with its dutifully current contents. The novel she hesitated over, then packed too, telling herself it was ballast. The register page she read a fifth time, then a sixth, hunting for something more—some postscript, some hidden meaning—and found only what was there. A roof ridge. A cocoa tin. *If Cy ever comes through.*

If. As if it were in question. As if her mother hadn't stocked a cave two days' climb from the cabin, hadn't chosen the overhang on the exact route any sane traveler would take toward the basin, hadn't placed the box behind a stone only someone trained to read caves would find. *If Cy ever comes through,* written by a woman who had built a lighthouse and then pretended it was a shed.

"You knew," Cyrus said to the fire. "You knew I'd end up back here eventually. You bet on it. You stocked for it." She poked the coals. "And you still couldn't pick up a phone."

But even as she said it, she heard how young it sounded, and how tired. Because she'd done the same thing, hadn't she? Ten years of silence, each of them waiting for the other to move first, both of them fluent in the same stubborn dialect. Her mother had at least had the excuse of distance and dying. Cyrus had had nothing but momentum.

Outside, the storm kept on, but there was a change in its voice near dusk—the shriek dropping to a growl, the gusts spacing themselves out like a runner finding rhythm. By her reckoning she had a hard window tomorrow, maybe twelve hours of moving weather before the next system stacked up behind this one. Twelve hours to close the gap on Vess, who had a day's lead, stolen notes, and no reason to stop.

Cyrus laid out her kit with the firelight gold on her hands—gloves that fit, matches that were dry, a book she'd pretend she wasn't glad to have—and understood, finally, the shape of what she was carrying. Not just the notes. Not just the evidence waiting in the basin. She was carrying the reply her mother had never gotten to send, and the answer Cyrus had never gotten to give, and the only way to deliver either was forward. Up. Into the unfinished business.

She banked the fire, tucked the register page into the inner pocket where she kept the things she couldn't afford to lose, and slept—really slept, deep and dreamless, warmer than she'd been in nine years.

She woke before dawn to a sky scrubbed clean and hard blue, and to the sight, when she crawled to the cave mouth, of fresh tracks in the new snow below the overhang.

Boot prints. One set, climbing. Recent—crisp-edged, unmelted, made sometime in the night or the grey hour before dawn. Gaunt man's stride, favoring the left leg slightly, headed straight up toward the basin.

Vess had passed within shouting distance of her fire while she slept.

Cyrus stood in the cave mouth with the cold pouring over her and her mother's gloves on her hands, and felt something settle in her chest that was equal parts dread and certainty. He was slowing down. He was hurting. And he was running out of mountain in exactly the direction she was going.

She shouldered her pack and followed.
