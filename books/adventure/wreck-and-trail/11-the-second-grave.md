## 11. The Second Grave

The cairn sat where nothing should have been.

Cyrus saw it from two hundred yards and stopped walking without deciding to. Above the tree line the basin slopes ran in long gray tongues of talus, and everything up here was either rock or snow or lichen doing its slow patient work on both. Nothing was stacked. Stacking took hands.

She stood very still for a moment, the way her mother had taught her to stand when the ground offered something up — *let it finish its sentence before you interrupt* — and then she crossed the slope in long switchbacking legs, because approaching a cairn head-on felt like something you shouldn't do, even to a stranger's dead.

It was well built. That was the first thing she noticed, and the second thing, and the third thing too, because there wasn't much else to notice and her mind kept circling back like a dog returning to a bone. Flat stones at the base, courses laid shingle-fashion so meltwater would sheet off instead of seeping in, capstones chosen for weight rather than size. Whoever raised it had known what they were doing. Whoever raised it had taken their time.

That was the part that made her stomach tighten. A panicked person throws rocks into a pile. This was masonry. This was someone standing over a dead man and thinking about drainage.

"Cold camp," she said aloud, to no one, because the wind had been in her ears for six hours and her voice sounded strange and human and good. "Somebody did cold camp work."

She set down her pack and circled the structure once, reading it the way she'd read the erased trail three days ago — edges first, then texture, then what was missing. The stones were lichened on their undersides. Not gathered fresh from the surface; pried up, turned over, which meant they'd come from nearby, which meant the builder hadn't wanted to wander far from the body. Which meant the builder had been here a while.

Or had wanted it to look that way.

Cyrus crouched and put her palm flat against the lowest course. Cold, yes, but the snow shadow on the north side was undisturbed, un-melted, un-anything. Whatever lay beneath had lain there through at least one hard freeze.

"Okay," she told him. "Okay. I'm going to look. I'm sorry."

She lifted the capstones one at a time, setting each aside with care, because whatever else had happened on this mountain, the man under the rocks deserved better than rubble dumped on his face. She'd dug enough graves in her life — for pack animals, once, horribly, for a horse named Biscuit who had opinions about river crossings — to know that the work itself was a kind of prayer whether you believed in anything or not.

Under the third layer she found wool. Under the wool, a face.

He'd been young. That hit her harder than she expected. Gray-frosted beard stubble, cheeks hollowed by altitude and hunger, eyes closed as if he'd simply decided mid-sentence to stop listening. There was frost damage along one side of his jaw, and his right arm was bent wrong at the elbow, and when she worked her way down past the torso she understood why the cairn was where it was: his left leg ended above the knee in the ugly flattened way that bones end when they've been driven up through muscle by a fall of some distance onto stone.

He hadn't died of the leg, though. She checked — not because she wanted to, but because checking was the job now, had been the job since the ranger put that transit case in her hands and asked her to look. No blood beyond what a break like that would weep. His lips were gray-blue, drawn back slightly. He'd frozen. Broken leg, high country, someone else's schedule. He'd frozen the way men freeze when the people around them make an arithmetic and the arithmetic doesn't include them.

"I'm sorry," she said again, and meant it more the second time, which was how apologies usually worked.

She was reaching to rebuild the cairn when she saw the corner of paper under a flat stone near his shoulder — placed deliberately, weighted deliberately, the way you pin something you want found by exactly one kind of person. Someone who reads ground. Someone who lifts stones.

Her heart did something complicated.

She slid the stone aside. A single journal page, torn cleanly from a bound book, folded twice. The handwriting was cramped and slanting, the writing of a man working fast in cold with numb fingers, and she knew before she opened it whose it was, because Tomas Reyes had described Vess's field book in detail — *he never let anyone hold it, he wrote in it every night like it owed him money.*

She unfolded it and read.

---

*Day 9. The guide is dead and I need to write this down before I forget which parts are true.*

*We were on the chute below the notch. I went first. The rock I used for a handhold came out. I have written that sentence four times now trying to make it true. The rock came out. It may have been loose already. Chutes are full of loose rock; anyone who works mountains knows that. Anyone who works mountains also knows you test your holds, and I didn't test mine, because I was moving fast and he was behind me and I wanted the lead.*

*He fell maybe forty feet. I got down to him. His leg was gone — I mean finished, the bone through, no argument about it. He said carry me. I looked at the route ahead and I looked at him and I did the math and the math said one of us reaches the claim and neither of us does if I stay.*

*I told him I'd go for help. I even believe I meant it when I said it. Then I built the cairn, because leaving a man under open sky felt like something, I don't know, unfinished, and I stacked it properly because stacking things properly is the only thing I have ever been good at.*

*Here is the part I will not write in any report: I took his rope before I left. And his stove fuel. And I did not leave him my coat, because I needed my coat.*

*If someone finds this: the team voted. Ask Okafor about the permits. Ask why a survey crew gets a government case with M.A.B.'s initials stenciled inside. I am not the villain of this. I am the last one still moving.*

---

Cyrus read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third read was the one where you stopped seeing the words and started seeing the man who wrote them, hunched somewhere out of the wind, telling himself a story in real time, revising as he went.

*The rock came out.* Four attempts at one sentence. She'd spent ten years around people who lied for a living — permit offices, grant committees, the polite machinery of institutions — and she knew what revision looked like. Revision was *it may have been loose already*. Revision was *I even believe I meant it*. The truth had been in the draft and he'd sanded it down sentence by sentence until it could be carried in public.

And underneath all of it, the thing he couldn't sand away: *I needed my coat.*

She sat back on her heels in the cold and looked at the young man's face and felt horror arrive not as a shout but as a quiet settling, like snow filling tracks. Because here was the thing she couldn't stop noticing: the cairn was *good*. Vess had been freezing, alone, carrying stolen rope and stolen fuel and a story he was already drafting, and he had still taken an hour — maybe two — to stack stones properly over the man he'd robbed and abandoned. Some part of him had needed the grave to be right.

Survival hadn't made Vess careless. That was the horror of it. Survival had made him *careful* — careful about holds he didn't test, careful about arithmetic, careful about which parts were true. It had hollowed him out and left the craftsmanship running like a machine in an empty house.

Cyrus thought about herself at twenty-two, walking out of the funeral parking lot with a duffel bag and a bus schedule, telling everyone including herself that she'd be back. She thought about the decade of contracts that ended at first snow, of towns she could name by their laundromats, of the small daily revisions — *I'm just passing through, I don't live anywhere, it's easier this way* — each one true-ish, each one sanded. She had never done what Vess did. But she recognized the workshop.

"That's the trick of it," she said to the dead guide, quietly. "You don't decide to become this. You just keep doing the math, and one day the math is all that's left."

The guide declined to disagree.

* * *

She rebuilt the cairn properly, taking her time, because the work was the prayer and also because her hands needed something to do while the rest of her caught up. Capstones last. Drainage considered. When she finished she stood with her bare hand flat on the top stone for a moment — a gesture she didn't remember learning, which meant she'd learned it before memory, which meant her mother's hands had once rested where hers rested now, on some other cairn, on some other range, teaching the same wordless sentence.

Then she dug out her notebook and did the other work, the work that mattered, the work Vess would hate.

Position, by bearing off the notch and the twin spires to the west. Altitude estimate. Condition of remains. Cause of death, written plainly: *exposure following fall; left femur comminuted; survival unlikely regardless of abandonment, but abandonment occurred.* She wrote that clause and then stared at it. *But abandonment occurred.* Five words that turned a tragedy into evidence.

Name unknown. She sketched his face anyway, quick and honest, the way her mother had taught her to sketch artifacts — capture what's distinctive, don't flatter, don't editorialize. Hollow cheeks. Frost line along the jaw. Young.

"The rangers will get you a name," she told him. "Somebody's missing you somewhere. Somebody's been setting a table."

She tore the page free, then hesitated, then copied Vess's confession word for word into her own book before repacking the original in oilcloth and sealing it in the side pocket of her pack, against her spine, where it couldn't be lost or traded or argued with. Two copies. Her mother's rule for anything that mattered: *paper travels in pairs.* One copy for the world. One copy for the fire, if it comes to that.

The fire comment surprised her. She filed it away to think about later, or never.

* * *

She ate standing up, watching the route ahead. The chute Vess described was visible from here — a steep gray gash climbing toward the notch, the same slide-chute geometry she'd found rerouted into the survey trail lower down. Everything on this mountain led through the same throat. Her mother had found the door; the consortium had hired men to walk through it; Vess had walked through it carrying notes that proved the door should have been locked forever.

And the notes were ahead of her. Still ahead. Always ahead, like everything else in her life.

She checked the sun. Early afternoon, maybe one o'clock, the light already going thin and sideways the way it did up here in October, shadows stretching like taffy. Vess had a day's lead, maybe less if the weather had slowed him — and the weather was coming, she could feel it in the way the wind had shifted northwest overnight, in the bruised color sitting on the far peaks like a held breath. First snows were due within the week. They might be due tomorrow. The mountain didn't send invitations.

Tomas was two days behind her, cached with her tent and the radio, a broken leg and her guilt, waiting for her voice or her absence. Every hour she climbed was an hour the rescue window for him narrowed. Every hour she descended was a claim signed in fraud and a basin bulldozed before spring. She'd done this arithmetic on the trail for three days now, turning it over like a stone, looking for the side without lichen.

There wasn't one. There never was. You just picked the weight you could carry and walked.

"You'd better be worth it," she said to the notes she hadn't seen yet, to the mother who'd written them, to the whole stubborn chain of custody that had dragged her up a mountain she'd sworn off at nineteen. "Both of you."

She shouldered her pack. The oilcloth-wrapped confession rode against her spine, and the guide's cairn stood clean and correct behind her, and somewhere above the notch a man who stacked stones beautifully was climbing toward a claim with her mother's handwriting in his pocket.

Cyrus looked at the chute, tested the first hold out of habit, and started up.

Halfway across the first pitch, she found the rope.

It was anchored badly — a single cam wedged into a widening crack, the kind of placement that holds until it doesn't — and hanging below it, frayed to threads, was the last six feet of a rope cut short. Cut, not worn. She knew the difference the way she knew the difference between swept tracks and scattered ones. Someone had shortened this rope on purpose, up here, above the fatal chute, and the arithmetic of that was simple and sickening: Vess had taken the guide's rope at the cairn, used it on the climb, and then cut it free rather than coil and carry the extra weight.

He'd taken a dead man's rope and then thrown most of it away.

Cyrus stood on the ledge with the frayed end in her glove and felt the horror settle another inch deeper, past anger, into something colder and more useful. Vess wasn't desperate. Desperate men hoard. Vess was *efficient*, and efficient men reach summits, and the summit claim was one ridge away.

She clipped the ruined rope to her belt anyway — scrap, but scrap that proved a pattern — and looked up at the notch, where the wind had begun to carry the first dry smell of coming snow.

"Still moving," she murmured, echoing his journal. "So am I."

She climbed.
