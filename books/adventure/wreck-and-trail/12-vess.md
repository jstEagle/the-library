## 12. Vess

The river told Cyrus where he'd be before she saw him.

It was a glacial thing, gray-green and loud, braiding itself around a spine of boulders in the middle of the valley floor, and there was exactly one place to cross it this time of year—a gravel bar where the current split three ways and lost its nerve. Anyone coming down from the high basin would funnel toward it like water into a drain. Her mother had called places like that *decision points*. You didn't track a person through wilderness, she used to say, tapping the map with a pencil stub. You tracked their decisions.

Cyrus crouched at the tree line for an hour, watching the crossing, chewing on the last of her jerky and not tasting it. The light went long and coppery. The wind came down off the icefield carrying that thin metallic smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it.

And then he came out of the willows on the far side, and her whole body went still in a way it hadn't since she was nineteen years old.

He was worse than she'd imagined. She had built him up on the trail—Vess the team leader, Vess who ordered men into a slide chute, Vess who left a surveyor with a broken leg because he was ballast. In her head he'd become something large and deliberate, a villain with shoulders. What limped out of the willows was a gaunt man in a shredded shell jacket, moving like each step was a negotiation he was losing. His beard had gone gray in patches. He carried a drybag clipped across his chest the way a mother carries a child, one arm wrapped around it, and when he reached the gravel bar he didn't look up at the mountain behind him or the valley ahead of him. He looked only at the water, the way a starving man looks at a locked pantry.

Cyrus stood up out of the shadows and said, "Evening."

* * *

Vess heard the voice come out of the trees and his first thought—not fear, not yet, his mind had stopped producing fear somewhere above the second river—was: *she found me.*

His second thought was that she was smaller than her reputation. He'd read the file. Brightwater. Daughter of the famous one. The ranger service had pulled her record the same week the consortium's lawyers started asking questions about the missing survey, which meant somebody important thought she mattered, which meant she was trouble. But standing there at the edge of the willows with windburn on her face and a coil of rope over one shoulder, she looked less like trouble and more like every other person the mountains had pared down to essentials.

"Evening," she said again, and came down onto the gravel bar without hurrying, which frightened him more than hurry would have. Hurried people could be bargained with. Unhurried people had already done their arithmetic.

"You're a long way from anywhere," he managed. His voice came out cracked. He hadn't spoken aloud in—he tried to count—four days? Five? He'd been talking to himself up high, keeping the words moving so they wouldn't freeze. "Cyrus Brightwater."

"You know my name."

"I know a lot of names." He meant it as armor. It came out pathetic.

Her eyes went to the drybag. Of course they did. Everyone's eyes went to the drybag eventually; he'd learned that much about people in thirty years of fieldwork. He shifted his weight so the bag swung behind his hip, and the movement cost him, and he saw her see the cost.

"The rest of your team," she said. "Reyes made it down. Broken leg, frostbite in two fingers, alive." She let that sit. "The guide didn't."

"I know," Vess said. "I buried him."

"I found the cairn. I found your page under the stone." She took a step closer. "You wrote *the slope failed without warning.*"

"It did."

"You were roped together."

The wind came off the glacier and pushed between them, and Vess wrapped his free arm tighter around the drybag and told himself the shaking was cold. "You weren't there," he said. "Nobody who wasn't there gets to—" He stopped, because even to his own ears it sounded like something a man says right before he starts lying in earnest.

"He fell," Cyrus said quietly. "Or you cut him loose. Or you untied. There are only three versions of that story, Mr. Vess, and you're the only witness left."

"That's not true," he said, and then, because some stubborn professional honesty in him refused to die quietly: "That's not entirely true. There's a fourth version. The one where I did everything right and it didn't matter."

* * *

Up close, Cyrus could see what the mountains had taken out of him, and how recently. There was frost-nip healing along his jaw. His lips were split in the pattern of a man who'd been licking them because he wasn't drinking enough. And his hands—the hand not holding the bag—trembled with the fine, constant shiver of someone running a calorie deficit so deep his body had begun liquidating itself.

She knew that tremble. She'd worn it herself, once, in a bad November in the Chugach, alone by choice, proud of it, counting it as proof of something. Proof of what, she couldn't have said now. That she could suffer better than other people? That suffering was a kind of freedom?

"Sit down," she said. "Before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You're a strong gust from being a headline. Sit."

To her mild surprise, he sat, folding down onto a driftwood log bleached white as bone. She dug in her pack, pulled out her stove, and set water heating without asking. It wasn't kindness, she told herself. It was technique. Frozen men couldn't talk, and she needed him talking. The fact that part of her wanted to feed him anyway—that part she declined to examine, the way she'd been declining to examine things for ten years.

While the stove hissed, she studied the drybag. Waterproof, government-issue gray, the kind with a roll-top closure. He'd been sleeping with it. Possibly wearing it. Whatever was inside, he'd decided it was worth more than the fingers he'd nearly lost.

"The notes," she said.

Something moved behind his eyes—too fast to name, but she caught its tail, and it looked almost like relief. Like a man who'd been carrying a piano upstairs finally hearing someone offer to help.

"They're mine," he said. "Legally. I was the principal investigator. The data belongs to the contract holder."

"The contract holder being?"

"A resource development firm. Halvard Minerals." He said it flatly, watching her face, and whatever he saw there made him keep going. "You already know. Okafor showed you the permits."

"She showed me the permits. Show me the truth."

The water began to mutter against the pot. Vess looked at it the way a man looks at a fire he's been denied, and Cyrus handed him a mug of the hot water plain, no tea left to put in it, and he held it in both hands like communion.

"We voted," he said.

"Excuse me?"

"The summit claim." His voice was steadier now, warmed through, rehearsed—she realized with a small cold shock that this was a speech, that he'd been giving it to himself for days, maybe weeks, polishing it in the white silence. "The permits lapsed at first snow. Everything below the basin was already mapped, documented, filed. But the upper claim—the summit approach—that needed ground-truthing, and we had six days of weather window. So we voted. Seven of us. To push for the summit claim before the window closed." He lifted his chin. "It was democratic. I want that understood. Nobody was ordered anywhere."

"And Reyes?"

A pause. A hairline crack ran through the speech. "Tomás disagreed with the route."

"The slide chute."

"The chute saved us nine hours. Nine hours, Ms. Brightwater, in a window that narrow, is the difference between a filed claim and nothing. Your mother understood that kind of arithmetic. She invented it."

"Don't," Cyrus said, sharper than she intended, "talk about my mother."

But he was already leaning forward, and she saw with real unease that the speech had a second act, and the second act was her. "You think I don't know what you've been doing out here?" he said. "Ten years, Cyrus. Ten years of seasonal contracts, never the same range twice, never a lease, never a forwarding address. I read the file. I read *her* file too, back when the firm was worried she'd file her basin evidence. Do you know what I thought, reading yours?" He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all day, because it was kind. "I thought, there's a woman who understands. Some things are worth outrunning people for."

The stove hissed. Somewhere upstream, a chunk of bank calved into the river with a sound like a door closing.

"I'm not like you," Cyrus said.

"No," Vess agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

* * *

The girl was crying again.

Vess stood outside the mess tent at base camp, seven years ago, coffee cooling in his hand, listening to Elena Brightwater's daughter shout through canvas thin enough to hear every word. *You always do this. You always pick the mountain.* And Elena's voice underneath, low and tired and terribly patient, the voice of a woman explaining gravity to someone who refuses to fall: *If I don't finish the season, the claims stand. If the claims stand, they pave the approach valley. It isn't the mountain, Cy. It's what's under it.*

Then the tent flap, the girl storming out with her pack half-strapped, and Elena standing in the doorway looking older than her fifty-one years, and Vess had opened his mouth to say something comforting and instead said nothing, because he was already calculating that the dig would lose its best field hand and the schedule would slip eleven days.

He'd thought about that nothing often since. It had a texture. It was the exact shape of the hole where a person should be.

Now the daughter sat across a driftwood log from him, feeding him hot water he'd have killed for yesterday, and he watched her watch him, and he understood—with the strange clarity that arrives near the end of things—that she had already placed him. Not as a monster. Monsters were easy. She'd placed him as a *possibility*, the way you mark a route on a map you hope never to take.

Good, he thought. Use it. It was the last tool he had.

"There's a finder's fee," he said. "Twenty percent to whoever brings the claim data in. That's four hundred thousand dollars, divided however the survivors agree. Right now the survivors are me." He turned the drybag around slowly, deliberately, and set it on the log between them. "Help me cross the river tomorrow, walk out ahead of the snow, and it's two hundred thousand and a signature on a confidentiality clause. You'd never work another seasonal contract in your life. You could be anyone. Anywhere. Isn't that the whole point of you?"

Cyrus didn't look at the bag. That was the first wrong note. He'd expected hunger; everyone was hungry; hunger was the universal language and he spoke it fluently. Instead she looked at *him*, with an expression he couldn't parse until he realized what it resembled: pity. She was pitying him, this gaunt half-frozen man with four hundred thousand dollars in a waterproof bag, and the pity was worse than anger, and he heard himself say, too quickly:

"It's a fair offer."

"I know it's fair," she said. "That's what scares me."

* * *

Cyrus looked at the drybag and let herself actually consider it, honestly, the way you test ice before committing your weight.

Two hundred thousand dollars. A name change was cheap. A bus ticket was cheaper. She had done it before—she had a whole decade of practice at becoming someone new, at the little ceremony of it, the new jacket in a new town, the alias that lasted three months until a coworker got curious and she moved on. She knew exactly what the money would buy, because she'd been buying it in installments her entire adult life: distance, anonymity, the sweet weightless feeling of having no past that could catch her.

And she knew, now, sitting across from the finished product, what it cost.

Because Vess had bought it too. Look at him. He'd spent thirty years being anyone, anywhere, and it had hollowed him into a man who slept clutching a drybag, who gave speeches to himself in the snow, whose only remaining skill was arithmetic—who had stood at the edge of a crevasse or a slope or whatever it was, with a dying man on the other end of his rope, and *calculated*. Nine hours. Window closing. The math was always clean. The math never mentioned the guide's name, because the math didn't require names, and a man who runs long enough stops requiring them too.

*Some things are worth outrunning people for,* he'd said, and he'd meant it as kinship, and the horror was that she recognized the sentence. She'd said versions of it to herself in a dozen trailhead parking lots. The difference—the only difference, and it was narrowing every year she kept moving—was that she'd never yet had to calculate with a human being on the other end of the line.

Never yet. Give it ten more years of one foot out the door.

The river roared. The light was failing. Somewhere above them, the first flakes were probably starting, fat and experimental, the scouts sent down ahead of the storm.

"No," Cyrus said.

Vess's face did something complicated—relief and despair fighting for the same ground—and she understood, suddenly, completely, that he'd wanted her to refuse. That some part of him had been praying she'd say no, the way a gambler prays to lose, because losing meant the game ended and he could stop.

"Mr. Vess," she said, and her voice came out gentler than she'd planned, "when's the last time somebody fed you?"

He blinked. "...What?"

"When's the last time somebody fed you, and sat with you, and didn't want anything?" She was already reaching into her pack. "Because I'm going to do that now. And then tomorrow morning I'm going to take the notes, and you're going to give them to me, and I'm going to leave you fire, food, shelter, and coordinates precise enough that even a consortium lawyer could find you. And then the rangers get you, and the inquiry gets you, and you tell the truth in a heated room instead of freezing honest in a ditch. That's the deal. It's not negotiable, and unlike yours, it doesn't require anybody else to die."

"You can't take them," Vess said, but his hands were already moving to the drybag's roll-top, fumbling at the buckle, and Cyrus watched him fight himself for three full seconds before his fingers gave up and stilled. "They're mine. The fee—my whole life is in that bag, do you understand? Without it I'm just—" His voice broke open. "I'm just a man who left someone on a mountain."

"Yes," Cyrus said softly. "I know exactly what that's like."

She held out her hand.

And Vess—gaunt, half-frozen, thirty years into flight, staring at the open palm of Elena Brightwater's daughter—began, horribly, to cry, and reached for the buckle again, and this time it came undone.

* * *

Later, with Vess zipped into her spare sleeping bag and snoring the ragged snore of the rescued, Cyrus sat by the fire with the notes in her lap and didn't open them.

She should. Every practical instinct said open them, inventory them, learn the route before dawn. But her hands stayed flat on the oilskin cover, on the familiar block letters—*E.M.BRIGHTWATER, VELL RANGE SURVEY, FINAL SEASON*—and she sat very still while the fire ticked and the river ran and the first flakes of the storm came down through the smoke, landing on the pages without melting.

Her mother's handwriting. Her mother's route. Her mother's last season, folded shut in her lap, addressed—to whom?

Cyrus turned the bundle over. On the back, in fresh pencil, in her own mother's hand but written years after the cover, someone had added a single line:

*For C., if she ever comes looking.*

The snow thickened. Cyrus pulled the notes against her chest, and looked north up the dark valley toward the basin, and understood that she had never once, in ten years, been running away.

She'd been running late.
