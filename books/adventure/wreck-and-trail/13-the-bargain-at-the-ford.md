## 13. The Bargain at the Ford

The river announced itself long before Cyrus saw it—a low grinding roar under the wind, the sound of water shoving boulders around like a bored giant rearranging furniture. She'd been following the sound for an hour, and Vess's tracks for two days, and somewhere in the overlap between those two facts her legs had stopped being legs and become an argument she was losing.

She came out of the trees onto a gravel bar and there he was.

Vess stood at the edge of the ford, hip-deep in gray meltwater, clinging to a half-submerged boulder like a man slow-dancing with it. His pack was gone. His parka hung open. He'd lost his hat somewhere behind him on the mountain, and his hair had frozen into shapes that suggested he'd been sleeping in it, which he had. When he turned and saw her, his face did something complicated—relief and dread fighting for the same real estate—and settled on a smile that fooled neither of them.

"Brightwater," he said. "You're a hard woman to shake."

"You didn't try very hard. You left tracks like a parade."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough bent him over the boulder until he was nearly folded in half. Up close he looked worse than gaunt—he looked *edited*, like something had gone through him and removed everything nonessential. His lips were the color of old candle wax. The notes were strapped to his chest inside a waterproof map case, and one of his hands stayed pressed flat against it even while the other hand gripped the rock, the way a man guards a wound or a child.

Cyrus stopped ten feet back, on dry gravel, and let the wind carry her voice to him instead of wading closer.

"The ford's bad," she said. "Water's up with the warm days. You cross here alone, you're going to end up in the lake at the bottom of the gorge, and they'll find you in spring looking like something the fish filed a complaint about."

"Then I'm glad you're here." He said it easily. Too easily. Ten days ago this man had ordered a survey team into a slide chute, and now he was smiling at her from a freezing river like a neighbor asking to borrow a cup of sugar. "I could use a rope partner."

"I'll bet you could."

"I mean it, Brightwater. Look at me." He spread his arms, still holding the rock, presenting himself like evidence. "I'm not going to make it without help, and you know it, and I know you know it, because you've been tracking me for two days when you could have turned back at the tree line."

She had asked herself that question roughly four hundred times since dawn—*why am I still walking toward this man*—and had produced no answer she liked. So she said nothing, which he took for agreement, because men like Vess took silence for agreement the way rivers took valleys: automatically, and all the way down.

"There's a crossing two miles downstream," she said. "Slower water. Braided channels. We can hop it on stones if the fog stays off."

"Two miles is two hours I don't have. My feet—" He lifted one boot out of the water and she saw the state of it and looked away. "I need to cross *here*. And I need you to cross with me. And I—" He paused, and she watched him decide to stop pretending, which was almost refreshing after two days of reading his lies off the ground. "And I'd like to make it worth your while."

"There it is," Cyrus said.

*

Here is what Cyrus Brightwater knew about money, and about distance, and about new names:

She knew that eleven years ago she had walked out of the funeral reception with forty-three dollars in her pocket and a duffel bag full of clothes that smelled like someone else's house, and that forty-three dollars had felt less like poverty than like permission. She knew that in the decade since she had worked under three surnames that weren't hers and one first name that wasn't either, and that each new name had fit like borrowed boots—fine for a season, then rubbing, then bleeding. She knew exactly what a finder's fee on a mining claim in the Vell Range ran, because she'd read the consortium's filings in the county records office with Okafor standing behind her breathing through his nose, and the number had followed her up the trail like a dog hoping to be fed.

It was a number that could buy a cabin. Not the leased kind—the owned kind, with a roof she chose and a door that locked and no landlord's nephew showing up in June to say the lease was done. It was a number that could buy distance measured in *oceans* instead of ridgelines. It was a number that came attached, of course, to a lie—to a fraudulent survey, to Tomas Reyes's broken leg, to a guide whose body lay under a cairn with a journal page pinned beneath a stone—but numbers never showed you their attachments until you were already holding them.

That was the thing about temptation nobody warned you about. It didn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrived looking like a door.

"All right," Cyrus said, and heard her own voice go flat and careful. "Let's hear it."

Vess's face opened like a window. He'd known she would listen. That was the worst part—that he'd counted on the part of her she least wanted to admit existed.

"A third," he said. "Finder's fee splits three ways—me, the consortium's man in the county office, and whoever brings the route in. The other two are dead weight now. You and I split it fifty-fifty. That's—" he named the figure, and the figure sat down in Cyrus's chest like a large bird landing.

"That's a lot of money," she said, because denying it would have been its own kind of lie.

"It's a *life*, is what it is." He eased himself around the boulder, keeping the water at his thighs, talking fast now, a man who could feel the current of the conversation and thought it was running his way. "You think I don't know your file? I read it before we ever hired you—or tried to hire you, before you turned us down, which frankly should have told me everything. Cyrus Brightwater. Daughter of Elena Brightwater. Walked away from the most famous name in range archaeology and built yourself into a ghost. No fixed address in six years. Paid cash. Contracts under aliases." He smiled again, and this time there was something almost tender in it, which made it worse. "You've already disappeared once. I'm just offering to pay you for the second time."

The wind came down the gorge and hit them both and Cyrus stood in it and let it push her, because it was easier than admitting what was pushing back.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said out loud in eleven years: some mornings, waking up in whatever bunk or lean-to or rented room she'd landed in, she had *liked* it. The lightness of it. The way a name you'd only used for four months could be shed like a coat. The way no one could grieve you properly if they couldn't find you. Her mother had died famous, pinned to a map like a specimen, mourned by strangers in three countries—and Cyrus had looked at that and thought, *never me, never that, I will stay so small the world can't get a grip on me.*

And the world had obliged. For eleven years the world had obliged beautifully, and all it had cost her was everything.

"Say the rest," she said.

Vess blinked. "The rest?"

"There's always a rest. Men like you don't offer thirds of anything; you offer halves and keep the arithmetic vague. Say what happens to Reyes. Say what happens to Okafor's report. Say what I'm buying with my share besides the cabin."

For a moment—one honest moment, she'd give him that—something tired moved behind Vess's eyes, something that looked almost like the man he might have been before the mountain got hold of him. Then it closed.

"Reyes is alive," Vess said. "You found him. Whatever story he tells is his business. Okafor gets a report saying the team was caught above the storm line, that the guide fell, that I barely got out. Tragic. Nobody's fault. The permits lapse, the claims refile in spring under clean names, and a basin full of rocks nobody cares about stays a basin full of rocks nobody cares about." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him visibly, his whole frame shuddering with cold. "Nobody dies in the story, Brightwater. Isn't that what you want? A story where nobody else has to die?"

"And the basin itself? What my mother proved was down there?"

Something flickered across his face at the name—Elena—quick as a fish under ice.

"The basin is a hole in the ground," he said. "Your mother spent nine seasons telling everyone it was a cathedral. Look where that got her."

The words went in clean, the way well-aimed words do, and Cyrus felt them lodge somewhere below her ribs, and she was surprised—not by the cruelty, she'd met plenty of cruel men on mountains—but by how little it moved her. Three days ago that sentence would have sent her walking. Three days ago any sentence containing her mother's name had been a door she exited through. Now it just sat there, a stone in her boot she'd apparently decided to keep walking on.

"No," she said.

Vess stared at her. "No?"

"No." She said it again just to feel the shape of it. It was, she realized, possibly the first time in her adult life she had refused money out loud, and it tasted strange, like drinking water after years of thirst and finding it sweet. "Not the money, not the ford, not the story. Here's what happens instead. You're going to come out of that river, because staying in it is suicide and I didn't track you two days to watch you drown before you can face a courtroom. You're going to sit by a fire I build you. And tomorrow morning you're going to walk out of these mountains under escort, with those notes, to Deputy Okafor, who is currently sitting at the ranger station doing paperwork and being patient, which is the thing he does best."

"You'd turn me in to *rangers*?" Vess actually laughed, a short disbelieving bark. "Brightwater. They'll take the notes, fine. But the consortium owns half the county. Paper burns. Men like me don't stay caught."

"Maybe," Cyrus said. "But here's the difference between you and me, Vess. You think getting away is the same as surviving. I've had eleven years to test that theory, and I'm the published study, and the results are grim." She crouched, unhurried, and began pulling the coil of rope from her pack, letting him watch her do it, letting him understand that the decision was already made and his participation was optional only in the details. "Stopping doesn't cost what you think it costs. That's the trick of it. You run so long you start believing the running is load-bearing. It isn't. You can put it down. I promise. I did it four days ago and I'm still standing."

Vess looked at her for a long time. The river ground its boulders. Somewhere upstream a ptarmigan complained about the weather, with some justification.

"You sound like her," he said finally, quietly. "Your mother. In the field. She used to talk like that—like the mountain was a problem with an answer and all you had to do was refuse to leave before you found it." His jaw worked. "I hated her for that. Made the rest of us look like tourists."

"She had that effect," Cyrus said, and was startled to find she was smiling.

Then the fog came off the water.

It rolled up the gorge the way fog does in the high country—all at once, a white wall with intent—and in the space of ten seconds the far bank vanished, then the near bank, then Vess, leaving Cyrus alone in a gray room the size of the world with the roar of the river coming from everywhere and nowhere.

"*Brightwater.*" His voice, off to her left, ragged. "The water's rising. If we're crossing, we cross *now*—"

"We're not crossing in fog, are you insane—"

A splash. Not a small one. The kind of splash a body makes.

Cyrus was moving before the thought finished forming, rope in one fist, driving into the white blind toward the sound, the cold hitting her knees, her waist, her chest, the current grabbing her like a debt collector—and her fingers found fabric, found an arm, found the strap of the map case pressed against a chest that was still, blessedly, heaving.

"Hold on," she shouted into the fog, to a man she'd just refused, whom she owed nothing, whom she was saving anyway. "Hold on. I've got you."

The river disagreed. But Cyrus Brightwater had stopped listening to things that told her to let go.

* * *

They came out on the far bank twenty minutes later, both of them, which by the standards of that ford constituted a miracle, and collapsed onto the gravel like two puppets with cut strings. The fog was already thinning, embarrassed at its own drama. Vess vomited half the river back into it, then lay gasping, the map case still strapped to his chest, his frozen hands too stupid with cold to work the buckles.

Cyrus reached over and undid them for him. The notes were dry. Of course they were dry. The whole rotten scheme was dry.

"Fifty-fifty," Vess wheezed, staring up at the brightening sky. "You could still—"

"Shut up, Vess."

"You'd be rich."

"I said shut up." She was already gathering wood for the fire, her hands shaking—from the cold, she told herself, purely the cold—and there was a strange, terrifying lightness in her chest, like a pack she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was there had finally come off. "Fire first. Arrest second. And if you say the word 'claim' one more time, I'm feeding the notes to the river and telling Okafor you ate them."

Vess managed a weak, wrecked laugh. Then, watching her stack the kindling with her mother's exact economy of motion, he said, almost gently: "She'd have crossed this ford alone, you know. Your mother. Wouldn't have waited for anyone."

"Yeah," Cyrus said, striking the match, and watched the flame catch and grow in the gray morning. "I know. That's why I'm not her. Watch me do it differently."

The fire took. Two miles downstream, the ranger station's radio crackled to life with a weather warning, and the first flakes of the early snow began to consider the sky—and Cyrus, warming her hands over the flames beside the man she'd just declined to become, understood that the hardest part of the trail was still ahead of her, straight up, into the basin, alone.
