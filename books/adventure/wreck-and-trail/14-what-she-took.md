## 14. What She Took

The river ran loud enough to swallow a conversation, which was probably why Vess had chosen it. Or maybe he'd just stopped where his legs had stopped carrying him. Cyrus couldn't tell anymore with men like that—the difference between strategy and surrender got thin when the cold got into you.

He stood on the far gravel bar, swaying slightly, the field notes pressed against his chest in a plastic map case the way she'd once seen pilgrims hold relics. His lips were gray. His eyes were not. That was the thing about Vess—whatever the mountain had taken out of him, it hadn't touched the part that did arithmetic.

"You're Brightwater's daughter," he said again, because he'd already said it twice and apparently hoped repetition would make it land differently.

"And you're a man standing on the wrong side of a river in October," Cyrus said. "We've both been identified."

The ford was knee-deep at its worst channel, but knee-deep water at this altitude ran at temperatures that made knees a philosophical concept rather than a practical one. She could cross it. She'd crossed worse. The question wasn't whether she *could*.

The question was what she'd be carrying back.

"Help me across," Vess said. "And I'll cut you in."

"There it is." She crouched on her side of the bank, deliberately unhurried, and began unbuckling her pack flap while he talked. It was a trick her mother had taught her without ever meaning to: when someone is trying to sell you something, keep your hands busy so your ears can do their work alone.

"Finder's fee on the summit claim runs six figures," Vess said. "Six figures, split two ways, cash, no paper trail. You walk away from here, you never testify, nobody ever has to know you found me. You could be in Whitehorse by Thursday under any name you like." He paused, reading her face the way she read ground, and misread it completely. "I can see you want it."

What Cyrus wanted, at that moment, was a sandwich. But underneath the sandwich there was a shape, and the shape was familiar. Six figures. No paper trail. Any name she liked. She had spent ten years assembling exactly that life out of smaller denominations—seasonal contracts, cash jobs, towns where nobody asked twice. Vess wasn't offering her anything new. He was offering her more of the same, faster, with better views.

That was the strange thing, standing there with the river roaring between them: the offer didn't frighten her. It *bored* her. Somewhere in the last nine days—somewhere between a cold camp where grief finally caught up and a cave where her dead mother had left her wool socks in exactly her size—the exit door had quietly stopped being an exit. It was just a door now. And doors only have power over people who are trying to get somewhere else.

"I'm going to ask you some questions," Cyrus said, "and you're going to answer them, because answers are free and lies cost calories you don't have."

Vess's jaw tightened. "You're making a mistake."

"Probably. I make several a week. Let's start easy. Did you order Reyes to take the slide chute?"

Silence. The river filled it.

"The chute," Cyrus said. "Rerouted switchback, unstable talus, a route that exists in exactly one set of field notes in the world. My mother's. Somebody on your team had copies. Somebody chose it for a team that trusted him. So: did you order it?"

"He voted for it," Vess said. "Reyes. We took a vote."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. There was a particular kind of lie that needed the truth wrapped around it to survive—a kernel of fact, a real vote, a real argument—and then the liar bent the facts around himself like a man bending a sapling into a snare. She'd heard it from poachers and permit-skippers and one memorable horse thief. It always sounded reasonable. That was how you knew.

"A vote," she repeated. "And when the vote went against you, you kept the notes anyway."

"The team was dying," Vess said, and here his voice cracked open just enough to show something underneath—not remorse, she thought, but exhaustion wearing remorse's coat. "Two days of storm. Food down to nothing. The permits lapse at first snow and everything we mapped is worth nothing after that. Everything. Do you understand? Years of work. I made a call."

"You made eleven calls," Cyrus said. "One per person. Nine of them were wrong."

She watched that land. Not as guilt—she doubted guilt still had an address where he lived—but as calculation recalculating. His eyes flicked past her shoulder, up-valley, doing the math on whether he could run. She let him look. Running required legs that worked, and his had stopped taking instructions somewhere below the last ridge.

"Here's what happens now," Cyrus said, and stood, and shouldered her pack. "I'm coming across. Not for you. For the notes."

"They're mine—"

"They're my mother's," Cyrus said, "and they're evidence, and they're going to a ranger station in a government evidence bag, and the last place they will ever be is inside your jacket. You can hand them over or I can take them off a frozen man, but either way they cross this river with me."

For a moment—one long, ugly moment—she thought he might actually fight her for them. She measured the distance, the current, the rock in her hand that she'd picked up without quite admitting why. Then Vess's shoulders dropped, all at once, like a tent losing its last stake, and he held out the map case across the water.

Cyrus waded in.

The cold hit her shins like a verdict. She kept her feet wide, planted her pole, moved through the deepest channel with her breath locked in her chest, and came up the far bank streaming and gasping and profoundly awake. She took the map case first. Only then did she look at him properly—at the frost-nipped cheeks, the hands that wouldn't close, the man who had traded nine lives for a claim that would now buy him a very good lawyer and a very long inquiry.

"Sit down before you fall down," she said.

* * *

She built him a fire.

Not because he deserved one—she was clear-eyed about that, almost clinical—but because she had decided something on the way down to the ford, and the decision required him alive. Justice by paperwork, she thought, feeding dry spruce bark to the flame she'd coaxed out of her stove. Not justice by mountain. The mountain had done enough. The mountain had done everything, actually, and none of it had helped anyone except the consortium's lawyers, who would love nothing more than a story that ended with everyone conveniently dead and the claims quietly refiled in spring.

No. Vess was going to live, and stand in a heated room, and answer questions from people whose whole job was questions. That was the sentence. That was the point.

She dug through her pack and laid out what she could spare: half the trail food, the bivy sack she'd been telling herself she might need, a candle stub, matches enough for three fires if he rationed them. She wrote the coordinates of the ford on the back of a survey marker flag in pencil, big block letters, plus the bearing to the ranger station's emergency cache two miles downstream.

"Rescue will come through here within two days," she said. "Okafor knows this valley better than I do, and I've radioed ahead twice. Stay put. Stay dry. When they find you, tell them everything. Every decision, every vote, every order. All of it. Especially the parts that make you look bad." She met his eyes. "*Especially* those."

"And if I don't?" Vess said. There was no threat in it. Just curiosity, hollowed out, a man asking about the weather in a country he was leaving.

"Then Tomas tells them instead, from a hospital bed, with nothing to lose and excellent recall. And the guide's family gets told whatever version survives the retelling." She tucked the flag into his jacket pocket. "You've spent weeks deciding what other people get to know about their own dead. Consider this your last chance to stop."

Vess looked at the fire. For a moment his face did something complicated—something that might, in a better man, have been the beginning of an apology.

"She was right, you know," he said instead. "Your mother. About the basin. About the claims being built on sand. I read her notes. I read all of them." A short, terrible laugh. "That's why they hired me. I was the only one who understood what she'd found well enough to bury it properly."

Cyrus stood very still. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass like a long exhale.

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm taking them up instead of down."

She turned and forded back.

* * *

The cold came back into her bones on the far bank and she let it, because the shivering was honest work and she had honest work to do. She stowed the map case inside her jacket, against her chest, where her body heat could keep the plastic from cracking and where she could feel it with every heartbeat—a small rectangular promise riding on top of her heart like a second passenger.

She shouldered the pack. She checked her bearing against the ridgeline, then against her mother's route descriptions, which she now knew by heart in the way you know a song you hated the first forty times and loved on the forty-first.

Then she stood there for a moment and did something she hadn't planned.

She looked back.

Vess sat hunched at his small fire on the gravel bar, a smudge of orange in all that gray, already shrinking into the landscape the way lost things do. And Cyrus felt—she searched for the word, tested a few, discarded them—felt *finished* with him. Not satisfied. Finished. The way you feel closing a book you're glad you read and never need to read again.

Ten years, she thought. Ten years of leaving things unfinished. Jobs, towns, friendships, one nearly-fiancé in Fort Nelson who probably still told the story of how she vanished between the second coffee and the third. She had gotten so good at leaving that leaving had become her native language, and she'd forgotten there were others.

This was not that. She turned uphill and felt no pull at her back, no phantom door swinging open behind her, no itch in her feet. For the first time in a decade, every part of her was pointed the same direction.

"Well," she said aloud, to nobody, to the pass, to a woman twenty years gone who had stocked a cave with socks. "Let's go finish your dig."

The trail climbed. The air thinned. Somewhere above the tree line, the first true snow was waiting, and she had hours, not days, and a basin full of evidence that powerful men had paid good money to erase.

Cyrus Brightwater adjusted the map case against her chest, found her mother's line through the talus as easily as if the ground itself remembered her, and started up.
