## 15. The Ascent

The tree line gave up without a fight.

One moment there were pines, bent and stubborn, combing the wind with their needles; the next there was only krummholz, then grass gone bronze and flat, then nothing at all but rock and sky and Cyrus Brightwater's own breath coming back at her in white rags. She stopped where the last stunted pine leaned east like an old man listening for something, and she allowed herself one look back down the valley.

Tomas was somewhere below her, three hours back along the ridge, cached in her tent with the radio and two days of food and instructions he'd repeated back to her twice, word for word, because he understood that she needed him to. *If I'm not back by the fourth day, you call it in and you stay put.* He'd said, "I'll stay put," and then, quieter, "You come back too." She hadn't answered that. She'd been answering things like that her whole life by not answering them.

Now she turned uphill and didn't look again.

Her mother's notes rode against her chest in a waterproof sleeve, inside her jacket, over the layer Vess had called *excessive* once, years ago, in another life. She'd taken them from him at the ford along with everything else worth taking: the route descriptions, the sketch maps drawn in Marguerite Brightwater's small, ruthless handwriting, the margins full of annotations in a second hand — her mother's own hand, older, correcting herself across decades like a woman arguing with a younger stranger who happened to be right sometimes.

Cyrus climbed.

* * *

The first landmark came at midmorning, exactly where the notes said it would be, which annoyed her more than if it hadn't.

*A split boulder, grey with orange lichen on the north face, resembling nothing so much as a molar.* Her mother had written that thirty years ago, and there it was: a molar, grey with orange lichen, big as a barn. Cyrus stood under it and felt the specific irritation of being known. Her mother had seen this rock. Had thought about how to describe it to someone coming after. Had chosen *molar*, which was funny, which meant she'd been in a good mood that day, or a bad one and covering it, and Cyrus hated that she could tell the difference from the handwriting alone.

"Stop it," she told the boulder.

The boulder did not stop it. Neither did the next landmark, or the one after. That was the trouble with climbing a dead woman's route: every cairn was a sentence, every switchback a paragraph, and Marguerite Brightwater had never learned to write anything briefly. By noon Cyrus had been shown the best handholds on a slab pitch (*trust the left side; the right flakes ring hollow — I tested this the hard way, sorry*), the one reliable spring that ran even in drought (*it tastes of iron; do not let Tomas drink it first, he will make a face*), and a ledge wide enough for two tents where her mother had once waited out six days of weather with a man named Aldous who, according to the margin note, *"cheated at cards and was forgiven, mostly."*

Cyrus had never heard of Aldous. She filed him away in the growing drawer of things she'd have to ask about someday, if asking were still possible, which it wasn't, which was the whole problem.

She ate standing up, facing into the wind, because sitting down made stopping feel permanent. Above her the mountain went up in long grey steps, and between the steps the snow line hung like a held breath. It should have been higher. It was October and it should have been a thousand feet higher, and every hour it crept down toward her like something patient.

She checked the sky, read it the way her mother had taught her — *clouds with flat bottoms are furniture; clouds with ragged bottoms are weather* — and saw furniture thinning into weather by evening, maybe sooner.

She climbed faster.

* * *

The storm caught her at the col, because storms always catch you at the worst place on purpose. That was not superstition; that was geometry. Cols are where the wind funnels, and wind is where weather lives.

It came out of the west as a grey wall with no bottom to it at all, and within twenty minutes Cyrus went from squinting to blind. The temperature fell through freezing like a stone through water. Snow arrived sideways, then arrived in every direction at once, which shouldn't be possible and yet is, above ten thousand feet, in October, when the mountain has decided it's done with you for the year.

She knew better than to fight it. Fighting weather is how people die politely, with good effort noted in the report. She dropped off the crest into the lee, found the wind-scoured hollow her mother's notes promised — *a shallow cave beneath the tilted slab, dry floor, room for two, snug as a coat pocket* — and crawled into it with the particular gratitude of the very cold for very small mercies.

It was, in fact, snug as a coat pocket. Her mother had a gift for describing shelter so accurately you could feel grateful before you arrived. Cyrus got the stove going in the entrance with numb fingers and a great deal of swearing, melted snow, forced herself to eat, and then sat with her back against stone older than continents while the storm said everything it had to say outside, which was one word, over and over, at volume.

Two days, the radio forecast had said, before she'd cached the set with Tomas. Two days pinned.

She took out the notes.

* * *

There wasn't much else to do. Sleep wouldn't come — her body was too busy being furious about the altitude — and so she read, by headlamp, in the blue-white cave-light, while snow hissed past the entrance like sand poured on glass.

She'd read these pages at the ford, quickly, by flashlight, with Vess watching her across the water with the expression of a man doing arithmetic. She'd read them again at Cold Camp, hunting route information. This was the first time she read them the way they were written: slowly, all the way through, including the parts that weren't about routes at all.

Marguerite Brightwater wrote field notes the way some people write letters they never send. Between the bearings and the elevations, in the spaces where a less honest surveyor would have left blank paper, her mother had put down what the day had actually been.

*Day 41. The consortium's man visited camp again. He smiles like a door closing. He asked whether I intended to file the basin findings this season and I said yes and he thanked me for my time, which is what men say when they intend to make sure your time runs out.*

*Day 44. Wrote to C. No reply expected. Wrote anyway. The doctor says this is healthy. The doctor has clearly never written to C.*

Cyrus read that line four times.

*Wrote to C. No reply expected. Wrote anyway.*

Ten years of silence, and here it was from the other end: her mother had kept writing into it. Letters that never came — because Cyrus had moved eleven times in nine years and left no forwarding address, because *no reply expected* had apparently not slowed her down at all — letters her mother had written anyway, season after season, and then, judging by the way certain pages were worn soft at the folds, reread.

"You could have found me," Cyrus said to the cave. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "You mapped half a mountain range. You could have found me."

But even as she said it she heard the lie in it, the comfortable kind, the kind you keep because it fits. Her mother had known where she was. The cache in the cave below — stocked with the exact things Cyrus would need, dated *after* the estrangement — proved it. Marguerite Brightwater had known exactly where her daughter was and had chosen, decade after decade, to leave a light on instead of kicking the door down. And Cyrus had spent those same decades telling herself she was hiding so well that no one could reach her, when the truth was uglier and simpler: nobody had been chasing her. She'd been running from a door that was never locked.

The stove hissed. The storm said its one word. Cyrus discovered she was angry, and that the anger had nowhere to go, and that underneath it — worse, much worse — was the thing anger is usually hired to stand in front of.

Grief, keeping pace. It had finally caught her somewhere with no exit.

* * *

She slept badly and woke to the storm still going, and spent the second day doing what her mother would have done: inventory, maintenance, route study. She dried her socks with a thoroughness bordering on ceremony. She re-waxed the boot seams. She went through the notes page by page and built the rest of the climb in her head, landmark by landmark, until she could walk it with her eyes shut.

Somewhere in the afternoon she caught herself talking aloud, narrating her work in the tone her mother used to use, and stopped, horrified, and then — alone, pinned, two days from anyone — started again, because the alternative was silence, and silence in a snow cave with a dead woman's handwriting is a very loud thing.

"It's fine," she informed the stove. "The route's sound. She was careful. She was—" Her throat closed on the word *careful*, because careful was the last thing anyone called Marguerite Brightwater, least of all Cyrus, who had grown up calling her reckless, obsessed, married to the mountain, all the words children use when they mean *you chose it over me*.

Except the notes said otherwise. The notes said: *standing guard*. The notes said: *wrote to C*. The notes said: a woman who tested the hollow flakes herself and apologized in advance to whoever came after, and stocked caches for a daughter who might never come, and fought a mining consortium with a pencil and a theodolite because it was the ground that mattered, and the ground doesn't care whose name is on the map.

Inheritance, Cyrus thought, staring at the flame. She'd assumed it was the fame. The name on the maps, the lectures, the legend — the weight she'd spent ten years setting down and picking up and setting down again. But that wasn't the inheritance. The inheritance was this: a route described so well a stranger could survive it. A cave called *snug as a coat pocket*. An apology to the future, written in advance, in the margin, in pencil.

The inheritance was care, performed at altitude, for people you'd never meet.

"Well," Cyrus said, and wiped her face with a glove, and blamed the wind, which was fair, since there was plenty of it. "That's — fine. That's manageable."

The stove flickered. Outside, the storm began, very gradually, to run out of things to say.

* * *

It broke near dawn on the third day, the way storms do — not ending but leaving, withdrawing westward like a debt collector satisfied for now. Cyrus crawled out into a world scoured and rebuilt: fresh snow to her knees on the level, the air rinsed to a blue so hard it rang, and the summit ridge laid bare above her, white and clean and close.

The snow line had come down to meet her in the night. From here on, every hour mattered. She packed the stove, kicked out the fire remains, and stood a moment at the cave mouth with the notes zipped against her chest.

"Snug as a coat pocket," she said. "Good cave. Terrible company."

Then she went up.

The route above the col was her mother's masterpiece — a line through the headwall that looked impossible from below and unfolded, step by step, into something merely demanding. Every landmark arrived on schedule: the molar's larger cousin, a fin of granite *shaped like a sleeping dog, do not wake it by trusting its east side*; a snow bridge her mother had flagged with a warning so precisely worded that Cyrus crossed it feeling personally supervised. She climbed through the morning and into noon, in snow to her thighs in the drifts, following a dead woman's handwriting through live weather country, and found — this was the strange part, the part she'd deny later — that she wasn't racing anymore.

She was keeping an appointment.

Near midday she topped the last shoulder, and there it was: the basin. Sheltered, bowl-shaped, holding its snow like a cupped hand, exactly where the notes said it would be. From this distance it looked peaceful. It looked like nothing at all.

But at its far edge, dark against the white, she could see the cut of excavated ground. Trench lines. A tarp weighted with stones, half-buried, flapping its one loose corner at the sky like a small persistent flag.

And beside the trench, planted upright in the snow, a wooden stake with fresh orange survey ribbon tied to it — new, this season, tied by hands that had come up here to bury her mother's work and call it geology.

Cyrus stood on the shoulder with the wind pulling at her hood and looked down at the unfinished dig, and at the ribbon, and at the small dark rectangle of the excavation her mother had died one season short of finishing.

"Okay," she said, to her mother, to the mountain, to whatever was listening. "I'm here. Show me what you found."

She started down into the basin — and stopped, three steps in, because the snow ahead of the trench was wrong. Disturbed. Recently disturbed, and not by weather.

Someone had been here since the survey team vanished.

Someone might still be here.
