## 16. The Unfinished Dig

The basin opened below her like a held breath.

Cyrus stood at the lip of the saddle, snow squeaking under her boots, and looked down into a bowl of stone and old ice that no map she'd ever stolen a glance at had bothered to name. Her mother's notes called it the Hollow—just that, in her cramped field hand, as if naming it anything grander would invite company. Walls rose on three sides, steep enough to shed the worst of the wind, and the fourth side was a jumble of talus through which Cyrus had spent the last four hours picking her way with the delicacy of a woman defusing something.

She had expected to feel triumph. That was what the notes had promised, page after page of bearings and elevation readings that ended in a single underlined sentence: *The basin holds what they're afraid of.*

What she felt instead was the particular vertigo of standing in a room she'd been told about her whole childhood and never once entered.

"You could have drawn me a picture," she said out loud, to no one. Her voice came back off the rock, flattened by the thin air, and she took that as agreement.

She descended into the Hollow the way her mother had taught her to descend into anything—slowly, testing each hold, reading the ground before trusting it. The scree here was old and settled, the kind that had stopped moving years ago and resented being asked. Halfway down, a cairn appeared at the edge of her vision, small and deliberate, and her chest did something complicated around it.

Not a grave cairn. A route marker. Her mother had stacked it—Cyrus could tell by the way the stones were chosen, each one flat-sided, fitted, almost loving. Her mother had built cairns the way other people wrote letters. Cyrus had spent a decade pretending she hadn't kept every one of those letters.

*You're stalling,* she told herself, and kept moving.

* * *

The camp sat in the lee of the eastern wall, and it was not a ruin. That was the first thing that undid her.

Tents would have rotted, but the site had been closed properly—fly sheets folded and weighted under stones, a cook ring dismantled and stacked, a supply crate with its lid lashed down against weather and animals. Her mother had left this place the way you leave a house you mean to come back to. The lashing knots were hers. Cyrus knew them the way she knew her own signature: the doubled hitch, the little vanity of a tucked end. She had learned them at nine years old on a picnic table in the foothills, and had spent ten years untying everything her mother ever tied.

She knelt at the crate and worked the knot, and her fingers, half-frozen and stupid, remembered it anyway.

Inside: rope in good condition, a spirit stove, fuel tins, a canvas document case, and a tin of lemon drops that had fused into a single amber meteorite. Cyrus held the tin for a moment longer than the situation warranted. Lemon drops had been a field luxury, doled out one at a time on long traverses, always with the same speech: *Sugar is morale, Cy. Morale is gear.* She'd rolled her eyes at it for years. She was not rolling her eyes at it now, alone in a basin at eleven thousand feet with her eyes stinging in a way she was going to blame on wind.

The document case was the reason her legs nearly went out from under her.

It was heavy. That was the first sign. Field cases that heavy held paper—lots of it—and her mother had been a fanatic about paper. *Rock lies,* she used to say, tapping her temple. *Paper doesn't, if you do the work.* Cyrus unlaced the flap with hands that had stopped pretending to be steady.

She spread the contents on the folded fly sheet, weighting the corners with stones, and read.

* * *

It took her an hour, and by the end of it she was sitting in the snow without noticing she'd sat down.

The consortium's claims—the ones with permits expiring at first snow, the ones Vess had sold his integrity to chase—were built on survey data from a season twenty years back. Her mother had gone looking for that original survey, expecting sloppiness. What she'd found was forgery. The claim maps showed mineral strikes along the basin's rim, and the strike samples were catalogued with coordinates, assay numbers, dates. Neat. Professional. Damning.

Except her mother had walked every one of those coordinates. The samples were supposed to have come from outcrops that did not exist. One "sample site" was in the middle of a lake. Another was a quarter mile inside solid cliff. The assay numbers belonged to specimens pulled from a different range entirely—she'd traced them, of course she had, through three years of records—to a claim that had made a different company rich before it collapsed.

She hadn't just found fraud. She'd built the case. Cross-references, photographs with scale bars, affidavits from two retired geologists, a signed statement from the original surveyor's widow saying her husband had never come within fifty miles of the Hollow that season. Page after page, organized, indexed, ready to file.

And at the front, a cover letter, drafted and redrafted in three versions, none of them sent.

The third draft was the shortest. Cyrus read it twice, then a third time, because the handwriting had changed halfway through—looser, faster, the letters of a woman writing past dark with cold hands and something urgent in her chest.

*To the Land Board: I am submitting the enclosed documentation regarding claims 4-118 through 4-124. I want to be plain about why this took me three years. I kept hoping I was wrong. I was not wrong. These claims are paper ghosts, and the ground they sit on is the last intact high basin on the western face, and I have watched what happens to ground like this when the permits clear. I am not submitting this to make a name. I am submitting it because I mapped this range for thirty years and this is the one place that never asked me for anything, and somebody has to say no on its behalf.*

*My daughter thinks I do this work for the glory. I have not found a way to tell her that the glory is the least of it, and that I am afraid she'll learn it too late, and from the wrong direction.*

Cyrus read that paragraph four times.

The wind moved through the basin, found the gap in the eastern wall, and went on about its business. Somewhere above the rim, the light was going the color of old pewter, and she knew—she'd grown up knowing—what that color meant at this elevation in this season. First snow was not coming. First snow had arrived at the trailhead behind her and was walking up the mountain to collect.

But she sat in the snow a while longer, because her mother had written *my daughter* in a letter to a land board, and Cyrus had spent ten years telling herself she'd been left out of the work on purpose. Written off. Filed under *doesn't have the stomach for it.*

Her mother hadn't hidden this basin from her. Her mother had been *waiting* for her. The cache in the cave, two days back, with the mittens sized for Cyrus's hands and the chocolate and the spare glasses—sized for her, chosen for her, stocked years after they'd stopped speaking. And this letter, unsent, with its one naked sentence that was not evidence of anything except that a tired woman on a mountain had loved her daughter and been bad at saying it in person and had tried saying it on paper instead, to strangers, because the strangers were at least going to read it.

"You were standing guard," Cyrus said. Her voice cracked in the middle and she let it. "The whole time. You absolute stubborn—"

She didn't finish. The word she wanted was *hero* and it had always embarrassed them both.

* * *

She understood the rest of it with the cold settling into her knees, and she understood it with a clarity that felt like a hand closing around her heart.

The survey team hadn't been lost. They'd been *sent*. Vess's permits expired at first snow, and the one thing standing between the consortium and those claims was a box of paper in a basin no road reached, in the custody of a dead woman who couldn't testify. The team's orders, whatever Vess had shown the rangers, had never been about mapping. They'd been about making sure the Hollow stayed empty. Burn the case. Bury the camp. Let the snow take the rest, and by spring the claims would be clean and the basin would be a pit.

Tomas had refused to follow, and the mountain had taken its toll on the ones who didn't refuse fast enough. The guide under the cairn. The others scattered somewhere behind her, or ahead, or gone. And Vess, gaunt and half-mad at the ford, clutching her mother's notes like a talisman that had stopped working.

He'd never found the basin. She could see that now. The notes he carried were route notes, not site notes—her mother had kept the two separate, and the site notes had stayed here, in the crate, in the case, waiting. He'd been wandering the rim for days looking for a camp that was tucked into a hollow only her mother's route could find, and the mountain had been picking him apart the whole time.

The justice of that was not lost on her. Neither was the urgency.

Cyrus got up. Her knees reported their objections and she overruled them. The light was failing and the first flakes were going to come off that pewter sky within hours, and she had work to do that her mother had left unfinished—not the science, her mother had finished the science; the *filing*. The case had to come down the mountain. Every page. And if the storm closed in before she could carry it out, then she would carry it through the storm, because she had crossed a range to get here and she would be damned, publicly and specifically, if the paper died within sight of the road.

She repacked the crate with a soldier's economy: stove, fuel, the rope, the lemon-drop meteorite because some inheritances were not negotiable. The document case she wrapped in the fly sheet and then, after a moment's thought, in her spare shirt, and then she strapped it against her chest under her pack's waist belt, where it sat like a second heartbeat.

The first flakes came down while she was tightening the last buckle. Small, dry, unhurried, the way snow starts when it means to be serious. Cyrus looked up and let one land on her cheek and melt there.

"Thirty years you kept this ground," she told her mother, or the basin, or the wind. "I can keep it for one more night."

She turned toward the eastern wall, where her mother's route notes said there was a chimney that went up through the rim like a staircase built by a patient drunk, and she started to climb—out of the Hollow, into the falling snow, with the evidence of everything held against her chest and the storm walking up the mountain to meet her.
