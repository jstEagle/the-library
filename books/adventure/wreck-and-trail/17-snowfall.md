## 17. Snowfall

The first flakes came down like a rumor.

Cyrus was kneeling at the edge of the trench—her mother's trench, cut into the frozen turf of the high basin with the clean, impatient geometry of someone who knew exactly what she was looking for—when she felt it land on the back of her neck. One flake. Then another. Then a third that sat on her skin just long enough to be insolent before melting.

She looked up. The sky had gone the color of old pewter, low and close, and the peaks around the basin had quietly put on their winter faces while she wasn't watching. Somewhere above the cloud deck, the snow was already falling in earnest. It was only a matter of minutes before the mountain remembered where it had left it.

"Hours," she said aloud, to nobody, to the trench, to her mother. "You couldn't have picked a basin with a shorter season."

The basin didn't answer. Basins never did. But the wind shifted, and the trench exhaled a smell of cold earth and old stone that Cyrus chose, generously, to interpret as agreement.

She got to work.

The survey team's equipment was still cached at the basin's mouth where Vess's party had abandoned it—a transit case, two field tablets in padded shells, a photogrammetry rig that looked like it cost more than the cabin she'd been renting for three winters, and a small, beautiful total station that hummed when she woke it and asked her, in polite green text, if she would like to run a calibration.

"I would like to run out of here before the snow closes the pass," Cyrus told it. "But yes. Fine. Calibrate."

It took her twenty minutes to remember how to drive the software—her mother had owned an earlier model, back when Cyrus was a teenager and had been expected, with a kind of loving tyranny, to carry it. Her hands remembered before her head did. That was the thing about inheritance nobody warned you about: it lived in your tendons. Ten years of running and her fingers still knew how to level a tripod, still knew to tap the screen twice and then wait, because impatience made the instrument sulk.

The flakes thickened. She worked faster.

The site itself was not large. That was what struck her, standing back from it with the tablet in her mittened hand. After everything—the vanished team, the erased trail, the dead guide under his cairn, Vess half-frozen at the ford clutching stolen notes like a man clutching a winning ticket to a lottery that had already been drawn—after all of it, the thing worth killing for was a trench eleven meters long and a scatter of survey markers on a shelf of ground no bigger than a churchyard.

But then she crouched at the trench wall and looked at what her mother had exposed, and understood.

The strata were wrong. Anyone could see it, once it was pointed out, and her mother had pointed it out in red grease pencil on the exposed face, in that upright, unhurried handwriting that Cyrus had spent a decade trying not to recognize in her own mirror. The layers didn't match the geology of the surrounding slopes. This was fill. Deliberate, historic, engineered fill—packed over something, sometime in the deep past, by people who had wanted it hidden. And in the fill, at the depth her mother had flagged with a small aluminum tag, were the cores: drilled samples, bagged and labeled, pulled from the seam of ore that ran beneath the basin like a vein under skin.

Her mother hadn't been digging for artifacts. She'd been digging for proof. The consortium's claim filings described this ground as unworked, virgin deposit. The cores said otherwise. Worked, tunneled, exhausted generations ago and sealed over—worthless, in other words, except as a lie, and the lie was worth a fortune in permits.

"You weren't chasing glory," Cyrus said quietly. "You were standing guard."

The snow was coming down steadily now, fat and unhurried, the kind of snow that meant business without raising its voice. She'd have preferred the shouting kind. The quiet ones buried you while you were being thoughtful.

She documented everything. Trench walls, east and west faces, scale bar in every frame. The grease-pencil annotations. The tag. The cores in their bags, each photographed with its label legible, each label cross-checked against the master log she'd found in the waterproof tube—her mother's field book, the real one, the one Vess had gone up the mountain to destroy and had never reached. Cyrus had it against her chest already, inside her shell jacket, in a dry bag, and she checked it the way she'd once checked a compass in whiteout: constantly, compulsively, with the flat calm of someone who knows panic is a luxury.

The tablets wanted to sync to a server. The server was a satellite ping and a half a world away, and the ping was failing in the building weather. Fine. She copied everything to both tablets and then, because she had grown up with a woman who trusted no recording medium further than she could throw it, and had inherited that suspicion along with the tendons, she copied it again onto the memory cards, popped the cards, and put them in different pockets.

"Paranoid," she said, and heard her mother's laugh in it, warm and unbothered. *Paranoid is just thorough with a better imagination, sweetheart.*

"Stop that," Cyrus told the basin.

* * *

The snow thickened. The light went strange and sourceless, the way it does when the sky gives up pretending to have a sun, and the trench walls began to soften at their edges as if the mountain were gently erasing her mother's work with a very large thumb.

Cyrus worked the perimeter next. Her mother's survey markers—small steel pins with colored flags, most of them still standing—needed coordinates. The total station shot them in neat, smug little bursts of numbers. Cyrus logged each one and, at marker seven, found something that stopped her.

A second set of marks. Older. Scratched into the pin's cap in a shorthand she knew the way she knew her own heartbeat: her mother's personal notation, the one she'd used for *verified, do not disturb*, the one she'd taught Cyrus to cut with a knife point when Cyrus was eleven and had held the pin crooked and been told, with terrible gentleness, that the mountain didn't care about her feelings but the record did.

Cyrus stood there in the falling snow with her thumb on the scratch mark and let herself feel it, for exactly as long as she could afford, which was about four seconds.

Her mother had expected someone to come check this work. Had expected it enough to leave a sign only family would read. Not a message. Not a plea. Just a professional courtesy across the years: *I was here, I checked it twice, it's good.*

"Okay," Cyrus said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared it. "Okay. Message received."

She shot the remaining markers. The snow was laying a clean white sheet over everything now, and the basin's walls were disappearing, and the cold had stopped being scenic. She packed the tablets, collapsed the tripod, cached the total station under a boulder overhang with a cairn on top because some equipment deserved to survive even if she didn't.

Then she did the last thing, the thing she'd been circling all afternoon the way a dog circles a spot before lying down.

She took out the field book.

It was a standard waterproof survey book, gray cover, her mother's initials tooled in the corner in the same tooling that had been on the transit case in the abandoned camp—*E.B.*, Elowen Brightwater, a name that had been a weather system in Cyrus's childhood, a name she had not said aloud in ten years and had now, in the last two weeks, said more times than in the decade before.

The last entries were in the shorthand. Cyrus read them standing in the thickening snow, reading fast, because her fingers were going and because some things you read fast so you can pretend they didn't go through you at full speed.

The final page was dated nine days before the entry the county had on file as her mother's last. Nine days of observations that had never been filed. Nine days in which Elowen Brightwater had finished the cores, verified the fill, documented the fraud, and written—Cyrus turned the page—a closing note.

It was addressed to her.

She knew it before she read a word, because her mother's handwriting changed on that page. The field shorthand loosened. The letters rounded. It was the handwriting from the margins of Cyrus's childhood schoolbooks, from the grocery lists, from the single birthday card that had arrived two years into the estrangement, forwarded three times, arriving on the wrong day, unsigned.

Cyrus didn't read it.

Not yet. Not here, with the snow erasing the world and her body's clock running down and the descent ahead of her—the descent, God help her, in whatever this was about to become. She'd earned the right to read it somewhere warm, or at least somewhere that wasn't actively trying to bury her, and she intended to exercise that right like a woman exercising a legal claim.

She put the book back in the dry bag, and the dry bag inside her jacket, against her chest, over her heart, which was the melodramatic placement and also the correct one, because it was warm there and because the book had spent nine years waiting in a cache for her and she was not going to make it wait one more minute in the cold than necessary.

* * *

She ate standing up—half a bar of the dense, joyless ration chocolate that tasted the way a boot smells—and drank, and checked the sky, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

The storm was early. Everyone had said it: the rangers, the old trappers, the almanac, her own bones. First snow was due in a week, maybe ten days. It was here in four. Which meant the pass below her was already closing, or would be within hours, which meant the descent she'd planned—the careful, switchbacked, two-day descent—was now a descent through worsening whiteout on a route she'd only ever read about in her mother's notes.

She got out the notes. Vess's stolen copies, which were now her recovered originals, or close enough; she'd have to sort out the paperwork of that with Okafor, and the thought of Okafor's face when she handed over a satchel of evidence recovered from a half-frozen fugitive at a river ford was, briefly, the warmest thing available.

Her mother's route descriptions were good. They were better than good—they were the work of a person who had walked this ground in every light and every mood and had written it down so that a stranger, or a daughter, could follow. *From the basin's south lip, bear left of the split horn, and do not trust the right-hand chute, which sloughs.* Cyrus could hear her voice in it. She could hear the patience in it, and the iron underneath.

She shouldered her pack. The snow was coming down in earnest now, the flakes fat and close together, the world contracting around her to a sphere maybe thirty meters across and shrinking. Somewhere under the new white lay the trail. Somewhere under the trail lay the switchback Vess's party had erased. Somewhere below all of it lay a ranger station, a deputy with a steady voice, a surveyor with a healing leg, and a life that Cyrus had, in the last two weeks, somehow agreed to have.

She took one last look at the trench. The snow was filling it, flake by flake, gently, the way you'd tuck in a child. By morning it would be a smooth white hollow, indistinguishable from the ground around it. But the record was in her pockets and the cores were photographed and the coordinates were shot and logged twice, and a lie that had been buried once by patient hands was not going to stay buried just because the weather had opinions.

"Guarded," she said to the basin. "Consider it guarded."

She thought she felt, absurdly, the particular quality of attention that her mother had radiated when pleased—warmth without weight, approval without condition. Then she decided it was the chocolate, and turned south, toward the split horn, and started down.

The snow thickened. The world shrank to the width of her shoulders and the length of her next step, and then to less than that. She navigated by the notes and by the count of her own paces, left of the split horn, do not trust the right-hand chute, and the field book rode against her chest like a second heartbeat, and somewhere below her in the gathering dark was a pass that was closing like a door.

She had hours. Maybe.

She spent the first one walking.
