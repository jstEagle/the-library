## 18. The Crossing

The snow did not fall so much as arrive, all at once, as if the sky had been holding it back out of politeness and had finally run out of manners.

Cyrus stood at the lip of the basin with her mother's field records buttoned into her jacket, against her chest, wrapped in the emergency blanket she'd cut into a sling. The documents were warm from her body heat. She kept thinking about that—about how paper could be warm, how it could feel almost like a pulse if she pressed her palm flat against her sternum and didn't think too hard about the fact that what she was really feeling was her own heart, hammering out a rhythm that said *go, go, go*.

The dig site was documented. The photographs were taken with the consortium's own survey camera, which felt like a small, private joke her mother would have appreciated. The coordinates were logged in three places: the notes, her own notebook, and—because she was not a fool—scratched into the handle of the ice axe she'd found in the cache. Her mother had always said redundancy was the sincerest form of self-respect.

Now there was only the small matter of getting down eleven thousand feet of mountain in a whiteout, on an ankle that had stopped being an ankle somewhere around the second moraine and had become, instead, a suggestion of one.

"Well," Cyrus said to the snow, because talking to herself had stopped feeling strange around hour nine. "Nothing for it."

She went.

* * *

The first hour was almost pleasant, if you were the sort of person who found pleasure in being slowly erased.

The snow came in sideways, thick as wool, and the world contracted to a sphere maybe fifteen feet across with Cyrus at the center of it like a very cold, very anxious marble. Above the tree line there were no landmarks left—no trees, no cairns she could trust, nothing but the slope's angle under her boots and the memory of a route she had read by headlamp in a cave two nights ago, in her mother's cramped, slanted handwriting, in a margin beside a sketch of a ridgeline.

*If the weather turns—and it will, it always does—descend the east shoulder. Do not trust the drainage. The drainage lies.*

The drainage lies. Cyrus had snorted when she first read that. Now, listening to the wind funnel up from somewhere below her left boot, she could hear exactly what her mother had meant. The gully looked like the easy way down. The gully was, in fact, a chute that would deliver her to a cliff band with the gentle efficiency of a mail chute.

"East shoulder," she said aloud. "East. My left. Left is east. Probably."

Her compass was in her hand, but the needle had developed an opinion of its own in the wind, swinging in lazy, drunken arcs, and she'd learned in the last hour to trust the slope more than the magnet. She traversed left. The snow squeaked under her. Her ankle screamed its one-note song, and she let it scream, because pain was information, and information meant she was still the kind of person who could feel her feet.

Somewhere below her was the ranger station. Two days' walk, in good conditions. In these conditions—

She didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts like that was how people sat down in the snow.

* * *

The tree line came to her as a smell before it came as a sight: resin, faint and impossible, threading through all that white. She stopped and stood with her eyes closed—her eyes had been mostly closed for an hour anyway, against the wind-scoured ice—and breathed it in like a woman smelling bread from a bakery she'd been told had burned down.

"Hello," she whispered. "Hello, you beautiful, living things."

The first stunted pine appeared out of the murk like a hand reaching up to steady her. She put her palm against its bark, snow-crusted and rough, and felt something in her chest unknot a single degree. Trees meant slope less steep than death. Trees meant fuel. Trees meant that if she fell, she would fall into something soft-ish rather than something that would scatter her over a quarter mile of scree.

Her mother's notes had a line for this too, from a descent in '09: *Below the tree line, the mountain forgives you. Try to deserve it.*

"I'm trying," Cyrus told her. "I'm trying so hard."

She descended through the pines with her hand on the windward side of each trunk, counting them, because counting was a thing her mind could do instead of panicking. Forty trees. Sixty. At eighty, the snow lightened from curtain to flurry, and at a hundred and twenty she could see—actually see—twenty feet instead of ten, and she laughed out loud, a ragged bark of a laugh that startled a ptarmigan into explosive flight two feet from her face, which nearly gave her a heart attack, which she also laughed at, hysterically, sitting down hard in a drift.

Her ankle had opinions about the sitting. She ignored them.

"Okay," she said, when she'd recovered. "Okay. Break. Food. Then on."

She sat against a trunk, dug out the last of the pemmican from the cache tin, and ate it slowly, making it last, the way her mother had taught her—*you chew like the food might change its mind.* The records pressed warm against her chest. She checked the button. Checked it again. It was becoming a tic, like a woman touching a locket.

Below her, through a tear in the weather, she caught one glimpse of the valley: gray-green, far down, with a thread of river catching what little light remained. And somewhere along that river, if the storm hadn't closed them already, were roads. A station. Okafor, who struck her as the kind of man who kept the kettle on out of pure stubbornness. Tomas, who'd made it down ahead of her or hadn't, and whose survival she had decided—firmly, in the cave, in the dark—was not her responsibility, and about whom she had thought roughly every forty minutes since.

She got up. She went on.

* * *

Dusk was a rumor. The storm closed again as the light went, and Cyrus made the call she'd been dreading: she could not navigate the river gorge in the dark. Not this ankle, not this visibility. She found a spruce with a low, dense skirt of boughs, tunneled in, stamped a platform, and sat with her back against the trunk, her pack in her lap, her arms around the pack, her chin on the pack, like a woman hugging a very important, very rectangular child.

The cold came for her toes first, then her fingers, then her resolve. She flexed everything in rotation. She recited things to keep her mind moving: the route descriptions, in order, like scripture. *East shoulder, not the drainage. Bench at 9,800 with the split boulder. The bench lies about its width; test it before trusting it. Below the bench, the old burn—deadfall for two miles, watch for widow-makers in wind. The river ford at the alder flat is knee-deep in September and a liar in every other month.*

She'd been reciting the alder flat line when sleep took her, mid-word, and she surfaced an unknown time later with snow sifting through the boughs onto her face and a terror in her chest that took a full minute to file correctly: *the records, the records, the records.* She patted her jacket. There. Warm. There.

"Fine," she croaked to the darkness. "We're fine. Everything's fine."

It was the most convincing lie she'd told all day, and she'd told herself several.

* * *

Dawn was a gray suggestion, then a fact. The storm had spent itself in the night; what remained was a soft, exhausted sifting, the kind of snow that seemed almost apologetic. Cyrus crawled out of her spruce den, stood up, and got a full look at the world for the first time in a day.

It was, infuriatingly, gorgeous. The burn below her was a forest of black spires wearing white caps, and beyond it the valley lay open and pale, and the mountains behind her—her mountains, the Vells, the ones she'd spent ten years pretending were just topography—stood in the clear morning light like something out of the illuminated books her mother used to read aloud from on storm nights, back when Cyrus was small enough to fit under one arm.

She let herself look for exactly ten seconds. Then she checked the button on her jacket and started down through the burn.

The deadfall was as advertised. Two miles of blown-down timber, snow-loaded, every fourth trunk balanced at the angle of a bad decision. She moved through it slowly, testing, listening for the creak that meant *widow-maker*, and somewhere in the middle of it her ankle gave a final, definitive protest and folded.

She went down hard, full length, chin cracking off a buried log, and lay in the snow for a moment seeing the particular white sparkle that means your brain has briefly left a note saying *back in five minutes.*

When the world reassembled, she took inventory. Teeth: present. Blood in mouth: some, tolerable. Ankle: had escalated from screaming to a deep, ominous silence, which was worse. Records: she was patting her chest before she'd finished the thought. There. Warm. There.

"Right," she said, spitting pink into the snow. "New plan. We hop."

She did not hop, exactly. She developed a gait that a zoologist would have struggled to classify—part walk, part lurch, part three-legged race with an invisible partner—and she covered the rest of the burn with it, and the alder flat beyond, and the river ford, which was indeed a liar: shin-deep where the notes promised knee-deep, because the cold had come early and clamped the river down. She crossed it on the ice anyway, because the water beneath was only a few degrees above murder and she didn't fancy testing it, and the ice held, creaking its objections, and on the far bank she stood dripping from the knees down and laughed again, that same unhinged bark.

"Knee-deep in September," she quoted. "A liar in every other month. You were *right*, Mum. You were right about everything, and I hate it, and I miss you, and I'm *bringing your notes home*."

Her voice cracked on the last word. She stood in the alders with the river talking behind her and let it crack. There was no one for six miles to hear it, and the one person who mattered had been hearing worse from Cyrus for a decade without the courtesy of being present for it.

Then she wiped her face with a mitt that was more ice than wool, and went on.

* * *

The last miles were the strange ones. The valley floor was gentle, the trail was a real trail, cut and blazed and maintained by people with salaries, and her body had passed through exhaustion into some flat, clear country beyond it where everything was simple. Left foot. Pole. Right foot, which was more of a formality now. Pole. The records against her chest, warm, warm, warm.

She thought about the cabin at the trailhead. She'd passed it a hundred times in her years of seasonal contracts—always the same thought: *one more season, then I'm gone, somewhere the mountains can't find me.* She thought about how the thought had changed weight in the last two weeks, how it had gone from a comfort to a question, and how somewhere on the east shoulder, in the white sphere with her at the center of it, the question had quietly answered itself.

She thought about Vess, half-frozen at the ford with his fire and his food and his coordinates, waiting for men with a stretcher and a warrant. She found, to her mild surprise, that she didn't hate him. She pitied him, which was worse and more useful. He had spent his whole crossing running from something, and it had hollowed him to a shell with a finder's fee inside. She had spent hers running too. The difference, she was beginning to understand, was not distance. It was cargo.

She was carrying something that mattered. He never had.

The light was going gold and long when she smelled it—woodsmoke, real woodsmoke, human woodsmoke—and her heart did something complicated that she chose not to examine. Around the last bend of the trail, the ranger station appeared: a squat log building with snow on its roof and, blessedly, smoke at its chimney, and beside the door, a figure in a ranger's coat who had been looking down the trail and went very still when he saw her.

Deputy Okafor. He took three steps, stopped himself, and stood there with his hands half-raised like a man unsure whether he was greeting a survivor or a ghost.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said. It came out reverent and appalled in equal measure.

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things," Cyrus said. Her voice was a ruin. "Mostly I'm supposed to be at your door, and look at that. Followed the route descriptions." She fumbled her jacket open with numb fingers, and pulled out the bundle—the records, the photographs, her mother's field notes, wrapped in the cut blanket, warm, still warm—and held them out to him with both hands, the way you hand someone something that is not really yours to give and never was.

"These need to get to the county. Tonight. Before the roads close."

Okafor took them carefully, like they might go off. Then he looked at her face—at the split chin, the ice-matted hair, the eyes that had seen the top of the world and come back down it—and something in his expression shifted from rescue to something more like witness.

"Miss Brightwater," he said. "You'd better come inside. There's a kettle on."

And behind him, from inside the station, propped up on a cot with his leg in a splint and a mug in his hand and an expression of such naked relief that it made her chest hurt, sat Tomas Reyes.

"You're alive," he said.

"Barely," Cyrus said, and stepped through the door, and let it close behind her against the snow.
