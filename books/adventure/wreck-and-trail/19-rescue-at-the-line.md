## 19. Rescue at the Line

### Cyrus

The last mile, she did not remember.

That was the honest truth of it, and Cyrus Brightwater had spent ten days learning to be honest with herself, so she might as well be honest about this too: somewhere between the tree line and the ranger station road, her body had taken over the driving while her mind sat in the back seat like a passenger on a long bus ride, watching the country go by without any obligation to name it.

She knew it was over when she smelled woodsmoke.

Not her own fire—she hadn't dared one in two days—but stove smoke, thick and resinous, the smell of a building that intended to keep burning all winter. It came down the drainage on the wind like an invitation written in a language she'd almost forgotten how to read.

She came out of the trees at the edge of the station yard at what she later learned was four in the afternoon, though her sense of time had gone soft as old rope. The sky behind her was the color of wet slate, and the first flakes were coming down in fat, unhurried spirals, the way snow does when it has decided to stay for the season and sees no reason to rush the announcement.

There was a truck in the yard. A plow, actually, chained up and idling, and a man standing beside it who turned when his dog started barking—a big shaggy thing with more enthusiasm than judgment—and stared at her the way people stare at things they don't believe yet.

Cyrus stopped walking. She considered this. Her legs had opinions about stopping, none of them favorable, but she made them hold anyway, because some part of her wanted to see the moment properly before she walked into it.

"Ma'am," the man said. Then, louder, over the wind: "Ma'am, are you—"

"Brightwater," she said. Her voice came out scraped, like a match struck on the wrong surface. She tried again. "Cyrus Brightwater. I'm coming down off the Vell Range. There are people you need to find."

The man's face went through several expressions in quick succession, like a card sharp shuffling. "We've got a chopper grounded since yesterday," he said. "Roads close tonight. We thought—" He stopped himself, which she appreciated. People who finished that sentence usually regretted it.

"I know what you thought," Cyrus said. "I'd like to come inside now, if that's all right. And then I'd like to talk to whoever is in charge of the search, because they're looking in the wrong places, and every hour they look there is an hour somebody freezes."

### Okafor

Deputy Okafor had been awake for thirty-one hours, and in that time she had drunk enough coffee to qualify as a watershed herself, so when the radio crackled with *"Brightwater. Walking out. Station yard,"* she assumed she had finally hallucinated.

Then she was running through the mudroom, past the drying racks hung with gear nobody had come back to claim, and out into the falling snow, and there the woman actually was—gaunt, frost-nipped, moving with the careful economy of someone whose body had been borrowed for something far beyond its warranty.

"You're supposed to be dead," Okafor said, and immediately wished she'd led with literally anything else.

"So am I, most days," the woman said. "Is Tomas Reyes here?"

Okafor blinked. "He is. He came out three days ago with your cache coordinates. He's been eating everything in the kitchen and telling anyone who'll listen that you went back up alone." She hesitated. "He's also been arguing with my supervisor about it. Loudly."

"Good," said Brightwater, and something in her face—the first real expression Okafor had seen on it, tired as it was—suggested this was exactly the news she'd hoped for. "Then let's not waste the argument. Get me a map and get me warm, Deputy. I have names, coordinates, and paperwork that's going to ruin somebody's week."

They got her inside. They got her soup, which she held in both hands for a full minute before drinking, just letting the warmth soak into her palms, and Okafor found herself watching this small ceremony with an unexpected tightness in her chest. Ten days missing. Everyone at the station had quietly moved her from *search subject* to *recovery file*, and here she was, holding soup like it was the most remarkable object in the county.

"Tomas," Brightwater said suddenly, setting the bowl down. "Where is he right now?"

"Kitchen. Where else."

She stood up. Her knees objected audibly. She ignored them with the authority of long practice and walked down the hall, and Okafor followed, and through the kitchen doorway they both saw it: Reyes, leg in a splinted cast propped on a second chair, mid-sentence in what appeared to be an ongoing lecture to two rangers and a very patient cook—

—who looked up, saw the woman in the doorway, and went absolutely silent.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Reyes said, "You're supposed to be dead," in a voice that cracked clean down the middle, and Brightwater said, "So I'm told," and he was up—or half-up, cast and all, wobbling against the table—and she crossed the room and caught him, and the two of them stood there in the middle of the kitchen holding each other upright, laughing in the ragged, disbelieving way of people who had done their arithmetic honestly and gotten a better answer than the sum allowed.

Okafor looked away, mostly. Some things deserved privacy even in a kitchen full of witnesses.

When she looked back, Brightwater had Reyes settled again and was pulling a folded, grease-stained bundle of papers from inside her jacket—papers she had clearly carried against her skin for days, the way you carry the one thing you cannot lose.

"Now," she said, spreading them on the kitchen table among the crumbs. "Who do I need to talk to about mining claims, fraud, and a man named Vess? And Deputy—" she glanced up, and her eyes were bloodshot and steady and absolutely certain, "—I'd start the helicopter warming up anyway. He's alive. Barely. But he's alive, and he's at these coordinates, and if we're fast, he answers to a judge instead of the mountain."

### Tomas

Tomas Reyes had spent three days doing two things: healing, and talking.

Mostly talking. His leg was pinned and wrapped and officially *non-weight-bearing*, a medical instruction he honored in the same spirit he honored most instructions, and he had worn a groove in the floor between his bunk and the kitchen, narrating to anyone trapped within earshot: the slide chute, the cold camp, the woman who had rigged a litter out of pack straps and pine poles and hauled him downhill for a day and a half while he apologized the entire way.

"She didn't complain once," he told the cook, for perhaps the ninth time. "I complained enough for both of us. I want that on the record."

"It's on the record," the cook said. "It's on the walls. Please eat your eggs."

And then Cyrus walked in—not dead, not frostbitten past saving, not a ghost, though she looked like she'd auditioned for the part—and Tomas forgot his eggs, his leg, and most of his manners, and got halfway up before physics filed its objection.

Holding onto her was strange. He'd known her for eleven days total, eight of them delirious or unconscious, and yet his arms seemed to have their own opinion about the matter, which was: *hold on.*

"You went back up," he said into her shoulder. "Alone. In front of a storm line. That is the single worst decision I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a man try to jump-start a helicopter."

"Flattering. Did you give them my coordinates?"

"Did I—" He pulled back to glare at her properly. "Cyrus. I gave them your coordinates, Vess's coordinates, the cache sites, the route descriptions, and a forty-minute speech about your competence that I intend to repeat at the inquiry until someone makes me stop. You saved my life. I have decided to repay this by being insufferable about it forever. That's the deal. No refunds."

Something happened to her face then—something small, quickly controlled, but real. He'd learned to read her in fragments, on the trail: the flicker of humor she tried to smother, the grief she carried like ballast. This was neither. This was closer to a person hearing good news in a language she'd stopped speaking.

"Fine," she said. "But I get to be insufferable back. Starting with: you followed the litter instructions perfectly, and I noticed."

From Tomas Reyes, this landed harder than he expected. He had spent his whole career being the guy who cut corners cheerfully, who improvised, whose field notes were famous for their creative spelling. Being *noticed*—accurately, by the one person whose noticing meant anything—sat in his chest like a coal.

"Yeah, well," he managed. "Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

### Okafor

By evening the station had transformed into something between a command post and a family argument.

The helicopter couldn't fly in the building weather—it had turned from snowfall into something with intent—so Okafor organized the ground response instead: two snowcat teams staging at the trailhead, rescue parties briefed on Brightwater's hand-drawn maps, which were, in Okafor's professional opinion, the finest pieces of route documentation she had ever seen. Every water source marked. Every slope angle noted. Little annotations in a cramped, confident hand: *exposed after noon — wind loads here.* Half the annotations were in a different hand, older, and when Okafor asked, Brightwater touched the page briefly and said only, "My mother's."

Okafor decided not to press. Some inheritances take longer than an evening to unpack.

At nine o'clock the roads closed—officially, with a phone call from the county, the plows pulled in, the gates dropped—and Okafor stood at the window watching the last truck's taillights vanish and felt the station settle around her like a ship making fast to a dock. Stranded. All of them. The inquiry would wait; the mountain would keep its prisoners a few days more.

She found Brightwater on the bunk-room porch, wrapped in a blanket that someone had put on her without asking, watching the snow come down through the yard light.

"Officially," Okafor said, leaning on the rail beside her, "you're supposed to be debriefed, examined, fed, and asleep. In that order."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, I brought you cocoa, because the coffee here is a war crime." She handed over the mug. "Also unofficially—I read the permit filings today. Before the roads closed. The consortium's counsel called twice asking whether the survey records survived."

Brightwater took a slow sip. "What did you tell them?"

"That all materials were recovered intact and in the custody of this office." Okafor smiled into her own mug. "Technically true. The custody being yours, and the office being wherever you happen to be standing."

That earned her a look—one eyebrow, dry as talus. "Careful, Deputy. That's almost humor."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them cold."

They stood together in the snow-hush, and Okafor found herself studying the woman sideways. Ten days ago Brightwater had been a recovery file and a rumor—a tracker nobody could quite vouch for, hired reluctantly, resented mildly. Now she was the reason two men were alive instead of statistics, the author of documents that were going to detonate a mining consortium's entire claim structure, and the person every ranger in the building kept finding excuses to walk past and nod at.

"You know what's strange?" Brightwater said at last, to the snow more than to Okafor. "I've spent ten years leaving places. You get good at it. There's a whole technique—you keep your gear packed, you don't learn names, you never sign anything longer than a season." She turned the mug in her hands. "Tonight I watched the road close behind me, and you know what I felt?"

"Tell me."

"Lighter." She laughed, short and wondering. "Ten days on a mountain, forty pounds of gear, no tent for the last stretch, and I feel *lighter* than I have in a decade. Because for once I walked toward something instead of away from it. Turns out that's the difference. Nobody tells you that."

Okafor considered saying something wise. What came out instead was: "The cocoa help?"

"Immeasurably."

Inside, muffled through the wall, Tomas's voice rose and fell—he was telling the story again, the litter, the storm, the woman who wouldn't quit—and beneath it the cook laughed, and someone dropped a pan, and the whole building creaked and glowed against the dark like a lantern set down carefully in the snow.

"The inquiry convenes as soon as the passes open," Okafor said. "Three days, maybe four. They'll want testimony from all three of you. Names, dates, the whole record." She paused. "Including your mother's. You understand that. The basin protection filing will need her documentation under her name—Maren Brightwater, principal investigator. It'll all be public. Speeches. Newspaper people, probably."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the porch roof. When she finally spoke, her voice was steady, and Okafor heard in it the sound of a decision already made, maybe days ago, high on a mountain, in a cave full of another woman's careful handwriting.

"Good," Cyrus Brightwater said. "Her name belongs on it. All of it. And I'll say it myself—in front of everyone." She drained the cocoa and stood, rolling her shoulders like a woman preparing to lift something heavy and knowing exactly why it was worth lifting. "Now. Before I sleep for approximately two days—show me where you're keeping Vess's seized effects. If his journal pages survived, there's a paragraph in them about the guide's fall that needs to be read *very* carefully, and I want to read it before his lawyer does."

Okafor pushed off the rail, grinning despite herself. "Right this way. And Brightwater? Welcome to the line."

Cyrus paused at the door, snow melting in her hair, and for the first time since Okafor had met her, she smiled like she meant to stay.

"Yeah," she said. "It's good to arrive somewhere on purpose."
