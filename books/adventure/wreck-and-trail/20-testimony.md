## 20. Testimony

The hearing room in the county annex had once been a grange hall, and it still smelled faintly of hay dust and old varnish, as if the walls remembered dances and seed auctions and couldn't quite believe what they were being used for now. Someone had dragged in folding chairs until there were too many of them. Someone else had set up a table at the front with a pitcher of water and five glasses, which struck Cyrus as optimistic, since only three people would be speaking and none of them, she suspected, would do much drinking.

She sat in the second row with her hands flat on her knees, wearing the one clean shirt she owned and a pair of trousers that had been brushed twice to get the basin out of them. The basin was still in her, though. She could feel it like grit worked into a seam—every hour of the descent, every landmark she'd climbed past, sitting somewhere behind her sternum where no amount of brushing would reach.

Deputy Okafor sat beside her, filling out forms he had clearly filled out already, because filling them out again gave his hands something to do. He'd shaved for the occasion. He'd also brought her a paper cup of coffee from the station, which she'd accepted because refusing it would have required conversation, and conversation was the thing she was dreading more than anything else in the room.

"You don't have to give them your whole life story," Okafor said quietly, not looking up from his pen. "They need the trail record. Dates, elevations, what you found. That's all."

"They subpoenaed my mother's name."

"They subpoenaed *M. Amberdale's field records*. Which happen to have your name on the accession card." He finally looked at her. "You could let me read it aloud. I'd be happy to."

Cyrus watched the room fill. Rangers, mostly, off shift and in their good jackets. A cluster of people from the valley co-op who had driven two hours through slush to be here. Two men in suits at the end of the third row who sat like furniture and did not take notes, and whom she recognized by posture alone: consortium lawyers, present the way a cold front is present. And near the back, half-hidden behind a pillar, Tomas Reyes on crutches, his leg in a cast up past the knee, having argued his way out of the hospital against medical advice because, he told her, "I've spent six weeks being carried around this range. I'm done being cargo."

The panel took their seats at ten o'clock sharp. Three of them: a regional land administrator named Hargrove with reading glasses on a chain; a hydrologist who introduced herself as Dr. Imbe and looked like she had personally measured every river in the territory and found most of them wanting; and a thin, precise man called Aldous Wren, who represented the survey board and whose voice, when he tested the microphone, made Cyrus think of ice forming over a puddle—one clean crack at a time.

The consortium went first. They had slides.

Cyrus listened to forty minutes of them. She learned that the Vell Range holdings had been "in continuous responsible development planning" for eleven years. She learned that the permits, expiring at first snow, represented "a significant sunk investment" and that an extension was "a procedural formality." She learned the phrase *environmental stewardship framework* and heard it used four times, always slightly differently, like a coat being turned to show its best side to each corner of the room.

What she did not learn was why a mining consortium needed a government survey team to walk a route into a high basin that their own filings described as "low-value marginal terrain."

Wren asked them about that. His voice cracked the puddle once, cleanly. "Your filing describes the upper Vell basin as marginal. Your survey team was equipped for a sixty-day alpine traverse. Can you reconcile those?"

One of the lawyers smiled the way you smile at a dog you're about to have put down. "Survey equipment is standardized across our operations, Mr. Wren."

"Mm," said Wren, and wrote something down, and the sound of his pen was very small and very loud.

* * *

They called Deputy Okafor first, and he was steady and thorough, walking the panel through the missing-persons timeline, the abandoned camp, the transit case stenciled M.A., the erased switchback. When he reached the part about the slide chute—the route that existed nowhere except in Marguerite Amberdale's field notes—he paused, and Cyrus realized he was giving her a runway. Building the moment when she'd have to stand up so that by the time she did, half the room already knew whose daughter they were looking at.

He finished. Hargrove thanked him. Then Wren said, "We'll hear from Ms. Brightwater now," and the name went through the room like a stone through a window, and every head turned toward the second row before she even moved.

She stood. Her knees worked. That was something.

She walked to the table at the front, and instead of sitting in the chair facing the panel, she turned the chair sideways, so she could see both the panel and the room. It wasn't a tactic. She just couldn't bear to speak with her back to Tomas.

"State your full name for the record," Hargrove said gently.

And here it was. Ten years of aliases on work contracts—C. Brightwater, C. Bright, once memorably C. Waters, which had fooled exactly nobody—and ten years of letting other people say her mother's name first, so she never had to.

"Cyrus Marguerite Brightwater," she said. "My mother was Marguerite Amberdale."

Saying it cost less than she'd expected. That was the strange part. Like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing and finding her shoulders still attached.

"You're listed as next of kin on the field records recovered from the Vell basin," Wren said. "Can you tell us how those records came to be there?"

So she told them. Not all of it—she kept the cave cache private, kept the last page of the notes private, kept the message addressed to her folded away behind her ribs where it belonged—but she gave them the shape of it. The unfinished dig. The season her mother died before it ended. The evidence her mother had documented and never filed: core samples, stratigraphy photographs, the survey benchmarks that showed the consortium's claim boundaries had been drawn over protected ground using coordinates that didn't match any legitimate datum. Falsified surveys, eleven years old, never challenged because the woman who caught them died with her findings in a tent at fourteen thousand feet.

"The consortium's original permits were granted on those falsified boundaries," Cyrus said. "Everything since is built on top of them. Their extension request isn't a formality. It's a renewal of a fraud."

One of the lawyers shifted. Furniture, rearranging itself.

"And the survey team?" Dr. Imbe asked. "Their role?"

"Tomas can tell you better than I can. But the short version—" and here Cyrus made herself look at the two suits directly, made herself hold it— "the team leader, Vess, was paid to produce a report declaring the basin low-value marginal terrain. To bury my mother's evidence under a fresh official survey. The rest of the team didn't know. When Reyes refused to sign off on falsified readings, Vess took the field notes and led the others into a route he had no business navigating, in weather he'd been warned about. One man is dead. Mr. Reyes has a compound fracture. Vess is alive because I left him food and fire and coordinates, which is more than he left anyone."

Silence. The good kind, the kind with weight to it.

Hargrove removed her glasses. "Ms. Brightwater. You crossed the upper range alone, above the tree line, in early storm conditions, to reach this site. Why?"

It was the question she'd been turning over for days, because the honest answer was complicated and the simple answer sounded like madness or heroism, and she was neither.

"Because the ground remembers," she said. "That's—my mother taught me that. People lie, paperwork lies, but ground doesn't. Somebody swept that trail to make the team unfound, and somebody rerouted it through a slide chute using my mother's own notes, and I was the only person alive who could read that. So I went." She paused. "I'd like to tell you I did it for justice. Mostly I did it because I couldn't stand watching someone use her work as a shovel. But it comes to the same place."

Dr. Imbe leaned forward. "The site itself. Describe what you documented."

Cyrus opened the folder Okafor had helped her assemble and walked them through it: the undisturbed strata her mother had photographed, the benchmark discrepancies, the core samples still sealed in their tubes, dated in her mother's hand. She read one date aloud—*September 14th, eleven years ago*—and her voice held, mostly, and where it didn't, nobody rushed her.

When she finished, Wren asked the last question, the one she'd known was coming since the subpoenas arrived.

"Ms. Brightwater. The consortium will argue that these records are unverified, that the chain of custody is compromised, that a grieving daughter's testimony is not admissible evidence. What do you say to that?"

Cyrus thought about it honestly, which surprised her. She thought about the years she'd spent making sure no one could ever accuse her of wanting anything, because wanting things meant losing them.

"I say verify it," she said. "Send your own surveyors. Send Dr. Imbe. The mountain's not going anywhere—it's the one party in this room that can't be paid off. My mother left the evidence in the ground precisely so it couldn't be lost or forged. All I did was carry the map." She set the folder down. "But I'll tell you what I'm not going to do. I'm not going to apologize for being her daughter to make the testimony more palatable. I spent ten years doing that. I'm done."

* * *

The deliberation took four hours, during which Cyrus sat on the annex steps in the cold and ate half a sandwich Okafor pressed on her and watched the roads fill with the season's first real snowplow convoys staging at the valley mouth.

Tomas came out on his crutches and sat down beside her without asking, which she was learning was his way.

"You did fine in there," he said.

"I called a dead man's work a shovel in front of lawyers."

"Yeah. Fine." He balanced a crutch across his knees. "For what it's worth—I signed nothing. I want that in the record someday. Whatever they decide about the permits, I never signed."

"I know," Cyrus said. "You stayed with a broken leg rather than follow him. That's louder than a signature."

They sat while the snow thickened, fat slow flakes that hadn't yet committed to winter.

"My sister keeps asking when I'm coming home," Tomas said. "I keep telling her after the inquiry. Now the inquiry's done and I'm going to have to come up with something new." He glanced at her sideways. "What about you? Where's home, for Cyrus Brightwater?"

It was, she noticed, the first time anyone had asked her that question straight, without irony, in a decade. And she noticed further that the answer didn't frighten her the way it would have a month ago, standing in a doorway with one foot already out.

"Ask me in spring," she said.

* * *

The panel returned at four o'clock, and the annex went quiet in a way that made the radiator sound enormous.

Hargrove spoke for them. "The permit extensions are denied. Further, the board refers the falsified boundary documentation to the territorial attorney general for criminal review, and suspends all consortium operations in the Vell Range pending investigation." She looked down at her papers, then up, and something in her face suggested the next line was hers personally, not the board's. "Additionally, this panel recommends the upper Vell basin—including the site designated in the Amberdale field records—for immediate nomination as protected scientific reserve. Dr. Imbe will lead the verification survey in spring."

The room did not erupt. Valley people don't erupt; they exhale, all together, a long collective breath that had been held since October. Somewhere in the back someone started clapping and it spread the way warmth spreads through a cold cabin—slowly, then everywhere.

The suits gathered their things without expression and left through the side door, and Cyrus watched them go and felt—not triumph. Something quieter. Something like the moment at the end of a long traverse when you crest the last rise and see the trailhead lights, and you don't cheer, you just start walking faster.

Okafor appeared at her elbow. "Attorney general will want formal statements. There'll be more hearings. Months of it, probably."

"Fine," Cyrus said, and heard herself mean it.

"That's it? Fine?"

"It's my mother's war," she said. "I'm just the one holding the map now."

* * *

That evening the ranger station smelled of stew and wet wool, and someone had pinned a hand-lettered sign to the corkboard that read VELL BASIN — PROTECTED (PENDING) with a smiley face that Okafor denied drawing and everyone denied believing him about.

Cyrus sat at the long table with her mother's field notes open in front of her, reading the route descriptions again, because in spring she would walk them with Dr. Imbe's crew, and because she had discovered that reading them no longer hurt the way it used to. It ached, yes—a clean ache, like a muscle worked hard—but underneath the ache was something that felt suspiciously like company.

Tomas limped over and set a mug beside her. "Imbe's crew leaves the valley the first week of June," he said. "They'll want a tracker who knows the route."

"They'll want a tracker who knows the route *and* can carry a radio," Cyrus said. "I hear she's particular."

"She is. She rejected two candidates already." He lowered himself carefully onto the bench. "So. Are you staying? Through spring, I mean. Past it."

Cyrus looked at the notes. At the last page, with its message addressed to her, which she had read eleven times now and would read eleven hundred more. At the window, where the snow came down steady and unhurried over a range that had finally stopped being a place she fled from and started being a place she belonged to.

She thought about the question the way she'd learned to think about river crossings—no drama, just depth and current and whether you could see the far bank.

"Yes," she said. "I'm staying."

Tomas nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd already written in his own field notes. "Good," he said. "Because Imbe asked me to ask you, and I hate asking twice."

Outside, the snow kept falling on the basin, on the dig, on the small stubborn evidence her mother had guarded with her life—falling soft and deep, the way snow falls on ground that is finally, officially, going to be left alone.
