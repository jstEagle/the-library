## 22. Where the Trail Ends

The lease was one page, and Cyrus read it three times anyway, the way she read everything now—slowly, as if the ground might shift under it.

*One year, month to month thereafter. Cabin and woodshed, trailhead parcel 4, Vell Range access road. Tenant responsible for woodpile, water line, and the stove, which is older than both of us and deserves respect.*

The last part was the landlord's handwriting, squeezed into the margin in pencil. Marta Okonkwo had owned the cabin for thirty years, rented it to fire crews and fish biologists and once, according to local legend, a man who claimed to be a retired pirate and turned out to be a tax auditor. She stood in her kitchen doorway with her arms crossed, watching Cyrus read, and she had the particular patience of a woman who had watched people sign things and unsign things her whole life.

"You can take it or not," Marta said. "The stove doesn't care either way. The stove has outlasted everybody."

Cyrus signed. Her hand did not shake. She had expected it to—she had braced for some dramatic internal weather, some final storm of the old restless self rising up to object—but what she felt instead was something smaller and stranger. The pen moved. The ink dried. The cabin, which had stood there through every winter of her absence, simply continued to stand there, now with her name attached to it like a tag on a pack.

"There," Marta said, taking the page. "Now you're a person with a woodpile."

"That's the most frightening thing anyone's said to me all year."

"You'll survive it. People do." Marta poured two cups of coffee without asking, which was the local custom and also, Cyrus suspected, the local test. "Deputy Okafor said you'd be by. She had a bet going with Tomas about whether you'd sign or whether you'd be gone by Thursday."

"Thursday's tomorrow."

"I know." Marta smiled into her coffee. "Tomas won."

* * *

The compass hung on a nail by the door by evening.

She had carried it through everything—the crossing, the whiteout, the inquiry, the long gray weeks of testimony in a county courthouse that smelled like floor wax and old paper—and she had told herself she was keeping it safe, keeping it for the right moment, and the right moment had kept not arriving, the way right moments do. So she made one. She found a nail in the tin of orphaned hardware on the shelf, hammered it in at eye height, and hung her mother's compass by the door where anyone leaving would pass under it.

It didn't point north anymore. She'd known that for years—the needle dragged, lazy, half a truth in every direction. She'd had it in her pocket the day she signed the lease and had caught herself, absurdly, checking it. *What are you going to do,* she'd thought, *navigate the kitchen?* But she hung it up anyway, because some instruments aren't for navigation. Some are just for looking at.

Her mother had bought it in Lhasa, according to the story, or possibly Kathmandu, depending on which version of the story she was telling, and it had survived three decades of fieldwork, one flood, and a decade of Cyrus's neglect, which was more than could be said for most relationships. The brass was worn smooth where her mother's thumb had rested. Cyrus's thumb found the same spot without her deciding to.

"Right," she said to the empty cabin. The stove ticked as it heated. Outside, the light was going gold and long, the particular light of a valley in the last week before the world starts closing its accounts. "Right."

She unpacked. Not all the way—she was not a woman who unpacked all the way, not yet, maybe not ever—but enough. The good boots by the door. The field kit on the shelf. The tin of tea that Tomas had sent up from the station with a note that read, in his careful surveyor's hand: *For the cabin. Drink it before it becomes a relic.* Under the window she set the framed photograph Okafor had pressed on her at the end of the inquiry—her mother at the basin, squinting into the sun, one hand shading her eyes, looking for all the world like a woman who expected someone to come up the trail behind her.

Cyrus had not looked at it directly for more than a second at a time. That night she looked at it for a full minute, and then hung it on the wall, and then made dinner, and the cabin held all of it without complaint, the way cabins do.

* * *

The contract came by mail, which felt right. Some things should have to travel slowly.

The Vell Range Conservancy wanted a seasonal-to-permanent trail and monitoring steward. The pay was modest. The term was *twelve months, renewable*—and Cyrus sat with that phrase for a long while, because every contract she had ever signed in ten years of moving had contained, somewhere in its fine print, a clause that ended at first snow. *Seasonal. Weather permitting. Term concludes with closure of the pass.* The world had always built her an exit and called it a schedule.

This one ran straight through winter. It said so in plain language: *duties include winter access patrols, snow-course measurements, and cabin maintenance during closure of the high route.*

She read that paragraph four times. Winter access patrols. Someone was going to pay her to walk into the cold months instead of out of them. She thought about the whiteout on the descent, the way the world had gone to wool and her own breath had been the only proof of direction, and she thought: *I could do that again. On purpose. And come home to a stove.*

She signed it at the kitchen table with the same pen, and then she went outside and split wood until her shoulders burned, because joy, she was discovering, needed somewhere to go, and her body had always been the place it went.

* * *

Tomas came up on a Saturday, walking with the careful rolling gait of a man whose leg had been broken well and properly and who had been told by three separate medical professionals that he was lucky, a word he had opinions about.

"You're supposed to be resting," Cyrus said, from the woodpile.

"I am resting. This is me resting. You should see me work." He lowered himself onto the chopping block with the dignity of a man boarding a throne and surveyed the cabin, the woodshed, the compass by the door. His eyes snagged on the compass and stayed there a moment. "That's hers."

"Yes."

"It's not pointing north."

"It's tired. It's earned it."

Tomas laughed, and then winced, and then laughed again more carefully. He'd brought a paper bag, from which he produced a jar of his mother's salsa, a surveyor's pencil, and a small brass hook. "For the compass. So it hangs straight. The nail's crooked—didn't you notice? Everything about that compass has been slightly crooked since the day I met you, so it seemed fitting, but still."

"You met me when I was dragging you out of a boulder field on a litter made of ski poles and regret."

"And I remember it fondly." He held up the hook. "May I?"

She fetched the compass down and he screwed the hook in, tested it with a tug, and hung the instrument so it hung true and level, needle swinging in its slow tired arcs. They both looked at it.

"Okafor says the basin nomination went through committee," Tomas said. "Preliminary protection, pending the final survey."

"I heard."

"She also says the consortium's appeal is dead. Vess's testimony did most of it. He talks now, apparently. In the hearings. Very thoroughly. Like a man who has discovered that talking is the one thing they can't take away from him." Tomas turned the jar of salsa in his hands. "He asked about you. At the end. Whether you got down okay."

Cyrus was quiet for a moment. "What did you tell him?"

"That you got down okay." He shrugged. "It's true. That's the whole point of you, isn't it? You get down okay."

* * *

Okafor came up the next week, ostensibly to deliver a copy of the protection order but actually, Cyrus suspected, to stand on the porch and drink coffee and look at the mountain, which was a thing the two of them had discovered they could do together in comfortable silence, a rare and durable form of friendship.

She was in uniform, which she almost never wore up the trail, and she stood with her hands on her hips looking at the woodpile with an assessor's eye.

"That's a winter's worth."

"That's a winter's worth and a half. I got carried away."

"First winter does that to people. They stack wood like they're building an ark." She accepted coffee. They stood. The mountain did its mountain thing—cloud moving off the high basin, the new snowfields up top going pink, then white again, indifferent and gorgeous.

"Read the order?" Okafor asked.

"Twice."

"Your mother's name's in it. Section four. The documentation basis." Okafor said it lightly, but she was watching Cyrus the way she watched everything, sideways and kindly. "Maren Brightwater. It's going to be on signs now. At the trailhead. *Protected basin, established on the basis of field documentation by Maren Brightwater.* Kids are going to read it and not know who she was, and some of them are going to go find out."

Cyrus looked at her coffee. "She'd have hated the sign. She hated signs. She said they were how the mountain got furniture."

"And yet."

"And yet." Cyrus smiled. "She'd have hated it, and then she'd have gone up and checked that they'd spelled her name right, and then she'd have complained about the font."

"That's the spirit." Okafor drained her cup, and then, at the edge of the porch, with the particular casualness that Cyrus had learned meant the question had been carried up the whole trail on purpose: "So. The contract runs through winter."

"It does."

"You're staying."

It was not really a question, but it was not not a question, and Cyrus understood that it deserved better than a shrug. She set down her cup. She thought about saying something clever, something deflected and light, the old machinery of it, one foot already out the door even standing still—and she felt the machinery try to engage, and she felt it find nothing to grip. The door was behind her. It had a crooked nail by it, with a tired compass hanging straight from a brass hook, and there was nowhere on earth she needed to be that wasn't here.

"Yes," Cyrus said.

Just that. One syllable. It weighed less than she'd expected. Most of the things she'd spent ten years not saying had turned out, once said, to be nearly weightless—the whole freight of them had been in the carrying.

Okafor nodded once, satisfied, and started down the trail, and then turned back. "The station does a solstice thing. Terrible food, worse singing, everyone's invited. Tomas is bringing the salsa."

"I'll bring wood."

"Bring yourself. We've got wood." She waved without turning around and went down into the trees, and Cyrus stood on the porch a while longer, in the gold light, in the quiet, with the mountain in front of her and the door behind her and her mother's compass hanging between them, pointing, in its slow and tired way, at all of it.

Then she went inside, banked the stove, and got out her mother's field notes—her own copies now, dog-eared, coffee-ringed, alive—and turned to the last blank page, and picked up a pencil, and began, in the margin at the top, in careful letters, to write the date.
