## 2. What the River Owes

**Lior**

The backchannels behind Sallow Bend were the kind of water no chart bothered with—flooded orchards, drowned fences, a church steeple poking up like a broken tooth. Lior threaded the *Kestrel* through them at half-throttle, running without lights, and told herself the knot in her chest was just the current.

The stranger slept in the wheelhouse, wrapped in Lior's only dry blanket, shivering out whatever the river had nearly finished with her. The seal sat in the chart drawer under a coil of spare line, and Lior could feel it the way you feel a stone in your boot. Heavy. Present. Wrong.

She'd had it in her hands for maybe ten seconds before the patrol boat's lantern swept the cave mouth. Ten seconds of turning that wax-and-brass disc over, reading letters worn almost smooth: *THE RIVER SPEAKS WITH ONE VOICE.* Then the light, then the shout, then the stranger grabbing her wrist and saying *run* in a voice cracked from swallowing half the Vessin.

So she'd run. With a body aboard. With a warrant aboard—the woman had been clutching it like a rosary, paper sealed and signed, ordering the recovery of the Magistrate's Seal by any means necessary.

Lior had spent nine years being nobody. It took one tide to end it.

She killed the engine and let the *Kestrel* drift into the shadow of a flooded boathouse, its roof just below the surface, and waited for dawn the way you wait for a tooth to finish aching.

The stranger woke at first light, looked around at the drowned orchard, and said, "You didn't report me."

"I don't report things," Lior said. "Reporting is how people find you."

Something moved across the woman's face—not quite a smile. "Sable Voss," she said. "Formerly of the Magistrate's Court. Formerly." She said it like a place she'd burned down behind her.

"Lior Iversen. Cargo. Only cargo."

"Nobody runs only cargo up the Vessin in low season, Iversen."

"Nobody who wants to stay unremembered does."

Sable sat up too fast and winced, pressing a hand to her ribs. "Then we have a problem, Captain, because as of last night, everyone on this river remembers your boat."

---

**Sable**

She'd rehearsed this conversation for eleven years, in bathhouses and border queues and the grey hours before sleep, and every version ended with her explaining herself so well that forgiveness arrived on schedule. Standing in the wheelhouse of a leaking riverboat, wrapped in a stranger's blanket, watching the stranger's face, Sable understood that no version survived contact.

"The seal is a writ of authority," she said. "Older than the province. Whoever bears it speaks with the full legal power of the drowned Magistrate's Court—tolls, arrests, pardons, sentences. All of it. Corvane has spent six years building an empire of tollgates on nothing but intimidation and forged paperwork. That seal makes his empire real—or makes someone else's."

"And the warrant?"

"Mine. I wrote it. Before—" She stopped. Started again. "I found evidence in the court archives. Corvane's toll ledgers cross-referenced against conviction records. Forgeries. Dozens of convictions built on documents he authored or altered. I pulled the warrant for the seal's recovery because a seal in Corvane's hands ends any chance of justice forever. And then he found out I'd found it, and I went into the river instead of into his cells."

Lior hadn't blinked through any of it. That was worse than anger. "Why were you in the cave?"

"Because the archives say the seal was lost there when the old court flooded. Because it was the only move left." Sable's voice came out flat. She hated how flat. "Because I've spent eleven years being the clerk who sentenced your father, Iversen, and I wanted to fix one thing before the river finished me."

There it was. The flinch. Small, but she'd spent her career reading faces across a sentencing desk, and she caught it clean.

"You knew my name," Lior said quietly.

"I processed four hundred convictions in nine years at the Vessin bench. I remember all of them." She held the other woman's stare. "Tomas Iversen. Smuggling. Evidence presented by Harbormaster's Office, countersigned by me. He got seven years and died in the second one, and the evidence was Corvane's forgery, and I stamped it anyway because the paperwork was *clean*, Iversen. That's what I am. I'm the person who checks whether the paperwork is clean."

The silence stretched long enough that Sable heard the river lapping the hull—and heard something else under it. A rhythm that wasn't the river.

---

**Lior**

The leak announced itself the way leaks do: late, and all at once. Three inches of black water sloshing in the bilge, cold around Lior's ankles while she tore open the floor panels.

"Hull seam, port side," she said. "We hit something in the dark coming out of the channel."

"The storm debris." Sable was already moving, shedding the blanket, rolling her sleeves like a woman who'd done deck work before. "Where's your patch kit?"

"Under the wheelhouse. There's tar, canvas, a clamp." Lior glanced up. "You've bailed a boat before."

"I grew up on a dredge barge before the court recruited me. You don't forget which end of a boat sinks first." Sable knelt at the bilge, scooping water with a bucket, efficient and unglamorous, and Lior felt something in her chest shift sideways and resented it.

They worked in the grey drizzle, passing tools without discussion, Sable holding the clamp while Lior set it, Liable holding the canvas while Sable painted tar in strokes so even they looked machine-made. The water slowed. Stopped. Somewhere in the middle of it Lior stopped thinking of her as *the clerk* and started thinking of her as *hands*, and hated that too, and kept working.

"There," Sable said finally, sitting back on her heels, tar to the elbows. "She'll hold."

"She'll hold until she doesn't. Everything holds until it doesn't." Lior sat down across from her, both of them filthy, breathing hard. "Why tell me about my father? You could've lied. I'd have never known."

"Because you'd have known eventually, and I've learned what happens when Corvane's version of events arrives first." Sable wiped her hands on the blanket, ruining it further. "He'll have a bounty out by now. On the seal, on me, probably on you. His version will be everywhere by nightfall. I wanted you to hear mine while it was still free."

Lior looked at her—at the court-trained posture slowly collapsing into exhaustion, at the hands that had stamped the paper and now wore tar like everyone else's. Eleven years of hating a signature. It turned out signatures didn't look like anything.

"My father always said the river keeps honest books," Lior said. "Even when nobody else does."

"Your father was right about the river." Sable's voice cracked, just slightly. "It was the people who lied."

---

**Lior**

Dusk brought the answer to a question neither of them had asked aloud. A fishing skiff slid past their hiding spot at dusk, its owner—a weathered woman Lior had traded with for years, who'd never once looked twice at the *Kestrel*—cupped her hands and called across the water:

"They posted it at the Tollgate, Iversen! Fifty crowns for the boat's location, five hundred for the seal, a thousand for the clerk alive!" And then, quieter, almost kindly: "I saw nothing. But others won't be blind."

The skiff moved off into the rain. Lior stood at the rail a long time.

"A thousand alive," Sable said behind her. "Corvane wants witnesses silenced properly. Trial records can be rewritten; bodies are harder."

"He doesn't want you silenced. He wants you *countersigning* again." Lior turned. "A conviction with your stamp on it would legitimize everything he's built. That's why you're worth more than the seal to him."

Sable went very still. "Yes," she said. "That's exactly why."

"Then we don't run anymore." Lior heard herself say it and didn't take it back. "Running is his game. He owns the channels, the tolls, the informants. We hide, we die tired." She pointed south, toward the distant lights of the Tollgate fortress, blazing through the rain like a wound in the dark. "Monsoon peaks in three days. Every boat on the river will be forced through his gates for storm shelter. Every boatfolk family standing in his courtyard."

"What are you suggesting?"

"That a seal isn't the only thing that speaks with authority." Lior met her eyes. "You said the paperwork was clean when you convicted my father. So make new paperwork. Cleanest there ever was. Testimony, notarized, copies distributed before he can burn them—and I'll stand in his own courtyard and say my father's name out loud where every boatfolk on the river can hear it."

Sable stared at her. Rain drummed the roof. And slowly, terribly, she began to smile—the smile of a clerk who has just found the flaw in a document she'd been ordered not to read.

"He'll search every boat coming through the gate," she said. "Which means the seal can't come through the gate with us."

"No," Lior agreed. "It can't."

They looked at each other across the ruined blanket and the patched floor and eleven years of river between them, and somewhere out in the dark, thunder rolled down the Vessin like a drumbeat counting down.
