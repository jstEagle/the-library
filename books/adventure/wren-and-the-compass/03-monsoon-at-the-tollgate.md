## 3. Monsoon at the Tollgate

Sable had spent eleven years learning how the law moved, and it moved like water—always downhill, always toward the path of least resistance, always pooling in the low places where power could sit and grow stagnant. Corvane's toll fortress was exactly that: a low place. A squat stone fist at the narrows where the Vessin squeezed between two cliffs, built to catch everything the river carried and tax it for passing through.

Now the river was trying to take the fortress back.

The monsoon had come up the valley two days early, black and howling, and the Vessin had risen six feet in a night. From the reed bank where she crouched with the seal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, Sable watched the flood hammer the fortress's lower gate. Water poured through the toll arches like the river had decided to collect its own fees.

*You have one chance,* she told herself. *One. And it's the worst one.*

Lior was already inside.

That was the part she couldn't stop replaying. It had been Lior's plan—of course it had, Lior's plans always sounded reasonable right up until they were suicidal. *Corvane wants the seal. So we give him a Lior instead.* Walk in, let herself be caught, keep his eyes and his patrol boats fixed on the prize he thought he'd won while Sable slipped down the flooded millrace with the real seal and the testimony ledger they'd salvaged from the drowned court archive.

*You're the clerk,* Lior had said, pressing the oilcloth bundle into her hands. Rain running off her chin, that crooked half-smile she wore when she was most afraid. *Clerks carry paper. Nobody stops a clerk carrying paper.*

*I stopped people,* Sable had said. *That was my whole job.*

*Then stop him.*

Now, through the rain, Sable watched torchlight move along the fortress battlements. Watched them bring Lior out onto the wet flagstones of the toll yard, hands bound, two guards on either arm, and Corvane standing above her on the toll platform with his grey cloak plastered to his shoulders and his face lit from below like a man performing his own statue.

The yard was not empty. That was the thing Corvane hadn't counted on—the monsoon had stranded half the river fleet against his walls, dozens of boatfolk huddled under tarps and awnings, watching. Watching everything.

Sable's hand found the oilcloth bundle. Inside, beneath the seal itself, she'd tucked the ledger pages: the toll records in Corvane's own hand, the shipping manifests altered to match, and—she'd checked the watermark three times with her clerk's glass—the original conviction documents for the Vessin docket of eleven years ago. Forged. All of them. Including one bearing her own signature, applied by a younger woman who had believed the paper in front of her was true.

*Iversen, Toma. Smuggling. Sentenced: forfeiture of vessel, ten years' labor.*

Lior's father. The sentence Sable had read aloud in a courtroom that smelled of lamp oil, while a girl of fourteen sat in the gallery learning exactly what the river did to people who were visible.

The rain came down harder. Corvane raised his arms, and his voice carried over the yard, pitched to reach the boats.

"People of the Vessin! You've all heard the rumors. That a seal has surfaced. That the drowned court's authority lives again." He let the murmur run, then cut it off with a gesture. "It's a lie. There is no seal. And this—" he gestured at Lior, "—is the smuggler who's been selling you that lie. But I am not without mercy. Bring me what she claims to carry, and she walks free. That is my offer. That is the law."

*That is a lie,* Sable thought. *And I am the only person on this river with the paper to prove it.*

She should go. That was the plan. Down the millrace, upstream, gone, and Lior's sacrifice buys the evidence safe passage. Lior had been very clear. *You don't come back for me. You swear it, Sable. Swear it on your father's court.*

She had sworn it.

She looked at the bundle in her hands. Then at the platform, where Lior stood in the rain with her chin up and her eyes sweeping the crowd—looking, Sable realized with a jolt, not for escape but for faces. Counting the boatfolk. Memorizing them. Even now, even bound, Lior Iversen was working out how to make her capture mean something.

*You swore it on a court,* Sable thought. *But the court was a lie. Every page of it.*

She stood up out of the reeds.

* * *

The water was up to Lior's knees on the platform, and rising. Corvane had been talking for ten minutes, and she'd stopped listening after the third lie. What she was doing was watching the crowd—old Maren from the eel boats, the Ferro twins, the whole ragged congregation the storm had pinned against Corvane's walls. If she was going to hang for this, the crowd was the jury. Corvane had made a mistake, gathering them. He wanted witnesses to his mercy. But witnesses worked both directions.

"So," Corvane said, descending to stand in front of her, close enough that she could smell the wet wool and the ink on his breath. "Where is it?"

"Where's what?"

"The seal." He smiled the smile he used on debtors. "You have until the water reaches my knees to decide. After that, the offer drowns with the yard, and you drown with it—slowly, and officially, as a smuggler resisting arrest. I have the paperwork." He patted his coat. "I always have the paperwork."

"You would," Lior said. "You wrote it."

Something flickered in his face. "Careful."

"Eleven years ago." Lior raised her voice—not to Corvane, to the yard. To the boats. "Eleven years ago, Toma Iversen was convicted of smuggling on this river. Anybody remember Toma? Ran cargo out of Ferro's Landing. Never paid a toll in his life because there weren't any tolls then."

The murmur ran through the crowd like current under ice.

"The evidence against him was a manifest. One page. And I have seen the archive copy of that page, and the watermark on it comes from the Harbormaster's own press—the same press that prints Corvane's toll receipts. The same hand. The same ink." She turned to Corvane. "You didn't catch my father, harbormaster. You invented him."

"Guards—" Corvane started.

"Check the docket!" Lior's voice cracked across the rain. "Every conviction from the drowned court, the last five years of it! Check who signed the manifests, check who collected the forfeited boats—check whose fleet got fat while yours rotted at anchor! You've been taxing this river with a dead court's forged seal for a decade, and the only real seal on the Vessin is the one you're drowning the river to find!"

The water hit Corvane's thighs. He didn't seem to notice. His face had gone the color of the flood, and his eyes weren't on Lior anymore—they were moving across the crowd, reading it, and what he read there was clearly not what he'd expected. Maren had stood up. The Ferro twins had stood up.

"Take her below," Corvane said quietly. "Drown the offer."

The guards moved. Lior braced—she'd get one arm free, maybe, one good—

And then a voice from the top of the toll stairs, thin and clear and absolutely official, cut through the rain:

"By authority of the Magistrate's Court of the Vessin, in session: Harbormaster Corvane, you are named in a bill of indictment, eleven counts, forgery and fraudulent conviction. The court's seal is present and verified. Stand down your guards."

Every head turned.

Sable stood at the top of the stairs, soaked through, holding the oilcloth bundle open in both hands, rain streaming down her face. Behind her, impossibly, half a dozen stranded clerks from the flooded archive—witnesses, notarized and shivering.

Corvane stared at her. "You," he said. "You signed my convictions. You're in my ledger as deep as I am. You think they'll take your word over mine?"

"No," Sable said. "I think they'll take the paper." She held up the ledger pages, and her voice shook only once. "I signed them because I believed them. That's the difference between us, Corvane. You forged the truth. I only failed to see it. And I've spent eleven years learning to see." She looked down the stairs, at Lior, water to their waists now, guards frozen mid-grab. "I swore I wouldn't come back for her. I'm breaking that oath. Consider it the last dishonest thing I ever did under your law."

The guards let go of Lior. Not because they chose to—because Maren and the Ferro twins and half the soaked crowd had come up out of the boats and were standing between them and the stairs.

Corvane looked at the water, at the crowd, at the seal in Sable's hands. For the first time in eleven years, Harbormaster Corvane had no paperwork for this.

The flood took his knees.

Lior waded toward the stairs, toward Sable, grinning through the rain. "You came back."

"I'm a clerk," Sable said. "I correct errors."

Below them, the river rose another inch—and somewhere in the churning water at the yard's edge, something glinted. The seal's chain, torn loose in the scramble, sliding off the platform into the flood.

Both of them saw it. Neither of them moved.

The river had the seal now. And the river, as every soul on the Vessin knew, always gave back what it took—eventually, and on its own terms.
