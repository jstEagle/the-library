## 4. High Water

The rain stopped at dawn, and the river kept rising anyway.

Lior stood on the deck of the *Wren* and watched the Vessin come back to itself. All season it had been a drained animal, ribs of mud showing through its skin, and now the monsoon's last breath had filled it to the banks and past them. The toll fortress at Grayharbor sat half-drowned, its lower floors swallowed, its great iron chain—the one Corvane had used to strangle every boat that passed—cut loose and drifting in tangled coils like something dead.

On the flooded quay below, clerks in gray robes were leading Harrow Corvane away.

He was not struggling. That was the strange part. Lior had imagined this moment a hundred times during the long weeks of hiding—imagined him raging, bargaining, clawing—but Corvane walked between his own clerks with his hands bound in front of him and his chin up, like a man being escorted rather than arrested. Only once did he stop, turning his head until he found Lior on the water. He looked for a long moment. Then he smiled, small and knowing, as if to say: *you'll see what it costs.*

Then they took him inside the customs house, and the door closed, and the river went on rising.

"You're staring," Sable said.

"I'm counting," Lior said. "Boats."

They had come all night, through the tail end of the storm, answering the call Sable had sent out on every channel the boatfolk still trusted—a notarized testimony, sealed with her own restored clerk's mark, naming Corvane's forged convictions one by one. Forty-one names. Forty-one boats seized or sunk or bled dry by invented crimes. And now they were here, dozens of them, riding high on the flood: mussel dredgers and timber rafts, eel-boats and grain barges, their lanterns still lit against the gray morning, a constellation scattered across the drowned harbor.

The fleet had come to see justice done. Now it was done, and no one seemed to know what came next.

Sable stood beside Lior at the rail, her court robes gone, replaced by oilskins three sizes too big that she'd refused to take off even when they dried. In her hands she carried the warrant case, and inside the warrant case, wrapped in waxed linen, was the seal of the drowned Magistrate's Court.

"Forty-one," she said quietly. "I keep thinking about the number. I sentenced eleven of them myself. I thought I was doing my job." She turned the case over in her hands. "Corvane forged the evidence. But I held the pen."

"I know," Lior said.

"That's supposed to be forgiveness?"

"It's supposed to be true. The rest is yours to carry." Lior looked at her. "You carried it here. That counts for something."

Sable didn't answer. Out on the water, someone began to sing—an old hauling song, low and wordless—and other voices took it up, boat by boat, until the whole harbor hummed with it.

* * *

They opened the warrant case at midday, on the deck of the *Wren*, because the boatfolk asked them to.

Not demanded—asked. A delegation of captains came alongside in a skiff: old Maren Kost from the dredger *Saltwife*, whose husband's name was third on Sable's list; the twins Osk and Pell, who'd run grain past Corvane's tollgates for years; a boy of maybe fifteen whose father had drowned in a seized boat two winters back. They climbed aboard dripping and solemn, and Maren said, "Show us. We want to see it with our own eyes."

So Lior unwrapped the linen, and there it was: the seal of the Magistrate's Court, bronze and green with river age, heavy as a sin. By law older than anyone could remember, its bearer held full authority over the Vessin and every tributary—tolls, courts, patrols, everything. Whoever held it *was* the river's law.

The boy stared at it like it might bite. "That's it? That little thing caused all this?"

"Little things usually do," Sable said.

Maren looked at Lior. So did the twins. So did the whole fleet, Lior realized—every deck within sight had people standing at rails now, watching, waiting. The singing had stopped.

"They'll want you to take it," Maren said. Not accusing. Just honest. "Some of them already do. There's talk. *Captain of the Seal.* You'd be fair, they say. Fairer than any magistrate the capital ever sent us."

Lior's hand hovered over the bronze. It was warm from the sun, or maybe Lior imagined that. Weeks ago, in a sea cave with the tide draining out around them, Lior had picked this thing up out of pure curiosity, and the world had ended—the invisibility that had taken years to build, gone in an afternoon. Hunted. Leaking. Cornered in a storm at a tollgate with chains on both wrists.

And yet.

Lior thought about what forty-one names meant. Thought about the boy's father, and Maren's husband, and the empty berths all down the river where boats used to be. Thought about how much good one fair hand could do, holding real power, on a river that had never once known it.

And then Lior thought about Corvane's smile. *You'll see what it costs.* Not a threat. A warning from a man who'd started out wanting to fix things too.

"Lior," Sable said softly. She hadn't touched the seal either. All this time, she'd left it to Lior—as if she knew better than anyone that the person holding it last would be the person defined by it.

Lior lifted it. It was heavier than it looked. Power always is.

"The law says whoever holds this governs the river," Lior said, loud enough to carry across the water. "Every captain here knows it. Half of you would follow me tomorrow if I claimed it."

Nobody argued. That silence was worse than argument.

"But I've spent my whole life learning how to stay invisible on this river. How to be too small to matter. And I learned something else, too, these last few months." Lior turned the seal over once, feeling its weight shift. "Power isn't a thing you hold. It's a thing that holds *you*. Corvane didn't steal authority. Authority stole Corvane. And I am not going to let a piece of bronze decide who I am."

Lior stepped to the rail, out over the floodwater, and held the seal above the river.

The whole harbor went still. Even the wind seemed to wait.

"This river doesn't need a master," Lior called out. "It needs captains. It needs clerks who check the ledgers. It needs neighbors who watch each other's moorings. Everything we need, we already have—we just forgot, because it was easier to let one man with a chain do our remembering for us." Lior's arm trembled, just slightly, under the weight. "So I'm giving the law back to the water. Where it came from. Where nobody can forge it."

And let it go.

The seal dropped, turning once, catching the light—bronze flashing green-gold against the brown flood—and then the river took it, the way the river takes everything, without ceremony, without thanks. The rings of water spread outward and flattened, and it was gone.

For a long moment, nothing. Then Maren Kost threw back her head and laughed—a rough, cracked sound, half grief—and started the hauling song again, louder than before, and this time the words came with it, old verses about the river owing nothing and owning nothing, and the fleet sang it until the banks rang.

The boy came up to Lior afterward, awkward, twisting his cap in his hands. "My da used to say you'd never stop moving. That you'd run cargo till the day you died because stopping meant belonging to something, and belonging meant losing it." He shrugged. "Guess he was wrong."

"Guess he was," Lior said, and found that the words didn't hurt.

* * *

Evening came down gold and calm. The flood was beginning its slow retreat; you could see it in the mud lines creeping down the quay stones, inch by inch, the river exhaling.

Lior and Sable sat on the cabin roof of the *Wren* with tea going cold between them, watching the lanterns of the fleet come out one by one across the harbor. Somewhere a fiddle had started up. The customs house windows glowed with clerks working late—notarizing restitution orders, Sable said, for every one of the forty-one. She'd go back tomorrow. Back to the ledgers, back to the work of unmaking what she'd helped make. She'd asked Lior, very carefully, whether that was a road worth walking alone.

It wasn't a question, really. Neither of them pretended it was.

"So," Sable said. "No seal. No bounty. No one hunting you anymore." She sipped her tea. "What does a captain do with a quiet river?"

Lior considered it. Considered the *Wren* beneath them, patched and scarred and seaworthy. Considered the low-season runs ahead, honest cargo, honest tolls—or no tolls at all, now, on the free stretches. Considered staying put through a whole winter for the first time in fifteen years, tied up at Grayharbor, close enough to walk to the customs house in the mornings.

"I hear the mussel beds upstream need ferrying," Lior said. "Come spring."

"That's months away."

"I know." Lior leaned back against the cabin wall and watched the first stars come out over the water. "I'm not going anywhere."

Sable laughed—quiet, surprised, like she'd been braced for anything except that—and settled her shoulder against Lior's, and the river ran dark and full beneath them, carrying nothing but boats.
