## 1. The Weight of a Borrowed Compass

The notice came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for laundry and ledger-balancing, for the small honest arithmetic of a life that adds up. A Tuesday was no day to learn your brother had died owing eleven hundred dollars to men who signed their letters with a law firm's name because even their cruelty wore a collar.

I read it twice at the kitchen table while my tea went cold, the way I'd read the telegram four months ago. *MISSING, PRESUMED LOST, MOUNTAIN DIVISION SURVEY.* Four months of presuming, and now this: not a body, not a grave, but a bill.

*To the estate of Jonah Amberdale,* it began, as if there were an estate. As if there were anything but me, sitting in the house we'd grown up in, drinking tea out of the chipped cup he'd always claimed because it held more.

Eleven hundred dollars. I did the math the way you do when grief needs something to chew on. Eleven hundred dollars was two years of my engineer's salary before they took my license. It was forty tons of rail. It was, apparently, what my brother's life weighed on somebody's scale, and the number came back light.

There was a second sheet beneath the notice. A parcel slip. *Held effects may be collected at the office of Harrow & Voss, Attorneys, 14 Cordwainer Street.*

I should have burned the slip. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. I sat there holding it and thinking, *burn it, Wren, let them keep their paper, let his name die clean instead of owing* — and then I thought about his name dying at all, and my hand was already reaching for my coat.

That's the thing about inheritance nobody warns you about. It isn't money. It's a hook.

* * *

Cordwainer Street smelled like tallow and wet wool, the way all money streets smell when the weather turns — like the city itself is being rendered down into something useful. Harrow & Voss occupied the ground floor of a building whose upper floors had opinions about themselves: carved stone, tall windows, men in good hats going up stairs to become slightly better men.

The office below was different. The office below was where the building kept its teeth.

A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took my name without looking up, wrote it in a book that already looked full of other people's bad news, and told me to wait. I waited twenty minutes, which I understood was not an accident. Twenty minutes is how long it takes a person to decide whether she's the kind who walks out or the kind who stays. I stayed. I noted, with some professional displeasure, that the chairs were angled so you couldn't see the door from where they put you. Somebody had thought about that. Somebody got paid to think about that.

When they finally called me through, it wasn't Harrow. It was a man I'd later learn to call Mr. Quill, though at the time he was just a gray suit with a voice like dry paper.

"Miss Amberdale." He didn't offer a hand. "Thank you for coming promptly."

"I wasn't aware I'd been given a choice."

"Everyone has choices." He opened a drawer — a real drawer, oak, the kind of furniture meant to outlive its contents — and set three things on the desk between us.

A compass. A folded map, tied with string. And a single sheet of paper he kept his own hand resting on, the way a man rests a hand on a dog that might bite.

"The compass first," he said, and slid it toward me.

I knew it before I touched it. Brass case, dinged on the north side where Jonah had dropped it off the trestle at Miller's Bend our first summer working the line together, back when we were both employed and both believed in the railroad the way you believe in a church you were raised in. He'd carried it every survey since. Our father's before him. The needle still swung true; I checked, because checking instruments is the one prayer I never lost.

"Where did you get this?"

"It was surrendered to us," Quill said, "as collateral."

"Surrendered by whom?"

"That's not the relevant question, Miss Amberdale. The relevant question is whether you intend to honor your brother's obligations."

I picked up the compass. It was warm, which meant someone had been holding it recently, and I found that detail more obscene than the debt itself. Some stranger's hands on my father's brass. "My brother is missing. Presumed lost. There's a word for collecting debts from dead men."

"There are several words," Quill agreed mildly. "Most of them appear in contracts. Which brings us to the map."

He untied the string himself, unhurried, and unfolded the paper flat. And I'll be honest with you, because lying to strangers is easy but lying to myself has gotten expensive: for one moment I forgot the debt, the suit, the whole tallow-smelling room. Because I knew maps the way Quill knew contracts, and this one was wrong in ways that made my hands itch.

It was a mountain division chart — the high lines, the ones the company had closed and struck from the public timetables thirty years ago. But it wasn't any official survey. The linework was hand-inked over a printed base, corrections in a cramped left-slanting script I'd have known anywhere, because I'd watched Jonah develop that slant copying our father's field notes at this same kitchen table. He hadn't drawn this map. He'd *finished* it. There were annotations running up the closed corridors like veins: elevations, bearings, dates. Dates going back two years. Dates from after he stopped writing to me.

And in the upper corner, where a title block would go, instead of a route name there was a single line in his handwriting:

*They only closed the tunnels. The mountain didn't agree.*

"What is this?" I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"A record of your brother's work," Quill said. "Work performed under contract. Work for which he was advanced funds. Eleven hundred dollars, as itemized." His hand moved to the third paper at last, turning it so I could read it right-side up. "The note is now due. The estate has thirty days."

"He's not dead."

"Presumed lost," Quill said, with the gentle precision of a man quoting scripture he didn't believe. "The law is patient with the missing, Miss Amberdale. Creditors are less so. Thirty days. After that, the note transfers to parties less inclined to patience, and the collateral—" he tapped the map, once, lightly, "—is forfeit. Along with whatever else the estate holds."

"Which is me."

"I wouldn't presume to characterize—"

"You just did. In furniture."

For the first time something moved behind his face, and I realized with a lurch that it might have been respect. That was somehow worse.

"Thirty days," he repeated. He slid the compass and the map across the desk together, and I noticed he did not slide the note. The note stayed under his hand. Of course it did. You don't give the hostage its own ransom letter. "Sign here acknowledging receipt of effects."

I signed. My signature came out ugly, which I chose to interpret as honesty.

At the door he said, quietly, almost kindly: "Your brother was mapping the closed lines for us, Miss Amberdale. He was very good at it. Whatever he found up there — well. Debts of that kind are rarely settled in dollars."

I stood in the doorway long enough for him to understand I'd heard every word, and then I walked out into the rain with my brother's compass in my pocket and his handwriting against my ribs, and I did not cry until I was three streets away, and then only briefly, and standing up, like a professional.

* * *

That night I cleared the kitchen table for the first time since the telegram. Laundry basket to the floor. Ledger to the shelf. I unrolled the map and weighted its corners with the salt cellar, the teapot, the chipped cup, and the compass, and I lit the lamp and read it the way I used to read track diagrams — bottom up, origin to destination, the way a train thinks.

Here is what I know about closed lines, and I know more than most, because closing lines was the last job they let me do before they took my license. A line gets closed for one of three reasons. It doesn't pay. It can't be maintained. Or it killed somebody, and somebody paid to make the killing quiet. The first two get you a notice in the gazette. The third gets you sealed tunnel mouths and a map pulled from every drawer in the division office and men like Quill keeping the only copies under their hands.

Jonah's annotations climbed the map like a man climbing. Every closed corridor had his dates beside it, and the dates weren't random — they marched. Two years of marching, season by season, higher each time, until the last annotation sat above the treeline entirely, on a glacier no railroad ever touched, marked with a bearing and a single word in his slanting hand:

*Ice cave. Ask her to come.*

Not *tell her*. Not *show her*. *Ask her to come*, as if he'd known I'd be reading it alone at a table with a lamp turned up too bright, and had decided, in advance, to leave the choice with me. That was Jonah all over. He could spend two years lying to his sister and still manage, at the end of it, to hand her the pen.

I sat there a long time. The lamp hissed. Outside, the first real snow of the season started coming down, fat and indifferent, already burying the year.

Eleven hundred dollars. Thirty days. A brother presumed lost, mapping lines that powerful men wanted closed twice — once with steel doors, and once, it seemed, with silence.

I never wanted the inheritance. I want that understood. I wanted my license back and my name out of the papers and a small house somewhere the trains passed at a polite distance. But a name doesn't die of owing. It dies of nobody carrying it forward, and I am, whatever else is true, my father's daughter and Jonah's sister, and I do not put down instruments that still swing true.

I picked up the compass. North-side ding and all. I set it on the map, and the needle swung, wavered, and settled — pointing not along any line Jonah had drawn, but up. Into the closed country. Above the treeline.

In the morning there was a knock at my door, and the man on the step wore a government survey badge and a face like a man carrying news he'd rehearsed and hated, and he said, "Miss Amberdale? They've gone missing. The whole team. And I'm told you're the only person alive who knows those lines."

I looked past him at the snow falling on Cordwainer Street, on Harrow & Voss, on thirty days ticking down like a fuse, and I heard myself say:

"How fast can you brief me?"
