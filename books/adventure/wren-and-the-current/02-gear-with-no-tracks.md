## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The telegram came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Bad news should have the decency to arrive at night, in the rain, with some ceremony. Instead it came at breakfast, folded into a yellow slip that smelled of machine oil, and I read it twice while my coffee went cold.

*GOVERNMENT SURVEY TEAM FAILING TO REPORT. POSITION: ABOVE TREELINE, KETTLE RANGE. RECOVERY PARTY FORMING. YOUR KNOWLEDGE OF CLOSED LINES REQUIRED. COMPENSATION OFFERED.*

Compensation offered. As if grief were a line item. But rent was rent, and the thirty days Voss's men had given me were already burning down like a fuse, and a man named Aldous Crane from the Interior Survey Office was offering to pay me — pay *me*, Wren Amberdale, whose name still smelled of smoke from the Ashford Junction inquiry — to walk back into mountains I'd sworn off.

I told myself it was the money. That was easier than admitting what I actually felt when I read the coordinates in the second telegram: a cold, bright recognition, like hearing your own name called in a crowd of strangers.

The survey team had been working the high route. Jonah's route. The closed lines.

* * *

Crane met me at the trailhead with a handshake that lasted half a second too long and a look that said he'd read my file and found it entertaining. He was forty-ish, square-built, with the particular sunburn of a man who spends his summers at altitude and his winters apologizing for it.

"Miss Amberdale." His breath fogged. "I'll be honest — you weren't my first choice."

"Nobody's first choice is a disgraced engineer."

"You'd be surprised. Disgrace is just experience with the failure modes." He handed me a clipboard. "Six men and one woman. Dr. Suri Okonkwo, glaciology. They went up on the fourteenth to run levels along the old right-of-way above the treeline. Last check-in was the nineteenth. Nothing since. Weather's been clear as glass for six days."

"Clear as glass," I repeated. "And no one went up after them?"

"We did. A constable and two volunteers made it to the lower camp yesterday." Crane's jaw worked. "That's why you're here. Come see."

We climbed through the morning, through pine that gave way to scrub that gave way to nothing but rock and lichen and the white shoulder of the range rising ahead of us like a held breath. The constable — a young man named Pell who kept touching his hat brim as if checking it was still there — met us at the tree line and would go no further.

"It's not right up there," he said. Not dramatic. Just factual, the way a man reports a broken rail. "Camp's fine. Everything's fine. That's what's not right."

* * *

He was correct, and correctness has never frightened me so much.

The camp sat in a shallow bowl below the glacier's snout, three tents pitched with textbook neatness, guy-lines taut, snow skirts anchored. The cook fire had burned down to ash in its stone ring. And everything was there.

Everything.

Rucksacks leaned against tent walls, packed and buckled. The mess kit hung from its tripod, washed and dried. In the largest tent, six bedrolls lay rolled and strapped, six pairs of spare boots stood in a row beneath the gear shelf, and the instruments — theodolite, levels, the brass-cased barometers — sat in their cases on the folding table, padded and latched, exactly as if the team had stepped out for an hour and expected to return before dark.

Coats. That was the detail that got under my skin and stayed there. Six heavy parkas hung on the ridge line, frost-stiffened, sleeves swinging faintly in a wind too small to move anything else. Whoever walked out of this camp — or didn't — had walked out without their coats, into country where the temperature dropped forty degrees between supper and dawn.

"They didn't leave," I said. "You don't leave a place like this without your coat unless—"

"Unless they left in a hurry," Crane said.

"No." I crouched by the fire ring. The ash was undisturbed, a perfect grey cone. Around it, the snow lay smooth as poured cream, unbroken from the tents to the edge of the bowl in every direction. "A hurry leaves tracks. A hurry knocks over the mess kit, tears a tent flap, drops something. This isn't a hurry. This is—" I stood, turned a slow circle. "This is a room where everyone stood up at once and the mountain closed over them like water."

Dr. Okonkwo's kit was in the third tent. I knew her name from the manifest; I hadn't known she kept a field journal in a waterproof case, or that she'd been logging ice cores along the old right-of-way, or that her last entry — dated the nineteenth, the day of the final check-in — ended mid-sentence:

*Core sample at 40 meters shows stratification inconsistent with natural accumulation. There's a layer down there that doesn't belong. Tomorrow we move up to the serac line to trace it. If it runs where I think it—*

The sentence simply stopped. Ink unblotted. Pen set down — the pen was there, capped, lying parallel to the journal as neatly as a soldier's rifle.

"If it runs where I think it," I read aloud, because saying it made it slightly less impossible. "Runs where? Runs *where*, Doctor?"

Nobody answered. Wind moved the empty coats on their line.

* * *

Pell wouldn't come into the bowl. He stood at its rim with his rifle held across his chest like a barrier and reported what he'd found yesterday in the flat voice of a man reciting scripture he doesn't believe: no tracks out. None in, either — the team's approach trail ended fifty yards below the camp, crisp and complete, six sets of prints walking in.

"And then?" Crane asked.

"And then nothing, sir. Snow's been dry since the seventeenth. No fall to cover prints. Ground truth is ground truth." Pell swallowed. "It's like they arrived and then the snow forgot them."

I walked the perimeter twice. I'm an engineer; when a thing makes no sense, I measure it until it does or until I've proven the senselessness is the finding. I measured the distance from the last footprint to the nearest tent — eleven feet. Eleven feet of untouched snow between a living person's boot print and their own front door. Six people had crossed that eleven feet, in both directions, for five days, and the snow held no memory of any of it.

Except it did hold one thing.

At the bowl's northern edge, where the glacier's snout reared up in a wall of blue-shadowed ice, the snow swept against the base in a long clean rampart. And partway along it, maybe chest-high, the surface was disturbed — not footprints, nothing so kind — a smear. A broad drag, perhaps two feet wide, running from open snow straight into the ice itself, where the meltwater channel had carved an archway no wider than a coffin.

I stood in front of it for a long time. Behind me, Crane was shouting orders, men were roping up, someone was unpacking signal flares. The wind moved the coats. And I thought about my brother, who had spent two years mapping lines that every map in the country agreed were dead, and about the compass in my pocket that pointed, I now suspected, at things other than north.

Jonah had gone up this same route in spring. His journal — the pages I'd memorized and told myself meant nothing — described a blue archway in the ice, and a layer in the glacier that didn't belong, and a sentence that ended the same way Dr. Okonkwo's did: mid-thought, pen down, as if the writer had been interrupted by something that required both hands.

Or both people.

"Amberdale!" Crane called. "You're the expert. What do we do?"

What we do, I thought, is exactly what they sent us to do, because whoever paid for this recovery party wants eyes up here, and I am starting to suspect those eyes are meant to see whatever it is and report to the wrong people.

What I said was: "We go up to the serac line. She wrote she was moving there next. If there are answers, they're higher."

Crane nodded and turned to his men, and I looked once more at the drag mark in the snow, the width of a body, aimed at the ice like a sentence pointing at its own ending.

The mountain wasn't hiding them. I understood that suddenly, with the certainty of a level bubble finding center. The mountain was *keeping* them. And somewhere above the treeline, in the blue dark under the ice, six people — seven, counting a brother who'd come up this way ahead of them — were waiting to be found by whoever was stubborn enough, or guilty enough, to keep climbing.

I checked the compass in my pocket. It swung once, twice, and settled — not north.

Up.
