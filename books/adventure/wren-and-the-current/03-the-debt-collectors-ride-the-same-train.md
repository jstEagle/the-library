## 3. The Debt Collectors Ride the Same Train

The supply train left Kestrel Junction at six in the morning, which meant it left at seven-forty, because mountain trains keep their own counsel about time. I spent the delay doing what I always did when I was nervous: counting things. Eleven crates of hardtack. Four coils of rope. One glaciologist named Suri Okonkwo who talked to the ice axes like they were horses she was trying not to spook. Two miners, Halloran and Pike, who smelled of lamp oil and grievance in equal measure. And one map of closed lines folded into the lining of my coat, where it sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Jonah's compass rode in my breast pocket on the other side. Balanced, I told myself. Symmetrical. A woman carrying her brother's debts on the left and his directions on the right.

The train pulled out at last, and the valley began its slow argument with the altitude — pines giving way to scrub, scrub to scree, scree to nothing much at all. I stood in the gap between cars with the wind pulling at my collar and tried to think like an engineer instead of a sister. The line we were riding had been closed for eleven years. You could tell by the rails: rust blooms orange at the joints after a decade of neglect, and these were rusted clean through in places, the ties soft as bread. The government had paid for our passage on a dead line because nobody alive remembered how to read a dead line except people like me and, once, Jonah.

"You're doing the thing," Suri said, coming up beside me. She had to half-shout over the clatter. "Where you stare at track like it insulted you."

"It did. Look at joint forty-odd back there. They let water sit in the fishplates all winter, year after year. This line didn't die of neglect. It died of arithmetic."

"Everything dies of arithmetic."

"Not everything." I said it before I could stop myself, and Suri, being Suri, heard exactly what I meant and had the grace not to ask. Some griefs you carry publicly; some you carry in coat linings.

We came around the shoulder of the mountain at the eight-mile mark, and that was where I saw the private car.

It was hitched behind ours — or ahead, depending on your politics — a black lacquered thing with brass fittings gone dull, the kind of car that doesn't ride trains so much as get carried by them. It hadn't been at the junction. Which meant it had been coupled on somewhere between, at a siding, in the dark, by men who knew schedules.

Suri followed my eyes. "Was that there before?"

"No."

"Should it be?"

"Ask me again after breakfast," I said, and went to find out.

* * *

Elias Voss believed in preparation the way other men believed in God — sincerely, and with a certain pleasure in the details. He had shaved that morning using water heated on the private car's small stove, dressed in wool the color of wet slate, and reviewed the file on Wren Amberdale for the fourth time while the mountains went by like slow arguments.

The file was thin, which he appreciated. Thin files meant honest lives. Railroad engineer, certified at twenty-three, dismissed at thirty-one for an incident involving a bridge inspection she had refused to falsify. The dismissal had been described in her personnel record as "insubordination regarding structural assessment." Voss read that as: she would rather be poor than wrong. People like that were difficult and useful in roughly equal measure, like good tools with bad handles.

Her brother's file was thinner still. Jonah Amberdale, surveyor, debtor, missing. Owing four hundred and ten dollars to interests Voss represented, plus interest compounding weekly, plus — though this appeared nowhere in any ledger — a debt of a different denomination entirely. The old man had sent six surveyors up that glacier to retrieve what Jonah Amberdale had found, and six surveyors had stopped writing letters. Now the sister was climbing the same mountain with the same map folded into her coat, and Mr. Aldous Crane wanted the matter resolved before the snows made resolution permanent.

Voss did not consider himself a violent man. He considered himself a thorough one. Violence was simply what thoroughness looked like from the outside, sometimes.

He checked his watch, straightened his cuffs, and went forward through the train to introduce himself.

* * *

He found me in the third car, sitting across from Halloran, and I knew him before he said a word — not by the clothes, though the clothes were a telegram all their own, but by the way he moved down the aisle. Most men walk through a moving train braced against it, fighting the sway. He walked with it, loose at the knees, like a man who'd grown up on ships. He stopped at our bench and tipped his hat maybe half an inch, the exact amount of respect that costs nothing.

"Miss Amberdale, I presume. Elias Voss." He offered a card. Heavy stock. No title, no firm name — just his name, as if that were institution enough. "I wonder if I might have a few minutes."

Halloran looked at me. I nodded once, and the miner extracted himself with the reluctance of a man leaving a fight he'd already picked out.

"Mr. Voss." I didn't offer the seat. He took it anyway, which told me plenty. "You weren't at the junction."

"I joined at Millbrook siding. Late business in town." He smiled like a man apologizing for weather. "I'll confess I requested this meeting rather than stumbled into it. I represent certain financial interests — creditors, if you like — with regard to the estate of your brother."

"Jonah's debts are settled."

"They're outstanding."

"They're *mine* now, is what they are, and I've got thirty days, and I don't discuss accounts with strangers on moving trains."

"Thirty-one days now," he said mildly. "You lost a day to the junction delay. And we aren't strangers, Miss Amberdale. We're colleagues, of a sort. You're going up the mountain to look for six missing surveyors. I'm going up the mountain because those surveyors worked for the man whose money your brother spent. It occurs to me we might both save ourselves considerable discomfort by comparing notes."

The train swayed. Somewhere behind us a coupling clanked, iron complaining to iron.

"What notes?"

"The surveyors were mapping the high ice. Your brother mapped the closed lines. Both parties were looking for the same thing, though I doubt either knew it." He folded his hands on his knee, patient as a bank. "My employer's interest is purely commercial. There is apparently something above the treeline worth a great deal of money, and Mr. Crane dislikes uncertainty. Six men go quiet, and suddenly the uncertainty has teeth. He sent me to close the account. Retrieve whatever documentation exists, confirm the site, and see everyone home safe." His eyes stayed pleasant and absolutely still. "Everyone, Miss Amberdale. Including you."

"And if I say no thanks, we'll manage?"

"Then I'll ride along anyway. The mountain's public, more or less. But I'd hoped for cooperation, because cooperation keeps ledgers short, and short ledgers are happy ones." He leaned forward slightly, and for the first time the pleasantness thinned enough to show the wire underneath. "Your brother owed four hundred and ten dollars. That debt is a rounding error to my employer. What he actually wants is the map your brother made of the closed lines — the original, with his field annotations. If you produce it, every dollar of Jonah's debt vanishes, and you walk away clean, and I go home having done a simple job simply."

The compass sat against my chest. The map sat against my ribs. Between them, my heart was doing honest work and getting lied to about it.

"How do you know about the map?"

"A creditor knows his collateral."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I'm authorized to give." He rose, smoothed his coat, and set another card on the bench beside me — identical to the first, as if one name weren't enough to remember. "We arrive at the trailhead tomorrow noon. Think it over tonight. Sleep on it, even. I'm told sleeping on things is restorative." At the end of the car he paused, half-turned, and added, almost gently: "For what it's worth, Miss Amberdale, I'm sorry about your brother. Genuinely. He was better at his work than anyone gave him credit for. That's precisely why this has become complicated."

And then he was gone down the aisle, walking with the sway of the train like it was a deck under his feet.

* * *

In the privacy of the black car, Voss poured two fingers of rye and allowed himself the small luxury of reviewing the conversation honestly, since no one was watching.

She had the map. That was near-certain — the way she'd touched her left side when he mentioned it, a small motion, unconscious, the body confessing what the mouth denied. She also had the brother's stubbornness, which was less convenient. Stubborn women could be bought, frightened, or broken, but rarely quickly, and the window was closing. Three days of calving season, the glaciologists said, then the high route sealed until spring. Whatever happened on that glacier would happen inside seventy-two hours.

He turned the rye glass slowly on its base and considered the arithmetic, because everything came down to arithmetic in the end.

Six surveyors missing. One brother missing. One sister ascending with the only surviving copy of a map that proved where the old tunnel ran — and, more to the point, where it had run *into*, and what the railroad's founders had paid to have buried along with forty tons of rock and eleven men whose families had been told a comforting lie about a freak collapse. Crane's grandfather signed that lie. Crane's fortune rested on it. If the map surfaced with its annotations intact, the fortune developed a leak, and leaks in fortunes were the one thing Aldous Crane could not abide.

So: the map burned, the witnesses scattered or silenced, the mountain given its second burial. Clean. Thorough.

Voss finished the rye and looked out at the peaks going violet in the evening light, beautiful and indifferent, keeping everyone's secrets the way they kept everything — temporarily, and at interest.

Tomorrow at noon, he thought, they'd all start climbing together. And he found, examining himself carefully, that he felt no dread at all. Only the mild professional regret of a man who could see exactly how many ways a thing could go wrong, and knew he'd have to be present for whichever one chose them.

* * *

I didn't sleep on it. I sat up in the supply car with a lantern turned low and laid it all out on the floor like track sections, because that was the only way I knew how to think.

Six government surveyors go up the ice and stop existing — but their camp stays perfect, coats folded, food cached, no tracks out. That's not a storm. Storms scatter things. That's a *selection*. Something up there decided who stayed and who went, or someone did.

Jonah maps the closed lines. Jonah owes money to men connected to the same family that owns half the railroads in three counties — including the one that sealed the tunnel on the line I helped survey before they shut it. And now a fixer in slate-gray wool rides our own train asking, polite as a hymn, for that exact map.

I'd been telling myself two stories. Story one: poor Wren, saddled with her brother's debts, doing an honest day's recovery work to square his name. Story two: poor Wren, caught in the middle of somebody else's mystery.

They weren't two stories. They'd never been two stories. The debt collectors hadn't come for Jonah's dollars — dollars were the receipt, not the bill. They'd come because Jonah had drawn a line on paper that ran through a tunnel the Crane family needed forgotten, and then he'd gone up the glacier to see where the line came out, and he hadn't come down. The surveyors were sent to finish his work. The disappearance wasn't beside the debt. The disappearance *was* the debt, called in full, in men.

And Voss had just told me, in his courteous ledger-keeper's voice, that if I handed over the map, everyone goes home safe. Everyone. He'd said it like a kindness.

I thought about six coats folded neat as Sunday in a camp with no tracks leading away from it, and I understood what kind of man uses the word *everyone* that smoothly, and what he does with the word afterward.

Outside, the moon came off the glacier fields and lit the car silver. Tomorrow noon we'd start climbing, and Voss would climb with us, smiling, counting.

I took out the map and read it one more time by lantern-light — Jonah's cramped annotations, his little arrows, the note in the margin of the high route that I'd taken for a surveyor's shorthand and now read differently:

*If anyone follows me up here — turn back. Not for the ice. For what they sent me to find.*

I folded it back into the lining, against my ribs, next to the compass.

Seventy-two hours of window. One fixer on the train. Six missing men and a brother at the top of it all.

The trap wasn't closing, I realized, staring at the dark glass and my own face ghosted in it. The trap had closed weeks ago, in Kestrel Junction, the moment a parcel arrived with my brother's handwriting on it. Everything since was just the jaws tightening at a speed too slow to feel.

Tomorrow we climbed into them.
