## 4. Calving Season

Suri Okonkwo drank her coffee like it had personally wronged her and she was being polite about it.

We were in the cook tent at the base camp below the treeline, the canvas snapping in a wind that had opinions, and she had spread her charts across the folding table with the confidence of a woman who knew exactly how much trouble we were in and intended to make us all share it.

"Three days," she said.

"Three days of what?" asked Hollis, our wrangler, who was a good man with mules and a bad man with metaphors.

"Of window." She tapped a contour line on the chart, up where the paper went white because nobody had bothered surveying above it recently — or ever, if you believed the closed lines on Jonah's map. "The Meridian Glacier calves every autumn. The serac field at the ice bridge shifts, cracks, and for about seventy-two hours the pressure opens a route through the lower col. A passable route." She looked up. "Then the cold sets the whole thing like concrete and it doesn't open again until spring thaw. If we're not through by the third morning, we winter here, and I mean *here*, at eleven thousand feet, eating mule."

"I'd rather eat you," Hollis said. "Less gristle."

"You haven't seen me in spring," Suri said, and went back to her charts.

I stood at the tent flap with my hands in my pockets and did arithmetic I didn't like. We'd lost two days already at the abandoned camp — two days photographing instruments sitting neatly in their cases, food cached under tarps, coats folded on bedrolls, and not one track leading out of it. The snow around that camp had been smooth as fresh paper. Whatever took those men hadn't walked away. It hadn't run, either. It had simply stopped being a thing that left tracks, and I had spent two nights telling myself there was an explanation that didn't involve the word I kept circling and refusing to land on.

*Wind,* I told myself. *Drift. Men can be careless with their boots.*

Men could also vanish from a sealed tunnel, but I wasn't ready to think about Jonah yet this early in the morning.

"Say we go through," I said. "What's the route actually look like?"

Suri pulled out a second sheet, hand-drawn, dense with her own annotations. "The bridge is a remnant of an old icefall. In summer it's rotten — you couldn't cross it with a prayer and a rope. In deep winter it's welded solid but buried under ten feet of new snow, and the approach gullies avalanche. But during calving, the main serac releases, the weight comes off the bridge, and the crevasse beneath it gaps open just enough to walk the spine." She drew a line with her finger, up and over and down. "It's maybe four hundred meters of walking on ice that is actively deciding what it wants to be. You move light, you move fast, you don't stop to admire anything."

"And if it decides while we're on it?"

"Then we're a footnote in somebody's report." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. Glaciologists, I was learning, had made their peace with death the way railroad men had made peace with grades: respectfully, professionally, and with the understanding that it always came down to numbers you didn't control.

Hollis scratched his jaw. "And Voss's men? They know about this window?"

"They have a glaciologist too, or they wouldn't have come this far," Suri said. "Which means they'll be moving when we move. The window isn't wide enough for two parties to pretend they don't see each other."

That landed in the tent like a dropped tool. Nobody picked it up.

* * *

I walked the perimeter after breakfast, ostensibly checking the mules' pickets, actually doing the thing I do when I need to think, which is put my body somewhere repetitive and let my mind argue with itself.

Here's what I knew, laid out honest:

Elias Voss had boarded our supply train at Kestrel Junction wearing a good coat and a better smile, and asked after a map with the courtesy of a man asking after the weather. He'd been polite about it. He'd been polite right up until he mentioned my brother's debt to the penny, including interest accrued since March, which meant he knew figures no stranger should know. Then he'd tipped his hat, gotten off at a water stop in the middle of nowhere, and left me holding the knowledge that the men who held Jonah's paper and the men who sent a government survey team into the high country were, at minimum, drinking from the same well.

Jonah had been mapping closed lines. The survey team had gone looking for whatever the closed lines hid. And now I was standing at the bottom of a mountain holding both debts in one satchel, and the mountain had eaten the last people who climbed it looking for answers.

A sensible woman would turn around. A sensible woman would take her return ticket — second class, Kestrel Junction, purchased in a moment of optimism back when I still believed this was a recovery job and not a reckoning — and go home and let the mountain keep its secrets and let Voss keep his ledger.

But then there was the other column. The camp with no tracks. The instruments left behind like a man leaving a table mid-meal. And underneath all of it, the thing I kept not saying out loud: Jonah's compass had come to me pointing north-northeast, needle steady, as if it knew where it was going even if I didn't. My brother had gone up this mountain once already. His journal said so — the pages I'd found sewn into the map's backing, in his cramped left-slanting hand, entries that stopped mid-sentence at the treeline. He'd gone up, and he hadn't come down, and the debt collectors wanted his paper more than they wanted his money, which told me the paper was worth more than money.

You don't leave family on a mountain. Even dead family. Especially dead family whose name is currently collateral.

"Amberdale."

Suri had come up beside me without making a sound, which I'd noticed scientists do — they spend so much time listening to things that don't talk that they learn to move quiet themselves.

"Your ticket," she said. "The conductor's leaving for the valley at noon. After that, nothing runs until spring."

"I know."

"Do you?" She studied me with the frankness of a woman who measured things for a living and found most of them wanting. "Because I've watched you circle this camp twice now, and I've read your file, and I know you didn't lose your engineering post because you were careless. You lost it because you refused to sign off on a bridge you thought would fail. So I know you understand margins. What I want to know is whether you understand that this one has already been spent. Two days gone. Three remain. There is no version where we wait and think about it longer."

"The bridge," I said. "How sure are you? Honestly. Not the version for the report."

She was quiet a moment. Wind moved through the pines above us, carrying that particular cold smell of snow that hasn't fallen yet but has made up its mind.

"Honestly? Ice doesn't promise anything. It offers windows and closes them, and it has never once cared who was standing in the doorway." She tucked her charts under her arm. "But it's the only door there is. So the question isn't whether the ice will hold. It's whether you're the kind of person who stands in front of a closed door wondering, or the kind who walks through while it's open."

"You rehearse that speech?"

"Every expedition. Somebody always needs it." Almost a smile. "Usually me."

* * *

At noon the supply train gave three short whistles — the signal that meant last call, the same signal I'd heard a thousand times from the footplate of engines I used to drive, back when my signature meant something and my name wasn't shorthand for a collapsed trestle in the newspapers.

I stood on the platform of the base camp depot — two planks and a lean-to, really, but the railroad had built it, and railroads build things to last even when nobody uses them — and took out the ticket.

Second class. Kestrel Junction. Warm valleys, cheap rooms, a life where Voss's men found me eventually anyway, because men like that always collect, and they'd find me alone, with no witnesses, no crew, no story worth printing.

Or.

I thought about Jonah at nine years old, teaching himself Morse code by tapping on the kitchen table until Mother threatened to sell him to the telegraph office. I thought about him at nineteen, going west with a survey chain over his shoulder, writing me letters full of elevations and jokes. I thought about his handwriting stopping mid-sentence, and a compass that pointed north-northeast like it was still following him.

Hollis appeared at my elbow. "Conductor's waiting on you."

"He's not waiting on me. He's waiting on the schedule."

"Same thing, out here." Hollis chewed something, watching me. He was a perceptive man for all his mule-brain act. "For what it's worth, I burned mine twenty minutes ago. Felt fine. Little dramatic. Recommend it."

I looked at the ticket a long moment. Then I struck a match against the depot plank and held it to the corner until the flame took, and I let it burn down to my fingers before dropping what was left on the rails, where the wind worried it along the ties toward the valley it would never carry me to.

It felt less like commitment than like subtraction — removing the version of myself that could still choose cowardice, so that only one Wren remained, the kind who walks through doors.

"All right," I said, turning to the crew gathered at the edge of the trees: Suri with her charts, Hollis with his mules, Tam and Reyes with the ropes, young Pell with the camera equipment he was too green to know was heavy. "We break camp in one hour. Light loads, double ropes, nobody on the ice stops for any reason. We cross tomorrow at first light, when the refreeze is firmest, and we're through the col before the sun softens anything."

"And after the col?" Pell asked. He was the youngest, and he asked the questions everyone else was too proud to ask.

"After the col," I said, "we follow a dead man's map to whatever killed the survey team. And we find out what my brother died knowing."

Nobody cheered. Sensible crew. But Suri checked her watch, Hollis spat and started breaking pickets, and within the hour we were strung out single-file on the switchbacks above the treeline, climbing into country where the maps went blank and the air went thin and the glacier waited above us with its three-day door standing open.

From the shoulder of the second ridge, I looked back once. Far below, small as toys, a line of six figures was coming up the valley behind us — moving fast, roped, disciplined, and carrying rifles slung across their backs where surveyors carry none.

Voss had found his window too.

I turned north-northeast, took out Jonah's compass, and led.
