## 5. What the Closed Lines Were Closed For

The map was drawn in my brother's hand, and it lied the way Jonah always lied — politely, and only about the things that mattered.

We'd been following it for two days above the treeline, Suri reading the glacier's moods like a woman translating a language she half-remembered from childhood, and me pretending I wasn't watching the compass needle settle every time we crossed one of Jonah's penciled bearings. The dead lines ran under our boots. You couldn't see them anymore — forty years of snowfall had buried the grades and the cuts and the culverts — but a railroad engineer doesn't forget a right-of-way any more than a surgeon forgets where the organs sit. My feet kept finding the old alignment on their own, walking the ghost of a roadbed that hadn't carried a train since before I was born.

"Here," Suri said, near noon.

She didn't point at anything. That was the unnerving part. She just stopped, planted her ice axe, and looked at a slope of featureless white with the expression of someone listening to a door she expected to open.

I caught up and looked where she was looking. Nothing. Snow, wind-scoured to glass in places, drifted soft in others. Behind us, Hollis and Tam were arguing about stove fuel in the low, persistent way of men who'd been cold for three days straight.

"There," Suri said again, and this time she tapped her axe against the slope, twice, and I heard it.

Not the dull knock of ice over stone. A hollow note. A drum note.

My stomach did something complicated. Because I knew that sound. Every railroader knows that sound — the sound of a bore, a tunnel mouth, air moving through a space that shouldn't be empty. And I knew, in the way you know things you've spent years refusing to know, exactly what tunnel it was.

"The Amberdale Cut," I said. "Number Three."

Suri raised an eyebrow. "Family name?"

"The family that built it." I crouched and brushed snow away from the drift line, and there it was: the corner of a concrete headwall, frost-rimed, the poured stone gone soft-edged with decades of melt and refreeze. Someone had sealed it. Not with rockfall, not with a slide — with intent. Steel plate bolted over the arch, then concrete poured over the plate, then the whole face landscaped with blasted talus so that from fifty yards it read as geology. Somebody had spent real money making a hole in the mountain stop existing.

"Somebody closed this very thoroughly," Suri said, echoing my thought with a glaciologist's bluntness.

"Somebody closed it twice," I said. "Once with steel, once with money."

Hollis came up puffing, took one look at the headwall, and said what I was already thinking: "Survey team came up here looking for something. This is something."

Tam spat into the wind. "Or they came up here and found nothing and froze, and we're digging at an old wall because a dead man drew us a map."

Nobody answered him, which was answer enough. We all wanted to dig.

* * *

It took four hours with drills and bars to open a gap at the edge of the steel plate, and I won't pretend my hands didn't shake doing it. Not from cold. From the specific dread of a person opening a door somebody else paid a great deal to keep shut.

The air that came out was old — that cellar-smell of stillness, mineral and faintly sweet with rust. Suri went first with the lamp, because she was lightest and because she volunteered before anyone could order her, which is its own kind of courage. Then me. Then Hollis. Tam stayed at the gap with the rope, which he announced as tactics rather than fear, and nobody argued.

Inside, the tunnel ran true and level, the old ballast still graded underfoot, ties rotted to black lace. The lamp beam found rails — actual rails, green with oxidation but whole — running back into the dark farther than the light wanted to follow. And along the walls, every twenty feet, the lamp found the thing that made Suri go quiet beside me.

Marks. Scratched into the concrete lining. Names, mostly. Dates. Little tally strokes, groups of five, hundreds of them. And deeper in, where the tunnel narrowed toward the old collapse zone, the marks changed character. They got desperate. Long gouges, not writing anymore, just the record of fingernails and belt buckles and whatever else a man could drag across poured concrete in the dark.

"They were walled in alive," Suri said. Her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "The collapse didn't kill them. The closure did."

I put my hand flat against the wall, over a cluster of tallies, and felt the cold come up through my glove like the mountain taking my temperature. Forty years ago, men had stood exactly here, on the exact spot where I stood, counting days by scratching stone, while on the other side of the steel their company decided the arithmetic of rescue versus liability.

And I knew whose company. Of course I knew. The name was cut into every station house on the line I used to run: **Voss & Amberdale Consolidated**. My grandfather's signature sat next to Elias Voss's grandfather's on the closure order — I'd seen it framed in the director's boardroom a hundred times without ever reading it properly. Two families, one seal, and a generation of silence bought and maintained until the last people who remembered were dead or disgraced.

Disgraced. Like me. Like Jonah.

"It wasn't a collapse cover-up," I heard myself say. "It was a cost decision. The bore was behind schedule, the money was bleeding, and then the ground came down on a night crew — and sealing them in was cheaper than digging them out and admitting the tunnel was unstable. So they sealed it, called it a geological fault, abandoned the extension, and wrote off thirty-one men as actuarial losses."

"How do you know it was thirty-one?" Hollis asked from behind me.

Because the tallies told me. Thirty-one sets of marks, thirty-one names, and one of them — low on the wall, small, careful — was cut in letters I recognized the way you recognize your own face in a dark window.

**J. AMBERDALE.**

Not my brother. My great-uncle. A man whose name had been sanded out of the family story so smoothly that I'd grown up thinking the blank space in the genealogy was just poor recordkeeping.

Jonah hadn't found this tunnel. He'd been *looking* for it. His whole map of dead lines, his whole debt-fueled obsession, had been a search for the wound our family had spent forty years bandaging with concrete — and the men holding his paper weren't debt collectors at all. They were the other family. The Voss family. Making sure the wound stayed shut, using my brother's own desperation as the knife.

"He owed them," I said aloud, "because they made sure he'd owe. The loans, the markers, the interest — it was bait. He mapped the closed lines because they let him think that's what would buy his freedom. And what he actually found—"

"What he actually found," Suri finished quietly, "is why the survey team stopped reporting."

The lamp flickered. Down the bore, past the collapse zone, the darkness went on farther than any sealed tunnel should. There was a second opening. There had to be — the surveyors hadn't come in through our hole. Something up-slope, some vent or adit, connected this grave to the glacier above, and that was the route Jonah's map had really been drawing. Not a railroad. A way *in*.

We marked the wall, photographed everything Suri's camera could survive the cold for, and backed out into the daylight, where Tam was standing very still at the gap and looking not at us but past us, up-slope, at the ridge line.

"We've got company," he said. "Two figures, maybe three, on the moraine. Been there ten minutes. Not moving."

Suri raised her glasses, lowered them, and handed them to me without a word.

Through the lenses: dark coats, deliberate spacing, the unhurried posture of men who had all the time in the world because they knew exactly where we were and exactly where we were going. And leading them, unmistakable even at that distance, a tall figure in a grey coat who walked the glacier like a man walking his own property.

Elias Voss had found the window too.

I looked down at the compass in my hand, my inheritance, my brother's last gift, its needle steady on a bearing that pointed up-slope toward the ice cave and whatever Jonah had died — or hadn't died — to leave there.

He'd written me a route. The least I could do was walk it ahead of the men who'd written him a trap.

"Pack up," I said. "We move now."
