## 7. The Man Who Came Back Down

We found him on the third morning after the storm, folded into the lee of a granite shoulder like a letter someone had given up trying to mail.

Suri saw him first — a dark smudge against the snowfield that resolved, as we came closer, into the shape of a man sitting very still with his back to the rock. My first thought was *dead*. My second was *frozen dead*, which is a different thing entirely and one you learn to tell apart if you've spent enough winters around rail camps. The frozen dead sit neatly. The living are always in some state of argument with their own bodies.

This man was arguing. His jaw worked without sound, and his hands were tucked into his armpits in the particular way of someone who has decided that dignity is a luxury item he can no longer afford.

"Hello," I called, because startling a half-frozen man is a good way to finish the job the cold started.

He looked up. His eyes were the worst part — not glassy, not gone, but *clear*, clear as a mountain lake in October, and full of something I recognized before I could name it. It took me another ten steps to name it. It was the look of a man who had been hoping nobody would find him.

"There's four of us," Suri said quietly, doing her own arithmetic. "The survey team was six."

"I know how many there were." His voice came out in pieces, like freight cars coupling. "I'm the one who counted them down."

* * *

His name was Aldous Pike, and by rights he should have been dead twice over — once for the exposure, once for whatever he'd done to end up alone at eleven thousand feet with no pack, no coat worth the name, and no tracks leading anywhere sensible. We got him thawed by degrees, which is the only way to do it: warm water, dry wool, small sips, and no questions for the first hour, because questions burn fuel a body doesn't have.

By midday he could hold a cup without Suri guiding it. By early afternoon he could lie convincingly, which I took as a sign of recovery.

"We got turned around," he said. "Whiteout. Same as you. The others went for shelter and I went wide of it. That's all."

"That's all," I repeated.

"That's all."

The trouble with men who say *that's all* is that they always say it exactly twice, and the second time is louder than the first, as though volume were evidence. I let it go. Not because I believed him — I didn't believe him any more than I believe a bridge report written by the man who built the bridge — but because a recovered witness is worth more than a broken confession, and I needed him walking under his own power before I started pulling threads.

Besides, I knew something he didn't know yet. I knew his camp. I'd stood in it two weeks ago, among the intact tents and the instruments wrapped in oilcloth and the coats hung on their pegs like the owners had just stepped out to argue about something. Six coats. No tracks leaving. And now here was coat number seven, walking and talking and lying to my face, which meant either the mountain had grown him out of the ice or he hadn't been at that camp when the others vanished from it.

"You're Pike," I said, when we were moving again. He'd fallen in near the rear, where men go when they want to be near an exit. "Aldous Pike. Instrumentation."

"Yes."

"Your coat's still hanging in your tent."

I felt him stop walking before I heard him stop. Snow has a way of amplifying the absence of a footstep.

"It's a big camp," he said finally.

"It's a small camp."

Ahead of us, Hollis — our youngest, all elbows and opinions — glanced back, then away, having learned already that watching me work was like watching someone defuse something. Suri kept her eyes on the route and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary.

"Mr. Pike," I said, "I'm going to ask you one question today, and you're going to have until tonight to decide whether to answer it. You can spend the day composing your answer. Compose it well. I've been lied to by professionals this month, and I've gotten better at telling the difference."

"And what's the question?"

I looked up the glacier, to where the ice bridge waited in its three-day window, white and patient as a held breath.

"What did Jonah Amberdale find?"

* * *

He didn't answer by nightfall. He didn't answer by the middle watch, either, when I sat up nursing the stove and watched him lying in his bedroll with his eyes open, staring at the roof of the tent like the answers were written up there in some alphabet only the freezing could read.

But he talked anyway. That's the thing about guilt — it doesn't need permission. Around midnight, with the wind doing its low work against the canvas, he started speaking to the ceiling as if I weren't there, and I had the sense not to interrupt.

"They sent us up to survey a closed line," he said. "That's what the papers said. Reassessing alpine routes, cost-benefit of reopening. Nobody reopens anything above the treeline; everyone knows it. But you sign where the money is, and the money was good."

"I know why they sent you," I said. "I've met the money."

"Have you." Something moved in his voice that wasn't quite a laugh. "Then you know it wears a nice coat."

We sat with that. Outside, the glacier ticked and settled, the sound it makes when it's dreaming about moving.

"The engineer," he said. "Amberdale. He wasn't part of the team. He was already up here when we arrived. We found his cache at the old tunnel mouth — food, coal, survey notes. He'd been living in the sealed section." A pause. "There's a gap in the ice inside. A cave. Warm, comparatively. The mountain keeps a room the way a hotel keeps a room, if you know which door."

My hands went still around the tin cup. "Go on."

"He'd been mapping something he shouldn't have been able to map, because the official records don't match the rock. The tunnel didn't fail the way the reports said it failed. There was no gas pocket. There was bad timber, bought cheap, and a collapse that killed eleven men, and a company that paid off the inspectors and sealed the tunnel with the men still—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Some of them are still in there. The ice took the section down-slope. They're not buried. They're *kept*."

Eleven men. I thought of the ledger in my brother's parcel, the neat columns of debt, and understood for the first time that the numbers had never been about money. You don't send a collector after a man who owes two hundred dollars. You send a collector after a man who knows where eleven bodies are filed, and whose notes could prove the filing was murder.

"Jonah knew," I said. "And they found out he knew."

"He tried to come down. Weather turned. And one of ours — Reyes — she fell coming off the moraine. Bad leg. Couldn't walk fast enough for the window." Pike's voice had gone flat, the flatness men use when feeling would otherwise be fatal. "Amberdale went back for her. Carried her up to the cave. Said he'd bring her down when the storm passed. He made me promise—"

He stopped again. This time I don't think anything could have restarted him.

"Made you promise what?" I asked.

"To tell his sister he was sorry about the compass." Pike finally turned his head and looked at me, and his clear terrible eyes were wet. "He said, *tell Wren the debt isn't hers.* Those were nearly his last words to anyone. Tell Wren the debt isn't hers."

* * *

He was gone before first light.

I'll say that plainly, because everything else about it was not plain at all. I slept two hours, badly, the way you sleep at altitude when your blood is arguing with the air, and when I crawled out to feed the stove his bedroll was empty, his boots gone, and the frost on the tent flap unbroken — which meant he'd left barefoot or through the back seam, and either way he'd left like a man who had practiced.

On top of my pack, weighted with a stone so the wind wouldn't take it, lay a field notebook with a cracked leather cover and a brass clasp I'd have known anywhere, because I'd bought it for him myself, eleven years ago, from a stationer in Calder Junction who overcharged us both and smiled while doing it.

Jonah's hand filled the pages. Survey grids, elevations, the tunnel sections drawn in his tidy draftsman's script — and then, in the last quarter of the book, the handwriting changing, loosening, the letters of a man writing fast in the cold because he might not get a slow chance later. Names. Dates. The timber contract. The inspector's signature and the second signature underneath it, the one that paid for the first. Eleven names of the dead, each one written out whole, first name and last, because Jonah Amberdale had never in his life been able to leave a thing unfinished.

And on the final page, dated nine days ago:

*Going back up for R. She can't make the descent alone and I won't leave her. If you're reading this, little sister, then somebody came looking, and I'm sorry for what that costs you. The debt was never yours. It was mine, and I've paid it the only way that counts — the truth is in this book, and the book is heavier than the money ever was.*

*Take the high line. Trust the glaciologist. Don't trust the fixer.*

*The compass points home. It always did. I just kept pointing it the wrong direction.*

I sat there until my fingers ached around the notebook, and the crew stirred behind me, and the eastern sky went the color of a bruise healing.

Hollis found me first. "He's gone," he said. "Tracks head up-glacier. Toward the bridge."

Toward the cave. Toward Reyes, and whoever else the mountain was keeping. Toward my brother, alive nine days ago, carrying a woman with a broken leg through weather that kills the prepared, because love is the one cargo that makes fools of every load calculation ever written.

I understood it completely. That was the unbearable part. I sat in the snow holding my brother's handwriting and understood the pull so completely it frightened me — because I felt it too, that gravity running uphill, and I knew that if the positions were reversed, if it were Jonah lying broken in the ice, I would already be walking.

"Voss will have reached the bridge by now," Suri said, crouching beside me, her breath smoking. "If Pike can cross in his condition, Voss can cross with twenty men and rope. Whatever window we have left, it's closing faster than the ice says."

I stood. I put the notebook inside my coat, against my chest, where the important things ride.

"Then we don't wait for the window," I said. "We take it."

Above us, far up the white throat of the glacier, something boomed — deep, tectonic, patient — and the snowfield shivered under our boots like a struck bell.

The calving had begun.
