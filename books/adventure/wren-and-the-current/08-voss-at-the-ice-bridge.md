## 8. Voss at the Ice Bridge

I have been called many things in my professional life — fixer, accountant, "the polite end of a bad conversation" — but never, until that morning on the ice bridge, an optimist. Yet there I stood, forty feet of blue glacier glass under my boots, the sun coming up over the col like a coin flipped by God and landing heads, thinking: *today might actually go well.*

I want it noted, for the record I keep in my own head, that I did not want to kill anyone that day.

This is not sentiment. Sentiment is for people who can afford it. It is arithmetic. Dead men generate paperwork, questions, widows with lawyers. A clean transaction generates nothing but a signature and silence, and silence is the only commodity I have ever traded in that doesn't spoil.

The trouble with Wren Amberdale, I had learned by then, is that she does not trade in commodities. She trades in debts. And you cannot buy a debt from a woman who intends to pay it.

* * *

We had come through the same window she had — three days when the calving loosens the ice bridge enough to cross and not enough to die on, though the margin between those conditions is thinner than my patience. My men grumbled about it. Hollis, who had crossed the pass twice in the army and hated it both times, said the glacier sounded like a whale being flayed. I told him whales don't scream, which he took as comfort, which tells you everything about why Hollis follows me.

There were six of us. Enough to be persuasive, few enough to look like hunters rather than an army. An army announces itself; a negotiation should arrive like weather — suddenly, inevitably, and with no one to blame.

We reached the eastern lip of the bridge at first light and waited, because arriving second looks weak and arriving first looks like ambush, and I wanted neither. I wanted to look *inevitable*. So we let them come to us, and they did, because the route only goes one way, and that is the beautiful thing about geography: it does all your negotiating posture for you.

They came around the moraine at half past seven — Amberdale in front, of course, because she would be, the glaciologist behind her with her instruments clinking like a wind chime in a hurricane, and four others strung out on the rope looking like men who had recently learned what whiteout means. They had. I'd watched the storm take their day from the far side of the ridge, and I confess I'd felt something adjacent to respect. Six hours anchored in that wind. Most people would have turned back. Most people, in fact, did turn back — two of hers didn't make it down the rope line, and I know what that costs a leader. I've invoiced for less.

Amberdale saw us and stopped so abruptly the rope went taut behind her. Good instincts. She read the situation in about three seconds — six armed men, one exit, morning light flat on the ice — and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done, and reach a different sum.

"Elias Voss," I said, spreading my hands, empty, gloved, harmless-looking. "Miss Amberdale. We keep meeting at altitude."

"You followed us."

"I used the same door you did. The door was open. That's not following, that's scheduling." I gestured at the ice beneath us, translucent blue going green where it thickened toward the far lip, meltwater ticking somewhere below like a clock nobody wound. "Though I'll grant the timing looks pointed. Shall we talk standing? I find standing conversations end faster."

* * *

Here is what I knew, going in:

Jonah Amberdale had been hired — through intermediaries, through paper, through the comfortable anonymity of money — to survey the closed lines above Greywater Pass. Not to reopen them. To document what was under them. The collapse of '09, the eleven men the company buried in the mountain along with the tunnel, the correspondence that proved the closure was ordered before the inspection, not after. Old Mr. Aldous Crane — whose name I will not say aloud even now, whose checks cleared like weather fronts moving across a county — wanted that documentation found and brought to him, so that it could stop existing.

Jonah found it. Jonah also, apparently, grew a conscience somewhere above the treeline, which is the most expensive place to grow one. He sent copies down the mountain. Then he went back up — for a surveyor, for someone, the details were always foggy — and the mountain kept him, the way mountains do, without leaving tracks.

The debt he owed was real. Eleven hundred dollars, gambling, mostly, borrowed from men who work for people like me. But the debt was also a lever, and levers are how you get engineers to walk into places engineers wouldn't otherwise go. His sister inherited both the compass and the collections notice, and I confess that part wasn't planned. Grief makes excellent leverage if you can aim it, and I am very good at aiming.

What I had not planned was that she'd hire on with the recovery party, burn her return ticket, and walk into the whiteout after a government team that Crane's other people had already gotten to. Or rather — that Crane's other people had gotten to first, badly, and left a survivor who talked too much to the wrong priest in Greywater, which is how the newspapers got a thread to pull. Threads are the enemy. Everything I do is threading prevention.

So: the map, the notebook, whatever Jonah copied, whatever the survivor left behind. All of it, into my satchel, and then silence, and then everyone goes home and lives long dull lives full of small regrets.

That was the offer. I rehearsed it on the climb. I even rehearsed her refusal, because I rehearse everything, and I had prepared answers for every version of Wren Amberdale except the one standing in front of me, who looked at me with her brother's eyes and said, before I'd finished my opening sentence:

"How much did he owe?"

I blinked. "Eleven hundred—"

"Not the number." Her breath came out white and fast. "The *price*. What was he supposed to bring down? Who sent him?"

"Miss Amberdale." I let the warmth drop out of my voice, just a degree, enough to show the machinery underneath. "You're negotiating backwards. Let me make my offer first, and then you can interrogate me."

* * *

I made the offer. I made it well. I have made offers in boardrooms with mahogany tables and offers in stockyards with blood on the gravel, and this one was calibrated precisely between those: the debt forgiven, paper burned, a letter of credit good anywhere she cared to disappear to, and no further interest taken in her or her crew. In exchange: the map, the notebook, any copies, any knowledge, and her signature on a statement saying the survey team died in a storm, which was nearly true, which is the best kind of true.

"And if I say no?" she asked.

"Then we're having a different conversation, and I'd genuinely prefer not to."

"You'd kill us on the ice." Not a question. She was watching my hands, not my face. Smart. Faces lie; hands just do what they're going to do.

"I'd prefer," I said again, and meant it, "to write this down as a business trip. But Miss Amberdale, I need you to understand the shape of your position. Behind you is a glacier that seals this route tomorrow. Ahead of me is the only way down. There is no third direction. There never is, in the mountains. Everything here converges."

The glaciologist — Okonkwo, sharp-eyed, sunburned, the kind of woman who counts things compulsively — spoke up from behind him. "The bridge won't hold six extra men standing still. Meltwater's running under the east span. You can hear it."

"I can," I agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I'm hoping for a quick signature."

And here is the thing I must set down honestly, because this account is the only record that will survive and I refuse to flatter myself in it: I meant the offer. Every word. The letter of credit was real; I'd drafted it in Greywater in my own hand. I did not intend to kill her. I intended to let her live with it, which is heavier, and cheaper, and mine to give.

But I had also given Hollis his instructions, and Hollis's instructions contained a contingency, and contingencies are how intentions die. If she refused — if the papers didn't change hands — then no one crossed this bridge carrying anything but themselves. No witnesses either way. That phrase, *no witnesses either way*, is the whole of my profession distilled to its ugly essence: accept the deal and be silenced by gratitude, refuse it and be silenced by gravity. Either way the story ends the same. Only the mechanism differs.

She looked at me for a long moment. Wind moved off the col and flattened against us. Somewhere under our feet, deep in the blue, the glacier groaned — a long, structural sound, like a ship changing its mind.

"You think this is about the map," she said quietly. "It isn't. It's about eleven men the Cranes sealed in a tunnel and wrote off as weather. Jonah found their names. He wrote them down. And you're offering me eleven hundred dollars to unwrite them."

"I'm offering you your life," I said. "The names are a rounding error."

"That's the difference between us, Mr. Voss." She pulled the oilskin packet from inside her coat — the map, the notebook, everything I'd come for — and held it, and did not extend it. "To you it's arithmetic. To me it's an inheritance."

And then the mountain, which had been listening the entire time the way mountains listen, decided it was tired of negotiations.

The sound came up through my boots first — a crack like the sky splitting along a seam — and then the far anchor of the bridge, the eastern serac we'd all been politely ignoring, shed a piece of itself the size of a chapel and dropped into the crevasse field below. The ice under us shivered. Okonkwo shouted something about the window closing early. Hollis looked at me, waiting for the contingency, waiting for the word.

Wren Amberdale tucked the packet back inside her coat, turned, and started running for the far lip — onto the failing bridge, onto the only road, taking the decision out of both our hands and giving it to the glacier, which has never once accepted a bribe.

"Hollis," I said, and heard my own voice come out wrong, too fast, "not yet—"

But he'd already raised his rifle, and the ice was already moving, and I understood, in the last clear thought I would have for some hours, that I had built the contingency so well I could no longer stop it.
