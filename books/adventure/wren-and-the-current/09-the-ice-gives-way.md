## 9. The Ice Gives Way

The glacier announced itself the way a bad debtor does — not with a threat but with a courtesy.

A sound like a door closing somewhere under the world. Then another. Suri stopped mid-stride and put her bare hand flat against the ice, and I watched her face do something I'd only seen on engineers reading a bridge gauge: the look of a person who has just learned the structure is lying to them.

"That's early," she said.

"How early?"

"Two days early. Maybe three." She lifted her hand. "The bridge is letting go from underneath. Warm water's been eating its spine all season and nobody told it to wait for us."

Behind us, down the moraine, Voss's men had stopped too. Four of them, roped in pairs, small as punctuation against the white. Voss himself stood apart, hands in his pockets, a man watching weather he believed he'd already purchased his way out of.

I had spent the last hour thinking about judges. It's what you do when you can't afford to be one yourself. A judge needs to be clean, disinterested, immune to bribes and grief, and I was none of those things. I wanted Voss's men to suffer for Jonah, which meant my judgment was worth exactly nothing. But a calving glacier doesn't take bribes. It doesn't hate anyone. It weighs every footstep on the same scale, and it has never once been wrong about who deserves to cross.

"Change of plan," I said to the crew. "We go now, fast, single file, and we don't stop for anything that isn't bleeding."

"And them?" Marta nodded downhill at Voss.

"They'll make their own choices." I checked the rope at my waist. "The mountain will check their math."

* * *

The ice bridge was a hundred yards of blue-gray arch spanning the crevasse field where the glacier bent around the shoulder of the peak — the only route up that didn't involve ropes and prayer in equal measure. In summer it groaned. In the window, Suri said, it sang. This morning it was doing neither, which she informed me was worse than either.

"Silence means the stress fractures have gone quiet," she said. "Quiet means they've stopped being warnings and started being facts."

"Thank you for the geology lesson."

"You're welcome. Walk lightly."

I went first. That wasn't bravery; it was arithmetic. I weighed the least of anyone carrying gear, and if the bridge wanted a sacrifice I preferred it took me before it took Suri, whose brain was worth more than all our boots combined. Behind me came Marta, then Okell, then the two porters, then Dray — the surveyor we'd pulled off the moraine two days ago, half-frozen and mute, who had looked at the bridge this morning with an expression I couldn't read and hadn't liked.

Halfway across, the glacier cleared its throat again.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible part. A deep settling, like a house at night, like the sound of a debt being called in. The bridge shivered under my boots — not cracked, not yet, just *shivered*, the way a horse shivers off a fly — and a seam of white light opened along the ice six feet to my left and ran the length of the arch faster than a man can run.

"Off the bridge!" I shouted it backward without turning. "Everyone off — now!"

And behind us, because men are men and fear is stupid, Voss's four came on.

They'd been waiting at the near lip for their employer's word, and Voss gave it — I heard him, calm as a stationmaster, *"Go, go"* — and they stepped onto a dying arch because a man in a good coat told them to. I watched the lead one, a heavyset fellow with a red scarf I'd been hating since the train, plant his boot where mine had been ten seconds earlier, and I watched the bridge decide.

The crack didn't travel this time. It simply *was*, suddenly, everywhere — a white line bisecting the arch, and then the whole span dropped an inch with a boom I felt in my teeth, and the far third of it folded inward like wet cardboard.

The red-scarf man went with it. So did the porter behind him — no. Not a porter. One of Voss's. The other three made it back to solid ice by some combination of luck and rope, and lay there gasping, and the bridge between us and them was gone. Just gone. A hundred feet of open air where a road had been, and below it the blue dark going down further than I wanted my eyes to go.

Voss stood at the near edge, looking at the gap, and then — and this is the thing I will remember until I die — he looked at *me*, across sixty yards of broken ice, and smiled.

"There's another way up," he called. His voice carried beautifully in the cold air. He'd have made a fine preacher. "There always is. And Miss Amberdale — the offer stands. The map for the debt. Think about who you're climbing toward."

I thought about it. I thought about it the whole rest of the day, honestly, because that's the trap of a clean exit: it stays clean right up until you notice the price tag is written in someone else's blood.

But mostly I climbed.

* * *

We found the cave at dusk, because Dray finally spoke.

He'd been silent since the moraine — silent through the storm, through the lost day, through the bridge — walking at the rear like a man attending his own funeral. When we reached the black seam in the rockface above the icefall, a fissure no wider than a coffin, he said, "In there," in a voice like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in months, and then sat down in the snow and would not come further.

Marta stayed with him. Suri and I went in with lamps.

The cave opened after twenty feet into a chamber the size of a chapel, walls of ancient blue ice lit gold by our flames, and everything in it was exactly as they'd left it. Two bedrolls. A spirit stove, cold. A tin of biscuits with two left. Survey instruments stacked with the care of men who expected to need them tomorrow. Coats folded over packs.

No bodies. I noticed that first, and my heart did something shameful and hopeful, and then I saw the second bedroll and understood. Jonah had come back up for someone. Dray. Dray was the someone. And whatever had happened to the others had happened out there, on the ice, where the mountain keeps what it takes.

Jonah's field notebook lay on the flat rock they'd used as a table, weighted down with a carabiner, open to the last written page as if he'd meant it to be found that way. As if he'd known exactly who would follow.

Suri held the lamp. I read aloud, because some things need a voice or they aren't real.

*Oct. 3. Found the tunnel mouth. The 1897 collapse wasn't a collapse. The charges were set inside, above the workers' gallery. Ashford & Voss's own survey confirms it — eleven men sealed in with the line closed over them. The company report says "act of nature." My map says otherwise.*

*Oct. 5. They know we know. Harker went down to negotiate. He didn't come back.*

*Oct. 6. If you're reading this, you're the one I hoped wouldn't come. Ren. Go home. Take the compass. The names are in the back pages — all of them, the signatures, the telegrams. It's enough. It was always enough. Don't let them trade you for me.*

*I'm staying with Dray until he can walk. Whatever comes up the glacier, it comes to both of us or neither.*

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from deeper in the cave, and in the moving light I saw the back pages were thick with pasted paper — telegram flimsies, a signed requisition, a letter on Ashford family letterhead instructing that "the matter of the upper line be concluded permanently and deniably." Eleven names. Eleven men sent into a tunnel and blown shut behind them, forty years ago, by the same hands that had sent Jonah up here to find proof and then sent Voss to make sure he never brought it down.

I stood in a chapel made of ice older than any of those signatures, holding my brother's handwriting, and I felt fury and clarity arrive together, the way they say grief and love do — not in sequence but in the same instant, occupying the same space, indistinguishable.

They'd wanted the map buried. They'd buried eleven men to keep the secret and my brother to keep the map, and they'd have buried me and Suri and Marta and sweet silent Dray without losing a night's sleep, and Voss would have written it all up in tidy prose for men in warm rooms.

Well. The glacier had already started burying things today. It had opinions about who.

"Ren," Suri said quietly. She was looking at the back wall of the chamber, where the lamplight caught something pale pressed into the ice. Not a body. A jacket — Jonah's, I knew the patched elbow anywhere — frozen into the face of the glacier, arm extended, hand open, pointing deeper into the mountain. Toward the old tunnel. Toward the place where the truth had been bricked over twice: once with stone, once with paper.

"He went in," Suri whispered. "At the end. He went in to see."

The draft was coming from that direction. Cold air moving through the mountain like breath, like something alive behind the wall, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, patient — a sound I recognized from a hundred rail yards and had hoped never to hear again this far from one.

Water. Running water, where no water should run.

The glacier wasn't finished giving way. And whatever my brother had found behind the ice, it was still down there, waiting in the dark with the eleven, keeping the last entry nobody had read yet.
