## 10. A Map With One Line Open

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it's decided isn't worth crushing.

We came down ahead of the sealing snows, six of us where we'd started as nine, and the glacier closed behind us like a ledger balancing itself. Suri said the ice bridge sealed at dawn on the fourth day, exactly as she'd promised, and I didn't look back to watch it happen because some things you don't need to see twice. The route was shut for the winter. Whatever was up there would keep until spring — and by spring, if I did my work right, it wouldn't matter who kept it.

I had Jonah's field notebook inside my coat, against my chest, where it had ridden since the cave. The pages were stiff with cold and one of them had frozen to the cover and torn when I opened it too fast on the first night down, and I'd felt that tear like a tear in my own skin. His handwriting got worse the higher he'd climbed. By the last entries it was barely handwriting at all, just the record of a man writing faster than his fingers could obey him, naming names: Aldous Crane. Meridian Pacific. The survey financed not to find a route but to confirm that nothing remained of the old tunnel collapse except silence and enough dead men to keep it buried.

Forty-one workers in 1898. Sealed in behind a timber bulkhead while the company's men stood outside and listened, and then the company bought the coroner, bought the newspaper, closed the line, and called it geology. My brother had found the payroll ledger in a records office in Denver and followed it up the mountain like a blood trail. And the men who held his debt hadn't lent him money at all. They'd hired him. They'd sent him up to see what could be seen, and when he saw too much, they turned his inheritance into an instrument — thirty days, pay up or else — to flush out whatever he'd left behind.

Me. I was the else.

* * *

We made Meridian Falls on the ninth day, and I'll tell you what I learned about coming down off a mountain: it is not relief. It is a slower kind of falling. Your body keeps bracing for ground that keeps arriving anyway, and your mind keeps running the arithmetic of the lost — Hollis and Tam, gone in the whiteout; Deacon, who walked into the storm rather than say one more word about what happened above the treeline; Jonah, who had been dead up there since March and had waited all that time for someone to come find him. Nine minus four. The math of it sat in my chest like ballast.

The others scattered at the station like birds off a wire. Suri shook my hand with both of hers and said, "You know what you're holding," and it wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. She took the northbound. I watched her go and envied her the simplicity of a woman whose only evidence was ice cores.

I checked into the hotel across from the depot because it was the last place anyone sensible would look for me, and because Voss was not a sensible man. He was a thorough one, and thorough men check the obvious rooms first. I paid cash under a name I invented on the spot — Mrs. Ellery Fenn, of all the absurd things — and sat by the window with the curtain cracked and watched the town do its evening business, and I thought about burning the notebook.

That was the offer, after all. Voss had made it plain enough at the ice bridge, before the calving turned his polite ambush into a scramble for survival: forgive the debt, forget the mountain, live a long quiet life. He'd even meant it, I think, in the way a man means a thing right up until the moment it becomes inconvenient. And then the glacier had taken two of his men and very nearly taken him, and he'd looked at me across the splitting ice with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it in the mirror for two years after the derailment that ended my career — the expression of a man recalculating everything.

He'd survive. Men like Voss always survive; it's practically their trade. But he'd report that the notebook went into the crevasse with everything else, because reporting otherwise would mean admitting he'd failed twice, and Voss would rather freeze than fail.

So as far as the world knew, I was clean. No debt — forgiven, or at least abandoned. No map — destroyed, or at least reported so. No witnesses but a glaciologist who'd gone north and a story no editor would touch without proof.

I could burn it. That was the whole shape of the choice, and I turned it over for three days in that hotel room while Mrs. Ellery Fenn ordered tea she didn't drink. Burn the notebook, keep the compass, take a job on some open line in some flat country where the only thing that ever collapsed was my own expectations, and live. Jonah had died for the truth, but dying doesn't obligate the living — that's the lie grief tells you, that the dead hand you their causes along with their effects. Nobody signs for that. You can refuse delivery.

Except.

Except forty-one men were still behind a timber bulkhead on a mountainside, unnamed, unmourned, officially never there. Except their families had been paid off in 1898 with sums that wouldn't cover a decent coffin, and their grandchildren were living towns over from the tunnel mouth without knowing why their great-grandmothers never spoke of that winter. Except my brother had climbed a glacier with bad boots and a borrowed compass to put their names on paper, and the only thing standing between those names and oblivion was a disgraced engineer sitting in a hotel room drinking tea she didn't want, doing arithmetic with someone else's courage.

I thought about what he'd written on the last legible page, the one I read by candlelight in the ice cave while the wind tried to take the roof off the world:

*If Wren ever reads this — I know what you'll want to do. Don't do it for me. Do it because the line was real. Forty-one men walked to work on a Tuesday.*

Don't do it for me. As if he'd known me better from a thousand miles away than I knew myself at arm's length. As if he'd known I'd stand exactly where I was standing, holding exactly what I was holding, looking for permission to be small.

* * *

On the third morning I went to the stationer's and bought, in three separate transactions at three separate shops, so no single clerk could later swear to anything: a ream of cheap paper, a bottle of ink, a pen with a nib fine enough for copying figures, and a railway timetable. Then I went back to the room and began the work.

It took eleven hours. I copied the ledger entries — dates, amounts, the initials A.C. beside every disbursement marked "line closure, contingency." I copied Jonah's journal entries verbatim, including the ones that named Crane and Meridian Pacific and described the survey's true purpose. I copied the survey team's manifest that Deacon had carried, which listed the government authorization number that proved the whole expedition had been funded through a discretionary account answerable to no one. And I copied the map — God help me, I copied the map, every closed line, every elevation, the tunnel marked with a small x and the words *41* in Jonah's hand, because a document without its geography proves nothing and convinces no one.

Three copies. Three envelopes. Three newspapers: the *Denver Clarion*, whose editor had a reputation for incorruptibility mostly because nobody had yet offered him enough to test it; a small socialist weekly in Chicago that printed things bigger papers wouldn't and survived on subscriptions from miners; and — this was the piece of spite I allowed myself — the *Meridian Falls Gazette*, the same paper whose predecessor had helped bury the story in 1898. Let them choke on it locally first. Let the town that lived in the shadow of the tunnel read its own grandfather's signature on the payoff.

I addressed them, stamped them, and then I sat with them for one more hour, because I'm honest with myself about the cost even when I've already decided to pay it. Publish and be hunted. Voss would deny everything, Crane's lawyers would descend like weather, and somewhere in it there would be a train I shouldn't have boarded, a bridge inspection I should have survived. I knew how these stories went; I'd spent two years living the epilogue of one. There was no version of this where I got to be safe and also clean.

But there was a version where I got to be free, and free and hunted are not opposites. I'd been safe for two years and it had felt exactly like being buried.

I mailed them myself, at the depot window, from a public booth, in front of a crowd waiting on the eastbound. Witnesses are a funny kind of armor. Hard to make a woman disappear in front of forty people who each half-remember her face.

Then I walked out onto the platform with the compass in my pocket and nothing else of Jonah's at all, and I stood in the cold and felt lighter than I had any right to feel, given that I'd just signed my own name to a war.

* * *

The compass still pointed north. It had always pointed north; that was the joke of it, that a thing so faithful had belonged to a man so lost. I turned it over in my gloved hands on the platform and read the inscription on the case again, the one I'd found in the ice cave scratched into the brass with a knife point, in letters that must have cost him his last fine motor control:

*FOR WREN — THE ROUTE IS THE GIFT. J.*

Not the debt. Not the truth, even, though the truth was in it. The route. The way through. He'd inherited a map of closed lines from our father and spent his life trying to open one, and what he'd left me wasn't an obligation or an accusation. It was instructions. Find the line. Walk it open-eyed. Accept what it costs.

The eastbound came in on time, steam and iron and the smell of hot oil, and I boarded it with a ticket I'd bought openly, under my own name, Wren Amberdale, disgraced engineer, because hiding was over — hiding was the old route, and it was closed.

I took a seat by the window. Somewhere behind me the mountains were sealing themselves for winter, keeping their dead and their secrets in the cold dark, and somewhere ahead of me three envelopes were starting their slow journeys toward ink and headlines and ruin — mine, probably, and Crane's, definitely. In between lay eight hundred miles of open track, and I intended to ride every mile of it awake.

The whistle blew. The platform slid away. I set the compass on my knee, needle steady, pointing home to a range that wanted me dead, and I smiled at it like an old adversary I'd finally learned to read.

Spring would open the high route again. And this time, I'd be the one deciding who came down.
