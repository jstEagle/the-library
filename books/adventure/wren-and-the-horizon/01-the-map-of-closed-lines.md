## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The rain had followed August Whitlock for three days, and by the time the ferry nosed into Greyharbor it had worked its way through every seam of their coat and settled into the bones beneath like a tenant who intended to stay.

They stood at the rail while the town assembled itself out of the murk — a scatter of slate roofs, a crane arm leaning over the water like a man too tired to stand straight, and behind it all the dark shoulder of the mountains, white-capped even in summer, holding the sky up out of habit rather than conviction. Somewhere past those shoulders lay the closed lines. August had spent eleven years putting rails across country like that, and now they could not afford the fare for a second-class carriage on any line that mattered.

The ferry bumped the pilings. The gangway came down. August shouldered their one bag — canvas, patched twice, light enough that a stranger would have guessed them a man with nothing to carry and been exactly right — and stepped onto planking slick with rain and fish scales.

*August Whitlock.* The name still opened doors, but only because people wanted to look at the face behind it, the way crowds gather at an accident. The engineer whose bridge came down. The engineer who signed the inspection reports that said the bridge was sound. Eleven dead, or ten, depending on which newspaper you believed; the eleventh had been found two weeks later, downstream, and the papers had run the correction small, on an inside page, where corrections go to die.

That had been fourteen months ago. The inquiry board had taken August's license in a room that smelled of wet wool and lamp oil, and after that the profession itself had closed like a door. No railway would touch them. No engineering firm returned letters. The blacklist was never written down anywhere — that was the elegance of it — but August's name traveled through the trade the way damp travels through plaster, silently, into everything.

So: courier work. Sealed orders carried by hand to places the wires didn't reach. It paid badly and asked nothing of a man but his legs and his silence, and August had both in surplus.

* * *

The broker's office stood above a chandler's shop on Rope Street, up a staircase that groaned like a ship in weather. The broker was a woman named Ferren with ink-stained fingers and eyes that did arithmetic on everyone who walked in. She had given August three jobs in eight months, each one further north than the last, and she had never once asked about the bridge. August suspected this was not kindness but economy: questions cost time, and time was the only inventory Ferren guarded more jealously than her ledger.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"The ferry was."

"The ferry is always late. You should plan around it." She slid a packet across the desk. Oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, sealed at both ends with stamps August didn't recognize — not government, not quite military either, something between, the way certain things in the north were always between. "Survey station at Kestrel Siding. End of the Marrow Line."

August turned the packet over. The seals were intact, heavy, the paper underneath thick enough to be money. "There's no telegraph to Kestrel?"

"There's a wire that half works when the wind's right. Orders like these don't travel by wire anyway." Ferren finally looked up. "You'll be paid on delivery. Forty crowns, plus passage back if you want it."

Forty crowns. August's debts ran past four hundred, accruing interest at a rate the lender described as *compassionate*, which told August everything about how the lender understood compassion. Still, forty crowns was forty crowns, and the north was cheap, and there was nothing keeping them in Greyharbor except the rain and the shame, and both were portable.

"Who are the orders from?" August asked. "Who's the survey team?"

Ferren's pen paused above her ledger for half a heartbeat — long enough for August, who had spent a career reading structures for the flaw hidden inside them, to notice the hesitation the way they'd notice a hairline crack in a girder.

"Crown Survey," she said. "Coastal division. They're mapping the old pass lines."

"There are no old pass lines. They were closed after the war."

"They're mapping them anyway." She pushed a second object across the desk: a folded sheet of paper, soft at the creases from handling. "Route authorization. Shows you what track is open to you, which won't be much, and what's closed, which will be everything else. Sign here."

August signed. Then, because the paper was already in their hands and curiosity was the one appetite debt had never managed to starve, they unfolded it.

It was a strip map of the northern network, printed in two colors. The living lines ran in confident black — Greyharbor to Marrow Junction, Marrow Junction to the coast, the coastal spur south. Everything else was overprinted in a flat bureaucratic red: *CLOSED TO TRAFFIC BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF WAYS AND WORKS.* The red covered the high country like a wound dressing. The Marrow Line climbing toward the pass. The Brandt branch dropping into the valley settlements. And threading through it all, drawn in fine dotted red as though the draftsman had wanted to record the lines' ghosts without admitting they'd ever lived, the old war-era routes through the glacier pass itself — the ones the trains had used when the mountains were a front and the timetable was written around avalanches.

August knew those lines. Not from maps — from stories. Their father had driven freight through the pass in the last year of the war, before the ice shifted and took a tunnel and the Ministry decided the mountains could keep their secrets. August had been six years old. They remembered only fragments: the smell of coal smoke in a cold cab, a hand larger than their whole head resting on the throttle, a voice saying *the mountain gives you six tides, little wren, and then it takes the road back.*

Little wren. Their father had called them that because August had been small and quick and always looking at the horizon instead of the ground. The nickname had died with him. The compass hadn't.

* * *

August carried the compass everywhere, though it no longer pointed anywhere useful, and had not since the day it arrived in a parcel along with a lawyer's letter and a bill for the shipping.

It was a surveyor's compass, brass-cased, heavy for its size, with a mother-of-pearl face gone the color of old teeth. The needle swung free and honest enough, but it had developed a fixed prejudice: left alone, it never settled on north. It always drifted east-northeast, a few degrees off true, stubbornly, every time, as if it remembered some bearing its owner had once held dear. August had checked it against church spires and known meridians in five towns. Always the same drift. Always east-northeast. Toward the pass, though August had never had the heart to confirm it precisely, because confirming it would mean admitting the thing was broken in a way that meant something.

Their father had been dead nine years when the compass came home. He'd walked out of August's life the ordinary way first — the slow estrangement after August chose the railways over the family land, the letters growing shorter, the visits growing rarer — and then the final way, in a hospital in Marrow Junction, alone, which August learned about eleven days later because the telegram had been sent to an address August had moved out of two years prior. That was the arithmetic August carried under the debt and the disgrace, heavier than either: eleven days. Their father had waited, perhaps, eleven days.

The letter had said only: *To my child, who reads lines. It doesn't point north. It never did. Figure out what it points to.*

August had figured out nothing in nine years. But they carried it, in the breast pocket, over the heart, where its small weight sat like a question they kept declining to answer.

On the ferry north, and now in the chandler's stairwell, and later in the third-class carriage with its torn upholstery and its smell of paraffin and wet dog, the compass rode where it always rode. Once, somewhere past the last signal box before the dead zone began, August took it out and watched the needle strain away from north, trembling, eager, like a dog at the end of a rope.

East-northeast. Toward the red lines on Ferren's map. Toward Kestrel Siding.

August put it away and told themselves it was a broken instrument and nothing more. They were getting worse at believing that.

* * *

The train gave them up at a halt called Tarnwater, which consisted of a platform, a water tower, and a stationmaster who looked at August's route authorization as though it were a confession.

"Kestrel Siding," he read aloud. "Nobody goes to Kestrel. Line's scheduled for closure next season. They pulled the maintenance gangs in spring." He handed the paper back. "You can walk the ballast. Fourteen miles. Or wait for the goods drag Tuesday, if it runs, which it hasn't since June."

"I'll walk."

He shrugged with the entire upper half of his body, a gesture that seemed to include the weather, the Ministry, and the general condition of the world. "Mind the culverts. Some of them are more suggestion than structure now."

The fourteen miles were the first honest work August had done in months, and their body — which had gone soft in the way of men who eat when they can and walk only to collect what they're owed — complained about it in a vocabulary of aches. But there was a steadiness in walking a line. Even a dying one. Even a closed one. The rails made two clean arguments running parallel into the distance, and August's feet found their rhythm on the ties, and for a while the shame receded to a low background hum, the way pain does when the body has real work.

The country climbed. The rain thinned to a cold drifting mist. The pines gave way to birch, the birch to scrub, the scrub to stone laced with snow in the shadows. And everywhere, the evidence of abandonment, arranged with a kind of municipal thoroughness: signals locked at danger and their glasses painted over. Sidings with their points chained and padlocked. Kilometer posts whitewashed with a black band — the Ministry's mark for *no traffic* — marching away into the mist like a funeral procession.

And yet.

At mile nine, August stopped. The rails ahead gleamed.

Not rusted. Not the soft brown bloom that claims unused steel within a season. These were bright along the crown, polished, the way railheads shine when wheels have passed over them recently and often. August crouched and laid a palm flat on the rail. Cold, of course. But smooth. Almost oily with use.

They walked on and counted ties, and at mile eleven they found the answer to the shine: wheel marks in the mud beside the track — narrow, dual, the tread of a handcar or a light inspection trolley — pressed deep and fresh, the edges still sharp, hours old at most. And beside them, boot prints. Several sets. Heading up-line. Toward Kestrel.

August stood in the mist with the rain gathering on their collar and felt the first cold thread of something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite excitement, but lived in the same neighborhood as both. A closed line did not polish its own rails. A closed line did not grow fresh wheel marks like mushrooms after rain. Someone was using this road that the Ministry had declared dead, and using it heavily, and whatever orders August carried in oilcloth and waxed cord were almost certainly connected to that fact, and none of that was a courier's business.

A courier delivers. A courier does not open the envelope, does not ask why the seals bear no ministry August recognized, does not wonder why a survey team needs armed-grade sealing wax on routine mapping orders.

August was a courier because it was the only work left. But underneath the courier, older and less employable, there was still an engineer, and the engineer looked at those bright rails the way a doctor looks at a pulse.

* * *

Kestrel Siding announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, cold and sour, the ghost of a fire rather than the fact of one. Then the buildings resolved out of the mist — a long low barracks, a store shed, an instrument hut with its shutter banging in a rhythm too irregular for wind alone, and beyond them the stub of the line ending in a buffer stop furred with orange lichen.

No smoke rose. No light showed. But the door of the barracks stood open, swinging gently, and doors do not stand open in country like this unless someone means to come back through them soon.

August set down the bag. Drew the packet from inside their coat, where it had ridden dry and warm against their chest for six days. The compass, in its pocket over their heart, felt suddenly loud, the way a watch feels loud in a quiet room.

They crossed the yard, boots crunching on cinders gone soft with moss, and stopped at the threshold of the barracks door. Inside: a table set for a meal. Plates. Bread, gone to green mold. A pot of tea, brewed and abandoned so long ago that the leaves had dried back onto the bottom in a black crust. Chairs pushed back in a hurry, one of them overturned.

On the table, weighted down by a theodolite case, lay a map of the pass country — the same map August had studied in Ferren's office, but annotated now, hand-inked, dense with measurements and marginal notes in at least three different hands. One annotation circled a point deep in the glacier's blue mass, far off any line ever surveyed, and beside the circle, in a quick urgent script:

*Tide three. Bring lamps. — O.*

The shutter banged again, out in the instrument hut. Bang. Pause. Bang-bang. Not wind. Wind doesn't keep time.

August stood in the doorway of the abandoned meal with the sealed orders in their hands and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives too late to be useful, that they had not been hired to deliver a packet. They had been hired to be the kind of person nobody would miss — a disgraced courier on a dead line, carrying sealed paper into empty country — and that the emptiness was the message, and the message had been waiting here longer than the bread had been molding.

Somewhere up-valley, faint as a memory, came a sound August knew better than their own heartbeat: the far-off knock of steel on steel. Rails being struck. Or rails being broken.

The compass needle, when August finally let themselves check, strained east-northeast and trembled there, patient as a held breath.

Fourteen miles of bright rail behind them. An open door ahead. And the light going out of the sky like a tide going out of a channel.

August Whitlock stepped inside, and shut the door against the dark, and lit the lamp.
