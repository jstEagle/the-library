## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The station appeared out of the rain the way a shipwreck appears out of fog — all at once, and wrong.

August Whitlock had been climbing for six hours, following the old survey road that switchbacked up the shoulder of the mountain, and in all that time they had passed no one. Not a mail rider, not a woodcutter, not a goat. The road itself had a strange quality of disuse, as though it had been abandoned mid-sentence: culverts still sound, grades still true, but the drainage ditches silting green, the marker posts leaning like drunk men. A road built to be used, then deliberately forgotten. August knew the difference between a road that had died and a road that had been killed. This was the latter.

Their father's compass hung at their chest on a leather cord, and August had caught themselves checking it more often than the map. The needle did not point north. It never had, not since August was old enough to notice — it swung a few degrees east of true and held there, stubborn as a mule, as though the pass itself exerted some private gravity on it. As a child August had thought it broken. As an adult they had thought it sentimental. Now, with the blacklisted name Whitlock signed into a courier's ledger for the first time in three years, they thought it might be the only honest instrument they owned.

The station sat in a saddle of the ridge where two closed lines met, a long low building of timber and slate with a signal mast rising from its roof like a fishing rod over still water. Smoke should have been rising from its chimney. It was not.

August stood in the rain at the edge of the yard and let the wrongness settle over them the way they had learned to let a bridge's vibration settle — not panicking, not dismissing, just reading. The yard was tidy. Too tidy. A stack of split cordwood under an oilcloth. A handcar on a stub siding, its handles wrapped in canvas against the weather. A dog kennel with a chain but no dog. Everything arranged for people who intended to stay through winter.

And the door of the station stood open six inches, swinging gently in the wind.

"Hello the station!" August called, and the rain swallowed the words.

They waited the proper interval. They called again, louder, adding their name and their business, because a man who had spent years being blamed for things learned to make his intentions legible. Nothing answered but the creak of the door on its hinges.

August crossed the yard with their boots squelching and pushed the door wide.

The smell hit first — cold tea, woodsmoke gone stale, lamp oil, and under it all the faint sweetish rot of a fire that had been left to die in its own ash. August stood on the threshold and did not go in, because the first rule of any scene was to read it before disturbing it, and this scene was shouting.

A meal sat on the table. Not a finished meal, not a prepared one — a *mid* meal. Three tin plates, each half-eaten: bread torn rather than cut, a pot of stew with the ladle still standing in it, a congealed skin over the surface like ice on a pond. Three cups. One of them had been knocked over, and the tea had dried in a brown starburst on the wood, and August's eye went to that starburst and stayed there because it was the first thing in the room that suggested haste rather than interruption. A man does not knock over his tea because he heard a knock at the door.

The instruments were laid out on the long table by the window as though someone had been in the middle of calibrating them: a theodolite with its case open and its lens cap off, a barometer, a set of drawing instruments pinned down on a half-finished chart of the pass — the same pass, August realized, that was inked on the sealed orders in their breast pocket, drawn here in finer detail by hands that knew it better. A pencil lay across the chart where it had been set down mid-measurement. Not dropped. Set down, the way you set down a pencil when you mean to pick it up again in a moment.

The fire in the stove was cold. August crossed to it and laid the back of their hand near the ash. Stone cold, and not freshly cold — the ash was compacted, damp at the edges from ambient moisture, days old at least. The tea on the table had dried to a crust that flaked when August touched it. Whatever had happened here had happened days ago, and the rain since then had been busy erasing the world.

August went back outside and circled the station slowly, and this was where the dread stopped being a feeling and became a fact.

The ground around the building was churned mud, soft as bread dough after six hours of rain. August's own boots left prints deep enough to hold water. The ground remembered every footstep it received — and it received none but theirs. No prints leading away from the door toward the woodshed, the outhouse, the signal mast, the road. No prints leading anywhere at all. Three men had sat down to a meal here, days ago, and had apparently evaporated through the floorboards.

Unless they had gone before the rain. August made themselves consider it, made themselves be the engineer and not the frightened courier. The rain had started the previous evening — a steady, soaking, print-erasing rain. The tea had dried, not washed away, which meant it had dried *before* the rain could reach it, which meant the door had been closed when it dried, which meant — August stopped in the mud and turned a slow circle — which meant the door had been *opened* since the rain began, by someone, and that someone had left no prints either.

August stood in the yard with the rain running down their collar and made themselves say it plainly, the way they'd once said *the span is deflecting* out loud in the wind because saying it made it real and real things could be engineered around.

"Someone opened this door after the rain started, and left no tracks."

Their voice sounded small against the mountain. Above the station, the signal mast swayed, and from its arm hung a signal lamp — a great brass-and-glass lantern of the kind used to flag trains through the pass, and as August watched it, it *ticked*.

Not swung. Ticked. A small, regular, mechanical sound, like a clock with a limp.

August climbed the mast ladder with their heart going harder than the climb justified. The lamp was an old semaphore-pattern burner, and its mechanism — a clockwork wick-feeder and shutter, built to hold a colored signal steady through the night — was running down. The shutter was trying to close and couldn't quite, catching, releasing, catching, each catch ticking through the frame like a pulse. August had serviced a hundred of these mechanisms. They ran eight hours on a full wind. Someone had wound this one eight hours ago, or twelve, or twenty — and then no one had come back to wind it again, and it was dying by inches in the rain, ticking out the last of someone's intention.

August reached through the frame and stopped the mechanism. The silence afterward was enormous.

* * *

Inside, August did what they had been hired to do and hated themselves a little for how long it took them to do it. The sealed orders were to be delivered to the station's strongbox, and the strongbox sat behind the stationmaster's desk, unlocked, empty, its door hanging open like a slack jaw. August set the oilskin packet on the desk, then picked it up again, then set it down. Delivering orders to an empty room felt like posting a letter into a grave.

Instead they read the room the way they had been taught to read a bridge after a failure — not for what was broken, but for what the breaking revealed.

Three men, then, or three places at a table. The stationmaster, the surveyor, and one more — the plates suggested three appetites of different sizes. Their gear was still here. That was the detail August kept circling back to, the one that would not sit level. Coats on their hooks. Boots by the door, two pairs, and a third pair under a bunk, resoled recently with good leather. A rifle racked above the stove with dust on the barrel — dust that had not been disturbed. Men who left a station in a hurry took their rifles. Men who expected to come back took their coats. These men had left behind everything a man needs to stay alive at four thousand feet in autumn, which meant they had not expected to leave at all.

And they had not gone out the door. August walked the interior twice, tapping floorboards, checking the cellar hatch (bolted from inside, the cellar empty but for a barrel of lamp oil), examining the chimney (too narrow for a grown man, and the flue cold for days). There was no other way out. There was the door, and the door opened on unmarked mud, and the mud had been drinking rain for a day and a night.

August sat down at the table, in the chair with the least-worn seat, and looked at the half-finished chart. The survey lines ran up the closed line toward the pass, and then they stopped. Not faded. Stopped — a clean pencil line ending in the middle of the glacier's tongue, with a small annotation beside it in a cramped, precise hand: *light anomaly, refrozen flow, see log.* The survey log was not on the table. August searched the station for it — drawers, bunks, the desk's locked compartment, which stood open and empty like the strongbox. No log. No field notebooks. No personal papers at all, in fact; the bunks held blankets and nothing else, no letters, no photographs, no books with names in the flyleaf. It was as if the men had been distilled down to their gear, their clothing, and their half-eaten supper, and everything that made them *them* had been carried away.

Or taken.

August's hand had gone, without their deciding it, to the compass at their chest. The needle swung its few degrees east of north and held, pointing up the valley, toward the pass, toward the place where the survey lines stopped. Their father had carried this compass through forty years on the mountain lines and had told August, the last time they'd spoken — a stiff, formal conversation conducted across the wreckage of August's career, two people who loved each other and did not know how to say so — that a compass didn't point where you wanted to go. It pointed where you were needed. August had thought it an old man's poetry. Sitting in a dead man's chair in a dead station, they were no longer sure.

* * *

The telegraph office was four miles down the valley at the junction settlement, and August walked it in failing light, telling themselves the whole way that the exercise was routine. A station found empty was a matter for the line agent, the magistrate, the telegraph. There were procedures. August had spent three years with no procedures of their own, no office, no line, no name worth signing — they had become very good at other people's procedures.

The junction was less a settlement than a knot: a water tower, a coal tipple, a row of company houses the color of wet slate, and the telegraph office with its one lit window, its sign reading *VOSS — LINES & MESSAGES* in paint gone gray. August knocked, and the door opened on a woman perhaps thirty, sleeves rolled, a telegram blank in one hand and a pencil behind her ear, looking up at August with the flat, rapid assessment of someone who read strangers for a living.

"You're off the mountain road," she said. Not a question. "You're the courier. Whitlock." She said the name without inflection, and August braced out of habit for the flicker — the *Whitlock, the bridge, the one who* — and it did not come. She only stepped back to let them in. "You're late. The wire from the capital said three days ago."

"The road's slow." August stamped rain off their boots on her mat and took in the office in a glance: the telegraph key on its brass stand, polished by use; a wall of pigeonholes stuffed with message flimsies; a cot in the corner, neatly made; a kettle just off the boil. A person lived inside this work the way a hermit crab lives inside its shell. "Sera Voss?"

"Sera." She poured tea without asking, which August decided was the best thing that had happened to them in a year, and set the cup in their hands. "You found the station."

It was not a question either, and August looked up sharply. "How did you—"

"Because you're standing here instead of there, and because you have the face of a man who's been reading mud." She sat down across the desk from them, and her eyes were steady and dark and entirely without comfort. "Tell me."

August told her. They told it in the order they'd found it — the open door, the meal, the cold stove, the unmarked ground, the dying lamp — and they watched her face while they did it, and this was the part that turned the dread from a cold thing into a living one: she did not interrupt, she did not frown, and she was not surprised. She listened the way a doctor listens to symptoms she has seen before.

When August finished, Sera Voss set down her cup with a small, precise click.

"Three men," she said. "Stationmaster Grell, surveyor Okafor, and the third one — the big one, the cook — was named Brandt, out of the valley settlement. They were provisioned for six weeks." She looked at the window, at the rain. "The government wire said the survey team would arrive on the fourteenth. I was to relay their daily position reports up the line." Her jaw tightened. "No team ever arrived."

August set down their own cup. "That's not possible. I've *been* in their station. They arrived, they ate a meal, they started a survey—"

"I have sat at this key every day for eleven years," Sera said, and her voice did not rise, which somehow made it worse. "Every train that moves on any line within sixty miles of this junction passes through my hands. The survey team never sent a position report. Never sent a single word. And no one came down the mountain road to tell me otherwise, because no one came down the mountain road at all." She met August's eyes. "You walked past my window this afternoon. I saw you on the road an hour before you reached me. You are the first person to come *down* that road in nine days. The first person to come *up* it was you, this morning."

"The rain—"

"The rain started the evening of the fifteenth." She said it flatly. "You've done the arithmetic already. I can see it. The tea was dry, the ash was cold, and the door was open in the rain. So either those men walked out before the rain and the mud is lying to you, or—" she stopped herself, and August watched her decide not to say the thing, and then watched her say it anyway, because she was evidently a woman who did not flinch from arithmetic. "Or something came up that road after dark on the fifteenth that made three grown men leave their coats, their boots, their rifles, and their supper, and go out into the night without a sound."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the world.

"Or," August said slowly, "someone came who they knew. Someone they'd open the door for, and go with, quietly, at night." They thought of the chart, the survey lines ending mid-glacier, the annotation in the cramped hand. *Light anomaly, refrozen flow.* They thought of the empty strongbox, the empty log compartment, the distilled bunks. "And someone came back after the rain began and opened the station door and took the papers and left no tracks."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then she stood, went to the pigeonhole wall, and pulled a flimsy from a slot marked with a date nine days old. She did not hand it to August; she read it aloud, her voice level as a spirit gauge.

*"Advise capital survey party delayed en route. No cause for concern. Crane."*

"Crane," August said.

"Aldous Crane. Salvage and reconstruction. He's been provisioning a fleet at the port for a fortnight — barges, engines, two hundred men. Buying up every scrap of blasting powder within a hundred miles." Sera set the flimsy down on the desk between them, face up, like a card. "The government wire that hired your survey team was countersigned by his office. I checked. I check everything. It's the only way to stay honest with a key in your hand." She looked at August, and for the first time something moved behind the steadiness — not fear exactly, but its first cousin. "You delivered sealed orders to an empty station, Mr. Whitlock. The question isn't where those men went."

She tapped the flimsy once with one finger.

"It's what they found up there that made a man like Crane send that wire — nine days before anyone else knew they were missing."

The compass hung at August's chest, and beneath their shirt, without any wind to move it, the needle held its stubborn line — east of north, up the valley, toward the pass, toward six tides that were, though neither of them yet knew it, already draining away.

August looked at the wire, and then at Sera, and heard themselves say the thing they had walked up a dead road to say.

"Show me the timetable for the tides."
