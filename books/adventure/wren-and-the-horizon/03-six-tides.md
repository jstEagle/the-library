## 3. Six Tides

The telegraph office at Voss's Crossing smelled of lamp oil and cold iron, and Sera Voss had spent eleven years learning to read it the way other people read faces. The building breathed. In summer it sighed through warped boards; in winter it groaned like a man carrying too much. Tonight, with the storm gone and the stars hard as nailheads over the ridge, it was quiet — the particular quiet of a place that had just heard something it didn't want to believe.

She sat across the desk from August Whitlock and watched them turn the survey team's last telegram between two fingers. The paper had gone soft at the creases, handled too many times by hands that wanted it to say something different.

"You're telling me," August said slowly, "that no one came through here."

"I'm telling you what I know." Sera pulled the logbook toward her and turned it so the lamplight caught the columns. "Every train, every handcar, every rider on the closed line — I log them. It's my job and half my sanity. The last entry before your courier run is the mail sledge in March." She tapped the page. "No survey team. Six men, government papers, instruments — they'd have come through this office or they wouldn't have come through at all. There is no other way onto that line except over the pass, and nobody crosses the pass in March unless they're trying to die."

August didn't answer right away. In the lamp's low light their face was all angles and shadow, a face built for being looked past — which, Sera suspected, was exactly how they'd survived the last few years. She'd read about the bridge failure in the coastal papers, back when the story still had legs: the *Whitlock Span Collapse*, three dead, an engineer struck from the rolls. The papers had used a photograph that made August look either guilty or haunted, depending on how generous you felt.

Sera was not, by nature, generous. But she knew what a lie looked like when it arrived by wire, and she remembered thinking, even then, that the numbers in the inquiry report didn't sit right. Load tolerances quoted to two decimal places from a bridge whose inspection records had been "regrettably lost" in a filing fire.

"So where are they?" August said finally. Not to her, quite. To the room. To the dead lamp ticking on its shelf behind her.

"That," Sera said, "is the question I've been asking for nine days."

* * *

She'd found the station on the fourth day after the telegrams stopped.

That part she hadn't told anyone yet, not fully — not the magistrate's clerk who'd taken her statement with the enthusiasm of a man filling out a form, and not the port authority, who had more interest in freight tonnage than in six missing men on a line the government itself had closed. But she told August, because August had come four hundred miles on a courier contract to deliver sealed orders to people who were already gone, and because there was something in the way August held silence — carefully, like a full cup — that made her want to put things down.

Kettle Station sat at the mouth of the high valley, where the closed line bent north into country no timetable acknowledged. She'd ridden up on the maintenance velocipede, alone, because the operator's code said you checked your own line before you reported it dead. The door had been unlocked. That was the first wrong thing; Okafor's team had been meticulous, the kind of government men who locked boxes inside boxes.

Inside, the meal was still on the table. Tin plates, bread gone to green fuzz, a pot of tea brewed strong enough that the tannin had stained the bottom black. Six chairs. Five pushed back in a hurry, one still tucked in neat as Sunday. Their instruments lay out on the workbench — theodolite under its canvas, levels racked, a barometer with its needle frozen mid-fall as if the air itself had flinched. The stove was cold, but the ash in it wasn't old. And the signal lamp on the shelf — the emergency lamp, the one you lit if you were trapped and wanted the next patrol to find you — had burned down to nothing and left its wick ticking as the metal cooled. Ticking. Nine days later, when she'd first heard it, she'd nearly shot the shelf.

"No tracks," August said now. They'd gone very still. "You said no tracks beyond the door."

"Fresh snow the night they vanished, and none since until yesterday. Whatever happened at Kettle Station happened indoors, or happened without touching the ground." Sera closed the logbook. "And here's the part that keeps me up. The door wasn't forced. Nothing was taken. If Crane's people wanted them gone quietly, why leave a table set for six?"

"Crane?" August looked up sharply. "Aldous Crane?"

"You know him."

"Everyone in my profession knows the name." August's voice had flattened into something careful. "He buys railroads the way other men buy gloves. Salvage, receivership, restructuring — he owns the debt and then he owns the road. What's he doing at this end of the world?"

"Provisioning." Sera rose and went to the window, partly to look at the harbor lights strung along the dark water below the town, and partly because she didn't want to watch August's face while she said the rest. "Three steamers at the coal wharf since Tuesday. Cranes — actual cranes, ship-mounted — and enough timber and blasting powder to build a town or take one apart. They've hired every strong back and loose mouth on the coast. They fly his house flag. A black kestrel on gray."

Through the glass she could see the wharf lamps doubled in the still water, and the dark shapes of the steamers breathing smoke against the stars. Even at this distance, the fleet looked less like ships than like animals gathered at a carcass.

"The government closes a line," she said, "and within the season, Aldous Crane sends a salvage flotilla to a glacier at the ass-end of nowhere. You don't provision three steamers for ice nobody's certified as navigable. He knows something's in there. He's known for months."

August was quiet long enough that she turned around. When she did, she found them looking at the map — the old one, the courier's sealed orders spread open beside the survey team's route overlay, and beneath both, weighted at the corners with her inkwell and a hammer, the thing August had carried off the mountain lines like a relic: a surveyor's map of the closed system, hand-annotated, decades out of date, with certain lines traced over in faded violet ink.

"Your father's?" she asked.

"My father's." August touched the violet tracery without quite pressing down. "He drove these lines before the closure. After the war, when they were hauling everything that hadn't rotted back down the coast." A pause. "He never talked about the pass. He talked about everything else — grades, bridges, weather — but not the pass. Just marked it."

"And the compass."

August's hand went to their coat pocket, an unconscious motion, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it's still there. "Family piece. The needle doesn't settle north. It pulls toward the pass, always has. My mother swore it was broken. My father swore it wasn't." Something moved behind their eyes — grief, maybe, or the beginning of a suspicion with teeth. "I thought it was sentiment. Now I'm standing in a telegraph office looking at a map he traced in violet, and I'm wondering what else he knew."

* * *

Sera made coffee — real coffee, hoarded, because whatever the next days held they would not be kind, and kindness began with coffee — and while it boiled she laid out the geography the way her grandfather had taught her, with a pencil stub and the flat of her hand.

"Here's what matters." She drew the coastline, then the fjord cutting inland, then the white mass at its head. "The Meridian Glacier comes down to tidewater here — the calving face. Behind it, the old war-era supply route ran through a tunnel the ice has mostly swallowed. Your survey team's route overlay puts them heading for exactly that stretch. The wreck — if there is a wreck — is somewhere in the ice above the face."

"There's a wreck," August said. Not a guess. The certainty in it startled them both; Sera saw August hear it themselves and file it away.

"Then here's the clock." She tapped the glacier's foot. "Twice a day, the tide goes out. And twice a day, for a few hours, the water drops far enough that the tunnel mouth at the base of the face stands open — dry passage, if you can call anything dry under a hundred feet of moving ice. The old drivers called it the tide gate. My grandfather ran supplies through it in the last winter of the war, when the port was shelled. He said you rode in on the ebb and you prayed you rode out on the next one, because the ice doesn't care about your schedule."

She drew six small marks along the fjord, one after another, like stitches closing a wound.

"But the gate is closing for the year. Calving season starts in — I make it nineteen days, maybe twenty. Once the face starts shedding, the whole lower tongue destabilizes. No more open tides. No more tunnel. Whatever's in that ice stays in that ice until the glacier decides otherwise, and glaciers decide in centuries." She looked up. "Six usable tides left, August. Twelve openings, if you count ins and outs separately, but the safe count is six round passages. After that, the door shuts."

"Six tides." August said it the way a man reads a sentence of a death warrant, testing each word for mercy and finding none. "And Crane's fleet is provisioning now."

"Coal by the ton. Powder. They brought a steam donkey engine in pieces on the second steamer — I watched them land it. That's not an expedition, that's an operation." Sera wrapped her hands around her own cup, needing the heat. "They'll go in on the same tides we would. Probably the same first one."

"Then the survey team—" August stopped. Started again, quieter. "Okafor's orders were sealed. Government seal. I was paid to deliver them, not read them, and I didn't. But a survey team doesn't vanish mid-meal and leave a signal lamp burning unless someone wanted them stopped. And nobody spends three steamers' worth of money to stop a survey team unless the survey team found—"

"Found, or was about to find," Sera finished.

"—something worth killing for." August set the telegram down at last, smoothing it flat against the desk as though it might confess under pressure. "Wrecked supply train. War's end. The official record says the loss was weather — the whole train, written off in a single line. I've seen that line. Everyone in engineering school saw that line; it was the textbook example of terrain beating machinery." Their jaw tightened. "Textbooks don't ask what was on the train."

"And you think Crane knows."

"I think Crane doesn't gamble. I think he sends three steamers and a steam donkey to a certified-dead glacier only when he's holding a document that says what's under the ice and what it's worth." August stood, restless, and walked to the window beside her. Up close they smelled of woodsmoke and the long road, and there was a tremor in their hands they kept trying to disguise as adjustment of cuffs. "Which means somewhere there's a manifest. Or a ledger. Or a man who signed something he shouldn't have. And Okafor's team was close enough to it that Crane decided six government men were cheaper drowned in paperwork than allowed to file a report."

Outside, the harbor lay black and glittering, and one of the steamers — the nearest, the one flying the kestrel — let go a long plume of smoke as her fires were banked up for the morning. Preparing. Counting tides of her own.

"They'll sail for the head of the fjord tomorrow," Sera said. "Weather's fair. Tide tables favor them."

"Then we're already behind." August turned from the window, and Sera watched the change come over them — not confidence, nothing so clean as that, but a settling, the way a bridge settles onto its piers when the load comes on. Shame and hunger and fury finding a structure to hold. "Six tides. One vanished team. One salvage fleet. And me — a blacklisted engineer with a courier's fee, a dead man's compass, and a map with violet lines."

"You forgot one thing," Sera said. She reached under the counter and brought out the velocipede key and her own coat. "You forgot the only person on this coast who can read the ice, the tides, and Crane's charter filings — because I filed half of them myself when I was younger and stupider and worked for a shipping concern he swallowed."

August looked at her for a long moment. "Why? You don't know me. By every account you should be showing me the door."

"Because somebody set a table for six and blew out the lamp," Sera said, "and I've been listening to this office tick for nine nights, and I am done listening." She killed the lamp herself, deliberately, and the dark came in clean. "First tide is day after tomorrow, at dawn. We ride up tonight."

In the black water below, the kestrel steamer answered with a whistle — long, low, and patient, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

August was already pulling on their coat, the compass a small hard weight in the pocket, pointing, as it always pointed, toward the pass.
