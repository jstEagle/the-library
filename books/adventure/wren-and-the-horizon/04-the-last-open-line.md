## 4. The Last Open Line

The handcar sat where Sera said it would, under a tarp of patched canvas behind the telegraph station, its handles worn to a shine by decades of palms. It was a relic from the years when the line had lived — when ore cars and mail sacks and whole families bound for the coast had rattled through the pass on schedule. Now it looked like what it was: a machine waiting for a world that had stopped needing it.

August ran a hand along the rail beside it. Cold iron, dry, no rust bloom worth mentioning. Someone had been oiling this stretch of track.

"You keep it maintained," August said.

Sera stood in the gray light with her collar up and a canvas satchel over one shoulder, looking less like a telegraph operator than she had the night before. In daylight she was all angles and economy, a woman who moved like she'd already counted the cost of every step before taking it. "The valley keeps it. Ilsa Brandt's people, mostly. They never believed the line was dead. Only sleeping." She patted the car's frame. "Grandfather Brandt drove the last train through. His granddaughter intends there'll be a first one after."

"And the company?"

"The company closed the books and went home to the capital. What they don't know won't cost them." She swung the tarp off. "You've read the orders?"

August had. Twice. The sealed envelope from the courier job had turned out to be exactly what it appeared: instructions for a government survey team that had gone silent eleven days ago, along with authority — thin, provisional, printed on cheap paper — for the bearer to ascertain their whereabouts. It was not much. It was more than August had held in three years.

"Six tides," August said. "You told me. How long between them?"

"Roughly thirty hours at this season, if the meltwater runs true. The glacier mouth drains at low water and the tunnel stands open. Then the river rises and fills the lower gallery, and you wait for the next drain. Six drains before calving season, and then the ice gives birth to itself and nothing goes in or out until next summer." Sera looked north, toward the white shoulder of the pass visible even from here, a pale scar between two dark peaks. "The survey team knew the schedule. They went in on the second tide of the season."

"Which means?"

"They've missed four already." She said it flatly, but August heard what was underneath it, the same thing August felt in their own chest: arithmetic that only ended one way, unless someone moved fast.

They loaded the car in silence — rope, lamps, tools, the cold rations Sera had traded for in the village, August's father's compass riding in the breast pocket where it always rode. Then they took the handles and pumped, and the handcar rolled out onto the closed line, and the closed line carried them north into country no timetable remembered.

* * *

For the first hour the track behaved like any track. Gravel ballast furred with moss, ties graying, weeds pushing up between the rails in slow green insubordination — but sound. Level. The grade climbed so gently it felt like level, which was the mark of engineering done by people who respected both the mountain and the men who'd have to work it. August found themselves reading the roadbed the way other people read faces, and liking what it said.

Then the culvert came, and the liking died.

It was a small crossing, a mountain stream channeled under the fill in a stone arch older than the railroad, and it had been blown. Not washed out, not collapsed with age — blown. The charge had been set low, tucked into the downstream face where the stone was thinnest, and it had lifted the crown of the arch clean off while leaving the fill above intact enough to settle rather than spill. A careful job. An economical job. Enough damage to stop a train, not enough to waste powder.

August set the brake and got down and walked it, and the longer they walked it the colder they felt.

"This wasn't done by farmers," they said. "Or by boys with stolen dynamite playing at war against the company."

"No?"

"Farmers blow the middle. Boys blow too much." August crouched at the crater lip, turning over a shard of stone with a toe. "This is drilled work. Single charge, placed by someone who read the rock first. Whoever did this wanted the line stopped without making noise about it. Without making the company send an inspector, or the magistrate send questions. Just a quiet hole in a quiet place on a line nobody officially uses anymore."

Sera had come down beside them. She looked at the culvert the way she looked at telegrams, he thought — extracting meaning, discarding decoration. "Someone who wanted the route unusable," she said slowly, "except to people who didn't need the route. Who could walk around a broken culvert carrying what they needed on their backs."

"Or who knew every gap in the line because they'd helped build it." August stood. Their hands had gone cold inside their gloves, and it wasn't the morning air. There was a signature in this destruction, a handwriting, and it was nagging at them from some drawer of memory they hadn't opened in years.

They pumped on.

* * *

The second sabotage came at midday, and it was worse, and it was better made.

Rails spiked. That was how August thought of it before they even saw it clearly — the handcar began to shudder a quarter mile out, a rhythm wrong in the wheels, and August called the halt and walked ahead alone, and there it was. Three lengths of rail, each driven through from below with a length of drill steel, the points ground sharp and punched up through the foot of the rail into the flangeway. Not enough to derail a slow handcar — August had felt it coming and eased across — but certain death to anything moving at speed. A locomotive hitting those at forty miles an hour would leave the rails, leave the embankment, and leave the world.

The drill steels were new-ground. The burrs on the punch holes were bright. This was days old. Perhaps hours.

And the placement — August knelt and measured it with their eyes, because they couldn't help measuring, it was the disease they'd been born with — the placement was textbook. Not at a curve where wreckage would be obvious, not on a grade where a crew might be watching. Here, on a straight and shaded stretch where the rails sang clear and a driver would have opened the regulator wide. Where the sabotage would do its full work.

"Whoever did this," Sera said behind them, "wanted a train to die here."

"Yes."

"Not just stopped. Killed."

"Yes." August straightened. The word hung between them, and neither reached for it again, because there was nowhere to reach. A culvert blown could be caution; could be sealing a route against pursuit. Rails spiked for speed was murder with an engineer's license number on it.

And still the handwriting nagged. August stood over the spiked rail and let the memory come up on its own, the way a fish comes up to light, and when it surfaced they almost wished it hadn't.

There had been a man at the works, in the years before the bridge — before the bridge, before the inquiry, before the blacklisting that had eaten August's name like rust eats iron. Halvard. Old Halvard, who had taught a hungry apprentice that a structure confesses before it fails, that you listen with your hands. Halvard, who could read rock like scripture and place a charge so precisely the stone seemed to apologize as it came apart. Halvard, who had signed August's journeyman papers, who had once said — drunk, one winter night, in the warm smell of the forge — *you're the best thing I ever built, Whitlock, and I built better bridges than God.*

August had not thought of him in two years. You did not think of the people who might have testified and hadn't.

But this — the drilled culvert, the economical charge, the textbook cruelty of the spike placement — this was his grammar. Every engineer had one, the way every writer did. The way you chamfered an edge, the order you worked in, the sins you permitted yourself. August had spent three bitter years learning to recognize their own grammar in everything they touched. And this was Halvard's, letter for letter.

"Sera," they said. "The survey team that vanished. Who recommended them for the commission?"

She frowned. "I don't— the ministry papers said a consulting engineer vetted the route plan. Why?"

"What was his name?"

"I don't remember. Something northern. H—" She stopped. Her eyes came up to August's face, and whatever she saw there made her go very still. "Halvard," she said. "Halvard Renn. Yes. He certified the survey route himself."

August looked north, up the line, toward the pass where the glacier waited with its six tides and its silence, and felt the unease in their chest turn over and show its underside, and the underside had a face they knew.

"He taught me everything I know about breaking things cleanly," August said. "Now get on the car. We're losing the light, and I want to see what else my teacher left on this line."

* * *

They made nine miles before dark, and camped in the lee of a cut bank with a fire the size of a cupped hand, because Sera insisted and because she was right — smoke travels far in cold valleys, and they did not yet know who else was on this line. They ate hard bread and tinned fish and drank tea brewed twice, and neither spoke much, and the silence was not uncomfortable, which surprised August. Silence with most people was a wall. With Sera it was more like a shared instrument, both of them listening to it for different things.

The stars came out brutal and close. Somewhere upslope a ptarmigan muttered in its sleep. August lay with their hands behind their head and watched the pass, a blacker black against the sky, and turned the day over.

Spiked rails. Blown culverts. A survey team gone quiet. A consulting engineer whose name was on the commission and whose handwriting was on the sabotage. And underneath all of it, unspoken, the question that had been growing since the culvert: *what does a man destroy a railroad to protect?*

Lines were closed for dull reasons — freight volumes, ledgers, politics. But nobody sabotaged a dull closure. Sabotage meant something was using the line that shouldn't be. Or something was going to use it. Or —

Or the line led somewhere, and someone wanted to be the only one who knew the way.

August's father's compass lay against their sternum, and without thinking they drew it out and held it up to the starlight. The needle swung, settled, pointed — as it always did, as it had since August was a child — not quite north. A few degrees east of north, faithful as a dog, useless as a clock with a bent hand. His father had died with the thing in his pocket and never explained it, and August had carried the mystery the way you carry a debt: without thinking, until suddenly always thinking.

East of north. Toward the pass.

"Huh," August said aloud.

"What?" Sera, from her bedroll, not asleep either.

"Nothing. An old puzzle." They put the compass away. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

Morning gave them the third sabotage, and it changed the shape of everything.

It was a tunnel — short, bored through a granite spur maybe eighty yards from portal to portal, the kind of work that took a crew a season and a fortune and was therefore the proudest mile of any mountain line. The south portal stood open and innocent. August walked it with a lamp, expecting rubble, expecting another charge.

Instead they found the tunnel intact. Perfectly intact. And lined, on both walls, at waist height, with chalk marks.

Numbers. Elevations, chainages, offsets — a running survey, fresh, the chalk still crisp, marching up the wall in Halvard's unmistakable square hand. And at intervals, painted over older paint, a symbol August knew from a lifetime ago: a circle with a single tick through it, the field mark Halvard used for *verified, safe passage.* Dozens of them. The whole tunnel marked like a man walking his own house in the dark, counting the rooms.

At the far end, on the north portal's inner face, one final notation, larger than the rest:

**T-3 OPEN. T-4 COMPROMISED. USE SOUTH GALLERY ONLY.**

And beneath it, smaller, as if added later and reluctantly:

**W — if you are reading this, stop.**

August stood before it a long time. The lamp hissed. Behind them, water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of geology.

"'W,'" Sera said quietly. She had come up beside them, and she was reading over their shoulder, and her voice had gone careful. "Whitlock."

"It's addressed to me." August's own voice sounded strange in their ears. "He knew I'd come. He knew I'd follow the line." They raised the lamp higher, chasing the shadows off the chalk. "He blew the culverts to stop everyone else, and then he marked the safe road through for one person. Me. He wants the line dead and me alive, and he wants me—" August stopped, because the sentence finished itself in a place they didn't want to look. *He wants me to understand why.*

"Or," Sera said, "he wants you to follow exactly this far, exactly this way, into exactly the trap he's built."

"Yes," August said. "That's the other possibility. That's always the other possibility with a good engineer's work — you can't tell the confession from the lure until you're inside it."

They stood together in the dripping dark, two small flames' worth of light in eighty yards of granite, and August thought about the bridge. About the report they had signed, the inspection they had passed, the span that had come down in a green spring flood with a freight crew aboard. About the inquiry, the lawyers, the quiet way a career dies — not with a verdict but with a phone that stops ringing. About how, in three years, they had never once been able to make the failure make sense. The numbers had been right. August had checked them ten thousand times in the dark. The numbers had been *right*, and the bridge had fallen anyway, and that impossibility had been the splinter under the skin of everything since.

And now here was Halvard's handwriting on a wall, saying *stop* — and beneath the fear, beneath the wariness, something in August rose up and answered it the way it had answered every impossible number for three years.

*No,* it said. *Not until I know.*

"We go on," August said.

Sera studied them. Outside the portal, morning light lay on the rails like a promise being reconsidered. "You understand he may have sealed your survey team in that ice," she said. "You understand the man who taught you may be the reason eleven people stopped answering their telegraph."

"I understand." August picked up the lamp. "And if it's true, I want to hear him say it to my face. That's not forgiveness. That's an audit."

Something moved at the corner of Sera's mouth — not quite a smile, but related to one. "An audit," she repeated. "You and your profession."

They stepped out of the tunnel into the cold bright morning, and August took the handles, and the handcar began to climb.

Two miles on, rounding the shoulder of the spur, the pass opened before them at last — and August set the brake without deciding to, because the sight took the strength out of their arms.

The glacier filled the head of the valley like a sea that had forgotten how to move and then remembered, all at once, forever. Blue-green ice, a thousand feet of it, shouldered down between the peaks, and at its mouth, far below, a thread of meltwater flashed silver where the first of the six tides was beginning to drain. And there — faint, at this distance, but unmistakable to anyone who had spent a life reading iron — a sound riding down the wind that had no business in a closed valley.

Engines. More than one. Down at the port, or coming up the coast road.

Sera heard it too. She had gone very still, her head cocked, her face doing the arithmetic again.

"That's not the fleet," she said at last. "The fleet's still provisioning. That's smaller. Faster." She turned to August. "That's scouts. Somebody sent scouts up the valley ahead of the ships."

August looked at the glacier, at the draining silver thread, at the chalk-marked tunnel behind them and the dying message of a mentor strung along the rails like breadcrumbs — and understood, with the calm that arrives at the center of bad weather, that the race had already started, and they were not winning it.

"Pump," August said.
