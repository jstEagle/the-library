## 6. Halvard's Ledger

The markers began at the third culvert past the valley, and they were wrong.

That was the first thing August understood, standing in the thin morning light with the wind combing down from the icefields and tugging at their collar. A man who wanted to be followed left cairns — neat, deliberate, stacked like arguments. A man who wanted to hide left nothing at all. But Halvard had done neither. He had left marks that looked accidental: a rail spike driven half into a tie at an angle no workman would choose, a strip of signal-cloth knotted to a birch limb where no signal had ever hung, three stones set in the mud of a stream crossing in a pattern that meant nothing unless you had spent eleven years learning to read track geometry from the man who placed them.

August crouched at the stream and touched the stones. Cold water ran over their knuckles. The pattern was a cant — a slight lateral offset of the middle stone relative to the outer two — and any engineer who had ever ridden a curve too fast knew what it said. *Slow down here. The ground is lying to you.*

Halvard had taught them that on the Kestrel Grade, twenty years ago, when August was nineteen and hungry for the work and Halvard had been the finest roadmaster on the mountain division, a big quiet man with frost-scarred hands who could look at a mile of ballast and tell you which tie would fail first and in which season. He had taught them everything. And then, four years ago, he had sat in the inquiry hall with his eyes on his notes while the commissioners took August's license, August's name, and very nearly August's life, and handed them all to a bridge collapse that August still could not explain.

*Slow down here,* the stones said. *The ground is lying to you.*

August stood, wiped their hand dry on their coat, and walked on.

Behind them, Sera Voss kept pace with the particular economy of someone who had grown up walking distances other people rode. She carried the survey team's recovered logbook wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, and she had not spoken in an hour, which from Sera was not silence but attention. Twice she had stopped without being asked and looked back down the line they'd climbed, reading it the way she read telegraph tape — not the words but the intervals between them.

At the second marker — the signal-cloth, faded almost white, knotted with a hitch only a railroad man would use — she finally spoke.

"Whoever left this wanted one person to find it," she said. "Not us specifically. One person. Anyone else would walk past."

"Or they'd follow it straight into a rifle sight." August tested the knot with two fingers. It held, then released cleanly under a specific pull — a release-hitch, the kind used on temporary signals, the kind you tied when you intended to come back and untie it yourself. "He expected to come back."

"You know him."

"I did." The word *did* sat in August's mouth like a coal. "Halvard Reine. He was my mentor. My chief. He signed my journeyman papers." They turned the cloth over. The dye had run in old rain, but the weave was mill-stock from the coast, government pattern. "And then he sat in that inquiry and let them bury me."

Sera considered this with her head tilted slightly, the way she listened to a line go quiet mid-transmission. "People do two things when they've helped ruin someone," she said. "They avoid the person, or they leave a trail of stones in a river and wait four years."

August didn't answer. But they walked faster.

* * *

The camp sat in a fold of the mountain above the old service spur, hidden from the main line by a shoulder of granite and a stand of wind-bent pines, and it was abandoned the way the survey station had been abandoned — which was to say, not abandoned at all, but *interrupted*.

A canvas lean-to still stood, guyed properly against the prevailing wind. A cast-iron stove sat outside it on a flat stone, its firebox swept clean, its flue capped against rain. A washbasin held rainwater and a single tin cup, upright. A pair of felt boots stood side by side beneath the lean-to's edge, toes out, drying. Everything in order. Everything waiting for its owner to come back from somewhere just out of sight.

Except there was nowhere to come back from. August circled the camp twice, wide, reading the ground the way Halvard had taught them to read ground, and found no tracks leaving. Only tracks arriving — boot prints, weeks old by now, weathered soft, coming up from the spur in a steady commuter's rhythm, dozens of trips' worth — and then, near the lean-to, a churned patch where something had happened that August's eye refused at first to parse. Scuffed earth. A dropped lantern, glass cracked, oil long since evaporated. A smear across the flat stone where the stove sat, dark with old rust or old blood, impossible to say which after this much weather.

"He was taken," Sera said quietly. She had gone to the boots first, touched nothing, and was now studying the churned ground with her arms folded against the cold. "Same as the survey team. Mid-task. No struggle worth the name, or he'd have broken more than a lantern."

"Or he went with them." August heard their own voice come out flat and hard. "He'd have gone quietly if he thought it would buy something. He was always good at spending himself."

They ducked into the lean-to.

Inside, the space was narrow and immaculate in the way of men who had lived alone a long time: bedroll rolled tight, tools hung on pegs in silhouette-outline so a missing piece announced itself, a shelf of tinned food arranged by size. A folding camp desk stood at the back, and on it, weighted under a smooth river stone, lay a ledger bound in gray board, its corners worn round, its spine cracked white from use.

August stood over it for a long moment before touching it.

There is a strange arithmetic to dread. Four years ago, August had sat through nine days of inquiry listening to men read aloud from documents, and every document had been a knife, and every knife had been sharpened by someone who knew exactly where to cut, and August had spent those nine days watching Halvard's face for one flicker — one twitch of the jaw, one drop of the gaze — that would mean *I know this is wrong*. And Halvard had given them nothing. Not a flicker. Not a glance. He had testified precisely, moderately, with the authority of thirty years on the mountain division, and his testimony had been the keystone of the verdict, and August had walked out of that hall with a revoked license and a debt and a father's compass in their pocket pointing at nothing, and had hated him with a hatred so clean and total it had felt like the last honest thing they owned.

Now August lifted the stone and opened the ledger, and their hands were shaking.

* * *

It was a roadmaster's ledger, the ordinary working kind — columns for ties replaced, ballast depth, drainage grades, culvert inspections — except that the entries stopped being ordinary a long way back. The early pages were routine, dated years ago, in Halvard's square engineer's hand. Then the dates climbed toward the year of the Verrin Bridge, and the handwriting changed. It got smaller. It got faster. Marginal notes appeared, cramped into the column gutters, written as if the pen were trying to outrun the man holding it.

August turned pages by lamplight — Sera had found the lamp oil and lit the camp's own lamp without asking, and the small yellow flame made the lean-to feel briefly like a room where people lived — and the marginalia assembled itself into a confession written in installments, a man arguing with himself across months.

*Verrin steel shipment inspected 14th. Flange thickness under spec on 40% of lot. Rejected lot per division standard.* That entry was clean, official, dated fourteen months before the collapse.

Then, three pages later, in the gutter beside a routine entry about culvert gravel: *Reversed. Crane's office countersigned acceptance. Told me spec variance "within tolerance recalculated." Recalculated by whom? Steel went up anyway.*

Then: *Told Whitlock the span loads were nominal. I told them that. I had the recalculation sheets in my hand and I told them nominal.* Here the pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. *I told myself the variance was paper. I told myself Crane's engineers knew something. I told myself a great many things.*

August read that line four times. The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a train passing on far rails, and was not a train.

Sera had moved close enough to read over their shoulder. She said nothing. She was very good, August was beginning to understand, at letting silence do the heavy lifting.

The ledger's final dated entries were from the week after the collapse. August knew the date before reading it; they knew it the way one knows the date of a wound. *Inquiry convened. I gave the division position. I gave the position because Crane holds the contracts that keep this whole line fed, and because if the variance came out, it comes out on my signature — I accepted the steel, whatever I was told to accept. My name or theirs. I chose mine.* A long gap. Then, lower on the page, the letters cramped nearly illegible: *Whitlock took the verdict like an engineer takes a report. Read it all the way through. Didn't argue. God forgive me, that made it easier.*

The last entry was undated, written in a different ink, in a hand shakier than the rest:

*I am going up the closed line to find the wreck. Not the gold. The manifest. The original shipping manifest for the bullion consignment is in the strongroom records — I helped draft the sealing protocol after the war, I know what was locked in that car. If the wreck is found intact, the manifest proves what was loaded, and what was loaded proves what was stolen, and what was stolen leads back through Crane's ledgers to the day he bought forty percent substandard steel and called it tolerance. The bridge was never an accident. It was cost recovery. He shorted the valleys on their gold and he shorted my bridge on its steel and both times the difference went into the same pocket.*

*I owe Whitlock a signature. I intend to give them a better one.*

August closed the ledger.

For a moment they could not move. The feeling that rose in them was not the vindication they might have imagined — not the clean bright relief of a debt cancelled. It was fury, first, vast and impersonal, fury at the machinery of it: a financier in a coastal office recalculating tolerances on paper, a mentor choosing his own name over his apprentice's, an inquiry hall full of honorable men reading honorable summaries of dishonorable arithmetic, and at the bottom of all of it, August's life tipped over like a handcar off the rails, four years of hunger and blacklisting and shame, and all of it *purchasable*, all of it a line item, cost recovery, tolerance recalculated. They had spent four years believing they had failed. That was the theft underneath the theft. Crane hadn't just taken their license. He had taken their certainty that they were any good, and sold it back to them as doubt, compounded daily.

And underneath the fury, worse and warmer, was grief. Because Halvard had known. Had known for four years, had carried it up this mountain in a gray ledger, had written *God forgive me, that made it easier* — and had chosen, month after month, the trail of stones instead of a letter, the knotted cloth instead of a testimony. He had been willing to spend himself on a glacier hunting a manifest rather than walk into a magistrate's office and say five sentences aloud.

"August." Sera's voice, low. "Your hands."

August looked down. Their fists were white around the ledger's cover, and they made themselves open them, finger by finger.

"He framed me," they said. "No. He *let* me be framed. There's a difference and I'll want to remember it later, when I'm deciding what he owes." They heard their own voice shake and hated it and let it shake. "Crane engineered the failure. Halvard falsified the inspection record — that's what the inquiry read, his certification of that steel, his seal. He built the frame and then he stood in the hall and watched it close around me, because the alternative was his own neck."

"And now?"

"Now he went up the closed line alone, after a wreck everyone says doesn't exist, to steal back the proof." August looked at the camp again — the swept stove, the paired boots, the outline-pegs with their tools all present. "And somebody took him. Same as the survey team. Same method, same silence."

Sera was standing at the mouth of the lean-to, looking north along the shoulder of granite toward where the line climbed toward the icefields, and the low sun caught her face in profile. "Then we're following the same trail twice," she said. "His, and theirs. And theirs is fresher."

August wrapped the ledger in the oilcloth from their pack, snug against the wet, and put it inside their coat, over the breast, where the compass rode. For a moment the two objects lay together — the needle that pointed at the pass, and the book that pointed at the truth — and August felt the weight of both like a yoke fitted to shoulders that had finally been given something worth carrying.

Outside, the light was going long and coppery. Two tides remained before the second drain of the glacier mouth, and the salvage fleet was somewhere behind them, and Halvard was somewhere ahead — alive or dead, prisoner or collaborator, and August could no longer pretend those were simple questions.

They struck nothing in the camp. Left the stove swept, the boots in place, the lamp burning low in its glass. If Halvard came back, let him find a light on.

At the edge of the trees, Sera stopped and pointed. On the granite shoulder above the spur, fresh against the weathered rock, was a new mark — chalk, days old at most, a single vertical stroke crossed by two diagonals. A surveyor's datum symbol. The same mark August had seen burned into the badge found at the tunnel mouth.

Okafor's team had passed here. After Halvard. Heading up.

"They found him," August said. "Or he found them."

The wind came down off the icefield, cold and clean and smelling of deep time, and above the black shoulder of the mountain the glacier's edge glowed faintly blue, like a door left ajar in the world.

August buttoned the coat over ledger and compass and started climbing.
