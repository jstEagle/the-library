## 7. First Tide

The tide began to leave at dawn, and the glacier let it go grudgingly.

August stood on the shingle at the mouth of the pass with the wind pulling at their coat and watched the water fall away from the ice like a curtain being drawn. It did not drain evenly. It went in pulses — a hand's depth, then a pause, then another hand's depth — as though the sea were reconsidering each inch it surrendered. And where the water withdrew, the ice was revealed: not the grey, dirty rampart August had imagined from Ilsa Brandt's stories, but something that caught the early light and held it, blue as deep water, blue as the underside of the world.

Sera Voss stood beside them, arms crossed against the cold, saying nothing. She had been saying nothing for an hour, which from Sera was its own kind of speech. She had woken before August, had already checked the tide tables twice by lamplight, and had said only, "It's going out," in a voice carefully stripped of everything she felt about it. Now she watched the same revelation August watched, and her silence had a weight to it, like ballast.

"It's a tunnel," August said. Their voice came out rougher than they intended, scraped raw by three days of cold camp and shorter rations than pride had admitted to.

"It's a throat," Sera said. "That's what my grandmother called it. The mountain swallows you six times a year and spits you out if it feels like it."

August could see what she meant. The tunnel mouth was not an arch cut clean through the ice but a hollowing, a place where the glacier's foot met the tidewater and the two had argued for a century, the sea carving and the ice pressing back, until between them they had made a passage tall enough to drive a locomotive through — because, once, men had driven one through. The old rail bed ran straight toward it across the shingle, ties rotted to black lace, and vanished into the blue.

The compass hung at August's neck under their shirt, and August did not need to take it out to know what it was doing. It had been doing it since the valley: needle fixed not north but up-slope, into the pass, faithful as a dog at a graveside. Their father's compass. Their father's map. The closed lines drawn in his hand with a note in the margin August had read so many times the ink might as well be printed inside their own skull: *They never should have shut this road. There's more through there than anyone admits.*

Well. Someone had gone in to find what more there was. Six of them, with instruments laid out mid-meal and a signal lamp ticking down its last hours, and no footprints beyond the door.

"Water'll be off the floor of it within the hour," Sera said. "Ilsa says low tide gives us four, maybe five hours of dry passage before it comes back. After that—" She made a small gesture, closing her fist. "After that the throat closes."

"And we have five more tides after this one."

"Five more *opens*." Sera turned to look at them, and in the flat grey light her face was all sharp planes and stubbornness. "Calving season doesn't wait on our arithmetic. When the ice starts shedding, this whole route goes under a hundred feet of new glacier, and whatever's inside stays inside until God decides otherwise."

August nodded. They had done the arithmetic too, lying awake in Ilsa's loft listening to the settlement dogs argue with the dark. Five opens. Two already spoken for — one to go in, one to come back, if the coming back happened on schedule, which nothing in August's life ever had. That left three tides of margin, and somewhere behind them, eating those margins alive, were engines.

They both heard it again then, faint over the wind: the far-off percussion of machinery, iron on iron, carried down the valley like a pulse. The salvage fleet. Crane's fleet. It had reached the lower valley sometime in the night — they'd seen the smoke standing above the tree line at first light, three columns of it, orderly and patient, the smoke of men who had money enough to wait and guns enough not to have to.

"They'll wait for the second tide," August said. "Crane didn't get rich by walking into holes first. He'll let someone else test the floor."

"You're volunteering?"

"I'm already here." August looked at the tunnel again. "And whoever went in before us didn't have the luxury of waiting."

* * *

The floor of the tunnel, when they stepped onto it, rang.

Not literally — though close, close enough that August stopped after ten paces and crouched, gloved palm flat against the ancient ice, and listened with the whole body the way they'd been trained to listen to bridges. Underfoot the ice was groaning, a long subsonic complaint, the sound of a mass the size of a cathedral settling its weight as the sea let go of it. Not dangerous, probably. Not yet. But alive. Everything here was alive in the way that stone and water are alive when they're moving faster than men can see.

"Talk to me, engineer," Sera said behind her. Her voice bounced strange in the blue, arriving twice, once direct and once from some impossible angle overhead.

"It's holding," August said. "For now. The tide takes the pressure off the base and the whole thing relaxes. That's why the passage exists at all — the water keeps the foot of the glacier buoyed, and at low tide it sags just enough to open. Come calving season the balance changes and it won't sag anymore. It'll bear down." They stood. "We're walking through a held breath."

"A held breath full of what?"

"That," August said, "is the question."

Because the light — God, the light. August had spent a life among structures, had crawled through culverts and stood inside bridge trusses and walked the black throats of tunnels bored through living rock, and none of it, none of it, had prepared them for this. The ice overhead was forty feet thick, sixty, and the daylight came down through it transformed, sifted, made liquid. The walls glowed. Every surface gave off light instead of merely receiving it, so that the tunnel seemed lit from within, from everywhere, a nave built by something that understood reverence better than any architect who had ever lived. Streaks of older ice ran through the walls like strata in marble — bands of white, bands of green-black, threads of frozen air in silver chains. Where meltwater had refrozen in long curtains, the light broke apart into colors August had no names for.

Sera had stopped walking. She stood with her head tipped back and her mouth slightly open, and for once in their acquaintance she looked entirely unguarded, a woman seeing something she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe.

"All right," she said finally, quietly. "All right. I take back the throat."

"Don't," August said. "Respect the throat. Pretty things kill people out here."

But August's own voice had gone soft, and they both heard it, and neither remarked on it.

They followed the rail bed. It was easier going than the shingle had been — the old ties, though rotten, still gave purchase where the bare ice would have been a skating rink — and it led them true, deeper into the blue, the light changing by degrees as the entrance fell away behind them. August counted paces without meaning to; it was an old habit, the engineer's rosary. At four hundred paces the daylight thinned. At six hundred they lit the lanterns, and the blue around them deepened by a shade, and the cold came down out of the walls like a presence.

At eight hundred paces, August found the boot prints.

They stopped so abruptly that Sera walked into their shoulder. "What—"

"Look."

The prints were pressed into a film of refrozen melt along the edge of the rail bed, crisp-edged, unweathered. Fresh. Days old at most — the ice here reformed nightly, Ilsa had told them, so anything older than a week would have been healed over like a scar. These prints were healed at the edges but shallow, recent. A man's boots, hobnailed, government issue by the pattern. Several sets, overlapping, headed inward.

And beside them, half-melted into the surface, a spent flare stick, its casing split, its chemical guts long cold. Then another. Then a third, fifty yards on, and August crouched over it and felt their stomach tighten, because flares were burned in sequence, at intervals, by teams that wanted to leave a trail home. Somebody had been afraid of losing their way in a tunnel that was, at low tide, a straight line.

People burned flares like that when they were afraid of the dark. Or afraid of what else might be marking the trail.

"Sera."

"I see them." Her voice had changed — flattened, professional, the telegraph operator's voice, the voice that sent bad news in clean code. "Three, four sets of prints. Heading in. None heading out."

"None that I can see."

They walked on. The prints multiplied, converged, scattered, converged again — the signature of a party moving with purpose and then, further in, moving less purposefully, the spacing shortening, the overlaps growing confused. August read the ice the way they read track diagrams, and what it told them made the skin crawl along their spine: these were men who had started confident and ended hurried. Something ahead had changed their minds about the pace.

At eleven hundred paces, wedged in a crease of the wall where someone had jammed it deliberately, deliberately, so it wouldn't blow away or wash out — they found the badge.

Brass, oval, worn smooth at the edges. A surveyor's insignia, the crown-and-line mark of the Government Boundary Office, and stamped beneath it a number August knew without knowing how they knew it, because Halvard had recited it often enough in the old days, proudly, the way a man recites the number of a regiment he helped raise: Station Seven. The team from the station with the cold fire and the ticking lamp and the meal abandoned halfway to mouths.

August turned it over in their glove. On the reverse, scratched hastily with a knife point, in a hand that dug hard enough to tear brass:

*WE FOUND IT. IT'S NOT SUPPOSED TO BE HERE.*

Below that, smaller, almost illegible:

*THEY SEALED THE BACK WAY. COUNT YOUR TIDES.*

Sera read it over August's shoulder, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the tunnel breathed its slow groan around them, and the blue light lay on the scratched brass like a benediction or a warning, and August could not tell which.

"The back way," Sera said at last. "Sealed. By whom? Sealed how?"

"By whoever didn't want them leaving with what they found." August's mind was running the numbers and hating every result. A team of six goes in. No prints coming out. A message about sealed exits, written in haste, left where only a careful follower would find it. Either the team was dead — and the ice here showed no sign of violence, no blood frozen black, no dropped gear beyond the badge itself — or they had gone deeper, past the point where the tide reached, into the part of the glacier that never opened, and were now—

Or they were trapped. Behind a collapse. Rationing whatever they carried, burning their lamps down to fumes, listening to the ice breathe and counting tides they couldn't see.

"We keep going," August said.

"August." Sera put a hand on their arm. In the lantern light her eyes were steady and very serious. "If Crane's people sealed a tunnel behind six government men, they'll do worse to two. We should go back, mark the location, bring numbers. Ilsa has cousins with rifles. The magistrate at Thornden owes me favors—"

"And the tide comes back in four hours, and the next open is tomorrow, and the fleet is in the valley *now*, and every hour we spend raising a posse is an hour Crane's divers or his dynamite men or whatever he's brought are working the same problem." August heard their own voice rise and forced it level. "Six people are in this ice, Sera. Maybe dead. Maybe not. I've spent four years being the engineer whose bridge fell down. I know exactly what it costs when nobody goes back for the people everyone else has written off."

Sera held their gaze. Then, slowly, she took her hand off their arm and unslung her pack and checked her lamp oil, which was, August was learning, how she said yes.

"Deeper, then," she said. "But we mark every hundred paces. Chalk and flare stubs. If the tide turns while we're in here, I want a trail home even if the lights fail."

"Agreed."

They went on. The tunnel narrowed by degrees, the ceiling descending, the walls drawing in, and the quality of the light changed with it — less cathedral now, more crypt, the blue gone darker, denser, as though they were descending into the ice rather than passing through it. August marked the pace count aloud every hundred, and Sera chalked the number on the wall in her quick operator's hand, and the numbers climbed: twelve hundred. Thirteen. Fourteen.

At fifteen hundred paces, the rail bed ended.

Not faded, not buried — *ended*, sheared off clean where the ice had pinched the passage down to a crawl, and beyond the pinch the tunnel continued, lower and darker, and the air coming out of it was different. Warmer by a degree. Carrying a smell.

August knew the smell. Every railroad person knew it. Coal smoke, old and cold and soaked into wood and iron for years, the smell of a locomotive's ghost.

Sera's lantern shook very slightly. "August," she whispered. "Is that—"

"Steam coal," August said. "Years old. Decades." Their heart was slamming against their ribs, and beneath the fear was something else, something vast and rising, the feeling they'd chased their whole life across every closed line on every map: the feeling of standing at the edge of the thing that wasn't supposed to exist anymore. "There's a train in there, Sera. There's a train in this ice."

And that was when they heard the engines.

Faint, muffled by a thousand feet of stone and glacier, but unmistakable — the heavy idle of marine diesels, many of them, carrying up the valley on the morning air. Not at the mouth. Not yet. Down in the lower valley, where the smoke columns stood, where the fleet was provisioning, where Aldous Crane's money was turning into men and equipment and intent.

Sera looked at August. August looked at the dark pinch in the ice, and the coal-smell breathing out of it, and the scratched words on a dead man's badge: *count your tides.*

Four hours of tide left. Six people missing somewhere ahead. A fortune in gold, maybe, and a graveyard, maybe, and somewhere behind them both, a rich man's machines beginning to move.

"Second tide's theirs," August said quietly. "This one's ours. Move."

They ducked into the dark together, and behind them, far down the blue throat of the glacier, the ice groaned like something waking.
