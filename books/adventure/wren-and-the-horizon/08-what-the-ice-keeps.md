## 8. What the Ice Keeps

The tunnel changed the deeper they went. What had been a corridor of blue became a throat of it — walls pressing close enough that August's lantern threw their shadows huge and trembling against ice so old it had turned the color of deep water, of a sea at night seen from very far above. Sera walked with one hand on the wall because the floor was a river of ridged meltwater frozen mid-flow, and her boots kept wanting to slide sideways into history.

She counted her breaths. It was something she'd learned as a girl on the coast, when the fog came in off the sound so thick you couldn't see your own feet and the only honest thing left was the rhythm of your own body. In for four. Out for six. The tunnel did not care about her fear, but the counting gave the fear somewhere to sit besides her chest.

They had been walking — climbing, really, the passage rising in slow switchbacks where the glacier poured over some buried shoulder of rock — for nearly two hours since the first boot print. The prints were everywhere now, pressed into a skin of refrozen melt like fossils: dozens of them, overlapping, going in. None coming out. Beside several of them lay spent flare casings, their cardboard tubes split and frost-furred, and once, caught in a crease of ice like a leaf in amber, a brass surveyor's badge stamped with the crown and the words *Dominion Boundary Commission*.

August had picked it up without a word, turned it over twice, and put it in their coat pocket. That silence had frightened Sera more than anything else in the ice. August Whitlock was not a silent person when there was work to be done; they muttered over gauges, argued with broken machinery, narrated their own calculations under their breath like a man praying in arithmetic. But for two hours they had said almost nothing, and their jaw had set into a line Sera recognized from the night she'd found them standing over the abandoned station's cold stove: the look of someone adding up a sum they already dreaded finishing.

"Light," August said suddenly, and stopped.

Sera saw it a heartbeat later. Far up the passage, where the blue thickened into darkness, there was a smudge of yellow — faint, unsteady, more suggestion than light. A lamp behind too many veils of ice, or a lamp nearly dead.

"That's not daylight," she said. "We've been climbing away from the mouth."

"No." August's voice came out rough. "No, it isn't."

They moved faster then, caution fraying with every step, boots scraping on the frozen flow. The light grew — a dirty, oil-colored glow leaking around a bend — and with it came sounds that made Sera's scalp tighten: a low rhythmic scrape, metal on stone, patient as a clock. And beneath it, thinner, a human sound. Not a cry. Something worse than a cry. A voice being used carefully, the way you ration bread.

August rounded the bend first and stopped so abruptly Sera walked into their back.

The passage opened into a chamber where the glacier had hollowed itself a room — a vault of ice forty feet across, its ceiling ribbed like the inside of a shell. Against the far wall, behind a rampart of collapsed ice blocks taller than a freight car, the yellow light burned: a single storm lantern, its glass blackened with soot, sitting on an improvised shelf of survey cases. And below the light, moving with the slow economy of people who had learned to spend nothing, were figures.

Sera counted five before her eyes finished adjusting. Five people in government wool gone gray with glacial flour, bearded, hollowed, moving among the rubble with hand picks and a length of pipe, chipping at the base of the collapse with the terrible patience of prisoners digging a tunnel with a spoon. One of them was a woman with a bandaged arm who worked one-handed. Another sat apart, wrapped in a tarp, shivering in the way that meant fever rather than cold.

The nearest figure heard them first. He turned, and the pick dropped from his hand, and he made a sound that started as a word and ended as something older than language.

"Dear God," August breathed beside her. "It's Okafor."

The man came forward three steps and stopped, as if his legs had forgotten how to do more. Up close Sera could see what the ice had done to him: cheeks fallen in, lips cracked and bleeding at the corners, eyes sunk back into a face that had been strong once and was now mostly architecture. But the eyes themselves were alive — burning, in fact, with a ferocity that made her take half a step back.

"You're real," Okafor said. His voice was a ruin. "Say something. Say something real. Tell me something I couldn't make up."

"The tide went out this morning," Sera said, because it was hers to say, the thing no one in this room could know. "First tide of six. The mouth is open. We came up from Brandt's valley."

Okafor made a noise that might have been laughter if laughter hadn't forgotten him. "Brandt's valley. Ilsa's people." He looked at August, and something in his face shifted — recognition arriving slowly, through exhaustion, like a train through fog. "Whitlock. They sent *you*."

"They sent sealed orders," August said. "To a station with nobody in it and a fire three days cold. So I followed the map instead." They gestured at the collapse wall, the picks, the impossible patience of it all. "How long?"

"Nine days," Okafor said. "Ten, maybe. We stopped counting by lamps. We counted by oil instead." He nodded at the storm lantern, and Sera understood, with a lurch, exactly what she was looking at: the light was the last of it. When that glass went dark, they would have been sitting in absolute blackness behind a thousand tons of ice, waiting for a rescue that the world had every reason to believe unnecessary, because officially no team had ever arrived.

"We thought—" One of the others, a young man with frostbitten ears, had come forward and was staring at Sera and August as though they might evaporate. "We heard engines yesterday. Through the ice. We thought it was Crane coming back to finish it."

The name landed in the chamber like a dropped tool. Sera felt August go still beside her.

"Crane," August repeated. "Aldous Crane."

Okafor closed his eyes. For a moment he looked ten years older than he had a moment before, and Sera realized that whatever lived behind that name had been living down here with them, rationed like the oil, all this time.

* * *

They got the trapped ones talking in pieces, the way you get any story out of people who have been saving it too long. The woman with the bandaged arm was called Reyes; she'd broken it in the original collapse and set it herself with a survey stake and strips of shirt. The feverish one was Mbeki, and he was past talking, though he opened his eyes when Sera knelt and pressed her flask to his lips, and watched her with an attention that hurt to receive. There was a fourth, Okonjo, who had given up speaking entirely and communicated by pointing, and a fifth, Lindqvist, who wept steadily and silently while he worked, tears freezing in the stubble of his cheeks, and never once stopped working.

The story, assembled, was simple the way avalanches are simple.

They had found the wreck on the third day in. A supply train from the war years, sealed in a lateral pocket of the glacier where the ice had flowed over and around it without crushing it — a locomotive and six cars lying in their own private museum, paint still readable, couplings still bright. And in the strongroom car, behind a door the survey team had spent four days opening with tools never meant for the job: bullion. Sealed crates, stenciled markings, gold enough to buy the whole valley range twice over.

"And the manifest," Okafor said. He said it flatly, but his hands had closed into fists at his sides. "In the cab. The engineer's copy. It wasn't supplies they were carrying south when the line failed. It was the region's gold reserves — seized 'for safekeeping' during the war, hauled out over these mountains, and scuttled. The pass didn't fail. The brakes were cut. Somebody wanted that train lost in a place where lost meant forever."

"You have proof of that?" August asked quietly. "Not suspicion. Proof."

"I have the manifest in my breast pocket, Mr. Whitlock, and I have slept on top of it for nine nights, and I will show it to you the moment—" Okafor stopped himself. Drew a breath that shook. Started again, quieter. "Yes. I have proof."

"Then let's get you out," Sera said. "That's the order of things. People first. Paper after. The tide gives us—"

"No."

The word came out level and final, and it stopped the whole chamber. Even Lindqvist's weeping seemed to pause around it.

Okafor straightened against the rubble wall, and Sera watched him gather himself with visible cost, spending strength he did not have, because what he was about to say mattered more to him than his own legs.

"You don't understand what happened here," he said. "Three days after we opened the strongroom, men came up the tunnel. Crane's men — salvage scouts flying no colors, carrying papers offering us a contract. Certify the wreck as derelict and unclaimed. Sign our findings as the Boundary Commission's official record. In exchange, a fortune each, and quiet postings for life." His mouth twisted. "He knew our names. He knew our debts. He'd done his reading. Two of my people were ready to sign, and I don't blame them — you sign away your signature, not your soul, is what they told us. But I read the certification language. Derelict and unclaimed means salvage rights transfer. To Crane. The gold that was stolen from these valleys becomes his property, legally, cleanly, forever. And the manifest — the proof of who cut those brakes — gets filed as an appendix and buried in a records office until the paper rots."

"So you refused," August said.

"So I refused. And that night they brought charges down and fired them at the roof of the passage behind us." Okafor lifted his chin toward the collapse wall, and Sera followed his gaze and saw it now — saw the scorch shadows on the ice, the shattered blocks, the violence written in a language she'd spent her life learning to read from telegraph reports of mine disasters. "They didn't try to kill us. Killing a survey team brings investigators. They *sealed* us. Buried the entrance and let the glacier do the paperwork. As far as the world knows, we never arrived. As far as the world knows, in a year the calving takes the whole pocket and there's nothing left to find and no one left to find it."

The silence after that was the kind Sera had only heard in the hour before a storm makes landfall.

"Then we dig," August said. "All of us. From both sides if we have to. We have blasting powder in the valley—"

"With respect, Mr. Whitlock — no." Okafor's voice cracked on it, and Sera saw the cost of the refusal written across his ruined face: this was not stubbornness. This was a man holding the only door shut against a flood. "You bring powder near this chamber and you'll bring the ceiling down on the wreck, the manifest, and everyone in it. And even if you didn't — even if you dug us out clean tonight — think about what walks out behind us. Crane's fleet is in the valley. You said it yourself at the mouth — you heard the engines. The moment we surface, we're witnesses. Witnesses can be discredited, or worse. My team signs nothing, testifies to nothing, until the manifest is logged — copied, witnessed, entered into the record in a form Crane cannot burn. Then, and only then, we walk out."

"Mbeki has a fever," Sera said, and her voice surprised her with its steadiness. "Lindqvist hasn't stopped shaking since we got here. Your lamp oil is down to fumes. You're telling me you'll spend these people to protect a piece of paper."

"I'm telling you," Okafor said, and now his voice did break, fully, and he let it, "that I have spent nine days deciding whether I got my people entombed for principle or for pride, and I have concluded that it doesn't matter, because the answer is the same either way. If we come out of this ice and say nothing, the valleys lose everything twice — once to whoever cut those brakes, and once to me, for flinching. I will not flinch. I will log the finding. I will need perhaps six hours with my instruments and my notes, and then, Mrs.—"

"Voss," Sera said. "Sera Voss. Telegraph operator."

"—then, Miss Voss, I will walk out of this glacier at whatever pace my people can manage, and I will put that manifest in front of a magistrate if I have to crawl to the courthouse." He looked around at his team — at Reyes working her one good hand, at Mbeki's fever-bright eyes, at gentle weeping Lindqvist — and his face did something complicated and terrible. "If any of you want to go now, go. I release you. I mean it. Nobody stays for my conscience."

Nobody moved. Reyes snorted — a dry, wrecked sound that was unmistakably contempt — and went back to chipping ice.

Sera looked at August. August was staring at the collapse wall with an expression she couldn't parse — fury and something else underneath it, something that looked almost like recognition, like a person seeing their own reflection in a dark window and not liking the fit.

"The tide turns in four hours," August said finally. "Second one tomorrow morning. If we're hauling five weakened people up that passage, we want to start with the water low and the ice stable." They pulled off a glove and pressed their bare palm flat against the collapse wall, the way an engineer touches anything they intend to argue with. "Six hours of logging. Fine. But we dig from this side while you write — slowly, by hand, no powder — and widen that gap enough that if anything goes wrong up top, you have a hole to breathe through. Those are my terms. I don't seal anyone in a second time."

Okafor studied them for a long moment. Whatever he saw — and Sera suspected he saw a great deal, because ruined men learn to read faces the way she read wire — he nodded once.

"Agreed."

* * *

They worked. It was strange work, intimate work — passing tools through a gap barely wide enough for a bread loaf, hearing the survey team's voices come through the ice thin and doubled, learning people by their hands before their faces. Sera took the watch rotation without being asked; someone had to count the hours, and counting was her trade. She sat with Mbeki and fed him melted snow off the flat of a survey case lid, drop by drop, and told him about the valley — about Ilsa Brandt's kitchen and the grandfather who'd driven trains through this very pass, about the wren that nested in the station eaves every spring and refused every other eave offered to it. Mbeki listened with his eyes closed, and once, faintly, he smiled.

Through the gap, she could hear Okafor dictating in a low, relentless voice, and the scratch of a pencil, and August's occasional questions — precise, professional, the questions of someone who understood exactly what a document needed to survive a courtroom. Somewhere in the second hour the dictation paused, and she heard Okafor say, quietly:

"You were on the Meridian Bridge inquiry. You wrote the report that cleared the design."

There was a long silence.

"I wrote the report they used to end my career," August said.

"Then you know," Okafor said, "what it costs to tell the truth into a room that's already been paid for."

Another silence, longer. When August answered, Sera had to lean toward the gap to hear it.

"That's why I'm here," August said. "Digging."

Sera sat back against the ice and looked up at the vaulted ceiling, where the lantern light turned the ancient blue the color of a bruise healing, and she thought about the engines they'd heard at the mouth that morning — distant, many, unhurried. Crane's fleet, unloading in the valley while they rationed lamplight in the belly of a glacier. Four hours until the tide turned. Six hours of logging, maybe seven, and then a slow climb out with people who could barely stand.

And somewhere above them, on the other side of a thousand tons of ice, men with charges and orders were already asking the mountain where the survey team had gone.

The lantern flickered. Just once — a stutter in the flame, the oil running low — and in that instant of dimming Sera saw every face in the chamber turn toward it, five survivors and two strangers, all of them thinking the same thought at the same moment.

Then the flame steadied, and Okafor's pencil began scratching again, and outside, far off through the ice, the tide began to turn.
