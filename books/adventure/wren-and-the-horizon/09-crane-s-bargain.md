## 9. Crane's Bargain

The lamp oil was down to a finger's width in the second canister, and Okafor measured the dark the way he had once measured rail gauge — by eye, by habit, by the stubborn arithmetic of a man who refused to round up in his own favor.

They had been behind the collapse for eleven days.

He knew this because he kept the log, because keeping the log was the last piece of the work that was still his to do. Eleven days of rationed biscuit and melted ice, of six people breathing in a pocket of stone and blue glass no wider than a chapel, of listening to the glacier groan overhead like a ship working loose from its moorings. Eleven days since the world had come down across the tunnel mouth with a sound like God slamming a ledger shut.

Now they sat in a half-circle around the lamp, and they waited for him to speak, because he was the one who had brought them here, and because Adaeze — his sister, his second, the only person on the survey who outranked him in stubbornness if not in title — had said, an hour ago, with the flat patience of someone who has decided not to die angry:

"Tell them. All of it. From the beginning."

Okafor turned the lamp key down until the flame was a blue seed. He wanted their eyes on him, but he also wanted the shadows back. Some stories are easier told in shadow.

"The beginning," he said, "is a letter."

* * *

It had arrived in June, when the survey team was still four months from the mountains, still camped in a government depot outside Meridian Falls where the heat came off the rails in visible waves and the mosquitoes rose off the sloughs like smoke given appetite.

Okafor remembered the letter precisely because it was beautiful. Heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with wax instead of gummed paper — nobody used wax anymore except men who wanted you to feel the weight of their correspondence before you read a word of it. The crest embossed at the top was a crane, wings folded, standing on one leg above a horizon line. Beneath it, in copperplate:

*Mr. T. Okafor, Chief Surveyor, Northern Boundary Commission.*

*Aldous Crane requests the pleasure of your professional judgment.*

The meeting was held aboard Crane's private car, which was moored — *moored*, Okafor noted even then, the way you'd moor a ship — on a siding outside town, behind a screen of poplars. The car was longer than any passenger coach Okafor had ever ridden, paneled in walnut gone honey-dark with age, lit by lamps with real silk shades. There was a globe on a stand near the window, brass-ribbed, and Crane stood beside it with one hand resting on the top of the world as though steadying it.

He was smaller than his reputation. That was the first thing. Okafor had expected a mountain of a man, some bluff titan of industry out of the illustrated papers, and instead found a neat, dry gentleman of perhaps sixty, silver-haired, dressed in gray so fine it made every other color in the room look like an argument. His hands were small and clean and utterly still. It was the stillness that frightened Okafor later, when he understood what it meant: hands that never fidgeted because they had never once been refused.

"Chief Surveyor." Crane's voice was mild, almost apologetic. "Thank you for coming. I'll be brief, because I know your time is billed by the mile."

"Government time," Okafor said. "It isn't billed at all."

Something flickered at the corner of Crane's mouth. Approval, or the memory of approval. "Then let's not waste it."

He did not offer tea. He did not offer a chair, though there were chairs. He talked standing, hand on the globe, and what he offered was this:

Crane Salvage & Recovery held a charter — old, dusty, legally impeccable — granting right of first salvage on any derelict property recovered from the closed mountain lines. The charter predated the war. It predated the Commission itself. And somewhere up in those mountains, sealed inside the Vesper Glacier since the winter of the retreat, lay a supply train that the government had written off as lost with all papers — *derelict, unrecovered, value nil*. Crane had spent three years and a small fortune confirming its location through channels he declined to name.

"All I require," Crane said, "is certification. Your signature, Chief Surveyor, on a standard form declaring the wreck abandoned property under the terms of my charter. A day's work. Perhaps two, once you've seen it."

"And then?"

"Then I recover it. At my expense. At my risk. The government loses nothing it hasn't already lost twice over." Crane smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, the smile of a man who had learned that warmth opened more doors than force. "You'd be paid a consultant's fee, of course. Five thousand. And your team — each member — a thousand apiece, payable on signature."

Okafor did the arithmetic without meaning to. A thousand dollars was four years of a surveyor's wage. Five thousand was a house, was his mother's debts settled, was Adaeze's surgery in the city she'd been putting off for want of funds. He watched the same calculation move through Crane's eyes — watching him do it — and understood that the number had been chosen precisely, tuned to the exact pitch where refusal becomes expensive.

"I'd need to see the manifest," Okafor said. "What's on this train?"

For the first time, Crane's hand lifted from the globe.

"Supplies," he said. "Rail iron, boiler plate, quartermaster stores. Nothing that concerns you. You're certifying a derelict, Mr. Okafor, not appraising cargo. The form asks one question: is the property abandoned? The answer is yes. It has been yes for nine years." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into something almost kind. "I've read your file. I know about the commission you didn't get. The one that went to a man whose uncle chairs the appointments board. I know what this government pays men like you, and I know what it owes you, and I am offering — for the first time in your career, I suspect — to pay you what you're actually worth."

The lamps hummed. Somewhere beyond the poplars a freight train ground past, hauling somebody else's fortune east.

"I'll consider it," Okafor said.

"Of course." Crane returned to the globe, resumed his gentle grip on the Arctic. "Consider it carefully. But consider this too: I have already hired men to find the wreck whether you certify it or not. The only question your signature answers is whether the finding is orderly — or whether it is simply mine by whatever means remain. I would prefer order, Mr. Okafor. Order is cheaper for everyone." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Especially for you."

* * *

"He showed you the manifest?" That was Reyes, the youngest of them, a chainman with a broken wrist splinted from a tent pole. In the lamplight his face was all questions. "Before you signed anything?"

"He never showed me the manifest." Okafor adjusted the flame. "That should have been my first answer. A legitimate certification doesn't need to hide the cargo. But I was twenty-nine years old and five thousand dollars is five thousand dollars, and I told myself the secrecy was vanity. Rich men are vain. I gave him every excuse I'd have refused anyone else."

Adaeze said nothing. She had been there — not in the walnut car, but afterward, when Okafor had wired Crane's office accepting the commission, and she had looked at him across the depot table and said only, *"Tomás. Why does a man buying your signature hide what he's buying it for?"* He hadn't answered her then. He was answering her now, eleven days late, to an audience of six.

"So we went up," he went on. "August, September, into October. Full Commission kit, government mandate, and Crane's money moving our supplies along the closed lines like oil moving a machine. And I'll say this plainly: I took the money's convenience and told myself it wasn't the same as taking the money. Every step of that road I found a new word for what I was doing. *Consultation. Verification. Due diligence.* Words are wonderful things. They'll carry a man anywhere he's afraid to walk."

* * *

They found the tunnel mouth on the fourth of October, exactly where Crane's unnamed channels said it would be — a seam in the glacier's flank, hidden behind a shoulder of rock, breathing cold air that smelled of iron and creosote and, faintly, impossibly, of coal smoke nine years dead.

The ice had taken the train whole.

Okafor would remember that first sight for the rest of his life, however long the glacier allowed it to be. They went in on the low tide, through a corridor of blue so deep it seemed lit from inside, and forty rods along, the passage opened and there it was: a locomotive the size of a church, frozen mid-stride, drive wheels locked in clear ice like a beast in amber. Behind it, cars. Behind the cars, more, swallowed nose to tail into the white dark. Frost ferned the windows of the cab. An icicle hung from the whistle cord like a tongue.

Reyes swore softly. Nilsen, the instrument man, took photographs with hands that shook. And Okafor walked the length of what he could reach, tapping the ice with his hammer, reading the wreck the way he read any line — and what he read stopped his breath.

Because the cars weren't loaded with rail iron.

The nearest car's door had been sprung by the ice, gapped just wide enough for a lantern. Inside, packed in straw gone brown and brittle, were strongboxes. Dozens of them. Stenciled on each lid, in paint the cold had preserved like a fly in sap: *PROVINCIAL TREASURY — VALLEY DISTRICT REVENUE.*

Gold. Not all gold — but bullion among it, bars wrapped in oiled canvas, and beneath the boxes, crates of banknotes bundled and banded, currency issued against the valley districts' own deposits, the taxes and tolls and savings of every settlement from the pass to the sea. Seized. Loaded in wartime under emergency authority. And then scuttled into a glacier by the men who had loaded it, because the men who had loaded it had already spent most of it on paper, and the train was the proof, and the proof had to vanish.

"It was never lost," Okafor heard himself say. His voice sounded strange in the blue cathedral. "It was *hidden*. By our own side. This is the valleys' money. This is — " He put his hand flat against the ice over the strongboxes, as though taking a pulse. "This is theft. On a scale I don't have words for yet."

Nilsen photographed everything. Okafor logged everything. And that night, camped at the tunnel mouth with the tide rising behind them, he wrote the report that would have ended it — the true manifest, the treasury stencils, the photographs, the names of the officials whose signatures appeared on the seizure orders, including, in the third position, a name Okafor recognized from the newspapers: *A. Crane, Special Commissioner for Wartime Transport.*

Aldous Crane hadn't found the wreck. Aldous Crane had *loaded* it.

* * *

"They were going to certify their own crime," Adaeze said quietly. It wasn't a question. Around the lamp, the others had gone very still. "The charter, the fee, your signature — the whole apparatus existed to launder it. If a government surveyor declared the wreck derelict, the record would say *lost, valueless, lawfully salvaged*, and every bar Crane pulled out of that ice would be clean. Legal. Documented. Yours truly signing the receipt."

"With my name on it," Okafor said. "And yours. And everyone in this circle. That was the design. Not just my signature — complicity, distributed. Six witnesses who could never testify without hanging themselves." He looked at the flame. "It was elegant. I keep coming back to that. It was genuinely elegant, and I hate that I can see it."

"What happened?" Reyes asked. "We know what happened after, but — the message. The one that came up the wire on the eleventh."

Okafor was quiet for a moment. Outside, far off through the ice, the glacier spoke — a long, tectonic sigh, the sound of a thing deciding.

"On the eleventh," he said, "we received new orders. Not from the Commission. From Crane's office, countersigned by someone at the Ministry — a name I've since concluded was forged, or bought, or both. The orders instructed us to proceed with certification immediately, to transmit the declaration by wire, and to surrender all photographic plates to Crane's agents for 'security review.' Security review. Of evidence of embezzlement." He almost laughed; it came out as breath. "And attached was a personal note. One line, in that same copperplate hand:

*'The fee stands. So does the alternative.'*

"No threat named. No threat needed. We had four days' supplies left, sixty miles of sabotaged line behind us, and winter coming down the pass like a closing door. I wired back a refusal. My refusal, my language, my responsibility — I told Adaeze to stay out of it and she told me, in front of God and the telegraph operator, that she'd been in it since the depot table. Then we broke camp to come home the long way, around the glacier's foot, and we'd made maybe two miles when we heard the blast."

Nilsen's splinted wrist shifted in his lap. "The charges at the seam," he murmured. "Behind us."

"Sealed the tunnel mouth and brought down the rock shelf over the low-tide approach in one shot. Professional work. They'd surveyed our route — probably from the moment we arrived — and they'd mined it while we were underground admiring their stolen gold." Okafor's voice stayed level, but his hands, he noticed, had closed into fists in his lap, and he made himself open them. "We ran for the secondary entrance — the fissure on the north flank, the one Ilsa Brandt's grandfather's timetables marked as the winter door. We got inside ahead of the weather. And then the second charge went."

He gestured, without looking, at the wall of the collapse — the tumbled mass of ice and stone that had been their ceiling, their horizon, their sentence, for eleven days.

"That one wasn't precision. That was volume. Enough powder to close a cathedral. They weren't sealing a door anymore. They were building a tomb, and they built it over six government employees, and whoever pressed the plunger either didn't know we were inside or didn't care, and I have spent eleven days unable to decide which of those possibilities is worse." The lamp guttered; he fed it a careful thread of wick. "By morning there was nothing above us but silence and a hundred feet of ice, and the tide schedule, and each other."

* * *

Silence took the little room then, the particular silence of people re-measuring a story they thought they knew. Reyes stared at the floor. Nilsen's jaw worked. Adaeze watched her brother with an expression that had traveled a long way from the depot table and arrived, at last, at something like peace.

"You could have signed," she said finally. "Even after the manifest. Even knowing. Five thousand dollars, Tomás. Nobody would ever have known but us."

"Everyone would have known but us," Okafor corrected gently. "Every valley from here to the coast. Every family that paid revenue into that treasury. That's the scale of it, Ade — it isn't one thief, it's a *system*. Crane, the Ministry, the forged countersignature, the appointment boards, the whole quiet machinery that decides what a man like me is worth and pays him exactly that and no more. Signing wouldn't have made me rich. It would have made me a part." He shook his head slowly. "I spent my whole career believing the lines belonged to the government and the government belonged to the people. Down here, in the dark, I finally understand that the second half is a thing you have to *make true*. Nobody gives it to you. Not for five thousand dollars. Not for fifty."

The lamp burned low. Someone — Adaeze — began, without being asked, to divide the evening biscuit into seven portions, her hands steady, her face calm, and Okafor loved her so fiercely in that moment that he had to look away, back to the log, back to the work, because the work was still his to do.

Then he froze.

The sound came again — faint, rhythmic, arriving through a hundred feet of ice like a heartbeat through a wall. Not the glacier's groaning. Not settling snow.

Knocking. Deliberate. Spaced. Three strikes, pause, three strikes — and then, unmistakably, muffled but certain, a voice calling down through the blue dark, thin as a wire:

*"Hello the ice! Survey party! Hold your lamps — we're coming through!"*

Okafor rose so fast the lamp swung and shadows leapt across the faces of his people — alive, all of them, staring up — and he grabbed the iron bar and began to answer, strike for strike, hammering on the collapse with everything eleven days had left him, while somewhere above, strangers with lanterns started digging toward the sound of his heart.
