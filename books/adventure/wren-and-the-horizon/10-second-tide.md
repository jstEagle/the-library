## 10. Second Tide

The tide gave them four hours and took back two of them in the first hundred yards.

August learned this the hard way, hauling Okafor's second-in-command — a wiry woman named Petrescu whose left leg had been broken by the collapse and badly splinted with survey staves — across a shelf of ice that sloped toward the tunnel mouth at an angle no sane engineer would have certified for anything but water. The rescued team moved like ghosts. Nine days behind a wall of fallen ice on half-rations of lamp oil and biscuit had hollowed them out; their eyes had gone strange in the blue light, and their hands shook so badly that August ended up doing most of the rope work himself, knotting lines around shoulders and waists while Sera anchored the far ends and called out the count.

"Again," she said. "One — two —"

Petrescu came up the slope in a series of small, bitten-off cries she refused to let become screams. August admired her for it and hated the necessity of it, both at once.

Behind them, deeper in the cathedral of blue, the rest of the survey team followed at a shuffle: six figures, seven counting Okafor, who walked last and kept turning to look back down the tunnel as though the wreck he'd refused to leave without logging might still call him home. He carried his notebook strapped against his chest like a breastplate. August had watched him refuse, twice now, to hand it over even to be carried.

"You'll lose it in a crevasse," August told him, not for the first time.

"I'll lose my hands before I lose it," Okafor said, which was not reassuring, because his hands were precisely what worried August — cracked, frost-nipped, barely closing.

The light ahead changed. That was how August knew they were losing the race before anyone said it aloud. The tunnel mouth had been a coin of grey daylight when they'd started hauling; now it was a broad, flat wash, the color of pewter, and the shadows inside the ice had shortened and sharpened. The tide was coming back. Somewhere beyond the glacier's snout, the sea was climbing the shingle again, filling the channel that made the pass passable, and when it reached its height the lower reaches of the tunnel would flood to the ceiling, and anything still inside would be swimming or dead.

"Faster," August said, and heard how thin their own voice sounded, scraped raw by cold and shouting.

They made the mouth with forty minutes to spare. It should have felt like victory. Instead August stood in the widening daylight with the wind off the water hitting them like a slap, and looked down the shingle beach at the boats.

Three of them, drawn up above the tideline. Long, low, engine-driven launches with hulls painted the grey of gun metal, and men beside them — eight, ten, twelve, more coming up from a steam tender anchored out in the channel — unloading crates and coil and, unmistakably, rifles.

Sera stopped at August's shoulder. Her breath went out slow and controlled, the way it did when she was keeping herself level by main force.

"That's Crane's mark on the crates," she said. "The anchor and chain."

"I see it."

"They're between us and the overland trail."

"I see that too."

Okafor came up beside them, squinting against the light he hadn't stood in for nine days. He said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant: "They found the mouth faster than I thought they would."

"They had the same map," August said. "And better boats."

* * *

What happened next happened in the strange, stretched time of exhaustion, where every minute is simultaneously too long and gone too soon. August got the team moving along the base of the ice cliff, away from the launches, toward a fold in the rock where the shingle rose into a defensible shoulder — not safety, only geometry, a place where numbers mattered less. The weakened surveyors could not run. They could barely walk. So they walked, fast, heads down, while behind them voices lifted and a whistle blew three sharp notes, and boots began to crunch up the beach.

The salvage scouts reached them at the fold. Four men in oilskins, rifles slung but hands near them, led by a broad, red-faced man with a foreman's voice and a ledger-keeper's eyes who introduced himself as Dreele and asked, with terrible courtesy, whether they were all alive and accounted for.

"Some of us," August said. "You've been shooting at the mountain, I hope."

"We don't shoot at people we haven't been paid to shoot at." Dreele smiled without warmth. "Mr. Crane's instructions are generous about property and specific about persons. You're the engineer. Whitlock."

Not a question. August's stomach dropped through the shingle. *He knows my name.* Which meant Crane had read the inquiry files, or bought them, or simply remembered — and remembering was worse, because it meant August Whitlock was not an obstacle to be cleared but a variable to be managed.

"You have something Mr. Crane wants," Dreele went on. "Or you will have it. Manifests, logs, whatever the survey team pulled out of that wreck. He's prepared to pay for cooperation. He's prepared to do other things for the alternative, but he'd rather pay. Paperwork gives him hives."

"We have injured people," Sera said, stepping forward, her voice flat as lake ice. "We have nine days of starvation standing behind me, one broken leg, and a tide schedule that doesn't care whose flag you fly. Whatever your employer wants, he can't want it enough to explain to the magistrate why a rescue party froze to death waiting on his negotiation."

Dreele looked at her the way men look at weather they've decided to work around. Then back to August. "Here's the shape of it. Nobody moves on nobody until the next tide drains. We're short-handed for a fight and you're short-handed for everything else, so we all pretend to be civilized for eighteen hours. Your people get our medical kit — the good one, with the morphine — and hot food. In exchange, you tell us what's down the tunnel, and when the fifth tide opens, we go in together, and any paper recovered gets read by everyone at once. In front of witnesses." He spread his hands. "That's the truce. Write it down if you like. I'll sign it."

"And if we say no?"

"Then we wait for the tide after that, and things get less polite, and I'll be honest with you, Engineer — the mountain does half our work for free. People fall on ice. It's a tragedy."

It was a good offer and a bad one, and August could feel both truths at once, the way you can feel a rail humming before the train arrives. Accept, and Crane's men would be woven through their column on the next tide, close enough to take the manifest the moment it surfaced. Refuse, and nine days of survival would end on a shingle beach over a crate of morphine.

Before August could answer, Okafor spoke.

"We accept," he said. "With one amendment. The papers stay on my body. I carry them. Anyone who wants them takes them from me, in front of your witnesses and mine, and explains it to the magistrate afterward." His voice didn't rise at all. "I've already been buried once by Mr. Crane's arrangement. I'm willing to make the paperwork difficult."

Something flickered across Dreele's face — respect, maybe, or the professional annoyance of a man whose script has been improved without permission. "Done," he said. "I'll send up the kit."

* * *

The truce, such as it was, settled over the beach like fog. Crane's camp went up two hundred yards down the shingle: canvas, a stove, crates stacked into a windbreak. August's people kept the fold in the rock, and the space between filled with the awkward traffic of enemies sharing a shoreline — a medic walking up with the promised kit, Petrescu's leg being re-splinted properly at last, tins of soup heating on borrowed flame. The survey team ate like animals and slept like the dead, wherever they fell, and August envied them and could not join them.

Because there was a figure at the edge of Crane's camp that August's eyes kept finding and refusing to believe.

He stood apart from the others, near the waterline, where the tide lapped at his boots and he seemed not to notice. Tall, stooped now in a way August's memory insisted was new. Grey in the beard. Wearing salvage-company oilskins over a surveyor's sweater, which was such a specific wrongness that it hurt to look at. He wasn't watching the camp or the tunnel or the boats. He was watching the glacier itself, with the fixed, hungry attention of a man reading a sentence he'd spent years trying to finish.

Halvard.

August crossed the shingle before deciding to, boots crunching, heart going like a hammer on a stuck rail. Up close the change was worse. Halvard had always been a large presence, a man who filled a room the way a locomotive fills a shed — heat and noise and certainty. This version of him had been emptied. The eyes were the same color and none of the same fire. When he finally turned and saw August standing there, he did not startle. He looked, if anything, relieved, the way a condemned man is relieved to see the priest.

"Whitlock." His voice was gravel poured slow. "You got my markers."

"I got your sabotage." August heard their own anger arrive late and thin, worn down by everything between here and the bridge that broke. "Spiked rails. Blown culverts. Your hand, your habits, your spacing. I could have written the report for the magistrate myself."

"Yes." Halvard looked back at the ice. "I meant it to stop everyone. It stopped almost everyone." A pause. "It didn't stop you. I should have known it wouldn't."

"You were Crane's man at the bridge inquiry. You signed the inspection that buried me."

"I know what I signed." For a moment something moved behind the emptiness — not defiance, not excuse. Grief, old and settled, like silt. "I've had nine days in a hole in the ice to know it. And longer than that to know it before that. Ask me what I know, Whitlock. Not tonight — I can't — but ask me. There are things you need to hear from a living mouth, and I am not sure how much longer I qualify."

The words landed strangely. August searched the ruined face for the mentor who had taught them to read stress in steel by ear, and found him and didn't find him, both at once, the way you find a house after a fire — the walls familiar, the life gone out of them.

"Why are you here?" August asked. "With them. After everything."

Halvard was quiet long enough that the tide turned. He looked down at the water climbing over his boots, and when he answered, he sounded like a man reciting his own indictment.

"Because I helped build the lie that put you under the mountain, and I wanted to see what the lie was worth. And now I've seen it." He turned those tired eyes on August fully for the first time. "It isn't worth anything. None of it is. But there's a manifest in that wreck, and it's the only true thing left in this whole affair, and I need it to reach the right hands before Crane's do. That's why I'm here. That's all I am now — a courier for a correction."

From the camp, Dreele's voice rang out, calling the watch rotation, and Halvard straightened like a man returning to a cell.

"Eighteen hours," he said. "Sleep if you can. Watch your people. And Whitlock—" He hesitated, and for one instant the old voice came through, the classroom voice, the voice that had said *check it twice and then check it again* to a young apprentice twenty years ago. "When the fifth tide opens, stand close to me in the tunnel. Whatever happens in there, you'll want to know which way I'm facing."

He walked back toward the fires of the camp that had ruined August's life, and August stood alone on the shingle with the sea rising, understanding suddenly and completely that the truce on this beach was worth exactly as much as the weakest man in it — and that the weakest man in it might be either Dreele or Halvard, and there was no way yet to know which.

Above the glacier, the light was failing. Two tides gone. Four left.

Somewhere under a thousand tons of ice, a strongroom full of stolen gold waited for all of them, patient as only the dead are patient.
