## 12. Third Tide

Sera Voss had grown up believing that ice was patient. Her grandmother used to say the glacier was the oldest thing in the valley — older than the railroad, older than the church, older than any name anyone had given it — and that patience was its whole character. Ice did not hate. Ice did not scheme. It simply waited, and then it closed.

She had never believed she would learn how quickly ice could move.

The truce lasted four hours. She would remember that number afterward with a bitter precision: four hours from the handshake at the tunnel mouth to the first gunshot's echo, four hours of Crane's foreman — a slab-faced man named Dreele who smiled like a man counting other people's money — pacing the edge of their fire and asking questions that were not questions. *Where is the manifest? Who holds it? What condition is Okafor in? Can he walk?* Each one laid down like a rail spike, and Sera had spent those four hours pretending not to hear the hammer coming down.

They had made camp in the lee of an old signal hut, half-swallowed by the moraine, its roof caved under decades of snowmelt. August had propped Halvard's ledger against a stone to dry the pages dampened by tunnel spray. The original wreck manifest — the thing every gun on this glacier now wanted — lay wrapped in oilcloth inside August's coat, and everyone knew it, because there was no way to hide knowledge once it existed. That was the trouble with truth. It weighed nothing and could not be buried quietly.

Okafor lay near the fire, her left leg splinted from knee to ankle where a shelf of ice had shrugged loose during the scramble at the strongroom approach. She had refused the morphine tablets in the survey kit with a flatness that frightened Sera more than screaming would have. "I need my head," she'd said. "Whatever comes next, I need my head."

What came next came at dusk, when the light went the color of old brass and the temperature dropped so fast the snow squeaked underfoot.

Dreele walked into the firelight without invitation. He had six men behind him, and Sera counted them the way her father had taught her to count cattle at market — not their faces but their hands, their weight, which of them stood like they'd used what they carried. Two of them had. The rest were dock labor and hired muscle, men who had signed on for wages and had not yet understood they'd signed on for a war.

"Evening," Dreele said pleasantly. "Thought we'd settle accounts before the tide."

"There's no account to settle," August said. They hadn't stood; they sat with their back against the signal hut's fallen beam, hands loose on their knees, and Sera recognized the stillness for what it was — an engineer reading load paths, calculating where the structure would fail.

"The manifest." Dreele held out his hand as if this were a transaction at a counter. "Mr. Crane's offer stands. Pardon, reinstatement, your name cleaned off every blacklist from here to the coast. All you have to do is hand over a piece of paper that isn't yours."

"It isn't his either," August said.

"No," Dreele agreed, smiling wider. "It's nobody's. Which means it belongs to whoever can hold it."

Sera saw it before it happened — saw it in the way the two heavy-handed men drifted apart, widening their arc, in the way Dreele's weight rolled forward onto his toes. Later she would understand that August had seen it too, and had chosen to keep talking anyway, buying seconds for the rest of them the way you'd shore up a failing trestle knowing it was going down regardless.

"The gold is public funds," August said. "Stolen out of these valleys. Every coin of it—"

The world went white and loud.

Not a gunshot — a flare, fired low, and Sera realized dimly that even Crane's people didn't want to shoot holes in a glacier they intended to walk back through. The flare burst above the camp and everything became hard shadows and long knives of light, and in that chaos Dreele lunged for August's coat and one of the heavy-handed men grabbed for the ledger and someone shouted and the fire scattered in a wheel of sparks—

—and Okafor, splinted leg and all, swung her survey staff off her lap and took Dreele across the knees with it.

He went down hard. The second man got a hand inside August's coat, and Sera did the only thing her body knew how to do, the thing she'd done since she was nine years old and her father put a telegraph key in front of her: she acted fast, precisely, and without hesitation. She threw the camp kettle — full, boiling, dragged off the fire without thought — into the man's face.

His scream was terrible. She would hear it for years. She did not regret it.

"Run," August said, already up, already hauling Okafor's arm across their shoulders. "Sera — *run.*"

She ran.

* * *

They went north, because north was away, and because north — though none of Crane's port-bred men could know it — was where the glacier stopped being a road and started being a trap.

Behind them: shouting, the flare's dying red glow, boots on scree. Ahead of them: the crevasse field the valley people called *Marens Tänder* — the maren's teeth — a mile-wide stretch where the glacier's surface had been chewed into ridges and hollows, seracs leaning like rotten teeth, and beneath the snow bridges, a hundred feet down in places, the blue-black water moving with the tide.

Her grandmother had taught her the field. Not from maps — there were no maps; the government surveys had marked it with a single dismissive hatched line labeled *impassable* — but by walking it, season after season, when the valley needed a route the railroad couldn't watch. *The ice tells you,* her grandmother said. *Snow lies. Ice doesn't.*

Now Sera ran onto it in the dark with three people who trusted her and a fourth — Ilsa Brandt's cousin Anders, who had caught up to them at the tunnel mouth that morning with a rifle and a face like a shut door — bringing up the rear, and she felt fear rise in her throat like floodwater.

Because here was the thing about Marens Tänder: in daylight, at leisure, with a season's knowledge, it was passable. In the dark, pursued, carrying a wounded woman, it was a graveyard with excellent acoustics. She could hear their pursuers behind — maybe eight of them now, Dreele's voice cracking across the moraine, furious, promising things — and she could hear something else too, deeper, a groan and shift in the ice as the third tide began to drain and the whole glacier's skeleton flexed.

"Sera." August, beside her, breathing hard under Okafor's weight. "Tell me you know this ground."

"I know it."

"How well?"

She thought of her grandmother's hand on her shoulder. *Well enough to bring us home or kill us trying, and the difference is whether you listen.* "Well enough," she said. "Stay exactly behind me. Step where I step. If I say *down*, everyone goes down and nobody asks why."

The first snow bridge took her weight with a sound like a struck drum — hollow underneath, but thick. She marked it, moved on. The second sagged. She skirted it along a ridge of blue ice polished slick by wind, driving her boot edges in, and reached back to grip Okafor's wrist and pull her across. Okafor didn't make a sound. Sweat had frozen in beads at her hairline. Her eyes, in the starlight, were perfectly clear.

"You've done this before," Okafor said.

"My grandmother has. I was cargo." Sera heard her own voice come out steady and was faintly surprised. Fear was still there — fear was everywhere, in the dark and the depth and the voices behind — but it had changed shape somewhere between the kettle and the first bridge. It had become information. That was the secret nobody told you about competence: it wasn't the absence of fear. It was fear, finally useful.

A serac the height of a church leaned over the route ahead, creaking as the tide pulled the ice down and inward. Beyond it the field opened into a chaos of pressure ridges, and beyond *that*, if her memory held, a ramp of compacted snow climbed toward the high bench where the wreck's valley narrowed — a lead of perhaps two hours on anyone who tried to follow blind.

Two hours. One tide. It might be enough.

Then the serac finished its argument with gravity.

There was no dramatics to it — that was the horror. A sound like a door closing, a softness in the air, and forty tons of ice came apart and folded down across the route fifty yards ahead, and the concussion rolled through the field and set every bridge within earshot ringing like bells.

Behind them, the pursuit stopped. Sera heard Dreele's men halt at the field's edge, heard the argument — one voice saying *we can't track that in the dark*, another saying *Crane wants it tonight* — and she pressed her party flat into the shadow of a pressure ridge and waited, heart slamming, while the glacier settled around them with small, intimate sounds, like a house at night.

"They'll come at first light," August whispered. "With ropes and lamps. Maybe with the dog team they unloaded at the mouth."

"Then we're not here at first light." Sera raised herself just enough to read the field ahead. The collapse had changed it — the ice always changed, that was the whole lesson — but change cut both ways. Where the serac had fallen, it had crushed one route and *revealed* another: a lane between two ridges that had not existed yesterday, floored with old wind-packed snow, running the direction she wanted.

Her grandmother again, quiet in her skull: *When the ice closes a door, look for the window it broke.*

"This way," she said. "And after this, I stop asking permission from the map."

* * *

They crossed for six hours.

She measured it not in distance but in decisions — ninety, a hundred of them, each one a snow bridge tested with a probe pole, each one a listening pause, each one a choice between the fast-looking route and the true one. Twice she turned them back on a path that looked open and found, thirty feet along, the telltale sag and the gray discoloration of thin bridging over void. Once the ice spoke to her directly: a deep, rising note underfoot, a harmonic she felt in her teeth, and she said *down* and all five of them flattened as a crevasse breathed somewhere beneath them, a cold exhalation through a crack no wider than a hand.

Okafor kept up. Sera never learned how. The surveyor's breath came in short iron strokes, and her splinted leg left a single-file signature in the snow that Sera noted with a professional's unease — a trail, plain as telegraph wire, for anyone following. Nothing to be done. You could not cross a glacier invisibly. You could only cross it faster than grief could follow.

Anders ranged the flanks without being asked, rifle slung, moving with a valley-born economy that made Sera realize how much of the settlement's skill had gone unrecorded, unwritten, passed hand to hand below the level of any official map. The government lines were closed, but the valleys had never needed them. The valleys remembered.

Near what she judged to be the deepest hour of night, they came down onto the high bench and she allowed the first rest. No fire. They sat shoulder to shoulder in the wind-shadow of an ice block, passing a flask of coffee frozen to syrup, and Okafor leaned her head back against the ice and laughed — a low, cracked, genuine laugh.

"You know what I keep thinking?" the surveyor said. "Back in the tunnel, rationing lamp oil, I told myself: if I get out, I will never again trust a document. Certifications, manifests, salvage rights — paper is how Crane kills people. Paper and patience." She lifted the flask toward Sera. "And then the thing that saves us is a woman who reads ice instead. The oldest document there is."

"It can be forged," Sera said. "Warm winters forge it. That's why the calving season matters. Why the tides matter."

"Everything is a ledger," August said quietly. They were looking back the way they'd come, toward the distant smudge of the salvage fleet's lights at the glacier mouth, a string of orange stars that had no business being stars. "That's what Halvard understood too late, and what Crane understands entirely. The question is only who keeps honest books."

"And who burns them," Anders said.

Sera looked at the lights a long moment. Six tides. This was the third, draining now beneath their feet; the fourth would open at midday, the fifth and sixth after that, and then the sea would take the low channel and the calving would begin and the whole coast of the ice would start shedding itself into the water like a snake leaving its skin. Whatever happened next happened inside a closing fist.

"We should move," she said. "There's a route down the far side of the bench that meets the old service spur above the wreck. We can be there before the fourth tide turns. And—" she hesitated, then said it, because honesty was a kind of engineering too, "—if Dreele follows with lamps and ropes, he'll make better time than we did. We'll have hours, not days. When we reach the wreck, we dig, we log what Okafor needs logged, and we decide what that manifest is *for*. Because paper that just sits in a coat gets people killed. Paper has to go somewhere."

August looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a bridge you've decided to trust your weight to — and nodded once.

"Lead on, then," they said.

* * *

They came down off the bench as the sky went the gray of unpolished steel, and below them, for the first time, Sera saw the wreck.

She had heard it described — Ilsa's grandfather's stories, the surveyors' fragments — but description was to sight what a timetable was to a journey. The locomotive hung in the glacier's flank like a fly in amber, tilted nose-down, its boiler and running gear sheathed in a rind of refrozen meltwater so that it seemed less buried than *grown into* the ice, part of the mountain's own anatomy. Behind it, the ghost-line of freight cars descended into the blue in a curve, each one fainter, until the last was only a shadow-shape, a suggestion, a rumor with rivets.

A supply train. Sealed since the war. And somewhere inside it, in a strongroom built to government specification, the seized gold of these valleys — restitution, inheritance, bait — waiting under a thousand tons of patience.

Sera stood on the slope and felt the fourth tide begin to stir, a shiver through the ice under her boots, the glacier inhaling.

Below, at the wreck's flank, something glinted that should not have been there.

Fresh rope. Fresh stakes. A cache box, government-stenciled, set into the ice beside the locomotive's exposed tender — and beside it, planted upright in the snow like a flag over claimed territory, a surveyor's stake bearing a strip of fresh-painted board.

On the board, in neat block letters, in paint still tacky enough to shine:

**CRANE SALVAGE CO. — WORKING CLAIM — TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED**

And tacked beneath it, fluttering in the wind off the ice, a single sheet of paper, weighted with a stone.

Sera skied down the last stretch — there was no other word for the controlled fall her boots made — and pulled the paper free, and read it aloud because August and Okafor had come up beside her and she could not keep it behind her teeth.

*"To the engineer Whitlock,"* she read. *"You have the manifest. I have the fleet, the foreman, and the fourth tide. Meet me at the wreck when it opens, alone, and we will conclude our business like professionals. Refuse, and my men will take the tunnel mouth behind you — and your friends in the valley will learn what my patience costs. Come alone. Come armed if it comforts you. — A.C."*

The wind took the corner of the letter and worried it, back and forth, like a dog deciding.

Above them, far up the glacier, a lamp flickered in the gray dawn — one, then two, then a slow descending chain of them, coming down the route she had opened.

"He followed the trail," Anders said grimly, unslinging his rifle. "My trail. My fault — I ranged wide on the flanks."

"No," Sera said. She folded the letter into her pocket, and found that her hands had gone very steady, and understood that the fear had finished its transformation — it was not gone, it was *assigned*, given work to do. "Nobody's fault. He was always coming. The only question was whether we'd be standing at the wreck when he arrived, or already inside it."

She looked at August. At Okafor, jaw set, splinted leg braced against a boulder. At the wreck hanging in its tomb of blue, and the fourth tide rising through the ice beneath their feet like a pulse.

"One tide," she said. "We have until the fourth tide turns. Let's spend it well."
