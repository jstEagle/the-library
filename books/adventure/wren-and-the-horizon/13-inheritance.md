## 13. Inheritance

The wreck did not look like a thing that had been lost.

It looked like a thing that had been *kept*.

August stood at the edge of the ice shelf where the tunnel widened into a vault of frozen blue, and the locomotive hung above them — actually hung, nose-down at a steep angle, its cowcatcher buried in ice that had flowed around it and stopped, the way a river stops around a stone. Forty years of glacier had neither crushed nor released it. The boiler's riveted plates gleamed under their lanterns, dark iron furred with frost, and behind the engine the tender and three boxcars followed in a broken chain, each car tilted a few degrees further than the last, as though the whole train had been caught mid-plunge and the mountain had simply decided to hold it there forever.

"It's flying," Sera said quietly. "That's what it looks like. Flying, and the sky closed over it."

August didn't answer. There was a feeling in their chest they had no clean name for — the feeling they'd had once, as a child, standing before their father's open toolbox, understanding for the first time that every tool in it had a story and that the stories were not theirs yet. Reverence, maybe. Or grief arriving early.

They had come down from the crevasse field with the sun still on the western rim, the third tide's lead still in their hands, and Okafor's people carried on litters to a hollow the ice offered like a cupped palm — a chamber above the wreck where the air moved faintly, fed by some hidden chimney through the glacier's crown. Okafor had insisted on being propped upright, his splinted leg stretched before him, his survey ledger open on his knees even as the morphia took the edge off his voice.

"Log it," he'd said. "Everything you see down there. Not for Crane. Not for the magistrate. For the record. The ice keeps what we write into it."

Now August stood below, lantern raised, and wrote.

*Locomotive, class 4-6-0, works number matching the Valley Central rosters of the war years. Tender marked VC-77. Three boxcars, couplings sheared on the second and third — she was moving fast when she went in. No burn scarring. No impact damage forward of the pilot. She didn't crash. She was driven in.*

The last line stopped their pen. They looked at it a long time.

Every engineer knew the shape of a wreck. August had spent three years of their life being blamed for one — they knew the geometry of failure the way a doctor knows the anatomy of a wound. A train that derailed on a curve tore itself sideways. A train that met a washed-out bridge went nose-first and folded. A train that slid on ice left a long scar of its own making, a plowed furrow you could read like a sentence.

This train had entered the glacier mouth at speed, on rails that the ice had long since swallowed, and the entry wound was *clean*. Straight. Committed.

Someone had opened the throttle on a dead line and pointed her into the white.

"August." Sera's voice came down from the shelf above, careful. "You've been standing there ten minutes. The light's going."

"I'm coming."

They weren't, quite. They were looking at the second boxcar, whose door had been sprung — not by the ice, not by the survey team, but by hands, recently. Okafor's people had opened it and logged it and closed it again, wedging the door with a piton. The frost around the wedge had been disturbed within days.

August climbed the ice skirt to the door, set their shoulder to it, and pulled.

* * *

Inside, the cold had its own quality — a dry, mineral stillness, the cold of deep caves, of places air had forgotten how to move. Their lantern threw a circle of gold across the floor of the car, and the floor was not floor. It was cargo.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked in the careful interlocking pattern of men who expected a long journey, each stenciled with the same marking in fading black:

**TREASURY OF THE VALLEY DISTRICTS — UNDER BOND — RETURN TO CAPITAL ON RECEIPT**

And beneath that, smaller, in a different hand, a stamp that had been applied in haste and slightly crooked: *RECEIVED IN LIEU OF ASSESSMENT.*

Ilsa Brandt's voice came back to August across the miles and the days — her firelit kitchen, her grandfather's photograph on the shelf, the way she'd said it, not as a legend but as a grievance handed down like a christening gown: *They took the harvest money. They took the bridge tolls, the school levies, the sawmill bonds. Grandfather drove the train that carried it out, and he said to his dying day the cars rode too light for what the manifests claimed.*

They rode light because the manifests lied. They lied because the gold was never meant to reach the capital. It was meant to reach a glacier.

August set the lantern on a crate and worked at the lid with their knife. The wood had frozen into something like stone, but the nails had rusted to lace, and the lid came up with a groan that echoed down the car like a word spoken in a church.

Sacking inside. And under the sacking, in the lantern light, the color that needs no name — the color every human eye is built to find. Gold coin, mint-stamped, packed in rows, each little cylinder of coins bound in paper bands that had gone the color of old bone. One band had split, and a spill of coins had frozen into the floor of the car, a scatter of small suns caught in the ice.

August did not touch them.

That was the thing they would remember later — that the moment of contact, the moment they'd been walking toward since the courier job, since the blacklisting, since the bridge, since childhood — did not arrive as a touch. It arrived as a *reading*. They stood in a frozen boxcar holding a lantern, looking at the life they had been promised would redeem them, and they understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes once or twice in a life, that none of this gold had ever been meant for them. Not as a gift. Not even as a temptation.

It had been meant for the valleys. It had been *taken* from the valleys. And every year it sat in the ice, the taking continued — a theft renewed each season, compounding like interest in a bank of cold.

* * *

The manifest was in the strongroom car — the third boxcar, the one with the iron door and the hasp that Okafor's badge had been found beside. August worked the lock with Sera holding the light, and the mechanism, forty years asleep, surrendered with a click that sounded absurdly small.

The strongroom was half the size of the cargo car and twice as cold. A iron-bound chest sat bolted to the floor, and on a shelf above it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the paper.

August unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon. The oilcloth had protected it perfectly — the glacier was a better archive than any government office — and the pages came out stiff and pale, the ink unfaded in the airless dark.

*Manifest of conveyance, Valley Central Railway, wartime emergency schedule. Consignment: specie and bonded instruments, Treasury of the Valley Districts. Gross declared weight: 41 tons, 6 hundredweight.*

August's eye went where an engineer's eye always goes first — to the numbers that could be checked against the machine. They had spent the walk down doing the arithmetic in their head, the way Halvard had taught them, the way Halvard had taught every student he ever ruined or saved: *the rails don't care about your paperwork. The rails care about weight.*

Forty-one tons of declared cargo. But the boxcars they'd opened held, at a generous count, perhaps half that. And the locomotive — a 4-6-0 of that class, with tender, on the mountain grades, on the old iron rails of the war years —

"Sera. The grade out of the pass. What does Ilsa's grandfather's logbook say it ran?"

"Four percent, worst section. Why?"

"Because a forty-one-ton load behind a class-ten engine on a four percent grade doesn't make it to the glacier mouth at speed. It crawls. It doubles the hill or it stalls. But she went in *flying* — clean entry, no stall, couplings sheared by momentum." August laid the manifest flat on the chest and set the lantern beside it. "She went in light. Half the declared weight, or less. The manifest was written for forty-one tons because forty-one tons was what the Treasury was told left the valley. The difference went into somebody's pockets before the train ever rolled — and the train itself was the receipt. Drive her into the ice, and there's nothing to audit. No arrival, no discrepancy. Just a lost train and a war to blame."

Sera was quiet for a moment. The lantern hissed.

"So the wreck isn't the crime," she said slowly. "The wreck is the *cover*. The gold in these cars is the part they couldn't carry away. The rest —"

"— the rest never existed. Or it existed in ledgers in the capital, drawn against the harvests of these valleys, signed for, and vanished." August looked down at the pages. "Okafor was right. It's not what's in the train that matters. It's the signatures."

Because there, in the column of authorizations, the names stood in a row like fence posts — officials of the wartime administration, receivers, commissioners, and at the bottom, countersigned as *salvage guarantor* for the recovery expedition that had, according to every record, never been mounted: a name August had seen only days ago, stamped on the provisioning manifests at the port, on the fleet's charters, on the letter of credit that paid the men hunting them through the ice.

**A. CRANE.**

He had been there at the beginning. Young then — a clerk, a factor's assistant, some minor instrument of the scheme — but *there*, his name on the paper, his signature the hinge between the theft and the silence. Forty years later he'd come back for the receipt. Not for the gold — the gold was almost incidental, a bonus. He'd come back for the manifest. Burn the paper, and the last copy of the crime died in the dark, and the valleys' stolen decades became a rumor forever, and Aldous Crane became simply a rich man, which is what the world forgives.

"He doesn't want the treasure," August said. "He wants the evidence. We've been guarding the wrong thing since the tunnel mouth."

Sera looked at the spill of coins frozen in the cargo car behind them, the small suns in the ice. "Then why does he want *this*?" She touched a coin's frozen edge with one gloved finger. "Why does any of it matter, if it's the paper he's after?"

August turned the manifest over. On the reverse, in the same wartime hand, was the schedule of assessments — the levies, district by district. *Brandt's Valley: harvest bond, bridge toll, school fund.* The names of the settlements ran down the page, and every name was a valley they had walked through or slept in, and beside every name a figure, and the figures together came to the wealth of a region's entire war.

"It matters," August said, "because it's *theirs*. That's what I keep getting wrong. I came up this line thinking like a man who'd lost something — a name, a license, a future. I thought if the gold came out, it would balance the ledger. Mine. Halvard's. Everybody's." They laughed, and the laugh came out strange in the cold, half a breath of steam. "But I was never on this ledger. None of us were. This is a debt owed to people who've been told for forty years that their grandparents were poor because the mountains are hard. They weren't poor. They were *robbed*, and the robbery was signed, witnessed, and buried in a glacier."

Sera watched them with that stillness she had, the stillness of a woman who spent her working life listening for signals under noise. "And what do you do with a debt like that?"

"You don't *claim* it," August said. "You *answer* it."

* * *

They carried the manifest back up to the chamber in its oilcloth, wrapped now also in Sera's spare shirt and strapped against August's ribs, inside their coat, over the heartbeat — because August had thought it through in the cargo car, coldly, the way they'd once calculated bridge loads: the paper was worth more than the gold now, and worth exactly as much as it could be *produced*. A document hidden was a document lost. A document carried was a document that could be taken. The only safe place for it was somewhere it could do its work, and its work was not in the ice.

Okafor was awake when they climbed in, gray-faced, propped against his instrument case, and when August laid the manifest open on the blanket between them, the old surveyor read it in silence for a long time, his cracked lips moving over the names of the valleys.

"I catalogued the cargo cars two days ago," he said at last. "I estimated the weight against the declared manifest. I reached the same figure you did." He looked up, and his eyes were clear above the morphia haze — the eyes of a man who had spent six weeks in the dark deciding what mattered. "I refused Crane because he asked me to certify the wreck as derelict. Unclaimed. Ownerless. Do you understand what that word does, legally? *Derelict* means the debt dies. Derelict means no one was ever owed. He wasn't buying salvage rights. He was buying an epitaph."

"And now?" Sera asked.

"Now I have a signature, a manifest, and a weight discrepancy I can demonstrate with a schoolbook and a set of scales." Okafor's hand closed gently on the page's corner, not taking it — anchoring it, the way a man anchors a boat. "This goes to the magistrate. All of it. The ledger you found in Halvard's camp, the team's testimony, the manifest, the coins — photographed, weighed, witnessed, before anything else happens to any of us. The gold can stay in the ice a hundred more years and be no poorer for it. The *record* is the inheritance. The record is the only thing in this mountain that can't be melted down."

August looked at the old man and felt something settle in their chest that had been loose since the bridge — since the hearing, since the headlines, since the morning they'd stood in the ruins of their own reputation and heard the word *negligent* applied to their name like a brand.

"Okafor," they said. "The inquiry into my bridge. Halvard's ledger names Crane's company as the supplier of the steel. If the magistrate reopens one file, he'll have to reopen the other."

"Then we make sure he reopens both." Okafor almost smiled. "You came up this mountain to clear your name, engineer. It seems you'll have to clear a valley's while you're at it. The paperwork is regrettably entangled."

Outside the chamber, far down the tunnel, the ice made its long sounds — the groans and tickings of a glacier adjusting its grip, and under them, once, faint but unmistakable, the flat report of a blasting charge, echoing up from the direction of the mouth.

Sera's head came up. "That's not calving. That's *drilling*."

August was already moving to the chamber's mouth, looking down the blue cathedral of the tunnel toward the distant gleam of the entrance. The light there had changed. It was no longer the clean silver of open sky on ice. It was cluttered, broken, moving — the light of many lanterns, and behind them, at the very threshold, the dark shapes of masts and derricks, the salvage fleet unloading at the glacier's door.

And coming up the valley floor behind them — small, on foot, at the edge of hearing — a sound August had not expected and did not yet believe: the creak of wagon wheels, many of them, and voices. Dozens of voices. Valley voices.

Ilsa had said the valley remembers.

It seemed the valley had decided to *answer*.
