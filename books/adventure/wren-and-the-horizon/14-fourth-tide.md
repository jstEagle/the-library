## 14. Fourth Tide

Ilsa Brandt had not carried a rifle since the winter her father died, and the weight of it across her back felt less like a weapon than like a question — one she had been turning over in her hands for three days while the valley argued itself awake.

The powder was easier to decide about. Powder from the old works at Kessel's Bend had been sitting in wax-sealed crates under floorboards and behind false walls since the line closed, because valley people did not throw away things that could still do work. Her grandfather had helped bury it there in '09, when the company men came through with their inventory lists and their talk of liability, and he had told her once, laughing over his pipe: *A mountain keeps its own counsel, girl. But a crate of powder keeps yours.* She had thought it an old man's joke then. Now she walked at the head of eleven neighbors and forty crates of kept counsel, and the joke sat in her chest like a stone.

They went overland, the way no map showed. That was the point of the route — Sera Voss had drawn it for them on butcher paper before the second tide, tracing lines with a pencil stub worn to the wood: *Here the shelf above the moraine. Here the scree chute; loose rock, go quiet. Here you'll see the glacier mouth, and God help you, don't come down onto the ice without someone who knows it.* Ilsa had folded the paper into her coat where it rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Behind her, the volunteers came on in single file through the thin morning fog. Old Piet Maren, who had driven section crews for thirty years and now walked with a stick but refused the mule's lead rope. The Halvorsen brothers, silent as their own sawmill. Young Anneli, sixteen, who had never seen the pass open and talked about it the way other girls talked about the sea. And Marta Oduya, the schoolteacher, who had stood up in the meeting hall three nights ago and said the thing that ended the arguing:

"They took our gold out of our ground and sank it in our ice, and then they told us the mountain ate it. Now they've come back to eat the mountain too. I say we get there first."

Ilsa had watched August Whitlock's face when Marta said it. She had been watching that face for days, the way you watch weather. There was something in it that reminded her of her grandfather's last winter — a person standing at the edge of a thing they believed they did not deserve, waiting to be told to leave. When Marta finished speaking, the hall had gone quiet, and August had looked around at all those faces as if expecting them to turn away, and none of them had, and something in Whitlock's shoulders had finally come down half an inch.

That was why Ilsa carried the rifle. Not for Crane's men. For whatever it was that still expected to be turned away.

* * *

They came down off the shelf at midday and saw the mouth of the glacier for the first time, and Anneli's breath went out of her audibly, and Ilsa did not blame her.

She had grown up on her grandfather's stories of the pass — the blue cathedral, he called it, the door God forgot to shut — but stories are small things, hand-sized, and the reality filled the whole end of the valley. The glacier came down between two shoulders of black rock like a river that had been told to stop and had obeyed so completely it had forgotten how to move. At its base, where the summer melt ran low, a tunnel opened into the ice, and even from a mile away Ilsa could see the light inside it: not white, not gray, but a deep submarine blue, as if they were looking down into the throat of the sea.

And below it, on the gravel flat where the old rail bed ran out against the ice, were the tents.

Crane's fleet had arrived. Even at this distance Ilsa could count the shapes — canvas shelters, a cook tent with smoke rising straight in the windless air, stacked crates, and figures moving among them with the unhurried efficiency of men who had done this many times in many places. Two dories and a barge were drawn up on the shore of the meltwater pool, and beyond them, out on the gray water, the fleet's steam launch lay at anchor, its engine ticking over like a patient animal.

"Forty men," said Piet Maren, squinting. "Maybe fifty."

"Eleven of us," said his brother-in-law Torvald.

"And forty crates of what their grandfathers buried," Ilsa said. "Count again."

But her voice came out flatter than she intended, because she was looking past the tents now, at the tunnel mouth, where a smaller camp clung to the rocks — three lean-tos, a fire ring, and figures that moved differently. Slower. One of them stood at the edge of the ice with a spyglass raised, watching the salvage camp the way a shepherd watches wolves at the edge of the flock.

"Whitlock's there," she said. "See the one in the patched coat? That's them."

"You're sure?"

"I'd know that coat anywhere. It's been patched twice since I sewed it myself." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "Third time's mine too, if we live long enough."

They made camp in the rocks above, screened by a ridge of glacial till that smelled of stone dust and old snowmelt, and set to the work of being invisible. No fires. Tents pitched low, brown canvas against brown rock. The powder crates distributed among four caches so that no single discovery would cost them everything. Piet took first watch with the spyglass, lying flat on his belly among the boulders like a man napping in the sun, and reported in a murmur that the salvage crew was building something at the tunnel mouth — timber frames, block and tackle, a rail spur being laid across the gravel toward the ice.

"They mean to bring it out whole," Torvald said. "Locomotive and all, if they can."

"Can they?" asked Anneli.

Piet was quiet a moment. "My grandfather pulled a wrecked engine out of the Kessel gorge in '88," he said. "Took him two years and it killed two men. This lot thinks they can do it in six tides." He spat, delicately, into the scree. "That tells you what they think of the mountain."

* * *

Toward evening, Ilsa went down alone.

It was not a decision she announced, because announcing it would have started an argument, and arguments cost daylight. She left word with Marta — *watch, whistle twice if the camp moves* — and descended the goat track along the ridge with the light going amber and long, until the shadows of the peaks reached down and touched the gravel flat and the temperature dropped ten degrees in as many minutes.

August was at the edge of the third lean-to, feeding paper into a small stove, and heard her boots on the rock before she meant to be heard. That was another thing she had learned about Whitlock in the days since the meeting hall: they moved like someone always listening for the sound of doors closing behind them. They turned, saw her, and something crossed their face — surprise, then a quick calculation, then, unmistakably, relief — before discipline smoothed it over.

"You shouldn't be down here alone," August said.

"Neither should you." She nodded at the camp. "Where's Voss?"

"In the tunnel. With Okafor's people. They're bringing the worst of the survey team out tonight — the ones who can't walk far. She knows the crevasse field better than any of us." August hesitated. "There was a collapse. Deep in. The team's been living behind it for weeks on lamp oil and hardtack. We got most of them out on the second and third tides, but—" The sentence stopped, and Ilsa watched August decide how much truth to spend. "Two didn't make the first pull. Frostbite. One of them may lose the foot."

"The fleet know they're alive?"

"They know *someone's* in there. They don't know how few." August looked past her, up the ridge, toward where the valley camp lay hidden. "You brought them. All of them. Ilsa — you should understand what you're walking into. Crane doesn't negotiate. He acquires. If he can't buy this mountain he'll take it, and if he can't take it he'll burn his own ships rather than let anyone else have it. I've read his contracts. I've read what's left of the men who signed them."

"So have I." She sat down on a rock beside the stove, uninvited, the way you sit in your own kitchen. "My grandfather drove trains through that pass for nineteen years. My mother was born in a company house at the mile-twelve station. When the line closed, they told us it was economics. It wasn't economics, was it? It was a train full of our money at the bottom of the ice, and men who preferred the ice to the arithmetic."

August fed the last of the paper to the stove. The firelight moved on their face, and Ilsa saw the exhaustion there, deep as the crevasses, and under it something harder that hadn't been there in the meeting hall.

"Halvard gave me the manifest," August said quietly. "The original. The gold was public funds — road money, school money, grain subsidies. Stolen before it ever left the coast, sunk deliberately to hide the theft. It isn't treasure, Ilsa. It's a debt. And debts to dead men are the hardest kind, because they can't tell you how they want them paid."

"How do *you* want to pay it?"

August was silent long enough that the stove ticked as it cooled. Out on the flat, the salvage camp lit its lanterns one by one, a string of yellow eyes waking along the gravel.

"There's powder in the old works at Kessel's Bend," August said at last. "Enough, if it's placed right, to bring the ice shelf down across the tunnel mouth. Seal it. Permanently. Calving season will do the rest — the whole face comes off every spring anyway. In five years there won't be a mark of it."

"And the gold goes under with it."

"And the gold goes under with it." August's jaw tightened. "Every coin. Because the moment one bar of it reaches Crane's hands, it stops being restitution and becomes a treasury. He'll buy the magistrate. He'll buy the newspapers. He'll buy the valley's debts right out from under it, and he'll do it with money that was stolen from the valley in the first place, and everyone will call it progress."

Ilsa looked at the fire, and thought of her grandfather's pipe, and his laugh, and the crates under the floorboards at Kessel's Bend that he had guarded for fifty years without ever saying plainly what they were for. *A mountain keeps its own counsel.* Maybe the old man had known, all along, that someday the valley would have to choose between what the ice held and what it was worth.

"My grandfather used to say the pass was the only honest thing the company ever built," she said. "Rails don't lie about where they go. Men lie about why." She stood, and brushed the rock dust from her coat, and looked down at August Whitlock — this stranger with a ruined name and a borrowed compass, whom the valley had decided, in the space of one meeting and three hard days, to call their own. "We didn't come down here to watch you decide, engineer. We came to help you carry it. Whatever you choose."

August looked up at her, and for a moment the discipline failed entirely, and what was underneath was so raw and so young that Ilsa had to look away out of simple decency.

"No one's ever—" August began, and stopped, and tried again. "Since the bridge, everyone I met wanted something. A signature. A confession. A scapegoat. You people want nothing. You just *came*."

"We want the mountain," Ilsa said. "Same as you. That's not nothing. That's everything, held in common." She turned toward the track up the ridge. "Get some sleep, Whitlock. Tomorrow the fourth tide drains at noon, and I'd wager my grandmother's silver Crane means to use it. So will we."

* * *

The fourth tide came in gray and cold, with a wind off the icefield that sang in the rocks like a finger round a wet glass rim.

From the ridge, Ilsa watched the salvage camp break apart and reassemble itself into purpose. Fifty men became columns; columns became a working front at the tunnel mouth; and by nine o'clock the sound of their blasting reached the valley camp — flat, wrong-sounding cracks, echoes with nowhere honest to go, rolling up the valley and dying against the peaks. Each blast shook a fine veil of snow from the glacier's shoulders. Each veil of snow was answered, a minute later, by a deep groan from somewhere inside the ice, the sound of a very large thing being asked a question it did not like.

"They're cutting a shortcut," Piet said, glasses pressed to his eyes. "Parallel gallery, upslope of the main tunnel. They mean to come at the wreck from above."

"That'll destabilize the whole face," said Torvald. "Any fool can see the fracture lines from here."

"Crane's fools aren't paid to see," Ilsa said. "They're paid to dig."

Below, at the small camp by the ice, movement: August and two of Okafor's recovered surveyors — one limping badly, wrapped in every blanket the camp owned — moving toward the tunnel mouth with lamps and rope. Going in. Using the tide, same as the enemy, racing them through the same blue dark toward the same frozen prize.

Ilsa found she was holding her breath, and let it out slowly.

"Marta," she said. "Get the caches broken open. All of them. Bring the powder down to the shelf above the tunnel — quietly, in relays, and pack the crates in snow so they don't show. Piet, you and Torvald walk the shelf and find me the places where the ice meets rock — the seams, the joints, everywhere the mountain is holding itself together by good manners. If we're going to close this door, we'll close it properly, the way my grandfather would have."

Marta paused, schoolteacher's chalk-dust patience on her face. "And if Whitlock chooses different? If they decide to take the gold out after all — claim it, testify with it, buy the valley's future with it?"

Ilsa watched the tunnel mouth, where a lamp had just vanished into the blue.

"Then we'll have carried forty crates of powder down a mountain for nothing," she said, "and I'll write Whitlock the rudest letter of their life. But look at them, Marta. Look at who went in first, ahead of us, ahead of the money, with two frostbitten strangers on their back. People show you what they'll choose by what they choose when choosing costs them." She shouldered her rifle. "Now move. The tide's draining, and we've got maybe six hours of honest dark to work in."

They worked. All day the valley people moved like water finding its level — up and down the shelf, crates passed hand to hand, drills biting into the seams where fifty years ago brandt-men and company men had drilled together to build the pass and now drilled to end it. By late afternoon Ilsa stood in a cut trench above the tunnel mouth with her sleeves rolled and her hands black with powder dust, looking down at the salvage camp's timber frames and block and tackle, and felt her grandfather beside her so strongly she almost spoke to him.

At dusk, the tunnel exhaled.

Not a groan this time — a long, resonant sigh, deep enough to feel in the teeth, and every head on both sides of the valley lifted. High on the glacier's face, a crack opened with a sound like a rifle shot, ran forty yards, and stopped. Snow sifted down. The silence afterward was enormous.

In the camp below, lanterns swung, and men's voices rose and fell, and Ilsa saw a tall figure in a gray coat come out of the big tent and stand looking up at the crack for a long time. Aldous Crane, then. Smaller than she had imagined. Still as a nail.

"He knows," Torvald murmured. "He can hear it too."

"Good," said Ilsa Brandt, and chambered nothing, and wished for nothing, and only watched. "Let him lie awake counting what the mountain's about to charge him."

Down at the ice, a lamp reappeared at the tunnel mouth — one lamp, then two, then a slow procession of exhausted figures coming out into the last of the light. August was among them, carrying a child-sized bundle of blankets that resolved, horribly, into a man. Behind them, deeper in the blue, the salvage crews' blasting began again, closer now, more urgent, each report shaking fresh snow from the wound in the glacier's face.

The fifth tide would come at dawn.

Ilsa counted the powder crates in the trench, counted the hours, and sent Anneli running for the fuse wire.
