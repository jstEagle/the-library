## 16. Fifth Tide

August woke to the sound of the glacier breathing.

It was a sound they had learned over four tides now — a long exhalation from deep in the ice, like a bellows drawn slow, the whole mass of the pass contracting as the sea pulled back. When it came at dawn, gray light still pooled in the valley like spilled milk, August knew without looking at Sera's tide tables that the fifth window had opened.

Five of six. One left after this, and then the mountain would shut its throat for another year.

They broke camp in silence, the kind of silence that had stopped being awkward days ago and become a language of its own. Ilsa's volunteers moved among them with the economy of people who had hauled freight through worse weather than this — checking lashings on the powder satchels, testing lamp flints, saying nothing that didn't need saying. Okafor sat propped against a supply crate with his splinted leg stretched out before him, his face gray but his eyes clear, and when August passed he pressed the surveyor's badge into their palm and closed their fingers around it.

"You go first," he said. "You're the only one of us who's ever driven iron through ice."

"That was my father."

"Then you've got it in the blood, and blood doesn't wait." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Bring back what we bled for."

The tunnel mouth stood open in the dawn like the doorway of a cathedral built by something with no interest in human worship. Blue light fell out of it across the shingle — not reflected light but *stored* light, decades of compressed winters glowing up through the ice, and August felt the old pull in their chest, the same pull they'd felt as a child standing at the rail of their father's cab, watching track unspool into country no map had ever bothered to name.

Sera appeared at their shoulder, coiling the last of the rope. Her breath smoked. "Crane's camp is moving," she said quietly. "I counted lanterns coming down from their ridge line twenty minutes ago. They're not waiting for the full drain."

"They can't run the tunnel before mid-drain. The floor's still flooded below the second bend."

"They know that too. Which means they're going to do something about it." She looked at August, and in her face was the arithmetic she'd been doing all night. "Blasting powder shortens waits, August. Blasting powder also brings down cathedrals."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I watched you sleep last night, and you slept like a man who'd already decided how this ends, and I'd like to hear it said out loud before we walk into the dark together one more time."

August turned the badge over in their hand. Okafor's name was stamped inside the rim, worn nearly smooth by years of thumbing.

"I decided," they said, "that whatever happens in there, the manifest comes out. And if the ice starts talking — really talking, not just groaning — we leave everything else and we don't look back. Not the gold. Not the wreck. Nothing."

"And Crane?"

"Crane made his bargain with the mountain when he started cutting corners in it. I'm not going to die making sure he keeps his end."

Sera held their gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, and that was the whole ceremony. They shouldered their packs and went into the blue.

* * *

Inside, the tunnel was different on the fifth opening. August had noticed it changing since the third tide — the walls sweating, meltwater running in bright threads down surfaces that had been dry as kiln brick — but today the change had a voice. Somewhere above them, deep in the hundred feet of ice that roofed the passage, something vast shifted its weight, and the sound that came down was less a crack than a sigh, long and patient, the sound of a thing that had all the time in the world and knew exactly what it intended to do with it.

"Keep moving," August said, and hated how much the words cost.

They moved. The floor was a riverbed of cobbles slick with runoff, and they went fast where it was safe and slower where Sera's raised hand said it wasn't, threading the crevasse mouths she'd mapped on earlier runs — black slots in the ice floor breathing cold air, some wide enough to swallow a handcar whole. Behind them came four of Ilsa's strongest: Brandt cousins, all of them, with rope coils and spare lamps and the grim competence of people who'd grown up on stories of this exact passage and had never expected to walk it themselves.

At the second bend, August stopped so abruptly that Sera walked into their back.

The air ahead smelled of burnt cordite.

A haze hung in the tunnel, thin but unmistakable, drifting toward them from deeper in. And along the right-hand wall, where the ice rose in a smooth blue sweep toward the ceiling, a scar had been cut — fresh, ragged, blackened at its edges. A blast hole. Someone had fired a charge into the wall not hours ago, maybe less, punching a rough shelf of shattered ice outward onto the floor.

"They're cutting a shortcut," Sera breathed. "Through the bend."

"Through the *ice*." August put their palm flat against the tunnel wall and felt it — not heard it, felt it, the way an engineer feels a bridge deck under a passing train. A tremor ran through the mass, faint, arrhythmic, wrong. Solid ice spoke in long tones. This was speaking in stutters. "Every charge they fire is a hammer on glass. They don't care. They think they'll be out with the gold before the bill comes due."

"Will they be?"

August looked down the tunnel, into the haze and the blue, toward the wreck that waited somewhere past the fourth marker. Cracks spidered overhead where the lamplight caught them — hairline veins branching through the vaulted ceiling like frost on a windowpane, more of them than there had been yesterday, more than there had been that morning.

"No," they said. "But they might be fast enough to kill us proving it."

They went on.

* * *

The third marker passed. Then the fourth. The floor rose out of the floodwater and the going quickened, and August drove the pace until the Brandt cousins were breathing hard and Sera had to catch their sleeve twice to point out crevasse lips hidden under melt-slick. Twice the glacier sighed above them, and both times everyone stopped without being told, lamps tilted upward, watching the blue vault as if reading it.

The second time, a vein that hadn't been there before now ran forty feet along the ceiling, straight as a surveyed line.

"That's new," one of the cousins said. Young — Marta, August thought her name was. Her voice tried for steadiness and mostly arrived.

"It's honest, at least," August said. "Ice tells you what it's doing if you listen. It's steel that lies." They thought of a bridge deck at dawn, eleven years ago, and the sound it had made, and pushed the memory down where it lived. "Move."

The wreck announced itself before they saw it — a smell of old coal and colder rust threading through the glacial air — and then the last bend opened and there it was, exactly as August had left it two nights before, and no less impossible for the seeing again.

The locomotive lay on its side in its tomb of ice, mid-flight even now, drivers frozen in the act of turning, tender jackknifed behind it, the long spine of freight cars strung away into the blue like vertebrae. Meltwater had begun to pool beneath it. In two more tides the water would reach the axles; in three, the calving would take the whole cathedral down and fold it into the sea, and every ton of it — engine, gold, history — would be gone past any salvage fleet's reach forever.

Which was, August reminded themselves, the plan.

"The strongroom's the third car back," Sera said, consulting the sketch Okafor had drawn from his team's survey. "Behind the mail compartment. Reinforced door, combination lock — Okafor's man copied the sequence off the manifest fragment."

"Then let's—"

The glacier screamed.

Not sighed. Screamed — a report like artillery, somewhere above and behind, and the tunnel filled with a white hiss as a sheet of spalled ice sheared off the ceiling fifty yards back and came down across the passage they'd just walked. The concussion rolled through the floor and up through August's boots, and dust of powdered ice hung in the lamplight like the ghost of snowfall.

Nobody spoke. Then, faintly, carried down the tunnel, came voices — human voices, shouting, and the flat hard bark of a blasting horn sounding three times.

Crane's crew. At the collapse. Either clearing it or causing the next one.

"They're in," Marta whispered. "They're *in*, behind us—"

"Then we have exactly as long as it takes them to dig." August was already moving. "Third car. Go."

* * *

The strongroom door was a slab of riveted steel gone brown with age, set into the car's flank like a bank vault dropped into a tomb. Okafor's copied sequence was inked on a scrap of paper Sera had sewn inside her coat lining, and while she read it out by lamplight, August worked the dial with numb fingers, listening — half to the mechanism, half to the tunnel behind them, where the blasting horn sounded again, closer now, and beneath it a new sound: the ring of steel tools on ice, rhythmic, patient, professional.

*Click.*

The wheel spun. The door swung inward on hinges that shrieked like something dying, and lamplight fell into a chamber hardly bigger than a cell — shelved ledgers swollen with damp, a clerk's desk bolted to the floor, drawers yanked open by hands eighty years dead. And at the back, a strongbox the size of a coffin, its lid thrown back, empty.

Empty.

For one lurching heartbeat the world tilted — *it's gone, it was never here, Halvard lied, all of it for nothing* — and then Sera's lamp swept the box's interior and caught the gleam of paper. A packet, oilcloth-wrapped, wedged into the false bottom. And beside it, laid deliberately across the box's lip where no one could miss it, a single brass railway spike, polished bright by someone's keeping.

Halvard's spike. His trail marker. His signature, written the way he'd taught August to write theirs: in hardware.

"He's been here," August breathed. "He came ahead. Last night, during the fourth drain — he took the gold out of the box and—" They lifted the oilcloth packet. Inside, wrapped around a core of something dense and cold, was a note in a hand August would have known blindfolded, the letters squared and sure:

*You'll want proof, not weight. The metal stays with me until you decide what it's for. If your answer is the valley's, meet me at the sixth marker. If it's yours alone, take the manifest and go, and I won't follow you. — H.*

Beneath the note, the original wreck manifest itself — brittle, foxed, stamped with the treasury seal of a government that had stolen from its own mountains — and beneath that, the dull lead-gray gleam of a single bullion bar, left as pledge and promise both.

"He split it," Sera said slowly. "He took the gold and left you the choice. That's either trust or a trap."

"With Halvard it was always both." August's hands were shaking, and not from cold. Eleven years of shame, and here it was in oilcloth: the truth, portable, light enough to carry out of a collapsing glacier. All they had to do was walk.

From the direction of the tunnel mouth, the blasting horn sounded a fourth time — and then, instead of falling silent, kept sounding, over and over, urgent, ragged, nothing professional about it anymore.

And above them, the ice began to talk in earnest. Not sighs now. Reports — sharp cracks chasing each other across the ceiling like footsteps running, and through the strongroom's little steel doorway August watched a crack race along the vaulted blue overhead, branching, doubling, spidering out toward the wreck itself, toward the locomotive frozen mid-flight, toward the hundred feet of glacier that had held this grave for eighty years and was, under Crane's hammers, beginning at last to let go.

"Marta," August said, very calmly, because calm was contagious and panic was too, and right now eight lives depended on which one traveled faster. "Run back the way we came and tell the others at the mouth: everyone out on this drain or not at all. Tell them August Whitlock said the mountain's done negotiating."

Marta went, lamp bobbing, boots slapping the wet cobbles, and her footsteps faded into the blue.

Sera looked at August. August looked at the manifest in their hands, and the bar, and the empty strongbox, and the door of the wreck's mail compartment hanging open behind them — and from deep in the tunnel, between one hammer-blow of cracking ice and the next, came a voice they knew, cracked and hollowed and carrying anyway, calling down the dark:

*"Whitlock! Sixth marker! You want your answer, come take it — and come RUNNING!"*

The ceiling split with a sound like the world's largest door unlocking, and the fifth tide began, all around them, to close.
