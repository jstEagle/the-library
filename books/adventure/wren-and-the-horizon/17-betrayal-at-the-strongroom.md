## 17. Betrayal at the Strongroom

Halvard had spent forty years reading ice, and the ice was telling him it was done.

He heard it before he saw it — a sound like a great door groaning on rusted hinges, deep in the glacier's bones, beneath the shriek of Crane's drills. The tunnel had held for a hundred years because nothing had ever asked it to hold anything. Now men with powder and greed were asking, and the ice was answering the only way it knew how.

He stood at the mouth of the strongroom corridor with his back against the frozen hull of a supply car, and he made his decision the way he had made every bad decision of his life: quickly, and too late to be anything but costly.

* * *

It had been different, at the beginning. That was the lie he told himself, and he told it well, because he was an old man and old men need their lies to walk on.

It had been different when Crane first came to him, seven years ago, with charts and charm and a handshake like a vise. *The lines are dying, Halvard. The valleys are dying. Somebody has to make the numbers work, or they'll close everything and let the mountains take it all back.* And Halvard, who had spent his life believing that rails were the only honest prayer a man could offer — steel laid true, carrying people to one another — had wanted so badly to believe there was a way to save it all that he had let Crane teach him a new arithmetic. One in which a bridge could stand at eighty percent and be called a hundred. One in which an inspection report was not a record but a tool.

He had written the numbers that broke the Kestrel Crossing. He had watched the span come down with August Whitlock's name on the work orders, and he had told himself the sacrifice was necessary, that one engineer's ruin was cheap against the salvation of a hundred miles of line. Crane had promised the money saved would go back into the mountain routes. Halvard had believed him the way a man believes in a church he has stopped attending — out of habit, and because the alternative was unbearable.

Then came the pass. Then came the wreck.

Halvard had been the one who found the first survey markers, years ago, when he was still doing Crane's quiet errands. He had known what slept in the ice — every man who ever drove the mountain line had known the story of the scuttled supply train, the gold that went into the glacier instead of into the treasuries it was stolen from. He had told Crane because he thought Crane would want it documented, catalogued, returned. He had not yet learned what Crane wanted anything *for*.

And then he had walked the tunnel with the salvage crews, in the second and third tides, and seen the survey team's lamp oil spent down to nothing, and heard Okafor describe the sound of Crane's men sealing the collapse behind them, and understood — with the slow, cold certainty of ice forming — that he had spent his whole career building the road by which this man traveled.

* * *

Now the fifth tide was draining and the strongroom stood open before him.

The wreck's rear car had been a treasury car in another life; the salvage engineers had cut through the frozen coupling with thermite and patience, and now the door hung open on a vault of crates, and the crates held the dull gold gleam that made grown men forget the mountain was dying around them. Crane's foreman, a slab of a man named Grett, was directing the haul — ropes and runners and a sledge train being assembled with the efficiency of men who had done this in other people's countries.

Crane himself stood a little apart, gloved hands clasped behind him, regarding the gold the way a bishop regards a reliquary. He had not touched it. He never touched anything. That was his genius and his obscenity — the world arranged itself around him while his hands stayed clean.

"Halvard." Crane's voice carried without effort. "Your assessment. How long do we have?"

Halvard looked up. The tunnel ceiling above the strongroom corridor had changed since the blasting — the blue had gone white along a seam, and the seam had gone from a hairline to a finger's width, and from the finger's width it was weeping. Meltwater ran in bright threads down the ice, catching the flare light. The glacier was bleeding from a hundred small wounds, and every one of them was Crane's doing.

"Hours," Halvard said. "Not tides. Hours. The shortcut you blasted cut the arch at its knee. When the calving starts, this whole corridor goes."

"Then we work faster." Crane smiled at him, and it was the smile from seven years ago, and Halvard felt something in his chest close like a fist. "That's what I keep you for, old friend. To tell me what the mountain permits."

*What the mountain permits.* As if the mountain were a clerk in Crane's office. As if a hundred years of ice were a line item.

Halvard looked past Crane, down the corridor, to where August Whitlock stood with the manifest in his coat and Sera Voss at his shoulder. The boy — no, not a boy anymore, a man weathered into something harder and cleaner than Halvard had ever managed to be — was watching the salvage crew with the flat, exhausted vigilance of a soldier who has stopped expecting the truce to hold. Their eyes met. August's face did not soften. Halvard had not earned softness. But there was something in the look that was not hatred either, and Halvard had been turning that something over in his mind for two days like a stone with a fossil in it, trying to read what was written there.

Forgiveness, maybe. Or its foundation. Or simply the recognition that they were both men the mountain had already judged, and the mountain's judgment was the only one left that mattered.

The gate was the answer. Halvard had been studying it since the salvage crew cut the car open — a freight door on a manual screw-lock, war-era iron, built to be sealed from inside the car against thieves. The screw mechanism still turned. He had oiled it that morning with grease from his own kit, quietly, while the others slept, and told himself he was maintaining equipment, because he was still, even now, telling himself stories.

The gate could be locked from the outside. The handle could be jammed. The iron was thick enough that Crane's powder, used carefully — and Crane's men would use it carefully, this close to the gold — would take an hour to breach. An hour. The ice did not need an hour. The ice needed a seam to fail, and the seams were failing now, he could hear them, a ticking like the dying signal lamp in that abandoned station, like the whole world counting down.

* * *

He waited for the tide to turn. That was the engineering of it, the only part he had left to contribute: timing.

The water in the tunnel floor drained toward the mouth in long, glassy sheets as the sixth tide began its retreat, and the salvage crew's rhythm changed with it — they worked the haul in the low water, when the sledge runners could bite. Grett shouted the change. Twenty men bent to the ropes. Crane turned to watch the first sledge come out of the strongroom, and for ten seconds his back was to the corridor, and his attention was on his gold.

Halvard crossed the distance to the gate.

His knees were seventy years old and the ice was slick, and he went down once, hard, and no one noticed because every eye was on the sledge. He got up. He reached the gate. The screw-lock turned under his hands with a resistance like the mountain itself considering the matter — and then, smoothly, terribly easily, it turned.

He threw the lock. He drove the jamming bar — a length of drill steel he had carried on his back all day, heavy as a sin — through the handle and braced it against the door frame, and hammered it home with three blows of his maul that rang through the tunnel like a bell.

The sound turned every head in the glacier.

"Halvard." Crane's voice, for the first time in seven years, was not smooth. It came out cracked, disbelieving, almost wounded — a man discovering his own arithmetic had been stolen from him. "What are you doing?"

"What I should have done at Kestrel Crossing," Halvard said. His voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "Telling the truth about the load."

Grett was already moving, bellowing, half the salvage crew with him, crowbars and rifles and the ugly momentum of men who have been promised a fortune and see it walking away. Halvard did not run. He was too old, and it was too far, and some part of him — the engineer's part, the part that had always known the exact load a structure could bear — had already calculated what he was for.

He put his back against the gate.

They came at him in the narrow corridor where only three could stand abreast, and for a moment — a strange, bright moment — it was almost like the old days, like holding a bridge against a flood, like every fight he had ever picked with the mountain on behalf of the people who crossed it. He got his maul up once. He caught Grett's wrist with the drill steel and heard the man howl. He was aware, distantly, of shouting — August's voice, Sera's, the crack of a rifle that was not aimed at him — and then a rifle that was.

The shot took him high in the chest, from the edge of the crowd, from a man whose face he never saw. It did not hurt, at first. It felt like the ice had finally reached in and touched the place where all his cold had lived.

He went down against the gate, and his weight went into the jam, and the gate held.

* * *

They reached him through the smoke and shouting — August first, dragging him back from the door while Sera and two of Okafor's people formed a line between the salvage crew and the corridor. The salvage men milled, leaderless without Grett's wrist, and above them all the glacier groaned its long iron groan, and somewhere in that sound even the greediest man present heard the arithmetic change.

Crane's voice cut through it: "The breach charge. Bring the breach charge—" and then the ice spoke louder, a crack like the sky splitting, and the first sledge of gold, abandoned in the corridor, slid a foot on the draining water, and the salvage crew's nerve broke into argument, into factions, into the noise of a thing coming apart.

Halvard lay on the ice with August kneeling over him, and the flare light was very blue, and he found that he was cold in the ordinary way at last, the human way, the way a man gets cold when the fire goes out.

"August." His mouth was full of copper. "August, listen. The gold — leave it."

"We can still get you out. Sera knows the crevasse field, there's a line—"

"The gold." He caught August's sleeve with a hand that would not stop shaking, and made himself say it plainly, because he had spent a lifetime saying things that were not plain, and he would not die doing it. "It isn't worth the tunnel. It was never worth anything. Stolen money — every coin of it — stolen from the valleys, and the valleys don't need it back, they need it *gone*. The calving will take it all tonight anyway. Let it. Let the ice keep what the ice was always going to keep."

August's face was very close, and very still. "The manifest—"

"Is the inheritance. Not the gold. You understand? The *record*." Blood at the corner of his mouth; he wiped it with the back of his hand, an old man's gesture, fussy, absurd. "I falsified the Kestrel reports. I did it for Crane. I watched them bury your name for it and I said nothing for seven years. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to *finish* me. Take the ledger and the manifest and the testimony and bury Crane's empire the way I'm going to be buried — under the truth, with the gold, where nobody can spend it."

The glacier cracked again, closer, and a thread of meltwater struck the ice beside Halvard's head and steamed in the flare light.

"Halvard." August's voice was rough. "You locked the gate."

"I locked the gate."

"Seven years," August said, "I built my whole life on the ruin you made. And you spend your last hour holding a door against it."

"I know what I spent." Halvard looked up at him, and his eyes were going dim, but they were clear. "I'm an engineer, boy. I know exactly what everything costs. I just — I paid late."

August's hand closed over his. It was not a soft grip. It was an engineer's grip, and it said: *I have read your numbers, and I find them true.*

"Forgiven," August said. "Not forgotten. Forgiven. Now hold still."

But Halvard could feel the structure of him failing, the way he had felt it in a hundred bridges, that particular settling that no inspection could lie away. He turned his head, the last of his strength going into the turn, and looked down the corridor toward the mouth of the tunnel — where the light of the open sky came in thin and silver, where the sixth tide was draining away, where the horizon did what it had always done.

"The charges," he said. "The old works powder — Ilsa's people brought it. You know the arch. You know where the knee of it is." His breath rattled. "You were always the better engineer, August. I only ever taught you the numbers. You taught yourself the *judgment*."

He gripped August's sleeve once more, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, a secret passed across a desk, across a grave.

"Blow it. Seal it. And when they ask you why — tell them the mountain keeps its own books."

His hand went slack on the ice, and the great door of the glacier groaned above them, and somewhere far off, deep in the white heart of the pass, the calving began.

* * *

August rose. Halvard watched him rise — watched him through a blue and dimming light, watched him turn to Sera and say something short and certain, watched him pull the manifest from his coat and press it into her hands before turning back toward the wreck with the old charges in his arms. The salvage crew was shouting. Crane's voice rose over all of it, ordering, promising, and no one was listening anymore, because the ice was speaking now, and the ice had the only voice left that mattered.

*The mountain keeps its own books,* Halvard thought, and found that he was smiling, and that his last thought was not of gold, or of bridges, or even of the boy he had broken and the boy he had saved, but of a morning forty years ago when he had first driven an engine through this pass, and the snow had come off the peaks in the sun, and he had believed — wrongly, wastefully, but with his whole heart — that the rails he laid would outlive him.

They had not. But the truth would. He had finally laid it true.

The gate of the strongroom held behind him. The glacier opened its white throat. And the sixth tide, the last of them, began to drain.
