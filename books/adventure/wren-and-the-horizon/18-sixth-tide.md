## 18. Sixth Tide

The water began to leave before the light did.

August stood at the tunnel mouth with the tide gauge's brass needle trembling against its stop, watching the sea drain out of the glacier's throat in long, silver ropes. The sixth tide. The last one. Somewhere behind them, the calving face groaned like a ship working loose of its moorings, and the sound carried through the ice the way a scream carries through a wall — muffled, intimate, worse for it.

"Forty minutes," Sera said beside them. She had her watch open, its lid propped against her wrist. "Maybe thirty-five if the drain runs fast."

August didn't answer. They were looking at the tunnel.

It had changed since the first tide, when it had stood open like a cathedral, all vaulted blue and hanging light. Crane's shortcut blast two tides ago had broken something fundamental in it. The ceiling sagged in places where it should have soared; whole slabs hung from threads of older, denser ice, and through those threads the daylight came down in fractured columns that moved when the glacier breathed. The wreck lay four hundred meters in, past the strongroom, past the place where Halvard had died with his hand on the gate lever and his eyes on August's face.

They had buried him at dawn, above the tideline, under a cairn Ilsa's people built from beach stone because the ground was iron-hard and would not take a spade. Ilsa had spoken the old words her grandfather used for men lost on the line. August had stood there with the manifest inside their coat, pressed against their ribs like a second heartbeat, and understood that everything now depended on what they did in the next half hour.

The gold was still in there. Four hundred meters of failing ice away, sealed in a locomotive's strongroom, worth enough to buy every valley from here to the coast twice over — or to corrupt them past saving, which was the same thing in different arithmetic.

And the charges were already set.

Ilsa's volunteers had worked through the night by lamplight, carrying the old blasting powder up from the valley works in oilcloth bundles, packing it into the fracture lines Halvard himself had mapped years ago — the same markers he'd left on the closed line, repurposed now for demolition instead of sabotage. August had walked the placement three times, checking angles, checking depth, checking the run of the fuse line with an engineer's cold attention while their hands shook and would not stop. It was good work. It would hold. That was the terrible part: it was *good* work, and it would do exactly what it was designed to do.

"You don't have to be the one," Sera said quietly.

"Yes, I do." August turned from the tunnel. "Crane's people are coming back. His foreman got word out before the truce broke — you saw the boats. If we leave that tunnel standing, they come back with more powder than we have and better lawyers than any of us will ever afford, and they take it out piece by piece until the valleys owe Crane's estate for the privilege of being robbed." They looked down at the gauge. The needle had begun its long fall. "This is the only tide where we're inside the decision instead of outside it."

Sera held their gaze for a moment. Then she nodded once, and went to check the fuse line's anchor point without another word, because that was the kind of person she was — the kind who showed trust by doing the work.

* * *

The problem, August had told Ilsa the night before, was Crane himself.

The fleet had come back at slack water with forty men and a barge-mounted drill, and Crane had come with them, which he had not done before. He'd stood at the tunnel mouth in a city coat that belonged nowhere within a hundred miles of this ice, and he'd made his offer one final time — pardon, reinstatement, the bridge inquiry closed in August's favor, a salary with zeros enough to bury the debt that had followed them since the failure. All of it in exchange for walking away and letting professional salvors handle what he called *the asset*.

"And if I say no?" August had asked.

Crane had smiled the way a ledger smiles. "Then you're a saboteur destroying government property, Mr. Whitlock, witnessed by forty sworn men, and no magistrate in the territory will ever believe a blacklisted engineer over me. You've spent five years learning what my signature on a report can do. Don't make me write another one."

That was the trap, and August had lain awake inside it most of the night, turning it over. Not the gold — they had stopped grieving the gold somewhere around the third tide. The trap was that Crane was right about the law. Everything August did next would be read through whatever document survived. If August collapsed the tunnel and died inside it, they were a madman. If they collapsed it and lived, they were a criminal — unless the manifest and Halvard's ledger reached the magistrate first, unless Okafor testified, unless the truth arrived ahead of the accusation.

Which meant August had to live. And Crane knew it. Which meant Crane would try to make sure they didn't.

* * *

At twenty minutes to low water, the fleet moved.

No ultimatum this time. No bargain. Just forty men coming up the shingle in loose order with tools and lamps and the particular silence of men who have been paid enough to stop asking questions, and behind them, at the mouth, Aldous Crane himself, following at a distance, because men like him always follow at a distance.

"They're going for the shortcut," Ilsa said. She had posted her people along the ridge with signal flags, and now the flags were talking fast. "If they widen his blast corridor they can reach the strongroom without using our tunnel at all. Twenty minutes' work with that drill."

August ran the numbers and hated every one of them. The shortcut corridor ran parallel to the main tunnel for its final stretch — close enough that a charge placed well could bring both down together, but close enough too that anyone caught in the corridor when it fell would have no way out except forward, toward the wreck, where there was no exit at all.

Anyone caught in the corridor.

August looked at the advancing line of men, and then at the figure in the city coat bringing up the rear, and felt the choice arrive not as an idea but as a weight, physical, in the chest.

"Ilsa," they said. "How certain are your people of the fuse timing?"

"To the second. Why?"

"Because I need everyone out of the blast radius in eighteen minutes," August said, "and I need Crane's men to keep walking."

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and August would spend the rest of their life assembling them.

They let the salvage crew enter. That was the decision, made at the mouth of the tunnel with Sera's hand tight on their sleeve and the tide draining below, the blue light rising as the water fell away and the ice sang its high thin note of stress. They let forty men walk into a cathedral that August had already mined for demolition, and they did it because stopping them meant fighting, and fighting meant losing, and losing meant the gold came out anyway, slower but just as surely, and the valleys paid for it forever.

Instead August gave them the tunnel.

The crew fanned inward, lamps swinging, boots loud on the wet stone shelf beside the drained channel. August watched them go from behind an ice ridge near the entrance, counting heads, counting minutes, listening to Sera call the time in a voice kept flat by effort. Twelve minutes. Ten. The advance party reached the wreck — August could see their lamps clustering around the locomotive's frozen hull, tiny stars trapped in blue glass — and the drill team turned into Crane's shortcut corridor with their equipment, exactly as planned, exactly as feared.

Eight minutes.

Then Crane stepped into the tunnel.

He had waited until his men were committed — of course he had — and now he came up the shelf alone except for two guards, unhurried, a man inspecting his purchase. And August, who had rehearsed a dozen versions of this moment, found that none of them survived contact with the actual sight of him: small against the vast blue vault, coat dark, face lifted to admire the ice his money had cracked open.

August stepped out onto the shelf.

Crane saw them and stopped. For a moment neither spoke, and the glacier filled the silence with its slow interior thunder, and somewhere far above a crack propagated with a sound like a rifle shot.

"You're standing in my tunnel," Crane said.

"It was never yours." August's voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Not the pass, not the line, not the gold. It belonged to the people who built it and the people who lost it, and you've spent your whole life buying things so you'd never have to belong anywhere. This is what that costs."

Crane smiled. Behind him, deep in the blue, the drill team's engine coughed to life. "There it is. The sermon. I've bought three magistrates, Whitlock. Do you imagine I can't buy a narrative? A disgraced engineer destroys a public asset out of spite — it writes itself."

"Not with the manifest in the magistrate's hands by Thursday. Not with Okafor alive to testify. Not with Halvard's ledger." August took a step backward, toward the mouth, toward the light. "You're right that whoever holds the story holds the valleys. So I'm going to make sure the story holds *them*."

Something shifted in Crane's face then — not fear, not yet, but the recalculation of a man realizing the asset was about to become a liability. He glanced back toward his drill team, toward the corridor, and August saw him arrive at the same arithmetic they had: the charges, the timing, the narrowing window.

"You'll die for this," Crane said. "Or hang for it."

"Probably one of the two," August agreed. "But not today, and not here."

And they turned and ran.

* * *

The fuse burned at a rate Ilsa's people had tested three times on the beach, and August had the count in their head like a heartbeat: seven minutes from the moment they lit it, six and a half if the wind funneled cold down the tunnel and slowed it, which it would. They lit it at the mouth junction with Sera already running ahead of them, and then they ran, both of them, along the shelf above the drained channel with the blue walls streaming light and the whole cathedral beginning, very softly, to sing.

Behind them, shouts. Crane's voice cracking into command. The drill engine stalling as men abandoned it. Lamps scattering like startled birds.

August did not look back. Looking back cost speed, and speed was the only currency they had left, and they spent it all — lungs burning, boots slipping on wet stone, Sera's hand catching theirs at the bend where the shelf narrowed, hauling each other through the place where Halvard had locked the gate and taken the bullet for it. August touched the gate frame as they passed. Just briefly. Just once.

Five minutes. The singing in the ice rose to a pitch they felt in their teeth. Above, through the fractured ceiling, the daylight columns began to swing, wide and wider, and fine snow sifted down like flour through a millstone's works.

Four minutes. The mouth was a bright coin ahead, growing. And then — August heard it before they understood it — a new sound underneath the others, deeper, structural, the sound of a very large thing deciding to move. The calving face. The warm water of the last days of the season, the blasts, the weight of forty men's engines and ambitions: the glacier had been keeping its own count all along, and its count had reached zero.

"The face is going!" Sera screamed. "It'll outrun the fuse!"

She was right. August knew it in their knees before they knew it in their head — the tunnel would not wait for the powder. The collapse was coming from the seaward end whether they triggered it or not, and when the calving face went, the shockwave would run the length of the passage and bring everything down in sequence, and Crane and his forty men were still inside.

Three minutes. Two hundred meters.

And here was the moment August would carry longest: not the running, not the cold, but the half-second at the mouth when they turned, finally, and looked back down the blue throat of the tunnel, and saw the lamps of Crane's crew swarming toward the exit like bees whose hive was falling, and saw, far back in the deepening dark, one lamp that did not move. One man standing still on the shelf in a city coat, looking up at the ceiling with his head tilted, as though listening to something only he could hear.

Crane had time to run. August watched him not take it — watched him stand in his own shortcut, in the geometry of his own greed, while the ice above him opened like a book — and August understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that some men cannot leave a fortune behind even to save themselves, and that Crane was not choosing death. He was choosing the strongroom. He was already calculating how much could be carried.

August lit nothing further. There was nothing left to decide. The glacier had taken the decision out of human hands, and all that remained was to survive the consequence.

They came out of the mouth into white light and screaming wind, and Ilsa's people hauled them up the ridge, and behind them the world ended in order: first the powder charges, sharp and close, coughing smoke from the seam lines; then the calving face letting go with a sound like God clearing his throat; then the tunnel, the cathedral, four hundred meters of ten-thousand-year ice folding down upon itself, upon the wreck, upon the strongroom, upon the gold and the man who had wanted it more than air.

The wave took the fleet's barges and threw them ashore like toys. The spray reached the ridge itself, cold as judgment. August lay on the stones with Sera's grip bruising their wrist and watched a berg the size of a church topple slowly, gracefully, into the sea, and felt the concussion come up through the rock into their ribs, into their teeth, into the place where five years of shame had lived.

When the sound finally faded, the pass was gone. Sealed. A smooth white wall from ridge to waterline, featureless, final, already beginning to glitter in the sun as if nothing had ever been inside it at all.

* * *

They counted the living twice, because the first count seemed impossible.

Thirty-one of Crane's men had made it out — stumbling, burned, half-drowned, some weeping, some silent, all of them empty-handed. The drill team had not. Neither had the two guards. Neither had Aldous Crane.

Ilsa's people moved among the survivors without hatred, giving blankets, giving water, because that was who the valley was. One of the salvage men, a boy hardly older than August had been at their first bridge inspection, sat shaking on the shingle and said, over and over, "He wouldn't run. We told him. He just stood there looking at the ice."

August knelt beside him. "I know," they said. "I saw."

"What was he looking at?"

August thought of the tilted head, the listening posture, the man hearing the sum of his life called out in the language of splitting ice. "His own arithmetic," they said. "He ran out of numbers."

Sera sat down beside them as the sun broke through the smoke of the powder charges, and for a long time neither of them spoke. Below, the sea was still settling, bergs drifting in the new-made bay, and the wall of the glacier stood clean and blank and absolute.

"It's done," Sera said at last. "Whatever happens now — the inquiry, the magistrate, Crane's partners — it's done. Nobody gets that gold. Nobody buys those valleys with it."

August nodded. Their hands had finally gone still. Inside their coat, the manifest pressed against their ribs, lighter now than paper had any right to feel — because it was no longer a claim to anything. It was a record. A debt entered honestly in the world's ledger, owed to people who had never been able to collect.

Down the ridge, one of Ilsa's signal flags began to climb the pole — the old pattern, the one her grandfather's stationmen had used to tell the valley that the line was clear.

August looked at it for a long moment, and then at the sealed pass, and then at Sera, and found that the words, when they came, were simple ones.

"We should get the testimony to the magistrate," they said. "Okafor first. Then the ledger. Then—" They stopped. Down the coast road, small with distance, a rider was coming toward them at a gallop, raising dust, and Ilsa's scouts were already signaling it in. "Then whatever comes next."

Sera followed their gaze. "Trouble?"

"No," August said slowly, watching the rider come, watching the flags talk. "I think it's news. I think it might even be good news." They stood, joints aching, salt-stiff, alive. "But we won't know until it arrives — and something tells me the magistrate's office isn't the only place that's been waiting to hear what happened to Aldous Crane."
