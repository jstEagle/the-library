## 1. The Debt Comes Ashore

The last crate came up at 4:17 in the afternoon, and I remember the time because Junie called it out over the radio like a game-show hostess—"Four-seventeen, ladies and gentlemen, and the crowd goes wild"—and because for eleven years I have made a private religion of noting the exact moment a job stops being dangerous and starts being money. It's the only liturgy I keep.

"Wild," said Okafor, flat as ballast stone, from the wheelhouse doorway. He was holding two cups of coffee and wearing the expression of a man who has already done today's arithmetic and found it wanting.

"We're rich," I said.

"We're solvent," he corrected, handing me a cup. "For one more quarter."

The *Wren* sat low in the water off the Sound with forty-one thousand dollars of pre-war brass riding in her hold, recovered from a fishing tender that had gone down in '61 and been left to the crabs ever since. Nobody had claimed it. Nobody had even looked. That was the whole trick of our operation, if you could call three crew and a forty-year-old salvage boat an operation: we went where the charts were wrong or missing, we took what nobody else wanted badly enough to fight us for, and then we disappeared back into the fog like we'd never been there. Small. Quiet. Off the books, mostly, and off the charts entirely.

My mother would have called it cowardice. She'd have called it that loudly, from the deck of some hundred-foot expedition vessel, while signing autographs. I call it survival, and I've never once confused the two.

"Bring her around," I told Okafor. "Slow. Let's not wake anybody up."

"You always say that."

"It's always true."

He took the helm and I went aft to supervise the securing of the cargo, which is captain-speak for standing around while Junie lashed crates and talked enough for both of us. Junie is twenty-three, built like a heron, and dives the way other people breathe—without thinking about it until something goes wrong. She'd spent the whole recovery down at ninety feet on mixed gas, prying brass fittings loose with a crowbar and narrating her findings to me through the comm like a nature documentary.

"You should've seen the eel that lives in the galley," she was saying now, cinching a strap. "Huge. Old. Face like my grandmother's divorce lawyer. I named him Gerald."

"You can't name things you're robbing," I said.

"I didn't rob Gerald. Gerald robbed the boat first. I'm just recovering stolen property." She patted the crate. "Gerald sends his regards, by the way. He bit my glove."

"That's not regards, that's assault."

"In eel culture it's regards."

I let her talk. That's most of what managing Junie consists of—letting her talk—and it works because underneath the chatter she is the best young diver I've ever put in the water, and because talking is how she keeps the cold out. Everybody on this boat keeps the cold out their own way. Okafor does arithmetic. Junie does monologue. I do silence, and I've gotten so good at it that most days I forget there's anything under it.

We made the harbor at dusk, running dark past the fleet anchorage where the corporate tenders sit like office buildings that learned to float. I've spent a decade learning exactly how much light to show and how little, and I'll tell you the secret: none. A boat that shows no lights is either dead or hiding, and harbors are full of people too tired to wonder which.

We tied up at the far end of the public pier, behind the fish cannery, where the pilings are rotten and the rent is paid in cash and nobody writes anything down. Home. Or the closest thing I've got to one, which is a berth where the neighbors mind their business because they're all doing the same thing I am.

That's when I saw the man on the dock.

You learn to read people on a pier the way you read weather. Fishermen walk like the ground owes them money. Tourists walk like the ground might bite. Dockworkers walk like the ground is a coworker they tolerate. This man walked like the ground had signed something and he was here to enforce the terms. Gray suit, gray case, gray face, shoes too clean for the boards. He stood at the head of our slip with his hands folded on his briefcase, patient as a lien.

"Company?" Junie asked quietly, all the chatter gone out of her.

"Not ours," I said.

Okafor had come down from the wheelhouse without being called. He does that—appears at the first sign of weather. "Could be insurance," he offered. "About the tender job."

"The tender job doesn't exist."

"Then maybe he's lost."

"He's not lost." I stepped onto the dock. "Nobody lost wears shoes like that."

I walked up the pier with my hands loose and my face set to neutral, the way I'd practiced in ten years of harbors, and stopped six feet from him, which is close enough to talk and far enough to move.

"Can I help you?"

"Captain Nakamura." Not a question. He opened the briefcase. "My name is Aldous Wren—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't tell me your name is Wren. That's a coincidence somebody wrote on purpose."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. The first honest thing about him. "Aldous Prentiss, then. Of Prentiss, Hale & Marsh. I represent certain financial interests." He produced a folder, thick, tabbed, the kind of document that arrives with its own gravity. "Do you know a Vesper Kaine?"

The name landed in my chest like a hook set deep. I kept my face still. Ten years of practice.

"No," I said.

"Mm." He looked at me the way auditors look at receipts. "Then I'll summarize. In 2019, this vessel—the *Wren*, hull number 4417—was used as collateral against a working loan from Pacific Meridian Credit Union. The loan was refinanced twice, consolidated once, and sold in a portfolio package eight months ago. The current holder of that paper is Ms. Kaine's company. She has instructed me to extend you a courtesy before formal proceedings begin."

"Formal proceedings."

"Repossession, Captain. Of the boat." He said it gently, the way doctors say *tumor*. "And, I'm afraid, of everything secured to it. Which, per the collateral schedule, includes your dive equipment, your cargo manifest rights, and"—he turned a page, savoring it or suffering through it, hard to tell—"your mooring agreement at this facility, which Ms. Kaine has also acquired."

The cannery hummed behind us. Somewhere across the harbor a party boat played music too cheerful for the hour. I stood very still and did the math Okafor hadn't gotten to yet: no boat, no gear, no berth. Eleven years of careful invisibility, foreclosed in a folder.

"There's a balance," I said. "What is it?"

"Two hundred and sixty thousand. With fees and accrued interest, call it three-forty." He closed the folder halfway, a salesman's gesture, leaving the door open just enough. "But Ms. Kaine isn't interested in your money, Captain. If she were, she'd have sent a collection agency and saved my fee. She's interested in a conversation. One job. She's prepared to be generous."

"I don't take jobs."

"So I've heard. That's rather the point of you, isn't it? The ghost of the Sound. Never registered, never chartered, never photographed." He smiled thinly. "You should know that anonymity is a commodity now. There are people who buy it and people who sell it. Ms. Kaine bought yours eight months ago and has been very patient."

Behind me I heard Junie's boots on the dock, and Okafor's hand closing on her shoulder before she got close enough to hear anything actionable. Good. Whatever this was, it wasn't crew business yet. It wasn't anything yet.

"Tell Ms. Kaine," I said, "that I don't know her, I don't owe her, and I don't take meetings."

"I'll pass that along verbatim." Prentiss tucked the folder under his arm. "Though I should mention—one courtesy deserves another. Your mother was Elena Nakamura."

It wasn't a question either. That's how I knew how long he'd been sitting on it, saving it, the way you save the good knife for last.

"What about her," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost me.

"Nothing, Captain. Only that Ms. Kaine asked me to say the word *Meridian* to you, and to note whether you flinched." He adjusted his cuffs. "For the record: you didn't. Very impressive. She'll want to see you Thursday. The address is inside. If you don't come, proceedings begin Friday morning, and I'm told the fleet tenders make excellent repossession vessels—they have cranes."

He nodded, once, like a man concluding a funeral, and walked back up the pier with his clean shoes and his gray case, and I watched him go and realized my hands were shaking around the coffee cup, which had gone cold without my noticing.

*Meridian.*

One word, and ten years of careful silence cracked straight down the middle like lake ice in spring.

* * *

We didn't talk on the way back to the boat. Junie watched me the whole way, chewing the inside of her cheek, which is what she does instead of asking questions. Okafor walked behind me, and I could feel him doing arithmetic again—not money this time. Probabilities.

In the galley I put the folder on the table between the coffee mugs and the salt-stained chart of the Sound, and I didn't open it, and neither of them said anything for a long moment, which on my boat is a kind of applause.

Finally Junie couldn't stand it. "Who's Vesper Kaine?"

"Nobody."

"Nobody with a law firm and a folder."

"Everybody with a law firm has a folder. It's how you tell them from fishermen."

"And the Meridian?" Okafor asked. Quiet. Careful. He knows better than anyone alive what questions cost me, and he asks them anyway, because that's the job I gave him: the part of myself I can delegate.

I looked at the folder. My mother's name was in it somewhere—I could feel it, the way you feel weather coming in a healed fracture. Elena Nakamura died eleven years ago chasing a wreck sealed in a tidewater glacier up north, a wreck the corporate fleets have whispered about ever since, a wreck I have spent a decade pretending I never heard of, on charts I never look at, in a sea I never sail. I built my whole quiet life on top of that grave, and a man in clean shoes had just said its name out loud on a public pier.

"She's my mother's oldest rival," I said. Because lying to Okafor has never once worked, and I'm too tired tonight to try.

Junie's eyes went wide. "The rival? From the stories? The one who—"

"Yes."

"But your mom's been dead—"

"Eleven years. Yes." I finally opened the folder. Contracts, schedules, a photograph of the *Wren* taken from the water by someone who'd known exactly where to wait. And on top, a single card, heavy stock, handwritten in a slanted, impatient script I recognized from thirty-year-old expedition posters in my mother's old cabin:

*Thursday. Come alone. Bring nothing. —V.K.*

Underneath, in Prentiss's neat print, a line that hadn't been spoken aloud on the dock:

*Ms. Kaine wishes you to know she has been waiting for you to surface for quite some time. She says you are the only person alive who knows the approach to the Meridian—and she intends to find out why.*

I set the card down. Outside, the tide was turning, the *Wren* shifting gently against her lines, pulling at the ropes like she already knew we weren't going to like what came next.

"Captain," Okafor said carefully, "what's the Meridian?"

I looked at the chart on the wall—the safe one, the local one, the one with nothing on it but the waters I permit myself—and thought about the other charts, locked in the cabin below, in my mother's handwriting, in mine, in a route I swore I'd forgotten.

"A debt," I said. "Apparently we've owed it for a long time."
