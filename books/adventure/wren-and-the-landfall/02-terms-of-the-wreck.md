## 2. Terms of the Wreck

The lawyer's name was Pellham, and he had the kind of face that looked like it had been ironed. Not pressed—ironed, flat and deliberate, with a crease down the middle where his conscience might have been if he'd ever bothered to grow one. He sat in my galley with his knees together and his briefcase squared on his thighs like a small obedient dog, and he watched me pour coffee into two mugs with the expression of a man cataloguing evidence.

"Take it black," I said. "The milk's been on this boat longer than most of my crew."

"I don't take coffee," he said, and then, as if realizing that refusing hospitality was bad strategy, added, "but thank you."

That was my first lesson about Vesper Kaine: she sent people who were almost polite. People who'd been trained to be courteous right up until the moment courtesy stopped being useful. It was like being threatened by someone reading from a script about how much they respected you.

I sat down across from him. The *Magpie* rocked gently at her mooring off Kessler Point, and through the galley window I could see Okafor on deck, coiling line with the slow, deliberate movements of a man pretending not to listen through the hull. He wasn't fooling anyone. He never fooled anyone. That was both his best quality and, on certain mornings, the thing I wanted to throw overboard.

"So," I said. "You bought our debt."

"Ms. Kaine acquired the paper, yes." He opened the briefcase. "Along with the vessel lien. The *Magpie* is listed as collateral."

"She's not collateral. She's a person with an engine problem."

Pellham looked at me the way you look at a dog that's barking at the mail slot. "Ms. Nakamura. You owe Harrow & Finch one hundred and forty-two thousand dollars. The loan has been sold twice. Ms. Kaine purchased it at discount, which means—" he paused, savoring the arithmetic, "—she paid less than you owe, and can now demand all of it. Or the boat."

I drank my terrible coffee and let the silence do some work for me. Outside, a gull screamed something editorial about the state of the world. Ten years. Ten years of running quiet jobs, staying off the charts, keeping the *Magpie*'s name out of registries and her transponder honest only when honesty was cheap. Ten years of building a life so small and so dull that no one would ever bother to look at it. And some woman I'd met exactly once, at a funeral where we'd stood on opposite sides of the grave like rival generals surveying a battlefield neither of us had won, had reached into her checkbook and picked the whole thing up by the scruff of its neck.

"What does she want?" I asked.

Pellham smiled. It was a professional smile, deployed at intervals, like a flare. "She'd prefer to tell you herself."

"Then she should have come."

"She doesn't travel for conversations." He slid a card across the table. Heavy stock. Embossed. The kind of card you could use to cut glass or defend yourself against a mugger. "But she did record something. In case you were reluctant."

He produced a tablet, laid it flat between us, and tapped the screen.

Vesper Kaine's face filled it. She was older than the last time I'd seen her—of course she was; grief ages everyone, even when it isn't your grief—and her hair had gone the color of old silver plate. But the eyes hadn't changed. They were still the eyes of a woman who had once, according to my mother, bet a whole season's salvage on a coin flip and won, and then told everyone it hadn't been luck.

"Beatrix," the recording said, and hearing my name in that voice was like stepping on ice and hearing it crack under somebody else. "I know you won't take my calls. I know you've spent ten years making yourself boring, and honestly, congratulations—it almost worked. But here's what almost worked gets you: a debt you can't pay and a boat I can take. So let's skip the part where you tell Mr. Pellham no, because I already know you will, and I've budgeted for it."

On screen, Kaine leaned closer, and the camera caught the fine network of lines around her eyes, the particular set of her mouth that meant she was enjoying herself and hated that she was enjoying herself.

"One job," she said. "One wreck. You bring back what's in her hold, and the debt dies. All of it. The boat, the crew's wages, the interest Harrow & Finch has been bleeding you for since before your mother died. Gone. One dive."

My hands had gone cold around the mug. I kept my face doing nothing, which is a skill, and I recommend practicing it, because there will come a day in every life when a dead face is worth more than a live one.

"The Meridian," the recording said.

There it was.

"You know the coordinates. Your mother certainly did. She died eleven miles short of them, in water so cold the coroner wrote 'instantaneous' on the report like it was a mercy instead of a fact. I'm offering you the chance to finish her run. And I'm telling you—if you say no, I'll send the fleet anyway, and I'll take the boat, and you'll watch from shore. Think about which version of this story you want to be standing in."

The screen went dark. Pellham folded his hands.

"Well," I said.

"Ms. Kaine anticipated a strong initial reaction."

"My initial reaction is that she can swim to the Meridian herself."

"That was also anticipated." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. There was a folder in the briefcase, thick as a brick, and he patted it once. "This contains the full terms. Salvage shares, insurance riders, liability waivers. It's generous, Ms. Nakamura. Suspiciously generous. I'd encourage you to read it closely, because generosity from Vesper Kaine is never a gift. It's a hook."

"At least you're honest."

"I'm paid to be accurate. Honesty costs extra." He stood, buttoned his jacket, left the coffee untouched like a small insult. "You have seventy-two hours. After that, the offer stands but the terms change. Ms. Kaine is very clear about tides. She believes everything in life is tide-dependent." At the companionway he paused, and for one unguarded second the ironed face creased. "For what it's worth—I read the file. On your mother. I'm sorry."

Then he was gone, down the ladder, into the tender, away across the gray water toward the white boats and clean money of the other side of the Sound.

* * *

Okafor came below before I'd finished my coffee. He didn't ask. He just took the second mug, poured himself the dregs, and sat down in the seat still warm from a lawyer's trousers, which I felt was symbolic of something but didn't have the energy to name.

"Well?" he said.

"You heard."

"I heard a tablet. Tablets are quiet. I heard you not talking, which is louder." He wrapped both hands around the mug. Okafor is a big man built like a load-bearing wall, with a shaved head going silver at the temples and a way of looking at you that makes lying feel like a waste of both your time. "Say it out loud anyway."

"It's the Meridian."

He closed his eyes briefly. "Of course it is."

"She wants the hold. Whatever's in it. And she wants me to be the one who opens it, because apparently watching me finish my mother's death run is worth a hundred and forty-two thousand dollars to her."

"And if you say no?"

"She takes the boat."

Okafor nodded slowly, the way he nods when he's already done the math and is just waiting for you to catch up. "And if you say yes, you're diving a glacial wreck in calving season, on a route that killed the best captain either of us ever served under."

"When you put it that way it sounds bad."

"It sounds bad every way, Bea. That's the problem. It's bad in every direction. That's not a choice, that's a vice." He drank. Set the mug down with the care of a man who needed something to do with his hands. "What aren't you saying?"

I looked out the galley window. The gull had found a friend and they were screaming at each other now, which felt about right for the week I was having.

"There's a reason my mother was chasing it," I said. "A real reason. Not salvage math. Something she knew. Something she—" I stopped. Started again. "She left me charts. Her survey charts, the old ones, hand-annotated. I've never gone through them properly. Couldn't. And now Kaine's holding a gun made of my own boat to my head and asking me to follow my mother's last route, and I don't know whether that's a trap or an inheritance, and I hate—not knowing."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone gentle, which from him was more alarming than shouting. "You could refuse. We could sell the *Magpie*, pay off half, start over with a skiff and a grudge."

"We'd lose Junie. And Tess. And the Reyes twins would go back to cannery work, and you'd—" I shook my head. "No. I'm not selling these people's livelihoods to make a point."

"No," he agreed. "You're not."

"So I refuse. We find another way. We always find another way."

"Sure," Okafor said, and his voice was so carefully neutral that I should have heard the warning in it, should have recognized the sound of a man agreeing with you while reaching for a pen. But I was tired, and the coffee was bad, and grief had come up from below decks where it usually lived and was walking around my chest in heavy boots. "We'll figure it out," he said. "Get some sleep, Captain. Seventy-two hours is a long time."

It was not a long time. It was the shortest time I have ever been given. But I believed him, because I always believe him, because ten years of trust doesn't dissolve in one evening no matter what the water temperature.

I slept. That's the part that still gets me. I actually slept.

* * *

I found out at dawn, the way you find out about most betrayals—too late, and in daylight, which is the crueler lighting.

Tess woke me, banging on my cabin door with none of her usual ceremony. "Bea. Bea, get up. Okafor's on the dock. He's—" She couldn't finish it, which from Tess, who narrates disasters for sport, told me everything.

I came up on deck barefoot with my jacket over my pajamas, dignity being a luxury item and out of stock. Down on the fuel dock, in the flat pink light of early morning, Okafor stood at Pellham's elbow signing a document on a clipboard. Pellham held the tablet up—recording the signature, of course, everything recorded, everything witnessed, everything ironed flat—and Okafor signed his name in his big careful block letters, and then handed the pen back like he was returning something borrowed.

The crew lined the rail above him. Nobody spoke. The Reyes twins stood shoulder to shoulder. Junie had both fists wrapped in the lifeline, knuckles white, watching Okafor with an expression I couldn't read yet and would spend weeks learning to read: not anger. Relief. That was the worst part. She was relieved. They all were, a little, and they were ashamed of being relieved, and the shame was already curdling into something that would need years to burn off.

I went down the ladder. My feet were freezing on the dock boards and I didn't care.

"Captain," Okafor said.

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't 'captain' me. What did you sign?"

"Terms of engagement. Kaine's contract. Debt forgiveness contingent on recovery of Meridian cargo, salvage shares per schedule C, liability waivers per schedules D through G." He recited it flatly, like a man reading his own autopsy. "Crew wages guaranteed regardless of outcome. That clause was mine. I made him add it."

"You made him—" My voice cracked upward and I hauled it back down. "Okafor. It was my decision."

"It was your decision to refuse. Alone. For all of us." He didn't flinch. He never flinches; it's infuriating; I'd follow him into a burning building partly because of it. "You've decided alone for ten years, Bea. Quiet jobs, small risks, keep everyone invisible. And it worked, mostly, except it didn't, because invisible just means nobody sees the knife coming. I watched you sit in that galley last night and choose your mother's ghost over six paychecks, and you didn't even know you were doing it. So yeah. I signed. Because somebody on this boat has to protect the living."

The dock was very quiet. Somewhere behind me Junie made a small sound and stopped it.

"You don't get to dress this up as loyalty," I said.

"No," Okafor said. "I don't suppose I do." He looked at me then, straight on, and there was something in his face underneath the stubbornness—something that looked, God help me, like apology wearing armor. "But I'd do it again. That's the part you should be angry about. Not that I betrayed you. That I'd do it again tomorrow."

Pellham cleared his throat, ironed face freshly pressed for the occasion. "Ms. Nakamura. The contract requires the captain's countersignature within forty-eight hours. Ms. Kaine asked me to convey that she expects you'll see reason." A pause. "Her word. Reason."

I stared at the clipboard. At the signature block with my name printed under it in tidy type, waiting, patient as a trap. Behind me my crew stood along the rail of the only home I had, silent, split down the middle like a hull on rocks, and every one of them was waiting to see which way their captain would jump.

Kaine had known. Of course she'd known. She hadn't sent the contract for me. She'd sent it for Okafor, because she'd studied my mother for thirty years and knew exactly how crews break—not from outside, but from inside, from love wearing the mask of duty. Betrayal dressed as loyalty, loyalty dressed as betrayal, and the tide going out on all of it.

I took the pen.

Not because I forgave him. Not because Kaine had won. But because the *Magpie* was collateral, the crew was committed, and the Meridian was waiting at the bottom of a glacier with my mother's secrets in its hold—and whatever was true about that night eleven years ago, I was going to be the one who found it. Not Vesper Kaine. Not the fleet. Me.

I signed. Pellham collected the clipboard like a man pocketing a soul, nodded once, and was gone.

Okafor and I stood on the dock in the cold morning light, not looking at each other, the contract between us like a third person neither of us would acknowledge.

"Six tides," he said finally, quietly. "Before calving season closes the route. I ran the numbers last night. We have six."

Above us, the gulls went up off the rigging all at once, screaming, like the sea itself had read the terms and found them acceptable.

Six tides. I turned and walked back toward my ship.
