## 3. Six Tides

I keep the ship's accounts the way I keep my hands: clean, calloused in the right places, and never showing anyone the whole picture. It's a discipline I learned from Beatrix's mother, back when I was nineteen and thought a ledger was just numbers. Elena Nakamura taught me that a ledger is a confession you write in advance. Every line item is a promise you're making to your future self about who you'll have been.

So when I sat down at the galley table the night after Kaine's lawyer left, with the tide tables for the Meridian fjord spread out next to the fuel receipts and the wage sheet and the debt notice with its wax-seal weight of finality, I already knew what the confession was going to say.

I did the math anyway. That's the job. You do the math even when you know it's going to look at you like something drowned.

* * *

The *Pelican's Rest* has a galley built for four people to hate each other comfortably, which is exactly what we were doing by 2100 hours. Beatrix had spent the day in her cabin not talking to anyone, which was its own kind of weather system, low pressure and getting worse. Junie had spent the day trying to get anyone to talk to her about anything else. Reyes had spent the day doing engine work that didn't need doing, which was how he prayed. And I'd spent the day on the radio, in the careful, boring voice I use when I want information without anyone remembering they gave it to me.

What I came back with: three harbors reporting corporate tenders taking on glacier-grade equipment. Ice anchors. Thermal dive suits. A hydrographic survey launch chartered out of Cordova under a name that traced back, through two shell companies and a very tired maritime lawyer, to Kaine Consolidated Salvage.

"They're provisioning," I said, and put the printouts on the table like a man laying down cards he wishes were someone else's.

Junie picked one up. She reads fast, Junie—faster than she lets on, because reading fast doesn't fit the image she's built of herself as the young one, the green one, the one everyone gets to explain things to. "That's a lot of money for a wreck nobody's confirmed."

"Kaine doesn't spend money on things she hasn't confirmed." I pulled the tide tables toward me. "Which brings us to the part where I ruin everyone's evening."

Reyes wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last season. "You always ruin my evening. It's tradition."

"The Meridian sits inside the tidewater face of the Serrano Glacier. The approach channel opens twice a day on the flood, closes on the ebb, and right now—" I tapped the table, "—right now it's open wide enough to take a boat our size through, barely, if the ice behaves. Calving season starts in earnest in about nine days. When it starts, the channel fills with bergy bits and growlers, and for maybe two weeks it's suicide. Then the calving front stabilizes, the brash clears, and you get a second window."

"How long?" Junie asked.

"Three days, maybe four, before winter consolidation shuts the whole thing until spring." I looked around the table. "So. Six usable tides. Six windows where we can get in, work the wreck, and get out. After that, the glacier eats the calendar."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The diesel heater ticked. Somewhere aft, the bilge pump cycled, that sound every boat makes that means *I'm still here, are you?*

"Six tides," Reyes said finally. "To locate, dive, and strip a wreck sealed in ice, against a corporate fleet with sonar mapping and thermal suits and probably a submarine, because why wouldn't they have a submarine."

"They don't have a submarine," I said. "I checked. They have something worse. They have the ex-diver."

The name landed the way it always lands, like a tool dropped on a steel deck. Junie flinched. She'd only been aboard two years; she knew the story secondhand, the way you know a scar you didn't earn. Tomas Vey. Four years with us, best cold-water diver I've ever seen, and then whatever happened off Kodiak that made Beatrix put him ashore at gunpoint—metaphorical gunpoint, mostly—and strike him from the crew book so thoroughly that we don't say his name above decks.

"He's with them?" Junie asked quietly.

"He's consulting for them. Harbor gossip says he walked onto their tender with charts already drawn." I let that sit. "He knows these waters better than any of us except Beatrix. Maybe including Beatrix."

Beatrix chose that moment to come out of her cabin, which told me she'd been listening through the door, which told me nothing new. She poured coffee she didn't drink and stood at the end of the table with the particular stillness she gets when she's deciding whether to be captain or survivor.

"You've all seen the papers," she said. "The boat's collateral. Kaine holds the debt. If we don't take her job, she takes the *Rest*, and we're all looking for berths on boats run by people who aren't me." Her voice was flat, professional, the voice she uses to make bad news sound like logistics. "I'm telling you now, so there's no confusion later: I would rather lose this boat than go to the Meridian. I've told Kaine the same. She didn't care."

"Then why are we having a vote?" Reyes asked.

"Because it's not my boat anymore. Not entirely. It hasn't been since the lawyer handed over those papers." She set the untouched coffee down. "It's collateral, and collateral belongs to whoever holds the paper, and the paper says the decision isn't mine alone. So we vote. All of us. And whatever we decide, I'll live with it, and I'll expect the same from the rest of you."

I watched her say it and I thought: *that's Elena's trick too.* Make the hard thing sound like procedure. Give people a choice so the choice can be held against them later, gently, or forgiven later, gently. The Nakamuras don't manipulate, exactly. They architect.

"All right," I said, and uncapped the pen. "Let's lay it out honest, because that's what the ledger's for."

* * *

Here's what the ledger said, and I read it aloud because I promised myself years ago I'd never be the first mate who hid the numbers:

Assets: the *Pelican's Rest*, worth maybe eighty thousand on a good day to a buyer who wasn't looking too close. Our gear, our reputation, and eleven months of quiet profitable recoveries that kept us fed and invisible. Wages owed: six weeks each, current. Cash aboard: enough for fuel to the fjord and back, or provisions, not both without cutting into the reserve.

Liabilities: the debt. Two hundred forty thousand, principal and accrued interest, sold twice, bought once, now sitting in Kaine's portfolio like a hook waiting for a fish. Terms of forgiveness: one recovery job, the Meridian, salvage rights shared per Kaine's contract—which, I'd read it four times, gave Kaine sixty percent of declared value and one hundred percent of anything classified as documents or personal effects, pending "review."

That last clause was the trapdoor. I flagged it. Beatrix went very still when I read it aloud, and I saw her decide not to say what she was thinking, and I decided not to ask, because that's also part of the architecture, hers and mine both.

Risks: calving season. A rival fleet with better equipment and our ex-diver. A wreck site with a fatality history—Elena's—and no confirmed structural survey. And the unquantifiable line item I wrote down anyway, because a ledger that lies to itself is worthless: *crew cohesion, status uncertain.*

"Six tides," I said. "If we go, we go tomorrow's flood. We work the site on the five tides after that. We're out before the ninth day, calving or no calving, found or not found. That's the envelope. Anything outside the envelope kills us."

"And if we don't go?" Junie asked.

"We lose the boat. Kaine calls the note, takes the *Rest* at auction, and hires a new crew at market rates, probably including some of us, because she's not cruel, she's efficient, and efficiency looks a lot like mercy from far away." I clicked the pen closed. "Those are the options. Vote's simple. Yes or no. Majority rules, and Beatrix's vote counts double only if she needs it to break a tie, which I'm putting in writing now so nobody argues later."

"That's not how votes usually work," Reyes said.

"Nothing about this is usual. Vote."

* * *

Here's how it broke, and I'll tell it straight because someday somebody's going to ask me what happened that night in the galley, and I'd rather they hear it from me than from the version that gets polished in the retelling.

Reyes voted yes. Not because he wanted the Meridian—he crossed himself when the name came up, an old habit from a grandmother I never met—but because he's fifty-eight and his pension is the boat and his hands, and losing both to a corporate auction was a math problem he couldn't survive. He said yes the way men say yes to surgery. Eyes shut. Trusting the knife.

Junie voted yes, and hers was the loudest yes at the table, and I understood that too. She's twenty-three. She signed on to a salvage crew because she wanted the water and the deep places and the story of herself doing something that mattered. The Meridian was the biggest story on the coast. I could see her building the telling of it even as she voted—the version she'd give in some harbor bar in ten years, *I was there, we beat the fleet, one tide to spare.* Youth votes for stories. Sometimes the stories kill them. Mostly they don't. You roll those dice when you're young because you haven't yet learned the dice are loaded.

I voted yes. I want to tell you it was for noble reasons. It wasn't, entirely. I voted yes because I ran the numbers three times and the numbers said the debt was unwinnable any other way, and because I've been with the Nakamura family for twenty-two years and I watched Elena die chasing this same wreck, and there is a part of me—old, stubborn, unsentimental in every way except this one—that needs to know what's down there. What she was chasing. What killed her. Ledgers confess things, but so do wrecks.

And Beatrix voted no.

She voted no with her whole chest, flat and clear, no performance in it. "No. For the record, and for the log: no." And then she looked at the tally—three yes, one no—and something went out of her face, some small light behind the eyes that I recognized because I'd seen it go out of Elena's face once, in almost the same room, over almost the same question.

"It's decided," Beatrix said. "We sail on the morning flood."

"Bea—" Junie started.

"Provisioning list is on the clipboard. Okafor has the watch schedule. We're burning the reserve for food, so it's rice and canned everything, and nobody complains to me about it." She picked up her coffee, still untouched, and carried it back to her cabin, and the door closed soft, which was somehow worse than a slam.

* * *

Afterward, Reyes and Junie went forward to start the lists, and I sat alone in the galley with the ledger open, doing the thing I do at the end of hard nights, which is writing the true entry underneath the official one.

Official entry: *Crew voted 3–1 to accept Kaine contract. Departure scheduled 0640 flood.*

True entry: *Captain voted against her own survival and got outvoted by people who love her. Watch what that does to her. Watch what it does to all of us.*

I've served under captains who got drunk on authority and captains who got drunk on grief, and the second kind are more dangerous, but they're also more honest, eventually, if they live long enough. Beatrix has been running from the Meridian for ten years the way you run from a house fire—fast, forward, never looking back, because looking back is how you learn the fire followed you. Now the vote has turned her around. Now she has to look.

I closed the ledger and went up on deck. The harbor was doing its nighttime business, lights doubled in the black water, and out past the breakwater the Sound breathed its big slow breaths. Somewhere south, a corporate tender was loading thermal suits and ice anchors and a man named Tomas Vey who used to eat dinner at this table.

Six tides. One window. A glacier that keeps its own books and forgives nothing.

I stood my watch and I did the math one more time, the way you touch a bruise to check it's still there, and the answer hadn't changed: it was going to be close, and close, on the water, is another word for dead.

At 0640 the flood tide turned, and Beatrix brought the *Pelican's Rest* out of the harbor without a word to anyone, and I noticed she'd taken her mother's old survey charts up to the wheelhouse—taken them out of the locked drawer where they'd sat for ten years, taken them out and spread them open on the chart table like a wound she'd finally agreed to look at.

I didn't ask. But I marked it in the log, because that's the job.

Some entries you write in advance. Some you write because you already know.
