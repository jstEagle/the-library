## 4. What Mother Left in the Charts

The night after Okafor signed, I did what any reasonable person does when their life gets foreclosed out from under them: I cleaned.

Not the boat. The boat was fine. The boat was the one thing in my existence that had never betrayed me. I cleaned the chart room instead—the little cabin aft of the wheelhouse where my mother's survey charts lived in flat steel drawers like specimens in a morgue, which, given how she'd died, felt about right.

I told myself it was preparation. Six tides, Kaine's lawyers had said, and the fleet already provisioning, and if we were going to chase a wreck inside a tidewater glacier we'd better know the approach better than the people who owned satellites. That was true. It was also a lie I was telling myself so I wouldn't have to admit what I was actually doing, which was looking for my mother in the only place she'd ever stayed put.

Maren Nakamura surveyed the Sound for thirty-one years. Not for the corporations—they'd have paid her a fortune and she'd have laughed at them—but for harbormasters, for insurance men, for fishermen who wanted to know where the rocks were before the rocks found out where they were. Her charts were famous in a small way, the way certain things are famous among people who need them: precise, unglamorous, annotated in her narrow architectural hand with soundings and hazards and the occasional dry observation in the margin. *Bottom fouls here. Anchor drags in a northwester. Do not trust the green buoy; it lies.*

She'd been dead ten years and her handwriting still bossed me around. I found that comforting, mostly.

I worked through the drawers by lamplight while the *Kestrel* ticked and settled at her mooring, Junie's laughter going faint from the galley where Okafor was teaching her to lose at cards. The early charts first—shallow water work, harbor approaches, the boring cartography of staying alive. Then the middle years, when her commissions got stranger and her margins got quieter. Then the last drawer, the one I always left for last because it held the charts from the final three years of her life, the years when she'd started asking questions about the Meridian and stopped coming home on schedule.

That was when I found the route.

It wasn't drawn on any official survey. It was penciled onto the back of a 1987 bathymetric sheet of the northern fjords—a working copy, soft paper, the kind she used for drafts—and it ran from the open Sound up through the ice fields to the face of the glacier itself, marked in her hand with depths and turning points and, at the end, a single word: *GATE.*

I sat down on the deck plate with the chart across my knees and read it three times. A route through the ice-choked approach to the Meridian. A route nobody had ever published, because nobody had ever found the wreck and come back to say so. My mother had found it. Of course she had. She'd spent the last years of her life hunting that ship, and Maren Nakamura did not hunt things without mapping the way in.

But that wasn't the part that made my hands go cold.

The part that made my hands go cold was the second hand on the page. Smaller than hers. Rounder. The letters leaning slightly left, the way mine do, the way they've done since I learned to write. A child's careful capitals spelling out a note beside the third turning point:

*DON'T TRUST THE GREEN ICE.*

And below the gate itself, in the same round childish letters, a name:

*ASK VESPER.*

I don't remember writing that.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else in this story I can account for. I remember being eight years old and seasick in the wheelhouse. I remember learning knots at six and sounding line at nine. I remember my mother teaching me to read charts the way other mothers teach bedtime stories—"tell me what the water's doing, Bea, tell me what it's hiding"—and I remember the summer she took me north, the one summer, the only summer, when the *Kestrel* smelled of glue and drafting film and I got to hold the pencil while she called out numbers.

I remember that summer. I remember it in pieces, the way you remember anything from when you were eight: heat and light and my mother's voice, but no sequence, no shape. What I do not remember—not at all, not in any fragment—is ever seeing this chart. And yet there was my handwriting on it, older than my handwriting now, younger, the fat round letters of a kid who pressed too hard because she thought important things deserved deep marks.

*Don't trust the green ice.*

Had she said that to me? Had I written it down the way kids do, transcribing the gospel of a parent without understanding a word of it? Green ice is rotten ice, every northerner knows that—it means the berg has rolled, or the old floe is candled through, ready to fall apart under a gull's weight. It would be exactly the kind of thing she'd say. Exactly the kind of thing I'd write down and then forget, because children forget everything except what they feel, and what I felt that summer was mostly that my mother loved me and the water was very big.

But *ask Vesper.* That wasn't navigation. That wasn't a child parroting safety talk. That was a name, and not just any name—Vesper Kaine, the woman who now owned our debt, our boat, and, as of yesterday morning, the next six tides of my life. My mother's oldest rival. The woman whose name was never spoken in our house without a particular tone, a tone I learned to reproduce before I learned long division.

Why would an eight-year-old write *ask Vesper* on a secret route to a sealed wreck?

Unless the eight-year-old hadn't been the one who decided to.

I turned the chart over. The front was the official survey, all clean lines and printed depth numbers, nothing personal. But along the bottom edge, in my mother's hand, half worn away, was a date: June of the year I turned eight. The summer. Our summer. She'd been working this exact sheet the season she took me north.

So the picture assembled itself, piece by piece, whether I wanted it or not: my mother drafting a route to the Meridian at the same drafting table where her daughter sat holding pencils. Me, copying down fragments of her talk the way kids copy down curses and prayers without knowing the difference. Her letting me. Or maybe not letting me—maybe not even noticing, because Maren Nakamura could get so far inside a chart that the room could burn down around her.

And then she'd folded the route into the back of a drawer and gone out one November night after a bigger, better version of it, and the sea had kept her.

I should have stopped there. Any sensible person would have stopped there, poured a drink, and gone to bed with the ordinary grief of an orphan who'd found her mother's fingerprints on an old map. Instead I did what I always do with my mother's things, which is keep digging, because somewhere under ten years of missing her I still believed that if I dug deep enough I'd find the instruction manual. The part where she tells me what to do.

The next drawer down held her field notebooks—dozens of them, oilcloth-wrapped, dated in order. I pulled the ones from her last year and read until the lamp burned low. Most of it was routine: soundings, weather, fuel logs, complaints about a leaking skiff. But in the last notebook, the last quarter of it, the entries changed. Shorter. Coded in her private shorthand, the one she'd taught me and no one else—a squiggle for fathom, a circle for bearing, a triangle for danger.

I decoded slowly, my lips moving like a schoolboy's. *Approach confirmed. Gate holds at low water. Ice green past the second turn—rotten, unstable, seasonal.* And then, on the final filled page, four days before she died:

*Saw her lights on the water again. She knows I'm close. If I don't come back, the girl will find this. Tell her the route is true but the reason is not what anyone thinks. Tell her—*

The entry stopped. The next page was blank. The one after that held a single line, pressed hard enough to dent three pages beneath it:

*MERIDIAN WAS SUNK ON PURPOSE.*

I sat in the chart room for a long time after that, listening to the *Kestrel* breathe against her lines, listening to Junie laugh at something Okafor said, feeling the whole quiet architecture of my life shift a few degrees off true. My mother hadn't died chasing treasure. She'd died chasing an answer, and the answer had a name in it, and the name was on the back of a chart in my own eight-year-old handwriting, and I couldn't remember putting it there.

*Ask Vesper.*

Tomorrow I'd have to stand on the deck of my own boat and lead my crew toward a glacier, following a route my mother drew and a child annotated, owing my freedom to the woman both notes pointed at. Tomorrow I'd have to decide whether to tell Okafor about any of this, and how much, and whether loyalty meant sharing the load or carrying it so the others didn't have to feel its weight.

Tonight, though, tonight I did one small thing. I took a photograph of the chart with my phone, then a photograph of the notebook page, and then I cut the route free of the 1987 sheet with a razor blade, careful as surgery, and slid it into the waterproof case I keep my own charts in. My mother's route. My childhood handwriting. My inheritance, such as it was: a way in, a warning, and a name.

From the galley came a burst of laughter—Junie, losing spectacularly again—and then Okafor's voice, warm and low, saying something I couldn't make out. My crew. My people. Two of them asleep within earshot of a captain who'd just learned that the wreck they were racing toward might not be a wreck at all, but a grave someone dug on purpose.

I clicked off the lamp and sat in the dark with the case against my chest.

*Ask Vesper,* the child had written.

Fine. If Kaine wanted a race, she'd get one. And somewhere between the second tide and the sixth, one way or another, I was going to ask her—and this time I intended to remember the answer.
