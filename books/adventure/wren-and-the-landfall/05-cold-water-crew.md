## 5. Cold Water Crew

The thing nobody tells you about salvage diving is that ninety percent of the job is shopping.

I learned this my first week on the *Pelagic Rose*, when I signed on thinking I'd be spending my days descending into shipwrecks like some kind of maritime archaeologist, and instead spent four days in a Costco parking lot helping Okafor argue about the tensile rating of ratchet straps. Okafor wins arguments the way glaciers win arguments—slowly, completely, and with total indifference to how you feel about it. We left with the expensive straps.

So it was that on the third day of provisioning for the Meridian run, I found myself in the back of Harlan's Marine Supply in Cordova, holding two dive lights up to the fluorescent tubes while Beatrix Nakamura stood beside me not saying anything, which is her version of a full paragraph.

"Left one's got a better beam," I said.

"Left one's got a better beam," she agreed, and took four.

That's the whole negotiation, usually. I've been diving with Beatrix for three years and I've gotten good at finishing her sentences, which is either intimacy or a warning sign, depending on which crew psychology article Okafor has most recently left lying around the galley. He leaves them around on purpose. He thinks none of us notice. We all notice. It's like living with a very competent raccoon.

* * *

Here's the crew, so you can keep score at home.

Beatrix, captain, owner of the *Pelagic Rose*, forty-one years old, built like a knot in hardwood. She talks maybe a third as much as a normal person and means roughly four times as much when she does. Ten years ago she decided her salvage operation would be small, quiet, and off every chart the state keeps, and she has defended that decision the way other people defend children. I once watched her turn down a contract that would have covered our fuel for a year because the client "talked too much on the phone." I thought it was paranoia. I was nineteen. Everything looks like paranoia when you've never lost anything.

Okafor, first mate, engineer, quartermaster, and the only person on board allowed to touch the compressor. Sixty if he's a day, Ghanaian by birth, Alaskan by stubbornness, with hands like tree roots and a memory for numbers that I'm fairly sure is illegal in at least two countries. He can tell you what we paid for hydraulic fluid in 2019 and in what month, and whether it rained. He calls everyone "small one" except Beatrix, whom he calls "Captain" with a weight that makes the word sound like it has a hyphen in it.

And then me. Junie Okonkwo-Bell—no relation to Okafor, though we've let dockside gossips believe otherwise for years because it makes a better story. Twenty-two. Diver, tender operator, and officially designated youngest, which means I get the worst bunk, the first watch, and unlimited license to complain about both. I came up through the commercial dive program in Seward on scholarship and a temperament, and Beatrix found me when I was twenty and still doing the thing where you volunteer for everything dangerous because you haven't figured out yet that fear is information. She figured it out for me. That's the short version of why I'd follow her into a glacier.

Which, as it turns out, is not a hypothetical anymore.

* * *

The Meridian run had been decided two nights earlier, badly, the way important decisions on small boats always are. I wasn't in the cabin for the vote—crew votes happen at the table and I'm crew, but there's a kind of vote that happens in the space between Beatrix and Okafor, and everyone understands that the real one is the second kind. The first kind is ceremonial. Like a coronation.

What I knew going into Harlan's was this: we owed money we didn't know we owed, to a woman named Vesper Kaine who my captain visibly flinched at hearing named, and the price of the debt was one job on a wreck that Beatrix's mother died trying to reach. I knew that Beatrix had said no. I knew that Okafor had signed the papers anyway, and that Beatrix had looked at him for a long, long moment in the galley, and that neither of them had raised their voice, and that the silence afterward had a texture, like water pressure.

And I knew that we were now buying dive lights and thermal underwear and an amount of rope that made Harlan himself come out from behind the counter to look at it, whistling.

"You going somewhere cold," Harlan said. Not a question. Harlan has sold gear to every salvage outfit in the Sound for thirty years and he reads manifests the way other men read tea leaves.

"Going somewhere cold," Beatrix said.

"North?"

"North."

Harlan looked at the rope again, then at Beatrix, and I watched him decide not to ask the next question. That's a skill. Half of Cordova has it; the other half is why we usually provision in Whittier.

* * *

Back at the dock, Okafor had the hold open and was conducting what I privately call the Inventory Liturgy, in which every item is named, weighed, and assigned a location, in that order, aloud, to no one. I've stopped offering to help. You help by staying out of the geometry.

"Small one," he said, without turning around, because he always knows it's me, "you are standing on the fuel line."

I moved. "How can you tell it's me?"

"You breathe loud. Like a kettle." He set a case of spare O-rings down with the tenderness other people reserve for infants. "You have the list?"

I had the list. The list was four pages, in Beatrix's handwriting, which is small and slanted and looks like it's leaning into a wind. Reading Beatrix's lists is a kind of intimacy too. There was a line item on page three that I kept coming back to: *flares, 12-gauge, x24*. Twenty-four. We'd carried six on every job I'd ever been on. I didn't say anything about it. I've learned that on the *Rose*, the numbers do the talking, and if you wait, someone will translate.

The someone, that afternoon, was Okafor, while we coiled line in the late light.

"Calving season opens on that glacier in five weeks," he said, conversational, like he was discussing weather. "Before it opens, the ice dam thins enough to let a boat through, if the boat is small and the captain is stupid. After it opens—" he made a small gesture, a flower blooming, an iceberg birthing, "—the route is gone. So we have a window. Six tides, maybe. The fleet has a window too, but the fleet is heavy, so their window is smaller, except they don't care, because they can afford to lose a boat and we cannot, because our boat is also our house."

"Six tides," I said.

"Six tides." He looked at me then, and his face did the thing where all the humor drains out of it and what's left underneath is just old, old care. "Small one. You can still say no. I want you to know that. The captain will never say it to you, because she thinks saying it is a kind of insult, and the first mate is not permitted to say it because I signed the papers. But you are young and your lungs are new, and no one on this boat would think less of you."

I thought about it for exactly as long as it took to be polite. "No one would think less of me," I said, "but *I'd* think less of me."

Okafor nodded slowly, as if I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was taking. "Yes," he said. "That is the disease. Welcome aboard."

* * *

Dinner that night was rice and smoked salmon and the particular tension of four—no, three, it's three of us, I keep doing that, three of us eating in a galley built for two, with a fourth chair that nobody mentions, because the fourth chair belonged to a diver who left under circumstances I've never gotten a straight answer about. There's a shape in the crew where that person used to be. I feel it most at meals. Okafor sets out three plates and there's a half-second where his hand hovers, like the ghost of a fourth plate.

Beatrix ate the way she does everything—efficiently, completely, and while looking at something else. Charts, that night. Her mother's charts, the old paper ones, spread across the table under the lamp, and I noticed she was looking at them the way you look at a person's face when they're asleep and you're trying to memorize it.

"Those are the survey charts?" I asked. "The old ones?"

"Mm."

"Can I see?"

For a second I thought she'd say no, and then she rotated the top one toward me, and there it was: the coast north of the Sound, inked in a fine old hand, soundings in fathoms, and then—scattered over it like bird tracks—annotations in pencil. Some in an adult's careful script. Some in a kid's wobbly print.

"That one's not your mom's," I said, pointing at a wobbly *DANGER — ICE FALLS FAST* scrawled across a bay.

"No," Beatrix said.

"Whose is it?"

The lamp hummed. Okafor, washing dishes, had gone very still in a way I recognized: the stillness of a man listening hard while pretending to wash a pan.

"Mine," Beatrix said. "I was eight."

I looked at the handwriting. I looked at her. I did the math out loud, which is my worst habit and my defining feature. "You dove with her when you were eight?"

"She didn't dive. I snorkeled. She surveyed." Beatrix gathered the charts with a care that was almost violent. "She'd put me in a drysuit and tow me along the shallows on a line while she ran the fathometer. I'd hold the pencil." A pause. "I held it tighter back then, apparently."

There was something under the words. I'm not great at a lot of things, but I am great at hearing what's under words, because I grew up in a house where nobody said anything directly either. What was under the words was: *I don't remember writing this, and that frightens me.* She didn't say that. She rolled the charts, tied them, and stowed them in the chart locker above her bunk, which is the closest thing the *Rose* has to a reliquary.

Later, Okafor told me a little. Not much. He was on the boat the summer Beatrix's mother drowned—no, that's wrong, he wasn't crew yet, he was running fuel dock in Whittier, and Beatrix's mother used to buy coffee from him, black, always, even in July. He told me that Beatrix was seventeen when it happened, that she'd been left ashore that season—first season ever, some fight about school—and that the boat came back with a different name on the helm and a hole in it that never really got explained. He told me that Beatrix spent two years diving that coast alone before she bought the *Rose*, and that when she hired him, the first thing she asked wasn't his certifications. It was whether he'd ever worked the glacial fjords.

"I said yes," Okafor said. "This was a lie. I learned very fast after."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you look at her like she is made of steel," he said, "and steel is fine, but you should know what it is made of. She is not fearless, small one. She is afraid in the correct direction. There is a difference, and the difference is why we are all still alive."

* * *

The last provisioning day, Beatrix and I went out to test the new lights in the harbor, which is a thing we do that's really just an excuse to be in cold water together, which is a thing we do that's really just how she talks.

We swam out past the breakwater where the water goes green-black and cold enough to make your jaw ache through the hood. Beatrix led, kicking that slow economical kick of hers, and I fell in behind at her left shoulder, which is my station, which I chose and she never corrected, and which I have come to understand is the closest thing to a declaration of trust that woman will ever issue.

Down at fifteen feet, the harbor floor is a junkyard of a century: tires, chain, a school bus nobody talks about. Beatrix flicked her new light across it, and I flicked mine, and we did the wordless thing where you check each other's gear by eye—valves, hoses, gauges—because you can't talk down there, and because the checking is the talking.

And that's when I saw it. Not on the harbor floor. On her.

We were hovering in the green dark, and she turned to check my gauge, and for about two seconds her face was three feet from mine, unguarded, lit by my own light bounced back at her. And her eyes—her eyes were doing something her hands never do. Her hands are always steady. Her eyes were afraid. Not of the water. I know what fear-of-the-water looks like; I've seen it in greenhorns, wide and animal. This was older and more specific. This was a person looking at cold water and seeing a particular day in it. A particular boat. A particular absence.

Then she blinked, gave me the thumbs-up, and ascended, and by the time we broke the surface she was Beatrix again, all competence and economy, telling me the left light was better after all and we should get four more.

But I'd seen it. And once you've seen it, you can't unsee it, and it changes everything, and here's the strange part: it made me trust her *more*. Because a fearless captain is just a captain who hasn't met the thing yet. A captain who's met the thing, named it, and goes anyway—that's a captain who's going to get you home. Or that's what I told myself, anyway, twenty-two years old and in love with the idea of her, floating in Cordova harbor with my teeth chattering, absolutely certain I understood everything.

* * *

We cast off the next morning on a gray tide, the *Pelagic Rose* riding low with fuel and rope and twenty-four flares and four more of the good dive lights, and the whole town pretending not to watch us go, and half of them watching anyway.

Okafor had the helm first. Beatrix stood at the bow with her mother's charts in a waterproof tube, and I stood at the rail behind her, because that's my station too.

"Hey, Captain," I said. "The annotations. The kid handwriting. You really don't remember making them?"

She didn't turn around. The bow went up and down through the long swells. When she finally answered, she was looking north, at the white line of the mountains where the fjords started.

"No," she said. "And I've decided that scares me more than the glacier does."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I said nothing, which on the *Pelagic Rose* is a full sentence, and we ran north into the cold.
