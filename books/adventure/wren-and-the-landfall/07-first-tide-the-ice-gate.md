## 7. First Tide: The Ice Gate

The glacier announced itself three hours before we saw it, the way a debt collector announces himself with a phone call before he shows up at your dock. First it was just a cold coming off the water that didn't belong to the season. Then the sea went glassy and strange, greased flat between drifting floes, as if the whole bay were holding its breath. Then came the sound—a long, tectonic groan rolling out from behind the headland like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Tell me that's normal," Junie said from the bow rail.

"It's normal," I said.

"It sounds like the mountain is chewing."

"That's also normal."

I'll give her this: she didn't go below. She stayed at the rail with her hands jammed in her armpits and her breath pluming white, watching the ice field open up ahead of us like a broken plate someone had tried to glue back together without much conviction. Growlers the size of kitchen tables. Bergy bits the size of cars. And beyond them, where the fjord bent north toward the tidewater face, shapes the size of buildings, blue-white and patient, waiting for us to do something stupid.

The *Corvina* handled it better than I deserved. Ten years of quiet salvage work will teach you what a boat can do; ten years of keeping her underweight and over-maintained will teach you what she can't. We idled through the outer scatter at two knots, Okafor on the bow with a boat hook he mostly used for pointing, calling depths and drifts in that low, unhurried voice of his that made even "hard to port, now" sound like a lullaby.

"Contact," he said, an hour in. "Two o'clock. Submerged, maybe a meter down."

I saw it before he finished—the wrongness in the water, a pale smear moving against the current. Growlers are liars. They sit nine-tenths hidden and drift faster than you think, because the wind pushes their little visible tops while the current drags the rest sideways, and if you plan around what you can see you deserve what you get.

"I have it," I said, and took us wide, and the smear slid past our stern close enough that I could count the bubbles frozen in it. Nobody breathed until it was fifty meters astern.

"You've done this before," Junie said. It wasn't quite a question.

"My mother did this before," I said. "I was cargo."

That was true, though not the whole truth, and I felt the not-whole-truth settle into my chest like ballast. Somewhere back in my cabin, rolled in oilcloth inside my mother's old chart case, was the route she'd drawn—or half-drawn, or that I'd half-drawn, depending on which version of my own childhood I was willing to believe—and every meter we advanced made the annotations less like a dead woman's guesswork and more like instructions.

Which should have been comforting. It wasn't. Instructions imply an instructor who expected you to arrive.

* * *

We anchored at noon in the lee of a granite islet the charts called nothing at all, because my mother had never bothered to name it and neither had anyone else worth consulting. From there you could see the ice gate.

I want to describe it honestly, which means admitting that my first reaction was not awe but arithmetic. The Meridian's approach ran up a narrow channel between the glacier's outflow moraine on one side and a shelf of grounded old ice on the other—maybe forty meters of navigable water, maybe thirty, narrowing by the hour as the tide dropped and the floes closed like a fist. The official survey—Kaine's survey, the fleet's survey, the one that had gotten my mother's expedition laughed out of every underwriter's office on the coast—showed the channel silted shut since the nineties. My mother's annotation, in my own eleven-year-old handwriting, showed otherwise: *gate opens 2m above low, closes fast, don't be clever.*

"Don't be clever," Okafor read over my shoulder, when I spread the chart on the wheelhouse table. "Your mother wrote that?"

"Allegedly."

"You don't remember writing it."

"I remember being here." That part was true, or true enough. A summer, maybe two, when I was small enough to fit into places divers couldn't, riding in the skiff while she worked the ice line. "I remember the smell. Cold and iron. I remember being told to hold onto something. I don't remember drawing anything."

Okafor studied me the way he studies hull damage—not unkindly, but thoroughly, looking for the crack that means the rest is compromised. He'd signed the papers with Kaine's lawyer six days ago and hadn't said a word about it since, and I hadn't let him, because the alternative was saying words about it and I knew exactly how those words would go. He'd saved the crew's wages. He'd also sold my boat's soul to the woman my mother hated most, and both of those things could be true at once, and I was tired of carrying both of them, so I carried neither and let the ice do the talking.

"The fleet's tender left the fuel depot yesterday," he said instead. "Sato heard it on the marine band. They're a day behind us, maybe less if they run dark."

"They won't run dark. Kaine wants us to know she's coming. Fear is free advertising."

"And does it work?"

I looked at the gate. The tide was dropping; the channel mouth was already showing its teeth, floes grinding against each other with a sound like slow applause from a very large, very bored audience.

"We go in on this tide," I said, "and we're inside before she's even in the bay. After that, the ice doesn't care whose flag you fly."

* * *

The thing nobody tells you about threading pack ice is how loud it is. You expect silence—white wilderness, hushed cathedral, all that. What you get is percussion. Ice flexing against the hull. Floes kissing and separating along our sides with wet smacks. The engine working harder than it likes, the whole boat shuddering in a way that reads, unmistakably, as complaint. By the time we committed to the gate I'd stopped hearing it as noise and started hearing it as conversation, the *Corvina* telling me everything she felt, and me answering with the throttles.

We entered on the flood, per the annotation, with Junie aloft on the wheelhouse roof as eyes—I'd have put Okafor up there except I needed his hands on the fenders more than I needed his judgment, and anyway Junie had the best young eyes on the coast and knew it, which is the only kind of confidence that holds up under pressure.

"Bearing's good," she called down. "Channel opens up ahead—wait. Wait wait wait. Captain. There's a big one mid-channel. Big. It's—it's not drifting, it's *rolling.*"

I saw it then. A growler the size of a fishing boat, grounded shallow on the bar but not settled, caught by the flood tide and slowly, majestically capsizing as its center of buoyancy shifted. Rolling ice doesn't move fast. It moves *inevitably*, which is worse, because you can't outrun a fact.

"Options," Okafor said, beside me now, calm as a man discussing lunch.

"Port side's got a gap. Maybe eight meters. Maybe six."

"Maybe?"

"The growler's going to close it in about ninety seconds either way."

He looked at the gap. He looked at the growler. He looked at me, and whatever he saw in my face must have been sufficient, because he turned and bellowed fender stations without another word, and that—that right there—is the entire reason I've kept him for ten years. Not loyalty. Not the debt. The man hears *maybe six meters* and starts working instead of arguing.

I took us through on the port gap at four knots, which is insane, which is the speed you use when slowing down means stopping and stopping means dying. The hull kissed ice twice—port quarter, starboard bow—each touch a long scraping exhalation I felt through the soles of my feet. Junie was narrating from above in a voice pitched somewhere between a sports commentator and a prayer. The growler completed its roll behind us with a boom I felt in my teeth, closing the channel we'd just come through like a door.

And then we were through, and the fjord opened up, and I did the thing I'd been postponing for six days.

I looked up.

The glacier face stood across the whole northern end of the water, five kilometers of ice rising two hundred meters sheer off the tideline, blue in its depths and white on its shoulders and streaked with gray moraine bands like the strata of some enormous mind. Meltwater fell off it in threads. Somewhere inside it, sealed under decades of accumulation, was a ship, and inside the ship was a strongbox with my name on it, and possibly also the answer to the only question I'd ever really asked, and the glacier held all of it the way the rich hold grudges: patiently, and with total confidence in the outcome.

"Well," Okafor said quietly. "There she is."

Nobody said anything else for a while. Even Sato, who complains professionally and continuously, said nothing. The engines idled. The ice ticked against the hull. Somewhere far up-valley, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, and I noticed everyone's heads turn toward it at once, the way birds all look at the same empty sky.

"She's talking," Junie said.

"She's always talking," I said. "It's when she stops that you worry."

* * *

We found the anchorage at 1530, tucked behind a shelf of grounded berg that the annotation marked with a small, precise cross and the word *safe-ish*, which remains the most honest nautical term I have ever encountered. The holding was good gravel over clay. The shelf broke the current. From the deck you couldn't see the wreck site at all—you couldn't see anything but ice and granite and the long blue shadow of the glacier—which meant that anyone coming in behind us would have to thread the same gate we had, blind, on a falling tide.

I allowed myself exactly four minutes of feeling clever. Then I started feeling watched instead.

It was nothing I could point to. No light on the ice, no engine note on the wind. Just the specific quality of attention that a place has when it has been recently attended to. Ten years of salvage teaches you to read rooms; apparently it also teaches you to read fjords. There was a freshness to the meltwater running off the near shelf, a discoloration in one patch of snow on the granite islet that could have been anything or could have been a campsite scrubbed in a hurry. Or I could have been inventing ghosts because my mother's handwriting was in my childhood hand and nothing about this trip respected the border between past and present.

"First dive tomorrow, on the morning tide?" Okafor asked, coming up beside me at the rail.

"Superstructure recon only. Junie and me. Nobody goes inside, nobody goes deep, nobody gets heroic on day one."

"You realize you just described yourself as the heroic risk."

"I described myself as the captain. Same thing, cheaper insurance."

He smiled at that, briefly, and then didn't, and I knew what was coming the way you know a wave is coming—by the hole it leaves in the water ahead of it.

"Beatrix. About the papers."

"Don't."

"He was going to take the boat, Beatrix. Kaine's lawyers, not Kaine—the lawyers. I signed before she even made the offer. It was the only move that kept us afloat long enough for there to be a choice at all."

I watched the ice shelf across the anchorage. Somewhere under it, the Meridian sat in the dark with her holds full of contradictions, and my mother's strongbox, and whatever truth had gone down with her, and I thought: everything I love ends up underwater, and everything underwater eventually gives up its secrets, and the trick—the whole trick—is making sure you're still breathing when it does.

"I know why you did it," I said. "I'm not saying I forgive it yet. I'm saying I know why."

"That's more than I hoped for."

"Don't spend it all in one place."

He went below. I stayed at the rail while the light went long and gold and then gray, and the temperature fell the way it does here, all at once, like a decision. Junie brought me coffee without being asked and stood next to me not-talking, which is a skill most people never learn and she'd mastered by twenty-two.

"Captain," she said finally. "Can I ask you something weird?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the weirdness."

"When you were a kid. On your mom's boat. Did you ever dive here? Like, actually in the water?"

I looked at the black water, and the annotation in my own handwriting rose up in my memory unbidden—the depth figures, the bearing marks, the little cross at the anchorage, all recorded with a precision no eleven-year-old improvises. *Don't be clever,* I'd written, in a child's careful block letters, and beneath it, smaller, almost squeezed into the margin as if added later, or as if I'd been afraid to write it larger:

*She said come back when I'm ready.*

"No," I said. "But I get the feeling somebody planned for me to."

Junie looked at me for a moment, and then out at the glacier, and I watched her decide not to ask the follow-up, which was wise of her, because I didn't have an answer. I had a strongbox with my name on it sitting under two hundred meters of ice and a decade of salt, and a route drawn in my own hand that I couldn't remember drawing, and a rival fleet a day behind me, and a first mate I'd forgiven on credit.

The morning tide would let us down to the wreck. The morning tide would show us what my mother had spent her life—and, one bad night, her actual life—trying to reach.

I went below, checked the anchor twice, and slept badly, dreaming of cold water and a voice saying *come back when you're ready*, and woke at 0400 to a sound I couldn't identify until I was on deck with binoculars: far up the fjord, at the very edge of visibility, a single white light moving along the top of the ice shelf.

Moving. Where nothing should be moving. Where nobody but us had any business being.

Not the fleet. The fleet was a day south and wouldn't travel dark.

Somebody else had found the gate first.
