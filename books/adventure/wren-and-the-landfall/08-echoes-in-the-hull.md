## 8. Echoes in the Hull

The thing nobody tells you about diving a wreck inside a glacier is that the water doesn't feel like water. It feels like being held. Cold enough to stop your thoughts at the surface of your skin and press them flat, so all you've got left underneath is the animal part, the part that counts your breaths and knows exactly how many you have left.

I counted mine the whole way down.

Two hundred and ten on the gauge when I rolled off the skiff, which was generous by Junie standards. Okafor had drilled the numbers into me for three days straight—bottom time, deco ceiling, what to do if my regulator free-flows, what to do if the ice above us shifts, what to do if I see Beatrix's light go still for more than ten seconds. *You come up,* he'd said. *No matter where she is or what she's found. You come up and you tell me.* And I'd nodded like a person who agrees with things, because that's what you do with Okafor. You nod first and argue with yourself later.

The Meridian came up out of the green dark the way wrecks do—not gradually, not piece by piece, but all at once, the way a face comes out of a crowd. One second it was nothing, a suggestion of geometry where geometry had no business being, and then it was a ship, a whole entire ship, sitting upright on the seabed with her superstructure rising toward the ice ceiling forty fathoms overhead like a hand reaching for a door that closed a hundred years ago.

She was big. That was the first thing. Bigger than the charts made her look, bigger than the models in Beatrix's cabin, bigger than the word *wreck* had ever meant to me before. Wrecks, in my experience, were broken things. This wasn't broken. This was *kept*. The glacier had calved over her in some slow century-long shrug and sealed her in cold black water that barely moved, and the cold had done what cold does—it had stopped everything. No rust bloom softening her lines. No silt burying her rails. Her bridge windows were still in their frames, furred white with a skin of ice-melt mineral, and when my light swept across them they glowed like the eyes of something patient.

Beatrix hung in the water off the port wing of the bridge, her exhaled bubbles going up in a slow silver rope and flattening against the underside of the ice ceiling, spreading there into a shimmering sheet that caught our lights and threw it back down doubled. Two ships. Two crews. Everything twice, mirrored and slightly wrong.

She looked at me through her mask and did the signal we'd agreed on: *you take the boat deck, I take the bridge, stay in sight of each other's lights.* Then she turned and pulled herself through the open door of the wheelhouse, and her light went inside, and I was alone with the Meridian.

Here is what you should know about me, since apparently I'm the one telling this part: I'm twenty-three years old, I've been diving salvage for four years, and before the *Nakamura* I worked a fish processor out of Kodiak where the most interesting thing I ever saw underwater was a halibut the size of a door. I came aboard as deckhand and stayed as diver because Beatrix watched me clear a fouled anchor line in freezing chop one afternoon and said, without looking up from her charts, "You don't panic. That's rarer than talent." Which is the nicest thing anyone has ever said to me and also the most terrifying, because now every time I'm underwater and something goes sideways, there's a voice in my head that says *don't panic, that's rarer than talent*, and it sounds exactly like her, and it makes me want to panic just to spite it.

I swam the boat deck.

It was like swimming through a photograph. Deck chairs, or the frames of them, stacked against a bulkhead where somebody had lashed them down before the end—which told me the crew had known, hadn't been surprised, had taken the time to put things away. A cargo winch with its cable paid out neat as fishing line. A davit swung out over an empty spot where a lifeboat should have been, except there were davits for six boats and only two empty cradles, which meant four boats had gone down with her, which meant—

I stopped myself. Math like that, on a wreck, will follow you back up. I'd learned that on my first job, a trawler off the Forelands, when I spent the whole decompression hang doing arithmetic about how many bunks were in the fo'c'sle versus how many boots I'd found. You can't un-know those numbers. They move into your head and pay no rent.

My light found the entrance to the grand staircase enclosure, and that's where I saw the marks.

At first I thought they were old damage. Salvors had been after this wreck for a hundred years on paper—every chart in the region carried the Meridian as a hazard, every rumor mill from Juneau to Seattle had a story about her cargo—and I figured somewhere in that century somebody had come down with a cutting torch and gotten greedy. But torch cuts are distinctive. Torch cuts are ragged, slagged, melted at the edges, drunk-looking. These weren't.

These were clean.

I put my glove against the bulkhead plate and traced one. A straight line, precise as a ruler drawn in ink, cut through steel that should have laughed at anything short of industrial equipment. And beside it, another. And beside those, a pattern—a rectangle maybe two feet by three, scored out on the plating in guide-cuts, the kind you make first so your real cut follows true. Except the real cut had never been finished. The rectangle was outlined but not opened, like a door drawn on a wall in a cartoon, waiting for someone to push through.

And here's the thing that took my stomach and turned it over slow, the way you turn a stone to see what's under it:

There was no rust in the cuts.

Steel underwater rusts. Steel underwater in *any* water rusts, even cold water, even slow. A hundred-year-old cut would be furred orange, softened, grown over with whatever the sea plants on everything eventually. These cuts were bright metal. Bare. Shining faintly under my light like scratches on new tin.

Somebody had made these cuts recently. Recently as in *this season*. Recently as in possibly *this month*.

I hung there in the green silence with my breath counting itself—hundred and sixty-two, hundred and sixty-three—and felt the whole shape of the situation rearrange around me. We hadn't beaten the fleet here. We hadn't even beaten whoever came before the fleet. Somebody had been inside the Meridian already, quietly, illegally, with equipment good enough to cut hull-grade steel and nerve enough to do it under a tidewater glacier, and they had stopped mid-job and left, and I was looking at their unfinished work.

I did what you're supposed to do. I signaled Beatrix—three sharp flashes on my light, the *come look at this* code—and kept my own light moving while I waited, because Okafor's other rule is *never stare at one thing underwater; the thing you're not staring at is the one that gets you.*

That rule saved me, maybe. Or maybe it just let me be the one who saw it.

Because past the staircase enclosure, down along the promenade deck where the hull curved up toward the ice, there was more. More cuts. A whole line of them, following the hull plates aft, spaced regular as fence posts, and at the end of the line—where the cuts stopped—the steel was bulged. Not torn, not breached. Bulged outward, smooth, like something behind the plating was pressing. Like the ship had swallowed something and it was still in there, pushing to get out.

And pinned to a stanchion beside the last cut, fluttering in the imperceptible current, was a marker buoy streamer. Fleet-issue. I knew the color because Kaine's corporate tender had been provisioning in the same harbor as us, and their gear has a look—all high-vis yellow and a logo like a stylized compass rose. Except this streamer was faded. Sun-bleached. Tied off in a knot I recognized, because it's the knot every working diver learns and then spends years unlearning because it jams.

It wasn't a fleet knot. It was a salvage knot. An old-school one.

Which meant either the fleet had someone aboard who learned knots the honest way, in salt and cold, before they got corporate—or somebody else had been here, wearing borrowed colors, doing borrowed work.

My light was shaking. I made it stop. Hundred and fifty-one.

Beatrix's light appeared at the edge of the wheelhouse doorway, and I waved her over, and she came fast—that economical pull-pull-glide she does, like the water owes her money and she's collecting. She got to the bulkhead and put her own light on the cuts, and I watched her go through the same sequence I had: confusion, recognition, arithmetic. Her shoulders changed. Even through the drysuit, even upside-down and mirrored in the ice ceiling above us, I could read her. Ten years of watching a person teaches you their weather.

She touched the bright metal. Ran her glove along the guide-cut. Then she looked at the bulged plating, and she stayed looking at it for a long time, long past comfortable, long enough that I checked my gauge twice, and when she finally turned to me she didn't do any signals at all. She just looked at me through her mask, and behind the glass her eyes were doing something I'd never seen them do underwater, which was *nothing*. Completely blank. A person reading a letter she'd hoped never to receive.

Then she pointed at the streamer on the stanchion. Made a fist. *Take it.*

I untied the jammed knot—of course it jammed, of course—and worked the streamer free, and the moment it came loose the current took it, streaming out yellow and ghostly, and Beatrix grabbed it and stuffed it into her thigh pocket, and that was when the glacier spoke.

I want to be careful how I describe this, because people who haven't heard it always think I'm exaggerating. It wasn't a sound like thunder. Thunder is air. This was ice under stress, miles of it, shifting its weight the way a sleeping man shifts, and the sound came through the water and through the hull and through my own bones, a groan so deep and so long that my teeth ached with it and my gauge glass buzzed. Somewhere above us, tons upon tons upon tons of frozen centuries moved a fraction of an inch, and the whole ocean flinched.

Beatrix's hand closed on my arm. Squeezed once. *Up.*

We went up together, her light ahead of mine, through the green and the silver sheet of our own trapped breath still hanging under the ice like a ceiling of coins, and I did not look back at the bulged plating, and I have thought about it every hour since.

* * *

On the surface, the world was violently ordinary. Gray sky, gray water, the *Nakamura* riding at anchor half a mile off the ice gate with her running lights on against the gloom, and Okafor's face at the rail wearing the expression he saves for when we surface early, which is a cross between relief and suspicion, because early surfacing means either something wonderful or something wrong and he's stopped believing in wonderful.

We came up the ladder and he stripped our masks and hoods with the brisk rough tenderness he pretends isn't tenderness, and Beatrix said, "Get everyone in the wheelhouse," in a voice I'd heard maybe twice in four years, both times when the weather fax showed something ugly coming.

We crowded in—me dripping, Beatrix dripping, Okafor, Reyes with his coffee, little Tam leaning in the doorway pretending he wasn't listening because technically he was off-watch. Beatrix put the yellow streamer on the chart table and smoothed it flat with one hand, and everybody looked at it, and nobody said anything, because the logo was unmistakable and so was the knot, and the knot contradicted the logo, and everyone aboard knows knots the way priests know Latin.

"Found these below," Beatrix said finally. "Guide-cuts on the hull plating. Bright metal. Fresh." She looked around the wheelhouse, meeting eyes. "Someone's been on the Meridian within the last few months. Maybe weeks."

"The fleet's still provisioning," Reyes said. "We watched their tender load yesterday. They can't have divers down yet."

"So it wasn't the fleet." Beatrix tapped the streamer. "But it's their flag."

Okafor picked the streamer up and turned it over slowly, and I watched his jaw work, and I thought: *he knows something.* Not what it is. Just that shape of knowing, the way he goes quiet and careful when a piece fits a hole he'd been hoping stayed empty.

"Could've been stolen gear," Tam offered from the doorway. "Scavengers lift fleet kit all the time. Makes people think twice about chasing them."

"Or," Okafor said, "someone's diving for the fleet who used to dive for us."

The wheelhouse got very quiet. Coffee steam rose off Reyes's mug and bent in the draft from the heater. Beatrix looked at Okafor for a long moment, and whatever passed between them was old and heavy and had names attached to it that nobody said out loud, and I stood there with glacier water still running out of my hair and understood that the *Nakamura* had a history longer than my four years aboard, and that history had just reached up out of the water and grabbed the rail.

"There was a diver," Beatrix said at last, and her voice was level in the way that costs effort, "before some of you. Before Junie. He—" She stopped. Started again. "He made choices I couldn't keep him aboard for. He'll have a name you've heard me say once, maybe, and never twice."

"And you think he's cutting the Meridian open for Kaine," Reyes said.

"I think the knot is his," Beatrix said. "I taught it to him."

Outside, the light was going. The glacier across the bay had turned the color of old bone, and the ice gate we'd threaded that morning stood dark between its walls, and somewhere beyond it, forty fathoms down, a rectangle was drawn on a wall of steel, waiting for somebody to finish the door.

Beatrix folded the streamer once, twice, and put it in her pocket next to where her mother's charts lived, and said, "Double watch tonight. Nobody dives alone, nobody leaves the pool of the floodlight, and if anybody sees a light on the ice that isn't ours—"

She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't have to. We all heard the rest of it: *then we were never alone out here at all, and we haven't been for a while.*

That night I lay in my bunk listening to the hull tick and the glacier mutter out in the dark, doing the math I'd promised myself I wouldn't do. Four lifeboat davits, filled. Guide-cuts, unfinished. A knot tied by hands Beatrix trained. And a bulge in the plating, smooth and patient, like something the Meridian had swallowed a hundred years ago and was only now getting ready to give back.

Somewhere out on the ice, right then, I'd have bet my share there was a light moving.
