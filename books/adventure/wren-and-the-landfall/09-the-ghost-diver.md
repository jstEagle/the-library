## 9. The Ghost Diver

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes off a glacier at three in the morning, and it is not the friendly kind. It's the quiet of a thing holding its breath. The Meridian's approach had gone still around midnight—the wind died, the water flattened to black glass, and the ice field settled into a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse ticking in my ears like a faulty depth gauge.

I'd taken the middle watch because Beatrix hadn't slept properly in two days and because someone on this boat has to pretend to be the responsible adult, and Junie at twenty-three is constitutionally incapable of it. So there I was, wrapped in every layer I owned, thermos of coffee gone bitter in my hands, watching a tidewater glacier do absolutely nothing with tremendous menace.

The *Pelican* sat anchored in the lee of an iceberg the size of a parish church, riding a slack tide that would turn in four hours. Four hours until we went back down. Three tides left after tonight, if the math held, and the math never holds.

That's when I saw the light.

It came from up-ice, maybe six hundred meters out along the shelf where the glacier met the frozen slush zone—a small, steady point, moving. Not bobbing the way a headlamp moves on a person walking. Gliding. Pausing. Gliding again. The rhythm of somebody being careful, which on this ice meant somebody who knew what they were doing or thought they did.

I did what any sensible first mate does at three in the morning on a glacier: I stood very still for a full minute and hoped it would go away.

It didn't. It moved closer to the gate instead, angling toward the channel we'd threaded on the first tide, and I felt my stomach drop through the hull, through the water, through the silt, all the way to whatever passes for bedrock under a place like this. Because there were only two reasons to be walking our approach channel at night, and one of them was scouting it for somebody else.

I woke Beatrix. That was policy—anything on the ice, anything in the water, anything that isn't weather, wake the captain. She surfaced from her bunk with the speed of a woman who'd never really been asleep, eyes sharp before her hair was even untangled, and listened while I gave it to her straight.

"Scavenger," she said.

"Or scout."

"Same animal, different collar." She was already pulling on her boots. "Take the skiff. Take the flare gun. Do *not* take Junie."

"Junie's already awake."

"Then don't tell Junie where you're going." She caught my sleeve as I turned. Her face in the lamplight was all angles and exhaustion. "Okafor. If it's the fleet—if Kaine's people are already measuring our channel—we need to know tonight, not at dawn."

"And if it's not?"

She didn't have an answer for that, which told me she'd already considered the third option and hated it. On the *Pelican*, the third option is usually the right one.

* * *

The skiff crossed the black water without lights, engine at idle, oars doing most of the work. I'd left my rifle aboard—that was deliberate, a choice I made at the gunwale and then spent the whole crossing regretting—and carried the flare pistol instead, which is a weapon only in the sense that being hit by a thrown dictionary is a mugging. Behind me the glacier filled half the sky, blue-black and faintly luminous, the way deep water looks when the moon gets behind it wrong.

The light had stopped moving. I found its source beached on a low shelf of old ice near the channel mouth: a sled, a coil of rope, a camp stove burning low, and a figure sitting cross-legged beside it with their back to me, hood down, steam rising off a tin cup.

I grounded the skiff and came up the ice slow, boots crunching, making sure they heard me. Surprise is for amateurs. Surprise on a glacier gets people killed.

"You're trespassing," I said.

The figure didn't startle. They finished their sip, set the cup down on the ice with a small ceramic click, and said, in a voice I knew better than I wanted to:

"Technically, so are you."

Marisol Reyes turned around, and eight years fell on me like a dropped anchor.

* * *

You should understand something about the *Pelican*'s history, and about Beatrix Nakamura's particular talent for amputation. Crews like ours run on trust the way other boats run on diesel, and eight years ago we had a diver named Sol Reyes who was the best free diver I ever worked with—calm at depth, generous with air, funny in the way that keeps a cold crew warm. She dove with Beatrix's mother on two surveys back when Mrs. Nakamura was still alive, which made her family, or close enough.

And then one season, off a wreck in the Aleutians, Sol took a cut of salvage she hadn't earned. Quietly. Off the books. Sold coordinates to a broker in Kodiak. It wasn't much money. It was enough. Beatrix found out, and Beatrix didn't shout, didn't threaten, didn't do any of the things you see in the movies. She just stood on the dock at Haines and said, "You're not on my boat anymore," and that was the whole trial and the whole sentence. Sol packed her gear bag and walked down the pier and none of us said goodbye, because loyalty on the *Pelican* is a load-bearing wall and you don't knock holes in it to wave at the people outside.

Eight years. And here she was, sitting on our glacier, drinking coffee out of the same dented tin cup she'd had since before I met her, looking older and leaner and wearing a fleet-issue parka with the corporate logo half scraped off, like she couldn't decide whose side the jacket was on either.

"Hello, Okafor," she said.

"Sol."

"That's still my name, last I checked." She nodded at the ice beside her, offering me a seat I had no intention of taking. I took it anyway, because sitting down makes a conversation longer and longer conversations give up more than short ones. Old negotiator trick. Also my knees are fifty-two years old and the skiff crossing had been murder.

"You're working for Kaine," I said.

"I'm contracting for the fleet. There's a difference. Employees get benefits." She smiled, and it was the same crooked smile, and I hated how much easier it made everything. "You look good, Okafor. You got old, but you got old well. Some of us just got old."

"What are you doing on our approach channel at three in the morning?"

"Measuring it. Same as you did yesterday." She tipped her cup toward the dark water. "Depth soundings, current readings, choke points. Your captain threaded that gate like she was born in it—which, fine, genetics—but the fleet doesn't do genetics. The fleet does data. I'm the data."

"So you're scouting them in."

"I'm scouting them in," she agreed, without a flicker of shame, and that flicker-less quality was the thing I'd forgotten about her, the thing that had gotten her exiled in the first place. Sol could stand inside a burning building and describe the fire with perfect professional calm. "They'll come through on the next tide or the one after. The big tender can't make the narrows, but the two inflatables and the dive barge can, and once the barge is inside, they own the site. You know how this goes. You've been on both ends of it."

"Not this end."

"No," she said, studying me. "You're the loyal one. How's that working out? Still getting paid in gratitude and leftover chowder?"

"The chowder's excellent."

She laughed—a real laugh, brief and surprised out of her—and for a second we were just two divers on the ice at three in the morning, and then the laugh died and the eight years came back between us like a tide line.

"Why tell me?" I asked. "Why let me walk up on you? You could've killed your light and been gone. You're the best free diver I ever worked with; you're certainly quiet enough to vanish."

Something moved behind her eyes then—not guilt exactly, but the neighborhood guilt lives in. She looked at the glacier instead of at me.

"Because I wanted to warn you," she said. "Off the record. Not for the fleet, not against them. Just... off."

"Warn us about what?"

"About the schedule, partly. They're coming early. Kaine pulled the weather window forward—she thinks the calving season's going to open the route wide and then slam it shut faster than anyone's modeling, and she wants to be inside when it happens. Your three tides are about to become two, maybe less." She paused. "And about the wreck itself. There's someone else who's been diving the Meridian. Not us. Not the fleet. Fresh cutting marks on the superstructure, plasma torch, done within the last month. My employers think it's a rival outfit. I think—" She stopped herself.

"You think what?"

"I think whoever it is knew exactly where to cut." She finally looked at me straight on. "Okafor, those cuts aren't random. They're following a route into that hold. A specific route. And I've seen that route before, drawn in pencil, in the margin of a survey chart, in a hand I used to watch fill out dive logs across the galley table from me."

The cold went through me then, real cold, colder than the ice under me. Because I knew whose hand that was. Everybody on the *Pelican* knew whose hand that was. We'd spent the last week pretending we didn't.

"Beatrix's mother," I said.

"Beatrix's mother," Sol agreed softly. "Whoever cut into that wreck was using Ruth Nakamura's route. Which means either her charts got out after she drowned—or somebody's been carrying them around for ten years waiting for the ice to open." She drained her cup and stood, brushing frost off her parka, businesslike again, the window closing. "That's my warning. Free of charge. Don't say I never gave you anything."

"Sol." I got up too, slower. "Why are you really here? Eight years, and you take a contract that puts you on *this* glacier, on *this* wreck, working for the woman Beatrix hates more than anyone alive. That's not a job. That's a pilgrimage."

For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. The glacier groaned somewhere behind us, a long tectonic complaint, and neither of us flinched, because you learn not to flinch or the ice owns you.

"Because I want to see her," Sol said finally. "Not to apologize. She wouldn't take it, and I'm not sure I'd mean it right anyway. I just—" She gestured at the black water, the blue ice, the buried ship underneath it all. "Ruth Nakamura taught me to dive. Everything I am underwater, she put there. And now she's down there, in a way, and I wanted to be here for it. Even if I have to be here on the wrong side of the line." She shrugged, and the shrug was eight years old and hadn't healed. "The past has employees, Okafor. Somebody's got to sign for the deliveries."

I walked back to the skiff. At the waterline I turned, because there was one more question and I'd been carrying it since I saw the light.

"The fleet comes through tomorrow," I said. "Early. Are they armed?"

Sol looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she decided to leave out of her answer, I watched her decide it.

"They're corporate," she said. "They bring lawyers everywhere. But lawyers travel with security now. Get your captain to move fast, and get her to stop keeping secrets from her crew, because secrets sink boats faster than ice does, and I'd know—I sank mine."

Then she killed her stove, shouldered her sled, and walked north along the shelf until the dark took her, and I was alone on the ice with a warning shaped like a ghost.

* * *

Beatrix was awake when I got back, of course. She was sitting in the galley with the chart spread out under the lamp, and when I gave it to her—all of it, Sol, the schedule, the early advance, the torch cuts, the route—she didn't move for a long time. Just stared at the chart, at the penciled annotations in her mother's hand, or in her own childhood hand, depending on which of them you believed had drawn them, and I watched her arrive at the same place Sol had arrived at, and hate it more.

"She was using Mom's route," Beatrix said quietly. "Somebody cut into my mother's wreck following my mother's map."

"Or your map," I said. "The one you drew when you were nine and don't remember drawing."

She looked up at me then, and there was something raw in her face I hadn't seen since the day we signed Kaine's papers. Fear, yes, but underneath it, worse: the beginning of a suspicion she wasn't ready to say out loud.

"Get Junie," she said. "Wake the others. We dive on the next tide, not the one after. Whatever's in that hold—whatever my mother left down there—I want it in my hands before anybody else's are on it."

"And if the fleet's already inside by then?"

Beatrix folded the chart along its old creases, careful, almost gentle, the way you handle a letter from the dead.

"Then we race," she said.
