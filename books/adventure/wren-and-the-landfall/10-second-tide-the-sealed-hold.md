## 10. Second Tide: The Sealed Hold

The second tide came in gray and mean, the kind of light that makes everything underwater look like it's already dead. I stood at the rail of the *Wren* with a thermos of coffee I had no intention of drinking and watched Junie check her gear for the fourth time.

"You're going to wear out the buckles," I said.

"I'm going to make sure the buckles don't wear out me." She snapped one anyway. "You slept?"

"Define sleep."

"That's a no."

"It's a maritime definition of no. There are legal distinctions."

Okafor came up from below decks carrying the cutting rig like it was a sleeping child, which, given what it cost us to rent, it basically was. He'd been up since four running numbers on air mixes and bottom time, and he had the particular cheerfulness of a man who has done math all night and found the math survivable.

"Tide turns in forty minutes," he said. "Gate holds for six hours if the wind stays down. We go in on the ebb, we come out before it thinks about turning."

"And if the wind doesn't stay down?"

"Then we come out faster."

I looked at the glacier. From this distance—two miles of black water and drifting brash—it looked almost gentle, a white wall with a blue heart, the kind of thing they put on calendars sold in airport gift shops. Up close it groaned like something enormous trying to remember how to breathe. This morning it had groaned twice while I was pretending to sleep, and both times my body had known exactly whose voice that was before my brain caught up.

My mother had died under that ice. Officially. The report said hull failure, cold shock, a dive she shouldn't have made alone on a wreck she shouldn't have been chasing. I'd read the report so many times as a teenager that I could recite the investigator's name, and I'd hated him for the flatness of his sentences without ever once asking whether the flatness was hiding something.

Now I had manifests in my cabin that said maybe it was.

I hadn't told anyone. Not Okafor, who'd signed us into this without asking me, and not Junie, who looked at me like I was the reason the sun came up, and certainly not Reyes, who would take one look at evidence of corporate fraud and start planning our retirement. The manifests sat in a waterproof bag under my bunk, and every hour I didn't mention them they got heavier, the way secrets do—they gain weight by being carried alone.

"Beatrix." Okafor's voice, careful. "Where do you want the cut?"

I made myself look at the chart instead of the past. "Aft hold. The annotations mark a seam portside, below the third frame. If the hull's settled the way I think it has, the silt line should be at the deck plates. We cut low, we get clean access to the cargo bay without going through the superstructure."

"The superstructure where you found fresh cuts yesterday."

"The superstructure where somebody else found fresh cuts yesterday," I corrected. "We're just visiting their crime scene."

Junie laughed, then stopped herself, because laughing at crime scenes is apparently not professional. Okafor allowed himself half a smile, which from him was a standing ovation.

"Cutting team is me and Junie," he said. "You're topside."

"I'm diving."

"You're captain."

"I'm captain, which means I decide where I am, and I'm diving. That hold is—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "That hold matters. I'm not sending two divers into it blind while I drink coffee up here."

Okafor studied me with the patience of a man who had been studying me for eleven years and had learned to read the difference between stubbornness and fear, and knew I knew he could. "Bottom time at that depth gives you twenty-two minutes of work window," he said finally. "After that, deco stops eat your margin. You go long, I pull you out by the ankles."

"Noted."

"And you tell me what's down there. All of it. Whatever you find."

Something in my chest did a small, guilty maneuver. "All of it," I said, and hated how easy lying had gotten, and how recently.

* * *

The Meridian sat in her grave like a ship that had been buried alive and had opinions about it. Yesterday's dive had shown us the exposed superstructure, the bridge house canted fifteen degrees to starboard, the funnel collapsed across the boat deck like a felled tree. Today we went lower, along the hull's flank, following Junie's tether light through water so full of glacial flour it was like swimming inside milk glass.

The seam was where my mother's handwriting said it would be.

That was the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, down there in the cold, with my breath hammering through the regulator and my knife finding the old survey marks she'd scratched into the plating thirty years ago—the *N* with the bar through it, her personal sigil, repeated at intervals like breadcrumbs. She'd marked this hull. Personally. Before the Meridian ever sank, or after—I couldn't date the scratches—but either way, she had stood where I was standing, in water she'd never have survived, and left me directions.

Or her charts had, anyway. In my own childhood handwriting, which was its own category of nightmare I was saving for later.

Junie set the cutting torch and went to work, and the seam opened with a sound I felt in my teeth more than heard, a long structural complaint, the ship objecting to being opened after all these years. Silt billowed. Visibility dropped to nothing, then to less than nothing, then slowly, grudgingly, back to about eight feet as the current took the cloud away.

And there was the hold.

I want to describe it honestly, because dishonesty is the theme of my week. Cargo holds in wrecks are supposed to be dramatic—gold bars stacked like firewood, chests bursting with coins, the whole pirate-movie inventory. What we actually saw was dunnage rotting into lace, crates slumped into each other like tired commuters, and a smell you could taste through the regulator even though that's not physically possible: old wood, old iron, old time. The Meridian's official manifest had listed machine parts, timber, and general freight. What I was looking at, once I pried the first crate lid loose, was crated machinery stamped with a shipping company that had gone bankrupt in scandal two years before the sinking—and beneath it, nested and padded like eggs, cases of something wrapped in oilcloth that clinked when Junie's light touched them.

"Captain," Junie's voice said in my ear, very controlled, very young. "Is this what machine parts look like? Because I've seen machine parts, and—"

"I know."

"There's a lot of—"

"I know, Junie."

Silver. Not treasure-heap silver, but industrial quantity, bullion-weight, packed for transport by people who had wanted it to disappear. The official report said the Meridian went down with insured freight and nothing worth salvaging, which was why nobody had come for thirty years except my mother, who had known better, and whoever had been here last month with a plasma cutter.

Grief hit me sideways, the way it does—not for the silver, but for her. For the woman who'd stood in this dark and figured this out alone, who'd carried it alone, who'd died carrying it. My eyes stung behind the mask and I let them, because nobody can see you cry underwater, which is possibly the only good design feature of diving.

Then my light swept the aft bulkhead and stopped.

There was a compartment built into the hull itself—a false space, welded steel, seamless unless you knew to look, and I knew to look because there, scratched beside it at eye height, was the barred N. My mother's sigil. A signature on a door.

"Okafor," I said. "Come take a look at this."

He finned over, and I watched his light trace the weld lines, and I heard him go quiet in the way that meant his brain was running numbers again. "That's not original construction," he said. "That's an addition. Installed after launch. Somebody built a room inside this ship."

"Somebody with access to a yard," I said. "And a reason."

The hatch was a pressure door, small, rated, real. The wheel spun stiffly and then, with a groan of breaking corrosion, freely. Inside: dry. Actually dry, sealed well enough that three decades of seawater pressure hadn't found a way in. Our lights swept across shelving, and on the shelving sat a strongbox.

Not a ship's safe. Not cargo. A personal strongbox, the kind you buy at a hardware store and trust too much, green steel, dented at one corner. And on the lid, in marker that time hadn't touched because time hadn't gotten in, in handwriting I would have known blindfolded, upside down, in the dark, at the bottom of the ocean:

**BEATRIX.**

Just that. My name. Her letters, square and unhurried, the same hand that had written my lunchbox notes and the tide tables on the galley chalkboard and, eventually, nothing at all.

I don't remember deciding to reach for it. My hands decided. My hands have always been ahead of me.

"Beatrix." Okafor's voice, gently. "Twenty minutes. We're at eighteen."

"I see it."

"What is it?"

I held the box against my chest, felt its weight, its terrible ordinariness. Around us the hold waited with its illegal silver and its lies, and above us the glacier groaned—a long, tectonic complaint that came through the water as vibration, as a hand pressed flat against the hull of the world.

"It's mail," I said.

* * *

We surfaced into weather that had decided, in our absence, to become an event. Okafor ran the recovery deck like a man defusing several bombs at once, getting Junie's gear off, getting mine off, getting the strongbox into a freshwater rinse before the salt could finish what the sea had started. Reyes appeared from somewhere, took one look at the box, and asked no questions, which for Reyes was heroic restraint.

I carried it below to my cabin and sat with it on my knees for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't wait politely for convenient moments. It doesn't schedule itself around tides. It ambushed me right there, sitting cross-legged on a bunk with my mother's name in my hands, and for a while I just breathed and shook and let the *Wren*'s engine noise cover whatever sounds I made, because I'm the captain, and captains cry below decks or not at all.

Then I opened it.

Inside, on top, wrapped in a plastic bag gone brittle with age: papers. Documents. And beneath them, at the bottom, a smaller envelope also bearing my name, unopened, its flap still glued—thirty years of waiting compressed into one rectangle of paper I suddenly could not make myself touch.

The papers on top were cargo manifests. Originals, carbon copies, some typed, some handwritten, with corrections in margins. I read them by lamplight while the tide turned outside and the crew moved overhead, and the longer I read, the colder I got, and not from the dive.

The official sinking report—which I had memorized at sixteen, which I had hated, which I had built a whole life on top of like a house on a bad foundation—said the Meridian carried machine parts, timber, and general freight, total insured value modest, loss attributed to hull failure in heavy weather.

These manifests said otherwise. These manifests said silver, said machinery from a disgraced company, said routing through ports that weren't on any published schedule. And in the margin of the final page, in handwriting I recognized with a lurch that had nothing to do with the sea—my mother's handwriting, younger, sharper—there was a note:

*Manifest filed with Lloyd's does not match actual loading. Someone wanted this ship to sink empty on paper. Ask why.*

Ask why. As if she were leaving me homework. As if she'd known, even then, that she might not be the one to answer.

I sat there while the lamp hissed and the glacier muttered to itself two miles north, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the lawyer came ashore with Kaine's papers. My mother hadn't died chasing treasure. She'd died chasing proof. And the proof was sitting in my lap, contradicting a report signed by a government investigator, implicating people with money and reach, explaining perhaps why she'd been diving alone in impossible water—because she couldn't trust anyone, because the truth had teeth, because—

Because someone had scuttled the Meridian. Deliberately. On paper and probably in fact. And my mother had known.

And now I knew. And Okafor was twenty feet above me, having signed our lives to Vesper Kaine to get here, believing we were salvage crews doing a salvage job. Junie was up there too, humming while she rinsed gear, trusting me with the uncomplicated wholeheartedness of the young. They thought we'd come for a debt. We'd come for a cover-up, and every person on this boat was now standing inside it.

I looked at the sealed envelope with my name on it. I reached for it. Stopped. Reached again. My fingers actually touched the flap, and then the glacier groaned—long, deep, right overhead somehow, though it was miles away—and I flinched, and the moment broke, and I found I wasn't ready. Not yet. Some doors you open in daylight, with witnesses, or never.

So I did the cowardly thing, the protective thing, the thing I was already getting too good at. I repacked the manifests, zipped the bag, slid it under my bunk next to its predecessor, and went up on deck with salt-damp hair and a captain's face.

"Find anything?" Okafor asked.

"Cargo," I said. "Old cargo. Nothing that pays off Kaine's debt."

It was the smoothest lie I'd ever told, and it tasted like the bottom of the harbor.

Junie grinned at me, holding up a thermos. "Coffee's hot, Captain. You look like you saw a ghost down there."

"Something like that," I said, and took the cup, and drank, and said nothing, while under my bunk my mother's letter waited in the dark, patient as ice, for a tide brave enough to open it.
