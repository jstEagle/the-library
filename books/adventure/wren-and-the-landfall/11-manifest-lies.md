## 11. Manifest Lies

The galley lamp had a stutter in it, some fault in the wiring that Okafor kept promising to fix and Junie kept threatening to fix worse. I sat under it with a mug of coffee gone the temperature of the sea outside, and I did not open the strongbox.

That was the plan, anyway. The plan lasted about four minutes, which, for one of my plans, was practically a career.

The strongbox sat on the chart table where it had sat since the second tide, since we'd come up through the black water with it slung in the net bag, since I'd lied to my crew with a straight face and a shrug—"just ballast weights, some old trim plates"—and stowed it below like something ashamed of itself. It was a good box. Brass corners, canvas wrapping gone stiff as sailcloth, a lock that had no business still turning after thirty years under the Meridian. But it had turned. My mother had built things that turned when they were supposed to. It was one of the many things I'd spent a decade not thinking about.

The manifests, though. The manifests weren't in the box.

They'd come up loose in the same recovery bag, rolled in oilcloth, three of them, and I'd told myself I'd read them in the morning, with the crew, properly, like a captain who had nothing to hide. Then morning came and I read the first page alone in the wheelhouse while Junie slept and Okafor stood his watch, and by the time the sun cleared the ice wall I had learned that the Meridian was not a wreck.

The Meridian was a murder. Or the next thing to one.

I sat in the galley now with all three rolls spread flat, weighted at the corners with the salt shaker, a spool of solder, and my own hand, and I read them again, because the first two times I'd been hoping I'd misread them, and the third time I was just punishing myself, which is a family tradition on my mother's side.

Cargo manifests are boring documents. That's the point of them. They exist so that nobody ever has to wonder what was in the hold. And the Meridian's manifests were beautifully boring, right up until they weren't.

The official story—I'd had it recited at me my whole childhood, at the inquiry, in the newspaper clippings, in the careful voices of adults who thought I wasn't listening—was that the Meridian went down in the storm of November '94, overloaded, taking water, driven onto the shoal line and then under. Tragic. Regrettable. Eleven souls. My mother, who had been running survey support out of the same harbor, had spent the next two years of her life trying to prove the loading figures were wrong, and the harbor had spent those same two years deciding she was grief-mad, and then she'd spent the decade after that chasing the wreck itself, and then she'd drowned in the ice above it, and the harbor had stopped even bothering to have an opinion. Silence is the most efficient verdict there is. No appeal process.

The manifests in my hands said she was right.

The tonnage didn't match. Not by a little—by forty tons. The Meridian had cleared port certified for a cargo weight that could not have fit in her hold as configured. Somebody had signed for forty tons of machinery that the hold drawings said didn't exist. And the hold drawings were here too, folded into the third roll, survey copies in my mother's drafting hand, with her little annotation ticks in the margins—the ones she used when she'd measured something herself and didn't like the answer.

Forty tons of nothing, signed for, insured for, and then lost at sea in a storm that, according to the weather log clipped to the manifest—my mother had gotten the harbor master's private log, God knew how, God knew what that cost her—was barely a storm at all. Force six. The Meridian had been built for force ten.

Ships don't sink in force six. Not ships like that. Ships like that sink when somebody opens something, or fails to close something, or loads them wrong on purpose, and then everyone stands around afterward saying *tragic, regrettable, the sea is a hard mistress*, which is what people say when they mean *don't ask*.

My mother had known. That was the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. She hadn't suspected, hadn't chased a rumor. She'd had the numbers. She'd had them for years, and she'd carried them out onto the ice every season, alone, in a boat with a crew of two, because nobody else would go with her and she wouldn't stop. And the last time, the ice had taken her, and the numbers had come home without her, and I had put them in a drawer and not opened the drawer for ten years, because I was twenty-two and grieving and the drawer was easier.

I'd told myself the drawer was respect. It's amazing what you'll tell yourself at twenty-two. It's even more amazing what you'll still believe at thirty-two, if you water it.

* * *

The lamp stuttered. Outside, the ice groaned—that long complaint the glacier made at night, like a house settling, if the house were a mile thick and hated you. We had four tides left. Maybe. Okafor's math was conservative and Okafor's math was usually optimistic.

Footsteps on the ladder. I swept the manifests into the oilcloth and put my elbow on them with the practiced ease of a person who had absolutely nothing under her elbow, and Okafor came down into the galley filling most of it, the way he did.

"Captain." He looked at the chart table, at my elbow, at my face. Okafor had a face that could have read a closed book in the dark. "Coffee's cold."

"It's character-building."

"Mm." He poured himself the dregs anyway, drank them standing, and didn't leave. That was the thing about Okafor. He was a patient man in a profession full of people who weren't, and he used patience the way other people used crowbars. "Junie's got the second dive prepped. Wants to run the hold breach line again before slack water. Kid hasn't slept more than four hours since we came through the gate. Neither have you, by the look."

"I sleep."

"You sleep like a cat. Eyes open, one ear working." He set the mug down. "Something on your mind, Captain?"

There it was. The open door. I could feel the manifests stiff under my elbow, the paper warm from my skin, and I could feel the other thing too—the shape of the truth, and how much it would cost to hand it over. Because that was the real question, the one I'd been circling since the wheelhouse at dawn. Not *what do the manifests say*. I knew what they said. The question was *who do I owe this to*.

My crew had followed me into a glacier. Okafor had signed a debt contract behind my back to keep their wages alive—a betrayal dressed as loyalty, or loyalty dressed as betrayal, I still hadn't decided which way the sentence parsed. Junie had nearly died on the first tide and come up grinning. They had given me their trust the way you give someone your rope in bad water: all of it, no reserve, because a reserve gets people killed.

And the manifests said the wreck we were diving was a crime scene. Which meant every dive we made from here on, we were diving into somebody's cover-up. And the somebodies who'd arranged the cover-up had been powerful enough, thirty years ago, to sink a ship and bury the question. People like that didn't stop being powerful. They got board seats.

If I told the crew, they'd want to do the right thing. That was the danger. The right thing was to surface the manifests, call the authorities, blow the job, void whatever protection the salvage claim gave us, and hand Vesper Kaine the wreck on a plate while we sat in port answering questions from men whose fathers had maybe signed those tonnage figures. The right thing might also get us all killed, depending on how nervous the right people got.

And underneath all of it, the thing I couldn't say out loud even to myself: my mother had died holding this truth, alone, and some stubborn, rotten part of me wanted to keep it that way. Not shared. Not diluted. Mine. Grief does that. It takes the one thing the dead person left you and turns it into a room you don't let anyone into, and then one day you realize you've been living in the hallway for a decade.

"Captain," Okafor said again, quieter.

"I'm working through the cargo figures," I said. "The Meridian's displacement. It'll matter for the lift plan if we go for the hold cargo."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. Okafor looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide—consciously, deliberately, I saw him do it—to accept it. That was somehow worse than if he'd pushed. A crew that stops pushing its captain has either given up on her or started protecting her from herself, and I genuinely did not know which one I was looking at.

"Four tides," he said. "Whatever you're working through, work fast."

He climbed out. The lamp stuttered. I let my breath out slowly and took my elbow off the paper.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told the crew I was going over the lift plan, which had the advantage of being a thing I could actually be seen doing, and I sat in the wheelhouse with the oilcloth bundle and the strongbox and did the thing I'd been avoiding since the second tide.

The lock turned. Of course it turned. It had always been going to turn; my mother had never once in her life made a lock her daughter couldn't open. That was the whole design. She'd built her secrets like she'd built her charts—annotated, addressed, waiting.

Inside: a waxed-canvas roll, a small tin that smelled of camphor after thirty years, and papers. Letters, by the look of them, sealed, addressed in her hand. One said *Beatrix*. I put that one down first, fast, the way you put down something hot, and told myself I'd earn it. The others were copies—more manifests, correspondence fragments, a page of the harbor master's log in the original. And a single sheet, not sealed, just folded, that was a working note in her drafting hand, the margin ticks marching down the side like a pulse.

*Forty tons signed, thirty-one tons manifested, nine tons unaccounted at the insurance filing. The nine tons is the story. Find the nine tons and you find who signed twice.*

Nine tons. Not forty. The forty was the fraud's gross weight; nine tons was what had actually gone into the water that shouldn't have—nine tons of something valuable enough to fake a shipwreck over, heavy enough to need a ship at all, and worth more, presumably, than eleven lives, because someone had done the arithmetic and liked the answer.

I sat in the wheelhouse with the glacier groaning outside the glass and understood, finally, with total clarity, what my mother had been doing out on the ice all those years. She wasn't chasing a wreck. She was chasing a signature. And she'd known, the way I now knew, that the signature belonged to someone who could still reach her. That was why she'd gone alone. Not because no one would come. Because she wouldn't let anyone else carry it.

And I was about to do the same thing. I could feel it happening, the way you feel a squall line building on the horizon—myself, alone, holding the truth, deciding who got hurt by it. She'd at least had the excuse of having no crew. I had five people sleeping thirty feet away who thought we were here for salvage.

I rolled the manifests back into their oilcloth. I looked at the letter with my name on it, and I didn't open it, because I knew—with the same certainty I knew the tide tables—that once I read what my mother had to say to me, I would either burn the whole job down or bury it deeper, and I didn't trust myself yet to choose well. There were three tides of work between me and that letter. I needed them. I needed to be the captain my crew believed in for three more tides before I found out what my mother believed about me.

I stowed the box under the berth, under the spare anchor flukes, in the one locker nobody opened without asking, and I went up on deck.

* * *

The ice field at midnight was a country of its own. Blue-black, cracked into plates, the glacier face a pale wall off the bow with the Meridian somewhere beneath it, holding her secret and ours. Junie had the watch. She stood at the rail with a thermos and her hood up, and when she saw me she didn't ask questions, which was its own kind of mercy. She just poured a cup and handed it over.

"Can't sleep either?" she said.

"Working."

"On the glacier?"

"On the ship."

Junie nodded, solemn as a judge, and looked out at the ice wall. She was twenty-three and she thought I was the best captain on the coast, and every day she thought that was a day I got away with something. "My dad used to say the sea keeps better books than any company," she offered. "Everything gets written down eventually. Just takes it a while to file."

"Your dad sounds like a wise man."

"He was a bookkeeper. It's the same thing." She sipped her coffee. "The ice is talking different tonight. You notice? Lower. Like it's thinking about something."

I listened. Under the usual groan there was something new—a deeper register, a note held longer, the sound of a very large thing shifting its weight. Okafor would call it nothing. Okafor would also quietly move the skiffs to the seaward line at dawn, because Okafor heard everything and dismissed nothing.

"Then we'll listen back," I said.

Junie grinned at me, and I grinned back, and it was almost easy, standing there, almost like the old quiet years—except that under my berth lay nine tons of somebody's arithmetic and a letter I was afraid to open, and the glacier was thinking about something, and in three tides I was going to have to be a person who could look her crew in the eye.

The ice groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere down in the dark, under a hundred feet of blue, the Meridian kept my mother's secret for her.

Three tides. I could carry it that long.

I was almost sure I could.
