## 12. Third Tide: Calving Warning

The glacier woke us at 3:40 in the morning, and it did not say excuse me.

I'd been sleeping in my drysuit again, which Okafor says is a bad habit and Beatrix pretends not to notice because she does the same thing. There's a logic to it. When you're diving tidewater three times a day, the fifteen minutes you save not wrestling back into rubber is fifteen minutes of bottom time, and bottom time on the Meridian is the only currency anybody aboard cares about anymore. So I sleep like a seal, half in the suit, half out of my body, and when the ice groaned I came up out of that half-state already moving, which is how I learned I'd been waiting for this sound without knowing it.

It wasn't a crack. Cracks are sharp. This was a groan, long and low, the sound a whale would make if a whale were two miles wide and had been holding its breath since the last ice age. It came up through the hull of the *Vela* and into the deck plates and into the frame of my bunk and into my teeth, and every hair on my arms stood up under four layers of thermal.

Then it stopped. And the silence after was worse.

* * *

By the time I got up to the wheelhouse, Beatrix was already there with her chart case open and her coffee untouched, which is how you know she's rattled—Beatrix Nakamura's coffee is a religious observance, and an abandoned cup is a flag at half-mast. Okafor stood at the window with binoculars trained on the face of the glacier, which even in the flat gray pre-dawn light was doing something I didn't like, which was nothing. Glaciers aren't supposed to be still. They're supposed to crawl. This one had gone quiet the way a dog goes quiet before it bites.

"Talk to me," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"Serpentine moved," Beatrix said, without turning around. Serpentine was what her mother's charts called the glacier, and the name had stuck to our mouths inside of a day, the way names do when they're old enough. "Half a meter of forward surge sometime in the last six hours. The pressure ridges near the gate have reorganized."

"Reorganized," I repeated. "Like a company."

"Like a fist closing." She finally turned, and she gave me the look she gives people when she's deciding how much truth they can carry. I've been getting more of that look lately. Less of it than Okafor gets, though. That's its own data point, and I'm trying not to take it personally. "The dive window holds. Barely. But the skiff route along the east channel is compromised. We shift to the west approach."

"The west approach has the overhang."

"The west approach has the overhang," she agreed, "and the overhang has been stable for forty years according to these charts, and my mother was never wrong about ice." A pause, small enough that most people would have missed it. "Almost never."

Okafor lowered the binoculars. "Junie, I want you running safety diver today. Not point."

"I'm the best cold-water diver on this boat."

"You're the youngest cold-water diver on this boat, which is a different sentence with the same words in it." He said it gently, but he said it like a man who had already won the argument, which is Okafor's superpower. He wins arguments before other people know they're happening. "You watch the ice face. You call anything bigger than a breadbox detaching. You do not penetrate today."

"That's—"

"That's the job. Take it or sulk about it, both are fine by me."

I took it. I sulked about it too, quietly, into my second cup of coffee, while the *Vela* picked her way west through brash ice that crunched against the hull like someone eating crackers just behind your ear.

* * *

The overhang was worse in person than on the chart.

Picture a shelf of blue-gray ice the size of a church roof, jutting thirty feet out over black water, and picture your dive skiff—the skiff, mind, not the boat, the little aluminum egg that carries you and your tanks and your one fragile human life—sliding directly underneath it because the west approach leaves no other lane. That was my morning. I sat in the bow with a borrowed spotting scope and watched meltwater drip off that shelf in ropes, each drop hitting the surface with a sound like a finger snap, and I counted the snaps, because counting is what I do instead of panicking. Four hundred and eleven snaps before the first real event.

The event started as a tick. Then a hiss. Then a sound like the whole sky clearing its throat, and the base of the serac fifty yards up-glacier from the overhang let go.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only thing I own outright: I screamed. Not words. Just volume. And then training took over, the boring repetitive gift that Okafor beats into all of us during the slow months, and I was calling it—"Calve! Calve! East side, two hundred tons, give or take!"—while Beatrix put the skiff hard over and Okafor hauled the working divers' tag line in hand over hand like the rope owed him money.

The berg hit the water maybe sixty feet astern of where we'd been holding station. The wave it threw lifted the skiff like a toy in a bathtub and slammed it down again, and we rode it out sideways, all five of us drenched and deafened and alive, while the water around us went white with churned ice and the air filled with a smell I will never forget—glacial flour and ancient compressed air, the smell of time itself getting broken open.

When the surface settled enough to see faces, I did a head count out of pure animal reflex. Beatrix. Okafor. Reyes. Hollis. Me. Five.

Five.

We were supposed to be six.

* * *

Hollis didn't come back to the wreck site that afternoon.

That's not dramatic phrasing. He sat in the skiff for the remainder of the aborted dive window with his arms wrapped around his knees, and then he climbed aboard the *Vela*, and then he went to the aft cabin and closed the door, and when Okafor went to check on him at dusk, Hollis was packing.

Not frantically. That's what got me, watching through the doorway gap. He was packing the way you pack at the end of a season—neat rolls, gear sorted, dive log squared away. A man packing to leave a job, not fleeing a war. Somehow that was scarier.

"I'll work the transit home," Hollis said. "Engine room, galley, whatever you need. But I'm not going back in that water, and I'm not going back under that ice, and I'd rather say so now than at the worst possible moment later."

"Hollis—"

"No." He held up a hand, and his voice cracked on the word, and I saw that his hands were shaking, fine and constant, like tuning forks. "Three days ago I told myself the Meridian was just a wreck. Wrecks are dead things. Dead things can't hurt you. But that glacier isn't dead, Okafor. That glacier is *alive*, and it's angry, and it's got our names written somewhere in all that ice, and I heard mine this morning. Clear as a bell."

Okafor sat down on the opposite bunk, unhurried, the way he does everything. I stayed in the doorway. Eavesdropping felt justified. My heart rate was involved.

"Do you know what I did before I crewed salvage?" Okafor asked.

"Ran tramp freighters. You've told us."

"I ran away from tramp freighters. There's a difference." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Third year, my captain pushed a weather window he shouldn't have, because the cargo was worth it and the schedule said so. We lost a man overboard in the Coral Sea. Never found him. And I decided right then that I would never again be on a boat where the schedule mattered more than the people." He looked at Hollis steadily. "So hear me clearly: if you want off, I will put you ashore at the fuel cache myself, and I will shake your hand, and there will be no shame attached. That offer is real. But I want you to make the choice with the full math, because I don't think you have it yet."

"What math?"

"The math that says we're not diving tomorrow either way." Okafor's voice stayed level, almost conversational, which somehow made it land harder. "Serpentine surged. The fleet is staging at the gate. If we pull out now, Kaine's people take the Meridian uncontested, and everything Beatrix's mother died chasing—and everything Beatrix is chasing, which I suspect is the same thing—goes into a corporate hold forever. And you and I go home and spend the rest of our lives knowing we were warm and dry and safe when it happened." He paused. "Versus: three tides left. Three. We dive smart, we run shallow profiles, nobody penetrates alone, and when the clock runs out, win or lose, we leave together with the story intact."

"And if the calving comes early?"

"Then the math changes, and I'll tell you first. Before Beatrix. Before anyone." He put out his hand. "That's the deal. Full information, always, from here to the end. You have my word on it."

Hollis looked at the hand for a long time. Through the doorway, I found myself holding my breath, which was stupid, because I needed it for other things.

"The overhang," Hollis said finally. "If we go west again, I want a spotter on the ice face every second, and I want a hard abort signal everyone agrees on, and I want it drilled until we're bored of it."

"Done."

"And if I change my mind mid-dive, nobody argues."

"Nobody argues."

Hollis shook the hand. And then, because he was Hollis, he ruined the moment completely by saying, "Your word better be worth more than this boat's bilge pump," and Okafor laughed, and the shaking in Hollis's hands slowly stilled, and I slipped back down the passageway feeling like I'd witnessed something I hadn't earned.

* * *

We drilled the abort signal until midnight. Three short blasts on the skiff horn means everyone drops tools and surfaces, no questions, no heroics. We ran it nine times. By the seventh repetition Hollis was calling the drill himself, barking it like he'd invented it, and Beatrix stood at the rail watching him with an expression I couldn't read—something between gratitude and grief, as if she were watching a man decide to stay and knowing exactly what it would cost him to have decided.

Later, I found her alone at the stern, looking north at the glacier. In the dark it glowed faintly, that deep glacial blue that seems lit from inside, like something buried and breathing.

"Three tides," I said.

"Three tides." She didn't look at me. "My mother's notes say the Meridian's hold was sealed from the inside, Junie. Did you know that? Sealed from the inside. Somebody wanted that ship shut, and locked, and forgotten." Her breath fogged. "And now the ice wants it shut too. Everybody keeps telling that ship to stay dead."

"Maybe it should listen," I said, before my brain could stop my mouth.

She turned and looked at me then, and for a second I thought I'd crossed some line, but what was in her face wasn't anger. It was exhaustion, deep and old, the kind you could date like tree rings.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "Maybe it should."

And that scared me more than the calving did—not the glacier, not the fleet, not the shrinking clock, but the sound of Beatrix Nakamura agreeing with the idea that their whole reason for being here might be a door best left closed. Because captains who start thinking like that, in my limited experience, stop calculating risk and start calculating something else entirely.

Out past the anchor line, deep under the dark water, something shifted against the hull—a long, slow scrape, like a hand testing a lock.

Nobody mentioned it at breakfast.
