## 13. Kaine's Confession, Half Of It

I have boarded a hundred vessels in my career—hostile takeovers, hostile harbors, one memorable hostile wedding—and I can tell you that the hardest part is never the guns. The guns are simple. The hard part is the moment your boot hits their deck and you become, briefly and irreversibly, a guest.

The *Wren* was not a large ship. She'd been built for quiet work in shallow water, the kind of salvage boat that lives its whole life just under the notice of people like me, which is precisely why Nakamura had kept her that way for ten years while I built a fleet. Standing on her deck, I felt like a woman who had broken into a house only to find it small, warm, and better kept than her own.

Okafor met me at the rail with a shotgun held in the universal language of *we are being polite about this*. Behind him stood the diver—the young one, Junie, all elbows and watchful eyes—and a wiry engineer whose name I had never bothered to learn because names are leverage and I wasn't spending any yet.

"Flag of truce," I said, and held up both hands, empty. "You have my word."

"Your word," Okafor said.

"Yes."

"From Vesper Kaine."

"I'm told it's worth something. Less than it used to be, but something."

He considered me the way a man considers weather he doesn't like but has decided to tolerate. Then he stepped aside. "Captain's in the wheelhouse. She knows you're coming."

Of course she did. Beatrix Nakamura had known I was coming since before I knew it myself. That was always the trouble with her family.

* * *

The wheelhouse smelled of diesel, coffee gone too long on the burner, and old paper. Charts everywhere—not the clean digital overlays my fleet used, but paper, soft at the folds, annotated in three generations of handwriting. I recognized one of those hands instantly and had to stand very still for a moment until the feeling passed through me like cold water through a wetsuit.

Beatrix sat at the chart table with a mug she did not offer to refill. She looked tired in the specific way of someone who has been diving deep and sleeping worse, and there was a bruise along her jaw from the growler incident—I'd heard about it on the fleet channel, everyone had; her crew threading an ice gate by improvisation was already becoming the kind of story crews tell each other, and stories are currency too, though she didn't seem to know it.

"You're either brave or stupid," she said.

"Both. It's how I made my money."

"Sit down before you fall down, then. The deck's ice."

I sat. Across the chart table, between us, lay a folded oilcloth the size of a breadbox, and I would have bet everything I still owned that the strongbox was under it. Beatrix watched me look at it and did not move it away, which was its own kind of message: *I know what you came for, and I want you to know I know.*

"You've been inside the hold," I said.

"One tide's worth of inside."

"And?"

"And it's my mother's wreck, Ms. Kaine. You'll forgive me if I don't narrate."

Her mother. There it was, laid on the table between us like a third person. I had come aboard with a speech prepared—I prepare speeches the way other people prepare meals, carefully and with more butter than necessary—but hearing that name in this room, surrounded by these charts, rearranged something in me. So instead of the speech I said:

"Mara drew that route."

Beatrix went very still. Not the stillness of surprise. The stillness of a woman deciding whether to spend a bullet.

"What route," she said.

"The one your diver found. The gate. The dogleg through the shoal field where every official survey says there's no passage." I nodded at the charts pinned above the wheel. "There's no passage because Mara never filed it. She kept it in her head and in her hand, and she gave it to exactly two people in her life. One of them is dead. The other one is you, whether you remember receiving it or not."

"We found the gate ourselves."

"You found it because she left it for you to find. There's a difference, and the difference matters." I leaned back. The bench creaked. Everything on this boat creaked; it was a ship that talked to itself, honest about its age in a way my fleet never was. "May I tell you a story, Captain? A true one. I'll even leave out the parts that flatter me, which will make it short."

"Make it anything you want. I'm not signing anything."

"There's nothing to sign. That's rather the point."

* * *

Here is what the industry record says, and the record is wrong, because I helped write it: Vesper Kaine and Mara Nakamura were rivals. Competitors. Two captains chasing the same wrecks up and down the coast, trading salvos in the trade papers, each building the legend that made the other legible. Rivals make good copy. Rivals keep the story simple.

What we actually were, for eleven years, was partners.

Not on paper—Mara didn't believe in paper, which should have been my first warning about how she'd treat a contract, including mine. Partners in the older sense. We ran two boats as one operation. My money, her instinct. My permits, her nerve. When the *Corvina* went down off the point in '09 with a hold full of pre-war instruments, it was Mara who read the current wrong on purpose—deliberately, gloriously wrong—to get us into water no licensed operator could touch, and it was me who sold the recovery so cleanly that three museums bid against each other. We split it fifty-fifty, sitting on the deck of the *Wren*, eating terrible noodles out of the same pot, laughing about the lawyers we were making rich.

I loved her. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only apology I have left. Not the way the trade papers would eventually insinuate, and not the way Beatrix was probably imagining right now with her jaw set like that. I loved her the way you love the only person who ever beat you fairly and then bought you dinner after. She was the best captain I ever saw and the worst business partner in maritime history, and I chose her twice a year for eleven years, every time she asked me to choose again.

Then came the Meridian.

Everyone in the trade knew the story: passenger-cargo hybrid, lost in the narrows forty years back, official report blamed ice and error, cargo manifest sealed by the insurers, wreck location disputed and then, conveniently, forgotten. Mara found the location. Of course she did—she found things the way water finds low ground. And when she brought me the coordinates, I did what I had spent a decade doing: I ran the numbers. Insurance value, recovery cost, legal exposure, the sealed manifest and what unsealing it would cost in court fees alone.

And I said no.

"Not worth it," I told her. "Too deep, too sealed, too litigated. Pick another one, Mara. There are a hundred wrecks. This one's a lawsuit with barnacles."

She looked at me for a long time. I have replayed that look for twenty years and I still can't fully inventory it. Disappointment, yes. But also something like pity, and underneath the pity, fear—which I mistook, then, for her being dramatic. Mara was never dramatic. That was my second mistake.

"That's not what's down there," she said.

"It's exactly what's down there. It's what's down there on every wreck. Money and paperwork."

"No." She rolled up the coordinates. "It's what they *say* is down there. And what they say is a lie, Vesper. I've read the report four times. The Meridian didn't hit ice. Ice doesn't open a hull from the inside."

I laughed. I actually laughed, because the alternative was listening, and listening to Mara about the Meridian meant following her somewhere my ledgers couldn't go. "Inside out. Sure. Ghosts, then."

"Ghosts with a scuttling order," she said quietly, and that was the last thing she ever said to me about it, because I told her if she went after the Meridian she'd do it without me, without my money, without my name on the permits—and she said, "Understood," in that flat voice she used when she'd already stopped asking, and she walked up my gangway and out of my life, and eighteen months later she walked into tidewater and didn't walk out.

That is the story. Half of it.

* * *

Beatrix listened without interrupting, which unnerved me more than shouting would have. When I finished, she turned her mug a slow quarter-turn on the table, considering.

"You're telling me you sent her to her death."

"I'm telling you I refused to go with her, and she went anyway, and I've spent twenty years not knowing which of those sentences is the heavier one." I heard my own voice do something it almost never does on a negotiation—waver—and I hated it, and I let it stand, because a certain kind of honesty buys more than a polished lie. "But that's not why I'm here."

"No. You're here for the strongbox."

"I'm here to offer you a trade, and the strongbox is the currency, yes." I put my hands flat on the table, palms down, the posture of a woman surrendering weapons she hadn't brought. "Here are the terms. You give me the box. Unopened. In exchange, I give you the rest of the story—the half I've been carrying since the funeral. What Mara found before she died. What she sent me. Why the official report is wrong, and who wanted it wrong, and what I've spent two decades and most of a fortune making sure stays buried—because burying it was the only way to keep people alive, including, as it turns out, you."

Something moved behind her eyes. Interest, maybe. Or the hunter's flicker of a woman smelling blood in the water and hating herself for smelling it.

"You're saying you know what killed her."

"I'm saying I know what she was afraid of, and it wasn't the glacier."

The wheelhouse ticked around us. Out on the ice, the light was going long and gold, the hour when the glacier looks almost gentle, which is the cruelest thing about it. Somewhere below decks, her crew was moving—Junie's laugh, quickly smothered; the engineer's radio murmuring weather. A living boat. I envied it, God help me.

"And if I refuse?" Beatrix said.

"Then tomorrow we both dive on the sixth tide, your crew and my fleet, and the glacier decides, and whatever's in that box goes down with everything else. You'll have your truth, Captain—or the shape of it, water-damaged, half-drowned, the way truths come up from that depth. And I'll have nothing, which is what I deserve." I smiled, and it hurt. "Or you give me the box, and I give you twenty years of answers tonight, over coffee, before the light goes. One or the other. I didn't come all this way to haggle."

She stood. Slowly. She crossed to the chart rack and took down one of the old surveys—her mother's, by the wear pattern—and laid it on the table between us, and I saw the annotations in the margins, and my chest did something complicated, because some of that handwriting I recognized from letters Mara used to send me, back when we sent letters.

"You want to know what I found in the hold?" Beatrix said. "A strongbox with my name on it. My mother packed it, addressed it, and sank it, Ms. Kaine. She chose the bottom of the sea over delivering it. Whatever's in there, she decided I was safer not reading it." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "And now you're offering to trade me answers for it. Answers from the woman who refused to follow her. Do you understand why I'm having trouble with the arithmetic?"

"She didn't sink it to hide it from you. She sank it to hide it from *them*. There's a difference—"

"There's always a difference with you. There's a difference between rival and partner, between refusing and sending, between buried and drowned. You talk in differences." She put her hand flat on the oilcloth, over the box, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you weren't sure was tame. "My mother trusted you once. I've read enough of her margins to know that. And you priced her out. So here's my counteroffer, and it's the only one: you tell me your half of the story for free. Right now. No box, no trade. You give it because keeping it has been costing you, and I'm not cruel enough to charge a woman for putting down weight."

For a moment—one long, glacial moment—I genuinely considered it. Twenty years of carrying it, and here was a girl with her mother's stubborn chin offering to take the weight for free. That's the trap of honesty: once you start paying it out, the whole vault wants to empty.

But the rest of the story isn't mine alone to give. That's the part Beatrix couldn't know, the part that makes the box non-negotiable, the part that has kept me awake in good hotels for two decades: Mara didn't just send me coordinates before she died. She sent me a page. And the page names a third party, and the third party is still alive, still respectable, still sitting on boards and endowing chairs—and still, I am quite sure, watching this glacier the way I am, for reasons that have nothing to do with cargo.

If I tell Beatrix the name without the box in my hand, she does what her mother would do: she goes loud. And loud gets people killed. It got Mara killed. I've done the math on that too, and unlike my ledgers, this one balances.

"No," I said.

Beatrix nodded slowly, unsurprised, and I understood that I had just failed a test she'd designed the moment I walked aboard—that the free-story offer had been a probe, and my refusal had confirmed whatever she'd suspected. She picked up the oilcloth-wrapped box and stowed it in a locker behind the helm, locking it, pocketing the key.

"Then we dive the sixth tide," she said. "Your fleet and my crew. May the best captain win."

"Your mother used to say that."

"I know," Beatrix said. "I've been reading her margins."

I let myself out. Okafor walked me to the rail with the shotgun still in its polite language, and as I dropped into my tender and turned back toward the fleet, I looked up at the wheelhouse window and saw her silhouette against the gold light, holding something small in her hand—a key, or a page, or a decision.

Behind us all, deep in the ice, the Meridian waited with its lies, and the glacier groaned once, long and low, like something clearing its throat before it speaks.

Three tides left. Maybe fewer. And somewhere out there, the third party I haven't named is already moving.
