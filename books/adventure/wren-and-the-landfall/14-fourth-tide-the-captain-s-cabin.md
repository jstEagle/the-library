## 14. Fourth Tide: The Captain's Cabin

The tide table said we had four hours and eleven minutes. The glacier said nothing at all, which I'd learned to distrust more than anything it actually said.

I sat on the deck of the *Wren* in the blue hour before dawn, pulling on my drysuit with the particular resentment of a woman who has done this ten thousand times and never once enjoyed it. Drysuits are not clothing. They are arguments you have with your own body, and the body always wins eventually.

"You're up early," Okafor said, materializing beside me with two mugs of coffee. He handed me one without asking if I wanted it. Ten years of crewing together means you stop asking questions whose answers are already known.

"I didn't sleep."

"Shocking. You've been sleeping so well since a corporate lawyer showed up on our dock with a knife made of paperwork."

"Okafor."

"Captain." He drank his coffee and looked out at the ice gate, which glowed faintly in the pre-dawn like something breathing. "You're going to tell me where you're diving today, or you're going to lie about it, and either way I'm going to stand on this deck and watch you go down, so let's skip to the part where you tell me."

I had been planning to lie about it. That's the thing about me that people who read my mother's obituary never understood—she was the honest one, the loud one, the woman who told salvage boards exactly what she thought of them in language that got her blacklisted from three harbors. I'm the quiet one. I keep things. I've kept things so long some of them have grown roots.

"The captain's cabin," I said. "Deep penetration. Aft section, below the third deck."

Okafor was quiet for a moment. The coffee steamed between us.

"That's a solo run," he said. "Or near enough. Line time alone past the debris field is how divers die, Beatrix. It's how *she* died."

"It's not how she died." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "She drowned in open water, forty meters from her skiff, with a full tank. We've been over this."

"We've been over this every year for ten years, and every year you say it a little quieter." He set his mug down on the rail. "Take Junie."

"She's not rated for penetration."

"Nobody on this boat is rated for what you're about to do. Take Junie anyway. She holds line better than anyone I've seen, including you, and she'll do it without asking why, which is either her best quality or the reason I worry about her most." He paused. "And because if you go down there and find whatever it is you're clearly expecting to find, you shouldn't be alone when you come back up."

I wanted to argue. I want to be clear about that—I had arguments loaded and ready, good ones, the kind that sound reasonable at six in the morning. But Okafor had watched me bury my mother and then watch me spend a decade building a life designed so that no one would ever again ask me to go looking for anything. He knew what today was before I did.

"Four hours," I said finally. "Hard out at three-forty regardless of what I've found."

"Regardless of what you've found," he agreed, and we both pretended that was a promise either of us believed in.

* * *

The Meridian's aft section is a cathedral that someone dropped into cold water and forgot about. On the fourth tide, with the silt settled and our lights cutting cones through water the color of old glass, it felt less like a wreck than like a held breath.

Junie swam point on the line, small and precise, her fins moving in that economical rhythm she'd developed somewhere before we ever met her. She'd tied off at the debris field exactly as briefed, checked my gauges twice without being asked, and now hung in the water ahead of me, waiting for my signal with the patience of someone who understood that inside a wreck, hurry is just another word for dying.

I gave it. We moved aft.

The corridor to the captain's cabin runs along the port side, and forty years underwater has turned it into something between a hallway and a throat. Overhead panels hang down like shed skin. A handrail, furred white with bacterial mat, guides the way the way it once guided sailors—and probably guided them badly, given how the ship ended up. Junie's light swept ahead of mine, steady, disciplined, touching each doorway before we passed it. Good habits. Someone had trained her right, and I allowed myself a private, unearned pride about that.

At the second junction she stopped and pointed. Her light rested on a stenciled sign half-eaten by rust: **CAPTAIN — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**.

I'd been here before. Not here-here—the cabin itself I'd never entered—but this corridor, twenty years ago, holding my mother's backup light while she surveyed the outer hull, both of us breathing hard through our regulators and laughing about nothing, because that's what laughing sounds like underwater. I remembered the sign. I remembered thinking *authorized personnel only* was funny, because my mother had never once in her life waited for authorization.

My eyes were burning behind the mask. At depth, you can't always tell grief from mask squeeze. I equalized and went through the door.

The cabin had collapsed inward on its port side, the hull plating folded like paper, but the starboard bulkhead still stood, and against it, miraculously, stood the desk. Bolted down, as ship's desks are, which is the only reason it hadn't wandered off into the dark decades ago. The drawers had long since rotted open or been rifled by anything curious enough to swim in—but the desk wasn't what I'd come for.

Above the desk, bolted to the overhead, was a chart tube. Brass caps. Sealed threads.

Salvage ships don't leave chart tubes on the overhead. They mount them there because charts are the one thing a captain needs fast and can't afford to lose, and my mother—whoever else she was, whoever she'd been to Vesper Kaine, whatever she'd ordered or obeyed or buried—had been a salvage captain to her bones. If she'd kept a logbook out of the safe, away from the manifest drawer, away from anything a boarding inspector or a rival crew might grab first—

She'd have put it where only another Nakamura would think to look up.

Junie hovered at the doorway, keeping the exit sightline, exactly as briefed. I worked the brass cap loose with a dive knife blade, slowly, feeling the threads give after forty years of corrosion, and the tube exhaled a small silver bloom of trapped air that rose past my face like a question.

Inside: a oilskin-wrapped bundle, and inside that, a logbook bound in canvas, its spine cracked, its pages swollen but whole. My mother's initials were stamped on the cover. **E.N.** Elinor Nakamura. The woman the Coast Guard report called *a licensed operator operating outside permitted waters*. The woman Kaine called, in the one conversation we'd ever had about her, *the best and worst decision I ever made*, and then refused to elaborate on for the remaining ninety minutes of a helicopter ride.

I tucked the book into my chest harness, checked my gas—two-thirds remaining, plenty for the exit—and put my hand on the doorframe to signal Junie.

That's when I saw the safe.

It was set into the deck beneath the desk, mostly buried in silt, its door hanging open. Empty, obviously—every safe on every wreck within a hundred miles of a harbor gets emptied by somebody, legally or otherwise, usually within the first year. But wedged in the hinge gap, visible only from this exact angle, this exact light, was a corner of plastic. A dive slate. And written on the visible corner, in a handwriting I would know anywhere, in grease pencil gone ghostly gray but legible:

**B — if you're reading this, look in the**

The rest disappeared into the silt and the hinge.

I dug. Of course I dug. Forty years of sediment does not move quickly when you're working one-handed with a knife tip while trying to manage your buoyancy in a collapsing compartment, and I dug anyway, tearing at the silt like it owed me money, clouding the water until my own lights bounced back at me in a brown fog. Junie appeared at my shoulder—she must have seen the silt bloom from the junction—and put a steadying hand on my arm.

I shook it off. Dug. Got two fingers under the slate's edge, pulled—

And the slate came free in two pieces. The bottom half stayed in the hinge gap, and the message broke across the fracture line like a bone.

**look in the** — and then nothing. The rest of the sentence had been on the missing piece, which was now somewhere under forty years of silt in a footprint of debris I could search for an hour and not finish searching before my gas ran out.

Junie's hand on my arm again, insistent this time. She pointed at my gauge. I was at half. When had that happened? Time goes strange down there. Grief goes stranger.

I took the two pieces of slate, tucked them with the logbook, and signaled the turn.

* * *

Coming up the line into daylight is supposed to feel like being born. Mostly it feels like being returned merchandise.

I broke the surface inside the ice gate's shadow, spat my reg, and hauled myself onto the dive ladder while Junie de-kitted below me with her usual wordless efficiency. Okafor was waiting at the rail with a towel and an expression he'd been practicing.

"Well?" he said.

"Found the logbook." I stripped my hood. "Mother's personal log. Off the books, hidden separate from the ship's records."

His eyebrows went up. "And?"

"And I haven't read it yet."

"Beatrix."

"There was also a slate. A message. Half of it." I heard my own voice doing the flat thing again and couldn't stop it. "The other half is under a debris field in the aft compartment, and I burned too much gas digging for it, and if you say 'hard out at three-forty' right now I'm going to throw your towel overboard."

He draped the towel over my shoulders instead, which was worse, somehow. Gentler. "The book will keep until you're warm and dry and have eaten something. That's not a suggestion, Captain. That's dive medicine. Hypothermic hands drop important documents into the ocean, and then where are we?"

He was right, which was annoying, so I didn't say anything. Below us, Junie surfaced and grinned up at me through the water streaming off her face, young and alive and completely unaware that she'd just spent ninety minutes watching me come apart in slow motion forty meters down.

"Did you see the safe?" she asked, climbing the ladder. "What was—"

"Later," Okafor said, and she caught something in his voice and let it go, which is a skill most people twice her age haven't managed.

* * *

I lasted forty minutes. I ate half a tin of soup, changed into dry clothes, paced the length of the pilothouse twice, and then locked the cabin door—which fooled no one, least of all me—and opened the logbook under the lamp.

The early pages were ordinary, and that ordinariness nearly undid me. Fuel logs. Weather notes. A running complaint about a charter client who chewed sunflower seeds on her dive deck. My mother's handwriting marched across the pages in quick, slanted strokes, impatient even on paper, and reading it was like hearing a voice I'd convinced myself I'd forgotten. There was a margin sketch of a seal she'd named Commissioner. There was a grocery list. There was, on a Tuesday in March, a single line: *B lost a tooth today, demanded payment in ice cream, negotiated up to two.* I was seven years old. I have no memory of the tooth. She wrote it down like it mattered, because to her it did, and I sat in a locked cabin on a boat full of people who trusted me and cried over a grocery list.

Then, three weeks before the sinking, the entries changed.

The handwriting tightened. Shorter entries. Dates without weather. And on the fourteenth of April, a page that stopped me cold:

*Saw the manifests tonight. Numbers don't work. Cargo listed as machine parts weighs wrong, sits wrong, drafts wrong. Either someone at the loading office can't do arithmetic or the Meridian is carrying something nobody wants found. Asked Harrow directly. He smiled at me. Nobody smiles like that unless they think you're already dead.*

Harrow. I knew the name from the official report—Meridian's chief officer, lost with the ship, posthumously cleared of any wrongdoing. Cleared by whom? By the same office that had signed off on cargo manifests listing machine parts that didn't weigh like machine parts?

I turned pages faster. The next entry, two days later:

*Told Kaine. She says tow the line, take the contract, keep my head down, we can't fight the underwriters and the shipping company both. She's probably right. She's always probably right. But those holds are going to the bottom with something aboard that someone paid a great deal of money to make disappear, and everyone on that crew trusts me to bring them home, and I don't know yet which of those things I am.*

Kaine. Vesper Kaine had known. Had been *consulted*. My mother had gone to her oldest friend, her partner, her rival-in-all-things-but-this, and Kaine had told her to keep her head down.

I should have stopped reading. Every instinct I've built in ten years of staying invisible said stop, close the book, put it in the strongbox, deal with it on land, deal with it never. Instead I turned the page.

The final entry was dated the day before the Meridian went down. The handwriting had gone very small and very controlled, the way hers did when she was furious enough to be calm.

*Order came through tonight. Scuttle order, dressed up as an insurance survey recommendation. Signed authority above Harrow. I have until tomorrow's tide. I can refuse and they'll replace me and sink her anyway with a crew that doesn't know, or I can take her out myself, get my people clear in the boats, and put her down where I choose instead of where they choose. Deep water. Cold. Where whatever's in those holds stays honest.*

*I won't write the name here. Paper floats.*

*If B ever reads this: I'm sorry. It was the only version of this story where everyone comes home. Not the only true version. The only survivable one.*

*The name is*

The name is where the water damage began.

Not torn—water-damaged, ink bloomed into a gray shadow shaped almost like letters, almost like a word, sitting at the end of the page like a person standing just beyond lamplight. I held the page to the lamp. Tilted it. Tilted it back. The blot resolved into shapes that might have been four letters, might have been five, might have been a smear of forty-year-old seawater and nothing more.

Paper floats, she'd written. So she'd kept the name off the page. Which meant the name was somewhere else—in the other half of the slate, under the debris field; in the strongbox addressed to me that we'd found yesterday and I hadn't opened; in the head of a dead woman who had trusted her daughter to follow a trail she'd laid twenty years in advance, annotated in a child's handwriting, through a glacier that was trying to kill us all.

A knock at the cabin door. Two knocks, then a pause, then one—Junie's knock, the one she'd invented herself and thought nobody noticed.

"Captain?" Her voice was careful. "There's a boat coming through the gate. Running no lights. Okafor says—it's fleet colors. It's Kaine's tender, and she's asking to come alongside under a truce flag."

I closed the logbook on the blurred name and sat in the dark for one breath, two, listening to the glacier groan somewhere out in the black like a door opening in a house where everyone swore they were alone.

"Tell her," I said, "she can tie off on the starboard side. And Junie—nobody touches the strongbox. Whatever happens tonight, nobody opens that box but me."
