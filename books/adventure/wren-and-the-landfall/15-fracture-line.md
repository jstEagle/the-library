## 15. Fracture Line

I've been running boats for thirty years, and in that time I've learned that a crew doesn't break all at once. It breaks the way ice does—silently, along lines nobody marked, until one morning you step on what you thought was solid deck and it gives way under your boot. You don't hear the crack that killed it. You only hear the one that finally announces it.

The crack that announced ours came at four in the morning, in the galley, over a pot of coffee I'd made too strong because I wasn't sleeping anyway.

Let me back up.

We'd come off the third tide with the skiff dented, Junie's hands shaking so badly she couldn't work her own zippers, and Halvorsen saying out loud what everyone else was thinking: *the glacier is waking up, Captain.* Okafor—that's me, though aboard this boat everyone just says "Okafor" like it's a job title and a diagnosis at once—I talked him down. That's my role. Beatrix decides; I make the deciding survivable. Twenty years of it. Long enough that when she tells me something, I file it as fact and move on to the next problem.

That's the mistake I made. Filing instead of reading.

* * *

It started small, the way these things do. We were cataloguing the hold recovery after the second tide—the strongbox, the crates of manifest folios we'd cut free from the purser's office—and Beatrix took the manifests below to "log them against water damage." Reasonable. Paper that's been underwater forty years turns to porridge if you breathe on it wrong. She said she'd have them inventoried by morning watch.

By morning watch there was no inventory. By the next morning watch there was still no inventory, and Beatrix had developed a new habit: sitting alone in her cabin with a lamp burning past midnight, door shut, which on our boat is less privacy than it sounds because our boat has the soundproofing of a tin whistle. You can hear everything through those bulkheads. What you can't do is make out words. So the whole crew got to lie in their bunks listening to their captain whisper to herself in the dark, and nobody said anything about it, because that's the other thing crews do—they keep score of silences.

Junie noticed first, because Junie notices everything about Beatrix and files none of it cynically. "She's reading them," Junie told me, folding dive line with the mechanical precision of someone keeping her hands busy so her mouth doesn't get ideas. "The manifests. She's not logging them, Okafor. Logging takes an hour. She's been down there three nights."

"Maybe she's thorough."

"She alphabetized the spare parts locker last month when she was stressed. This isn't thorough. This is hiding."

I should have gone to Beatrix right then. Instead I did the loyal thing, which is often just the cowardly thing wearing a better coat. I waited. I told myself the captain was entitled to process whatever she'd found before sharing it. I told myself a lot of things, and the trouble with telling yourself things at sea is there's no one around to tell you to shut up.

* * *

The fourth night, I caught her.

Not dramatically. There was no banging open of doors. I came off mid-watch, cold through, wanting coffee, and the galley lamp was already lit, and Beatrix was at the table with the folios spread out in front of her—not the whole stack, I noticed, not the whole stack at all—a handful of pages, maybe eight, and the rest conspicuously absent from where the rest should have been.

She looked up when I came in. And here's the thing I'll carry: she didn't look guilty. She looked tired, and guarded, and like someone who had been waiting for this conversation and rehearsing ways to avoid it.

"Coffee's fresh," she said.

"You've had those folios five days."

"I know."

"The crew's asking."

"I know that too." She squared the pages in front of her, aligned the edges, a little ceremony of control. "They're water-damaged, Okafor. Half of them are illegible. I'm working through them."

I poured coffee I didn't want, because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't reaching across the table. "Then you won't mind showing me what's legible."

A pause. On our boat, pauses are paragraphs. This one ran long enough to have chapters.

"It's not ready," she said.

"Not ready." I set my mug down. "Beatrix. It's cargo paperwork from a shipwreck. It doesn't ripen."

"It's not about the paperwork."

"No," I agreed. "That's exactly my point."

She looked at me then with the expression she gets when she's deciding how much truth a person can carry without dropping it—she's been doing that since her mother died, deciding for other people what they could bear, and I've let her do it to me for twenty years because mostly her arithmetic is good. Mostly.

"What did they say?" I asked quietly. "The manifests."

"They said the Meridian was carrying half the tonnage she was registered to carry."

I waited.

"And her ballast was wrong," Beatrix went on, voice flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who has read a sentence so many times it's stopped meaning anything. "Wrong in a specific way. Stone and scrap iron packed where fuel bunkers should be. She was weighted to go down fast and sit level on the bottom. That's not an accident profile, Okafor. That's a scuttling profile."

The galley heater ticked. Somewhere forward, the hull popped against the ice, the glacier talking in its sleep.

"Who scuttled her?"

"That's what I'm trying to find out."

"Is it." I sat down across from her. "Because from where I'm standing, it looks like you already found out something, and you've been sitting on it for five days while the rest of us dive into a calving glacier on your say-so."

Her jaw tightened. "If I tell the crew the wreck might have been sunk deliberately—"

"Then they know what they're diving toward."

"—then they start diving scared," she finished. "Scared divers die, Okafor. You know that. I needed to understand it first. I needed to know if it was real before I put it on them."

"And if it was real? What was the plan? Carry it alone all the way home and bury it in a drawer next to everything else you carry alone?"

"That's not fair."

"No," I said. "It isn't. Fair would have been telling us on day one."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of the evening: I was angry, but the anger was only the top layer. Underneath it was something worse. Her mother had done the same thing—Elena Nakamura carried secrets the way other captains carry ballast, low and centered and never discussed—and I watched Beatrix doing it now, same posture, same flat voice, and I thought: *we sailed ten years to get away from this exact woman and she's been dead in the water the whole time, living in her daughter's spine.*

Maybe I said some version of that out loud. I honestly don't remember whether I said it or only thought it loudly enough to count.

What I remember clearly is the sound of the bench creaking aft of the galley doorway, and turning, and finding the whole crew standing there in the passage like a jury that had been sworn in without being asked.

* * *

Junie had her arms crossed, which she does when she's trying to look older than she is. Halvorsen was leaning on the doorframe with his face carefully empty, which from Halvorsen is a shout. And Priya—quiet Priya, who steers like she's defusing something—was looking at Beatrix with an expression I'd never seen on her, and it took me a moment to place it, because I'd never seen it aimed at Beatrix before.

It was doubt.

"How much," Halvorsen said, "of the last five days have we been diving blind?"

Beatrix stood. Slowly. A captain standing up in front of her crew. "You've been diving exactly what I told you. The route, the tides, the risks—none of that changed."

"But the why did," Junie said. Her voice cracked on it, young and furious. "You knew the ship might have been *sunk*, and you let us go down on it anyway without—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "I trusted you."

"Junie—"

"No. I *idolized* you. That's different, and it's worse, because idolizing means I wasn't watching. I wasn't checking your math. I just believed you, because you were the person who never lied to us." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "And the whole time you were in here every night reading papers you didn't share, and we were lying in our bunks listening to you whisper."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight. Beatrix looked at each of them in turn, and I watched her do the arithmetic again—how much truth per person, what load each hull could take—and I saw the moment she decided to keep calculating, and I understood that we were going to lose her if she did.

"Tell them," I said. Not angry now. Just tired. "All of it. Whatever's left. Because they're going to follow you into that ice two more times, Beatrix, and they deserve to know whether they're following a captain or a ghost."

Halvorsen shifted. "There's more, isn't there. There's something else she hasn't said."

Everyone looked at Beatrix.

And Beatrix Nakamura—who has stared down debt collectors and corporate tenders and a growler the size of a church—looked at her crew of four and could not make her mouth work.

"There's a logbook," she said finally. "In the captain's cabin. My mother's logbook. The manifests point to it. The scuttle order will be in there, and the name of whoever gave it." A breath. "I'm diving for it on the fourth tide. Alone if I have to."

"You don't have to," Priya said quietly. "That was never the question." She pushed off the doorframe. "The question is what else you're not saying. And I think we both know there's something, because you just told us the *least* dangerous secret you had."

Beatrix said nothing.

Priya nodded slowly, like a verdict. "Right," she said. "Okay." And she walked aft to the wheelhouse, and one by one the others peeled away behind her—Junie last, pausing in the doorway with red eyes and a look I couldn't translate, though I suspect it meant *come back to us*—until it was just the two of us and the cold coffee and the lamp.

"You knew that would happen," Beatrix said.

"I hoped it wouldn't. I knew it might." I gathered the folio pages, squared them, set them in front of her. "Here's the thing about fault lines, Captain. They were always there. Your mother drew them. You inherited them. But cracks only open when there's pressure, and we've got two tides of pressure left, and a fleet coming through that gate, and a glacier that wants us all dead." I stood. "So whatever you're still holding—whatever Priya smelled—you've got until morning watch to decide whether this crew follows you because they trust you or because the ice leaves them no choice. One of those holds a boat together. The other one just delays the sinking."

I got to the doorway before she spoke.

"Okafor." Her voice was rough. "What if the name in that logbook is someone I love?"

I stood there with my hand on the frame, and I thought about Elena, and about all the years we'd spent not talking about her, and I gave the only answer I had.

"Then you'll finally be carrying it with company."

* * *

Morning watch came gray and windless, the glacier holding its breath. I found Beatrix already at the helm, charts out, the strongbox sitting on the chart table between her hands like a third party to the negotiation. She'd been up all night—I could see it in her—but her shoulders were square, and when the crew filed in for the briefing, she met their eyes one at a time, no arithmetic in her face at last.

Or almost none. There was still one door behind her eyes, shut, and I'd learned to recognize it.

"We dive the captain's cabin on the fourth tide," she said. "All of us. No more solo reads, no more closed doors. Everything I find, you see the same hour I find it." She paused. "I owe you that apology, and I owe you the truth, and I'm going to pay both. Starting now."

It was a good speech. It landed. Junie's chin came up; Halvorsen grunted, which from him is absolution. Even Priya nodded once, slow, the frost thawing by degrees.

But I'd been watching Beatrix for twenty years, and as she turned to the chart and began tracing the route to the cabin, her hand passed over the strongbox—rested on it, briefly, the way you'd touch a wound to check if it's still bleeding—and I saw that the box hadn't moved from where it sat the night before.

Unopened. Still sealed. Still hers.

Whatever was in there, she hadn't shared it with the crew. She hadn't even opened it herself.

And out beyond the ice gate, faint on the horizon like a rumor, a shape was moving where no shape should be—too early, too many masts, moving with the tide instead of against it.

The fleet wasn't waiting for the fifth tide anymore.

Neither, it seemed, was anyone else.
