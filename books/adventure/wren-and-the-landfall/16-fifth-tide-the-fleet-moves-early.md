## 16. Fifth Tide: The Fleet Moves Early

I was on ice watch when the fleet came through, which meant I was the first to see it and, for about ninety seconds, the only one who believed it.

The trick with watching a glacier is that it's always doing something. It groans. It ticks. It sheds little sighs of meltwater that sound like footsteps if you want them to be footsteps. So when I heard engines—actual engines, plural, throttling down in the channel where no sane captain would put an engine—I told myself it was the ice settling, and then I counted three separate engine notes, and then I stopped lying to myself and ran for the wheelhouse.

"Beatrix." I came through the door at a speed that made Okafor look up from his coffee like I'd interrupted a funeral. "Boats. In the gate. Three of them."

Beatrix didn't ask me how many times. She didn't say *are you sure*. She just stood up from the chart table and took the binoculars off their hook, and I watched ten years of careful invisibility end behind her eyes.

"They're early," she said.

"The tide's not even full," Okafor said. "Nobody runs that gate on a half tide. Nobody."

"Somebody does," Beatrix said, "if somebody told them where the deep water hides."

We all knew who somebody was. None of us said his name out loud, because saying it would make him real again, and for six years we'd done the healthier thing and kept him a ghost. Reyes. The diver Beatrix exiled before I ever joined the crew—the one whose name comes up in galley conversations the way you'd discuss a fire that got put out mostly by luck. He'd been on the ice last night with a headlamp and a salvage bag, and now here were three corporate tenders sliding through the ice gate like they'd been given a map drawn in crayon by someone who loved them.

Which, in a way, they had.

* * *

By the time we got the skiff launched, the lead tender had already nosed into the pool below the glacier face—a broad blue amphitheater of ice that the charts called the Landfall Basin, because my mother named it that, because apparently every place in this family's history has to come pre-haunted. Their dive gear was already coming over the side. Crates. Winches. A small crane, because corporations don't dive, they *extract*.

Okafor held us at the edge of the basin, engine idling, and looked at Beatrix.

"We can't stop them," he said. "Three boats, twelve people, and they've got a crane."

"I'm not trying to stop them," Beatrix said. "I'm trying to be seen stopping them. There's a difference, and the difference is leverage."

"That's lawyer talk."

"I grew up around worse."

She turned to me. "Junie. You're the best swimmer we've got and the least likely to get shot. Get out on the ice shelf between them and the wreck line. Plant our marker buoy where it's visible. If they cross our line, they cross it in front of witnesses."

"What witnesses?"

"Them," she said. "Witnesses work both ways."

So that's how I ended up standing alone on a shelf of thousand-year-old ice at seven in the morning, hammering a stake into a crack while a corporate dive crew watched me from forty meters away like I was a zoo exhibit that had learned to use tools. My breath was loud in my ears. The glacier groaned underneath me, long and low, the sound it makes when it's thinking about something.

And then the figure in the lead of their group pulled back his hood, and it wasn't thinking about anything anymore.

Reyes looked older. That was the first thing—not meaner, not harder, just *older*, the way a boat looks when it's been left on a mooring too long. He walked toward my marker line slowly, hands visible, which was either courtesy or training, and stopped two meters short of the stake.

"Hey, Junie," he said. "You probably don't remember me."

"I remember the stories," I said. "They're not flattering."

"Yeah." He looked at the stake, at the line, at the buoy riding its little circle of open water. "She drew a line, huh?"

"She draws lines. It's kind of her whole thing."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Is she okay? Under all of it?"

"You'd have to ask her."

"I did ask her. Six years ago." He crouched, studied the stake without touching it. "You know why she sent me off? Everyone thinks it's because I took a cut I shouldn't have. It wasn't. I surfaced with something she didn't want anyone else to see, and she couldn't look at me after that without seeing it too. So she made me the villain instead of the witness." He stood. "I've had six years to decide whether I mind. Some days I don't."

"And today?"

"Today I need the paycheck, Junie. Same as everybody." He glanced back at the tenders, at the crane swinging its arm into position. "But you should know something, since you're standing on my old route. The calving's early this year. I've been reading this face for two weeks. The bergschrund above the basin opened four days ago, and the meltwater's running brown. When she goes, she goes fast, and she won't send an invitation."

"So help us leave."

"I can't help you leave. I can only tell you what I know." He stepped back over some invisible boundary of his own. "Tell her the schrund's the clock. Not the tides. The tides are a schedule. The schrund is a countdown."

Then he walked back to his crew, and I hammered the stake deeper, because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

The standoff, when it finally formalized itself, happened the way standoffs do—slowly, then all at once.

Kaine came across the ice herself. Of course she did. Vesper Kaine doesn't send deputies to confrontations; she considers delegation a form of cowardice with better tailoring. She crossed the shelf in a parka that probably cost more than our entire vessel, flanked by two crew members who walked like people who had been briefed on the phrase *flag of truce* but not on the concept.

Beatrix met her at the midpoint, which was also, coincidentally or not, my marker line. Okafor and I flanked her. Across the ice, Reyes stood slightly apart from Kaine's people, in the position of a man who belongs to neither army and knows exactly how much that costs.

"You're on my site," Kaine said.

"Your site has a wreck on it that belongs to the insurance consortium of a company that dissolved in 1987," Beatrix said. "Legally, this is nobody's site. Practically, it's mine. I've been diving it for three tides."

"And I've been buying the debt on your hull for two years," Kaine said. "Practically beats legally, most mornings."

"Vesper." Beatrix said her name flatly, the way you'd set down something heavy. "Look up."

Kaine looked up. We all did. The glacier face rose two hundred meters above the basin, white going blue going green at the waterline, and somewhere inside it the schrund was doing whatever schrunds do when they're deciding to become an avalanche.

"It's going to calve early," Beatrix said. "Your own diver knows it. Ask him."

Kaine's jaw did something small and expensive. "Reyes reads ice. Reyes also wants a bonus. His readings have been pessimistic all week."

"My readings have been *accurate* all week," Reyes said quietly, and it was the first time I'd heard him raise his voice above a murmur, and it landed on the ice like a dropped tool.

Silence. The glacier filled it, groaning, patient.

Then Kaine did the thing that made me understand, finally, why my mother's generation had feared her and respected her in equal measure: she recalculated. Right there, in front of everyone, you could watch it happen—her eyes going from Beatrix to the glacier face to the tenders to the meltwater running brown off the shelf edge, and whatever math she was doing coming out to a number she didn't like.

"Truce," she said. "Both crews dive the next tide together. Shared lines, shared safety divers, split recovery by manifest claim. And when the face starts moving, everyone is out of the water before anyone argues about cargo. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Beatrix said. "One condition. Your people don't touch the strongbox."

Kaine's mouth curved. "Still chasing that box."

"It's not cargo. It's mail."

"Everything's cargo, Captain. That's the first lesson of salvage." But she stuck out her hand, and Beatrix shook it, and the handshake lasted exactly as long as it takes two people to confirm that neither one trusts the other further than the length of an arm.

* * *

We spent the rest of the day rigging shared lines, which is a strange intimacy—two crews who'd been prepared to hate each other, kneeling on the same ice, splicing rope and checking each other's regulators because the alternative was dying stupidly together. One of Kaine's techs taught Okafor a knot he'd been tying wrong for twenty years, and Okafor accepted the correction with the face of a man swallowing a fishbone. I ended up buddied with Reyes on the safety rotation, which was either Kaine's idea of a joke or Beatrix's idea of surveillance, and possibly both.

"You dive like her," Reyes said, as we checked tanks. "Same breathing. Economical. Like air owes her money."

"She taught me everything."

"No," he said. "She taught you everything she could stand to pass on. There's a difference. You'll figure it out around year five."

The glacier groaned again, longer this time, and both crews stopped working and listened to it the way you listen to a floorboard in a house where something is wrong.

Evening came down blue and cold. From the rail of our boat I watched the two fleets rafted close in the basin, lights doubled in the black water, and I thought about what Reyes had said about witnesses working both ways. Tomorrow we all go down together. Tomorrow we all come up together, or some of us don't come up at all, and the arguments about cargo will be settled by whoever survives to argue.

I went below and found Beatrix at the chart table, not looking at the charts, looking at nothing.

"The schrund's the clock," I told her. "That's what he said. Not the tides."

"I heard him." She tapped the table once, twice, a rhythm I recognized as the thing she does instead of crying. "Six years I kept that man off my boats, and the first time I let him near one, he saves all our lives. That's the joke, isn't it. That's the punchline."

"Maybe it's not a joke."

"Everything's a joke," Beatrix said. "Some of them just take decades to land." She rolled up the chart and looked at me, and for a second she looked less like a captain than like a woman holding a letter she hadn't opened yet. "Get some sleep, Junie. Fifth tide comes early."

And underneath us, under forty fathoms of black water and the keel of the Meridian, the glacier began—very softly, very patiently—to count.
