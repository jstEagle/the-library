## 17. The Name in the Water

I didn't sleep. I want to say that like it's a virtue—like insomnia is a kind of heroism—but the truth is I lay in my bunk listening to the glacier talk to itself and tried very hard not to think about a water-blurred page, and failed at both with impressive consistency.

The logbook sat in the chart table drawer where I'd stowed it, wrapped in a dry towel like something sick. Every hour or so I got up and looked at it. The page didn't improve. Water damage doesn't negotiate. The ink had run in long gray tears down through the final lines of my mother's last entry, and the name—the name that would tell me who ordered the *Meridian* scuttled, who signed off on a ship full of people's livelihoods going to the bottom of a tidewater fjord—was a smear. A bruise on paper. Ten years of wondering, and the answer had drowned before I could read it.

Somewhere around three in the morning I stopped being pathetic about it.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about growing up on salvage boats: your mother teaches you to read wrecks the way other mothers teach you to read. And when she couldn't take me into deep water yet, she taught me another trick—reconstruction. *If the record's gone, Trixie, you build it from what touched it.* Sediment lines. Tool marks. The way things fall. She'd set me up with a wrecked model boat in the galley sink and knock it over herself, different ways, and make me figure out how it died.

She never knew she was training me for her.

* * *

I came up into the pilothouse at dawn with the logbook under one arm and every survey chart she ever drew spread across the deck below, weighted down with coffee mugs and a wrench because we were fresh out of proper chart weights and dignity.

Okafor found me there an hour later, standing over them in my socks.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I've been doing archival work."

"Uh-huh." He came in slow, the way he does when he thinks I'm a loaded thing that might go off. After yesterday—after the crew heard me admit I'd hidden the manifests—we were all speaking carefully, like people walking on river ice. "Junie made eggs."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. It's one of your three defining traits, along with stubborn and—" He stopped. Looked at the charts. Looked at the logbook. "You figured out how to read it?"

"No," I said. "That's the point. I'm not going to read it. I'm going to remember it."

He pulled up a stool, which was his way of saying *I'm staying whether you like it or not*, which has been his way since roughly 2019.

My mother's charts are a language. Most surveyors draw clean lines—soundings, hazards, channels. My mother drew conversations. Little annotations in the margins, arrows, depth numbers circled twice when they mattered once. And starting the summer I turned nine, a second hand joined hers. Mine. Small round letters, the handwriting of a kid who'd been handed a grease pencil and told her observations counted.

I remembered making them. That was the horrible part—I didn't remember *making* them until I saw them, and then it all came back with the smell of the old boat's cabin, diesel and cedar and the lemon candy she kept in the chart drawer to bribe me through long soundings. We'd done this together. A whole summer of it, maybe two. She'd ask me questions no adult asks a child: *What do you see? What do you think happened here? Why do you think the current runs wrong there?*

And I'd answered. In pencil. In a child's certainty that had no idea what it was cataloguing.

"Start at the beginning," Okafor said. "What did she have you mark?"

"The approach." I put my finger on the oldest chart, the one with the fjord mouth. My childhood letters marched along the eastern channel: *rocks sing here* — I'd meant the harmonic the current made over the shoal — and further in, *ice goes out fast after big calve, mama says count to ten tides*. Counting to ten tides. I'd been nine years old, counting tides like a nursery rhyme, and she'd been building a map of exactly how and when the Meridian's grave opened and closed.

"She wasn't teaching you," Okafor said quietly. "She was using you."

"She was testing a route." My voice came out flatter than I meant it to. "On the only diver she trusted more than herself. Somebody too small to argue."

* * *

By midmorning Junie had drifted in, then Reyes, then Harrow, drawn by the same gravity that pulls gulls to anything interesting on a deck. Nobody said much about yesterday. Harrow brought coffee and set it near my elbow without comment, which from Harrow is a full apology, a pardon, and a marriage proposal all at once.

I walked them through it, chart by chart, following the trail my own small hand had left across twenty years of paper.

The second chart took us inside the gate. There, in my nine-year-old scrawl: *the door is where the two blue lines kiss.* Two blue lines—my mother's depth contours, the ones that converged at the submerged sill beneath the ice face. The sill. The shelf of rock that held the wreck off the bottom, that let the *Meridian* sit shallow enough to reach. I'd marked it as a game. She'd recorded it as coordinates.

Third chart. The wreck itself—or rather, the space above it, because my mother never drew the *Meridian* whole. Every other wreck on every other chart got a little profile sketch, her habit, almost affectionate. The *Meridian* got nothing. Just a rectangle of blank space with a single annotation in *her* hand, not mine: *Trixie's window.* And next to it, mine: *mama says we can only look from here, never down.*

"Why?" Junie asked. She was hugging her elbows, diver's posture, like she was cold even in the warm cabin. "Why wouldn't she let you dive it?"

"That's what I spent all night asking." I smoothed the fourth chart flat. This one was the strangest, and it was the one that finally broke the thing open in my chest like a hatch under pressure.

It was a cargo plan. Not of the *Meridian*'s hold—that would have been normal, salvage prep, knowing what you're diving toward. It was a cargo plan of a different ship entirely, an older vessel, the *Corvane*, a freighter that went down thirty years before I was born, forty miles south. A wreck nobody salvaged because there was nothing to salvage. Everyone knew the *Corvane* story: hull failure, quick loss, sad footnote.

Except my mother had drawn its holds in obsessive detail. And beside each hold, in my mother's hand, a list. And beside each list, in my hand, age ten, the words I'd copied without understanding:

*mama says some loads are heavier wet.*

"What does that mean?" Reyes asked.

"It means dry cargo floats and lies about its weight," I said. "Sealed drums. Pressurized containers. Things that flood and get denser, or worse—" I looked at the lists. Chemical designations, half of them faded, all of them industrial, none of them precious. No gold. No bullion. No specie. "Or worse, things that shouldn't be in water at all."

The cabin got very quiet. Outside, the glacier gave one of its long groaning exhalations, the sound it makes when it's thinking about moving.

"She wasn't mapping the *Corvane*," Okafor said slowly. "She was comparing."

"Matching manifests," I said. "Ten-year-old me copied them out as penmanship practice. Thirty-four-year-old me just now recognized them." I tapped the lists, one by one. "These match the *Meridian*'s cargo manifest. The real one. The one from the strongbox that doesn't match the official sinking report. Same consignments. Same shippers. Same weights." I looked up. "The *Corvane* sank with this cargo in 1961. Officially. And then eleven years later, the *Meridian* sailed carrying the identical manifest—and sank too. Two ships, thirty years apart, same load, same story, same lie."

Harrow said, "So somebody moved the cargo from one wreck to another ship and sank that one too."

"Somebody moved the *story*," I said. "Maybe the cargo. Maybe neither. Maybe the manifests were the cover both times." I pressed my palms flat against the chart, against my mother's careful lines and my own childish letters, and felt the shape of it finally, horribly, close around me. "You don't scuttle a ship to hide treasure. Treasure keeps. You scuttle a ship to hide a liability. Something that costs money forever if it surfaces. Sinking it isn't losing it—it's paying storage."

"And your mother knew," Okafor said.

"My mother knew." The words tasted like cold metal. "She spent my whole childhood mapping this fjord. Every tide, every sill, every route in and out. I thought she was obsessed with the wreck. With the prize. Everyone thought that—that's the legend, right? Mara Nakamura, chasing the *Meridian*, dying for the score." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She wasn't chasing it. She was *guarding* it. She was making sure nobody else got in either. Including me. *Never down, Trixie. We only look from here.*"

* * *

I sent them all out around noon. Not angrily—I just needed the cabin empty, and they knew it, and for once in this voyage everybody did what I asked without a vote.

Then I sat alone with the last chart. The newest one. Her final season.

This was the chart she'd had aboard the night she drowned, reconstructed from the copies Kaine's lawyers had attached to the debt file like bait. On it, in her last months of handwriting—tighter now, hurried, the hand of a woman running out of time—was the route. The exact route I'd followed through the ice gate on the first tide. And beside the route, in the margin, one word, repeated three times in three places, like a woman underlining a warning to herself:

*Alone. Alone. Alone.*

Not *danger*. Not *turn back*. Alone. She hadn't been warning herself off the dive. She'd been insisting on it. Whatever she intended to do down there, she'd wanted no witnesses, no crew, no daughter.

Because you don't want witnesses when you're committing a crime. And you don't want witnesses when you're confessing one.

I picked up the logbook. Unwrapped it. The blurred name waited where it had waited all night, a gray wound across the page. *Scuttle order confirmed,* she'd written. *Given by—*

Everyone assumes the dash hides a villain. I'd assumed it. Kaine assumed it. The whole ugly architecture of our rivalry, my grief, this entire frozen race—we'd all built it on the assumption that behind that smear was the name of some company man, some owner, some monster who'd sunk a ship and drowned a dream.

But I didn't need the ink anymore. I had something better. I had a ten-year-old girl's handwriting, and a summer of questions, and a mother who taught her daughter to reconstruct a wreck from what touched it.

Who gives a scuttle order? Whoever owns the liability. Whoever carries the secret. Whoever has been quietly, obsessively, across decades, keeping everyone—including the people she loved most—away from the water above it.

Who mapped the fjord so precisely that a corporate fleet needed a traitor and a stolen chart to find the gate?

Who wrote *never down* in a child's hand and meant it as a fence?

Whose last words in her last log were *alone*—because the order was hers to give, and hers alone to carry, and the only way to keep it was to go down with it?

The name in the water was Nakamura.

My mother scuttled the *Meridian*. Or finished what someone else started—sealed it, buried it, made the fjord her co-conspirator. To bury something worse than treasure. Something that, if it ever came up wet and heavy into the light, would poison everything it touched. Maybe the water. Maybe the coast. Maybe just the truth. I may never know which, because she chose the glacier over the answer, chose the blank over the confession, chose to be remembered as a greedy dead woman rather than be understood as whatever she actually was.

And I had spent ten years hating the sea for taking her, when really she'd walked into it on purpose, holding the door shut behind her.

I sat in the pilothouse of my mother's daughter's boat and cried the way you cry when the grief doesn't change but the story does—ugly, brief, and privately, because the crew was one bulkhead away and I'd already given them enough reasons to doubt me this week.

When it was done, I dried my face on the towel that had wrapped the logbook, which felt appropriately circular, and looked at the chart one more time.

*Trixie's window. Never down.*

Except I had gone down. Three tides running, I'd gone down, breached the hold, cracked the strongbox, pulled the manifests into the light. Everything she'd fenced off, I'd opened. And the fleet was coming through the gate tomorrow on the fifth tide, with Kaine hungry for vindication and my mother's secret sitting in the hold like a drum that gets heavier wet.

There was one thing left in the fjord that could still prove any of this—the captain's cabin, the deeper sections, whatever else she'd sealed. One tide of safe water remaining after tomorrow. Maybe less, the way the glacier had been talking all night.

Okafor put his head in the door without knocking, which meant it was bad.

"Tide's shifting early," he said. "And Junie spotted mast lights past the point. The fleet's not waiting for the fifth tide, Beatrix. They're moving *now*."

I folded my mother's chart closed, over twenty years of a child's handwriting and a woman's silence, and stood up.

"Then we'd better get to the water," I said, "before somebody else reads her mail."
