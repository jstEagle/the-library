## 18. Sixth Tide: The Race

There's a saying among salvage men that the sea doesn't care about your schedule. It's a comforting lie we tell each other over bad coffee, and like most comforting lies it collapses the moment you actually need it.

The sixth tide was scheduled to open at 0410. The glacier had other ideas.

I was on deck at three-thirty, doing what I'd done every morning for a week—listening to the ice. You learn a glacier's voice the way you learn an engine's, and ours had developed a new note overnight. Not the deep organ groans we'd been hearing all week, those long tectonic complaints that Junie had started calling "the whale songs." This was higher. Sharper. A cracking sound, like someone testing the spine of a frozen turkey, somewhere up in the blue dark above the ice gate.

"Okafor." Beatrix stood at the rail beside me. She'd been there a while; I'd heard her come up, heard her not say anything, which is its own kind of conversation with her. "You hear it too."

"I hear it."

"How bad?"

I made myself answer honestly, because the whole ugly lesson of this trip—the manifests she'd hidden, the fight in the galley two nights ago, the crew overhearing her lie through the bulkhead—was that honesty costs less now than it will later. "Bad enough that I'd call this our last tide even if it weren't."

She nodded slowly. Above us, the sky was doing that arctic thing where night and morning can't agree on whose turn it is, everything smeared violet and iron-gray. The Meridian sat below us under forty feet of green water, her superstructure snagged on a shelf of rock where the glacier had calved around her decades ago, holding her like a hand closed loosely around something it meant to keep.

"Then we go now," Beatrix said. "Both boats. Everyone who wants to dive dives. Anyone who doesn't stays topside and I won't think less of them." A pause. "Much."

"That last part true?"

"No," she said, and for the first time in days, she almost smiled.

* * *

The fleet was already moving when we got the skiffs down.

They'd come through the ice gate at midnight—early, hours ahead of any sane schedule—using knowledge they shouldn't have had. We all knew where that knowledge came from. Reyes. Our Reyes, once, before Beatrix exiled him for reasons she'd never fully explained to me and I'd never fully pressed her on, because every crew keeps one locked drawer and you don't pick your shipmate's locks. He'd been standing on their lead tender's bow when they came through, arms crossed, guiding them through the growler field by some private geometry only he knew. Watching him do it had felt like watching someone read your diary aloud in a language you'd forgotten you spoke.

Now their lights were strung along the ice shelf like carnival bulbs, and their divers were already splashing as we came alongside the truce line we'd agreed to two days ago—an armed truce, technically, though the guns had stayed holstered and mostly served as ballast for everyone's tempers. The deal was simple and insane: both crews dive the final tide together, because neither crew could finish alone, and because the calving didn't care whose flag was on your tender.

Kaine herself stood at the edge of their platform in a drysuit older than most of her employees, gray hair cropped short, looking less like a corporate executive than like exactly what she'd once been—a diver, a rival, a woman who'd spent thirty years losing arguments to Beatrix Nakamura's mother and intended to win the last one posthumously if necessary.

"Nakamura." She nodded across the gap between skiffs. "Your boat sounds terrible."

"She always does before she performs miracles."

"I've got four divers in the water and a cutting team on the hold seam. We're going for the strongbox compartment and the aft manifest safe."

"We know about the manifests," Beatrix said flatly.

Something moved behind Kaine's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "Do you."

"It's my mother's wreck, Kaine. Whatever's in it, I'm diving it."

For a moment the two women just looked at each other across black water, and I understood—I think for the first time—that I wasn't watching a debt collector and a debtor. I was watching the last living chapter of a story that had started before either of them owned anything, back when Vesper Kaine and Elena Nakamura were partners splitting salvage fifty-fifty out of a boat barely bigger than ours. Kaine had told us half of it under flag of truce. Partners, then rivals, then whatever it was that had kept her buying debts and chasing wrecks for a decade after Elena drowned. Not gold. She'd said it herself, and I'd believed her even when no one else did: vindication.

"Then let's not waste the tide arguing about whose grief gets priority," Kaine said finally. "Your people take the captain's cabin. Mine take the hold. Nobody touches anybody's find until we're topside. Agreed?"

Beatrix looked at me. I shrugged, which in our private language means *it's a terrible idea, which makes it probably the best idea available.*

"Agreed," she said.

* * *

Junie went down first off our skiff, then Beatrix, then me, with Tomas running surface and the fleet's tender covering our bubble watch in a show of cooperation that would have been touching if the glacier weren't groaning overhead like a ship breaking up.

The descent was familiar now—fifth time through that green cathedral of light, past the shelf ice that hung over the wreck like a ceiling in a house nobody should live in. But today the cathedral was talking. Every few minutes a shock rolled through the water, a pressure wave you felt in your teeth and your chest before your ears caught up, and each time, a fine snow of ice particles sifted down through our work lights like the world's coldest confetti.

We reached the superstructure at eighteen meters. The Meridian lay on her starboard side, rail buried in silt, hull furred with twenty years of white bacterial bloom so she looked less like a ship than like the skeleton of one, picked clean and left in a field. Her name was still legible on the stern plate, and I did what I do every dive, which is touch it once, quickly, the way you'd touch a headstone.

"Comms check," Beatrix said. Her voice came through clear, flattened by the helmet into something calm and far away. "Okafor, you're with me to the cabin. Junie—"

"I know, I know. Cargo deck, tag anything that looks like documentation, don't be a hero." Junie's voice was bright and steady, and only I could hear, underneath it, the thin wire of fear she'd been carrying since the near-miss on the third tide. She was twenty-three and the best young diver I'd trained in fifteen years, and she was scared, and she was going anyway. That's not bravery exactly. It's something better. Bravery is not being scared. This was love, wearing a drysuit.

"Go," Beatrix said.

* * *

The captain's cabin was aft and down, through a corridor that the calving had buckled into a shape no architect ever drew. We'd widened the entry on the fourth tide, and Beatrix went through the gap first, shoulders rolling, breath rasping in my earphones.

Inside, the cabin was a museum of the moment the ship died. A chair floated loose, tethered by its own decayed upholstery. A chart table stood against the wall, drawers swollen shut with silt. And on the desk, exactly where the childhood annotations in her mother's charts had promised, sat the logbook—wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire, preserved by cold and depth the way glaciers preserve everything, which is to say perfectly and against everyone's wishes.

Beatrix took it with both hands. Through her gloves, through the water, through forty years and a drowning, I watched her hold her mother's handwriting.

"Elena," she said quietly. Just the name. Then: "Okafor. The page."

I helped her ease the wire. The oilcloth opened like a flower. The logbook inside was water-damaged at the edges but the core pages held, ink ghosted brown, and Beatrix turned to the last written page with hands that shook in a way I'd never seen them shake, not in blowouts, not in the fight with the crew, not once in ten years of knowing her.

And I watched her face do something complicated.

"The name," I said. "Is it legible?"

"No." Her voice was strange. Flat and full at once. "It's gone. Water took it. But—" She stopped. Turned the book toward her light. And I saw her stop reading and start remembering instead, her eyes going distant, going backward, going home to a girl diving wrecks with her mother and memorizing annotation trails she didn't understand.

"Beatrix. What is it?"

"I know the name," she said. "I've known it since I was nine years old. I just never—" Her breath hitched in the rebreather. "Okafor, the scuttle order wasn't given *to* her. It came *from* her. The signature—it's hers. It was always hers. My mother sank the Meridian on purpose."

Above us, distantly, the glacier cracked like a rifle shot, and the whole wreck shuddered, and neither of us moved, because some truths pin you harder than any ice.

* * *

That was when the fleet's emergency channel lit up.

"—Kaine has broken from her dive plan, repeat, Kaine has gone deep, she's past the hold, she's descending into the lower compartments, copy—"

Tomas's voice, relayed from surface, tight as wire. On the fleet's own channel, their dive supervisor was saying the same thing in the particular exhausted tone of a man whose boss has stopped listening to him: *"Ms. Kaine, you are below planned depth, Ms. Kaine, respond—"*

Static. Then Kaine's voice, calm and terrible, coming through on the shared band because she wanted us all to hear it:

"I'm fine. I'm going to the strongbox. Alone. Nobody follow me."

"Vesper." Beatrix's voice cut through like a blade through wet rope. "The glacier is coming apart. Get out of there."

"You don't understand what's down here." For the first time, Kaine's composure slipped, and what showed underneath wasn't greed. It was grief, old and unhealed, thirty years deep. "Elena told me once what she buried. She told me and I called her a coward and I've spent my whole life being wrong about her and I need to *see it*, Beatrix, I need to see it before the ice takes it—"

"Vesper, listen to me—"

"Thirty years, Nakamura. Give me five minutes."

Below us, her dive light winked deeper into the wreck, down through a hatch into compartments no survey chart had ever mapped, chasing vindication into the one place the glacier was least willing to give it.

And above us, the ice gave its answer.

It wasn't a crack this time. It was a roar—the sound of several hundred thousand tons of tidewater glacier deciding, all at once, that it had been patient long enough. The water around us turned to chaos, pressure waves slamming through the hull, our lights strobing with debris, and Tomas's voice screaming through the comm: *"CALVING! CALVING AT THE GATE—everybody UP, everybody UP NOW—"*

Beatrix looked down into the dark where Kaine's light was shrinking. Then at the logbook clutched to her chest. Then down again.

I already knew what she was going to do. God help me, I'd known since the day I signed that debt contract behind her back.

"Okafor." Her voice was steady. Steadier than mine would have been. "Take the book. Take Junie. Get everyone topside."

"And you?"

Her dive light swung toward the hatch, toward the deep, toward the woman who had loved and hated and lost her mother in ways none of us would ever fully map.

"I'm going to get Vesper Kaine," Beatrix Nakamura said, "even if I have to drag her out of 1962."

And she dropped into the dark, and the glacier came down behind her.
