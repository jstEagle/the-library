## 19. What the Glacier Keeps

The glacier announced itself the way a liar does—quietly, then all at once.

I was forty feet down the Meridian's main corridor, my lamp beam swimming through silt, when the sound came through the water. Not a crack. Not a groan. A *voice*—deep and structural, the kind of sound you feel in your molars before your ears get around to it. The whole wreck shivered like a struck bell.

Every diver knows that sound. Every diver prays they'll never hear it at depth.

*Calving.*

My gauge read eleven minutes of bottom time. My brain did the math it always does—air, distance, decompression—and then threw the whole ledger out, because somewhere above me, in the black collapsing throat of this fjord, Vesper Kaine had gone deep against orders, chasing whatever vindication she'd carried for thirty years, and the Meridian was about to come apart on top of her.

I dropped the prize cargo.

That's the sentence I'll be explaining to myself for the rest of my life. The manifest chest—the one with the scuttle order, the one with the page that could clear my mother or condemn her, the one I'd bled three tides to reach—was sitting right there in the captain's strongroom, strapped into its cradle, close enough to touch. Eleven minutes. Maybe eight, once you counted the swim back through the corridor. Enough time to cut it free and haul it up if everything went perfectly, and nothing had gone perfectly since the day Okafor signed our lives over to Vesper Kaine.

Instead I turned my back on it and swam toward the sound of Kaine's dive light, which was flickering somewhere below me, down past the collapsed section where the hull had folded in on itself like a fist.

"Kaine!" The words came out as a roar of bubbles. Useless. Water eats voices alive.

The glacier spoke again, longer this time, and the silt around me lifted in a slow brown bloom, and somewhere in the dark above, ice the size of a cathedral began to let go of the face it had held for ten thousand years.

* * *

You want to know what I thought about, in those seconds? Not the debt. Not the manifests. Not even my mother, though God knows she's never far from me when the water closes in.

I thought about Junie. Nineteen years old, first real job, idolizes me the way I used to idolize my mother—which should have been my first warning about how this story would end. I thought about Okafor, who betrayed me in the kindest possible way and has been paying for it ever since. I thought about my crew, my small quiet invisible crew, and how every one of them was topside right now in two skiffs below a calving face, and how the only thing standing between them and a wall of displaced water was me getting everyone out of the fjord fast enough.

And underneath all of that, quieter than any of it: I thought about what my mother would have done.

She'd have gone after Kaine. That's the ugly truth of it. Thirty years of rivalry doesn't mean hate; sometimes it means the only person who understands the shape of your life is the one you've been fighting it against. My mother drowned chasing this wreck. Kaine went deep against orders chasing my mother's ghost. And I was the only one positioned to notice that neither of them had ever learned to quit.

So I went down.

The corridor narrowed past the bulkhead breach, and my lamp found the first sign of her: a dive line, freshly clipped, running down through the debris field at an angle no sane person would rig. Below it, a light moved—not steady, not panicked either, just *busy*, the way lights move when someone is doing something they shouldn't be.

I followed the line. Bad idea. Good idea. There wasn't time to decide which.

Kaine was wedged into the gap between the engine housing and a fallen plate, working at something with a pry bar. Her bubbles came fast and shallow. She'd blown through her reserve planning—I could tell by the way she was moving, all economy and no margin, a woman spending air she didn't have on a thing she couldn't carry alone.

She saw my light and froze. Even at forty feet, even through a mask, I could read the expression: *of all the people in the ocean.*

I pointed up. She shook her head. I pointed up again, harder, the universal gesture for *the glacier is eating us*, and she shook her head again and jabbed her thumb at the thing she'd been prying at—a sealed case, half-buried under the plate, marked with faded stenciling I recognized from the manifests.

Her personal safe. My mother's rival had found my mother's private cache, and she'd rather die than leave it.

Fine. Then we'd both die stupid, because I wasn't leaving without her, and the glacier wasn't negotiating.

I grabbed the pry bar out of her hands. She fought me for exactly two seconds—a short, furious, wordless argument conducted entirely in shoves and glares—before the third groan came through the hull, closer now, and we both felt the wreck shift beneath us like a ship remembering it was a ship. The plate holding the case sagged. Somewhere aft, metal shrieked.

We got the case loose together. Neither of us will ever admit that part.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about dragging a stubborn sixty-year-old woman up through a collapsing wreck while your own air gauge ticks past the red: it's mostly anger. Fear is there, sure, humming under everything like current through wire, but fear is useless at depth. Anger works. Anger has torque.

I was angry at Kaine for going deep. Angry at myself for following. Angry at my mother for dying here and leaving me a map made of my own forgotten childhood handwriting, a strongbox full of letters I hadn't opened, and a truth so heavy I'd hidden it from the people who trusted me most. I was angry at the glacier for choosing this tide, this hour, this exact moment to shrug off a hundred years of patience.

And I was angry, most of all, at the chest still sitting in the strongroom behind me. Because I knew—I *knew*, with the terrible clarity that comes when the body understands things before the mind consents—that I was never coming back for it. Six tides. This was the fifth. The sixth belonged to the fleet and the failing ice, and by the seventh the Meridian would be under a hundred feet of new glacier, sealed again, this time for good.

The evidence that would clear my mother or condemn her. The proof of what really happened the night she drowned. The answer to the question I'd built my entire adult life around avoiding.

I left it in the dark and kept kicking.

The corridor was coming apart behind us. I heard it more than saw it—plates letting go, the long tearing complaint of steel that had held its shape for ninety years finally being allowed to fail. My lamp caught Kaine ahead of me, kicking hard now, her earlier stubbornness burned off and replaced by the flat efficiency of a woman who very much wanted to live. Whatever she'd been trying to prove down here, the proving was over. Survival is a great simplifier.

We cleared the superstructure as the first true collapse hit. I felt it as pressure—a fist of displaced water slamming through the wreck behind us, shoving us forward like leaves in a gutter. My mask fogged with silt. My regulator bucked. And then we were out, into open gray water, under a surface that had stopped being a surface and started being a ceiling of churning white.

The skiff was where Okafor swore he'd keep it, because Okafor keeps his word the way other men keep their wallets. Junie hauled me over the gunwale with a grip that would've impressed a dock crane, and then went straight back for Kaine without a second's hesitation, which told me everything I needed to know about who Junie was becoming.

"Beatrix—" Okafor, at the wheel, his voice stripped down to bare wire. "The face. Look at the face."

I looked.

The glacier was calving. Not the small stuff, not the growlers and bergy bits we'd dodged on the way in. This was the real event—the whole western face leaning out over the water like a man deciding whether to step off a ledge, fractures shooting up its height in white lightning-quick lines. The sound was beyond sound. It was weather. It was geology clearing its throat.

"Go," I said. "Everyone down in the well. Go *now*."

* * *

We ran the fjord with the calving wave chasing us.

I won't dress it up. There was no heroics to it, no skill worth writing down. Okafor drove that skiff the way a man drives when his family is in the boat, and the rest of us lay in the bilge holding onto each other and listening to the mountain come apart behind us. The wave caught us once, lifted the stern, spun us half around, and set us down again like a hand testing fruit. Junie screamed—not from fear, I think, but from the sheer animal outrage of being small in a world that size.

Then we were through the ice gate, such as it was, because the gate wasn't a gate anymore—it was a memory of a channel, choked and shifting—and then open water, and then the *Vireo*, our boat, our home, riding at anchor with her lights on like the last lit window in a drowning world.

We got aboard. We counted heads. All present. All breathing.

Behind us, the fjord filled with white thunder. Icebergs the size of courthouses settled into the water and rolled. And somewhere under all of it, going down by inches and then by fathoms, the Meridian took my mother's secrets back—the manifests, the scuttle order, the damaged name, the whole buried argument between what happened and what was reported. Everything that could have cleared her. Everything that could have condemned her.

Gone. Given back to the glacier, which keeps what it keeps and tells no one.

Kaine stood at the rail beside me, dripping, her recovered case clutched to her chest like a child. She didn't say thank you. I didn't expect her to. But after a long time she said, quietly, "You went back for me."

"You were between me and the exit," I said.

She almost smiled. "Liar."

"Occupational hazard." I watched the ice dust settling over the fjord like snow falling upward in reverse. "What was in the case, Vesper?"

She looked down at it. Her knuckles were white around the handle. "Proof," she said. "Of a kind. Your mother and I—we made promises down there, thirty years ago. Before everything. This was hers to keep and mine to break, and I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think I'm too tired to break anything anymore."

She held the case out to me.

I didn't take it. Not yet. My hands were shaking too badly to be trusted with someone else's inheritance, and besides, some part of me understood that whatever was inside would finish what the glacier started—burning every bridge back home, just like I'd always feared, just like I'd spent a decade building a small quiet invisible life to avoid.

But the fjord was empty now. The race was over. The fleet would turn back, and the debt would stand or fall on Kaine's word alone, and the truth—the real truth, my mother's truth—was either in that case or in the strongbox I still hadn't opened, waiting in my cabin like a letter I'd been afraid to read since chapter one of my own life.

"The sixth tide comes at dawn," Okafor said behind us, gentle as a man announcing a funeral. "Fleet's already moving for the gate. If we're going to make a final run—"

"There's nothing left to run for," I said. "Not down there."

Junie appeared at my elbow, wrapped in two blankets, lips still blue, eyes enormous. "Then what are we racing for?"

I looked at the case in Kaine's arms. At the strongbox below decks with my name on it. At the horizon, where the corporate fleet's lights were already turning away from the ice, cheated of their prize by a mountain that kept its own counsel.

"For landfall," I said. "And for whatever my mother left me that the glacier couldn't take."

Kaine set the case on the rail between us, and together, without a word, we began to work the latches.
