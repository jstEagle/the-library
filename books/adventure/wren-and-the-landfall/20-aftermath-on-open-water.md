## 20. Aftermath on Open Water

The first thing I learned about surviving a calving glacier is that you don't. Not really. You survive the *before* and the *after*, and the middle is just a hole in your memory shaped like a mountain falling into the sea.

I was in the water when it came down.

I want to be clear about that, because for the rest of my life people are going to ask me what it was like, and I'm going to have to decide, every single time, whether to tell them the truth or the version that lets me sleep. The truth is: I was forty feet down on the tether line, hauling Kaine's dead-weight body toward the surface while Beatrix Nakamura swam above me with a grip on my harness strap like God's own hand, and the sound came through the water not as noise but as *pressure*—a fist closing around the whole bay—and then the world turned white from below, all the silt and shattered ice coming up at us like a ceiling where the sky used to be.

The version I'll probably tell: I don't remember much. It happened fast. We were lucky.

Both are true. Only one of them is useful.

* * *

We limped out of the ice gate on the last of the sixth tide, and I mean *limped*. The *Wren* had a cracked viewport in the wheelhouse, a bent prop shaft singing a note no prop shaft should sing, and a hull scar the length of a church pew where a bergy bit had kissed us goodbye. The fleet tender wasn't much better—Kaine's people had lost two ROVs, a dive skiff, and, by the look on their faces when we passed them in the channel, something harder to replace than equipment.

Nobody spoke as we threaded the growler field. There's a kind of silence that happens on a boat after everyone almost died together, and it isn't peaceful. It's the silence of people doing arithmetic they don't want to finish. Okafor had the helm because Beatrix's hands were still shaking—not visibly, but I could see it in how she held her coffee mug, two-handed, like it might bolt. She'd been shaking since she dragged Kaine out of the collapsing wreck with ninety seconds of air left and no plan beyond *not letting go*.

I kept thinking about what she'd chosen. Not the strongbox. Not the manifests. Not the truth that would've cleared her mother's name or damned it, depending on which version you believed. She chose Kaine. A woman who'd bought their debt out of spite, who'd shown up with a corporate fleet and a flag-of-truce smile, who'd gone deep against every order and every instinct of self-preservation because vindication was down there and vindication doesn't float.

Beatrix went in after her anyway.

I keep a mental list of things I know about Beatrix Nakamura, and that day I had to cross out half of it and start over.

* * *

By evening we were anchored in the lee of a granite island twelve miles south of the glacier, close enough that on a quiet night you could still hear the ice talking to itself—a low groaning, like an old house settling, except the old house was a hundred thousand years old and had just eaten a shipwreck whole.

Okafor made stew. He always makes stew after bad days; he says it's because you can't argue with someone while handing them a bowl. The crew gathered in the galley in the loose, exhausted way of people who are too tired to maintain their grudges but not too tired to feel them.

Because here's the thing nobody says out loud: we were angry at her.

Not about the glacier. Not about Kaine. About the manifests. About the three days she'd sat on what she'd found in the hold—the evidence that the Meridian hadn't sunk, she'd been *sunk*, deliberately, on orders—and told us nothing. We'd been diving a crime scene thinking it was a treasure hunt. Reyes had nearly died on the second tide for cargo Beatrix already suspected was a lie. And she'd let us.

"You could've told us," Reyes said finally. He said it to his stew, which was braver than saying it to her face, and also less brave, and both at once.

"I know," Beatrix said.

"That's it? That's the whole speech?"

"I was going to tell you. After."

"After *what*? After we cleared out with whatever wasn't nailed down? After the fleet—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "You didn't trust us with it."

"I didn't trust *myself* with it," she said, and there was something in her voice that made the galley go quiet, that raw scraped-out tone of a person who has been carrying something heavy for a very long time and just set it down and discovered their arms don't know how to be empty. "My mother spent ten years making sure nobody knew what happened on that ship. I grew up inside that secret without knowing it existed. When I found the manifests, I—" She looked at her hands. "I did what she would've done. That's not an excuse. That's the diagnosis."

Silence. Okafor ladled more stew into Reyes's bowl without being asked, which in Okafor language means *sit down and think about what you're saying before you say worse*.

It was Junie—that's me, hi—who broke it, because apparently I have a gift for saying the thing everyone's thinking at exactly the wrong moment. "You know she was going to tell us," I said. "On the fifth tide. Before the fleet moved. She had the logbook out. I saw her copying pages."

"You saw her *hiding* pages," Reyes said.

"I saw her deciding," I said. "There's a difference. It takes longer."

Reyes looked at me for a long moment, and then at Beatrix, and whatever he found in her face made him exhale through his nose and pick up his spoon. "One more secret," he said. "One more, Captain, and I'm swimming home."

"One more," she agreed, "and you can have the boat."

He snorted. It wasn't forgiveness. It was a down payment on forgiveness, the kind you make in installments over years, and everybody at that table understood the terms. But he picked up his spoon, and Okafor refilled the pot, and outside the porthole the ice groaned its long slow sentence, and that was as reconciled as any of us were going to get that night.

Bruised reconciliation. That's the honest term for it. You don't come back from a thing like that clean. You come back scarred, and the scar is the proof you stayed.

* * *

Kaine's tender came alongside at dawn.

I was on deck watch, wrapped in everything I owned, watching the light come up gray and thin over the water, when I heard the engines. My first instinct was to reach for the flare gun, which tells you something about how the last week had recalibrated my nervous system. But the tender came in slow and unlit, riding high in the water—lightened, I realized later, by everything they'd abandoned on the seafloor—and Vesper Kaine stood at the rail looking like a woman who'd been through a wringer and come out pressed flat.

She came aboard without asking permission, which was very her, and stopped in front of Beatrix, who came up from below with her hair still wet from a sleepless night and her face arranged into polite nothing.

They looked at each other for a long time. Ten years of rivalry, one drowned woman between them, one glacier, one debt. I stood by the rail pretending to check a line and listening with my entire body.

"I owe you my life," Kaine said. It clearly cost her. Every word came out like a tooth.

"You owe me nothing," Beatrix said. "I'd have done it for anyone."

"No, you wouldn't." Kaine's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, more like the ghost of one visiting a house it used to live in. "That's the thing I never understood about your mother, either. She'd have let me drown. She told me so once, to my face, over cards." Her voice caught on the name she wasn't saying, and recovered. "But you're not her. Thank God for that, honestly. One of her was enough."

"Vesper—"

"The debt's void." Kaine said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "All of it. The paper's worthless now anyway—half my backers pulled out when the Meridian went down with the evidence, and the other half will pull out when they hear I let a salvage crew beat the fleet by a tide. I'm ruined, Nakamura. Professionally speaking." She straightened. "Which means the debt is an accounting fiction, and I refuse to spend the rest of my life being fictional at you."

Beatrix blinked. Of all the outcomes she'd war-gamed on the run out of the ice, I'd have bet anything this wasn't one of them. "You're voiding it without being asked."

"I'm *telling* you I voided it. There's a difference. One of them means you asked." Kaine glanced around the deck—at the scarred hull, at me pretending not to listen, at Okafor standing in the wheelhouse doorway with his arms crossed and his face carefully neutral in the way of a man who remembers every bad deal he's ever watched this woman make. "Your mother and I kept accounts our whole lives. Who won, who owed, who got the last word. I thought that was what she took from me when she cut me out. The ledger." She looked out at the water, at the gray horizon where the glacier wasn't visible anymore but somehow still dominated the view. "Turns out what she took was the part where you stop keeping score. I'm too old to learn it and apparently too stubborn to die without trying."

"Is that an apology?" Beatrix asked.

"It's a *down payment*," Kaine said, and I swear I saw Okafor almost smile. "The rest arrives by mail. Probably. If I can figure out what the rest even is." She turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and said, without turning around: "There's a letter. In her effects, if she left any. She always said she would. Your mother. She was a woman who wrote things down and then hid them where only dead men and daughters would find them." A pause. "If it exists, and it's addressed to me—I'd want it. Even now. *Especially* now."

"She'd have burned it," Beatrix said quietly.

"Yes," Kaine said. "Probably. That was rather the point of her." And she stepped down into her tender and was gone, the fleet withdrawing south behind her like a tide going out, taking their cranes and their contracts and their claim stakes with them, leaving behind nothing but wake and the strange new fact of a world where Vesper Kaine didn't own us.

Beatrix stood at the rail a long time after the sound of the engines faded. I didn't go to her. Some conversations a person needs to have alone, even in company.

But I saw her hand go to her jacket pocket, where the strongbox key had lived since the fourth tide, and I saw her take it out and look at it, and put it back.

Not yet. But soon.

* * *

We ran south for two days on a prop shaft that sang like a dying whale, and somewhere off Cape Decision the crew started telling stories, which is how you know a crew is going to hold. Reyes reenacted the growler incident with salt shakers and a bread roll and cast himself as the hero, which was historically inaccurate and universally accepted. Okafor told the story of the exiled diver—Sable, the one who'd sold us out to the fleet—in a flat voice with no ending, because there wasn't one yet; she'd walked off the tender at the last port with her pay and no backward glance, and some debts don't void, they just change hands. I told them about the pressure-fist of the calving, the white ceiling rising, and I gave them the true version, mostly, and found that the telling made the memory smaller. That's the other thing stories are for.

And Beatrix listened, and laughed once—actually laughed, short and rusty, like a hinge that needed oiling—and said almost nothing, and carried her pocket-shaped secret around the deck of the wounded *Wren* like a woman pacing a cell whose door she hasn't decided to open.

I caught her at the rail on the last night, both of us supposed to be off-watch, neither of us asleep. The stars were out in that obscene northern abundance, the whole Milky Way spilled across the sky like someone had knocked over the good liquor.

"You're going to open it," I said. Not a question.

"In harbor," she said. "Alone."

"Okay." I turned my mug in my hands. "For what it's worth—whatever's in there, letters or ledgers or the answer to everything—you already survived the worst version. You survived *not knowing*. Everything else is just reading."

She looked at me sideways, and for a second I saw her weighing whether to deflect, and then not bothering. "When did you get wise?"

"About four minutes before the glacier fell on me. It focused the mind."

That earned the second laugh, fainter. Then she said, "Junie. What I hid. Would you have done different?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to Beatrix Nakamura had a documented failure rate. "No," I said. "I'd have hidden it worse. At least you told us eventually. Eventually counts."

"Eventually counts," she repeated, like she was filing it somewhere she'd need it later.

Below us, the engine room ticked and settled. Ahead of us, past the dark water and the dark islands, home harbor waited with its lights and its gossip and its charts that still showed the Meridian as a rumor. And in her pocket, Beatrix carried a small brass key and whatever promise her mother had sealed under it ten years and one lifetime ago.

Two days later we raised the breakwater, and Beatrix went below, and came up with the strongbox in both hands, and set it on the chart table where everyone could see it.

"Before we dock," she said, "there's something you all earned the right to witness."

Okafor killed the engine. The boat drifted, quiet, rocking, home in sight and unhurried.

She put the key in the lock.
