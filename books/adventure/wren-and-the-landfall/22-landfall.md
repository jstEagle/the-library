## 22. Landfall

The harbor came up out of the fog the way home always does—too soon and not soon enough.

I was at the wheel for the last three hours, which surprised nobody, least of all me. Okafor had tried to take it twice, and both times I'd told him I was fine, and both times he'd looked at me with that expression he saves for my lies, the one that means *I'll allow it, but I'm keeping receipts.* Junie sat on the foredeck with her feet dangling over the anchor windlass like she hadn't spent a week breathing compressed air inside a dying glacier. Ruiz was below, re-tying knots in lines that didn't need re-tying, because Ruiz processes grief and relief through his hands and we'd all learned not to interrupt him mid-process.

Behind us, the fleet's tender held station at the edge of the shipping lanes, close enough to watch, far enough to pretend it wasn't watching. Kaine's flag of truce had come down two days ago. What replaced it wasn't hostility exactly. It was something more uncomfortable: attention.

"They're still there," Junie said, coming into the wheelhouse with two mugs of coffee she'd doctored with enough condensed milk to qualify as dessert. She handed me one. "You want me to go say something?"

"What would you say?"

"I don't know. 'Nice glacier.' 'Sorry about your vindication.'" She leaned against the chart table. "She did void the debt."

"She did."

"Without being asked."

"I noticed."

Junie sipped her coffee and studied me over the rim. Twenty-three years old, lungs like a seal, and a talent for asking questions that sound casual until they've already cut you. "So what's eating you? Because you got what you wanted. The truth, mostly. Kaine off your back. Everybody alive, which after Tuesday I'd have called ambitious." She paused. "It's the harbor, isn't it."

I kept my eyes on the water. A loon crossed our bow, unimpressed by everything we'd survived.

"When we tie up," I said, "people are going to ask where we went. And I have spent ten years teaching this crew that the answer to that question is always *nothing, nowhere, nobody's business.*"

"And now?"

"Now the honest answer is a wreck full of scuttled cargo and a dead woman's confession, sitting under a glacier that ate half our dive gear and nearly ate us."

"So you tell them the boring version," Junie said. "Salvage run, bad ice, came home early."

"That's the version I'd tell."

"But?"

I looked at her. She was watching me with that open face she never quite learned to guard, the one that made her dangerous as a diver and worse as a witness. "But my mother spent her whole life making sure nobody ever wrote down where the Meridian was. She hid it so well she died in the hiding. And then she scuttled the thing herself, because whatever was on those manifests was worse than treasure, and she decided the ocean should keep it." I turned the mug in my hands. "And I've been doing the same thing my whole career. Smaller wrecks, smaller lies, same instinct. Hide it, sit on it, keep the crew safe by keeping them ignorant."

"You kept us safe," Junie said. "Mostly. You also lied to us for about four days, which Okafor has a whole spreadsheet about."

"He showed me the spreadsheet."

"There are color codes."

"I know. I saw the red column." I almost smiled. Almost. "Here's the thing, Junie. My mother's letters—the ones in the strongbox—they weren't apologies. They were instructions. *Tell it plainly,* she wrote. *A secret kept out of fear rots. A story told out loud becomes a map other people can use to stay alive.* She buried the Meridian because she thought silence was protection. It cost her everything. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life paying interest on her mistake."

Junie was quiet for a moment. Then: "Okafor owes me twenty dollars."

"You bet on me?"

"We bet on *when* you'd figure it out. He said by the sixth tide. I said after, once we were actually home, because you're stubborn in transit but you're honest at anchor." She drained her coffee. "For the record, I think you're doing the right thing. Also for the record, I think it's going to be a mess."

"Those can both be true."

"That's what I keep telling Ruiz."

* * *

We came through the breakwater at slack water, which felt like a small mercy from a sea that had shown us very few. The harbor was the same harbor we'd left eleven days ago—same rust-streaked cannery building, same gulls conducting their eternal argument over the fish waste dock, same knot of old-timers who gather at the fuel house every morning to appraise every boat that comes in like judges at a livestock show.

What was different was us. I could feel it in the way the crew moved on deck. We'd left as a quiet boat running quiet work. We were coming back as survivors of something, and survivors carry a look, and harbors read looks the way charts read soundings.

Okafor came up beside me as I brought us in. "Dock four's open," he said. "Harbormaster flagged it yesterday. Word travels."

"Word about what? Nobody knows where we went."

"Nobody knows where you went. Everybody knows Vesper Kaine's tender has been sitting outside the lane for two days flying your wake like a remora. In this town, that's a headline." He took the wheel without asking, and this time I let him, because some arguments you win by losing. "Last chance, Captain. Boring version. Bad ice, came home early. I'll handle the fuel house crowd myself. They like me. I'm charming."

"You are not charming. You're intimidating and occasionally polite, which they mistake for charm."

"Same thing, if you dress well."

I watched the dock come closer. Our berth. The fenders hung where we'd left them, someone—Ruiz's sister, probably—having checked on the boat while we were gone, because that's what this harbor does when one of its own is out past dark. That small kindness hit me harder than the glacier had.

"No," I said. "The real version."

Okafor glanced at me. "You're sure."

"My mother left me two things. A strongbox and a habit of lying. I've opened the strongbox. Time to work on the habit."

He nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders let go—not surprise, because Okafor is never surprised, just a settling, like a man who's been holding a rope taut for days finally feeling it come right. "Then I'll get the paperwork started. Salvage registration, find report, the whole ugly stack. You know what that means."

"It means the state gets a percentage."

"It means the state gets a percentage, the historical society sends a committee, and some academic writes a paper calling you an important primary source." He grimaced. "You ready to be a primary source?"

"Ask me again after coffee."

* * *

The mooring lines went over at 0940. By 0955 we had an audience.

I'd expected curiosity. I hadn't expected the whole fuel house contingent plus the harbormaster plus Elena Vasquez from the regional records office, who materialized the way bureaucrats do, already holding forms, as though she'd been standing on the dock since before we left and simply been waiting for the plot to require her.

Beatrix Nakamura, I reminded myself. Celebrated novelist. Expert at telling stories. Terrible, historically, at telling this one.

I stepped onto the dock and faced them, and for a second the old reflex rose in me like ballast—*say nothing, smile, deflect, nothing nowhere nobody's business*—and I let it rise and let it pass, the way you let a swell pass under the hull instead of fighting it.

"My name is Beatrix Nakamura," I said, to the gulls and the harbormaster and Elena Vasquez and the whole listening gray morning. "Eleven days ago my crew and I located the wreck of the *Meridian*, lost 1998, position logged and registered as of this morning. I'm filing the find report today. Full coordinates, full survey, full account." A murmur went through the fuel house crowd, and I talked over it. "Some of you knew my mother, Mara Nakamura. Some of you remember that she disappeared chasing this wreck, and that the official report called it recklessness. It wasn't recklessness. She found something on that ship that somebody powerful wanted buried, and she chose to bury it deeper herself, and it killed her, and I spent ten years not knowing why." I paused. "Now I know why. And I'm going to tell you all of it, because she asked me to, and because secrets kept out of fear rot."

Silence. Then Elena Vasquez said, "The registration fee schedule changed in March," which is bureaucrat for *I am moved beyond words*, and the harbormaster took off his cap, which is harbormaster for the same thing, and old Anders from the fuel house said, "Mara Nakamura's girl," in a voice like a benediction, and I had to look at the water for a second because my mother's name still lands in my chest like a hook set deep.

Okafor put a hand on my shoulder. Just briefly. Just enough.

* * *

We spent the afternoon turning the wheelhouse into an office. Charts spread across the chart table—the old ones, my mother's, with her annotations and my childhood handwriting layered over them like sediment—and I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the sixth tide. I drew the new chart.

Not the secret one. The honest one. The *Meridian*'s true position, marked clean, depth noted, hazard boundaries drawn in red where the calving zone now reached. Where my mother had written *KEEP OFF* in letters meant to frighten, I wrote *MERIDIAN — SCUTTLED BY ORDER OF HER OWN MASTER, 1998. SEE FIND REPORT.* Where my childhood self had traced a route I didn't remember learning, I inked it permanent, because that route belonged to the record now, not to the fog.

Junie watched me work. "Doesn't it feel wrong?" she asked. "Giving away the route. Anyone with this chart can go dive it."

"Anyone with this chart can go die on it too," I said. "That's why the hazards are marked. That's the point. My mother thought she could protect people by keeping them ignorant. The ice proved otherwise—it doesn't care whether you know it's there or not. All ignorance does is make sure you meet it unprepared." I finished the last bearing and set down the pen. "A map isn't an invitation, Junie. It's a warning with good manners."

Ruiz appeared in the doorway, hands finally still, holding a printout. "Kaine's tender filed departure papers an hour ago. Heading south." He hesitated. "There's a note attached. Addressed to you."

I took it. Heavy paper, corporate letterhead, six words in handwriting far too elegant for the woman it belonged to:

*The chart is better than the apology.*

I laughed—actually laughed, first time in eleven days—and Junie demanded to see it, and read it twice, and said, "Is that a threat or a compliment?"

"With Kaine," I said, "it's both. It's always both."

* * *

Evening came down soft, the way it does here when the weather forgives you. The crew gathered on deck with the good coffee and the last of Ruiz's emergency cookies, which he guards like state secrets and had surrendered without being asked, which is how I knew he was okay. Mostly.

Okafor raised his mug. "To the six tides."

"To the six tides," we echoed.

"To the tide we didn't need," Junie added, "because the glacier quit early."

"To the spreadsheet," Ruiz said solemnly, "and its red column, now closed."

I let them talk, and I looked out at the harbor lights coming on one by one across the water, and I thought about my mother's last letter, the one addressed to me, the one I'd read alone in my bunk three nights ago with the boat rocking gentle at anchor and the whole crew pretending not to hear me cry, because that's what crews do.

*If you're reading this,* she'd written, *then you found it, which means you stopped hiding, which means I finally did one thing right as your mother even if I did everything wrong as a partner, a captain, and a friend. The truth was never the treasure, Bea. The truth was the chart. Mark it well and give it away.*

The Meridian sits under her glacier tonight, holds flooded, cargo surrendered, logbook dissolving by degrees into the cold black water that keeps what it keeps. Someday the calving will take the hull entirely, and someday after that even the charts will be history instead of warning, and that's right. That's how it should go. The wilderness remembers longer than we do, and it forgets faster than we fear, and both of those are mercies.

But the story's out of the ice now. It's on the register, in the record, in the mouths of the fuel house crowd who will embellish it beyond recognition by spring, and I find I don't mind. Let them make it bigger than it was. My mother spent her life shrinking the truth to hide it. I can spare a little exaggeration to honor it.

Junie bumped my shoulder. "Captain. You're doing the staring-at-the-water thing."

"I'm allowed. I earned the staring-at-the-water thing."

"You did." She was quiet a moment, then, softer: "What happens now? For the boat. For us."

I looked around at them—my fractured, loyal, spreadsheet-keeping, cookie-hoarding crew, bruised and whole and still here—and I felt the answer arrive the way landfall arrives, too soon and not soon enough, certain as a bearing.

"Now we take honest work," I said. "Registered wrecks, published surveys, jobs we can put our names on. It pays worse and sleeps better." I grinned at Okafor. "And first thing tomorrow, we start a new chart. Blank one. Whole coast on it. Everything we find, we mark."

Okafor snorted. "You realize a chart like that will make us famous."

"Worse," I said. "It'll make us *findable*."

Junie laughed, and Ruiz shook his head and smiled into his coffee, and out past the breakwater the first stars came up over water that had tried very hard to kill us and failed, and I stood there on my deck in my own harbor with my mother's name finally spoken aloud in the place she'd sailed from, and I understood, at last and completely, what she'd meant.

Landfall isn't a place. It's a decision—you make it, or you spend your whole life standing off in the fog, hailing a shore you're too afraid to touch.

We'd made it. The anchor was down. The light was on.

And somewhere south, a woman who'd spent thirty years chasing my mother's ghost was reading a letter I'd sent her, and I had a strongbox-shaped feeling that the story wasn't entirely done with any of us yet.
