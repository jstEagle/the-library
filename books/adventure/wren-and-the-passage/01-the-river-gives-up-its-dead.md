## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been eating the valley for eleven days, and by the time Petra Zhou brought her client down to its edge, it had begun, in the manner of rivers, to give things back.

She heard it before she saw it—the water running thick and brown with everything it had stolen upstream. Fence posts. A door, still on its hinges, turning slowly like a man trying to decide which way to go. A child's boot, upright, riding the current with terrible dignity. The Kessume River in flood was not a river so much as a debt collector, taking from the high country and dumping its spoils on the lowlands for anyone patient enough to wait.

"Is it always like this?" asked Edmund Voss, standing at a careful distance from the bank in boots that cost more than Petra's entire kit.

"Every spring," she said. "Some springs worse."

"And this one?"

"This one's showing off."

Voss laughed, delighted, the way rich men were always delighted by other people's weather. He was fifty-something, silver-templed, with the sunburn of a man who bought his adventures retail—three weeks of guided trekking through the flood country of the Serran lowlands, a wilderness experience curated to be survivable. He'd hired her through a broker who hadn't asked too many questions, which was how Petra preferred to be hired. Her references were excellent and entirely fictional. Her name on the contract was *Petra Zhou*, which was true enough; everything else about her was a house built on sand, and she'd been waiting twenty years for the tide to come in.

She knew rivers. That much of her was real. She could read water the way literate people read pages—the way it braided around a submerged fence line, the way it went glassy-smooth over deep holes, the way it told you where the ground beneath it had given up. It was the one inheritance from her mother that no border official could confiscate: you can take a girl out of a drowned country, but the drowned country stays in her hands.

They were three days into the circuit, camped above the floodline on a shelf of red clay, when the river gave up something that stopped Petra's breath.

* * *

It happened late in the afternoon, in that hour when the light goes long and amber and makes even wreckage look holy. Voss was back at camp nursing a blister and a bottle of single malt he'd insisted on packing, and Petra had gone down alone to check the gauge stake she'd driven into the bank their first morning—a guide's habit, measuring the river's temper twice a day, because floods receded slower than clients believed and faster than they feared.

The water had dropped perhaps forty centimeters since dawn. It had left a tideline of gifts along the clay: silt laid down in fine gray bands like the rings of a tree, a dead ewe bloated and peaceful-looking, half a rowboat, tangles of fencing wire furred with weed. Petra walked the line without much interest. She'd seen a hundred floods leave a thousand such offerings. The dead ewe she noted and moved past; there would be more, and worse, and none of it was hers to grieve.

Then she saw the corner.

It protruded from the bank itself—not the tideline but the cut face of clay above it, where the river had undercut the earth and then, retreating, exposed what it had been chewing on. A square corner, dark wood banded with iron, wedged in the raw earth like a tooth in a gum. The flood hadn't deposited it. The flood had *uncovered* it. Which meant it had been buried in the bank, or buried upstream and driven into the soft clay by the current with enough force to lodge it deep—and either way, it had not come here by accident.

Petra stood very still.

Twenty years of practice went into standing still. She let her eyes do the walking first, sweeping the far bank, the treeline, the ridgeline behind camp. Nothing moved except a heron lifting off downstream, unhurried, unbothered. The valley was empty of everything but water and light.

She climbed down to it.

The clay sucked at her boots, cold through the seams. Up close the object resolved into a chest—salvage chest, she thought immediately, the kind used on coastal wrecks, double-banded iron, corners reinforced, built to survive exactly this kind of abuse. Salt-water construction, hundreds of kilometers inland. Which meant it had traveled. Which meant it had come off a boat, or out of a hold, or off a wreck that had been moving before it died.

And stamped into the iron band, filled with verdigris-green corrosion but unmistakable, was the seal.

A wren in flight, wings spread over a broken crown.

Petra did not touch it for a long moment. She made herself look at the river instead, at the brown water shouldering past, at the hills going gold and shadow, at anything other than the thing her hands already wanted. Because she knew that seal. She had known it before she knew her own name, pressed in wax on the letters her mother burned one by one in a steel basin while Petra, seven years old, watched the ash curl. *Never keep them, little bird,* her mother had said, in the language they weren't supposed to speak anymore. *Paper is a trail. Wax is a confession.*

Her mother had been wrong about a lot of things, but she had not been wrong about that.

Veyra. The name rose in her like silt stirred from a riverbed. A country that had existed for four hundred years and then, twenty years ago, had been erased—not conquered, not renamed, simply *dissolved*, its borders absorbed by neighbors who divided it like heirs dividing a dead man's coat. Its maps were pulled. Its archives sealed. Its citizens scattered and, in some cases, quietly hunted, because a nation that stops existing leaves behind paperwork, and paperwork has a way of asking questions nobody wants answered.

Petra had been born under that broken-crown seal. She had left it at nine years old with her mother's hand crushing hers on the deck of a ferry, looking back at a coastline that would never appear on a map again. Everything since—two names discarded, two countries' worth of blacklisting, a career built on being exactly nowhere when anyone came looking—had grown from that single departure. She had become an expert in leaving. It was the only discipline she'd ever mastered completely.

And now the river had coughed up a piece of the country that owned her, and set it in the clay like bait.

*Objects carry histories,* her mother used to say. *Histories carry hunters.* Petra had lived by it. She had passed up salvage before—real salvage, valuable salvage—because value attracts attention and attention was the one thing she could not afford. She had once watched a man in a border town get taken away in a white van because he'd kept his grandmother's tea service too visibly. She knew the arithmetic. Every gram of the past you carry is weight added to your pack, and the pack was already heavy enough.

But her hand was already reaching.

That was the thing she would think about later, in the dark, in the days after: that her body decided before her mind finished voting. Her fingers found the iron band and the clay released the chest with a sound like a kiss, wet and reluctant, and she hauled it up the bank to the flat rock above the tideline, and she knelt there in the failing light with her heart going hard against her ribs.

The lock was a simple salt-corroded latch. It gave on the third try.

Inside: water, of course. River water, gray and cold, sloshing over a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, tied with cord that someone had knotted in a pattern Petra recognized before she understood that she recognized it—a fisherman's hitch, doubled, the way you tied things you expected the sea to test. Under the oilcloth, wrapped tight as a wound: papers, swollen and fused into a single brick. And beneath the papers, settled into the bottom of the chest as if it had been placed there deliberately, last, the way you place a stone on a grave—

A locket. Silver, small, dented along one edge. On its face, worn nearly smooth by years of a thumb worrying it, two engraved initials:

*M.O.*

Mara Okafor-Vey. Her mother.

The world went narrow and loud. The river's voice swelled until it was the only sound, and Petra knelt on the rock with the locket in her palm and felt twenty years of careful distance collapse all at once, like a snow bridge giving way, like a bank undercut by flood.

Her mother had been dead fourteen years. Buried in a country that wasn't theirs, under a name that wasn't hers, in a grave Petra had visited exactly once and never returned to, because returning to things was how you got caught. And now here was her mother's thumb-worn locket riding a flooded river two borders and a lifetime from anywhere Mara Okafor-Vey had ever drawn breath, inside a chest bearing the seal of a nation that officially had never existed.

This was not chance. Petra had survived too long to believe in chance. Chests did not swim. Lockets did not migrate. Someone had put this in the water—or lost it from a vessel upstream, a wreck, a sinking—and the river had carried it down through the whole drowned valley and set it at the feet of the one person in ten thousand who would know what it meant. The odds of that were not small. They were astronomical. And astronomical odds, in Petra's experience, were usually not luck. They were design.

She looked up at the ridgeline again. Empty. The heron was gone. The light was dying, blue shadows pooling in the clay cuts, and somewhere upstream the flood was still working, still prying loose whatever else the water had been keeping.

"Petra?" Voss's voice, distant, from the camp above. "Everything all right? You've been down there an age."

She closed her fist around the locket. Cold metal, warm hand.

"Fine," she called back, and her voice came out level, guide-calm, the professional mask sliding on so smoothly it frightened her. "River's dropping. We'll make good time tomorrow."

She worked fast after that. The papers stayed in the chest—fused, fragile, unreadable tonight—but the chest itself she could not carry openly, not with a client three meters from her tent flap. So she wrapped it in her spare tarp, lashed it under the willow scrub below the floodline where the clay overhung, and covered the disturbed bank with cut ferns, and if Edmund Voss noticed his guide return with mud to her thighs and a strange set to her shoulders, he had the good manners—or the blindness of the rich—to say nothing.

That night she lay awake listening to the river.

It talked in its sleep, the way big water does, a low continuous muttering, and Petra lay in her bag with the locket pressed between her palms and tried to think like a survivor instead of a daughter. Leave it. Bury it deeper. Walk away tomorrow, finish the circuit, collect the fee, be somewhere else by the week's end under another sky. That was the play. That was always the play. Twenty years of plays, and every one of them ended with her walking away, lighter, safer, smaller.

But the locket was warm now, warmed by her skin, and against every instinct she owned—the instincts that had kept her alive, the instincts that were her mother's final gift and final cage—Petra found herself doing the one thing she had trained herself never to do.

She was making plans. Not escape plans. *Toward* plans.

The manifest papers in the chest, fused though they were, had shown one legible fragment through the oilcloth's gap before the light failed: a printed header, partially dissolved, but with numbers she could read plainly. Coordinates. Sea coordinates, lat and long in the old Veyran format, pointing not upstream but *downriver*, out toward the coast, toward open water.

Something had sunk offshore carrying chests like this one. Something was still down there. And the river, patient as any debt collector, had just sent her the first installment.

Petra closed her eyes. In the dark behind them she saw her mother at the steel basin, feeding letters to the flame, saying *never keep them, little bird.*

"I'm sorry," she whispered to the dark, to the river, to the woman in the grave under the wrong name. "I'm keeping this one."

Outside, the Kessume muttered on, carrying its cargo of drowned things toward the sea—and somewhere down that brown road, Petra understood with a dread that wore curiosity's clothing, the rest of her past was waiting for the water to give it up.
