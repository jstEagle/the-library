## 2. Salt Hollow

The storm broke an hour before dawn, and Petra walked into Salt Hollow with the chest strapped to her back like a second spine.

She had meant to leave the flood country entirely—cross the ridge road, put forty miles between herself and that river before anyone thought to ask what else the water had given up. But the rain came down in ropes, and the ridge road washed out at the switchback, and by the time she'd doubled back toward the coast her boots were full of water and her hands had gone the color of old candle wax. The village appeared out of the murk the way villages do in bad weather: a suggestion of rooftops, a light or two burning yellow through shuttered windows, the smell of woodsmoke and fish guts and salt. A harbor town clinging to a shelf of rock above a gray sea, its boats hauled up and lashed down against the weather.

Petra had a rule about places like this. She knew them too well. She'd been born in one—or something like one, before Veyra stopped existing on paper—and she'd hidden in a dozen since, because harbor towns were full of people who understood that some questions weren't asked. But harbor towns were also small, and small places remembered faces, and faces were how you got found.

Still. The cold had gotten into her bones somewhere on the ridge, the deep kind that doesn't shake out, and the chest was heavy, and the tide was coming in over the only road south.

The inn sat at the crook of the harbor road, two stories, slate roof furred green with damp, a hand-painted sign that read *The Gull's Rest* in letters someone had touched up recently enough to still smell faintly of paint. Petra stood across the street for a long moment, letting the rain hammer her hood, doing the arithmetic she always did: How many exits. How many eyes. How fast could she be gone if the room turned wrong.

Two doors—the front and a kitchen entrance trailing steam from someone cooking breakfast. One road in, one road out, unless you counted the harbor, and nobody was putting a boat into that sea today. A handful of villagers moving between shelters, heads down, none of them looking at her twice. Fishermen learn quickly not to stare at strangers in storms; half of them had been strangers once themselves.

It would do. It would have to.

Inside smelled of bread and lamp oil and wet wool. A fire burned low in a stone hearth big enough to roast an ox, and behind the bar stood a woman built like the harbor pilings—broad, salt-bleached, immovable—with forearms that suggested she'd hauled more nets than any man in the room and a face that suggested she'd won every argument she'd ever bothered to have.

"You're dripping on my floor," the woman said, without hostility, as though stating a fact about the weather.

"I'll pay for the floor."

"You'll pay for a room and dry your clothes by my fire, is what you'll do." She jerked her chin toward the hearth. "Maren. I keep this place. You'll be wanting food, and don't tell me no, because I can see your ribs through your coat."

Petra's instincts said *leave*. Her body said *sit down* and overruled them. That was the danger of these places—they got you when you were too tired to argue. She set her pack by the fire, keeping the strap looped around her wrist, and lowered herself onto a bench whose wood had been worn smooth by fifty years of other tired travelers.

"Name?" Maren asked, sliding a bowl of fish stew across the bar without waiting to be asked.

"Petra." Just Petra. First names were currency; surnames were debts.

"Petra." Maren tasted it, shrugged. "There's a room at the top of the stairs. Two coppers a night, three if you want breakfast, which you do, because you look like a woman who hasn't eaten properly since the last government fell."

Something in Petra's chest flinched at that—old reflex, old country—and Maren caught it, because Maren missed nothing, but the innkeeper only refilled the bowl and moved on, and Petra told herself the flinch meant nothing. Twenty years of flinching meant nothing. That was the whole point of twenty years.

* * *

The chest sat under her bed all day while the storm worked itself out against the coast, and Petra lay on the narrow mattress listening to it not make a sound, which was somehow worse than if it had been ticking.

She'd examined it on the riverbank, of course. Before the client, before the long walk out, she'd knelt in the mud with the water roaring past and turned the thing over in her hands. Bronze-banded oak, salvage-stamped along one edge—a wreck tag, the kind harbormasters nailed to recovered cargo—and pressed into the lid, a seal.

A crest: a tern flying over three waves, beneath a broken crown.

Veyra.

Her mother had drawn that crest in the margins of her letters, always small, always quick, as if even ink could be arrested. Petra had been eight years old the last time she saw it, and she had spent the twenty years since making sure no document, no border stamp, no ledger anywhere connected her to that drowned nation. And now the sea itself had handed her the proof, wedged in a riverbank sixty miles inland, wrapped in fishing net like a gift.

Objects carry histories. Histories carry hunters. She'd pocketed it anyway, because her hands had decided before her head did, and her hands had been right more often than her head.

Now, in the gray afternoon light leaking under the door, she finally opened it properly.

The lock had rusted open years ago. Inside: water-stained cloth, a brass lantern gone green with age, and papers. Most were pulp. But one had been oiled or waxed against the damp, and it survived intact—a manifest, written in a careful bureaucratic hand, listing cargo by crate and seal number. Salvage manifest, she realized. From a wreck. And at the bottom, coordinates rendered in the old Veyran system, degrees and minutes she had to convert twice to believe:

A site four miles off the coast. Off *this* coast. The storm hadn't carried the chest here; the current had, all the way from a wreck lying on the seabed within sight of Salt Hollow's harbor light.

And beneath the manifest, wrapped in oilcloth so fine it had kept its contents dry across twenty years and however many miles of cold water: a locket.

Small. Silver, tarnished black. On the back, scratched in a hand Petra knew better than her own—because it was the hand that had taught hers—

*E.O.*

Elin Okafor. Her mother's initials. Her mother's locket, in a salvage chest from a wreck off this coast, in a village where Petra had stopped by accident.

She sat very still for a long time. Outside, the storm threw spray against the shutters like a fist demanding entry. Inside, Petra held the locket in her palm and felt the trapdoor open quietly beneath everything she'd built.

The past didn't hunt you, she thought. It just waited where you were going.

* * *

Maren found her at the fire that evening, because of course she did. Petra had chosen the corner bench with its back to the wall and a view of both doors, and Maren crossed the room and sat down across from her anyway, uninvited, with two cups of something amber and a look on her face like a woman settling in for a negotiation she'd already won.

"You opened it," Maren said.

Petra went cold from the scalp down. "I don't know what you mean."

"The chest." Maren nodded at the pack, which was closed, latched, and sitting exactly where it had been all day. "You carried it in like it was full of lead and guilt. You haven't let go of the strap since you walked in. And this morning you came down the stairs with silver polish on your sleeve." She slid a cup across. "I've run a harbor inn for thirty years, girl. I know what salvagers look like, what smugglers look like, what grief looks like. You're wearing all three."

Petra's hand drifted, casually, to the knife at her belt. Maren watched her do it and laughed—not cruelly, but with the weariness of a woman who'd seen the gesture before.

"If I wanted trouble for you, I'd have mentioned the chest to Harl already. He's the harbormaster, and he talks to everyone, and he'd have sold you to the first licensed fleet that anchored here inside an hour." She drank. "I haven't mentioned it. Ask yourself why."

"Why?"

"Because you looked at my harbor this morning like you were memorizing the exits, and then you looked at my fire like you'd forgotten what they were for." Maren set down her cup. "That's all. I'm not asking what's in the box. I'm telling you there's work, if you want it. My deckhand tore his shoulder on the storm lines. Boats need bailing, gear needs mending, and I pay fair. Stay the week. Work the week. Then decide whatever it is you're deciding."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no, and you pay for your room, and I wish you luck on the south road." Maren stood, joints cracking like rigging in a swell, and looked down at her with something that wasn't quite kindness and wasn't quite pity. "But I'll tell you one thing free, since you paid for the stew. This village has a way of making people stay who swore they wouldn't. Happens every winter. We call it the Gull's curse, and we charge extra for it come spring."

She left. Petra sat with the drink going cold in her hands and did the arithmetic again, the real arithmetic this time: a week of work meant a week of being seen. Being seen meant being remembered. Being remembered meant the next person hunting a woman named Petra Okafor would find her faster.

And yet.

She thought of her mother's initials polished bright in her pocket. Of a wreck lying four miles offshore, holding whatever else the sea had kept dry. Of a manifest with coordinates that pointed, like a finger bone, at the truth of why her family had been erased.

Warmth, she thought, was exactly how traps worked. You walked into them because they were warm.

Upstairs, she took out the locket and turned it under the lamp, and that was when she saw it: along the inner rim, so fine she'd missed it a dozen times, a line of numbers etched in her mother's hand. Not a date. Not coordinates.

A cipher key.

And on the back of the manifest, invisible until you knew to breathe on the page and tilt it to the lamplight, a ghost of ink answering it—her mother's handwriting, waiting twenty years underwater for someone who could read it.

Outside, far out past the harbor light, thunder rolled across open water, though the storm had spent itself hours ago.

Petra reached for her charts.
