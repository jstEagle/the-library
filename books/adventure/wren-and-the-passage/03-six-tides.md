## 3. Six Tides

The fleet came in on the morning tide, three hulls riding low with machinery, their dredge arms folded against the rails like sleeping insects. Petra watched them from the loft window of Maren's inn, a cup of tea gone cold in her hands, and felt the particular stillness that had always preceded her departures—the stillness of an animal deciding which way to run.

She did not run. That was the strange part. She stood at the glass and counted the ships the way she'd once counted switchbacks on a mountain face, and what she felt was not the old cold panic but something harder, something with edges. Purpose, maybe. Or its close cousin, stubbornness.

The lead vessel flew a government pennant—grey and gold, the colors of the Coastal Authority—and beneath it a working flag she recognized from her years in salvage ports: a black trident on white. Licensed recovery. Not pirates, not brokers, not the quiet men who paid in cash and asked no names. Worse, in its way. Bureaucracy moved slowly until it didn't, and then it moved like a landslide.

Maren came up the stairs with an armful of linens and stopped when she saw Petra's face. "Trouble?"

"Salvage fleet." Petra nodded at the window. "Three ships. They'll want harbor space, fresh water, charts."

"Then they'll want my inn too." Maren set down the linens with the unhurried economy of a woman who had weathered forty years of storms and tax collectors and considered both roughly equivalent. "You could be gone before they dock. Tide's fair till noon."

Petra looked at the harbor. At the boats knocking gently against the pilings, at the gulls arguing over the fish market scraps, at the crooked line of Salt Hollow's rooftops climbing the hill like a ladder someone had abandoned halfway up. She thought about the chest hidden beneath the loose floorboard of her room, the manifest inside it pointing seaward like a finger bone, the locket warm from her body even now, hanging against her sternum where it had slept for two nights.

"No," she said, and heard the word land in the room like a dropped anchor. "I'm done being gone before things arrive."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she didn't argue with it. "Then you'd best know who's hunting," she said, and went downstairs to make the fleet's beds.

* * *

They docked by midmorning. Petra watched them come up the harbor road without trying to hide, because hiding now would cost more than being seen: a woman alone, walking with a guide's rolling gait, carrying nothing worth stealing. Invisibility, she had learned, was not always absence. Sometimes it was ordinariness worn well.

The captain of the lead ship came ashore first. Petra knew her before anyone spoke her name—Ilsa Reyes, because the salvage world was small and gossip traveled faster than weather fronts. A compact woman in her fifties with grey shot through her black hair and the sun-cured leather of her face that came from thirty years of looking down into water. She walked like the deck was under her even on land. Two officers flanked her, and behind them came a man in a Coastal Authority coat who carried himself like paperwork given legs.

Petra bought mackerel at the fish stall and listened.

"—contract's sealed through the Authority," the coat-man was saying, his voice pitched for efficiency rather than secrecy. "Site survey puts the wreck in the deep channel off the Hollow. Dredges can't work it except on slack water, so we're tide-bound. Captain, your assessment?"

"Tide-bound is right." Reyes's voice was low and gravel-smooth, a voice used to cutting through engine noise. "Channel floor's soft silt over rock shelf. We'll need six clean windows minimum to run the suction lines and grid the site properly. Six tides, if the weather holds. Seven if it doesn't."

Six tides.

The number went into Petra like a splinter, precise and immediate. Twelve hours per tide, roughly, and the first already bleeding away while these people discussed logistics over fish and bread. She turned away from the stall, mackerel wrapped in paper forgotten in her hand, and began doing the arithmetic that had kept her alive in places where arithmetic was the only weapon allowed.

Tide tables were posted outside the harbormaster's office in every coastal village on this coast—law required it, for the fishermen's sake. Petra crossed the square, tore the top sheet from the board with the casual entitlement of someone checking the weather, and folded it into her jacket.

Back in her room, she spread it flat beside the manifest from the chest and worked by the grey window light.

The wreck lay in the deep channel—that much the coat-man had given away freely, and the manifest confirmed it with coordinates written in her mother's careful, slanted hand, coordinates that matched the channel's eastern edge where the old shipping lane bent around the shoal. The Meridian, or whatever the Authority believed lay there. They had a contract; contracts meant permits; permits meant schedules. And schedules, Petra knew from a lifetime of watching institutions hunt people, were the one thing bureaucracies could not bend, even for themselves.

She drew the tide curve on the back of the table sheet, a long breathing line of high water and low. Slack water at each turn—maybe forty minutes of calm in which dredges could work without fighting the current. The fleet would need six such windows to grid and clear the site. The table gave her the times: tonight's ebb, tomorrow's flood and ebb, the day after's pair, and then—

Her pencil stopped.

Four days. Ninety-six hours, give or take the weather Reyes had already begun to doubt. In ninety-six hours the dredges would chew through the channel silt and reach whatever the Meridian had carried for twenty years, and whatever proof slept in that hull would belong to the Coastal Authority, to the contract, to whatever quiet clause in some annex had sent a licensed fleet to swallow Veyra's last secret whole.

Petra sat back on the narrow bed and let the feeling move through her—not fear, though fear was there, familiar as an old boot—but something colder and cleaner underneath. For twenty years she had calculated escape routes. Exits. The fastest line off a mountain, out of a country, away from a name spoken too loudly in a port bar. Every plan she had ever made pointed away.

This one pointed toward the water.

It should have frightened her more than it did. Instead she felt the strange lightness she'd known only twice before—once on a ridge in the Karakoram with the storm coming and no descent left but the one she'd have to carve herself, and once, dimly, at nineteen, reading her mother's last letter by candlelight in a border town whose name she'd deliberately forgotten. The lightness of having nothing left to protect except the thing itself.

She took out the locket. It opened on its tiny hinge with a click she felt more than heard, and inside, the cipher her mother had pressed into soft metal waited the way it always waited: patient, certain, addressed to whoever loved her enough to learn her language. Petra wasn't finished with it—some of the notation still resisted her, rows of characters that repeated in patterns she almost understood, like a song heard through a wall. But she knew enough. Enough to know the wreck was real. Enough to know what it carried mattered more than the Authority's contract, more than her safety, more perhaps than anything else drowned in that grey channel.

A knock at the door. Petra's hand closed over the locket on pure reflex, twenty years of reflex, and she slid it beneath her collar before saying, "Yes."

Maren leaned in, flour on her forearms, eyes sharp. "The fleet captain's downstairs. Taking rooms. She asked—" Maren paused, and Petra watched her choose honesty over comfort, which was the thing Petra liked most about her and trusted least. "She asked if the village had any guides. Someone who knew the channel waters. Her survey charts are twenty years old."

The silence stretched between them like a rope bridge.

"She described you," Maren said quietly. "Said a fisherman told her there was a mountain guide staying at my inn. Said she'd like to buy your local knowledge, if you're selling."

Of course. Of course the world worked this way—every thread pulled tightens another. Petra felt the trap assembling itself around her, elegant and inevitable: refuse, and look like exactly what she was, a woman with something to hide; accept, and walk willingly aboard the ship that would grind the Meridian to fragments and carry the truth away in its holds.

Or.

Or walk aboard and see, with her own eyes, how far along they really were. Learn their schedule from the inside. Learn Reyes.

"I'll come down," Petra said.

Maren lingered. "You don't have to do whatever this is alone."

"Yes I do." Petra said it gently, and immediately regretted the gentleness, because it sounded like a door closing, and doors closing was the one habit she couldn't seem to break. "But thank you."

Downstairs, the common room smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool. Ilsa Reyes sat at the corner table with a chart spread before her, weighted at the corners with a mug, a knife, a compass case, and a stone—a captain's improvised paperweights, gathered from the world at hand. She looked up when Petra entered, and her gaze did the thing that experienced eyes did: went to Petra's boots, her hands, the set of her shoulders, cataloguing capability the way a merchant catalogued goods.

"You're the guide," Reyes said. Not a question.

"I'm a guide."

"Fleet's working the deep channel, four days, maybe five. My charts are older than some of my crew. I need someone who knows how that channel lies—where the silt shifts, where the current runs dirty." Reyes tapped the chart. "Authority pays well for local knowledge. No questions attached."

No questions attached. Petra almost smiled. Everyone in this business offered no questions, and everyone collected them anyway, quietly, in interest.

"The channel shifts after every spring flood," Petra said, pulling out the chair opposite Reyes and sitting down, because sitting down was a commitment and she had decided, somewhere between the fish stall and the stairwell, to commit. "Your charts will show the old lane. The lane has moved east, maybe two hundred meters. If your dredges follow the charts instead of the water, you'll spend all six tides digging mud."

Reyes studied her across the chart, and something passed over her face—not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. One professional reading another.

"You've worked salvage."

"I've worked everywhere."

A ghost of a smile. "That's not an answer either."

"It's the only one free of charge."

Reyes laughed then, short and genuine, and made a mark on her chart with a grease pencil. "Two hundred meters east. Fine. You're hired as channel consultant, starting tomorrow's flood tide. Room and board here, wages in Authority scrip." She rolled up the chart and stood, and for a moment her shadow fell across the table like a hull passing overhead. "One condition. Consultants ride the survey boat. I don't pay for knowledge I can't watch being used."

Tomorrow's flood tide. Aboard the fleet. Petra shook the offered hand—dry, strong, calloused in the pattern of winch work—and felt the splinter of the tide table drive deeper.

That night she pried up the floorboard and read the manifest again by candle stub, tracing her mother's coordinates, then the tide table, then the fleet's six windows, marking each one like a countdown scratched into a cell wall. Five tides after tomorrow's flood. Four after the next. The numbers marched toward the vault in the channel silt, and somewhere ahead of them, faster if she was clever, slower if she was desperate, she would have to go.

For once in her life, every calculation pointed the same direction: not away. Toward.

She blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the harbor breathe, and above the creak of mooring lines she could hear, faint and rhythmic, the sound of the fleet's pumps testing themselves in the night—like a heartbeat counting down.

Six tides. Then the sea would give up everything it had been keeping for her mother, and Petra Okafor-Zhou intended to be there first, standing on the deck of the enemy's ship, smiling, helpful, and lying with every breath.

Outside her window, the first stars came out over the channel, and beneath them, under forty feet of cold green water, the Meridian waited with its sealed doors and its twenty years of patience.

It had waited longer than she had. But she had farther to fall.
