## 4. What the Locket Knew

The locket was smaller than a memory should be.

Petra turned it over in her palm beneath the lamp in Maren's back room, where the walls sweated salt and the tide knocked its slow knuckles against the pilings below. It was the size of an oyster shell, tarnished to the color of old bronze, and it weighed almost nothing—less than the compass she'd carried across three borders, less than the knife she slept with under her pillow. And yet her hand trembled around it as though it were made of lead.

*E.V.* The initials had been etched into the back by someone with a steady hand and no time to spare, the serifs shallow, hurried. *Elira Veyra-Zhou.* Her mother.

Twenty years of running, and here was the first thing Petra had ever held that had known her mother's skin. She could feel where the hinge had been worn smooth—by a thumb, by worry, by years of the same gesture Petra herself made when she was frightened, touching metal until metal felt like company.

She set it on the table. She made tea she didn't drink. She let the lamp burn low because some part of her still believed that if she looked at this thing too directly, it would vanish like everything else from before the exile—the house with the blue door, the garden wall she'd measured her height against, the language her mother had sung to her in and then, gradually, stopped singing altogether, as though the words themselves had become contraband.

Then she opened it.

Inside, where a portrait should have been, there was a plate of dark metal scored with lines so fine they seemed at first like scratches, like damage. Petra tilted it toward the lamp. Not damage. Not scratches.

A cipher.

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door opens onto winter. She knew this cipher. She had thought she'd forgotten it, but knowing is not the same as remembering, and the body keeps what the mind buries: her mother's hand over hers on a kitchen table, tracing letters that weren't letters, saying, *When you can read this, you'll be old enough to know why we left.*

Petra had been seven. She had never been told why they left. She had only ever been told to keep moving.

* * *

She worked through the night.

It was not difficult, once she surrendered to it—that was the terrible part. The cipher was a child's cipher, a family cipher, built for a mother to teach a daughter on the far side of the world, using nothing but what a refugee carries: a candle flame, a windowpane, the hours between midnight and dawn. Each line of the plate corresponded to a fold of light; held at an angle to the lamp, certain grooves caught fire and others went black, and the ones that caught fire spelled out words in Veyran—a language Petra hadn't spoken aloud in two decades but which rose in her throat like water finding an old channel.

*If you are reading this, then the river has done what I could not.*

She read it aloud without meaning to, and her own voice in her mother's cadence nearly undid her.

*They will tell you we were thieves, Pétala. They have already begun telling everyone. Understand: a nation does not erase itself from the maps for stealing. A nation is erased for keeping something.*

Petra's hands had gone cold around the warm mug. Outside, a gull argued with the dark. In Salt Hollow, someone laughed behind the inn's thin walls—Maren, probably, or a fisherman home late—and the sound belonged to a world where people were not erased, where names stayed nailed down in one place like fence posts, and Petra sat inside that warmth like a diver sitting in a bell of borrowed air.

*The Meridian did not sink,* the plate said. *The Meridian was sunk. She carried the sealed archives—all of them, every record, every ledger, every testimony the Council ordered burned. My father was the Keeper of Archives. He loaded them himself. He believed the sea would keep what men would not.*

*There is a vault below the waterline, aft of the third hold. Iron-banded. Built to survive pressure, fire, and time. What it holds proves who gave the order, and why, and what they were afraid of.*

*What it holds proves that we were exiled, Pétala. Not criminal. Exiled. There is a difference, and the difference is a paper trail, and the paper trail is at the bottom of the sea.*

Petra set the locket down because her hands were shaking too badly to hold it steady.

Exiled. Not criminal.

She had built a life in the negative space of that sentence. Twenty years of it. She had learned to move like weather, to love like a guest, to sleep in rented rooms with her boots by the door. She had been blacklisted from two countries on the strength of a name attached to a crime that had never happened—a smuggling run through the Karst Pass she'd been nineteen for, a scapegoat with her family's surname stitched to the story like a brand. She had assumed, all these years, that the lie was local. A corrupt magistrate. A convenient foreigner. Bad luck with bad men.

But it wasn't local. It was architectural. The lie wasn't a crack in the foundation—it *was* the foundation. Someone had needed her family guilty, had needed them gone and discredited, had sunk a ship full of witnesses and records and then spent twenty years making sure the story stayed drowned. Her whole life of exile—every border crossed at night, every job taken under a false name, every friendship ended preemptively before it could be used against her—all of it had been load-bearing masonry in someone else's building.

Grief came first, quiet and enormous, like deep water pressing on a hull. Grief for her mother, who had died in a rented room in a country whose language she never learned, still folding ciphered warnings into jewelry, still hoping her daughter would someday read them. Grief for the grandmother she'd never met, the Keeper of Archives, who had believed the sea would keep what men would not—and had been wrong, or right, depending on what happened next.

Then the fury came, and the fury was worse, because fury was useful.

Petra stood up. She paced the small room, four steps and turn, four steps and turn, the floorboards complaining under her like an old crew. Her reflection ghosted in the dark window—thin, wind-scoured, a woman shaped entirely by running—and she hated it, and she loved it, because it had kept her alive, and it had kept her alone, and both of those things had been done to her rather than chosen.

*Someone else's lie.* She said it out loud, testing the weight of it. Somewhere out past the harbor bar, under forty fathoms of cold green water, lay the proof that could unmake it. A vault. Iron-banded. Aft of the third hold.

And above that vault, somewhere on the water, a licensed salvage fleet would arrive within days, dragging government contract papers and government intentions, hunting the same wreck—not to expose what was in it, she understood now, but to make sure it stayed buried. You didn't send a fleet to recover a nation's shame. You sent a fleet to entomb it.

The cipher's last lines confirmed it, in her mother's smallest, most careful script:

*They will come for it eventually. When they do, they will come with permission, which is how the worst things always come. Trust the sea, Pétala, but do not trust the men who claim to speak for it.*

Petra looked at the manifest from the chest, spread open beside the lamp. Coordinates. A wreck designation half-erased by water but still legible where it mattered. *MERIDIAN — VEYRAN MAIL & ARCHIVE SERVICE — LOST WITH ALL HANDS, 20 YEARS PAST.* All hands. Every soul who knew what the ship carried, drowned along with the evidence. That wasn't sinking. That was editing.

She thought of Maren downstairs, asleep now, the inn keys hanging on their hook, the smell of bread that would start before dawn. She thought of the dock, the gulls, the way the village had started saying her name like it expected her to answer. Six tides, maybe fewer. After that, the dredges would come down like questions with answers already written.

Petra picked up the locket and closed it gently, the way you close a book you intend to finish.

"You waited twenty years," she said to it, to her mother, to the drowned Keeper and the drowned ship and the drowned truth. "You can wait six tides more."

But even as she said it, she heard the flaw in her own voice—the thin place in the metal. Because waiting was not the same as winning, and the sea kept its promises only for those who got there first, and somewhere south of the horizon, engines were already turning.

She blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to pack.

* * *

Dawn found her on the dock with the tide tables copied onto her forearm in ink, doing arithmetic against the water.

Maren found her there, two mugs in hand, and followed Petra's gaze out to where the channel markers bobbed. "You've got the look," Maren said. "The look of a woman doing sums she doesn't like."

"The wreck offshore," Petra said. "The one the chest came from. I know what it is."

Maren waited. She was good at waiting; it was a fisherman's virtue.

"It's called the *Meridian*," Petra said. "And everything they say about my family is in it—buried or proven, depending on who reaches it first." She turned the locket over once in her fingers and put it away, deep, against her sternum. "A salvage fleet is coming for it."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes—calculation, or dread, or both. "How long?"

"Six tides," Petra said. "Maybe five."

Down the coast, faint as a rumor, a smudge of smoke sat on the horizon where no smoke should be.

Maren set both mugs down on the piling, untouched.
