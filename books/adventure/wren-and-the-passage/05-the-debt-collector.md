## 5. The Debt Collector

The storm had wrung itself out overnight, and Salt Hollow woke glistening, every surface rinsed and shining like something newly made. Petra stood at the window of her room above the inn, watching the harbor water settle from brown to green, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the weather changing.

She knew better. Twenty years of reading her own body had taught her the difference between a cold coming on and a warning. This was the warning kind—the animal certainty that the air pressure had shifted in some way that had nothing to do with weather. Somewhere out there, the shape of her life had changed, and she hadn't seen it happen.

She'd felt it first last night, at supper. Maren had laughed at something—a fisherman's story about a seal that stole hats—and Petra had caught herself laughing too, easy and unguarded, and the sound of it had frightened her more than any dredge or contract ever could. She'd gone to bed early and slept badly, dreaming of water that kept rising no matter how high she climbed.

Now, morning light lay across the chest on the floor where she'd hidden it under a folded blanket, and the locket sat warm against her sternum where she'd worn it since decoding the cipher. *Meridian.* The name turned over in her mind like a stone in a current. Her mother's handwriting, compressed into pinpricks of code, telling her that everything Petra had run from—every border crossing in the dark, every alias, every job taken under a name not her own—had been built on a lie someone else had told and the world had agreed to believe.

Exiled, not criminal. It should have freed her. Instead it made the walls feel closer, because if the lie could be proven, then there were people who needed it never to be.

She dressed, strapped the knife at her ankle out of habit, and went down to work.

* * *

Maren had her hauling crab pots off the *Kestrel's* deck, and for two hours the labor was mercy—rope burn and salt spray and the simple arithmetic of bait and catch. The other fishermen nodded to her now without the wariness of a week ago. Old Tomas had started calling her by a nickname, "the mountain goat," and the children on the dock had stopped staring and started waving. She catalogued each small acceptance the way she'd once catalogued escape routes, and hated herself a little for doing both with the same thoroughness.

It was midday, the tide slack and the harbor glassy, when the stranger walked down the pier.

She saw him before she consciously registered him—the way you notice a shadow fall across a path. He came without hurry, hands visible, coat too good for Salt Hollow, city boots ringing on planks that had known only rubber wellingtons for years. He moved like a man who had measured the distance between himself and everyone else and found it comfortable.

Petra set down the pot she was carrying. "Maren," she said quietly, "go check the lines on the north float."

Maren looked at her, read something in her face, and went without argument. That, too, was new—that Maren trusted her enough to obey without questions. Petra filed the feeling away where it couldn't distract her.

The stranger stopped six feet away. Up close he was older than his walk suggested, silver threading his temples, a scar through one eyebrow so old it had become just geography. His eyes were pale gray and entirely patient.

"Petra Zhou," he said. Not a question.

"You have me confused with someone."

"I don't." He smiled faintly. "But I admire the reflex. Keep it up as long as you like; I've got nowhere to be until the tide turns." He glanced at the harbor. "Which is in about four hours, incidentally. You've been counting. So have I."

Her hand drifted toward her hip, an old habit, and he shook his head gently, the way you'd correct a child reaching for a stove.

"Corvus," he said. "I deal in information, salvage rights, and occasionally in people's continued freedom. You may have heard the name. If not, ask anyone in the ports between here and Veyra's old waters—they'll tell you I'm the reason half of them are still alive, and the reason the other half aren't."

"I don't need anything."

"Everyone needs something. Most people just haven't been honest with themselves about what yet." He took a step toward the rail, unhurried, making it clear this was a conversation, not a confrontation. "You pulled a chest out of the flood river nine days ago. Stamped seal, Veyran crest, waterlogged but intact. You carried it forty miles along the coast road at night rather than risk the ferry manifest. And three days ago you opened it in a locked room above an inn while the innkeeper's dog slept outside your door—which, by the way, is a lovely detail. The dog. Very domestic. Very unlike you."

The harbor sounds seemed to recede. Petra became aware of her own breathing, slow and deliberate, the way she breathed on bad ledges.

"Who sent you?"

"No one sent me. That's what you keep failing to understand about how the world works now, Ms. Zhou. There's no one behind the curtain. There's just a great many people who've all noticed the same thing: a wreck called the *Meridian* has surfaced in local memory, a licensed fleet is steaming toward it with a government contract full of redactions, and a woman with your particular history has appeared in the same stretch of coastline. Coincidence is dead. Everything connects. My trade is knowing how."

"And your price?"

"First claim." He said it lightly, as if discussing fish prices. "Whatever you recover from the vault—documents, seals, personal effects—I get first look. First refusal. What I take, I take; what I leave, you keep. In exchange, you get my protection. Reyes's fleet won't touch you. Your old friends in the border ministries will suddenly find their files corrupted, their informants reassigned. You'll be a ghost again, but a ghost with a guardian."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvus looked out at the water for a moment, genuinely considering, and she understood that the performance of consideration was itself part of the offer—he wanted her to see him as reasonable, because reasonable men were harder to hate and harder to flee.

"If you refuse," he said, "I sell. Not maliciously—I'm not cruel, cruelty is inefficient—but I sell. Reyes gets your position, your description, and the fact that you've already dived the site once, which will let her lawyers frame you as a poacher and strip you of any legal claim to what you find. And separately, discreetly, certain parties who have spent twenty years preferring that the *Meridian* stay a rumor will learn that the daughter of Lian Zhou is alive, located, and holding evidence." He turned back to her. "You know what those parties do with information. You grew up on its consequences."

The name landed like a hook behind her ribs. He'd said her mother's name aloud, casually, in a fishing village where no one knew it, and the world hadn't ended—but something in Petra had tilted, the way ice tilts before it goes.

"How do you know about the vault?"

"Because I've known about the *Meridian* for twenty years, Ms. Zhou. I was a younger man with a smaller operation when she went down, and even then, even drowned, she was the most valuable object on this coast. Everyone assumed the archives were lost or scattered. Then a river coughs up a sealed chest with a manifest pointing to a specific hull section, and suddenly every ambitious person within six hundred miles starts doing arithmetic." He spread his hands. "You're simply the one who got there first. I respect that. I'd rather be in business with the one who got there first."

"The fleet has dredges. Contracts. Guns, probably."

"The fleet has bureaucracy. Bureaucracy is slow and predictable, which means it can be managed. I'm offering to manage it for you." His voice softened by a degree, and somehow that was worse. "You can't do this alone. You must know that by now. Six tides is nothing. One storm is less. And the people who wrote your family's history are not going to let a dive boat crewed by fishermen rewrite it."

A gull screamed somewhere overhead. Down the pier, old Tomas waved cheerfully at Corvus, assuming a buyer, and Petra watched the stranger wave back with perfect warmth and thought: *he belongs anywhere. That's what makes him dangerous.*

"Here's what I actually think," Petra said. "I think protection from you costs more than danger does. Danger, at least, tells you what it wants."

Something flickered behind the patience—respect, maybe, or appetite. Hard to tell with men like him; both expressions used the same face.

"Then we're at an impasse," Corvus said. "And impasses, in my experience, resolve themselves within a day or two, usually in favor of whoever prepared for them." He reached into his coat and she went very still, but he only produced a plain card, cream-colored, blank except for a single black bird stamped in the corner, and set it on the rail's cap beneath a stone. "When you reconsider—and you will, because the alternative isn't courage, it's just a slower kind of surrender—you can reach me. Or I'll find you. I'm good at finding people. It's the entire business."

He walked back up the pier without hurry, boots ringing, and did not look back, which was its own kind of message: *I don't need to watch you. I already know where you'll be.*

* * *

Petra didn't touch the card. She left it under the stone and finished the pots with hands that worked fine and a mind that wouldn't stop moving.

By late afternoon she'd walked the headland path alone, mapping the harbor from above the way she'd map a route: sightlines, choke points, the cove where Maren's late husband's boat sat on its mooring, tarped and waiting for a decision Petra hadn't given yet. She counted the fleet's masts still below the horizon and the tides remaining on her fingers and came up with numbers that were all too small.

Corvus was right. That was the claustrophobia of it—not the threat itself, but its accuracy. She could run. Running was the one skill she'd perfected; she could be over the hills tonight and gone by dawn, another name shed, another life abandoned mid-sentence. But running meant leaving the chest, or taking it and diving alone with no boat and no margin, and either way the *Meridian*'s truth stayed drowned, and her mother stayed a criminal in every record that mattered, forever.

Or she could stay. And staying meant Reyes, and Corvus, and whatever quiet, patient people preferred the drowned to stay drowned—all of them closing like water over a diver, pressure building with every fathom.

The price of survival keeps rising, she thought. At some point you have to stop paying it and start spending something bigger.

Below her, the harbor lights were coming on one by one, and Maren stood on the dock looking up at the headland, searching the dark for her. Petra watched her for a long moment—this woman who offered boats and belonging as if they were simple things—and felt the trapdoor open exactly as she'd always feared it would.

Except it wasn't a trapdoor. It was a choice, standing there waiting, and it was going to cost her everything either way.

She turned back toward the inn—and froze.

Down in the village, at the mouth of the harbor road, a car she didn't recognize idled with its lights off. Two silhouettes inside. Not Corvus's style; he traveled alone and in daylight.

Someone else had come to Salt Hollow. And they weren't even trying to hide.
