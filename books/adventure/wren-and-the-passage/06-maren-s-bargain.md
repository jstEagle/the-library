## 6. Maren's Bargain

Maren found out the way she found out everything in Salt Hollow: by watching.

Petra should have known better than to think she could hide it. She'd been laying out tide tables on her room's floor for three nights running, weighting the corners with candle stubs, tracing the fleet's projected dredge lines with a charcoal stub worn down to a nub. She kept the door locked. She kept the lamp low. But Maren had run an inn on a working dock for thirty years, and she read people the way Petra read water—the set of the current, the thing moving underneath.

So when Petra came down before dawn on the fourth morning, the kitchen was already lit, and Maren was sitting at the long table with Petra's own tide charts spread out in front of her, her reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair, her hands flat on the paper as if pinning it there.

"Six tides," Maren said, without looking up. "That's what you've been counting."

Petra stood in the doorway with salt wind at her back and said nothing. The stove ticked as it warmed. Somewhere outside, a gull argued with the morning.

"You dive," Maren went on. "You've been checking my husband's old gear locker every time you think I'm not looking. The tank valves. The line spools." Now she looked up, and her eyes were the flat gray of the sea before weather. "Tomas's boat is still moored at the north slip. The *Kestrel*. Nobody's taken her out since he drowned, but she floats, and she runs, and I keep her engine bled because I'm a fool who can't let go of things."

"Maren—"

"Sit down, Petra."

Petra sat. It was not a request that permitted refusal. The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and yesterday's bread and the brine that lived in everything in Salt Hollow, worked into the grain of the walls like a second timber. Maren poured tea without asking whether Petra wanted any, which was its own kind of answer.

"I know what you're planning," Maren said. "I don't need the details. You're going out past the point, ahead of that salvage fleet, down to whatever your mother left you, and you're going to do it alone, on borrowed gear, in water cold enough to stop a heart in twenty minutes. And then you're going to leave." She said the last part quietly, without accusation, and somehow that was worse. "Aren't you."

Petra wrapped her hands around the cup. The heat bit pleasantly into her knuckles. "The fleet reaches the site by the sixth tide. If they get there first, everything in that wreck gets dredged up under contract, catalogued, sealed away in some ministry archive where no one will ever see it again—or worse, it gets suppressed entirely. My family spent twenty years being called criminals for something we didn't do. This is the only chance to prove it."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer you're going to get."

Maren took off her glasses and folded them, slowly, deliberately, the way she did everything—no wasted motion, nothing hurried, a woman who had learned patience from tides and grief in equal measure. "I buried Tomas eleven years ago come spring," she said. "He went out in weather he shouldn't have, for reasons he wouldn't share, and the sea gave him back three days later at the base of the point. Do you know what I thought about, every day since? Not the storm. Not even him, though God forgive me, some days I can't hold his face anymore. I thought about the door. He walked out that door"—she pointed toward the front of the inn—"and I said *be careful*, and he said *always am*, and neither of us meant it. That was the last conversation. Be careful. Always am." She set the glasses down on the table with a small, precise click. "So no, I won't pretend I don't understand why you're going. I understand exactly. What I can't abide is watching another one walk out my door."

"Then don't watch."

The words came out harder than Petra intended, and she saw them land—saw the flinch Maren was too proud to show anywhere except the corner of her mouth. Outside, the first light was coming up over the harbor, turning the water the color of hammered pewter. A fishing crew was loading crates two slips down, their voices carrying thin across the still air.

"The *Kestrel* is yours," Maren said at last. "Fuel in her tanks, tanks filled, the good torch, the spare regulator. Take her tonight, take her now, I don't care. Just—" and here her voice did something Petra had never heard it do, something that cracked along an old seam, "—just promise me you'll come back. Not just this once. After. Promise me you'll stop running. Promise me this is the last time you walk out a door and disappear."

And there it was. The trapdoor, opening again—but this time Petra saw it clearly, saw that it wasn't a trap at all, that it was a hand extended across a table, and that made it infinitely harder.

She looked down at her tea. "I can't promise that."

"Why not?"

"Because promises are how people find you." Petra heard her own voice go flat and practical, the guide's voice, the survivor's voice, and hated it. "Because the moment I say *I'll be here*, someone uses that. Corvus already knows I'm in Salt Hollow. It's only a matter of time before the fleet does too, or worse. Every place I've ever stayed has ended the same way, Maren. I leave, or I'm made to leave. Those are the options. Promising to stay would just be lying to you with extra steps."

"Lying to me." Maren repeated it softly, tasting it. "Is that what you think this is? That staying somewhere is a lie?"

"I think it's a debt I can't service."

"No." Maren leaned forward, and her hands closed around Petra's wrist—not gently, not cruelly, but with the grip of a woman who had hauled nets and pulled drowning men from water and knew exactly how much force a body needed. "You listen to me, girl. Staying isn't a place. I've watched you these weeks, sleeping with your boots by the door, eating standing up, flinching every time a stranger walks in. You treat belonging like weather—something that happens to you if you stand still long enough. It doesn't work that way. Staying is a choice you make daily. Every morning. You wake up and you choose the door that keeps you here instead of the one that takes you away. And you choose it again the next day, and the next, until one day you look around and realize you haven't thought about leaving in a month, and that's home. There's no finding it. There's only choosing it."

"And when choosing it gets me killed?" Petra's voice rose despite herself. "When choosing it gets *you* killed? You think Corvus cares about your inn? Your fishermen? He'll burn all of it to get to what I'm carrying. The safest thing I can do for Salt Hollow is be gone from it."

"The safest thing you could have done was never come here at all." Maren released her wrist. "But you did. You were half-drowned and starving and you walked into my kitchen, and I fed you, and now you're mine to worry about whether you like it or not. That's how it works. That's the whole rotten, beautiful bargain. You don't get to decide alone anymore, Petra. That ship sailed the night I gave you a bed."

They stared at each other across the table, across the tide charts and the cooling tea, and Petra felt something in her chest pull tight—a seam straining, a hull plate flexing under pressure. She wanted it. That was the terrible truth underneath everything, the one she never let surface: she wanted the kitchen and the dock and the woman with the folded glasses and the daily choice. She wanted it so badly it frightened her more than the deep water did, because she knew exactly what losing it cost, and she had paid that price too many times to pretend otherwise.

"I'll bring your boat back," Petra said finally. Her voice was rough. "That I can promise."

Maren held her gaze for a long moment. Then she stood, gathered the tide charts, squared their edges against the table, and set them down in front of Petra like a verdict.

"Tomas used to say the sea doesn't take sides," she said, moving toward the stove. "It just takes. So do I. Go save your dead, Petra. But understand—" she turned, ladle in hand, steam rising behind her, and her eyes were bright and furious and grieving all at once, "—when you come back, and you will come back, I'm going to ask you again. Every day if I have to. Until the answer changes."

* * *

The *Kestrel* sat low and patient at the north slip, her paint gone chalky, her name faded to a ghost of itself. Petra loaded her in the blue hour before dawn: tanks, lines, the decoy chest wrapped in oilcloth, the real archives sealed in waxed canvas against her skin. The harbor breathed around her, slow and tidal. Behind her, the inn's kitchen window glowed—one square of amber light in the gray, and a silhouette in it that didn't move, that stayed there while Petra cast off, while she cleared the breakwater, while the point swallowed her whole.

She didn't look back. Looking back was the first step of staying, and she couldn't afford the first step.

But she caught herself, four miles out, with the wreck buoy just visible ahead and the fleet's lights still anchored beyond the horizon—caught herself calculating not the dive, not the currents, not the margin of six tides shrinking to five, but the exact minute she'd need to leave the site to reach Salt Hollow's dock by dark.

And beneath her keel, far below, the *Meridian* waited with everything her mother had died carrying—and somewhere behind her, a black-market broker who sold locations like fish, and a captain whose contract was built on a lie, and a woman in a lit window who had asked her for the one thing Petra had never once given anyone in twenty years of running:

a reason to be found.
