## 7. First Tide Down

The sea off Salt Hollow was the color of hammered pewter that morning, and Petra stood at the rail of Maren's dead husband's boat—the *Kindly Tern*, a name that felt like a dare—and watched the water decide whether to let her in.

She had been diving since she was fourteen, since her mother first strapped a tank to her back in the flooded quarries outside Veyra City and said, *the water is honest, Petra. It only wants to know if you're paying attention.* Twenty years of rivers and reefs and wrecks since then, and the lesson still held. The sea didn't care about her name or what two governments thought of it. The sea only wanted her whole attention or her life.

"Bottom's at forty-two meters," she said aloud, mostly for herself. The boat was small enough that speaking filled it. "Silt's going to be like swimming through milk."

The tide tables were folded in her jacket pocket, soft as cloth now from handling. First tide down: slack water at 6:40, a window of maybe fifty minutes before the current turned and began dragging the river's last sediment out over the site like a drawn curtain. Six tides total before Reyes's dredges could legally work the grid. She'd already spent one.

*Five left.*

She checked her gauges twice, then a third time because checking things was a form of prayer she'd never outgrown. The regulator hissed when she tested it—a sound like a snake deciding whether to bite. Her knife sat where her knife always sat. Her slate hung on its tether with the vault diagram copied onto it in her mother's cipher, transcribed from the locket's tiny etched plates: three concentric rings, a keel-mounted strongroom aft of the engine room, access through a maintenance trunk on the port side.

Her mother had drawn this from memory twenty years ago, in letters written in disappearing ink, mailed from cities Petra had learned to find on maps only after they'd stopped being safe. *If anything happens to me,* one letter had said, *the Meridian keeps our truth. Forty fathoms is a better archivist than any government.*

Petra rolled backward off the gunwale into cold that closed around her like a fist.

* * *

Down was a country of gray light and patient hunger.

The thermocline hit her at fifteen meters, a sudden chill like passing through a door into an unheated room. At thirty, the silt began—not the loose drifting kind but a suspended haze, fine as flour, that ate her dive light within arm's reach. She swam by feel and by the ghost-shapes her torch threw against nothing, descending the shot line she'd dropped yesterday under cover of dark, hand over hand, into the throat of the sea.

The Meridian announced itself before it appeared. A smell almost—old iron and old wood and the particular mineral sweetness of things that have been dying slowly for two decades. Then the hull rose out of the murk like a whale's grave: black plating furred with anemones, the ship's name still legible in flaking gilt along the stern—*MERIDIAN, VEYRA REGISTER*—and above it, barely, the crest. The seal of a nation that no longer existed, worn down to a suggestion, the way all of Veyra had been worn down to a suggestion.

Petra hovered there a moment and let herself feel it. Not the fear—that would come later, fear always came later, punctual as rent—but the other thing. The thing she refused to name while her hands were busy. Her mother had stood on that deck once. Had walked those boards toward whatever the Meridian carried in its belly, carrying Petra inside her, not yet born, already exiled.

*Hello,* she thought at the wreck. *I'm late.*

The port-side maintenance trunk was where the cipher promised, half-collapsed, its hatch crusted shut with barnacles the size of coins. She worked her pry bar into the seam and pulled, and the sea gave up its grip with a groan she felt in her teeth rather than heard. Inside: a service corridor, choked with silt, running aft toward the engine room and the strongroom beyond.

She went in.

It was diving a rib cage. The corridor's overhead had buckled in places, hull plates leaning together like conspirators, and between them hung curtains of netting and cable that moved when she moved, reaching for her tanks, her mask, her hair. Twice she stopped and cut herself free with short, surgical strokes of the knife. Once a plate shifted—a soundless, terrible settling, tons of iron adjusting its weight—and a bloom of silt swallowed her whole. She hung motionless in the whiteout, counting her breaths, feeling the corridor's geometry hold in her mind the way her mother had taught her: *the water is honest. Pay attention.* When the silt thinned, the way forward was still there. Narrower than before.

The strongroom door was steel, and it was open.

That stopped her. Open doors on wrecks meant either luck or company, and she'd had no luck worth mentioning in years. She played her light through the gap. The room beyond was intact—truly intact, a miracle of collapsed structure bracing itself, the deck above having folded down into a tent that shielded the compartment like a cupped hand. And against the far wall, bolted where it had been bolted since Veyra existed, stood the archive vault: a cabinet of green-painted steel, government-issue, its double doors sealed with wax-and-lead stamps bearing the crest she'd pulled from a riverbank a world ago.

Intact. Sealed. Waiting.

Exhilaration hit her like a second regulator, pure oxygen straight to the blood. Her hands shook as she fitted the pry bar—shook, and she noticed, and made them stop. The lead seals cracked. The doors swung inward on hinges that had waited twenty years for exactly this and were gracious about it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, stacked in rows like sleeping children: document cases. Hundreds of pages. The sealed archives of a drowned nation—the manifests, the ledgers, the orders, the names. Somewhere in those pages was the order that had turned her family from citizens into criminals, the lie typed on official letterhead, signed by someone whose grandchildren probably had good jobs.

Petra took four cases. Not all—she couldn't carry all, and taking all would tell the next diver everything. Four cases, wrapped again, lashed to her harness. She photographed the vault with the little camera in her glove, marked the corridor's collapse points on her slate, and began the long careful climb back out of the rib cage.

At the mouth of the maintenance trunk, she paused and looked back. The vault stood open in the dark, empty-handed now, and behind it the remaining cases waited with the patience of the drowned.

*Five tides,* she thought. *I'll come back for you.*

Then the hull spoke again—longer this time, a groan that traveled the length of the ship like a word passed ear to ear—and a plate somewhere aft let go with a muffled boom she felt through her chest. Silt bloomed. The Meridian was settling, finally, after twenty years of holding its breath. The sea was closing its book.

Petra kicked for the surface and did not look back again.

* * *

She broke into gray daylight with her lungs burning and hauled herself up the shot line hand over hand, the archive cases dragging at her like debts. The *Tern*'s hull knocked overhead, hollow and comforting. She came over the gunwale, stripped her mask, and breathed the ordinary air of an ordinary morning—salt, diesel, kelp—and for one full minute she just knelt on the deck and laughed, quietly, the unhinged laugh of a woman who has touched the proof of her own innocence forty meters underwater and lived.

Four cases sat dripping on the deck boards. Evidence. Exoneration. Her mother's handwriting was going to be in there somewhere; she knew it the way you know weather coming. She wanted to open them immediately, here, in the wind, and read her way back into a country that had erased her.

Instead she wrapped them in canvas, stowed them in the bilge locker beneath the spare anchor chain, and checked the horizon.

Which was when she saw the smoke.

Two smokes, actually—one close, from the direction of Salt Hollow's harbor road, thin and domestic, woodsmoke, nothing. The other far out, north-northeast, sitting low on the water like a bruise. Diesel exhaust. Big engines. Moving.

She got the binoculars off their hook and steadied them on the rail. The shape resolved slowly out of haze: a working vessel, gray-hulled, with the flat stern and gantry crane of a salvage tender. Behind it, smaller, a survey launch. Government paint. Fleet markings.

Reyes. But Reyes should have been south, working the licensed grid, still two tides away from anything near the Meridian's coordinates. Unless—

Unless someone had told her otherwise.

Petra lowered the glasses very slowly. Her skin did the thing it always did when she'd been found, a cold crawling lift along the spine, every nerve becoming a sentry. Corvus. Of course. The broker had smiled at her across Maren's taproom table three nights ago with the patience of a man who owned clocks, and he'd said, *I don't need to follow you, Ms. Zhou. I need only wait for you to go somewhere.* He'd known she'd dive early. He'd counted on it. Selling her position to Reyes cost him nothing and bought him leverage—or bought him chaos, out of which brokers harvest everything.

The fleet changed course as she watched, swinging wide, bow coming around toward the deep water east of Salt Hollow. Toward the shot line. Toward the *Tern*, riding alone and unlicensed over a wreck she had no permit to touch.

Every gain narrowed the margin. That was the arithmetic of her whole life, and she'd finally, stupidly, begun to believe she'd escaped it. One vault opened, one truth in hand—and the price was a gray hull on the horizon, closing at twelve knots.

Petra started the engine. The *Tern* coughed, caught, settled into its rattling heartbeat. She'd be in Salt Hollow's harbor inside the hour, anonymous among fishing boats, cases hidden, face down, invisible again—invisible, that old familiar coat, except it fit worse every year, and the woman she'd been becoming in this village kept leaving the door of it open.

Behind her, the Meridian's patch of sea lay smooth and secret, holding the rest of the dead nation's truth, and above it the fleet's wake drew a long white line across the water like something being crossed out.

Five tides. Maybe fewer now.

And in Salt Hollow, when she tied off at Maren's dock with shaking hands, the first person waiting on the pilings, arms crossed, expression unreadable, was Corvus—smiling like a man collecting on a debt she hadn't known she'd owed.
