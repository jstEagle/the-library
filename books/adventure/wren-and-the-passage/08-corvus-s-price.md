## 8. Corvus's Price

The note came tucked inside a fish crate, written on the back of a tide chart in ink that had bled with the damp: *The old woman makes good chowder. Shame if her kitchen burned. The pier, midnight. Bring what you took from the sea.*

Petra read it twice by the light of Maren's stove, and something cold slid down through her chest like meltwater into a boot.

Maren was at the sink, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands pink from hot water, humming something tuneless and old. Steam fogged the little window above the basin, and beyond it Salt Hollow lay dark except for the harbor lights strung along the dock like a necklace someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up. It was, Petra thought, the kind of scene she'd spent twenty years learning not to want. And Corvus had just made it a weapon.

"You're doing that thing," Maren said without turning around. "Where you go quiet and your jaw does all your talking."

"It's nothing."

"Nothing makes people's jaws work like that." She shut off the tap and dried her hands on her apron, turning at last. Her face was weathered into kindness the way driftwood is weathered into smoothness—by force, over years. "Whoever it is, they've found you."

Petra folded the note small, smaller, until it was a hard little pellet in her fist. "I can handle it."

"I know you can. That's what worries me." Maren untied her apron and hung it on the hook by the door, a gesture so domestic it hurt. "Handle it carefully, then. This house has stood two hundred years of storms. I'd hate for it to fall to a man with bad handwriting."

* * *

Midnight came down over the harbor like a lid. No moon; the clouds were low and woolen, and the water moved beneath the pier in long black swells that breathed against the pilings. Petra stood at the pier's end with the decoy chest at her feet—she'd built it over three sleepless days, a false archive of salt-stained folios and convincing forgery, weighted right, sealed with wax she'd tinted to match Veyra's official ochre—and waited.

He came out of the dark the way he always did, as if he'd been standing there longer than she had. Corvus wore a fisherman's oilskin now, which somehow made him worse; the broker's gray suit had been a costume, but this was camouflage, and camouflage meant he intended to stay.

"You got my note."

"I got your note." Her voice stayed flat. Twenty years of practice. "If you touch Maren, there isn't a port between here and the ice fields where you'll be safe."

"Maren." He said the name like he was tasting it. "Lovely woman. Terrible locks on her doors." Behind him, half-shadowed against a piling, one of his men shifted—a big silhouette with a boat hook held wrong, held like a weapon instead of a tool. "You understand my position, Zhou. You refused me once. Politely, even. But refusal is a currency too, and I've had offers on yours from both directions. The fleet wants you found. Your old friends in Veyra's ghost government want you buried. I'm a simple man caught between generous buyers."

"So this is the second offer."

"This is the last offer." He nodded at the chest. "Open it."

She knelt, slowly, letting him see her hands, and lifted the lid just enough. In the dark he couldn't see what she wanted him to see—but he saw enough: folios, seals, the ochre wax, the water-swollen paper of things that had spent twenty years under the sea. His breath changed. Greed has a sound, Petra had learned. A small catch, like a fish taking bait.

"The Meridian's archives," she said. "Every record the Republic drowned when it drowned the country. Including the exile rolls. Including my family's file."

"And you're giving it to me."

"I'm giving it to the highest bidder, and you're going to run that auction. That was always your business, wasn't it? Selling what other people bleed for." She closed the lid. "But it comes with terms. Maren left alone. Salt Hollow left alone. And I'm gone from here before dawn—you'll never see me again, and neither will anyone you might sell my location to."

Corvus smiled. In the dark his teeth were the brightest thing on the pier. "You're learning to negotiate like a criminal, guide. It suits you."

* * *

The handoff was set for the next night, at the wreck of an old skiff beached past the headland—a place with one road in and one road out, which cut both ways and Petra knew it. Which was why she'd spent the day making sure the road out mattered more than the road in.

She'd also spent the morning rowing out to the fleet anchorage in Maren's tender, under a flag of truce she improvised from a torn flour sack, and asking—of all people aboard Captain Reyes's ship—for the archivist.

Her name was Odile Ferran, and she had ink stains on three fingers and the particular stillness of someone who had spent her life among documents that could ruin nations. Petra had marked her during the first confrontation with the fleet: the only person on deck who flinched when Reyes mentioned *sanctioned disposal* of recovered materials. People who flinched at sanctioned disposal were people worth gambling on.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," Odile said, in the shadow of the tender, keeping her voice below the lap of the water.

"Both. It's a package deal." Petra unwrapped the oilcloth bundle: the real archives, the true folios pulled from the vault before the hull collapsed, her mother's cipher transcribed onto the flyleaf in her own careful hand. "Veyra's sealed records. Proof the exile decrees were forged—that the families sent out weren't criminals, they were witnesses. Witnesses to what the Republic did before it erased itself from the maps." She pressed the bundle into Odile's arms. "Reyes is under contract to suppress this. You're under contract to catalog it. Catalogs get copied. Copies get out. That's the whole architecture of your profession, and it's the only honest thing left in this harbor."

Odile looked down at the bundle as if it were warm. "If Reyes finds out—"

"Then she finds out what she's actually being paid for, and she gets to decide who she is. That's more than anyone gave me." Petra pushed off from the ship's hull. "Sixth tide's tomorrow. After that, none of this matters anymore—one way or the other."

* * *

The headland at dusk was all gorse and wind, the beached skiff lying on its side like a dead animal picked clean. Petra came down the path with the decoy chest strapped to her back and every nerve singing the old song—the song of the exit, the route out, the rock she could put at her back. Except this time the exit ran through Maren's kitchen, and Maren was somewhere behind her with a bruise on her cheekbone that she swore was from a cupboard door and Petra knew wasn't.

Corvus stood beside the skiff with four men now, spread wide, professional. He looked at the chest, then at her face, and his smile thinned by a degree.

"No escort. No tricks." He circled the chest, ran a thumb along the wax seal. "You know, I almost admire it. Twenty years running and you finally stand still long enough to make a trade." He snapped his fingers; two of his men took the chest between them. "A pleasure doing honest business, Zhou."

"That's it?" She kept her voice bored while her heart hammered the count—*not yet, not yet*—because the plan lived or died on timing, and timing was the one thing the sea never guaranteed.

"That's it." He turned to go, then paused, and the pause was the trap she'd been waiting for. "Although. Curious thing. My buyer in the fleet tells me their archivist requested shore leave this afternoon. First time in six years, apparently. Odd timing, don't you think?"

The world went very quiet and very large. Petra let nothing move in her face. "Archivists are strange people."

"Mm." He studied her the way he'd study a manifest with a smudged entry. "Well. If anything surfaces from this transaction that shouldn't have—if the real archives appear anywhere I didn't sell them—I'll know exactly whose craftsmanship it was. And I won't come back for you, guide. I'll come back for the village. Villages are easier to find than women who don't want to be found."

He walked away up the shingle with his men and her beautiful forgery, and Petra stood in the wind counting her pulse down from a hundred, watching the water, waiting.

It came on the third swell: far out past the point, a single lantern blinked twice, went dark, blinked twice. Odile's signal. The archives were aboard. They were real, they were copied, and by morning they would be in three different hands across three different harbors, because that was the whole architecture of the profession.

Petra exhaled for what felt like the first time since the note in the fish crate.

Then she heard the engines.

Not Corvus's launch—deeper, heavier, the diesel growl of a salvage vessel pushing hard against the ebb. She turned toward the channel and saw the fleet's lead ship rounding the headland early, searchlights already sweeping the water, dredge cranes silhouetted against the last bruised light of the sky. Reyes, coming for the Meridian a full tide ahead of schedule—which meant someone had sold her the site coordinates, and there was only one seller with the inventory.

Corvus hadn't come to trade. He'd come to deliver her, the chest, and the location in a single lot, and the fleet had simply arrived ahead of him.

Behind her, up the dark path toward Salt Hollow, a door slammed, and Maren's voice carried thin and furious on the wind: *"Petra! Get up here—they're at the house—"*

Petra ran.
