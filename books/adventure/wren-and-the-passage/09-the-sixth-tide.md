## 9. The Sixth Tide

The storm came in from the southeast like something with a grudge.

Petra felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of iron on the wind, the gulls abandoning the harbor in a single ragged exodus. By the time the first clouds stacked themselves over the headland, black as wet slate, the whole of Salt Hollow had turned out to haul boats above the tideline. Maren worked beside her in silence, rope burning through their gloves, neither of them mentioning the dive boat that would not be coming back up onto the shingle tonight.

The sixth tide. The last one.

By Petra's count—and she had counted nothing else for three days—the fleet's dredges would reach the Meridian's grave within the hour. Reyes had changed course after Corvus's tip, and now three hulls sat anchored over the site like carrion birds settling onto a carcass, their floodlights already cutting cones through the green murk. Somewhere below, in a vault sealed for twenty years, lay the truth of Veyra: the archives, the manifest, the record of a nation that had been drowned twice—once by water, once by decree.

And somewhere aboard the flagship was an archivist named Sef Oduya who now carried copies of everything that mattered, tucked into oilcloth against his ribs, waiting to see which way the day broke.

"You don't have to go out there," Maren said. She didn't look at Petra. Her hands kept working the rope, fast and furious, as if the knot itself were the argument they'd been having since dawn. "Storm's rising. Even Reyes will pull her people off the water."

"Reyes won't pull anyone." Petra tested the mooring line, then coiled it over her shoulder. "She's got a contract with a deadline. Governments don't extend deadlines for weather. They extend them for results."

"And you? What are you extending yourself for?"

Petra looked at her—at this woman who had fed her, hired her, lent her a dead man's boat, and asked only the one impossible thing in return. *Stop running afterward.* She wanted to promise it. God, she wanted to. But promises made in the shadow of a storm were just prayers with better grammar.

"I'm going to give Captain Reyes a reason to stop dredging," Petra said. "And if she doesn't take it, I'm going to make sure the whole coast hears what's in those papers anyway."

Maren finally turned. Rain had begun to fall, fat and intermittent, spotting the planks between them like dark coins. "You sound like my husband," she said quietly. "The week before he didn't come home."

"I know how to read water, Maren. I've been reading it since I was six years old."

"That's what he said too."

* * *

The skiff fought her the whole way out.

Petra ran it alone into a sea that had gone from green to the color of old bruises, swells stacking higher with every mile, the wind tearing spray off their crests and flinging it sideways like gravel. She had dived in worse—she'd guided clients through flood country where rivers wore whole forests as crowns—but never with so much hanging on the crossing, and never with her hands shaking quite like this. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent to fear. The tremor of a person about to spend the last coin of a twenty-year hiding.

The fleet resolved out of the rain in pieces: mast lights first, then hulls, then the great skeletal arms of the dredges, dripping and groaning against their anchors. The flagship *Vigilant* rode highest, government gray gone almost white under its work lights, and it was toward the *Vigilant* that Petra steered, riding a swell up its face and sliding down the other side, again and again, until the flagship's rail loomed above her and a voice crackled through a loudhailer, half-swallowed by wind:

*"Unidentified vessel, stand off. This is a restricted salvage operation."*

"My name is Petra Zhou!" she shouted back, cupping her hands. "I have documents your government will hang you for finding! Get Captain Reyes on deck or I'll board!"

Silence, except for the storm. Then, faintly, the sound of boots on steel, and a figure appeared at the rail—tall, weathered, hair plastered flat by rain. Ilsa Reyes looked down at the small skiff pitching in her ship's shadow, and Petra watched her do the arithmetic every captain does: risk against reward, orders against instinct.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," Reyes called down.

"Both! It's why I'm still alive!"

A pause. Then Reyes raised two fingers, and lines came over the side.

* * *

They put her in the chart room because the bridge was chaos—radar stuttering, crew shouting depth readings over the wind—and because Reyes, Petra suspected, did not want her officers hearing what came next. The door shut. The storm pressed against the portholes like a face. Reyes stood across the chart table from her, arms folded, sea boots braced against the roll of the deck, and waited.

Petra set the folder on the table between them. Not the decoy—that was gone, traded to Corvus in a warehouse that smelled of bilge and lies. This was the copy she'd kept back, the one Sef Oduya had photographed page by page before returning the originals to their oilcloth. Insurance. She'd learned insurance from a lifetime of having none.

"Your contract," Petra said, "isn't salvage. It's suppression."

Reyes didn't move. "Go on."

"The Meridian wasn't carrying treasure. It was carrying Veyra's national archive—the sealed records, the ones your employers spent twenty years pretending never existed. Land grants. Court proceedings. Exile orders." Petra opened the folder and turned it around. "Including the order that banished my family. Signed, dated, stamped. We weren't criminals, Captain. We were inconvenient. And someone paid your fleet to make sure the proof stayed on the seafloor."

For a long moment there was only the storm and the creak of the ship working against its anchor. Reyes looked down at the pages without touching them. Petra watched her face the way she watched rock faces for the telltale shift of loose stone—the flicker at the corner of the mouth, the slow blink, the jaw tightening not in anger but in recognition.

"You knew," Petra said softly. "Not the details. But you knew it smelled wrong. That's why you let Oduya aboard. That's why you haven't burned my copies when you could have."

"I follow contracts," Reyes said. "It's the only thing keeping forty people fed and licensed. You think I don't know what's underneath some of them?" She looked up, and her eyes were hard and tired and honest all at once. "But knowing a thing and being able to act on it are different animals, Ms. Zhou. If I break this contract, I lose the fleet. If I honor it, I—" She stopped. Outside, thunder rolled across the water like furniture dragged across heaven.

"If you honor it," Petra finished, "you drown the truth a second time. And this time you're holding the pump."

The ship lurched. Somewhere aft, metal shrieked—an anchor chain taking strain it was never built for. A young officer burst through the door without knocking, rain streaming off him. "Captain—the site buoy's dragging! Swell's at four meters and building, and the dredge arm on the *Kestrel* is fouled on something solid—we think it's the hull—"

"Foul means breach," Reyes said sharply.

"Yes, ma'am. And the vault section—if the plates go, whatever's down there scatters across the seabed."

Petra felt the words land in her chest like a dropped anchor. Twenty years of silence, sealed in iron, and now the sea itself was reaching in to tear it open—not to free it but to shred it, to scatter letters and seals and signatures across miles of cold sand where no one would ever reassemble them.

"Pull your dredges," Petra said, turning to Reyes. "Right now. Before the storm does it for you."

"And do what instead?"

"Let me go down." Petra heard her own voice come out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was nothing but falling. "One diver. No dredge. I know the vault layout—I've studied the Meridian's plans since I was a child; my mother drew them from memory on the backs of grocery lists. Give me one descent while your crews stand off, and I'll bring up what matters. Or pull everyone off and let the storm decide, and tomorrow there'll be nothing down there but confetti."

Reyes stared at her. The officer stared at her. The ship rolled, and the lamp overhead swung its shadows across the chart table like a pendulum counting down.

"You understand," Reyes said slowly, "that if I allow this, and you surface with those documents, I can't un-see them. My contract dies on your next breath. Everything after that is me choosing a side."

"Yes."

"And if you don't come back up?"

"Then I died doing the one thing I was ever actually good at," Petra said. "Reading water."

Something moved through Reyes's expression—there and gone, like a fish under ice. She turned to the officer. "Get the dive locker open. Rig a line from the stern with a surface marker. And signal the *Kestrel* and the *Tern*—all dredging stops, all personnel clear of the site. Now."

"Ma'am, the contract—"

"*Now*, Voss."

He went. Reyes looked back at Petra, and for the first time since the river had coughed up that seal-stamped chest, Petra saw something in another person's face that wasn't suspicion, or hunger, or calculation. It was, improbably, respect.

"One descent," Reyes said. "Fifteen minutes of bottom time, no more—I don't care what you find. The swell window closes after that, and I am not losing two people to Veyra in one lifetime." She paused, and something older moved behind her eyes. "My father ran cargo out of Veyra's southern ports. Before. He used to say the maps were wrong, that the country hadn't sunk, it had just been folded away." She shook her head once, hard, like a swimmer clearing water from her ears. "Suit up, Zhou. Prove him right."

* * *

The sea took her like it had been waiting.

Petra went down the guide line into water the color of deep tea, lit from above in stuttering silver, the storm translating itself into percussion against the surface far overhead. At ten meters the light failed and her torch took over, carving a tunnel through the murk, and at eighteen meters the Meridian rose up to meet her—a black spine of collapsed decking and ribbed hull, furred with twenty years of sediment, breathing silt in slow clouds as the storm's surge reached even here, even this deep.

The *Kestrel*'s dredge arm had done damage already. Petra found the wound immediately: a gouge torn through the hull plating near the bow, edges peeled back like petals, and beyond it, visible through the gap, the vault door—her mother's cipher etched faintly beneath the corrosion, still legible, still stubborn—its frame racked out of true by the impact.

And the plates above it were moving.

She felt it more than saw it: a groan transmitted through the water into her bones, a shimmer of sediment sifting down like snow. The storm surge was flexing the wreck, working the broken sections against each other, and the vault sat directly beneath the largest of them—a hull plate the size of a chapel roof, held now by rust and habit.

Fifteen minutes. The gauge on her wrist glowed green in the torchlight. Petra hooked her line, checked her knife, and swam into the wound in the ship's side, into the dark her mother had crossed an ocean to keep secret, while above her the sky tore itself open and the sixth tide began to fall.

The plate shifted. Sediment billowed. And somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms, she heard—or imagined she heard—the *Vigilant*'s alarm bell ringing, urgent and continuous, like a heart that couldn't decide whether to break.
