## 10. A Place Worth Returning To

The news came the way the tide did—without asking permission.

Petra heard it first from the boy who ran messages between Salt Hollow and the coast road, a wiry kid named Tam who arrived at the inn out of breath and half-drowned in his own excitement. "They're reading it on the wireless," he said, dripping rainwater onto Maren's clean floor. "The Veyra archives. Every station. They're reading the names."

Maren's kitchen went still around her. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, gulls argued over something dead in the shallows, and the smell of low tide—brine and black mud and old kelp—drifted through the propped door like it owned the place, which in Salt Hollow it more or less did.

Petra set down the mug she'd been drying. Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a kind of distant surprise, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"Read them," she said. "Read all of it?"

Tam nodded so hard his hood fell back. "The archivist woman—the one off Captain Reyes's ship—she's giving testimony in the capital. They're saying the whole record. The exile orders, the falsified charges, everything." He hesitated, then added, gentler than a boy that age had any business being, "They're saying your name, miss. Zhou. Saying it clean."

*Clean.* Twenty years she had heard her name spoken only in the grammar of suspicion—whispered by border clerks, spat by brokers, inked into watchlists with the careful venom of men paid to remember. *Petra Zhou, mountain guide, blacklisted from two countries, do not admit, do not assist, report if seen.* Her name had been a wound other people kept open for their own convenience.

Now a stranger on a wireless was saying it like it was just a name.

She thanked Tam, pressed a coin into his hand that he tried to refuse, and walked out into the rain because staying indoors suddenly felt impossible, like wearing a coat in summer.

* * *

The storm had passed three days before, but the sea hadn't finished with it. Swells still came in long and heavy, rolling out of the northeast with the patience of things that had crossed an ocean to arrive. Petra stood at the end of Salt Hollow's dock with the rain running off her hood and watched them come, each one lifting the fishing boats and setting them down again like a mother checking on sleeping children.

The Meridian was gone now—or what was left of it. After the sixth tide, after Reyes had made her choice, the salvage fleet had shifted from dredging to recovery, working the wreck site with a strange, almost ceremonial care. Reyes herself had overseen the raising of the last of the archive vaults, and when the government contract had been torn up—not quietly, not politely, but torn up all the same, in front of cameras and clerks and a hall full of people who had spent twenty years pretending Veyra had never existed—it was Reyes who stood and gave testimony.

Petra had not been there to see it. She'd been here, in Maren's kitchen, mending a net she didn't know how to mend properly, while Maren sat across from her pretending not to listen to the wireless with every bone in her body.

But Tam had brought the details, day by day, like a man delivering bricks for a house being rebuilt: how Corvus had been taken at the harbor in Corvane with the decoy chest still in his possession and a ledger on him that made even his own people flinch; how he'd fought the arrest until Reyes's testimony pinned him—her voice, flat and unglamorous and absolutely devastating, describing the brokered sale of a nation's memory; how the reversal order had come down with signatures Petra would have once sold years of her life to glimpse.

*Exile reversed. Record corrected. Petra Zhou: cleared.*

Cleared. As though she were a road, as though the obstruction had simply been removed and traffic could resume. There was no word for what twenty years of running actually did to a person, no bureaucratic phrase for the way fear calcifies into habit, habit into character, until you can't tell where the danger ended and you began.

She was still working on that one. She suspected she'd be working on it for a while.

Footsteps on the planks behind her—Maren's particular tread, uneven on the left, the dock knowing her weight the way it knew everyone's. She came to stand beside Petra without speaking, which was Maren's way. She'd offered words already, more words than Petra deserved, the night the wireless read the names and Maren had reached over and turned the volume up instead of down, so Petra couldn't pretend she hadn't heard her own redemption.

"You'll catch your death standing out here," Maren said now, which was as close as she came to tenderness before noon.

"I've caught worse."

"I know you have." A pause. The swells lifted the boats and laid them down. "Boat comes through tomorrow. The mail packet. It goes anywhere you want it to go—that's the thing about boats."

"I know how boats work, Maren."

"I know you know." Maren looked out at the water, her face weathered into something beyond pretty or plain, just true. "I'm saying it out loud so we both hear it. Nobody's keeping you. That was never the bargain. The bargain was you stop running. And I reckon—" she tilted her head, considering, "—I reckon a person can't stop running while she's standing still wondering whether to. So. Wonder out loud or don't, but don't stand there letting the rain decide for you."

Petra almost laughed. Almost. It came out as breath, white in the cold air.

Inside her pack, sitting in her room above the inn, were the things twenty years had taught her to keep: two changes of clothes worn to translucence, a climbing rack with one cam missing its spring, a knife, a compass, a folded map of mountain ranges she'd loved and left, a bundle of her mother's letters soft as cloth from handling. Everything she owned fit in thirty seconds' packing. That had always been the point. Thirty seconds and gone, before the hunters, before the questions, before anyone could learn the shape of her enough to miss her.

She had unpacked exactly twice in twenty years. Once in a hostel in Karstvaad, for four days, until she saw a man reading her description off a paper. Once in a rented room above a chandlery in a port whose name she refused to remember, for eleven days—a record—until Corvus found her there, smiling, holding out a contract like a bouquet.

Thirty seconds. That was the whole architecture of her freedom: a bag packed tight enough to carry, light enough to run with, empty enough never to hold anything that mattered.

And now there was nothing to run from. The watchlists were burning or already ash. The name on them was hers, and it was clean, and the strangest part—the part that kept catching her like a step that wasn't where her foot expected—was that she felt no lighter. Running had been terrible, but it had been *hers*, a shape she'd worn so long she'd mistaken it for a spine. Take it away and what was underneath? A woman standing on a dock in the rain, forty-one years old, who could go anywhere and had no idea where she wanted to be.

Except.

Except that wasn't quite true either, and she'd known it wasn't true since the morning she'd surfaced from the Meridian with salt stinging her eyes and seen Salt Hollow's rooftops smoking gently against the gray sky, chimneys lit, someone—Maren, probably—already hauling nets, and felt something in her chest unlock that she hadn't known was locked.

Belonging was never safety. Maren had told her that too, in the middle of their fight, in the doorway with the dive boat keys between them like a dare: *Staying is a choice you make daily, not a place you find.* Petra had thought it was sentiment then. Standing here now, she understood it was the opposite of sentiment. It was the hardest, most unsentimental truth she'd ever been handed. Safety was a fiction; the flood proved it, the wreck proved it, the whole drowned history of Veyra proved it. You could be erased from maps, from records, from memory. What you couldn't be erased from—if you refused—was the daily choosing.

Her mother had chosen it too, in her way. Writing letters to a daughter she might never see, sealing a locket with a cipher inside, trusting the dead language of family to outlive the living lie of exile. *The past has found me,* Petra had thought at the riverbank, weeks ago, a lifetime ago. She'd been wrong. The past hadn't found her. She had finally stopped fleeing long enough to turn around and meet it.

"Maren," she said.

"Aye."

"The boat tomorrow. I'm not on it."

Maren didn't smile—not exactly. But something moved through her face like light under cloud, and she said, "Well. I'll put another log on your fire anyway. Rooms get cold in this season," and turned and walked back up the dock with her uneven tread, leaving Petra alone with the sea, which was fine. Some decisions needed witnesses and some needed exactly none.

* * *

That evening, in the small room above the inn, Petra set her pack on the bed and opened it.

It took longer than she expected. Not the physical act—that was nothing, a matter of drawers and pegs—but the act itself, the meaning of it. Her hands shook slightly on the second drawer, and she let them. Grief and relief feel identical in the body; she'd learned that on mountains, descending from summits, and it was true here too.

Clothes in the wardrobe, such as they were. The knife went in the nightstand drawer, because a person didn't stop being who she'd been just because the world stopped requiring it. The compass she set on the windowsill, needle swinging, settling north, holding. The map of ranges she unfolded once, traced with one finger the ridgelines she'd crossed and the ones she hadn't, and refolded—and then, deliberately, put it in the bottom drawer instead of the pack. Not thrown away. Kept. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

Last, the letters. She untied the bundle and read the top one, though she knew it by heart, her mother's hand slanting and sure: *Wherever you land, Petra, plant something. Even if you dig it up in spring. The planting is the point.*

Outside, the tide was turning. She could hear it in the harbor's changed voice, water sliding back over the flats, filling the channels, the whole bay inhaling. Downstairs, Maren was laughing at something—one of the fishermen, probably, or the dog—and the sound came up through the floorboards like warmth through stone.

Petra folded the empty pack flat and slid it under the bed. Then she sat on the edge of the mattress in the lamplight and listened to the village settle around her, boat hulls knocking softly against the dock, and thought about tomorrow: the mail packet coming in, the nets needing mending, the ridge line inland that Tam swore had a view of the whole coast, and a climb in it, if a person were inclined.

She was inclined.

For the first time in twenty years, Petra Zhou slept with nothing packed, nothing planned, and nowhere to run—and woke, when the gulls began at dawn, already home.
