## 1. Miles, Not Years

There are forty-one sandbars between Port Meridian and the mouth of the Sable's lower reach. I know this the way some women know their own birthdays—intimately, without wanting to, and with a small private ceremony every time I pass one.

I don't celebrate birthdays. Haven't since I was nineteen, for reasons I keep in a waterproof bag at the bottom of my sea chest, next to a photograph I never look at and a letter I've never finished reading. But I count sandbars. Every one of them is a mile of river that has shifted since last season or hasn't, a shoal that will take your keel if you get lazy, a little argument between water and earth that the water is always winning, slowly, the way everything important happens.

The seventh one has grown a beard of reeds this year. The twelfth has split into two islands, which means the channel has moved east and I'll have to shift my marks. The twenty-third—the locals call it the Widow, because it took a ferry down in '09 with eleven souls aboard—is exactly where it's always been, stubborn as a mule in a stall, and I give it a wide berth out of respect rather than fear. Fear implies you think something might change. The Widow doesn't change. That's almost comforting, in a river where nothing else holds still.

People ask me sometimes why I count them. Dockhands mostly, and the occasional passenger who's had too much rum and not enough sense. They want to know what the numbers are *for*. Whether I'm keeping score against somebody. Whether there's a prize at forty-one, or a hundred, or whatever number I'm pretending to aim at.

There isn't. There's no prize. There's just the counting, and the fact that on a river, a thing you can count is a thing you can trust, and I have never trusted much else.

My name is Yara Hartwell, though most of the Sable knows me as Captain Hartwell of the *Wren*, and a surprising number of people who deal with me regularly have no idea those two facts belong to the same woman. Hartwell is a name I wear like a coat I can't quite take off but won't put on properly either—buttoned wrong, sleeves too long, good for shoving things into pockets where nobody looks. It's mine. I answer to it when the customs men come aboard, because lying to customs men is how boats get impounded, and the *Wren* is the only thing I own outright and I intend to die before she does.

Everything else about me is negotiable.

* * *

The *Wren* is sixty feet of riveted steel and bad decisions, built in 1938 as a government survey launch and sold surplus after a scandal I've never bothered to research because the scandal wasn't hers. She's got a diesel engine old enough to collect a pension, a wheel that fights you like a live thing, and a hull that sounds like a drum full of rain when the current slaps her sides. She handles beautifully in water that would fold a newer boat in half, which is the only reason I forgive her for drinking fuel like a dockworker drinks arrack.

She's also rotting. More on that later, or maybe not—I'm getting ahead of myself, and a captain who gets ahead of herself ends up on a sandbar, and I have forty-one reasons not to do that.

This morning I came down from Meridian with deck cargo: rice in sealed drums, kerosene, roofing tin, a crate of machetes someone in the interior had been waiting four months for, and—because I am not entirely a monster—a sack of boiled sweets for the ferryman's kids at Tallow Bend, who wave at me like I'm a carnival every time I pass. Which I am, sort of. A carnival that arrives twice a month whether they want it or not.

The run itself was unremarkable, which is the highest praise a riverman gives. Unremarkable means nobody died, nothing sank, and the cargo arrived dry. I took the eastern channel past the Widow because the western one silted up in June and nobody has surveyed it since, and I made Meridian by dusk with the light going copper on the water and the mosquitoes rising off the banks in clouds so thick they looked like weather.

At the dock, Old Piet was waiting with his hand out and his ledger open, same as always. Piet has run the Meridian freight office since before I had a license, and he is the closest thing I have to an employer, an accountant, and a confessor, though he'd deny all three. He's a dried-up man shaped like a question mark, with glasses thick as bottle bottoms and a memory for cargo manifests that borders on the supernatural.

"Forty-one again?" he asked, not looking up from his ledger. He asks that every time. It's our ritual. Neither of us remembers who started it.

"Forty-one," I said. "Twelfth bar split. Channel's moved east maybe thirty yards."

"You should tell the survey office."

"The survey office doesn't pay me."

"No," Piet said, squinting at a column of figures. "But the survey office talks to the dredging office, and the dredging office talks to the port authority, and the port authority could make your life easier or harder depending on its mood." He licked his pencil. "You could make friends, Hartwell. It's not fatal. I've done it for years."

"I have friends," I said. "They're all dead, imaginary, or named Wren."

Piet looked at me over his glasses then, the way he does maybe twice a year, when something in my voice tells him I'm not joking even though I'm using joke-words. He didn't say anything. That's why I tolerate him. A man who knows when not to ask is worth ten men who know when to help.

* * *

Here is what people say about me on the Sable, when they say anything at all:

That I'm the best pilot on the river. This is true, or close enough that arguing about it isn't worth the breath. There are men who've run these waters longer than I've been alive, and some of them are very good, but good is knowing the channel. What I do is different. I read the river the way other people read faces—the color of the water where it shallows, the V of the current folding over a hidden bar, the way driftwood collects on the upstream shoulder of a snag and tells you where the deep water isn't. My father taught me that, back when teaching me things was still something he did. I'll come back to him. Not yet.

That I'm strange. Also true. I live aboard, alone, by choice, in a cabin the size of a generous closet. I talk to the boat more than to people. I keep no photographs on the walls. When the mission boat comes through with its loudspeaker and its hymns, I don't attend, but I also don't mock it, because mocking the faithful is the hobby of people with nothing better to fear.

And that I never stay anywhere. This is the one that follows me from dock to dock like a dog that's decided I'm its problem. Meridian, Tallow Bend, Corso Landing, the fuel depot at Halfway—it doesn't matter where I tie up. Somebody eventually sidles up and asks, gently or not so gently, why a woman with my hands and my head doesn't settle somewhere. Buy a berth. Take a permanent contract. Marry the ferryman at Tallow Bend, who has asked, in his way, three times now, each time with slightly more boiled sweets involved.

I have answers. I keep them polished like the brass rail on the *Wren*'s bow. I'm saving money. I'm waiting for the right berth. The river's in my blood. I like the independence. All true, in the way that a screen door is a door. They keep the questions out without keeping anybody in.

The real answer is simpler and worse: staying anywhere means being findable, and being findable means being known, and being known means the day comes when somebody connects Captain Hartwell of the *Wren* to the Hartwells of wherever-it-was, and then everything I built gets measured against everything I left, and I lose either way. So I move. Miles, not years. I measure my life in miles because miles don't ask where you're from. A sandbar doesn't care what your father did. Water doesn't hold grudges; it just holds everything, briefly, and lets go.

Most days that's enough. Most days the work fills the silence the way ballast fills a bilge—heavy, useful, indifferent. You load, you run, you unload, you count your bars, you sleep with one ear on the engine's heartbeat, and you wake up and do it again, and the repetition is the point. Repetition is a kind of armor. Nothing gets in if nothing changes.

It was a most-days kind of day, right up until the radio crackled.

* * *

The set on the *Wren* is a marine band unit older than I am, salvaged off a wrecked patrol boat and rebuilt twice by a technician in Meridian who charges me in fish. It crackles and fades and picks up a Filipino station on clear nights when the atmosphere cooperates, playing songs from a country I've never seen, and I listen sometimes while I drink my coffee at dawn and pretend, briefly, that I'm the kind of person who goes places on purpose.

At 1900 hours, with the light gone and the mooring lines set, it crackled with something local instead.

"Station calling vessel *Wren*, vessel *Wren*, come in, over."

I made coffee strong enough to strip paint and answered, because ignoring a call is bad manners on a river where manners are the only law that gets enforced consistently.

"*Wren* receiving. Go ahead."

Static, then a voice I didn't recognize—young, clipped, the accent of somebody educated at expense. "Captain, this is Odell Crane, Crane Mercantile, out of Port Anselm. I understand you ran the lower Sable today. Impressive timing, per the freight office."

Crane Mercantile. I knew the name the way you know a name you've seen on crates and invoices—faintly, without interest. Supply house. Big outfit, southern ports, fingers in everything from kerosene to canned milk.

"Timing's the job," I said. "What can I do for Crane Mercantile?"

"A charter. Upriver." A pause, the sound of paper moving. "Kessari Basin. Above the last government station."

I put my cup down. Above the last station is a phrase with weight on the Sable. Below the stations, the river is commerce—charts, buoys, dredged channels, radios that mostly work. Above them, the river becomes rumor. The Kessari Basin especially. Headwaters country. Narrow water, shifting bars, no surveys worth trusting, and during monsoon season—which was coming, the air had been tasting of iron for a week—a place where the river stops being a road and becomes a weather system with opinions.

"Nobody runs above the station this late," I said. "Season's turning."

"That's precisely why we're offering triple rates."

Triple rates. I did the arithmetic in my head, the way you do when a number is designed to stop your thinking. Triple my standard charter would cover the *Wren*'s deferred maintenance with change left over. New planking. A proper overhaul. Maybe even the second fuel tank I'd been talking myself out of for three years.

"And the cargo?" I asked, because cargo tells you what a man really wants.

"General resupply. Tools, provisions, survey equipment." Another pause, longer. "For an expedition camp already established in the Basin. Scientific survey party. We're their logistics contractor. They've requested additional stores before the monsoon closes the upper river."

Survey equipment. My father had owned survey equipment. I filed that away in the pocket where I keep things I'm not ready to look at.

"What's the rush? If the monsoon's closing the river, seems like the smart move is wait for it to open again."

"The expedition's schedule doesn't accommodate waiting, Captain. And frankly—" the voice smoothed out, went reasonable, went salesman "—neither does yours, if the freight office gossip is accurate. Triple rates for a two-week round trip. You could refit that boat of yours top to bottom."

He knew about the boat. Of course he did. On a river this size, everybody knows everybody's business except the business that matters, and I felt the familiar prickle of being *known*, the feeling I spend my whole life avoiding, arriving anyway, wearing a polite voice and a fat contract.

"I'll need details in writing," I said. "Coordinates, manifest, contact at the camp, terms."

"All forwarded tonight. One condition." Paper again. "You'd be carrying a passenger. Our man, meeting the expedition. Quiet fellow, cartographer. He'll have his own gear, needs no accommodations beyond deck space."

A passenger. I hadn't taken a passenger in six years—not since a missionary's wife had spent nine days asking me questions about myself with the tireless precision of a customs interrogation, and I had spent nine days answering none of them, and had sworn off the whole species of human cargo forever.

"Passengers are extra," I said. "And I don't do conversation."

"He's not conversational," Crane assured me. "He maps things. You'll barely notice him."

Barely noticing a person is, in my experience, impossible. People are the loudest cargo there is. But triple rates buy a lot of tolerance, and I told myself what I always tell myself: two weeks, up and back, before the monsoon, money in the bank, boat fixed, anonymity intact. A clean transaction. In and out above the last station like a smuggler, visible to no one, memorable to no one.

Except that instinct—that cold little pilot at the base of my skull that reads water and weather and men, and has kept me alive through things I don't narrate at docks—except that instinct said *no*. Said it clearly, the way it says it when a bar has shifted overnight or when a passenger's smile is a half-second too slow. Something about Crane's smoothness. Something about the words *already established* and *requested additional stores* sitting together wrong, like furniture arranged to hide a stain. Expeditions that need rescuing send different messages than expeditions that need groceries, and this message had the specific polish of a man rehearsing.

I stood on the aft deck with the radio humming and the river running black under the first stars, and I had the argument with myself that I have perhaps twice a year, the serious one, the one where the two versions of me lay out their cases.

The captain said take the contract. Money, refit, done, gone. The captain is practical and tired and has earned the right to be.

The other one—the one with no title, the one who counts sandbars because counting is the only inheritance I've kept clean—said that upriver runs are how stories start, and I moved all those miles specifically so no story could ever find me.

The captain won. The captain usually wins, because the other one doesn't have a counteroffer, just a feeling, and feelings don't pay for planking.

"Send the papers," I told Crane. "I'll look them over at dawn. If the coordinates check and the manifest's honest, I'll run it."

"Excellent, Captain. You won't regret—"

"I regret plenty," I said, and signed off before he could finish the sentence, because finishing sentences is how salesmen win.

* * *

Piet came down at dawn with the contract folder and a face like a funeral. He'd read it before I did. He reads everything before I do; it's in our unwritten articles.

"Triple rates," he said, handing it over. "Real ones, in the schedule. And a clause I don't love—penalty for non-delivery, and a confidentiality provision. You don't discuss the charter, the cargo, or the client. With anyone. Including me, apparently, though I notice they wrote it before asking whether I existed."

Confidentiality. For a grocery run. I read the clause twice. It was broad enough to drive a barge through sideways. I read the coordinates third: a camp site in the Kessari headwaters, above the last government station, above anything charted, at a bend marked on my oldest inherited map with a notation in my father's handwriting that I have never let anyone else see.

I sat with that for a long moment, longer than a business decision deserves, while Piet fidgeted and the river went silver-pink and the mosquitoes held their morning council on the pilings.

Then I signed. Because the captain wins, and because the money was real, and because—this is the part I'll deny if anyone ever asks—some small, buried, treacherous part of me wanted to see that bend again. Wanted to see what grew there now, whether the old survey post still stood, whether the river remembered me the way I remember it.

I told myself it was just a supply run. Forty-one sandbars below, and then unmapped water, and then home before the rains.

The radio crackled at noon with Crane's final confirmation. Passenger confirmed. Elias Voss, cartographer, boarding at Dock Nine, Meridian, tomorrow, 0800. One sealed case, personal effects, no manifest required.

No manifest required. For a cartographer. Going to a survey camp.

I stood at the rail and watched the water carry yesterday downstream, and I thought: well. At least the money's good.

The river, which had heard me say some version of that lie my entire adult life, said nothing back. Rivers never do. That's the trouble with them. They just keep taking the miles—and lately, I've started to wonder what happens on the day the miles run out, and the only thing left ahead of me is the one direction I swore I'd never go.

Upriver. Toward the Basin. Toward whatever my father left there, and whoever else is looking for it.

Dock Nine, 0800. I'd be there. Against every instinct I owned.
