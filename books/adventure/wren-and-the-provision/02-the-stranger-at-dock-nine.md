## 2. The Stranger at Dock Nine

I was at Dock Nine by half past seven, which for me counts as punctuality and for everyone else on the Sable counts as a personality disorder. The fog hadn't burned off yet. It hung over the water in gray ropes, and the dock lamps made soft yellow coins of themselves, and the whole riverfront had that held-breath quality it gets before the day commits to anything.

The *Wren* sat low in the water where I'd moored her overnight, riding easy against the pilings. I'd spent the dark hours below decks with a lamp and a screwdriver, checking seams, tapping planking, listening to the hull talk back. Boats tell you things if you listen. Mine had been telling me things for two years, and like most people who can't afford the truth, I'd been nodding politely and ignoring it.

There was one plank, starboard side just aft of the midship bend, that answered my knuckle with a sound like a wet cough.

I marked it with chalk and told myself I'd deal with it in Corren's Landing, where timber was cheap and lies were cheaper. Then I went up top, put coffee on the little spirit burner, and waited for my cargo to show up and make itself my problem.

It showed up early too.

I heard him before I saw him—boots on the dock boards, an uneven rhythm, like a man walking with something heavy in one hand and his balance in the other. He came out of the fog carrying a case.

Not a bag. Not a pack. A *case*—hard-sided, brass-cornered, the kind of thing you see lawyers carry into courthouses or men carry out of banks. It was strapped shut with two leather belts buckled down flat, and he held it the way you hold a sleeping animal you don't entirely trust: close, careful, angled away from strangers.

He stopped at the head of the dock and looked at my boat for a long moment. I watched him do the arithmetic every traveler does when they first see the *Wren*—the patched awning, the paint gone chalky at the waterline, the wheelhouse that listed a degree to port like a man leaning into a joke only he found funny. I watched him decide whether he was desperate enough.

Then he looked up and saw me watching him, and I watched him decide to pretend he hadn't been doing any of that.

"Captain Hartwell?" he called.

Nobody had called me Hartwell in six years. My spine did something cold and involuntary, and I covered it by lifting my coffee mug about half an inch, which is as close to a wave as I get before sunrise.

"Depends who's asking."

"Elias Voss." He came down the dock toward me, boots ringing on the wet boards. Up close he was younger than his voice—thirty, maybe thirty-two—with the kind of face that gets described as "unremarkable" in reports and then turns up in your memory for weeks anyway. Brown hair going gray early at the temples. A cartographer's squint, like he was permanently adjusting for distance. And eyes that did the same thing mine do in mirrors: kept moving, never settled, always checking the exits.

"Voss," I said. "You're not my cargo."

"No." He set the case down between his feet, which I noted he did without letting go of the handle. "I'm hoping to be your passenger."

"I don't take passengers."

"I know. I asked around." He said it without embarrassment, which earned him a point. Most men would have dressed it up. "Three different dockhands told me you run supplies and nothing else, and that you once threw a magistrate's cousin off your boat for asking to ride along. Is that true?"

"He was drunk and he touched my wheel. Throwing him off was a safety measure."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. More like the memory of one, filed away somewhere dusty. "Then I'll keep my hands to myself and my questions short. I need to go upriver. To the Kessari Basin. Your contract runs through Kessari Bend—I checked the postings at the harbor office."

"You checked the postings," I repeated. "At the harbor office."

"I'm thorough. It's the trade I'm in."

"And what trade is that?"

"Maps." He said it simply, but his hand shifted on the case handle—a small motion, half an inch, the way a man touches a wound without meaning to. "Cartography. Survey work. There's an expedition camped past the last surveyed bend. They hired me on four months ago to chart the upper channels. I was supposed to travel up with them from Solenne."

"But you didn't."

"No."

He said it flat, no elaboration offered, and I recognized the tone because I use it myself—the voice you use when a question has gotten too close to something and you're closing the door on it before it sees inside. I've slammed that particular door so many times the hinges don't even squeak anymore.

So naturally, being me, I pushed.

"They left without you."

"They left without me." A muscle worked in his jaw. "We were supposed to depart on the twelfth. On the eleventh there was a—disagreement. About terms. By the time I got back to the dock their boat was a mile downstream and gaining."

"What kind of disagreement?"

"The kind where they kept the advance money and I kept the maps I'd already drawn." His chin dipped toward the case. "Which they wanted. Which is why I'm standing here instead of sitting in Solenne waiting for them to come back and collect."

I drank my coffee and let the silence do some work. Fog was lifting off the water in slow rags now; somewhere upstream a barge horn groaned, long and low, like the river clearing its throat.

Here's what I knew already, without knowing how I knew it: this man was lying. Not about everything. About the shape of things. Men who get left behind by their own expeditions over money disputes don't clutch their cases like that. Men who lose an argument about terms don't look over their shoulders at an empty dock the way he'd done twice since we started talking—quick, professional glances, the kind I do.

But triple pay is triple pay, and the Basin run was already paid for, and a passenger with cash was a passenger with cash.

"How much?" I said.

"Passage to the camp coordinates and back. Whatever you think is fair."

"That's not how I price things. I price things at whatever I think will make you flinch least and pay most. So: forty marks, plus board. You sleep in the hold with the crates, you eat what I eat, and if I say jump overboard, you ask how deep first."

"Thirty-five, and I don't sleep in the hold. There's a bench in the wheelhouse."

"There's a bench in the wheelhouse because it's where I sit when I want to be alone."

"Then we'll take shifts," he said. "I'm used to odd hours. Maps don't care about daylight."

I should have said no. I want that on the record, wherever records go. Every instinct I own—and I own good ones, honed on six years of staying alive by listening to them—said this man was trouble wearing a surveyor's coat, that the case was trouble packed in brass corners, that the whole arrangement smelled like the first page of a story where somebody ends up in the river.

But I'd also spent the night listening to my hull cough, and the repair bill was sitting in my chest like a stone, and forty—thirty-five—marks was thirty-five marks I didn't have.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"You don't ask where I've been, I don't ask what's in the case. We're two people going the same direction, that's all. The moment either of us forgets that, the arrangement's over, and I put you off at the nearest bank whether there's a town there or not."

He considered that. Really considered it—I could see him weighing it against whatever he wasn't telling me, testing its weight. Then he stuck out his hand.

"Two people going the same direction," he said.

His grip was dry and careful, calloused across the fingers in the specific pattern I'd learn later comes from decades of pen work. I let go fast.

"Deck Nine's yours until we load. Be back here by noon with whatever you're bringing aboard, and Voss—" I nodded at the case. "Whatever's in there stays strapped. I've carried gunpowder, medicine, and once a caged civet that got loose in Corren's Landing and ruined my reputation in three parishes. I know how cargo behaves. Cargo doesn't watch me while I sleep."

"It doesn't watch you," he said. "I do apologize for the case."

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to whoever made those straps, because they're doing all the work."

That got the almost-smile again, a fraction wider this time. He picked up the case—heavy, I noticed; heavy enough that his shoulder dropped under it—and walked back up the dock toward the town, and I stood at the rail drinking bad coffee and telling myself the cold feeling in my stomach was just the coffee.

* * *

Loading started at noon and went wrong immediately, which is normal.

My usual dock crew at Nine were the Ombasi brothers, twin stevedores built like doorframes, and they arrived with the expedition's freight on a mule dray: crated instruments, tinned food, canvas bundles, coils of rope, and a crate of survey paper so heavy two grown men carried it like pallbearers. The manifest said it all belonged to the Kessari expedition, consigned upstream, sender listed as a name I didn't recognize—some trading concern out of the capital, initials stamped on every crate in black ink.

K.R.C. I remember noticing that. Remembering it later cost me more than I'd like to admit.

"Where's your passenger?" Tam Ombasi asked, hooking a crate onto the winch.

"Fetching his things."

"Mm." Tam gave me the look brothers give you when they've known you since you were stealing apples off their father's tree. "Yara. That man came asking about you yesterday. At the office. Asked which boat ran the Basin route, how often, whether you traveled alone."

The winch cable creaked. Somewhere behind us a gull screamed its opinion.

"Did he now."

"Asked if you had family." Tam shrugged, all innocence, which from Tam is a weapon. "I told him captains are born from eggs, like lizards. He didn't laugh."

"No," I said. "He wouldn't."

I turned a crate label over in my hands and read it three times without seeing it. Family. Six years of building a woman with no family, no history, no name worth saying twice—and within a day of taking this contract, a stranger was asking after mine. Coincidence happens on rivers. But coincidence usually travels light, and this one had brass corners.

When Voss came back down the dock an hour later, I was ready for him. Or thought I was.

He'd changed into river clothes—canvas trousers, a salt-stained jacket—and he'd brought remarkably little. One duffel. A leather tube for charts slung across his back. And the case, still strapped, still clutched.

"Stowage is under the aft bench," I said. "Duffel goes there. Case goes where you can see it and I can see you."

"Fair."

He stowed his gear with a competence that surprised me—not the fumbling of a landsman but the quick economy of someone who'd lived aboard something before. He coiled his spare line correctly. He wiped his boots. When the Ombasis swung the first crate aboard, he caught it and walked it into place without being asked, reading the weight, setting it low and centered.

"You've shipped out before," I said.

"A survey launch, years ago. Coastal work." He settled the crate against its neighbor and braced it. "You load by feel. Most people load by list."

"Most people sink."

"So I've heard." He glanced at me sideways. "Your boat lists to port."

"She's not listing. She's leaning in to hear better."

This time the smile actually arrived, brief and unguarded, and then he caught himself and put it away like contraband. I pretended not to have seen it. We loaded for three hours in the building heat, and I'll say this for Elias Voss: he worked. No man who works like that is only running from a money dispute.

By late afternoon the *Wren* sat deep with freight, and the fog had long since burned away, and the river lay out ahead of us all the way to the horizon, brown and patient and full of yesterday heading downstream.

Voss stood at the rail beside me, uninvited, looking north upriver the way I was.

"The Basin," he said. "Have you been up there before?"

"Once. Years ago. As a child."

"With family?"

The question landed exactly where questions aren't allowed to land. I kept my eyes on the water.

"We're not doing this," I said. "Remember? Two people, same direction."

"Right." He was quiet a moment. "Only—it's strange. Four months on that expedition, and I never met anyone who'd been past the Bend. The old charts stop there. Blank paper, and a note in the margin from some surveyor sixty years dead: *here be the end of what is known.*" He shook his head slowly. "And you stood on that deck and said you'd been there. As a child. Before the maps ended."

"Children go places maps haven't heard of. That's the whole point of children."

He laughed—one short breath through the nose—but he didn't drop it. I could feel him holding the question the way he held the case: close, careful, angled away.

"Tomorrow at dawn," I said, stepping back toward the wheelhouse. "If you're late, I leave without you, and I keep the thirty-five marks as a lesson fee."

"I'm never late." He hoisted the case. "I'm thorough. It's the trade I'm in."

He went below, and I stood alone at the rail while the dock lamps came on one by one down the waterfront, little yellow coins in the blue dusk, and I thought about a man asking dockhands whether Captain Hartwell had family.

I thought about blank paper past the Bend, and a note in a dead surveyor's margin, and a case with brass corners that nobody puts down.

And underneath all of it, quiet as silt settling, I thought about my father's voice, thirty years gone, reading aloud from a chart by lamplight while rain hammered the cabin roof: *Past the Bend, little wren, the river keeps secrets even the mapmakers couldn't buy.*

I hadn't thought of that voice in years. Now it wouldn't stop.

Dawn was coming. Against every instinct I owned, so was the Basin.
