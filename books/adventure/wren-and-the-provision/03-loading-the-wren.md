## 3. Loading the Wren

The Wren was forty feet of stubbornness held together by paint, prayer, and a hull that had opinions about the weather. I'd bought her eleven years ago from a man in Tallow Bend who swore she'd run the Sable until the Sable ran dry. He hadn't mentioned that the Sable would try to run dry *through* her.

I found the rot on the second morning of loading, which is the way rot always finds you: not when you're looking, but when your hands are full and your schedule is tight and the river doesn't care.

I'd been hauling crates of rice up the gangplank since dawn, trading them from the dock to the deck to the hold in a rhythm I could do half-asleep, which was fortunate, because I had been half-asleep since the radio call. Triple pay. Kessari Basin. Ten days upriver through water I half-knew and half-invented. My instinct had said no. My bank account had said yes with the enthusiasm of a drowning man.

So I was carrying rice and not thinking when my boot went through the deck plank.

Not through-through. But the wood gave under my heel with a soft, rotten sigh, like an old man agreeing with something he hadn't heard, and I stood there with forty pounds of rice in my arms and my heart doing arithmetic I didn't like.

I set the crate down. Knelt. Pressed my thumb into the plank, and it dented the way overripe fruit dents. I pried up the edge with my knife and the wood came away in wet, gray-brown layers, soft as cake, smelling of mushroom and ruin.

"Well," I said to the hold, to the rice, to nobody. "There it is."

Rot in the hull planking. Below the waterline, almost certainly, because that's where rot likes to live, down where you can't see it and it can quietly decide whether you keep breathing. I'd been meaning to haul her out for a proper inspection since the dry season. I'd been meaning to since the dry season *before* that. There was never enough money to do it right and never enough courage to do it wrong and find out how bad it really was.

Now I was about to find out how bad it really was, with a paying contract and a passenger and a monsoon season breathing down the river's neck.

I spent an hour on my back in the bilge with a lamp and a screwdriver, poking at planking like a doctor who already knows the diagnosis and is just deciding how to say it. Three planks soft near the stern. One of them bad enough that my screwdriver went in a half inch without argument. The others were negotiable. In boat terms, negotiable means: it might hold, or it might open up like a mouth in eight feet of brown water, and you won't know which until it happens.

The proper fix was new timber, marine grade, tarred and fitted. I did the numbers twice, hoping the second time would be kinder. It wasn't. The proper fix cost more than I'd make on the Kessari run even at triple rates.

The improper fix cost a coil of tarred canvas, a bucket of pitch, and my self-respect, which was already heavily mortgaged.

I crawled out into the daylight, gray to the elbows, and found Elias Voss standing on my dock with a crate in each arm and the expression of a man who has been waiting to be noticed for some time.

"Where do you want these?" he asked.

"Anywhere that isn't the stern," I said. "And you didn't have to carry those."

"You were under the boat. Someone had to."

"I was under the boat because the boat is my business."

"And I'm your cargo," he said, setting the crates down with more care than most stevedores I'd hired and fired. "Which makes me your business too. Temporarily."

I looked at him. He looked back with that patient, surveyor's stillness, like he was used to waiting for rivers to reveal themselves and had decided I was one.

"Fine," I said. "Then make yourself useful. There's a pry bar by the fuel drums. The deck planking forward of the hold needs checking—press every third board. If it gives under your thumb, mark it with chalk."

He rolled up his sleeves without another word, and I went to have a bad conversation with a lumber merchant.

* * *

Sefu Okafor had the only timber yard in Port Lachry that stocked boat-grade planking, and he had the face of a man who knew it. He was a broad, cheerful, merciless old crocodile who had sold me rope, paint, and disappointment for a decade, and he greeted me now with the warmth of a favorite customer, which is to say he was already calculating.

"Captain Hartwell." He spread his hands over his stacks of lumber like a priest over an altar. "What can I—"

"Three planks. Hull grade. Tarred if you've got it."

His hands came down. "For the Wren?"

"For the Wren."

He clicked his tongue, which in Sefu's language was a full paragraph of sympathy and pricing. "You know what hull grade runs now. The mill upriver flooded in the spring rains. Supply's short."

"Sefu."

"Yara." He smiled like a knife. "You always say my name like that, like it's a discount."

I named a number. He laughed, kindly, the way you laugh at a child's drawing. I named a higher number. He shook his head with genuine sorrow. We went back and forth in the old dance, and I watched the numbers settle in a place that would eat most of my triple pay and leave the Wren patched with canvas and pitch and hope.

"Canvas and pitch," Sefu said, when I told him what I'd do instead, and for a moment the crocodile left his face and the old boatman underneath looked out. "You'll make the Kessari run on canvas and pitch?"

"Everyone makes the Kessari run on something."

"Not everyone comes back to argue about it."

I paid a deposit on two planks—the two worst, the ones I couldn't pretend away—and left the third to the pitch bucket. Outside, the morning had gone hot and white, and the river lay flat as hammered tin, waiting.

Elias was on the dock when I got back, sitting on a bollard with his notebook open, and he'd done something I hadn't asked him to do: he'd chalked the deck. Every soft board marked with a small, neat X. There were more Xs than I wanted to count, and I counted them anyway, because that's the kind of woman I am. Eleven.

"Eleven," I said.

"Eleven," he agreed. "I pressed all of them. Twice."

"You didn't have to press them twice."

"The first time, I assumed I was doing it wrong." He closed the notebook. "How bad is the hull?"

"Manageable."

"That's a captain's word, not a carpenter's."

"It's the only word I'm offering at this dock."

He nodded, unbothered, and I went to load fuel and he went back to carrying crates, and I tried not to notice that he carried them the way he did everything—carefully, deliberately, like each one had a place it wanted to be and he intended to find out where.

* * *

He was gone by midday. I assumed he'd gone to find food, or to haunt the harbor office, or to stand somewhere and look at the water, which cartographers apparently did the way other men smoked. I didn't ask. Asking is how conversations start, and conversations are how people learn things about you, and I had built my whole life on the principle that nobody needed to learn anything about me that a freight manifest couldn't tell them.

He came back two hours before sundown, and he came back with a barge.

Not his barge. A hired lighter, poled up the channel by two of Sefu's yard hands, riding low and heavy, and stacked on its deck under tarpaulin were planks. Good planks. I could tell from thirty feet—hull grade, dense and dark, the heartwood kind that sinks in water and outlasts the men who buy it. Enough of them for a hull. Enough for my hull.

The lighter bumped against the Wren's fenders and Elias stood in the bow with a receipt in his hand and an expression he was working very hard to keep neutral, which on him meant it looked slightly constipated.

"Before you say anything," he said, "I want to be clear about the terms."

I couldn't say anything. My throat had closed in a way I associated with grief and was surprised to find associated with timber.

"Terms," I managed.

"Terms." He held up the receipt. "This is a loan. Against your fee for the run. I'll take it out of what you're paying—out of what you're *not* paying me, I suppose, since I never negotiated a rate. You'll deduct the cost of the timber from my passage, and when the run's done, we're square, and neither of us mentions it again."

"That's not a loan. That's me owing you."

"It's arithmetic," he said. "There's a difference. A loan is a favor. This is just a number moving from one column to another."

"Sefu wouldn't sell me that stock. He said supply was short."

"He said that to you." Elias looked at the planks with something like embarrassment. "He said a different thing to me. Something about how the Hartwell girl haggles him down to the bone every year and he has to make his margin somewhere, and how it's a shame about the Wren's hull because she's the best hull on this stretch of river and it would be a crime to lose her to a plank."

"He said *Hartwell*?"

"He said the captain of the Wren. He used your name. I assumed it was yours." A pause, the first crack in his own careful surface. "Was I wrong to use it?"

I looked at the timber. I looked at the river. I looked at the man standing in the bow of a borrowed barge holding a receipt like a peace treaty, and I felt something in my chest shift its weight, the way cargo shifts when a boat takes a swell—nothing dramatic, nothing dangerous, just the load settling into a new configuration, and the boat riding differently after.

"No," I said. "You weren't wrong."

"Good." He handed the receipt down to me, and our fingers didn't touch, which I noted and dismissed and noted again. "The yard hands will help fit it. Sefu's sending his foreman in the morning. He says—" and here Elias's constipated expression deepened, because he was about to deliver a message he knew would land somewhere soft, "—he says no one runs the Sable like you, and it would be a bad year for the river to lose the Wren."

"Sefu said that."

"Sefu said that."

"Sefu, who charged me double for rope in March."

"People contain multitudes," Elias said. "I read that somewhere. Or maybe I mapped it."

I laughed. It came out of me before I could stop it, short and rusty, like an engine turning over after a long winter, and Elias Voss looked at me with an expression I chose to interpret as professional satisfaction and not anything warmer, because interpreting it as anything warmer would have meant the armor had a crack in it, and I had a run to make and a hull to fix and no time for cracks.

"Fine," I said. "The timber goes in the hold, aft of the rice. And you—" I pointed at him, and he had the good grace to look like a man being pointed at. "You eat on my boat tonight. There's stew. It's terrible. That's not a favor either, it's arithmetic—you work, you eat."

"That's the best terms I've been offered all year," he said.

* * *

We worked until dark and past it, the two yard hands and the foreman and Elias and me, and by lantern light the Wren's stern opened up like a wound being cleaned. The rotten planks came out in pieces that crumbled when you looked at them hard, and I stood in the bilge holding one of them and thought about all the water that had passed beneath it while it quietly rotted, all the miles I'd counted, all the sandbars I'd dodged, and how the whole time the real danger had been riding with me, patient, soft-spoken, waiting for a heavy load and a narrow channel.

There was probably a lesson in that. I put it down with the old planking and didn't pick it up.

The new timber went in true. Sefu's foreman was an artist with a adze, and Elias turned out to have hands that knew wood—surveyor's hands, I supposed, all that instrument work and tent-pole carpentry—and by midnight the three of us who were still awake sat on the deck with the tar smell rising off the fresh seams and the river making its small endless remarks against the hull.

"Sounds different," Elias said.

"What does?"

"The water. Against the new planks. It's a cleaner sound."

I listened. He was right. The river had a new voice against the Wren's stern, tighter, more honest, and I felt an absurd prickle behind my eyes that I blamed on tar fumes.

"Thank you," I said. The words came out stiff and strange, like a language I'd studied but never spoken.

"You're welcome," Elias said, and then, because he was apparently incapable of letting a decent silence stand: "You know, most people would have asked why."

"Why what?"

"Why a cartographer would buy a boat a hull of timber for a captain he met yesterday." He turned his tin cup in his hands. "Most people would want to know what I want."

"I know what you want. Passage upriver. You said so."

"I did."

"And people who want more than they say usually say more than they want to. You didn't. So either you're honest or you're better at this than most, and I've got ten days upriver to figure out which." I stood, joints cracking in chorus with the cooling planks. "Get some sleep, Voss. We load the last of it at dawn, and then we go find your survey team."

He looked up at me, lantern light on his tired face, and for a second he almost said something else—I saw it form and die, a word or a question or a warning—and then he just nodded and said, "Goodnight, Captain."

I went below to my bunk and lay listening to the river against the new planks, that clean, honest sound, and told myself the tightness in my chest was just the tar.

It was almost true.

At dawn, when I came up with the last of the fuel drums, Elias was already on the dock, and he wasn't alone. A harbor boy stood beside him, holding out a telegram flimsy, and the expression on Elias's face as he read it was one I'd seen before on the faces of men reading casualty lists.

"Voss," I said. "What is it?"

He looked up at me, and the harbor morning seemed suddenly very still.

"It's from the expedition office," he said. "They've stopped answering the radio."
