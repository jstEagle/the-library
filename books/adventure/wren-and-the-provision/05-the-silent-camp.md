## 5. The Silent Camp

The coordinates put us at a bend where the Kessari widened into something less like a river and more like a slow brown thought. Elias had been standing at the bow for the better part of an hour with his binoculars raised, and I'd been pretending not to watch him do it, because watching a man look for something he's afraid to find is its own kind of bad luck.

"Smoke," he said. Then, a minute later: "No. Wait. That's mist."

"Mist," I agreed, and throttled down anyway.

The Wren slid through water the color of steeped tea, past buttress roots that rose out of the flood like the knees of drowned giants. Somewhere in the green, birds were making a sound I can only describe as a rusty gate arguing with itself. It should have been peaceful. Instead my hands kept wanting to check things that didn't need checking — the throttle, the depth gauge, the little brass compass my father gave me when I was nine and which has never once pointed anywhere useful.

"You're quiet," Elias said.

"I'm piloting."

"You're piloting *quietly*. There's a difference. You've been narrating every sandbar since dawn like a woman doing commentary at her own funeral."

He wasn't wrong. I count things when I'm nervous — sandbars, bends, the seconds between lightning and thunder, back when there was lightning. It's how I keep the world from getting too big. And this morning the world had been getting very big indeed, because we were past the last mapped channel now, into territory that existed on Elias's charts only as a polite blank space labeled *unsurveyed*, and because the radio had been making a sound all night like someone breathing through wet cloth, and because three days ago a man in a dry office had paid me triple to bring supplies to people who, if they were still alive, were the only ones who could tell me what triple pay was actually buying.

"There." Elias lowered the binoculars. "Starboard bank. That clearing."

I saw it before he finished saying it: a gap in the canopy where the trees had been cut back, and beneath it, pale shapes. Tents. Or what used to be tents.

I brought the Wren in slow, engine idling, hull whispering against the reeds. The mud took our mooring line without complaint. The whole clearing had the particular silence of a place that had been loud recently and then stopped — not the silence of wilderness, which is never really silent, but the silence of a room after an argument.

"Hello the camp!" Elias called, and his voice came out steadier than mine would have. "Elias Voss! Survey team! Anyone here?"

Nothing answered except the rusty-gate bird, which I was beginning to suspect of having opinions about us.

* * *

The first tent had collapsed inward, poles snapped clean, canvas sagging over whatever it had been sheltering. I circled it the way you circle a sleeping animal. Under the flap: a cot, a duffel, a lantern still full of oil. Nobody home.

The second tent was worse, because it was tidy. Bed made. Boots paired by the door, toes out, like their owner expected to come back and step into them. A tin cup on a crate with tea gone to mold in the bottom. When a camp is scattered, you think weather, animals, panic. When a camp is *tidy*, you think the people left in a hurry carrying exactly what mattered and nothing that didn't, and your spine starts composing letters to your brain that you don't want delivered.

"Yara." Elias was crouched by the fire pit. Ash, rain-softened but layered — someone had burned paper here. A lot of paper. He lifted a blackened curl of it between two fingers and it crumbled, but not before I caught the ghost of a gridline. Map paper. Somebody had fed maps to a fire.

"Maybe they were done with them," I said.

"Surveyors are never done with their maps. Surveyors sleep holding their maps." He stood, and I watched him do the arithmetic I was already doing. "This isn't abandonment. This is erasure."

The equipment shed told the rest of the story, or enough of it. Crates pried open, not smashed — opened by someone patient, someone with time. Instruments gone: theodolites, levels, the good cameras. Records gone. What remained was the heavy stuff nobody steals — a generator with its fuel drained, rope coiled with insulting neatness, a stack of empty ration crates stamped with a logo I recognized from the manifest in my own hold. Odell Crane Expeditions. The financier's name was on everything, the way a name is on everything when a man wants credit and on nothing when he wants distance.

And then the radio.

It sat on a table under an awning, or what was left of it did. Smashed — and I want to be precise here, because precision was about to become the most important thing I owned. The casing wasn't broken outward. The dent was *inward*, driven by something swung hard from above, and the antenna was bent double, and the microphone cord had been cut, not torn. Someone had stood at that table, in the dry, under cover, and destroyed their own link to the outside world with a calm hand.

"No bodies," Elias said quietly. He'd come up beside me without my hearing him, which I chose not to hold against him. "No blood. No graves."

"People don't smash their own radio from inside a camp unless—" I stopped.

"Unless they're making sure no one calls for help," he said, "or making sure no one hears them call."

We looked at each other across the dead radio, and the adventure, such as it had been — the triple pay, the blank map, the two travelers playing at being brave — packed its bags and left without either of us noticing the door.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world gets too big. I counted.

Four tents, one collapsed, three abandoned tidy. One fire pit, two burn layers — cooking ash below, paper ash above. One radio, murdered. One shed, emptied of anything with a serial number. Zero people, zero remains, zero explanations.

Then I found the fifth thing, and counting stopped helping.

It was wedged under the third tent's floor edge, half-buried in mud, and I only found it because my boot nearly went through rotten planking and I grabbed for the frame like an idiot. A oilskin packet, army surplus, the kind river men use to keep logbooks dry. It had been pushed deep and covered fast. Whoever hid it had maybe thirty seconds to do it.

"Elias."

He came. We knelt over it together like mourners, which is more or less what we were. The ties were stiff. My fingers wouldn't work right, so his did, and inside the oilskin was a notebook — cheap, water-spotted at the corners, the handwriting of a man who wrote fast because he'd been taught that field notes wait for no one.

The first pages were boring, and I have never been so grateful for boredom. Depth soundings. Bearing notes. Complaints about the rice rations. A sketch of a kingfisher with the caption *this bastard stole my biscuit.* A person lived in these pages. His name was T. Okafor, according to the inside cover, and he drew little stars next to days when the survey went well.

Then the entries changed.

*Crane's men arrived Tuesday. Six of them, armed, said they're "security for asset recovery." Crane himself on the radio, wanted the survey boxes sealed and loaded. Prof asked recovery of WHAT. No answer.*

*They took the deed research today. All of it. Prof argued. They put him in a boat.*

*Radio's gone. I smashed it myself before they could take it — better dead than theirs. Mireille says head downstream, flag a trader, get word to Voss. If you're reading this, Voss: they know you stayed behind at the junction. Don't come up the main channel. They'll be watching it.*

My mouth had gone dry somewhere around *asset recovery.*

"They knew you," I said. "Your team. They were trying to reach you."

"I stayed at the junction to arrange resupply." His voice had gone flat, the way voices go when a man is holding something down that wants very much to come up. "They left without me. I thought — a scheduling error. A miscommunication." He laughed, one note, no humor in it anywhere. "Three weeks ago."

I turned pages. The last entry was different — bigger writing, written fast, written in the dark probably, by the light of a fire he was feeding maps into.

*If anyone finds this: the expedition found what Crane sent us to find. The old Hartwell grant. Prof said it proves the riverlands were deeded fair and square, decades back, and Crane's company's claim is built on forged surveys. Crane doesn't want the deed. Crane wants it GONE. He's taking Prof and the records downriver. Mireille and I are going upcountry to the high ground — the old survey station. If we make it, we'll hold there. The deed matters. Whatever Crane told you it was worth, it's worth more than money, and he knows it.*

*— T.O.*

I read it twice. Three times. The word *Hartwell* sat on the page looking at me, and I looked at the trees, and the trees looked back, and somewhere in my chest a door I'd kept locked for twenty years rattled once in its frame.

"Yara?" Elias said. "You've gone white."

"It's the light." It was not the light. "Deed to what, exactly? Did he say?"

"The Hartwell grant. The Kessari riverlands — all of this." Elias gestured at the river, the floodplain, the drowned green world, with a sweep of his arm that took in several million dollars and, apparently, my entire childhood. "There's a family name attached to the original title. I only saw fragments of the correspondence before the team moved upstream. Why?"

Because my father used to trace that same sweep of arm over kitchen tables in dockside bars, telling me stories about land we supposedly had, land we'd supposedly lost, while other men bought him drinks and laughed at him behind their hands. Because the last time I heard the word *Hartwell* spoken aloud in connection with property, it was spat, at a funeral, by a creditor. Because I had built a life on the principle that names are anchors and anchors drown you, and here was a dead man's notebook insisting my name was worth more than money.

"Family legend," I said. "Bad one."

He studied me with those cartographer's eyes, the ones that measure everything and commit nothing to the final draft. I could see him deciding not to push. It cost him something; I was starting to learn his costs.

"So," he said instead. "Okafor and Mireille went upcountry. To the high ground. The old station." He tapped the notebook. "That's two days' climb from here, if the trails aren't flooded. And Crane took Professor Anselm and the records *downriver* — which means downriver is where Crane is, and upriver is where the people who might still be alive went."

"And the monsoon's four days out at most," I said. "Barometer's been sulking since yesterday."

"Yes."

"And the main channel's watched."

"According to a man who may be dead, yes."

I looked at the Wren, riding easy at her mooring, my rot-hulled, debt-carrying, beautiful boat. Every sensible circuit in my body was screaming the same word: *downstream.* Supplies delivered, contract technically fulfilled, turn tail, outrun the storm, let the army or the insurance men or God sort out the rest. Sensible had kept me alive for twenty years. Sensible had a perfect record.

Sensible had also never once made me stay anywhere.

"He said don't come up the main channel," I said. "Fine. There's a side slough two bends north — my father fished it, before it silted. Half a meter of water in the dry season, maybe less now. Too shallow for anything Crane floats, too twisty for anyone who doesn't already know it." I heard myself say it and marveled a little, the way you marvel when someone else uses your mouth. "We go up."

Elias didn't thank me. He just nodded, once, and started rolling the oilskin closed with the care of a man handling evidence, which is exactly what it was. But as he tucked it inside his coat, he said, almost to himself:

"T. Okafor drew a star on his good days."

"Yeah?"

"Today's a bad day." He looked up at me. "But somebody's waiting on the high ground for a boat that isn't coming. So it might as well be this one."

I cast off the mooring line. The current took the bow like it had been waiting for us too, and I told myself the shiver down my arms was just the river air.

It was a lie, but it was a working one, and I've run farther on less.

Behind us the silent camp folded back into the green, and ahead the channel narrowed toward country no map admitted to, carrying two people who both traveled to avoid being found straight toward the one thing I had spent twenty years making sure could never find me.

My father's name, sitting in a stranger's notebook, asking to be said out loud.
