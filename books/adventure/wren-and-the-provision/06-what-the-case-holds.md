## 6. What the Case Holds

I have been asked, in the course of my professional life, to lie about many things. The elevation of a ridge. The depth of a ford. Whether I had seen anyone else's survey marks on land that was supposed to be empty. Lying is part of cartography the way mud is part of rivers—unpleasant, ubiquitous, and best dealt with quickly before it hardens into something you can't scrape off.

But I had never been very good at lying to Yara Hartwell.

She stood in the doorway of the Wren's cabin with her arms crossed and the rain coming down behind her like a curtain drawn across the world, and she said, "Open it."

"Yara—"

"You've had that case chained to your wrist for six days. You sleep with it. You eat with one hand so the other can rest on it. When we hit that snag off Second Bend you grabbed *it* before you grabbed the rail." She stepped inside, dripping, and shut the door against the storm. "So either it's full of gold, or it's full of something worse. Open it."

"It's survey documents," I said. Which was true, and also the most cowardly sentence I have ever spoken, because it told her nothing while pretending to tell her everything.

"Whose?"

I looked at the case instead of at her. Brass corners, leather gone dark with river damp, the lock worn shiny from years of thumbs. My father's case, originally—he'd carried it through two wars and one divorce—and then mine, because in our trade you inherit your instruments whether you want them or not.

"Elias." Her voice changed. Not softer, exactly. More careful, the way you speak near a wounded animal or a loaded winch. "We're three days from those coordinates. Three days from whatever happened at that camp. If there's something in that box that's going to bite us when we get there, I need to know now, not with my teeth already in it."

The rain hammered the cabin roof. Somewhere below us, the hull planking Elias-that-is-to-say-I had paid to replace was doing its work quietly, holding back the Sable, and I thought about how strange it was that a man could buy timber with money he didn't have and still not buy himself the courage to open a box.

"I'm going to tell you something," I said, "and then I'm going to show you something, and after that you're going to be angry with me, and I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide what to do about me."

Her eyes narrowed. "That's a lot of preamble for a man with nothing to hide."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

* * *

Here is what I should have done, and did not do: I should have told her on the dock at Mile Nine, the first hour we met, when she asked me why a surveyor would beg passage upriver alone and I gave her half an answer wrapped in a joke. Half answers are how cowards travel light.

Instead I will give you the whole thing now, as I gave it to her that night, standing in a rocking cabin while the monsoon rehearsed its opening act overhead.

The expedition she was resupplying—the one whose silence had turned her triple-pay contract into something closer to a rescue mission—had not been hired by Crane Consolidated to map the Kessari Basin. Mapping was the cover story. I knew this because I had been part of the mapping team, which is to say part of the cover story, which is to say, for six weeks, part of the lie.

Crane Consolidated wanted something in the basin. Something paper. And Odell Crane does not send armed men and sealed ledgers after paper unless the paper is worth more than the men.

"The camp went quiet," Yara said. "You were left behind. That's the story you told me."

"That's the true part of the story," I said. "The boats left at dawn on the ninth day. I was out running a traverse line up the tributary—they didn't wait. Or they weren't meant to wait. I've spent a lot of hours deciding which."

"And the rest of it? The part you've been sitting on since Dock Nine?"

I unlatched the case.

I'll tell you what was inside, because I looked at it every day for eleven days and never once opened it, and I think that tells you something about me that I don't love. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the documents. Originals, some of them, browned at the edges, stamped by survey offices that hadn't existed in forty years. Deeds. Grants. A chain of title running back through three colonial administrations like a rope through pulleys, each one hauling the same parcel of land a little further from the people who lived on it and a little closer to whoever held the end of the line.

And at the end of the line—at the bottom of the pile, where a thing like that always ends up—was the grant itself. The original. The one everything else pointed toward.

A conveyance of some four hundred square miles of the upper Kessari Basin. Riverlands. Wetlands. The flood forests and the high ground and every village channel between.

Deeded legally, irrevocably, and paid for in full—

—to a family named Hartwell.

* * *

I want to describe her face to you, and I find I can't do it honestly, because I was looking at the document and not at her, the way a man looks anywhere except at the thing he has just broken.

What I remember is the sound. The rain, steady. The engine, ticking as it cooled. And then nothing from her at all—not a word, not a breath—which is somehow louder than either.

"Hartwell," she said finally. Flat. Testing the weight of it.

"It's dated forty-one years ago," I said. "Conveyed to Tomas Hartwell. There are tax stamps. There's a registration mark from the provincial office at Corren. It's—" I stopped myself from saying *it's legitimate*, because she could see that it was legitimate; she was holding the legitimate thing in her hands. "Yara. Is that your father?"

She set the deed down on the chart table with a care that frightened me more than throwing it would have. She smoothed one corner flat. Her finger traced the name without touching the ink.

"Tomas Hartwell," she said. "Yes. That's my father."

The relief I felt lasted approximately one and a half seconds, which is exactly long enough to learn that relief is not the right emotion.

"He stole it," she said. Still flat. Still reading. "That's the story. That's the whole story of my life, actually, if you're wondering. He stole land from the basin communities, forged the papers, got caught, ran, and died disgraced in a town that wouldn't bury him in the churchyard. I grew up on that story. I built a entire way of living on top of it. Never stay. Never use the name. Never let anyone look too closely at you, because names get searched and pasts get found." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry in a way that made my chest hurt. "And you've known this. Since Dock Nine. You've been sitting in my boat for six days knowing this, watching me feed you, letting me teach you the channel markers, letting me—"

"Yara."

"—letting me *trust* you." She pushed away from the table. "Why? Why did Crane send you? What am I carrying up this river?"

"Nothing," I said. "You're not carrying anything for him. I swear that. I ran because—" And here is where my prepared speech, the one I'd been drafting in my head for eleven days, the eloquent one with the structure and the justification, simply left me. What came out instead was the truth, unarranged. "Because they were going to burn them. All of it. The night before the boats left, Crane's men brought kerosene cans into the records tent. I heard two of them arguing about whether to do it at camp or downstream where nobody could see the smoke. And I took the originals out of the strongbox and put them in my case and walked into the trees, and when I came back the camp was being struck and the radio was smashed and everyone was gone, and I have been running upriver ever since trying to decide who I could possibly give these to who wouldn't just get killed for holding them."

Silence. The rain filled it.

"So Crane wants them destroyed," she said slowly. "The deed to my family's land. He sent an expedition to find it and destroy it."

"No." I shook my head. "He sent an expedition to *find* it. Destroying it was the contingency. The deed is a problem for him either way—if it surfaces, his claims on the basin collapse, all the leases and options he's bought from the government over twenty years, everything built on the assumption that the Hartwell title was dead. But if it could be found and *quietly acquired*—a desperate heir, say, someone with no lawyers and no name and no idea what it's worth—"

"Someone like me," she said.

"Someone exactly like you. Yara, I think that contract of yours wasn't chance. I think Crane knows the Hartwell heir exists. I think he knows she runs a boat on the Sable under a different name. And I think he arranged for that supply run to go upriver because he wanted you here, close to the search, close to his people, easy to find once the deed surfaced."

She stared at me. And I watched her do the arithmetic—I watched her count the coincidences, the timing, the generous pay, the way the contract had appeared out of nowhere three weeks after a man named Ferris had come asking questions at her dock—and I saw the moment the sum came out wrong, and her face closed like a door.

"You should have told me at Dock Nine," she said.

"Yes."

"You should have told me at First Bend, when I fed you."

"Yes."

"You should have told me before I let you buy my hull planking, because now I owe you, and I hate owing anything to a man who's been lying to me since the minute we met."

"Yara—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and it was shaking, and she looked at it like it belonged to someone else. "Don't talk for a minute. Please."

I didn't. I stood in the rocking cabin and watched her stand over the deed to four hundred square miles of riverland, and I understood that I had made a terrible miscalculation somewhere back along the line. I had thought I was protecting her. I had thought the information was dangerous and the delay was kindness. But kindness delayed curdles. It becomes something else entirely, something with a smell to it, and she had smelled it for days without being able to name it, and now she could, and the name was betrayal.

"My father," she said at last, very quietly, "taught me this river. Every bend, every sandbar, every place the water lies about its depth. He taught me to read it the way other fathers teach their children prayers." Her voice caught and steadied. "And then he ran. And I spent thirty years being ashamed of a man I loved, because everyone said he was a thief, and thieves don't deserve to be loved, so I loved him in secret and hated him in public and never once asked him—never once sat him down and said, Papa, tell me it isn't true. Because I was afraid of the answer."

"Yara—"

"And now you're telling me the answer was in a box the whole time." She picked up the deed. Held it at arm's length like something that might be hot. "Paid in full. Registered. Legal." A sound came out of her that was almost a laugh and absolutely wasn't. "Do you know what I'm going to do now, Elias?"

"Tell me."

"I'm going to walk out into the rain," she said, "because if I stay in this cabin one more minute I am going to say things to you that I can't take back, and I might still need you to pilot me out of this basin alive."

She tucked the deed into her coat, against her chest, where a person carries the things they haven't decided about yet. Then she opened the cabin door and stepped out into the deluge, and the door slammed behind her, and I was alone with the case and the charts and the sound of a woman crying somewhere out on deck where she thought the rain would hide it.

* * *

I gave her ten minutes. It felt like ten years; I imagine it felt like ten minutes to her, which is the arithmetic of guilt in miniature.

When I finally went out, she was at the bow rail with her back to me, soaked through, hair plastered flat, staring upriver into the dark where the Kessari waited with its silent camp and its drowned secrets. I stopped a respectful distance away and said nothing, because I have learned that the second most useful thing a man can offer a woman in grief is his silence.

The first most useful thing, apparently, is a box of documents he should have opened six days ago.

"There's a village," she said, without turning around. "Two days up. Flooded forest country. My father used to point at it on the charts and go quiet. He'd touch the spot with one finger, like it was glass." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, which was pointless in the rain, and did it anyway. "If this deed is real—if the title really runs through there—then someone in that village might remember him. Might remember what actually happened."

"Is that where we're going?"

"We're going to the coordinates first," she said. "My contract says resupply, and even if the camp is empty, Nkechi Ade's team might not be. People come before paper. That's the one lesson of my life I got right." She turned then, and looked at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "But after that, Voss, you and I are going to have a longer conversation about what happens to men who carry other people's lives around in locked boxes and call it protection."

"I know," I said. "I'm sorry."

"Not yet, you're not," she said. "But you will be. Now get below and check that bilge. Storm's coming, and I need this boat floating, because I have a feeling—" she looked downriver, back the way we'd come, toward the low gray horizon where a certain armored launch might already be loading fuel, "—that before this is over, we're going to need every plank we've got."

She went aft to the wheel. I went below to the bilge.

And in the lamplight, packing the case with hands that weren't quite steady, I noticed something I had missed in eleven days of not looking: a slip of paper folded inside the oilcloth, tucked against the deed's backing, in handwriting I didn't recognize—a woman's hand, quick and old, ink faded to the color of weak tea.

One line, written decades ago and hidden all this time an inch from my own fingers:

*If you are reading this, the river kept its promise. Find Mama Sena.*
