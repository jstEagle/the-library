## 7. Father's River

I didn't sleep that night.

Not because of the rain—rain on a cabin roof is the oldest lullaby I know—but because Elias's case sat open on his chart table like a confession neither of us had finished making. The deed was still there. Hartwell, in faded ink, in my father's riverlands, in a legal language I could almost hear him reading aloud over his spectacles while I pretended to sleep in the berth below.

I lay in my bunk and watched the rain crawl down the porthole glass, and the past came for me the way it always does: not all at once, but mile by mile.

* * *

The first memory is water. It is always water.

I was six, maybe seven, standing on the foredeck of a boat called the *Meridian*—my father's boat, before it was anyone else's problem—and he had his hands cupped around mine on the wheel.

"Feel that?" he said. "She talks to you through the wood. Most people never listen."

"She's saying what?"

He laughed, a big rolling sound that came up from somewhere near his boots. "She's saying the current's lazy today. She's saying there's a sandbar two bends down that wasn't there last week. She's saying your old man owes her an oil change."

"Boats don't talk."

"No," he agreed, steering us around a floating log with a nudge so gentle I didn't even feel it. "But rivers do. And a boat is just how you shake hands with one."

That was Tam Hartwell all over. He talked to rivers the way other men talked to God—familiarly, without apology, certain he'd be answered. He knew the Sable from its headwaters to the delta mud, every channel, every deadfall, every village dock where the dogs knew his whistle. When we came into a landing, people came down to meet us. Not to trade, at first. Just to see him. To hear what the river was doing upcountry, where the fish were running, whether the rains would be kind.

I thought everyone's father was like that. I thought the whole world was a man standing at a wheel with his daughter's hands under his, telling her the water was talking.

It took me twenty years to learn how rare that was. And by then, they'd taken his name off everything but me.

* * *

He taught me the river the way you teach a child anything worth knowing: by making it a game and never admitting it was a lesson.

"Count the sandbars," he'd say, when I got restless in the long green afternoons between landings. "Every one you spot right, you get a story tonight."

So I counted. Sandbars like birthdays, like coins in a jar. And the stories—he had hundreds. Where the river changed course in the flood of '41 and drowned a whole orchard that still gave up apples after storms. Where the ferryman Oduya ran his boat aground on purpose to escape soldiers, and the soldiers' heavier boat followed him straight onto the same bar, because pride doesn't read water. Where the herons nested so thick on Heron Key that the trees bent white.

I thought the stories were entertainment. They were maps. Every one of them was a map—of channels, of hazards, of the river's long slow temper. My father was teaching me to read a living document that rewrote itself every season, and he did it so well that I didn't know I was being educated until I was already fluent.

That's why I count sandbars now. People think it's a quirk. A captain's superstition. It isn't. It's the only inheritance I ever admitted to having.

* * *

I was fourteen when the word "thief" entered our house, and it entered the way floodwater enters a boat—not through a hole you can point to, but everywhere at once, rising fast.

There were men at the dock at Corrin's Landing. Not traders. Traders smell like cargo and bad coffee. These men smelled like paper and money, and they had documents, and they wanted to talk to Tam Hartwell about the Kessari lands.

I remember because it was the first time I ever saw my father go quiet. He stood on his own deck with these men's papers in his hands, and his face did something I had no name for yet. Later I learned the name: a man reading his own death warrant and trying to look bored.

They said he'd stolen the deed. That the riverlands—the basin villages, the floodplains, the whole wet green heart of the Kessari—had been granted or sold or swindled, depending on who told it, and that Tam Hartwell held papers claiming them, and that no honest man could hold such papers.

"But I bought it," I heard him say once, through the cabin door, low and tired. "Fair purchase. Signed witnesses. You can check—"

"We intend to," one of them said, and the way he said it made my skin crawl even at fourteen, even through a door.

After that, the stories about us started. You couldn't call them rumors exactly; rumors are soft-edged. These were engineered. Tam Hartwell stole the deed. Tam Hartwell cheated widows. Tam Hartwell's papers were forged, or if they weren't forged, they were bought with blood money, and either way the man was a thief wearing a pilot's cap.

What struck me, even then, was how fast people believed it. People who'd known him fifteen years. People whose children he'd ferried to school. Belief traveled upriver faster than any boat I've ever run.

My father never fought it the way you'd expect. He didn't shout. He didn't hire lawyers or stand on docks denouncing his accusers. He went quiet, and quiet, on a river, reads as guilt. I begged him to fight. I remember that—I remember being fifteen and furious and saying, "Tell them the truth! Tell them!"

And he looked at me with that tired face and said, "Yara. Some truths cost more than lies. This one, right now, costs more than we have."

I thought he meant he was guilty. That's the cruelty of it. He was trying to protect something, and I read protection as confession, and I carried that reading for twenty years like ballast I couldn't jettison.

* * *

We ran. That's the part I've told myself a hundred versions of.

Officially: we moved downriver for work. Unofficially, in the way a child understands things without being told: we were fleeing. The accusations had grown teeth—there was talk of magistrates, of seizure, of men who visited other men's boats at night. My father sold the *Meridian* at a loss that made him flinch, and we went south, and he took work piloting freight he was overqualified for, and he drank more than he used to, and he stopped talking to rivers.

Or maybe they stopped answering. I was too young and too angry to tell the difference.

The worst night was Port Sable, three years on. He'd been drinking, and someone at the bar—a barge hand, nobody, a man who'd never seen the Kessari in his life—made a joke about thieves and deeds, and my father broke a bottle against the rail and said, "You wouldn't know an honest deed if it floated up and bit you," and the whole room laughed, and something in him finished dying right there among the laughter.

He died two years later. Liver, officially. I've always thought it was simpler than that: a man can only be called a thief so many times before he starts carrying the weight as fact, and facts sink.

At the funeral there were four people. Four. For the man half the basin had whistled down their docks. I stood there at nineteen with river mud on my boots and a head full of his stories, and I made myself a promise that felt like wisdom and was actually just fear:

Never stay anywhere long enough to be known. Never let anyone hand you a name. Keep moving. Miles, not years. If you measure your life in miles, nobody can measure it in shame.

I've kept that promise for twenty years. It has cost me everything a promise can cost, and I called the interest loneliness and paid it monthly without complaint.

* * *

Now there was a deed in a case on my chart table with my name's bones written across it, and the arithmetic of my whole life had stopped balancing.

Because here's what I kept turning over, there in the dark with the rain ticking on the roof: my father said he *bought* it. Fair purchase, signed witnesses. I heard him say it through a door when I was fourteen, and I filed it under the excuses guilty men make, because that's what everyone around me taught me to do.

But Elias Voss—a man who measures truth in coordinates, who has no reason on this earth to lie to me—had survey documents proving the Hartwell claim was real. Legal. Old. Documented.

And if the claim was real, then my father hadn't stolen anything.

Then why did he run?

I turned that question over and over, the way you worry a knot in a line. Guilty men run. But there's another kind of man who runs, and I'd never once considered him, because considering him would have meant reconsidering everything: the man who runs not from what he did, but from what others will do to get what he has.

A thief runs from the law. But a man holding something valuable—something men with paper-smelling hands and heavy boats want very badly—might run too. Might run for years. Might let himself be called a thief rather than tell a soul what he actually held, because the moment he told, every shark in the watershed would come upriver.

Might even let his own daughter believe the worst of him. Because a daughter who believes her father was a thief keeps her head down and stays small and survives. A daughter who knows her father was hiding a fortune gets asked questions by men who break bottles and worse.

Some truths cost more than lies. This one, right now, costs more than we have.

Oh, Papa. I pressed my forehead against the cool porthole glass. Was that it? Did you carry the thief's name so I wouldn't have to carry the target? Did you die disgraced on purpose, so your disgrace could be a wall around whatever you were protecting?

It sounded like madness in the dark. It also sounded exactly like him. The man who steered around logs with a nudge so gentle you never felt it. The man who hid lessons inside games. Of course he'd hide a rescue inside a ruin. Of course he'd let the whole river believe the lie, if the lie kept the wolves looking at a thief instead of a treasure.

And I had spent twenty years honoring his sacrifice by believing it was a crime.

I laughed, there alone in the dark, and it came out wrong, halfway to the other thing. Twenty years of miles. Twenty years of never staying, never belonging, never letting a dock put my name on it—all of it built on a foundation I'd never once examined, because examining it hurt, and I was very good at not hurting. I'd inherited his shame like a birthright and never asked for the bill of sale.

The rain thickened. Somewhere forward, Elias turned over in his bunk, and the deck creaked, and the Wren talked to me through the wood the way my father promised boats do. She was saying the anchor was holding. She was saying the rain would run till morning. She was saying, in her patient wooden way, that the current always tells you where it's going if you shut up and listen.

All those years, I thought I was running from my father's crime.

Maybe I'd been running from his gift.

* * *

I came out of the bunk at first light, hollowed out and steady, the way you feel after a fever breaks. Elias was already up, boiling water on the spirit stove, and he looked at me with that careful cartographer's expression—measuring, recording, deciding how much of me had changed overnight.

"You walked into the rain yesterday," he said.

"I did."

"You want to talk about it?"

I poured myself coffee and sat across from him, and the case sat between us, open, the deed catching the gray morning light.

"I want to ask you something instead," I said. "Your surveys. The provenance chain on that deed. How far back does it go—and does it say anywhere, in any of it, *how* the Hartwells acquired the land?"

He blinked. "Purchase. 1938. There's a bill of sale, witness signatures, registration with the provincial recorder. It's clean, Yara. That's what I kept trying to tell you—it's one of the cleanest title chains I've ever traced. Which is exactly why Crane wants it gone. A dirty deed you can dispute. A clean one you have to honor."

Clean. Fair purchase. Signed witnesses. Everything my father whispered through a door while men with paper hands circled his boat.

I wrapped both palms around my mug and looked out at the river, high and brown and rising, and felt twenty years of ballast shift in my hull.

"Elias," I said, "when this monsoon passes—when we find whoever's left of that expedition—we're not going downstream. We're going deeper. I need to see the land on that deed. All of it."

"Why?"

"Because my father died a thief," I said, "and I'm starting to suspect he was murdered by a rumor instead. And somebody upriver knows which."

Elias set down his mug slowly. "There's something else," he said. "Something I found in the provenance file and didn't show you, because yesterday you weren't ready. The bill of sale—the original—it's not in any archive. The recorder's office lists it, but the document itself was never deposited."

"So it's lost."

"It's not lost." He met my eyes. "The expedition's field notes referenced a private archive. A family repository, somewhere in the upper basin, tied to the original grant. Your father didn't just hide the deed, Yara. He hid the proof. And the survey team was following his trail when they went silent."

Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like a door slamming, and the barometer needle I'd been ignoring all morning gave a small, sick twitch toward the left.

The monsoon was coming early.
