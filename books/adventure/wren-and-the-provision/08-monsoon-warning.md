## 8. Monsoon Warning

The barometer on the Wren's bulkhead had been my father's, which meant it was older than me and twice as opinionated. It was a brass thing with a face like a disappointed schoolmaster, and for eleven years it had told me the truth about weather the way most people tell the truth about themselves—reluctantly, and only when it was too late to do anything about it.

That morning it read twenty-eight point nine and falling.

I tapped the glass. It didn't change its mind. Barometers never do; that's what I like about them.

"Bad?" Elias asked from the chart table, where he'd been hunched since dawn like a heron over a fishpond, inking in soundings from yesterday's run. He'd gotten ink on his cheek again. He always got ink on his cheek. The man could survey a river delta to within a hand's width of accuracy but couldn't keep a pen off his own face.

"Bad," I said. "The question is how bad, and whether we're smart enough to care."

"We're not," he said. "You especially. I've been keeping notes."

* * *

Here's the arithmetic of a monsoon in the Kessari Basin, for those lucky enough never to have needed it. When the big rain comes—and it comes all at once, not gradually, like the sky simply gives up pretending—the river doesn't rise. It *arrives*. Water comes down out of the highlands in a brown wall, and every creek and slough and nameless ditch for fifty miles dumps into the main channel at once, and the Sable goes from a river to something with opinions about geography. Boats that don't get behind good levees or up high creeks get wrapped around trees, or worse, get *kept*—lifted by the flood and set down somewhere unkind when it recedes, like a cat depositing a dead bird on a doorstep.

You have maybe six hours of warning once the barometer commits. Sometimes less.

We were at the mouth of the Kessari proper, forty miles below the silent camp, anchored in a side channel with good holding and tall banks. Downstream lay three days of open water and, if we ran hard, safety: the lower basin villages, the trading posts, dry bunks and hot food and no decisions harder than what to eat.

Upstream lay the camp. And whatever had happened there. And Nkechi Ade's note, which I'd read so many times I could recite it in my sleep:

*Crane took the boats. Said the deed burns with us if we talk. Went downstream ahead of the water. If anyone reads this — some of us went north on foot. Find us before the river does.*

North on foot. Into the flooded forest country, where the ground rose in long fingers toward the old survey hills. Where a party of exhausted people without boats would be racing a flood they couldn't see coming.

* * *

Elias laid his charts out on the table and weighted the corners with coffee mugs, a tin of pencils, and my spare knife, because he had a genius for using other people's belongings as office equipment.

"All right," he said. "Let's be adults about this."

"I hate when you say that. It's usually followed by an idea."

"The adult decision is downstream. We run now, we beat the worst of it, we raise the alarm at the lower villages, and somebody with more resources than a rotten riverboat goes looking for Ade's people."

"That's the adult decision."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this because?"

He looked at me over the charts. "Because I know you're going to say no, and I wanted it on the record that I tried." He tapped the note. "Six or seven people, on foot, in hill country, with a monsoon coming. If we go up, we might find them. If we stay here, we'll certainly survive. Those are the options."

"You forgot one."

"Which is?"

"We go up, and we don't find them, and the flood catches the Wren in the narrows, and we die stupidly in a place neither of us is from." I said it lightly, the way you do, because saying it heavy makes it true. "I'm just being thorough."

Elias was quiet a moment. Then: "Why did you take this contract, Yara?"

The question came sideways, the way his best ones did. Not *why are we going back up*, but the older, uglier question underneath it.

"Triple pay," I said.

"No. Triple pay explains taking the run. It doesn't explain why you stood on that dock at Dock Nine looking like a woman reading her own arrest warrant when I mentioned the expedition. It doesn't explain why you knew the bend at Kilometer One-Forty before I showed you the chart. It doesn't explain—" he paused, choosing, "—why you flinched at the name Hartwell on my survey documents and then walked out into the rain rather than tell me I was wrong."

"I walked out because it was raining and I felt like getting wet."

"It wasn't raining yet."

"It was spiritually raining."

He almost smiled. Then didn't. "I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm asking you to notice that whatever it is you're carrying, it points upstream. Every instinct you've got is pointed upstream. So let's stop pretending the choice is between safety and loyalty. For you, it isn't. You just haven't admitted it yet."

I looked at the barometer. Twenty-eight point eight now. Even the instruments were ganging up on me.

* * *

Here's a thing about running from your past: it works fine until you meet someone who reads maps for a living. Maps are just pasts drawn in ink. Elias had spent two weeks charting a river and had accidentally charted me—every bend where I'd gone quiet, every reach where I'd named things before he could, every mile where my hands had known the channel better than any pilot's should. He hadn't said it outright. He didn't need to. Cartographers don't accuse. They just draw the evidence and wait for you to walk into the frame.

My father taught me the Kessari when I was small enough that the wheel stood higher than my head. He'd stand behind me with his hands loose at his sides—not touching, never touching, *a captain's hands stay off the wheel unless the river asks*—and call out the water like scripture. *Sandbar walking left after the rains, sweetling. Snag on the starboard bank, half-sunk, she'll roll in high water. Here the channel hugs the far cut, trust the color, not your eyes.* He knew that river the way other men knew their own handwriting. And then one day men came with papers and torches and words like *thief* and *stolen land*, and my father put us on the Wren in the dark and we ran downstream, and he never once looked back upriver, and I learned from watching him that the way you survive shame is to measure everything in miles and never once in years.

Except now there was a deed with our name on it sitting in a sealed case in my cabin, and a dead man's note naming a financier, and a river full of people who might have been robbed by my father or protected by him, and I would not know which until I went where the answers were.

Loyalty versus self-preservation, Elias had called it. He was wrong, and I loved him a little for being wrong so kindly. It wasn't loyalty to them. It was loyalty to the girl my father had made, the one who knew that river by heart, the one who deserved to know what her inheritance actually was before the water took the question away forever.

* * *

"Get the anchor up," I said.

Elias blinked. "Just like that?"

"No, not just like that. First I'm going to stand here and look conflicted for a respectable interval, because I have a reputation." I held it for maybe four seconds. "Now get the anchor up."

He moved. That was the thing about Elias Voss that I was only beginning to admit I liked: he argued like a lawyer and obeyed like crew. He was up on the bow coiling line before I'd finished being difficult, and I brought the engine up from idle and felt the old girl answer—two thousand RPMs of stubbornness wrapped in rotting planking, God love her.

We came out of the side channel into the main stem of the Kessari, and the river told us immediately what kind of day it intended to be. The current had already picked up—a foot an hour, maybe more—and the water had gone the color of strong tea with milk in it, which meant the highlands were already bleeding. Driftwood ran past us in ragged processions, whole trees sailing downstream with their roots waving like drowning men. Somewhere upstream, the sky was emptying itself.

I put the nose into it and opened the throttle.

* * *

We ran four hours against the rising current, and I will not pretend it was anything but work. The Kessari in flood mood is a different animal from the Kessari at rest. Eddies form where none were charted; snags that sat peaceably on the bottom all season come alive and travel. Twice I took us through cuts I'd normally refuse, threading boils and whirlpools with my stomach somewhere around my tonsils, and both times Elias stood at my shoulder without being asked, calling out debris on the blind side, and both times I let him, which for me constitutes a marriage proposal.

Somewhere past Kilometer One-Ten, the rain found us.

Not the polite rain of the coast. This was the monsoon's advance guard—fat, warm drops the size of grapes, hitting the deck like thrown fruit, then thickening into a curtain so dense the far bank vanished. Visibility dropped to a boat-length. The thunder didn't crack so much as *roll*, continuous, like boulders being dragged across the sky's floor.

"Yara!" Elias shouted over it. He had to shout directly into my ear to be heard. "The barometer!"

I'd seen it. Twenty-eight point five and falling fast. The sky had stopped giving warning and started delivering product.

"There's a bluff cut two miles up!" I shouted back. "Left bank! We can tuck in and wait out the worst!"

"And Ade's people?"

I gritted my teeth. Two miles of shelter, sitting there like a cold drink on a hot day. And beyond it, thirty miles of narrowing channel, rising water, and people on foot who had maybe a night left before the low ground drowned.

"Keep the lead line ready!" I said, which was not an answer, and he knew it wasn't an answer, and he nodded anyway and got the lead line ready.

* * *

We passed the bluff cut doing eight knots. I want that recorded. There was shelter, real shelter, good holding, high banks, the whole litany of survival, and I drove the Wren past it like a woman walking past a bakery on a fast day, and Elias watched me do it and said nothing, and the nothing was the loudest thing aboard.

Because here's what I'd worked out in the noise and the rain, working the wheel, working the math: Ade's note said *north on foot*. North from the camp lay the old survey hills—the high fingers of ground my father had used as stations, back when he'd surveyed these waters himself, back before the word *thief*. If the expedition people knew the country—and they'd been camped under it for weeks—they'd climb for the same high ground any sensible animal would. Which meant they weren't lost. They were pinned. High and dry and surrounded by a flood that would keep rising for two days, with no boats, no radio, and no way down.

Nobody was going to rescue them from the lower villages. Nobody downstream even knew they existed except us, a cartographer with ink on his face, and a riverboat held together with prayer and Elias's replacement timber.

If we turned around now, they wouldn't drown because the river was cruel. They'd drown because I'd been careful.

I have been many bad things in my life—liar, runner, coward on at least two occasions I could name and several I couldn't—but I had never once been careful at someone else's expense, and I found I couldn't start today.

* * *

The narrows began at Kilometer One-Sixty, and by then the river had stopped being a river and become an argument.

Water stood in the trees on both banks—two feet of it, then three, the forest turned to a drowned congregation with its arms up. The channel markers I knew were gone under. I steered by memory and by the color of the water, the way my father taught me: brown over brown means deep, brown over green means trouble, and if you can see the bottom at all, you've already made your last mistake.

A log the size of a church pew came at us out of the murk, riding high and fast. I threw the wheel over; the Wren leaned, groaned, and slipped past it close enough to leave green slime on the hull. Behind us, Elias whooped—actually whooped, this grave man of maps—and I laughed, high and unhinged, the laugh you laugh when fear and joy get drunk together.

And then the engine stumbled.

Once. Twice. A third time, longer, the propeller biting nothing, the stern going light.

I knew before I looked. I knew from the sound, from the feel in the wheel, from eleven years of knowing that boat the way you know your own pulse. But I looked anyway, aft, through the rain, at the water coming over the transom well—

—and saw it sheeting up through the deck boards amidships, silver threads of it, too much, too fast, pumping up through a seam that the storm strain had opened like a wound.

"Elias!" My voice came out flat and calm, which scared me more than screaming would have. "Get the pump. Now."

He was already moving. Of course he was already moving.

Ahead, through the rain, the dark shoulders of the survey hills rose out of the flood—my father's hills, high ground, sanctuary, thirty miles gone and closing. Between us and them lay a drowning boat, a rising river, and whatever else the monsoon had decided to send.

The barometer read twenty-eight point two.

"Hold together, old girl," I whispered to the Wren, to the river, to nobody. "Hold together."

She answered with a shudder through the hull that felt, unmistakably, like a cough.
