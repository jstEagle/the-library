## 9. The Leaking Hull

I have kept journals since I was eleven years old. My first entry, in a schoolboy's hand, recorded the exact number of steps between our house and the well (forty-one) because I believed a map of anything was a way of owning it safely. Forty years later I still believe that, though I've learned that ownership is a rumor and safety is a place you visit.

Here is what no map can hold: the sound a boat makes when it decides to die.

It was past midnight on the third day upriver from the silent camp, and the Wren was talking to herself in the dark. Not the ordinary conversation of a working hull—timber ticking against water, the diesel muttering below decks—but something newer. A knock. Then a pause, as if listening to see if anyone had heard. Then the knock again, lower down, more insistent.

Yara heard it before I did. She always heard things first; I'd stopped being surprised by it somewhere around the second bend, when she'd named three sandbars by smell alone. But this time she didn't say anything. She just set down her mug of terrible coffee, tilted her head like a heron considering a fish, and waited.

The knock came again.

"That," she said, "is not good."

* * *

Let me be honest about my qualifications for what followed. I am a cartographer. I have spent my life measuring other people's disasters from a comfortable altitude—contour lines for landslides, floodplain shading for floods, little hatched symbols for "area subject to inundation." I have never once been inside the hatching. The closest I'd come to a nautical emergency before that night was dropping a theodolite into Lake Verany in '09, and I had mourned the theodolite more than most people mourn their dead.

So when Yara lifted the hatch in the main cabin floor and shone a lantern down into the bilge, and the light came back to us doubled—once off the frame timbers and once off the water standing shin-deep where water had no business standing—I did what any trained professional would do.

I said, "How much is too much?"

She gave me a look I would come to know very well over the following weeks: the look of a woman recalculating how much of her life she was willing to explain to a stranger. "Depends," she said. "On whether we're bailing faster than she's taking it." She swung the lantern. "Right now we are not."

The rot was in the port planking, aft of the midships frame—the timber I had bought her at Dock Nine, or rather the timber someone at Dock Nine had sold me while smiling. It hadn't rotted through all at once. It had been giving way in layers, quietly, the whole run upriver, and now the river had found the last layer and introduced itself.

"Get the pump," Yara said. "Aft locker, under the tarp. And Elias—" she caught my sleeve as I turned, and her hand was already slick with bilgewater, "—don't panic. Panic costs oxygen."

"I wasn't going to panic."

"You were doing arithmetic in your head. You get a look. Like a man adding up a bill he can't pay."

I went to find the pump.

* * *

The pump was a hand-cranked bilge pump older than either of us, its handle worn to the shape of every hand that had ever gripped it in exactly this kind of night. Yara fitted it into the deck socket, primed it with two furious strokes, and a gray gout of water arced out over the rail and back into the river it had come from.

"Like that," she said. "All night if we have to. Take the crank when your arms go."

"When my arms go?"

"Everyone's arms go. It's not an insult, it's anatomy."

We fell into it. That's the only word I have for what happened over the next six hours—not teamwork, which sounds organized, and not survival, which sounds dramatic. We fell into a rhythm like two gears that had never met but happened to be cut for each other. She pumped twenty strokes, I pumped twenty. We moved around each other in the narrow cockpit without collision, without instruction, without the small collisions of pride that sink more partnerships than rivers do.

Between turns, she worked. I want to record this properly, because it was the single most impressive thing I saw in all my months on the Sable, and I once watched a man talk a crocodile out of his fish shed. Yara stripped off her boots, took a hammer, a chisel, a wedge of canvas, a bar of tallow soap, and a length of pine board, and went down into the bilge itself—into the black water rising around her thighs—and began fighting the hull from the inside.

"Shouldn't you be outside?" I called down. "Patching from the—"

"The outside is underwater. You patch from the side you can reach." Her voice came up muffled and echoing, a voice in a well. "Canvas and soap. Old trick. The pressure pushes the patch into the leak instead of off it. Assuming the leak cooperates."

"Does it?"

There was a pause, then a bang of the hammer, then a word in a language I didn't recognize that I chose to interpret as no.

* * *

Hour two. The rain arrived without ceremony—one moment dry, the next the sky simply opened, monsoon-style, the way rain falls in the Kessari: not drops but a decision. The river rose to meet it. I could see it happening along the banks, the water climbing the mud like something patient coming home.

"How high does she get?" I shouted over the drumming.

"Flood stage is another meter and a half," Yara called back. "We'll know it when the trees start swimming."

"And if the patch doesn't hold?"

"Then we learn to swim fast." She surfaced from the hatch, hair plastered flat, face streaked with grease, and grinned at me—a real grin, unguarded, the first one I'd seen from her that reached all the way up. "You can swim, Voss? Please tell me the man who maps rivers can swim."

"I can float," I said. "With dignity."

"Dignity sinks last. Pump."

* * *

I want to write down what we talked about, because what we talked about was nothing, and the nothing mattered. Somewhere in hour four, both of us soaked, arms burning, the lantern guttering, she started telling me about a dog she'd owned at seventeen, a yellow mongrel who rode the bow of her first boat and barked at herons with what she described as personal grievance. I told her about my grandmother, who had surveyed half the northern coast on foot with a chain and a compass and a temper, and who had taught me that a measurement taken honestly is a promise kept to whoever comes after you.

Neither of us mentioned Hartwell. Neither of us mentioned the case, or the deed, or the name written in the old survey documents that I had shown her five days ago and that had sent her walking out into the rain without a word. We had not spoken of it since. She had come back wet, made coffee, and said, "We leave at dawn," and that had been the entire negotiation.

But here is the thing about pumping for your shared life until dawn: the truth stops fitting behind its door. Around hour five, hauling the crank while my shoulders screamed, I heard myself say:

"My team didn't leave me by accident."

Yara didn't stop pumping. "No?"

"They were paid to leave me. Crane's people. I was the one who found the original deed reference in the colonial archive—the Hartwell grant. Once I had, I was either a tool or a witness, and tools don't ask questions." I cranked. Water arced out into the dark. "They left me at Dock Nine with my kit and no boat. They assumed the basin would finish the job."

The pump handle kept moving under her hands. When she finally spoke, her voice was level, but I had learned by then to hear the effort in it, the way you hear the current under a smooth stretch of river.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because if this boat goes down tonight, you should know you weren't the only one on it who got lied to. And because—" I stopped cranking. My arms had gone, right on schedule. "Because you walked into the rain when you saw that name, and I've been waiting five days for you to walk back out of it, and I'm tired of pretending I don't know there's a Hartwell standing in front of me pumping bilge."

The rain hammered the deck. The pump knocked. Down in the bilge, the river breathed against the patched planking, patient as ever.

"I'm not claiming anything," she said at last. "Whatever that paper says, whatever my father did or didn't do—it died with him. I'm just a woman with a boat."

"A woman with a boat who's running supplies to a camp that was hunting her own family's deed."

Her jaw worked. For a moment I thought I'd gone too far, that the armor would come down and I'd be looking at the stranger from Dock Nine again, the one who counted sandbars instead of friends. Then she thrust the pump handle at me.

"Your turn," she said. "And Voss? If we live till morning, we're going to have that conversation properly. Over coffee. Real coffee, if Nkechi's camp has any left."

"Nkechi Ade," I said, taking the crank. "The surveyor whose note we found."

"She signed it. She wrote 'tell Ade' on the back of the manifest, which means she expected someone named Ade to come looking, which means she might still be alive to find." Yara pulled the tarp tighter over the forward hatch and looked upriver, into the rain, where the drowned forest was beginning to take shape along the banks like a congregation wading out to meet us. "Which means we're not turning back."

* * *

Hour seven nearly finished us.

The patch held until it didn't. I felt it go—a shudder through the deck plates, a change in the pump's appetite, the water suddenly coming eager instead of grudging. Yara was down the hatch before I'd finished swearing, and for ten minutes there was only the sound of her working in the dark, hammer blows and river-noise and once, terribly, a long silence that I filled by cranking faster than my body had any right to allow.

Then her hand shot up out of the hatchway, palm open.

"Wedge," she said. "Green locker. Now."

I threw it down. Her hand closed on it and vanished. More hammering. Then, faintly, from the bottom of the boat, in a voice scraped raw and almost laughing:

"There. She's holding. Barely. Get ready to hate me—we're beaching her."

"Beaching—"

"High bank, ahead, left side, there's a clay shelf above the flood line. I know it. My father used to winter boats on it." A beat. "Don't say anything."

I said nothing. I cranked. And she brought the Wren in through flooded timber in the black heart of a monsoon night, reading the water by feel and memory, grounding us on that clay shelf with a bump gentle enough to spill only the coffee—which, in fairness, was a mercy.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. We sat on the cabin roof, wrapped in the same blanket because there was only one, watching the river run brown and enormous where the channel used to be. The Wren leaned against her mooring line like a tired animal. Below decks, the patch held. It would need proper timber, proper caulking, a dry dock we couldn't reach—but it would hold.

"Strangers become crew," Yara said suddenly, as if continuing a conversation from hours ago. "That's how my father put it. He said you don't pick crew, you survive with them, and after that they're yours."

"He sounds like he'd have liked you."

"He sounds like a man who let everyone believe the worst of him so the worst wouldn't land on anyone else." She said it flatly, staring at the water, and I understood I had been given a piece of the truth the way you're handed something hot—quickly, and trusted not to drop it.

Downriver, maybe two kilometers, a thread of smoke stood up against the rain-gray sky. Thin. Deliberate. A signal fire, tended, fed—the smoke of someone alive enough to keep a fire alive.

Yara saw it the same moment I did. She was already reaching for the binoculars.

"Well," she said, glass to her eye, voice tightening into something between dread and purpose. "Someone survived. And if someone survived, Voss, they can tell us what happened at that camp—and who told them to burn a certain piece of paper."

She lowered the glasses. Her eyes had gone hard and bright as the river.

"Get the dinghy. We're going to meet a ghost."
