## 10. Smoke on the Water

The rain had been falling for two days, and I'd stopped thinking of it as weather. It was more like a roommate now—noisy, inconsiderate, always there when you woke up.

"Barometer's still dropping," Elias said from under the canopy, holding his watch up to the light like it might confess something. "And the water's come up another hand's width since dawn."

"I can read a river, Voss."

"I know. I'm narrating. It helps me feel useful."

He'd been useful, actually, which annoyed me. Two days of pumping bilge every hour on the hour, two days of him doing it without being asked, and the grudging respect I'd been trying to starve out of myself had put down roots like the mangroves along the bank. The patched planking was holding. Mostly. The Wren groaned now when she took a wave, a low complaint in her timbers like an old woman being asked to climb stairs, but she held.

We were pushing upriver because I'd said so, and Elias hadn't argued, which I'd decided was either loyalty or cowardice and hadn't yet determined which. Somewhere past the last mapped bend—*his* last mapped bend, the edge of his charts, which was a sentence I'd never expected to say about a man who carried forty pounds of drawing instruments up a monsoon river—there were six missing expedition members, a burned camp, and a note that named a financier and a deed. The deed. My father's ghost, typed out in a stranger's handwriting.

I kept that thought in the same compartment where I kept the memory of his hands on the wheel, big and scarred, teaching me to read current by the way foam braided on the surface. The compartment leaked. Everything leaks. That's practically the theme of this river.

It was midday, or what passed for it under the cloud lid, when Elias said, "Smoke."

I smelled it a half-second later. Not woodsmoke. Oiler, chemical, the smell of something that had burned hot and fast and wrong. I cut the throttle and let the Wren drift, and we both stood in the rain and listened.

"There." Elias pointed upstream and to the left, where the flooded forest began. The water had come up over the banks and into the trees, so the treeline was less a line than a suggestion, trunks standing hip-deep in brown current. And caught in the drowned brush, maybe two hundred meters off, something pale. Something that wasn't a tree.

I put the wheel over. "Get the boat hook. And the blanket in the dry box."

"You think someone's—"

"I think that's a tarp. And tarps don't swim."

We came up on it slow, engine idling, because the last thing you do around a person in the water is charge in like a rescue boat in a melodrama. People grab. People drown their rescuers out of pure gratitude. I'd seen it twice on the Sable, and once I'd been the person grabbing, which is a story I don't tell.

The pale thing was a surveyor's tarp, the heavy rubberized kind, and it was wrapped around something. Around *someone*. An arm came out of the tangle—dark-skinned, cut to ribbons, fingers still moving, still gripping the trunk behind it—and then a face, hollow-eyed, lips gray with cold, and I heard Elias say, "Oh God," in the voice of a man who'd been mapping an abstraction and had just met the map's cost.

"Easy," I called. "We've got you. Don't grab the boat, grab the hook."

The arm let go of the trunk and grabbed the hook. Good. That was a person who could still follow instructions, which put them ahead of half the passengers I'd ever carried.

Getting her over the gunwale was ugly. She weighed nothing, which is the worst thing a survivor can weigh, and the tarp came with her, and she was shivering so hard her teeth chattered in a rhythm I could have set a watch to. Elias wrapped her in the blanket and I got the engine up to a low rumble and put us behind the nearest high bank, out of the main current, because the first rule of rescue is you don't do it in the middle of a flooding river if you have any choice at all. I always have a choice. That's the whole point of me.

She was younger than I'd guessed from the arm—mid-twenties maybe, though the river had aged her a decade in what looked like a day or two. Her lips were cracked and her hands were a ruin of rope burns and lacerations, and when Elias pressed a tin cup of warmed water into them, she held it like it was the first honest object she'd touched in weeks.

"Name," I said. Not gently. Gentleness can wait; information can't.

"Nkechi." Her voice came out in pieces. "Nkechi Ade. Survey team. Kessari expedition." Her eyes found mine and there was something in them that wasn't just cold. It was the look of a person doing arithmetic with the dead. "You're not with Crane."

"Should I be?"

Her laugh came out as a cough. "No. No, you should absolutely not be." She drank, all of it, in one go. "Who are you?"

"Yara Hartwell. I run supplies. This is Elias Voss, who was supposed to be on your team and got left at the dock."

Nkechi's head came up at the name. At my name. I watched it land, watched her do the arithmetic again, and I braced for it—the flinch, the *Hartwell, the thief's daughter*, the look I'd been dodging for fifteen years in a dozen river towns.

It didn't come. What came instead was worse, or better, I still haven't decided.

"The deed," she said. "The Hartwell deed. You're—" She stopped herself, and I saw her decide, deliberately, to put the question down. Later, her face said. "You need to hear this. All of it. Before you decide anything about me."

"Start with why there's smoke," I said. "And why you were wrapped in a tarp in a drowned forest."

She looked at her hands. "The smoke was the records. Crane's men burned everything. Three days ago." She said it flatly, the way you say things you've said to yourself so many times the words have worn smooth. "Odell Crane. The expedition's financier. He came upriver two weeks into the survey, unannounced, with six men and an armored launch, and he told us the survey was concluded. Concluded. We hadn't finished the third quadrant. He said the findings had been received and the expedition was over, and we were to board his launch downstream to Port Meridian."

"And you didn't," Elias said quietly.

"Okafor didn't. He was our lead. He said a survey isn't concluded until the surveyor says it is, and he had the original field books, and Crane wanted them." Her jaw tightened. "Crane wanted them *burned*, Okafor wanted them filed, and for about six hours it was an argument. Then it wasn't an argument anymore."

The rain filled the silence for a while. I topped up her cup. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside my chest something had started moving, slow and heavy, like a sandbar shifting under a flood current.

"Six men with guns," I said. "Against a survey team."

"Against a survey team of eight, four of whom thought Crane was a benefactor." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "They came at night. Took the records tent first. Okafor tried to stop them and they broke his arm and put him in the water. I don't—" Her voice caught, and she let it, and didn't apologize for it. "I don't know if he made it to shore. The radio went next. Smashed from inside, like they wanted it to look like an accident, like we'd smashed our own lifeline. Then they burned the records tent and the fuel store, and in the light of that fire Crane stood there in his dry coat and told the rest of us the launch was leaving at dawn and anyone still in camp when the monsoon came would be a casualty of weather."

"He said that," Elias said. "Those words."

"*Casualty of weather.*" She repeated it like she was spitting out a bone. "Four of us took the survey boat that night. Tried to run downstream ahead of the flood. The boat hit a submerged fence line—old cattle fencing, from before—and went over. I came up holding the tarp. I've been holding the tarp since."

"Two days," I said.

"Two days." She looked at me, and her eyes were clear and furious and exhausted past the point where exhaustion is even a word. "The other three. Dami, Ruth, and the porters' head, Mensah. The river took them in the first hour. I heard them. I couldn't—" She stopped. Started again. "Crane's men will say the flood did it. And the flood *did* do it. But Crane put us in the water. He put us in the water and he'll sleep fine tonight."

I looked out at the drowned forest, at the brown water sliding through the trunks, patient as a debt collector, and I felt the thing in my chest finish its slow turn and come around to face me.

Righteous anger is a luxury. I'd spent fifteen years believing that. Anger gets people found, gets names spoken, gets fathers accused and ruined and dead. I'd built my whole life on the principle that the smart move is to keep your head down and your name short and let the river take what it takes.

But sitting there in my own wheelhouse, looking at a woman who'd held onto a tarp for two days so she could *testify*—that was the word that came, unbidden, *testify*—I felt the principle crack like the Wren's planking.

"Where's Crane now?" I asked.

"Downstream. He took the launch back to Port Meridian with the records he could carry. He thinks the flood closed the account." She almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "But he didn't get everything. Okafor kept copies. Duplicates of the key field books, the chain-of-title work, everything. He split them between us before the camp broke. I had mine in a waterproof wrap. It's—" She touched her chest, and her face went through something complicated. "It's still on me. Two days in the water and it's still on me."

Elias made a sound. I looked at him and he was holding his sealed case in both hands like it had suddenly gotten heavier, and I understood that we were now a boat with three copies of the same secret, which was either redundancy or a curse.

"The deed," Nkechi said. "You should know what the survey actually found, since you're—" She gestured at me, at all of me, the question she'd set down earlier now sitting back up and looking at her. "The Kessari riverlands were deeded. Legally, properly, sixty years ago. To a family named Hartwell. My work confirmed it. Chain of title, unbroken. The man everyone in these villages calls a thief bought that land fair and square and then let the world call him a thief rather than use the deed."

"Why," I said. It came out flat. Not a question. A door held shut.

"Because of men like Crane," she said simply. "The deed was the only thing standing between the speculators and the basin. As long as nobody could prove who owned the land, nobody could buy it, lease it, or clear it. He hid the proof to keep the land unownable. He let himself be the villain so the villages could stay free." She looked down at her ruined hands. "I came up this river to map it. I'm leaving it having learned that the most important thing on it was never on any chart."

The rain kept on. The river kept on. Somewhere upstream, past the last bend, there were four more people who might be holding onto tarps of their own, and somewhere downstream a man in a dry coat was sleeping fine.

I put my hand on the wheel and found it was shaking, just slightly, and I gripped until it stopped.

"Voss," I said. "How much water does Crane's launch draw?"

He blinked. "I—saw it at the dock. Armored hull, twin screws. Why?"

"Because armored hulls draw a lot of water," I said, "and the channel ahead of us draws very little, and it's rising, which means the shallows are moving, which means every chart he owns is now a work of fiction." I looked at Nkechi, half-dead and still holding her evidence, and something settled in me that I recognized, distantly, as the feeling my father must have had the day he chose to be called a thief. "And because I think it's time somebody showed Odell Crane what this river does to men who don't respect it."

Nkechi looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "There's something else. Something you need to hear before we go any further." She pulled the waterproof wrap from inside her shirt, and her hands, ruined as they were, held it steady. "Okafor made me memorize one thing. In case the wrap was lost. He said, *Tell them Crane isn't running from the flood. Crane is running because he knows someone else is looking for the deed—and he means to reach it first.*"

The rain came down harder, as if it had been waiting for its cue.
