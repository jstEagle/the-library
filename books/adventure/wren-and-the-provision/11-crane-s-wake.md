## 11. Crane's Wake

The thing nobody tells you about being a surveyor is that you spend half your life waiting for weather and the other half pretending the waiting doesn't terrify you. I'd been in the Kessari Basin eleven weeks when the men came upriver, and I remember thinking, in the last hour before everything went wrong, that the barometer was falling too fast for comfort and that Mr. Odell Crane's launch drew too much water for the channel below the bend. Both of those observations turned out to matter enormously. Neither of them saved anybody.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is what I do when I'm scared. My mother used to say I narrate my own life like a woman reading a report because reports don't cry. She was right, and I've made peace with it. Reports don't cry. They just tell you where the bodies are.

So. The report.

* * *

The camp sat on a bluff above the confluence — twelve tents, a radio mast lashed to a kapok tree, and a long table under canvas where we kept the survey books. Dr. Amara Osei ran it, a hydrologist with a voice like a landslide and a rule that no one touched the master charts but her. There were nine of us when the season began. By the time Crane's men arrived, we were seven. Okafor had gone downriver with the fever, and little Tomas had gone home to his mother with a shattered ankle and a story he'd be telling for the rest of his life about the snake that nearly got him. That left me, Osei, the Okonkwo twins, Ferreira, Baptiste, and a boy named Sefu who'd signed on to run the radio and had never once, in three months, received a message that wasn't a bill.

The work was simple in the way that honest work is simple: sound the channels, fix the benchmarks, map the floodplain reach by reach. We were mapping for Crane — that was the arrangement — a private commission, top dollar, no questions. Osei asked questions anyway. Osei always asked questions. I remember her standing at the table one evening with a benchmark sheet in her hand, frowning at the coordinates like they'd insulted her.

"These aren't survey points," she said. "These are parcel corners. He's not mapping the river. He's mapping *ownership*."

I said something clever, something like, "Rich men own rivers now, apparently," and she laughed, and we went back to work, and that was the last easy night we had.

Because here is what I understood only later, piecing it together in the water and the mud and the long dark afterward: Crane wasn't paying us to find out what the land was. He was paying us to find out what the land *wasn't* — wasn't deeded, wasn't claimed, wasn't already somebody's. And there was a name attached to the old claims, a name that made Osei go quiet when she found it in the colonial registry excerpts Crane's own lawyers had shipped upriver by mistake. A name she copied out and hid in the lining of her survey bag, because Osei believed in paper the way other people believe in God.

The name was Hartwell.

* * *

The launch came on a Tuesday, if Tuesdays mean anything in a place where the days are measured in rain and not-rain. Sefu flagged me at midmorning — he was a flagger, that boy, he flagged everything — and I came out of the instrument tent and saw her sitting low and gray on the brown water: an armored launch, the kind mining companies use when they expect to be hated. Crane stood at the rail in a white suit that had no business being white that far upriver, and beside him stood six men with the particular stillness of people who are paid to be still.

"Dr. Osei," Crane called up the bluff, cheerful as a funeral director. "Wonderful work. Truly. I've come to collect."

"Collect what?" Osei asked, and her voice was a landslide, and I loved her for it.

"The materials. The season's ended early. Monsoon's coming, and I've decided the survey is complete."

"The survey is not complete," Osei said. "We have the entire eastern floodplain to—"

"Complete," said Crane, in a tone that closed the subject the way a door closes on a hand.

I want to be fair to all of us, in this report. I want to record that we were not fools. Osei argued for two hours. She argued about the contract, about the benchmarks, about the ethics of a man who commissions a map and then refuses to let the mapmakers keep a copy. Crane smiled through all of it, and his six men stood still, and the barometer kept falling, and somewhere in the middle of the second hour I understood — the way you understand a current has caught the boat — that the argument was not for us. It was for the record. Osei was building a record. She wanted witnesses to every sentence.

Then Crane's men moved, all at once, the way weather moves, and the record stopped mattering.

* * *

They took the table first. That's what I remember most clearly, the sheer administrative brutality of it: two of them lifting the canvas table like a coffin, survey books sliding, Osei shouting, a man in a gray shirt sweeping the master charts into a waterproof case with the brisk tenderness of a nurse. They weren't thieves. Thieves hurry. These men were *organized*. They had a list. They checked items off the list. When Osei physically put herself between them and the instrument tent, one of them — I'll describe him as I saw him, because I saw him many times after, in dreams and in the water: broad, soft-faced, a scar through his left eyebrow like a signature — took her by the shoulders, lifted her, and set her aside the way you'd move a chair.

"You're hurting the science," he said, almost kindly. "We're just the cleanup crew, ma'am."

Then Sefu made his mistake, and it was the bravest mistake I have ever watched a person make. He went for the radio. Not to use it — to break it. He'd understood, faster than the rest of us, that the radio was the only thing Crane couldn't afford to leave working, because a working radio meant a working record, and Crane had come upriver specifically to erase the record. So Sefu, who weighed perhaps sixty kilos soaking wet and was, that afternoon, soaked to the skin, threw himself at the mast and started smashing the set with a survey mallet.

They pulled him off in about four seconds. But a smashed transmitter is a smashed transmitter. I watched the gray-shirted man look at the wrecked set, and then at Sefu, and then at Crane, and I watched something pass between them that I have thought about every day since: a small, tired negotiation, conducted entirely in eyebrows. *Do we deal with him?* And Crane, adjusting his white cuffs, saying nothing, which in the language of that afternoon meant *use your judgment*, which in the language of every country I have ever worked in means *yes*.

It was Osei who saved him, by doing the thing she did best: she made a scene so large it became the scene. She knocked over the fuel drums. She screamed about fire, about the dry season stores, about the whole bluff going up, and in the stampede of men who did not, apparently, want to die in a fire for Odell Crane's paperwork, Sefu got shoved behind the instrument tent, and I got shoved next to him, and the two of us crouched there in the dark smelling of kerosene while the world outside was taken apart.

"Records," Sefu whispered. His hands were shaking. "They're taking the records, not the instruments. Why the records?"

"Because the instruments only tell you where things are," I said. "The records tell you whose they are."

I didn't know then how true that was. I want that on the record too.

* * *

They let us keep the boats. That was the cruelty of it, or the carelessness — I've never decided. Six of us, seven boats' worth of gear, and Crane standing on his armored deck with the survey case locked under his arm, telling us the launch would escort us downriver as far as the confluence, for our safety, monsoon coming and all. He even smiled. He had a good smile. Men like that always do; it's how they get other men to do the stillness for them.

"The expedition is concluded," he said. "You'll be paid in full at Port Sable. My compliments to all of you. Fine work."

Osei stood at the water's edge with her survey bag — the one with the registry excerpts sewn into the lining — and said, "Mr. Crane. What was the survey for?"

And Crane looked at her for a long moment, and the smile went somewhere else, and what was underneath it wasn't anger. It was *pity*, which is worse. "Dr. Osei," he said, "the survey was for whatever I say it was for. That's what money is."

Then the rain started, and the world got loud.

* * *

Here is where my report becomes an account, and I ask whoever reads it — the magistrate, the insurer, the woman with the riverboat, whoever — to understand that everything after this point happened fast, in rain, at dusk, on a river in flood, and that memory under those conditions is not a photograph. It is a drowning person's sketch. But the sketch is all we have, so.

We loaded the two best boats. Osei, me, Sefu, the Okonkwo twins, Ferreira. Baptiste refused the boats — he said he'd wait for the launch, take the paid passage, no hard feelings, and I want it recorded that I did not blame him then and do not blame him now. Fear is not a character flaw. Fear is just weather in a person.

The plan was simple: run downriver ahead of the storm, make the confluence, radio from there — except the radio was kindling, so, make the confluence and *wait*, and hope. The river had other ideas. The monsoon didn't arrive; it *detonated*. I have worked the Kessari four seasons and I had never seen water come up like that, not rising but *arriving*, as if the whole basin had been holding its breath for a hundred years and chose that hour to exhale. The channel we'd sounded in the morning was gone by evening. The bluff we'd camped on became an island became a rumor.

We got perhaps two kilometers before the first boat swamped. The twins were bailing, Sefu was bailing, Osei was at the tiller shouting soundings she was making up out of pure instinct, and I was in the bow with a torch, looking for the sandbar that killed us. I found it. I will always have found it. The boat hit, slewed, and the river took the gunwale like a man taking a purse.

I remember the water being warm. Everyone expects cold; it was warm, blood-warm, thick with silt, and it didn't feel like drowning, it felt like being *handled*. I went under the hull, came up in a tree — an actual tree, standing in six feet of new river — and hung on a branch and watched our gear go by: the theodolite, the tent fly, Osei's hat, one of the twins' boots, spinning. I screamed names until my throat tore. I got one answer. Sefu, upstream of me, in the fork of another tree, screaming mine back.

We were the only two who answered all night.

* * *

I hung in that tree for — I don't know. The river took the night and gave it back in pieces. I know the rain stopped once, briefly, and the silence was worse than the rain, because in the silence I could hear the water moving things. Whole trees. A roof, still thatched. Something metal, turning over and over, ringing against the current like a bell with no church.

I know that at some point Osei's survey bag came past me, floating open, and I caught it without deciding to, the way you catch a child, and I held it against my chest in the fork of the tree because it was paper, it was only paper, and because Osei had sewn a name into its lining and names are the heaviest cargo there is. Hartwell. I held a dead woman's bag — I didn't know yet she was dead; I knew it the way you know a fever has turned, without evidence — and I held the name inside it, and the rain came back, and I made a report to no one, out loud, because reports don't cry.

*Survivor, Nkechi Ade, surveyor, second season Kessari. Vessel lost with all hands save two, location unknown. Cause: river. Cause, second entry, smaller handwriting: a man in a white suit.*

* * *

Dawn was a gray rumor. The water had dropped a little — dropped, in flood terms, meaning it had only swallowed the lower branches instead of the upper ones. Sefu was still in his tree, thirty meters off, alive by some arithmetic I will never finish. We shouted coordinates at each other like lovers quarreling, and between us we worked out the sun's bearing, and the current's set, and the one fact that kept us from despair: the flood was running us downriver, and downriver was people. Downriver, sooner or later, was a boat.

I got out of the tree at midmorning, onto a floating log, and paddled with my hands, and it was the stupidest and most necessary thing I have ever done, and I did it because Sefu couldn't swim and someone had to reach the debris raft tangled in the drowned fig and drag it back against the current for him. It took two hours. It took everything. When I finally hauled him out of the water and onto the raft, the boy put his head against my shoulder and said, "They checked us off a list, Nkechi. We were on a *list*," and I said, "Then we'll be on a list of witnesses instead," and he laughed, or cried, or both, the way people do when the river has finished with them and hasn't quite decided what they are now.

We drifted. We ate nothing. We passed the confluence without knowing it, because the confluence had become a lake the width of a sea. And on the second afternoon, half-delirious, lashed to our raft of fig branches and rope, we saw it: a riverboat, working up the flooded channel against all sense, a green-hulled thing with a patched sail and a name on the bow that the rain kept wiping away.

Sefu saw her first. He stood up on our scrap of forest, on legs that hadn't held him in two days, and waved both arms over his head like a man signaling his own rescue, which is exactly what he was doing.

"Wren," he read, squinting, as the boat came on. "The boat's called *Wren*. Who names a boat —"

"Hush," I said, because I was watching the figure at the tiller, a woman with river-gray eyes and a way of standing that said the water had tried to kill her before and she held a grudge about it. And beside her, at the bow, a man with a survey case — an actual survey case, I'd know the shape anywhere — and a look on his face like a man reading a map that had started reading him back.

I thought: *witnesses*. I thought: *finally, someone to make the report to.*

I did not yet know that the woman steering that boat carried the same name sewn into the lining of Osei's bag. The river hadn't told me yet. The river keeps its own records, and it releases them in order — and the first thing it was going to hand me, as that green hull swung alongside and hands reached down through the rain, was the beginning of an account that would not be closed until Odell Crane's white suit was a rumor on the floodwater and every stolen chart was back in the hands of the people the charts were drawn on.

But that's downstream. Everything, now, is downstream.

I grabbed the rail. I said my name out loud for the first time since the camp fell — "Nkechi Ade, surveyor, and I have evidence" — and the woman at the tiller looked down at me through the rain and said, in a voice like a locked door opening one inch:

"Get aboard. And whatever's in that bag — don't let it get wet twice."
