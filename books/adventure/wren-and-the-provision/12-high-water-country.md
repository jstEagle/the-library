## 12. High Water Country

The monsoon didn't arrive so much as it fell on us, the way a drunk uncle falls on you at a wedding—suddenly, heavily, and with every intention of staying the night.

I'd been reading the sky since dawn, or what passed for dawn under that bruised ceiling of cloud. The barometer on the Wren's bulkhead had been dropping all morning like a man sliding down a muddy bank, and by noon the wind had swung around to the northeast and started pushing the river backward. That's not a figure of speech. On the lower Kessari, when the monsoon proper hits, the backwater from the delta can actually reverse the current in the wide stretches, and you find yourself steering into water that's decided, for its own reasons, to go the other way.

"Tell me again why we're not running downstream," Elias said. He was wedged in the wheelhouse doorway with his journal pressed against his chest, protecting it from the spray like it was a child and I was driving us both off a cliff. Which, in fairness, I might have been.

"Because downstream is where Crane's launch is," I said. "And because Nkechi's fever needs a doctor, and there's no doctor between here and Port Sable except the one at Tamu Landing. And because—" I throttled down as the channel bent hard to starboard around a drowned willow— "because I said so."

"You said so is not a navigational principle."

"It's the only navigational principle that's ever kept me alive."

Nkechi lay wrapped in blankets on the bench behind me, shivering through her fever, her broken wrist splinted with a boat hook handle and torn sheet. Every bump of the hull made her groan, and every groan made me push a little harder, which made more bumps, which—I'll grant—is not sound seamanship either. But I've never claimed to be a good woman. Only a good captain, and even that claim has holes in it you could row a pirogue through.

The rain came for real about two o'clock. Not rain the way most people mean it—a gray curtain dropped over the world, then a gray wall, then nothing but water in every direction, falling, rising, blowing sideways, until the river and the sky stopped being two things and became one enormous wet argument we were having in the middle of. Visibility went to fifty yards, then twenty, then to the length of my own arm and my own stubbornness.

I knew the country, though. That was the thing Elias couldn't understand yet, no matter how many charts he drew. I didn't need to see. My father had walked me through this stretch of the Kessari a hundred times in my childhood—not on paper, never on paper, he was careful that way—but in words, in the dark, in the cadence he used for stories: *past the twin figs leaning left, the channel dog-legs south before the old ferry crossing; mind the submerged fence line at Kojou, the villagers drove stakes there in the drought years and never pulled them.* I had his river in my head the way other people have hymns. I'd spent twenty years pretending I didn't, and here I was navigating by it, mile after mile, unable to stop.

"Tamu Landing should be just ahead," I shouted over the din. "If it's still above water."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we improvise. Improvising is also a navigational principle."

* * *

Tamu Landing was underwater.

Not gone—villages on the Kessari don't drown, they *rise*. The houses there were built on stilts tall as church steeples, and when the flood came the whole settlement turned into an archipelago, walkways between rooftops, dugout canoes where the market street had been. As we came up on it through the murk, I saw lanterns swinging in doorways, laundry strung between poles and whipping like signal flags, a dozen small boats ferrying people and goats and one extremely displeased pig between the higher ground of the temple mound and the houses that still had dry floors.

There was no dock. There was, however, a man on the roof of the lowest house, waving us off with both arms, shouting something the wind ate whole.

"He wants us to keep going," Elias translated, uselessly, since neither of us could hear him either.

"I got that part."

But I'd already seen what the man hadn't counted on: a gap in the stilt line, wide enough for the Wren, leading back to a flooded courtyard where three houses broke the wind. It would be cramped. It would be ugly if the water rose another meter. But it was shelter, and shelter beats elegance every time the sky is trying to kill you.

I brought her in slow, bow into the wind, and Elias fended us off the pilings with a boat hook while I walked the throttle forward and back like a woman petting a nervous animal. We slid into that courtyard with maybe a hand's width on either side, and I got lines around two pilings before the wind understood what we'd done and started shaking us like a dog shakes a rat.

The man on the roof came down a ladder, waded to our gunwale, and looked at me with the flat expression of a person whose hospitality and judgment are fighting a duel behind his face.

"You bring a boat into my courtyard in the teeth of the monsoon," he said, in trade-accent Kessari, "and you are either very skilled or very stupid."

"Both, usually," I said. "We need shelter for the boat and a doctor for our friend. She's fevered, and her wrist's broke."

He looked past me at Nkechi, and something in his face shifted. "Bring her up. The Ondo woman knows bones." Then his eyes came back to mine, narrowed, and stayed there like a fishhook. "Where did you learn to handle a boat like that? Nobody comes through that gap in this weather unless they know these waters."

"I know these waters," I said, which was true, and also the most dangerous sentence I'd spoken in years.

* * *

They took Nkechi up into the dry loft of the Ondo woman's house, and the Ondo woman—small, silver-haired, forearms like mooring cable—took one look at the splint and said, "Who did this, a crocodile?" and reset the wrist while Nkechi screamed into a leather strap, and then fed her ginger tea and quinine until the shivering eased. Good bones, that one. In every sense.

Elias and I ended up on the covered veranda of the headman's house, watching the rain hammer the flooded street below. The village had settled into the rhythm it clearly knew well: children laughing in the dry lofts, someone singing over the drum of the rain, the smell of woodsmoke and cooking cassava drifting out through shuttered windows. It should have felt like chaos. It felt like a Sunday.

The headman's name was Bello, and he was perhaps seventy, with a face like a dried riverbed and eyes that missed nothing, including, I suspected, everything I was trying not to say. He brought us food we hadn't asked for—fish stew, fried plantain—and sat down across from us with the unhurried air of a man who owned the veranda, the rain, and quite possibly the river itself.

"You are running from the storm," he said. "Or from something else. Storms end. The other thing usually doesn't."

"We're looking for people," Elias said carefully. "An expedition camp upriver. And a man named Odell Crane."

At the name Crane, Bello spat over the rail, with real precision, into the floodwater. I decided I liked him enormously.

"Crane," he said. "The money-man. He came through last season with his maps and his promises. Wanted to buy the floodplain. Wanted to buy the *rights* to the floodplain, which is worse, because land you can see, but rights are chains you wear inside your skin." He shook his head. "The elders told him no. He smiled the way snakes smile and went on upriver. Now you say his men emptied a camp and left people to drown." His eyes came to me again. "And you. You still haven't said who you are."

Here it was, then. The question I'd dodged in a hundred ports, in a hundred voices, wearing a hundred small lies like cheap jewelry. *Hartwell* was a name that got you refused service, run off docks, occasionally shot at. I opened my mouth to produce one of the usual aliases—Maren, I'd used Maren twice, Maren was practically a coat I owned—

And Bello said, "You're a Hartwell."

The rain filled the silence. I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for Elias to go very still beside me. Long enough for me to notice my hands had closed around my cup hard enough to whiten my knuckles.

"I don't know what you—" I started.

"The way you read water," Bello said simply. "The way you came through that gap without touching a piling, in weather that would shame a minister. There was a man who ran this river like that, years ago. Before your time, girl, but not before mine. Josiah Hartwell. He taught half the young boatmen on this stretch their trade, whether they knew it or not. My own brother piloted for him two seasons." He leaned forward. "They said he stole the riverlands. That's the story that came down from Port Sable. But stories come down rivers the same as driftwood, and most of it floats wrong-side up."

"My father wasn't—" My voice did something humiliating. I stopped and started again. "Everyone says he was a thief."

Bello looked at me for a long moment. Then he stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and beckoned. "Come. Both of you. There are people older than me who remember better."

* * *

The elders were gathered in the largest house, out of the rain, a circle of them around a lamp—six old women and two old men, faces folded like maps of themselves. They made room without being asked. One of the women, ancient and tiny, with a shawl pulled over her head, looked at me for a long time and then said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"Josiah's girl. All grown."

"You knew him?" I managed.

"Knew him? Child, he saved this village." She gestured around at the walls, the roof, the lamplight. "Thirty years ago, the speculators came—the ones before Crane, they breed like mosquitoes—and they had papers saying they owned the floodplain, papers bought from some ministry clerk who'd never seen the river. They were going to fence the commons, charge toll on the water, move us off our own ground." She laughed, short and bitter. "And then a riverboat captain appeared with documents of his own. A deed, properly sealed, older than theirs, showing the riverlands belonged to him. He stood in front of the survey office in Port Sable and waved those papers like a torch, and the speculators' claims collapsed, because you cannot take land that already belongs to somebody else."

My mouth had gone dry. "That's not the story they tell."

"No," she said. "Because after, when the speculators' friends in the government came after him—when they called him thief and forged the accusations that drove him from the river—he let them. He never once showed the deed again. Never defended himself with it. He let them call him a thief rather than put those papers back in play, because the moment he did, the speculators would have found a way to break them, and then nothing would stand between us and the next Crane, and the one after him." She reached out and took my hand, her grip surprisingly strong. "So he carried the shame so we wouldn't carry the chains. That's what a Hartwell did for the Kessari. That's what your father was."

I have been shot at twice, shipwrecked once, and held for ransom briefly by pirates who turned out to be mostly lost fishermen with machetes and ambition. None of it undid me the way that little old woman's hand on mine did.

I thought of my father teaching me the channel markers by starlight. I thought of him dying inland, far from any water, in a room that smelled of dust and defeat, telling me *never use the name, Yara, the name is poison*. He'd believed it was poison. He'd believed it right up to the end. And all along it had been a shield he'd held over strangers, and they had known, and they had remembered, and they had waited thirty years for his daughter to come up the river in the rain.

"I don't understand," Elias said quietly, beside me. "Why hide it? Why let himself be ruined?"

"Because a deed is only strong while it sleeps," said the oldest of the men, who had not spoken until now. "Wake it too soon, and it dies. Wake it at the right hour—" he looked at me, and then, meaningfully, at the waterproof wrap under Elias's jacket where the deed rode against his chest— "and it can hold back a flood."

Every eye in the circle went to that wrap. I hadn't told them. I hadn't told anyone. But word travels upriver faster than boats, and somewhere between the silent camp and Tamu Landing, someone had talked, and now a room full of elders I'd known for three hours knew what I carried, and what it meant, and what Odell Crane would burn a village down to destroy.

Outside, the wind rose another register, and the rain went from hammering to howling. One of the old women rose without hurry and began shuttering the lamps, the way you do when a long night is starting.

"Rest," Bello said, clapping a hand on my shoulder. "In the morning, if the water holds, we'll talk about what's owed. And girl—" he paused at the door, silhouetted against the grey chaos of the flood— "you should know. Crane's launch passed downriver yesterday, armored, riding heavy. Heading upstream was a man asking questions about a boat called the *Wren*."

I looked at Elias. Elias looked at me. Somewhere out in the dark, the river rose and rose, patient as a debt coming due.

"He'll come back through," I said. "When the water drops. And he'll be looking for us."

"Then you'd best decide," Bello said, "what name you'll be standing under when he does."

I stood at the rail a long time after they'd gone, watching the flood swallow the street below, holding a name I'd spent my whole life burying and finding, for the first time, that it fit my hand like a tiller.
