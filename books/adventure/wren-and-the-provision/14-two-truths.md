## 14. Two Truths

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the time we reached Mama Sena's back room, the kind of steady, patient downpour that didn't rage so much as insist. It drummed on the palm-thatch roof like a creditor at a door, and I sat at a table scarred by forty years of knives and elbows, staring at a document older than I was.

The deed. My father's deed. The Hartwell name written in faded iron-gall ink across the top, and beneath it, the boundaries of the Kessari riverlands described in the careful legalese of men who'd never once gotten their boots wet.

Elias sat across from me. Nkechi had finally slept—truly slept, not the twitching half-doze of the last two days—in the corner, wrapped in one of Mama Sena's blankets, her evidence folder clutched to her chest even unconscious. The old woman herself had withdrawn behind a curtain, with the tact of someone who understood that some conversations need an empty room.

"So," Elias said. "Now you know."

"Now I know." I turned the deed over. The wax seal on the back had cracked sometime in the decades since it was pressed, a hairline fracture running through the impression of some long-dead magistrate's ring. "You know what's funny? I spent twenty years building a life out of not knowing. Twenty years of never staying anywhere longer than a season, never giving anyone my full name, never—" I stopped. Started again. "I counted sandbars instead of birthdays, Elias. That was the whole architecture of it. Miles, not years. Because miles don't ask questions."

"And now?"

"Now there's a piece of paper telling me my father wasn't a thief." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "And all I can think about is how much paper costs."

He frowned. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning paper is the most dangerous material on this river." I laid the deed flat between us. "More than rot. More than snags. You want to know what killed my father? Not the fever they put on his death certificate. Paper did. Accusations on paper, debts on paper, a name dragged through every trading post from here to the delta until he couldn't buy fuel without hearing whispers. He died in a rented room in Port Lassiter with nothing but a duffel bag and a reputation, and I was sixteen, and I learned my first real lesson about documents: they don't record truth. They record what people are willing to believe."

Elias was quiet for a moment. Rain filled the silence.

"Your father bought this land fairly," he said. "Mama Sena told us—"

"I heard what she told us." I held up a hand. "He bought it to protect the villages. He hid it. He let them call him a thief so the speculators would chase him instead of the deed. I've had two days to turn that over, and here's what I keep coming back to: it didn't work. They chased him anyway. They hounded him to death anyway. And the villages he was protecting spent thirty years thinking their benefactor was their robber." I tapped the cracked seal. "That's what truth bought him. That's what it buys."

"That's what *silence* bought him," Elias said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" I leaned forward. "Because from where I'm sitting, the man who told the truth got destroyed, and the man who burned records and smashed radios and left surveyors to drown in floodwater—he's still breathing, still rich, probably sleeping fine right now in an armored launch with a dry bunk. Explain to me how truth is winning, Elias. Walk me through the arithmetic."

"It hasn't won *yet*," he said. "That's not the same as losing."

"Oh, that's beautiful. Put that on a chart."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he pulled his journal from inside his jacket—it was swollen with damp, the cover warped—and set it beside the deed. Two documents side by side. One that could hang a family's honor back around its neck. One full of soundings and bearings and small neat handwriting that had become, over these weeks, the only voice I trusted besides my own.

"I've redrawn maps before," he said. "Do you know what happens when a map is wrong? Boats ground. People starve waiting for supply runs that went to the wrong bend. A village gets flooded because the levee was built off a bad contour line. Wrong maps kill people—not dramatically, not all at once, but steadily, the way rot does." He touched the journal. "My whole profession is the belief that recording things accurately matters. That the world is better when the chart matches the channel. Your father's deed is a map of a different kind, Yara. It says: *this happened. This land belongs to these people. It always did.* Crane wants it burned because a corrected map ruins his whole voyage."

"Crane wants it burned because burning it keeps me alive," I said. "You're skipping that part. Let's say I do what you want. Let's say I stand up in front of the basin and say *I'm Yara Hartwell, daughter of Tomas Hartwell, and here's the deed.* What happens next? Crane's lawyers descend. Crane's hired men descend. Every speculator and land-grabber who ever wanted the Kessari comes downriver to contest it, and every one of them knows my face and my boat and my route. I have spent half my life being no one, Elias. It's the one thing I'm genuinely good at."

"You're good at being afraid," he said quietly.

The words landed like a hull hitting a sandbar—the sudden stop, everything lurching forward.

"You don't get to say that to me," I said. "You don't know what—"

"No," he said. "I don't. I know my own version. I know why a man learns to draw coastlines instead of living on them. But I watched you take this boat upriver past the last mapped bend on instinct and dead reckoning, and I watched you read water that would drown a hydrologist, and I watched you go back for Nkechi when every sensible cell in your body was screaming downstream. That woman is not afraid. So whatever's left that flinches—it isn't fear of rivers. It's fear of this." He tapped the deed. "Of being *seen*."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down the flooded lane a dog barked twice and gave up, uninterested in anything that wasn't food or fire.

I looked at the deed and tried to feel what I was supposed to feel. Pride, maybe. Vindication. Instead I felt the specific cold weight of a hatch cover lifting onto deep water—the moment before you find out what's been leaking in the dark.

"You want to know the worst part?" I said. "For about ten minutes after Mama Sena finished talking, I believed it. I thought: *he did it for us. He did it for them.* And then I thought about him teaching me to read the Sable, pointing at a heron and saying *that bird wastes nothing, Yara, watch how it takes only what it needs*—and I thought about him dying alone under a false name in a boarding house, and I couldn't make those two men be the same man. If he was so noble, why did he leave me the shame instead of the truth? Why didn't he ever tell *me*?"

"Maybe he was trying to protect you the only way left to him."

"Then it was a bad plan. It was a bad plan all the way through, and everyone who's carried it has bled." I stood up. My legs were stiff from the raft, from the wading, from days of tension stored in muscle like silt. I paced the length of the room—six steps, turn, six steps back. "Here's my version of the arithmetic, cartographer. Burn the deed tonight, in the stove, while the rain covers everything. Crane thinks it's gone. His men stand down. Nkechi testifies about the camp, about the abandonment—that's enough to ruin him legally without ever touching the Hartwell name. The villages keep their land de facto because nobody contests what nobody knows exists. And I walk away still being nobody, which is a job I've held for twenty years with excellent performance reviews."

"And the next Odell Crane?" Elias asked. "The one who comes in five years, or fifteen, when everyone who remembers the truth is dead? There's no deed to fight him with, because you fed it to a stove. The villages lose the land permanently, and they lose it *because of you*, and you'll spend the rest of your life counting miles knowing that." He shook his head slowly. "You wouldn't survive it. You think anonymity is keeping you alive, but it's just a slower way of drowning. I've seen it. I lived it. You can't hide in motion forever, Yara. Eventually the river runs out of bends."

"Spoken like a man whose father left him a good name."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, fast, but I caught it. Everyone on this river was carrying cargo they wouldn't declare.

"My father," he said carefully, "left me a survey office and a debt I'm still paying off. So no. Not a good name. Just a name." He gathered the deed, squared its edges against the table with unnecessary precision, and held it out to me. "I'm not going to tell you what to do with it. It was never mine. But I'll tell you what I told myself the day I stopped running: the cost of the truth is paid once. The cost of the lie is paid forever, in installments, and the interest compounds."

I took the deed. The paper was cool and slightly damp, alive somehow, like something breathing shallowly.

"And if paying once kills me?"

"Then at least you'll die as someone," he said, "instead of drowning slowly as no one. But I don't think it kills you. I think—" he hesitated, and for once the precise man fumbled for precision—"I think you've been moored your whole life, Yara. Tied off to nothing, drifting at the end of your own line. This isn't an anchor dragging you under. It's the bottom. First solid holding you've ever had."

I wanted to throw it at him. I wanted to burn it. I wanted, more than either, to sit down and cry for a father I'd spent twenty years resenting and three days learning to mourn properly, and I had no time for any of it, because—

The curtain moved. Mama Sena stood there, one hand on the frame, and she wasn't looking at me. She was looking past me, through the slatted window at the flooded street, and her face had gone the color of the rain.

"Child," she said. "There's a light on the water. Coming up the channel. It's not a village boat."

I moved to the window. Far down the drowned lane, where the street became river, a searchlight swept the treetops—white, arrogant, methodical, the beam of a vessel that expected the world to get out of its way.

Crane's launch. Drawing too much water for this channel, but coming anyway.

I slid the deed inside my jacket, against my chest, where it lay flat and cold as a second heartbeat. Whatever I was going to decide, the river had apparently decided I'd be making the decision sooner rather than later.

"Elias," I said. "Get Nkechi up. Mama Sena—how many ways out of this village that a big boat can't follow?"

The old woman smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all.

"None, child," she said. "But there's one that a small boat can't neither."
