## 15. Crane Comes Upriver

The storm gave us a day's grace, and I should have known better than to trust it. Rivers don't give gifts. They make loans, and the interest is always paid in full.

We'd tied up in a cut of deadwater behind a stand of flooded bamboo—Nkechi's idea, actually, which surprised me. She'd spent three days half-drowned and one night on my deck wrapped in every dry blanket the Wren owned, and she still had more sense about hiding than I did. "Crane's launch will come upriver looking for us," she'd said, staring at the ceiling of the wheelhouse like it owed her money. "He doesn't leave loose ends. He left *us*."

So we hid. Elias plotted our position on his charts with a pencil so fine it looked like it was drawing on air. Nkechi slept. I sat in the pilot's chair with my boots up on the rail and watched the water rise, which is what river people do instead of praying.

The Wren was holding. Barely. The patch job from the leaking hull had cost us two planks of Elias's good timber, a roll of tarred canvas, and most of my dignity—I'd had to let him hold the light while I worked half-submerged in the bilge, cursing in three languages. But she floated, and floating is ninety percent of what a boat owes you.

I was counting sandbars out of habit—the river had eaten two since yesterday, birthed one new one shaped like a sleeping dog—when Nkechi came up the companionway fast enough to wake the dead.

"Smoke," she said. "West bank. Moving."

I took the glasses off their hook. Through the rain-haze, maybe four kilometers downriver, a gray shape was pushing against the current at a speed no honest boat manages. Armored. Gunmetal paint, a stubby mast bristling with antennas, and a bow that rode high and mean because it was built for rivers that didn't exist yet—rivers some engineer hadn't gotten around to dredging.

"That's the *Kestrel*," Nkechi said quietly. "Crane's launch. Twin diesels, armored pilothouse, draws—" she paused, doing math she hated, "—a meter and a half, loaded. Maybe more."

A meter and a half.

I lowered the glasses and felt something shift in my chest that I hadn't felt in years. Not fear. Something older and meaner. The thing my father used to call *river-sense*, the little cold voice that reads water the way bankers read ledgers.

Because here's the thing about the channel ahead of us: past the drowned village, past Mama Sena's stilt houses, the Sable pinches down through a stretch the old pilots called the Needle's Eye. Sand there moves like gossip—here today, gone by Thursday, back next week wearing a different shape. My father had taught me to read it when I was nine years old, standing behind me at the wheel with his hands over mine, saying *feel it, Yara, don't look at it, feel it.* A boat drawing a meter and a half could run the Needle's Eye exactly twice a year, in the driest weeks, if the pilot was either very good or very stupid.

Today, in monsoon flood, with sandbars migrating like frightened animals?

Today it would eat a boat like the *Kestrel* alive.

"Kill the engine," I said. "All lights. Elias, get below with Nkechi."

Elias didn't move. He'd come up behind me without making a sound, which was becoming an annoying habit of his. "You're going to talk to him."

"I'm going to *listen* to him." I checked the revolver in the drawer under the chart table—an old service pistol I'd bought from a man who swore it had never been fired, which I'd always suspected was a lie about the gun rather than a boast. "There's a difference. Talking is when you say things. Listening is when you find out what he knows."

"And then?"

"Then I decide whether he leaves this river as a customer or a cautionary tale."

Elias looked at me for a long moment. Rain drummed the wheelhouse roof. Somewhere under all that water, the Wren's patched hull held its breath along with the rest of us.

"You're enjoying this," he said finally.

"No," I said, and found, to my surprise, that it was mostly true. "But I'm not scared of it anymore, and that's new."

* * *

They found us anyway, of course. Crane's people weren't stupid—they were just rich, which looks similar from a distance. A skiff came ahead of the launch, two men with a loudhailer and the particular slouch of hired muscle who've been told they won't need to work hard today. They swept the flooded timber line with a spotlight until the light landed on the Wren's mast, tucked in the deadwater like a heron pretending to be a stick.

"Wren!" the loudhailer barked. "Captain Hartwell! Mr. Crane requests parley!"

Hartwell. My name, spoken aloud on my own river by a stranger's mouth. It went through me like a fishhook. Nkechi, crouched below the wheelhouse window, mouthed the word *how?* and I shook my head. Didn't matter how. Crane had bought the expedition's records; he knew who'd taken the resupply contract. Probably knew my shoe size by now.

I stood up where the light could see me, hands loose at my sides. "Come alongside slow," I called back. "Anybody steps aboard without asking, we're done talking before we start."

The skiff idled closer. Behind it, the *Kestrel* came on—and I made myself watch her the way my father taught me to watch any boat: not her guns, not her armor, but her *draft*. How deep she sat. How the water broke at her bowline. She wallowed a little in the flood current, heavy as a tax collector, pushing a bow-wave that said *I am carrying too much weight and I know it.*

Good, I thought. Be heavy. Be heavy all the way to the Needle's Eye.

Mr. Crane himself did not come aboard. Of course he didn't. Men like Odell Crane send their voices ahead of them the way kings used to send heralds. Instead a speaker crackled from the *Kestrel*'s rail, forty meters off our beam, and a voice came across the water—smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man who has never once been interrupted and intends to keep it that way.

"Captain Hartwell. What a pleasure. I understand you've had an eventful voyage."

"Eventful," I agreed, keeping my tone flat as a mudflat. "Ran into some weather."

"Weather," the voice repeated, amused. "Yes. I imagine that's what you'd call it. Captain, let me be direct, because I respect directness in a businesswoman. You are carrying cargo that belongs to me. Survey materials, documents, possibly a passenger or two who have wandered considerably outside their employment contracts. I'm prepared to pay generously for their return. Very generously. Enough that you could re-plank that hull of yours properly—yes, I know about the hull—and never take another contract you didn't like again."

Below the window, I heard Elias inhale. I put one hand down flat against the sill where he could see it. *Stay.*

"How generous?" I asked, and made my voice sound like a woman doing arithmetic. Greedy, cautious, tempted. It wasn't hard. Every captain on this river has played that role so many times it fits like an old coat.

"Ten thousand."

Pause. Let him think I'm swallowing.

"Fifteen," the voice amended smoothly. "And passage downstream under our escort, so nobody troubles you about what happened upriver. Whatever happened upriver." A small laugh, dry as paper. "You'd be a woman with money and no history, Captain. I understand that has appeal for someone like you."

*Someone like you.* He meant: someone hiding. And the worst part, the part that made my jaw ache, was that six months ago the offer would have worked. Fifteen thousand and a clean slate—that had been the whole architecture of my life. Miles, not years. Keep moving, keep unnamed, keep nothing that anyone could take.

I looked down at the deck boards Elias had bought with money he wouldn't explain, at Nkechi's blanket still drying on the rail, at the chart case lashed under the wheelhouse bench with the deed inside it—the deed to land I'd signed away in my head already, though the ink on that decision wasn't dry yet either.

"Captain?" the voice prompted.

"I'm thinking," I said. "Fifteen thousand buys a lot of silence."

"It does," Crane agreed. "Silence is the only commodity I deal in, and I've never once lost money on it."

*That,* I thought, *is your mistake. You think everything's for sale because everything always has been.* My father had thought that too, once—he'd believed the deed itself was the prize, the paper worth killing for. He'd died not understanding that what he'd really bought, all those years ago, was something Crane couldn't price: the gratitude of people whose names weren't written anywhere except in each other's mouths.

I leaned on the rail, letting the silence stretch just long enough to be interesting.

"Here's my problem, Mr. Crane," I said. "I picked up a contract to deliver supplies to that camp. Contract says deliver. Now, the camp's empty, so delivery's complicated. But my mother raised me to finish what I sign for." My mother had raised me to do no such thing—she'd died when I was four—but Crane wouldn't know that, and liars should always decorate their lies with family. "So. You want your papers back, your people back, and your problem to float away downstream. Fine. Here's my price."

"Name it."

"The fifteen thousand. Plus twenty more. And you forget the name Hartwell ever chartered a boat on this river. No records, no witnesses, no follow-up. I've heard what happens to people who carry your paperwork, Mr. Crane. I'd like to go on not being one of them."

Another pause. On the *Kestrel*'s deck, I could see shapes moving behind the armored glass of the pilothouse—men conferring, a hand gesturing. Nkechi had told me there were eight aboard, maybe ten, with rifles and one of those automatic weapons that sounds like tearing cloth. Against that, I had a revolver, a cartographer, a surveyor with a broken wrist, and a boat named after a bird that mostly hides in reeds.

The speaker crackled. "Thirty-five thousand. Agreed. Bring your vessel alongside and we'll conclude our business."

"Not alongside." I let a shiver of genuine nerves into my voice—it wasn't entirely acting. "Your boat's got guns, Mr. Crane, and mine's got rot. We come to you, we're cargo. You want the transfer, we do it at the old trading station past the village—neutral ground, open water, my terms. Tomorrow morning. Tonight I'll get the documents squared away."

More murmuring behind the glass. I watched the *Kestrel* idle against the current, her engines holding her in place, and I watched the way her stern sat low in the floodwater, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since I first spotted her smoke.

A meter and a half, loaded. Maybe more.

Between here and the trading station lay the Needle's Eye, and the Needle's Eye tonight was running shallow over a sandbar that had migrated a hundred meters east in the last two days—I knew because I'd sounded it myself that morning with a pole, alone, while the others slept, because a captain who stops counting sandbars might as well start counting her own days. There was a lane through it. There was always a lane. But it was a lane for boats that drew less than a meter, boats that answered their helms, boats piloted by somebody who could feel the bottom through the wheel the way my father taught me.

The *Kestrel* could follow me in. That was the beautiful, terrible truth of it. She could chase me straight into the shallows, all guns and confidence, right up until the river reached up and took hold of her keel and refused, politely and permanently, to let go.

"Tomorrow morning," the voice said at last, smooth as oil on water. "At the station. You have my word the transfer will be civil."

"Words float," I said. "I'll want more than words eventually. But tomorrow will do for now."

"One more thing, Captain." The voice cooled by several degrees, the way water cools when you swim out past the drop-off. "If I learn you've been less than straightforward—if those documents turn up anywhere other than in my hands—I will find you. Not your boat. *You.* Rivers end, Captain Hartwell. Everything on them ends up somewhere, sooner or later."

"So does rain," I said. "Doesn't make it scary."

I cut the exchange there, stepped back from the rail, and stood in the dark of my own wheelhouse listening to the *Kestrel*'s engines wind down as she dropped anchor in the deeper channel below us. Her searchlight swept the treeline once, twice, and went dark. Somewhere on her deck, a man laughed at something.

Nkechi rose slowly from below. Elias came to stand beside me at the window, close enough that I could feel him deciding whether to say something.

"You lied to him," he said finally.

"I negotiated with him. Lying's just negotiating with better posture."

"He thinks you're selling us out."

"He thinks I'm selling you out *tomorrow*." I turned away from the window and started pulling gear from the lockers—sounding pole, spare line, the storm lantern. "Which gives us one night to make sure that when he comes to collect, the river's already collected him first."

Elias frowned. "The channel past the village—"

"Draws a meter and a quarter in flood, if you know the lane." I slung the coil of rope over my shoulder and allowed myself, for the first time in longer than I could remember, an actual smile. It felt strange on my face, like a shoe on the wrong foot. "His boat draws a meter and a half. I sounded the bar myself this morning."

Understanding moved across Elias's face slowly, then all at once—the mapmaker seeing the territory. "You're going to lead him onto the sandbar."

"I'm going to *invite* him onto the sandbar. There's a difference." I killed the lantern and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the rain thicken overhead, to the river rising beneath us, patient as a debt. "He offered me money and no history, Elias. Clean slate, fresh miles, same as I've been running my whole life."

Outside, thunder rolled up from the south, where the real storm was still waiting, saving itself.

"But he made one mistake," I said. "He asked the wrong woman to forget her father's river."

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat at the wheel with the engine ticking over at bare idle, holding the Wren against the current in the deadwater, and I studied the dark water like a page I'd been reading my whole life and had only now noticed was addressed to me.

Somewhere below us, anchored in the channel, thirty-five thousand dollars' worth of armored steel sat waiting for morning. Somewhere above us, past the drowned village and Mama Sena's stilt houses, the Needle's Eye waited too—shifting, hungry, indifferent to money and names alike.

My father used to say the river keeps two ledgers: one for what men take, and one for what they owe. For twenty years I'd thought he died on the wrong side of both.

Tomorrow, I intended to introduce Mr. Odell Crane to the second ledger.

I eased the throttle forward a whisper, and the Wren began to move—upriver, into the dark, toward the trap I was building out of the only inheritance I'd ever wanted: the water itself, and everything it remembered.
