## 16. The Sandbar Trap

The trick to reading a river in flood, my father used to say, is remembering that the river is lying to you. The surface tells you one story—smooth water here, riffles there—and the truth lives underneath, in the shape of the bottom, in the way the current folds around a sandbar it built last week and will demolish tomorrow. Most pilots learn the river as a map. I learned it as a liar.

Which meant that when Odell Crane's armored launch came grinding up the Sable behind us, all chrome and diesel and bad intentions, I didn't see a warship. I saw a man about to trust a river that had never once told him the truth.

* * *

We'd left Mama Sena's village at gray dawn, the monsoon still two days out by the barometer but already announcing itself in the color of the sky—a bruised green-yellow that made the light look sick. Nkechi sat in the wheelhouse with a blanket around her shoulders and Crane's name in her mouth like a stone she kept turning over. Elias was below with the deed and her evidence, sealing them into waterproof wraps for the third time, because repetition is what people do when they're frightened and can't admit it.

I was doing what I always did when frightened. Counting.

"Sandbar off the left bank," I said. "New since the spring rise. See how the current kinks there? That's not a bend. That's the river going around something it doesn't want to touch."

Nkechi looked where I pointed and saw, I'm sure, nothing but brown water moving fast. "You can actually tell that from up here?"

"I grew up on this river." It came out easier than it would have a month ago. "My father taught me. He said every pilot has two choices: memorize the channel, or learn to read the river's face. Channels change. Faces don't."

"And which are you?"

"A reader." I checked the mirror Crane's men had rigged on our stern before the parley—or rather, the mirror I'd asked them to rig, because a captain who plays dumb should at least play it thoroughly. The launch was back there, maybe two miles down, holding our wake like a dog holds a scent. "And right now I'm reading that Crane draws too much water for what's ahead of us."

Elias came up the companionway, hair plastered flat, carrying tea he'd made strong enough to strip paint. "You said that during the parley. You said it like an insult."

"It wasn't an insult. It was arithmetic." I took the mug because refusing Elias's tea had stopped being worth the effort somewhere around the leaking hull. "His launch displaces better than four feet loaded. The channel above Crooked Mile shoals to three and a half this time of year—less if the storm pushes silt down from the tributaries. He can follow us so far. Then he either turns back or he grounds."

"So we outrun him," Nkechi said.

"No." I set the mug down. "Outrunning him gets us to the survey station with his guns behind us and a siege we might not survive. Outrunning him means we run forever, and I've done my years of running." I looked at the water sliding past, lying its smooth brown lie. "He follows us. We let him follow us. And then we take him somewhere the river keeps its real opinions."

Elias studied me over his own mug. "That sounds like a plan with a very narrow margin for error."

"It sounds like a plan with no margin at all," I said. "But the margin's mine, not his. That's the whole point."

* * *

Crooked Mile earned its name honestly. The Sable doubles back on itself there through a mile of country so flat you can stand on the bow and see your own wake curving away behind you like a question mark. Between the two legs of the double-back sits a braided reach—six channels splitting and rejoining around bars of sand and clay that rearrange themselves with every flood, like furniture moved by someone who hates you.

Every commercial pilot on the Sable gives Crooked Mile a wide berth. There's no marked channel, no soundings newer than a decade old, and the local traffic that does use it does so by eye and memory and prayer. My father ran it forty times a year. He taught me to run it when I was fourteen, standing behind me at the wheel with his hands loose at his sides, saying only, *You'll know when you've got it wrong.*

I got it wrong twice. The second time we spent six hours aground waiting for the tide, and he never raised his voice, just made me sit and watch the water until I could see what I'd failed to see. Then we went through again.

I hadn't run Crooked Mile in eleven years. But rivers keep their habits longer than people do, and I'd been reading the banks all morning—the cut lines, the drift piles, the color of the water where it shallowed over clean sand versus the olive murk where it dragged mud. The bars were where I remembered them being, give or take a boat-length. Rivers forgive. That's more than most things manage.

The plan, such as it was, fit in three sentences. Run the braided reach on the line only I could read. At the narrows past the second split, swing wide around the False Point—that was Papa's name for it, a bar that wore a respectable fringe of reeds and pretended to be solid ground—and drop back downstream into the slack water behind it, quiet as a held breath. Crane, following our wake at speed in weather that was about to turn filthy, would read the surface instead of the bottom. He'd cut the corner the way any sensible pilot would.

Any sensible pilot who didn't know that the False Point's reeds grew out of four feet of water over quicksand-soft silt.

"He'll have fathometers," Elias said, when I laid it out. "Depth sounders. Maybe charts."

"He'll have fathometers that read the bottom directly under his keel, which by the time they warn him is the same moment it's too late. And his charts—" I tapped the survey sheets Nkechi had salvaged, Crane's own expedition charts, "—were drawn by a team that came upriver in the dry season, when Crooked Mile was a different animal entirely. Look. They marked the main channel east of the False Point. In June, maybe, that was true. It isn't now. The spring rise moved everything west."

Nkechi frowned at the chart. "So his own maps will lie to him."

"His own maps will agree with the river's surface," I said. "Which is worse."

There was a silence in the wheelhouse, the particular silence of people doing math they don't like. Then Elias said, quietly, "And if he reads it correctly? If he slows down, takes soundings, comes through careful?"

"Then we've lost nothing. We come out the far side, he comes out the far side behind us, angrier and slower, and we make the survey station with an hour's lead instead of three." I shrugged. "But he won't. Men like Crane don't slow down for rivers. Rivers are beneath them. That's not a flaw in him—it's the handle."

"The handle?" Nkechi said.

"What you grab when you want to move a man." I turned back to the wheel. "Now batten everything down. When the storm hits mid-reach, I want this boat sealed like a drum."

* * *

It hit early, because storms in the Kessari Basin have no respect for schedules or plans, least of all mine.

The first gust came sideways across the bow an hour before noon, hard enough to lay the Wren over and put a shudder through her planking that I felt in my teeth—and felt, specifically, in the place aft where the patched hull had been complaining ever since the sandbar trap that cracked her the first time. Not a leak yet. A rumor of a leak. The Wren had developed a talent for ominous rumors.

"Hull's talking again," Elias reported from below, hand pressed to the planking like a doctor taking a pulse. "Same seam. It's holding, but it's holding angrily."

"Everything on this boat is angry," I called down. "Welcome to the family."

By the time we entered the braided reach, the rain was coming down in ropes and the wind had found its rhythm, gusting in long pulses that shoved the Wren's stern around like a drunk friend. Visibility dropped to a few hundred yards. The river rose under us even as we rode it—you could watch the banks shrink, watch debris run past in the current, whole trees traveling like commuters late for work.

Perfect conditions, in other words, for a man in a heavy launch to stop thinking and start chasing.

I ran the first split on instinct and memory, threading between two bars whose existence I confirmed less by seeing them than by seeing the water lie about them—the glassy tongue of current sliding over one, the faint boil below the other where the flow stacked up against something shallow. Behind us, half-glimpsed through the rain, Crane's launch held station a quarter mile back, big and black and steady, his searchlight sweeping the water ahead of him and finding nothing useful. Light doesn't read bottoms. It just makes the lies prettier.

"Still with us," Nkechi said, glued to the stern window.

"He will be." I eased the throttle up. "Right until the part where he isn't."

The second split was the commitment. Past it, the channel narrowed between the False Point and the western bank, and after that there was no graceful exit from the plan—only the swing, the drop into slack water, and the wait. I brought the Wren in a little faster than comfort allowed, because the plan needed Crane close enough to commit to the corner himself, and men judge distance by what they think they can catch.

The rain chose that moment to double its efforts. The world went white-gray and roaring. Somewhere aft, Elias appeared at the wheelhouse door, soaked, shouting something I couldn't hear over the drumming on the roof. I shook my head—*later*—and put both hands on the wheel.

False Point slid up on the left, a low green fringe of reeds bending in the wind, looking for all the world like firm ground wearing a reasonable disguise. Any pilot, any sane pilot, would give it the berth the chart suggested and cut the corner tight against it, because the deep water—according to every map Crane owned—ran along its edge.

I swung wide. Wide enough to look like error. Wide enough that a pursuing captain would mark the mistake and correct for it, the way confident men always correct for other people's mistakes.

Then, past the point, I hauled the wheel over, dropped the Wren into the eddy's slack water behind the bar, and killed the engine.

The silence was enormous. Rain on the cabin roof, wind in the reeds, the river running past us eight feet off the starboard beam—and underneath it all, the Wren settling into stillness like a animal lying down in tall grass.

"Now," I breathed, "we find out what kind of man Odell Crane is."

* * *

He came through the rain ninety seconds later, and I will say this for him: he came fast.

The launch burst out of the gray wall astern of us, searchlight stabbing, riding high and arrogant, and I watched through the salt-streaked glass as it bore down on the corner exactly the way I'd drawn it in my head—wide entry, cutting in toward the False Point, trusting the chart, trusting the surface, trusting the simple fact that the little riverboat ahead of him had clearly gone wide out of caution, and the safe water obviously lay inside her line.

The river disagreed.

I heard it before I saw it—a change in the launch's note, the diesel's confident growl suddenly loading up, laboring, the bow wave collapsing as the hull met bottom that rose to meet it faster than any fathometer could scream. The launch's bow lifted. Her stern squatted. She slewed half around with the momentum, plowing a brown furrow through four feet of water over silt that wanted nothing more than to hold onto her forever, and then she stopped.

Not gently. Stopped the way a train stops when it meets something it was not scheduled to meet. Her upperworks kept going for a heartbeat after her hull quit—crates shifting, something metal ringing off the deck—and then she settled into the sand with a groan I could feel through the Wren's deck plates, canted fifteen degrees to port, her propeller churning the river into chocolate behind her and going precisely nowhere.

For a long moment nobody in the wheelhouse said anything. Then Nkechi made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob. Elias said, "You grounded him. You grounded a gunboat with a sandbar."

"Not me." I let myself grin, and it felt strange on my face, like a coat I'd outgrown. "The river. I just introduced them."

Through the rain I could see figures on Crane's deck, small with distance, gesturing. A searchlight swept the water uselessly. Someone tried the engine again; the propeller clawed foam and accomplished nothing except announcing their position to every ear on the river. The False Point's reeds bent and hissed around a hundred tons of armored pride, and the storm leaned down harder, as if it had been waiting for the show to start.

"Three feet, four inches," I said softly, to no one, to Papa. "Told you."

* * *

Here is the thing about triumph that nobody puts in songs: it arrives with a bill attached.

I felt the first crack before I heard it—a shiver through the sole under my boots, wrong in a way that had nothing to do with the storm. Then the sound reached us, a report from below decks like a rifle shot, and Elias was moving before I finished turning.

"The seam," he shouted up the companionway. "It's opened. Yara, it's *opened*—we're taking water, fast!"

The next ten minutes were the ugliest work I've done on that boat, and I once unloaded a dead bull hippo by myself in July. The Wren's aft bilge, dry at our last check, had six inches of cold river in it and rising. The patch I'd set over the old crack—the one Elias had paid good money to help me timber, the one I'd told myself would hold until season's end—had peeled along half its length, and the flexing we'd given her in the swing, hard over at speed in a building sea, had done the rest.

We pumped. We stuffed. Elias held a plank over the breach while I drove wedges and tarred the seams with a brush that disintegrated in my hand, both of us braced against the roll, shouting over the storm, working by lantern light in a space the size of a coffin. Nkechi relieved us on the pump in shifts, small and tireless, bailing like the fate of everyone she'd lost depended on the rhythm. Maybe it did.

Somewhere in the middle of it, knee-deep in bilgewater, Elias looked at me across the breach and said—not shouting, which somehow carried anyway—"This is the second time this hull has nearly killed us since I came aboard."

"She's consistent," I gasped, driving another wedge. "I value that in a boat."

"You need a shipyard. Weeks on the hard, new planking, proper caulking—"

"I need her to float for two more days." I sat back on my heels, dripping, and made myself look at the gap in the planking, at the river pressing patiently through it, at the truth of what I'd done. I'd spent the Wren. Spent her the way you spend anything you love when the alternative is losing something you love more—her hull, her bones, whatever it took to put Crane's launch on a sandbar and buy the basin one honest chance. "Two days. Then she can fall apart in a dry dock with her name painted fresh and everyone telling her she's beautiful."

Elias held my eyes for a moment. Whatever he saw in my face, he didn't argue. He just put his palm flat against the strained plank, the way you'd steady a horse, and said, "Two days, then."

Above us, muffled by planking and rain, Crane's engine roared and roared, hauling at the sand, going nowhere.

* * *

We came up at dusk—if you could call it dusk, with the storm sitting on the sky like a lid—to assess the board.

Crane's launch lay where the river had decided she belonged, deeper aground than before, the falling barometer and the rising water conspiring to pin her with silt as the flood dumped its load. His crew had rigged a canvas amidships; lights burned in her windows; and along her rail, figures moved with the purposeful misery of men whose employer had promised them easy money and delivered a siege against geography.

"They'll try to kedge off," I said. "Anchor out, winch themselves free. Maybe tomorrow, if the water keeps rising. Rising water floats a lot of sins."

"And if it doesn't?" Nkechi asked.

"Then he walks." I watched the distant lights. "Or he sends men walking. Toward the survey station. Toward us." I turned to Elias. "How far to the station from here?"

"Half a day, in this. Less if the flooded forest lets us shortcut through." He hesitated. "Yara. The Wren can't make that run. Not with that seam. One more hard day and we sink in open water, in a storm, with everything we're carrying."

I knew it. Had known it since the crack, since the bilge, since the moment I'd chosen to spend her. But knowing a thing and hearing it aloud are different animals, and hearing it aloud hurt.

"I know," I said.

"So we go by water as far as she'll live, and then—"

"Then we go by foot, or by raft, or by swimming, if it comes to that." I looked at the wheelhouse—at the wheel my hands knew blind, at the chart table where Papa's pencil marks still ghosted under newer ink, at the tin cup Elias had left on the sill and Nkechi's blanket folded on the bench. Eleven years I'd kept the Wren at arm's length, the way I kept everything, ready to walk away. She'd repaid that distance by carrying me home anyway. "She got us this far. We'll get her as far as she goes, and we'll thank her for it."

Lightning walked the horizon, counting off seconds toward thunder. On Crane's stranded launch, a searchlight swung and steadied—pointing, I realized with a cold settling in my stomach, not at the river, but at the tree line. At the high dark country beyond it.

At the route to the survey station.

"He's figured out where we're going," Elias said quietly.

"No," I said, reaching for the throttle, watching the rain swallow the world. "He's figured out where we're going, and he's got boats' crews ashore, and we've got a dying hull and a head start measured in hours. So let's not waste them arguing."

I rang for slow ahead. The Wren answered—faithful, leaking, furious—and slid out from behind the False Point into the rising flood, while behind us the sandbar held its prisoner, and the storm came down like the end of the world, and somewhere ahead in the drowned forest, the last of my father's secrets waited on the high ground with room enough, I hoped, for all of us to live long enough to claim them.
