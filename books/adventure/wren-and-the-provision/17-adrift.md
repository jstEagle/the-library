## 17. Adrift

I have spent my professional life believing that a map is a promise. That if you draw the lines carefully enough, the world will hold its shape. Standing on the deck of the Wren as she went down by the bow in six feet of brown water, I understood, finally and completely, that this belief was a form of prayer — and that no god of rivers had ever answered mine.

The hull planking Crane's sandbar trap had cracked open was giving up its argument with the water. Yara had patched it twice since the grounding, once with canvas and pitch, once with a plank pried from her own cabin floor, and both times the river had accepted our offering with the patience of something that knew it would win eventually. Now it was winning. The pump Yara kept in the bilge — an ancient thing she called Petunia, because apparently even life-or-death machinery deserved a name — was churning out water at a rate the river found amusing.

"We have minutes," Yara said. Not panicked. Not calm either. She had a voice for arithmetic like this, flat and exact, the voice of a woman who had clearly done this math before on other boats, other nights, and had learned not to waste breath on anything but the numbers. "Maybe ten. Maybe less if we take weight wrong."

"Then we don't take weight wrong," Nkechi said.

She was already moving. I will say this about surveyors who survive being abandoned by their own expedition: they do not waste motion. Nkechi Ade went through the Wren's cabin like weather, pulling out the oilskin wraps Yara stored beneath the pilot's bench, shaking them open, laying them flat. "Deed first. My evidence second. Charts third."

"The charts," I started, "are replaceable—"

"Your charts are the only ones that show the channel past the third bend," Nkechi said, without looking up. "Crane's men don't have them. The villages don't have them. If you drown tonight, Elias Voss, the Kessari Basin goes back to being blank paper, and blank paper is exactly what men like Odell Crane want it to be."

I didn't argue after that. It is difficult to argue with someone who has just explained that your death has cartographic consequences.

* * *

Let me tell you what we saved, because the inventory matters more than any inventory I've ever taken.

We saved the deed. Forty years old, sealed in wax and oilcloth, signed by a government clerk whose name meant nothing to anyone except the four people on that sinking boat and the villages upstream who had never stopped waiting for it to surface. Yara wrapped it herself. Her hands were steady. I remember noticing that — how steady they were — and understanding that the steadiness cost her something enormous, the way a dam costs the river behind it.

We saved Nkechi's evidence: her field notebooks, the photographs of the camp ledger with Crane's forged requisitions, the carbon copies of orders signed by men who had smashed the radio and left their colleagues to the flood. She wrapped each item separately, the way you'd wrap a wound.

We saved my charts. All of them, including the ones I'd drawn in the last two weeks — the unmapped bends, the deep channels, the sandbar that had eaten Crane's launch still sitting fat and victorious under two feet of rising water where no chart in existence marked it except mine.

And then there was everything else. Yara's father's compass, which she held for a long moment before setting it down on the chart table, deliberately, like a woman closing a door. The spare engine parts. The tinned food. The blankets. The photograph I'd once glimpsed tucked behind the wheel — a young girl on a dock, squinting into sun, a tall man's hand on her shoulder — which Yara took, looked at, and put inside her shirt against her skin, because some things you save with your body or not at all.

"The Wren," I said, and couldn't finish it.

Yara put her palm flat against the bulkhead, the way you'd touch a dying animal. "She's taken worse than this," she said, which was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and neither of us said so, because there are lies you permit the people you love when the truth would only slow everyone down.

I want to be precise about that word, *love*, because I hadn't used it yet, even privately, and I'm not sure I used it then. What I know is that somewhere between the loading dock at Miles Not Years and this flooded forest at the end of the mapped world, the Wren had stopped being Yara's boat and become the only home either of us could name, and watching her go down felt like watching a house burn with all the photographs still inside.

* * *

Crane's men were wading ashore.

We heard them before we saw them — the slap-slap of boots in shin-deep water, the clink of gear, a voice carrying over the rain with the bored authority of someone being paid by the hour. Four of them, maybe five, coming off the grounded launch's skiff toward the strip of high ground where the Wren had run herself aground in her final act of loyalty, wedged between two cypress knees like a bird sheltering from a storm.

"They'll search the boat," Nkechi whispered.

"They'll find the boat," Yara corrected. "They won't find us."

Because we were already gone. Or almost gone. The last twenty yards of our retreat we made in water up to our chests, pushing through what had been, three days ago, dry forest and was now a drowned cathedral — cypress trunks rising black out of the flood, Spanish moss hanging in wet gray ropes, the whole world reduced to water and wood and the drumming of rain on leaves so thick overhead it fell on us only in occasional cold coins.

Yara led. Of course Yara led. She moved through the flooded timber the way she read water — by some sense that had nothing to do with eyesight, angling around submerged fences, deadfalls, the drowned skeleton of somebody's barn roof just breaking the surface. Behind her came Nkechi, one arm hooked through the strap of the evidence wrap, swimming the stretches too deep to wade with the economical strokes of a woman who had grown up on a different river and trusted none of them. I came last, holding the deed wrap above my head like an idiot, like a man carrying an umbrella in a hurricane, until Yara turned back and took it from me without comment and stowed it inside her jacket, against her chest, next to the photograph.

"I can carry it," I said.

"You can swim," she said. "One thing at a time."

* * *

We made our first landfall on the roof of a collapsed smokehouse, a triangle of tin and rot jutting eighteen inches above the flood. It held the three of us the way a dinner plate holds three cats — technically, uncomfortably, with constant renegotiation.

From there we watched the lights of Crane's men sweep across the Wren.

They reached her in the blue-white flicker of a storm lantern, and I watched a man climb aboard, and I watched the lantern light move through the cabin windows, and I thought about everything below decks going dark forever — the galley where Yara made coffee strong enough to strip varnish, the bunk where I'd slept my first week aboard listening to the river talk to itself, the chart table where I'd inked the third bend while Yara pretended not to watch over my shoulder and then, later, quietly corrected my soundings in pencil because I'd been two feet shallow on every line.

"They're taking the engine," Nkechi murmured.

"Let them." Yara's voice was very quiet. "It'll weigh them down."

But her hand had found the gunwale of the smokehouse roof, knuckles white, and I understood that *let them* was doing the same kind of work as *she's taken worse than this*. There is a category of sentence that exists entirely to keep a person upright, and Yara Hartwell spoke that language more fluently than anyone I've ever met.

A shout from the boat. Then another. Then the lantern swung toward the treeline, toward us, and a voice called out — not angry, almost friendly, the way a trap is friendly. "Captain Hartwell! We know you're out here! Mr. Crane's offer stands! Triple what the contract paid! Nobody needs to drown tonight!"

Nobody needs to drown tonight. As though drowning were a service Crane was reluctantly providing, and could call off if the price was right.

Yara didn't answer. I could feel her breathing beside me, slow and deliberate, the breathing of a woman counting something — miles, seconds, heartbeats, I never learned which. Nkechi pressed herself flat against the tin. And I lay on my back on a collapsing smokehouse in the middle of a monsoon, staring up through the canopy at rain that couldn't reach me, and did the only useful thing I know how to do.

I mapped.

Not on paper. In my head. The flooded forest, the direction of the current — still running hard toward the basin's interior, which meant the flood crest hadn't passed yet, which meant the water would keep rising. The cypress stand to the north, denser, the trunks closer together, which meant shallower footing between them once we got there. And east, beyond the drowned fields, a rise. A real rise. I'd charted its contour from the Wren's deck two days ago and dismissed it as insignificant — a hill is insignificant, when your whole world is a river — and now it was the only significant thing in the universe.

"There," I breathed, and put my mouth against Yara's ear so the words wouldn't travel. "Northeast. Half a mile. High ground. I charted it — forty feet above flood stage, maybe more. Old clearing. There was a structure on it — I marked it as ruins."

Yara was silent a moment. Then, so softly I felt it more than heard it: "The old survey station."

"You know it?"

"It's my father's." Something moved through her voice that I had no chart for. "He built it. Before I was born. He told me—" She stopped. Started again. "He told me stories about it. I always thought they were just stories."

* * *

We moved at the next lightning flash and froze at the next, three ghosts crossing a drowned world, and I will not pretend it was brave or graceful. It was miserable. The water went from waist-deep to chest-deep to a stretch where Nkechi and I swam and Yara — small, tireless, impossible Yara — swam dragging both evidence wraps lashed to her back, refusing help with a look that ended the discussion before it began.

Twice we heard Crane's men crashing through the timber behind us, calling out offers and threats in alternating verses, like a hymn with bad lyrics. Once a shot went off — wild, into the dark, a punctuation mark rather than a sentence — and we froze with our chins above water among the cypress knees until the searching light slid past.

At some point in that crossing, Nkechi's hand found mine in the dark. Not romance. Ballast. She gripped my wrist and I gripped hers and we towed each other through a hundred yards of black water, and I thought: this is what crew means. Not a contract. Not a wage. Just two people agreeing, without discussion, that neither drowns alone.

I had spent thirty-nine years believing I traveled light. One case, one kit, one life that fit in a satchel. Lying in the mud at the base of the hill while the rain tried to bury us, gasping like landed fish, I understood that I had never traveled light at all. I had simply never stayed anywhere long enough to find out what I was carrying.

What I was carrying was a woman who measured her life in miles because miles couldn't leave her the way people did. A surveyor who had watched her colleagues' boat disappear around a bend and survived anyway. A deed worth more than any of us, and less than nothing to the man willing to kill for its absence.

Everything else was at the bottom of the river. The engine. The galley. The bunk. The Wren herself, settled into the shallows like a tired bird, her name already blurring under the silt.

And it should have broken me. By every law of despair I knew, it should have broken me.

Instead, lying in the mud with the deed safe against Yara's chest and the hill rising black and solid ahead of us, I felt something I can only describe as the opposite of loss. Because for the first time since I'd drawn my first map at nine years old, everything I valued was in one place, and the place was *here*, and the place was *alive*, and the place was climbing.

* * *

We gained the tree line as the storm reached some new register of fury, the sky splitting open overhead in sheets of white. And there it was, half-swallowed by forty years of vine and rot: the structure I'd marked as ruins.

Stone foundation. Timber walls gone silver. A steep roof that had mostly held. And above the door, carved into a lintel board whose letters the weather had softened but not erased, four words that stopped Yara Hartwell mid-stride as though she'd walked into glass:

**HARTWELL — RIVER & LAND SURVEYS**

She stood in the rain and read it. Read it again. Her hand came up and touched the letters the way you'd touch a face in a photograph.

"Elias," she said, and her voice was strange, stripped of everything wry and armored, down to something young. "He wasn't hiding from them. He was *keeping* something for them. For all of them. This whole time."

Behind us, down in the drowned dark, a lantern light bobbed among the trees. Crane's men had found the base of the hill.

Ahead of us, behind that silver-weathered door, whatever her father had spent his whole disgraced life protecting was waiting to be found.

Yara wiped the rain from her face, set her shoulder against the door, and pushed.
