## 18. The High Ground

The hill rose out of the flooded forest like a promise nobody had kept.

We'd been wading since dawn—Elias in front with the deed wrapped against his chest like a newborn, Nkechi behind him favoring the leg Crane's men had nearly taken off with their boat propeller, and me at the rear with a machete and a grudge. The water was chest-deep in the hollows and knee-deep everywhere else, brown and warm and full of things I preferred not to think about. Somewhere behind us, through the rain, we could still hear the launch's engine coughing as Crane's men tried to free it from my sandbar.

"Tell me again," Nkechi said, "why we're climbing toward the people hunting us instead of away from them."

"Because they can't climb," I said. "Their boat draws four feet. The channel ahead of them draws two on a good day, and today is not a good day. They'll have to come on foot, slow, through country that wants to eat them." I hacked through a curtain of wet vines. "And because there's something up here I need to see."

"You said that about the last three hills."

"The last three hills were lies. This one's true."

It was true because I'd known it before I knew anything else. My father used to trace it on the table with his finger when I was small enough to sit cross-legged on his knee: *the survey station, little bird, on the bald knob above the second fork. Highest ground between here and the coast. If anything ever happens to me—* and then he'd stop, the way he always stopped, and pour another drink instead of finishing the sentence.

The bald knob was exactly where his finger had said it would be. Of course it was. My father had never been wrong about the river. Only about people.

The station squatted under forty years of strangler fig, a low stone building with an iron roof gone the color of old blood. The door was swollen shut. Elias and I hit it together, shoulder to shoulder, three times before it gave, and the smell that came out was dust and paper and camphor and time.

Then I got the lantern lit, and I stopped breathing for a second.

Shelves. Floor to ceiling. Charts rolled in oilcloth and tied with waxed string, ledgers stacked in soldier rows, a drafting table under a canvas sheet with instruments laid out neat as surgery. My father had built himself a library at the top of the world and told no one, and every inch of it had waited for him to come back, and he never had, and here was his daughter standing in the doorway with river water running out of her boots.

"Yara," Elias said quietly. "What is this place?"

"It's mine," I said. And then, because the words came easier than I expected and scared me more than the men behind us: "My name is Yara Hartwell. This was my father's station. I'm done pretending otherwise."

Nkechi looked at Elias. Elias looked at me. Rain hammered the iron roof like applause.

"Well," Nkechi said, lowering herself onto a crate with a grunt. "About damn time somebody said it out loud."

* * *

We spent the afternoon making the station ready to be hated.

There was a well behind the building, still sweet. There were shutters that could be barred from inside, and arrow slits—or whatever you call the gaps a paranoid surveyor leaves between his stones—that commanded all four approaches. My father had built this place to be defended by one man against the whole greedy world, and he'd built it well, because he'd known exactly what kind of world it was.

I found the archive within the first hour, because he'd wanted it found. A false drawer in the drafting table, released by pressing the brass compass mount, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth inside tin inside more oilcloth: the deed itself, heavy paper, seals intact, *Hartwell* written across the riverlands in ink that hadn't faded because it had never seen daylight. Beneath it, correspondence. Forty years of correspondence.

"Elias," I said. "Come look at this."

He read over my shoulder while I turned pages with hands I tried to keep steady. Letters from land companies. Offers, then threats, then legal notices. And then—the part that made my blood go cold and hot at once—a folder of telegrams, recent, crisp, addressed to a name I didn't know but Nkechi did.

"That's Crane's man," she said, leaning in. "Okafor. He ran Crane's logistics out of Port Sable. These are from this spring."

The telegrams told a simple story. Crane had learned the deed survived. Crane had funded an expedition to find it—not to claim it, not to honor it, but to destroy it, because a valid Hartwell deed made every acre he'd optioned in the basin legally worthless, and Odell Crane did not buy worthless acres. The final telegram, dated eleven days ago, was one line:

*BURN ON RECOVERY. LEAVE NO WITNESSES. PAYMENT ON CONFIRMATION.*

"He didn't lose his expedition," Elias said slowly. "He spent it."

"He spent it," I agreed, "and then he burned what was left so no one could testify otherwise. Except Nkechi floated."

"And except you two showed up asking questions," Nkechi said, "and except that deed is sitting on this table right now, drier than sin."

Outside, the light was failing and the barometer—I'd brought my father's barometer down from its nail, because some inheritances are practical—had fallen so far it looked sick. The storm wasn't coming anymore. It had arrived, and it meant to stay the night, and so did Crane's men.

We heard the first of them at dusk.

* * *

A siege, it turns out, is mostly waiting punctuated by terror.

They came up the south slope first, six or seven of them, and announced themselves with a rifle shot that spanged off the stonework above the arrow slit where I was watching. I put two rounds back through the gap without aiming at anybody—warning shots, though I'll admit the second one was closer to a warning than the first—and they pulled back into the treeline, muttering.

"They're testing us," Elias said. He'd stationed himself at the east slit with Nkechi's rifle, which he held the way a man holds a cat he's been asked to babysit. "Seeing if we shoot back. Seeing how many of us there are."

"There are three of us and they know it. They watched us wade in."

"Then they're testing our ammunition."

"We have enough," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and let it stand anyway, which is its own kind of kindness.

The rain came down in ropes. The lightning came close enough to taste. Between flashes, the darkness outside was total, and somewhere in it were armed men who'd already left one expedition dead in the water, and I stood at my father's slit in my father's wall with my father's rifle and thought: *he stood here too. Or meant to. All those years, ready for a night like this, and it came thirty years late and found his daughter instead.*

Around midnight they tried the north side, quiet, no shots, just boots in mud—and they might have made it if Nkechi hadn't heard the dogs. Not real dogs. Crane's men imitating dogs badly, a trick to cover their approach, and Nkechi, who grew up in basin country, laughed out loud at how bad it was and put a shot through the shutter seam where the sound came from. Somebody yelped. They withdrew again.

After that, the long hours. Two a.m., three a.m. Elias dozed sitting up against the shelf wall with the deed case strapped to his chest even in sleep. Nkechi cleaned her leg wound and pronounced it ugly but honest. I walked the walls.

At some point Elias woke and came to stand beside me at the south slit, and for a while neither of us said anything, which is the conversation I'm best at.

"You could have stayed a stranger," he said finally. "You could have burned that deed in the first hour and we'd be downstream by now."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

I watched lightning walk along the ridge across the valley. "Because I stood in front of those shelves and realized my father spent his whole life being called a thief for something he didn't do, and I spent my whole life running from a name that was never guilty. And burning the deed wouldn't have killed either lie. It would've just made sure nobody ever dug them up." I shrugged. "Turns out I'd rather be hunted as a Hartwell than safe as nobody."

He nodded slowly. "For what it's worth, I've been mapping rivers for eleven years to avoid going home to a family that thinks I failed them. So I understand the arithmetic. Even if I don't agree with it."

"What don't you agree with?"

"That safety and truth are opposites." He tapped the case against his chest. "My family thought I failed because I chose maps over the business. But the maps were the true thing. Everything else was the failure. You don't fix a lie by hiding from it, Yara. You fix it by putting it on the chart where everyone can read it."

"Easy to say at four in the morning behind good walls."

"I've said it in worse places."

Another flash. In the white instant, I saw them at the treeline—four figures, maybe five, moving low and fast toward the northeast corner, carrying something long and dark between them.

"Battering ram," I breathed. "Northeast corner. GO!"

We went. The ram hit the door twice before we got there, and the third blow cracked a lintel stone, and Nkechi fired through the slit above it and someone screamed, and then the night tore open.

Not gunfire. Water.

I felt it before I understood it—a shudder through the floor stones, deep, seismic, the hill itself flinching. Then a sound like the sky unzipping, rolling up the valley from the direction of the lower river, and every man outside stopped moving at once, and we all listened together to the thing we'd been outrunning for two days finally catching up.

"The levee," Nkechi whispered. She'd lived her whole life below that levee. Her face in the lantern light was terrible. "The old levee at the fork. It's gone."

The flood came up the valley like a tide with a grudge. We couldn't see it—we could only hear it, a roar that swallowed the rain, and feel it in the stones under our feet, rising, patient, enormous. Crane's men broke first. I watched them scatter from the treeline, dropping the ram, sprinting for high ground that was getting smaller by the minute, and the flood took the lowest of them before they'd run fifty yards. I heard it happen. I will hear it for the rest of my life.

And then, through the rain, a new sound: a launch engine, screaming, wide open, driven by a man who had just understood that his armored boat was now a coffin with a motor, tearing through black water toward the only hill in the valley.

Toward us.

"He's coming here," Elias said.

"Of course he is." I checked the rifle. "Everyone wants what's on this hill tonight."

The engine note climbed, desperate, and then changed—dropped, stuttered, caught something. Debris. A fence line. A drowned grove. I pictured the channel the way I'd mapped it in my head all my life, the way my father had taught me: the submerged orchard wall off the station's west slope, the one thing in the floodwater that doesn't move.

The engine died mid-scream.

In the silence after, over the rain and the roar of the flood, I heard voices—Crane's men, in the water, shouting. And one voice cutting through the others, high and furious and afraid, giving orders nobody could follow anymore.

The river had them all now. The river had us all.

"Hold the walls," I said. "Whatever washes up on this hill tonight—we hold the walls."

The first knock, when it came, wasn't a battering ram.

It was a drowning man, begging.
