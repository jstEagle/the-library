## 19. What the Flood Takes

**Yara**

The river told us before the sky did.

I'd grown up learning its moods the way other children learn their mothers' faces — the way the current went glassy and wrong before it turned, the smell of silt rising like a held breath, the birds lifting off the water all at once without any reason a sane person could name. The Kessari had been muttering all night. Now it went quiet, and quiet, on a river, is never peace. It's a man clearing his throat.

"It's coming," I said, and Mama Sena didn't argue. She'd already sent the children up to the survey station an hour ago, herding them along the flooded causeway with the calm of a woman who had done this arithmetic before and gotten the same answer every time: the levee at the old bend would not hold, and the village below it would become a brown sea with rooftops for islands.

Elias stood beside me on the station's porch, the deed wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to his chest like a second heart. "How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." I looked downriver, toward the shallows where Crane's armored launch sat grounded like a beached whale with opinions, its running lights stuttering in the rain. "And him."

Crane's men had been wading the flooded timber since dark, trying to reach us. Two had turned back when the water went from knee to waist. One hadn't turned back at all, and I'd stopped counting him among the living somewhere around midnight.

Then the levee spoke.

It wasn't a crash. I want to say it was, because that's what people expect — a wall of water, a thunderclap, something cinematic. But what I heard was a long, groaning sigh, like an old man lying down for the last time, and then the river simply *arrived*, all of it at once, the way a debt arrives after years of pretending you'd forgotten it.

The water came through the trees in a brown wall maybe four feet high, and it did not roar so much as it *took* — took the causeway, took the fishing racks, took a canoe and a chicken coop and half a memory of a dock I'd tied the Wren to in another life. The survey station shuddered on its pilings and held. Papa had built it on high ground for a reason. Papa had built everything for a reason, and I was only now, in the dark and the roar, beginning to read the blueprint.

"Inside!" Mama Sena shouted, and we went, and the world outside became a single sound.

* * *

**Elias**

I have spent my life believing that maps are a form of control — that if you can draw a place, you own it in some small way, you've made it behave. The flood cured me of that in about ninety seconds.

From the station's high window I watched the Kessari do what it had always done, before anyone drew a line on paper. It took the channel it wanted. It took the low ground it had been owed for a hundred years. And it took, with a terrible indifference, the armored launch.

I saw it happen. The water lifted Crane's boat off the sandbar where Yara had stranded it — a gift of buoyancy from the very flood he'd been waiting out — and for one moment it seemed he might actually escape, engine coughing, bow swinging downriver toward the open basin. Then the current took the boat broadside and walked it into a stand of drowned trees, and the sound of it coming apart was like furniture breaking in a house where someone is very angry.

Men went into the water. I counted three heads, then two, then — in the lightning flash — a figure clinging to the hull's remains, being carried toward the flooded village, toward the rooftops where the last families who hadn't made the station were sheltering.

"Crane?" Yara asked, beside me at the glass.

"No. One of his hired men. The young one." I'd seen him at the parley, standing at Crane's shoulder with the particular stiffness of a man who has just realized he is standing on the wrong side of history. "The others are gone."

Yara was already moving for the door. I caught her arm and received, in return, a look I imagine she'd once used on deckhands half again my size.

"The water will kill you," I said.

"The water might. But if I stand here and watch a man drown because he picked the wrong paycheck, then I'm just Crane with better manners and a smaller boat." She pulled free. "Rope. Now."

* * *

**Yara**

Here is what nobody tells you about floodwater: it is not water. It is the whole river wearing the land as a coat. You don't swim in it; you negotiate with it.

I went in off the causeway stanchion with a line around my waist, Elias paying it out behind me, and the current hit me like a debt collector. It didn't want to drown me, particularly. It just wanted me somewhere else, immediately, and it had the strength of an entire watershed to insist.

I caught the man on the third try. He was maybe twenty, maybe twenty-two, blue-lipped and clawing at a shattered plank of Crane's launch as if it were his mother's skirt. When I got an arm around him he fought me — of course he fought me; drowning men fight everything, including rescue — and we went under together, and the river took the opportunity to show us both who was boss.

I have nearly drowned twice in my life, and both times the same thought arrived, unhelpful and clear as a bell: *so this is the mile that doesn't get counted.* The first time I was nine and my father pulled me out and laughed at me and then held me so hard I heard his heart. This time there was no one to pull me out except me, and the rope, and a cartographer on the end of it who I'd known eleven days and trusted with my life, which is either madness or the fastest friendship of my career.

We surfaced. I got him to the stanchion. Elias and two of Mama Sena's grandsons hauled us in hand over hand, and I came over the causeway rail coughing half the Kessari out of my lungs while the boy I'd saved lay on the planks beside me, shaking.

"You're one of Crane's," I said, when I could talk.

He nodded. Couldn't speak yet. His eyes were doing something I recognized — the look of a man whose ledger has just been read aloud to him.

"Then you'd better start being useful," I said, "because the river's already settled your account, and you came out of it owing."

* * *

**Nkechi**

I had seen Odell Crane's men do terrible things at the camp, and I had told myself they were simply men who followed money, the way rivers follow the fall of the land. No malice in it. Just gravity.

But I watched that boy — his name was Tam, I learned later, Tam Osei, twenty years old, three months on Crane's payroll — and I watched Yara Hartwell, who had every reason on this earth to let the river keep him, go into that brown flood after him anyway. And I understood that the difference between the people on this station and the people on that launch was not money. It was what they believed a person was *for*.

Tam sat wrapped in blankets, coughing, and when Mama Sena put a bowl of broth in his hands he wept into it like a child. Nobody told him to stop. That was its own kind of verdict, I think. The village had already tried him, and the sentence was soup.

"Crane's gone," Elias said quietly, at the window. "The launch broke up against the trees. I saw three men in the water. We got one."

Yara came to stand beside him. Rain sheeted down the glass. For a long time none of us said anything, and the river filled the silence with the sound of everything it was taking.

"He wouldn't have come for us," Yara said finally. "If the positions were turned. He'd have negotiated."

"Yes," Elias said.

"And you're going to tell me that's why it matters that we came for his man."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were thinking it very loudly."

Elias smiled — a small, tired thing, the first I'd seen from him in hours. "The deed's safe," he said. "The evidence is safe. The village is on high ground. And the man who tried to erase a family's name for profit has been taken by the river his profit was built on." He paused. "If that's not an account settled, I don't know what my profession is for."

* * *

**Tam**

I am going to write this down because Nkechi Ade says testimony is the only thing that outlives a flood, and because I cannot sleep, and because if I do not put it somewhere outside my own head I will keep hearing it there.

I was on the launch when the levee went. Mr. Crane stood at the rail with his coat on and his collar up, and he watched the water come through the trees, and he did not look afraid. That is the thing I will remember longest — not the boat breaking, not the cold. The not-afraid. He turned to us and said, "There's always another river," like he was talking about a business opportunity.

Then the trees took the hull and the water took the rest, and I was in the dark with the whole sky trying to get into my mouth.

The woman who pulled me out is the one whose family Mr. Crane sent us upriver to ruin. I know that now. I helped carry the records off that camp. I held the radio while Mr. Fenn smashed it. I told myself it was just paper, just a job, just following the fall of the land.

The river didn't care whose side I was on. It nearly took me the same as it took him.

But she came in after me anyway.

I don't know what you do with that. I don't know what you do with a woman who owes you nothing, whose father's name your employer buried, who swims into a flood to drag you out of it, and then hands you broth and tells you to be useful. I know that when she said it, something in my chest unclenched for the first time since we left the coast.

I will testify. Nkechi asked me tonight, straight out, no softness in it, and I said yes before she finished the sentence. Mr. Crane's ledgers — the ones we hauled off the camp, the ones I was told to burn and didn't, God forgive me, because I couldn't make myself do that one small thing — they're in my pack. They survived the water. Some things float.

* * *

**Yara**

Dawn came gray and dripping, and the river began, slowly, to remember its banks.

The village below was a wreckage of rooftops and floating debris, but the people were alive — all of them, every soul Mama Sena had counted onto the causeway, and when she went down to the waterline at first light, they came to her the way water comes to a low place. I watched from the station porch, still wringing the river out of my clothes, and I saw her take their hands and count them again, aloud, and I understood that I was watching a woman balance her own ledger, and that it came out even.

Elias found me there with two cups of something hot and terrible.

"Crane?" he asked.

"Gone." I said it flat, because there was no other way to say it. The river had taken him and his launch and his other two men, and the Kessari does not return what it collects. "You know what gets me? He had a boat. He had a fast boat and a full tank, and he still lost. Because he never once asked the river for directions."

Elias huffed something that was almost a laugh. "And you did?"

"I grew up with it. It's the difference between a guest and a trespasser." I took the cup. Drank. It was terrible. It was the best thing I'd ever tasted. "There'll be questions now. Downriver. Magistrates, newspapers, men in clean coats wanting to know how an armored launch came to be in the Kessari Basin at all. Nkechi's testimony. The ledgers. Tam's." I looked at him. "Mine."

"Yours," he agreed. He didn't say *you could still burn it*. He'd learned that much about me in eleven days, which was eleven days more than most people had ever managed.

Below us, Mama Sena was pointing survivors toward the station, toward the dry rooms and the stores Papa had stocked against exactly this kind of morning, thirty years before I was born to need them. The archive had held. The deed had held. Everything Crane had come to destroy had spent the night on high ground, dry as a sermon.

"Last night," Elias said, "you went into that water for a man who was sent here to erase you."

"He was sent here to erase a name. He was just a boy standing in the rain." I shrugged, and my shoulder ached from the rope, and I found I didn't mind the ache. It was an honest ache. Earned. "Besides — the river settles its own accounts. I just do the rescues."

He looked at me a moment too long, the way he did when he was deciding whether a thing was safe to say. Then: "The flood's going down. In a day, maybe two, we can get a raft together. Nkechi needs a doctor. The ledgers need a magistrate." He paused. "And the basin needs to hear its own name said out loud. By a Hartwell."

I looked downriver, where the water was already beginning to fall, already revealing the sandbars I'd spent my whole life counting, one by one, like a woman counting her way out of somewhere.

For the first time I could remember, I caught myself counting them the other direction. Not miles away.

Miles back.
