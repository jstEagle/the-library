## 20. Afterwater

I woke to the sound of water dripping off leaves, which is the Kessari Basin's idea of silence.

For a while I didn't move. I lay on the survey station's floor with my back against a wall that my father had probably leaned against forty years ago, and I listened to the river doing its morning accounting somewhere below us in the drowned forest. The storm had spent itself sometime before dawn — I remembered it going out like a lamp, not all at once but dimming, dimming, until the rain was just a whisper and then just a memory and then just the drip, drip, drip of a world wringing itself out.

My hands hurt. That seemed important, so I looked at them. Split knuckles, a rope burn across both palms from the night Crane's men had come up the hill and we'd held the door with furniture and fury, and under all of it the deep ache you get from paddling until your shoulders forget they're attached to anything. I flexed them once, twice. They worked. In my experience that put them ahead of most things after a flood.

Elias was asleep sitting up, which shouldn't be possible and yet there he was, chin on chest, his precious journal still open in his lap, a pencil loose in his fingers like he'd died mid-sentence. Nkechi was curled in the corner with one of Crane's hired men's jackets over her — the man himself, Dossa, sat across the room watching her sleep with an expression I couldn't read and didn't much want to yet. Mama Sena's grandson Tam was already at the window, because boys wake up before disasters finish being over, and he turned to me with the particular gravity of a child about to deliver news.

"The water's going down," he said. "You can see the roof of the chapel."

I got up. Everything creaked — me, the floor, the old station around us, settling into the new shape of the world like an old dog finding its spot by the fire.

* * *

From the survey station's high porch, the basin looked like someone had taken a map and spilled tea on it.

The river was everywhere and nowhere. Where yesterday there had been channels and banks and a village called Sena's Landing, this morning there was just brown-gold water stretching to the tree line, studded with rooftops and the tops of palms and, here and there, the sad white belly of something dead turning slowly in the current. The chapel roof Tam had mentioned poked up maybe two feet, its cross bent sideways like a man shrugging. A canoe floated past upside down, unhurried, as if it had somewhere better to be.

But the water *was* dropping. You could see it on the station's foundation posts — a clean line above the mud line, an inch of pale wood where the flood had licked and retreated. An inch since dawn, Tam said. The river taking back what it had borrowed.

"River's settling its accounts," I said, mostly to myself.

"What?" Elias said behind me, awake now, pencil still in hand like a man who'd been drawing in his sleep.

"Nothing. Old thing my father used to say." And for the first time in my life, saying it didn't taste like ash. It tasted like something I might keep.

We counted survivors the way you count money you're afraid isn't all there. From the station: me, Elias, Nkechi, Dossa, Tam, and old Mama Sena, who had climbed the hill the night before on legs that had no business agreeing to it, and who was now on the porch demanding someone bring her coffee as if she hadn't been through the worst night in the basin's living memory. From the village below: eleven souls who'd made it to high ground in canoes and on rafts of lashed bamboo, including a fisherman named Okon who had spent the night standing chest-deep in the flooded church, holding three children above the waterline on the pulpit. He told it flatly, without drama, the way people tell the truest stories.

From Crane's side: four men. Two had gone into the water when the levee let go, and no one had seen them since. One — a big quiet fellow called Brannig — had been pulled out half-drowned by Yara Hartwell, captain of the Wren, who would like the record to show she did it without thinking, which is either heroism or a character flaw, depending on the day. And the last was Odell Crane himself, who had refused the rope Okon threw him, refused the canoe, refused everything, and gone downstream clinging to a section of his own armored launch's hull.

"He's alive," Dossa said quietly. He'd come out onto the porch, and he stood apart from us the way a man does when he knows he doesn't have a place in the picture yet. "I saw him catch the current past the second bend. His boat's hull floats. Barely."

"Then the river hasn't finished with him," Mama Sena said, sipping her coffee. She said it the way you'd say *the mail comes at noon*. No venom in it. Just weather.

* * *

We built the raft because the Wren was gone.

I'll say that plainly, because saying it plainly is the only way I've found to say it at all: the Wren was gone. We'd left her sinking at the sandbar trap, cracked along the rotten planking I'd never had the money to fix properly, holed twice more in the grounding, and by the time the levee broke she'd been a shape under brown water with her nameplate catching the last light. Twenty-two years I'd run that boat. I'd slept more nights on her deck than in any bed. I'd named her after nothing in particular, or so I'd thought, and then found my father's survey notes in the station archive and learned that the first boat he ever ran the Sable in had been called the Wren too, and I'd sat down on the floor of that archive and cried like the river outside wasn't already doing enough of it.

So: the raft. Elias designed it, because of course he did — the man cannot walk ten feet without measuring something — and the village men built it, because of course they did; you don't survive generations in the Kessari Basin without knowing how to make a boat out of whatever the flood leaves you. Bamboo bundles lashed with vine and salvaged rope, a platform of split planks pried from the chapel's collapsed annex — Mama Sena gave permission with a wave of her hand and a look that dared anyone to object — and two long steering oars carved that afternoon from a falled palm trunk. Crude. Ugly. Buoyant as a promise.

We loaded it light. Waterproof wraps, the ones we'd carried out of the sinking Wren through the flooded forest: the Hartwell deed, dry as scripture. Nkechi's survey records, the ones proving Crane had falsified the expedition's findings and ordered the camp stripped. My father's archive journals, wrapped in oilcloth. Elias's charts, warped at the edges but legible, every page annotated now in his small precise hand with corrections and depths and, in the margins, little notes I pretended not to read. *Yara says the channel shifts after the third island.* *Yara knew this bar existed before I mapped it.* *Ask Yara.*

He was going to redraw the maps. He'd told me that on the porch at first light, shy about it, which is a strange thing to see on a grown man holding a pencil like a sword. "The old charts are wrong," he said. "They've always been wrong — drawn from boats that never talked to the people who live here. I'm going to do it properly. Every bend, every bar, every village. With the deed boundaries marked." He paused. "With the Hartwell name where it belongs."

"You don't have to do that," I said, which is what I say instead of thank you, and he nodded like he understood the translation perfectly.

Nkechi stood at the raft's edge testing a lash point, and when she spoke she was looking downriver, toward the city, toward tribunals and telegraph offices and whatever machinery turns truth into consequences. "When we get to Harrow's Bend, I'm wiring the Survey Commission. All of it. Crane's orders, the falsified logs, the men he left at the camp." Her voice was steady, but her hands weren't, and I noticed she kept them busy so nobody else would notice. "Four people died at that camp, Captain. Maybe five. The paper trail ends with me."

"It won't end with you," I said. "It'll end with him."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way surveyors look at terrain they intend to cross. "You could stay anonymous," she said. "Burn it all, disappear up some other river. That was the plan, wasn't it? Before."

Before. Before the silent camp and the case full of my family's name, before the flood and the siege and the levee breaking like the sky cracking open, before I stood on my father's high ground in the worst night of the storm and heard myself say, out loud, to armed men in the dark: *This is Hartwell land, and you are trespassing on it.* Before belonging stopped being a thing I fled from and became a thing I'd bled for.

"That plan drowned," I said.

* * *

We launched at mid-morning, fourteen of us on a raft built for eight, riding low and slow down a river that was still deciding what shape it wanted to be.

Paddling the flooded Kessari is a strange kind of navigation. The banks are gone, so the banks are everywhere — you steer by treetops, by the angle of the current combing through the crowns of submerged palms, by the color of the water where a channel runs deep and clear versus shallow and tea-brown. Half the time I couldn't tell where the river ended and the country began, and I found I didn't mind. There was something honest about it. All those lines on Elias's maps, all those firm black borders between water and land, and the monsoon had come along and erased them in a night, the way it erases everything eventually, the way it had erased my father's reputation and my mother's grave-marker and, for twenty years, my own name.

Except it hadn't, had it. That was the thing I kept turning over as I steered, trading the big steering oar with Okon hour by hour while my shoulders screamed. The flood takes houses and boats and men like Odell Crane, but it doesn't take what people remember. Mama Sena remembered a young Hartwell who'd paid fair price for the deed and then hidden it, worn the word *thief* like a coat so the speculators would never come sniffing after the villages. Okon remembered Hartwell grain in the hungry years, sold at cost, no questions. Tam's grandmother remembered a song. A *song*. Somewhere in that drowned village was a song about my family, and I hadn't known, and my father had died not knowing, and the shame we'd both carried had been carrying us somewhere else entirely.

Grief and relief, I discovered, feel almost identical in the body. Same tightness in the chest. Same burning behind the eyes. The only difference is what you're grieving and what you're relieved about, and after twenty years I'd gotten so good at the grief part that the relief felt like drowning in reverse.

Around midday we passed the Wren.

Or what the river had left of her. She lay heeled over on the old sandbar trap, half-buried in new silt, her stern above water like a whale taking air. Someone — Brannig, I think, though none of us claimed it — had lashed a scrap of tarpaulin over her wheelhouse, a small useless kindness. I made the raft swing wide so we wouldn't foul on her, and I looked at her longer than was practical, and Elias, without a word, took the oar from me and steered for a while.

"I can rebuild her," he said finally. Not *we*, not *someone*. *I*. The man who'd bought replacement timber for a stranger's rotting hull at Dock Nine because he couldn't stand to watch a good boat die of poverty. "Village timber. There's sawyers at Sena's Landing — well, there will be, once there's a Sena's Landing again."

"She's buried in a sandbar in the middle of nowhere."

"So am I, professionally speaking," he said, and I laughed, which surprised us both, and the laugh went down the raft like wind through reeds until even Nkechi smiled and Dossa, sitting at the far end with his guilt and his jacket, almost did.

* * *

We camped that night on a rise that had been a churchyard and was now an island, and the strangest thing happened, which is that nothing happened.

No storm. No siege. No radio crackling with threats. Just fourteen exhausted people sharing smoked fish and hardtack, a fire built on a graveled mound above the waterline, and the biggest sky I'd ever seen coming out star by star over the basin like the river had washed the whole world clean and hung it up to dry. Tam fell asleep against Mama Sena mid-story. Okon taught Elias a fishing song with approximately nine hundred verses. Dossa sat at the edge of the firelight until Nkechi, of all people, moved over and made room, and whatever passed between them stayed between them, but after that he was inside the circle and not outside it, and I decided that was enough deciding for one day.

I walked to the edge of the island and looked downriver. Somewhere down there, Odell Crane was floating on a piece of his own empire, headed for the same tribunal we were. Somewhere beyond that, Harrow's Bend, the wire office, the Survey Commission, the whole slow machinery of justice that grinds fine but grinds late. And beyond that, the delta, the sea, the wide world where a woman could be nobody forever if that was still what she wanted.

It wasn't. That was the strange, simple fact of it, arriving at last like the water going down: an inch at a time, revealing solid ground.

Elias came and stood beside me, chart tube under his arm like a rifle he'd retired. "First light tomorrow puts us at the confluence by noon," he said. "Harrow's Bend by dark, if the current holds."

"Then we'd better sleep," I said, and neither of us moved for a long time, and the river ran on beneath us, patient as a ledger, carrying us home.

Because that was the word for it now, wasn't it. Home. Not a place I was hiding in. A place I was *going*.

Downstream, far off in the dark, something splashed — a fish, a log, or a man on a floating hull, the river keeping its own counsel. I watched the black water a moment longer, then turned back toward the fire, toward the sleeping raft, toward the deed wrapped in oilcloth that by tomorrow evening would belong to everyone.

Tomorrow, I'd sign my father's name back into the world. And give it away.
