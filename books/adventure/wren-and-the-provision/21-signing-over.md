## 21. Signing Over

The ink was the problem. Everything else had come together with the strange, bruised ease of things that were always going to happen—Mama Sena's council fire lit at dawn, the whole of Temberu village crowded onto the levee and the mud flats below it, Nkechi standing on a crate with her testimony written out in three fair copies because she trusted paper more than memory. But when Mama Sena pressed the pen into my hand—a reed pen, cut that morning, the nib split with a fishbone knife—I stood there like a woman who'd forgotten what writing was.

"You're hesitating," Elias said beside me. He'd shaved. That was how I knew this was an occasion.

"I'm reading," I said. "There's a difference."

The deed lay on the table between us, dried out now from its waterproof wrapping, its edges warped into little waves like it had learned to swim. My father's name sat at the top in his own careful hand: *Tomas Hartwell, holder in trust.* Trust. He'd used that word sixty years before I understood it. The old man had been hiding the truth inside the paperwork the whole time, plain as day, waiting for someone literate enough and stubborn enough to read past the first line.

Below his name, a blank space waited for mine.

"Read it aloud," Mama Sena said. She sat wrapped in indigo cloth, older than the levee and considerably more permanent. "A thing isn't finished until it's been spoken over water. That's the law here. Older than your paper laws."

So I read it. I read about the fourteen thousand acres of riverland and floodplain, the fishing rights, the ferry rights, the right to cut timber above the high-water line and no lower. I read the clause where Tomas Hartwell swore—*swore, before God and the Sable River as witness*—that this land belonged not to him but to the people who worked it, and that any heir who claimed it for themselves alone would be, in his own words, "a thief of a worse kind than the one they'll call me." I read the part where he named the villages by name. All eleven of them. He'd walked every one, apparently, in the years he was supposedly running from justice, collecting their grievances and writing them into the deed like stitches into a hem.

I got through most of it with a steady voice. I lost it a little at the part where he wrote, *To my child, if I have one, if you are reading this: I am sorry I taught you to count miles instead of counting on people. It was the only inheritance I thought I could give you that Crane's kind couldn't take.*

"He knew," I said, mostly to myself. "He knew there'd be a me."

"There is always a me," Mama Sena said. "That is why documents outlive men. Now sign, girl. The river is patient but I am not."

I signed. Yara Hartwell, in ink mixed with soot and river water, which felt appropriate. The name looked strange under my hand—too big, too loud, like a boat horn blown in a chapel. I hadn't written it in full since I was nineteen years old. For fifteen years I'd been Yara, or Captain, or nothing at all, a woman who paid cash and moved on and never gave a surname to anyone who might repeat it. Writing it down felt less like signing a document and more like surfacing after a long dive, that first breath that hurts because your body had gotten used to doing without.

The crowd didn't cheer, exactly. What they did was better. They went quiet, all of them, several hundred people, and then Mama Sena lifted her staff and struck the ground three times, and the sound rolled out across the flooded fields like the river itself clearing its throat.

Then the noise came. And it wasn't for me, which was the whole point, and which was somehow harder to stand under than applause would have been. They cheered the land. They cheered the names of their villages read back to them by a stranger's voice. An old fisherman near the front wept openly and without embarrassment, and when I caught his eye he nodded at me once, the way you nod at another working person, and that nod undid me more thoroughly than anything Crane's men had managed with rifles.

* * *

Afterward there was food, because there is always food, and I hid behind a bowl of peppered rice and let Elias run interference, which he did with the startled competence of a man who'd spent his life avoiding crowds and had recently discovered they could be survived.

"You're doing the thing," he said, dropping onto the bench across from me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at a crowd like it's a sandbar you haven't charted yet. You've been doing it since dawn. I've started keeping notes."

"You keep notes on everything. I've seen your journal. There's an entry about the quality of my coffee."

"It was a *bad* entry," he said. "Historically significant, though. Future cartographers will want to know."

Around us the village celebrated in the practical way river people celebrate—with repairs. Half the crowd had drifted off already to mend nets and patch roofs and argue about the new trust council, because a deed is a fine thing but a roof is finer, and nobody in the Kessari Basin has ever confused paper with shelter. I watched them go and felt something in my chest unclench by degrees, like a knot worked loose one pull at a time.

Nkechi found us there. She moved slowly still—one of Crane's hired men had caught her with a boat hook during the scramble at the camp, and the bruise along her ribs had gone through purple into a greenish yellow she described, with a surveyor's precision, as "contour-mapped." She set a folded newspaper on the table between us. Downriver paper, two weeks old, water-spotted.

"Thought you should see it before the council does," she said. "Page four."

Crane's face looked back at me from a half-page advertisement, handsome and silver-templed, announcing the expansion of Crane Navigation & Trade into "new upstream markets." Below it, a smaller item: the Kessari Development Consortium announcing an emergency session, Odell Crane chairing, regarding "irregularities in upstream operations." The article speculated, delicately, that the expedition's losses might exceed initial estimates.

"He doesn't know yet," Nkechi said. "About the deed. About us. His radio men are still telling him the basin went quiet in the flood. But he'll know soon. Men like Crane check their ledgers eventually. It's the only prayer they know."

"Let him check," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her smile—not warmly, exactly. With respect. The kind of smile one professional gives another across a table where the work has gotten serious. "I'm taking the first raft downstream tomorrow," she said. "Testimony goes to the district registrar, then to the papers. Three copies. One for the courts, one for the press, one hidden where even I can't find it in a weak moment." She tapped the newspaper. "When this reaches him—and it will—he's going to come upriver one more time. Not with hired men this time. With lawyers, maybe. Or worse ones. You should decide what you want to be standing on the dock when he arrives."

"I know what I want to be standing on the dock," I said.

"That's the first time I've heard you say 'dock' like it's yours." She picked up her bowl and left, moving carefully, like a woman conserving herself for the long row ahead.

Elias watched her go. "She's right, you know. This isn't over. Signing a piece of paper doesn't finish a story like this one. It just changes whose hands are on the wheel."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've got the look again. The sandbar look."

I turned my bowl in my hands. The rice had gone cold and I ate it anyway, because waste is a sin on a riverboat and some habits are deeper than grief.

"When I was nineteen," I said, "my father put me on a barge at Port Sable with a bag, a knife, and forty dollars, and he told me to disappear. Not hide—disappear. Hide means you stay in one place and hope. Disappear means you keep moving so fast that being found becomes somebody else's full-time job." I laughed, or something near it. "Fifteen years. Do you know how many miles that is? I stopped counting at ten thousand because the number started feeling like a debt."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a village where three hundred people watched me write my name on a document, and half of them can probably spell it already, and the other half will learn by Sunday." I looked up at him. "And the strange thing, Voss—the genuinely deranged thing—is that it doesn't feel like a trap. It feels like a mooring line."

* * *

Evening came down gold and slow over the flooded fields. The water had begun its retreat—you could see it in the mud lines on the stilts, each day's drop marked like rings in a tree—and the village smelled of woodsmoke and drying fish and the particular green rot of a basin exhaling after a monsoon. I walked the levee alone, which I'd earned, checking the Wren where she lay tied at the village dock with her wounded hull above the waterline, patched with tar and canvas and the particular stubbornness of boats that refuse to die.

She looked terrible. She looked wonderful.

"You know," Elias said, appearing beside me the way he did, quiet as a man used to being overlooked, "the survey station archive included your father's original field notebooks. I've been reading them. He mapped this whole basin by hand—every bend, every sandbar, every seasonal channel. His notation system is idiosyncratic but rigorous. Frankly, it's better than anything the colonial surveys produced."

"He counted sandbars," I said. "Like birthdays. He taught me that. A sandbar a year keeps the river honest."

"It's in the notebooks. Bar by bar, year by year. Twenty-two years of entries." Elias hesitated, and I realized he'd been building up to something, working the words around like a man testing ice. "Yara. The trust council is going to need charts. Real ones. The flood redrew half the channels in this basin, and the old maps are dead paper now. Someone has to resurvey everything—fourteen thousand acres, eleven villages, every mile of navigable water. It's years of work."

"Sounds like a job for a cartographer."

"It sounds like a job for a cartographer *and* a pilot who reads water like scripture and happens to be the only living person who inherited her father's notation system." He said it lightly, but his ears had gone pink, and I understood suddenly that I was being asked something larger than employment. "I could draft the proposal tonight. If you wanted. We could call it—well, we'd have to call it something. Surveys need names."

We stood there while the light went out of the sky and the first stars came up over the water, and I thought about all the versions of myself I'd planned to be. The one who vanished up some tributary and was never heard from. The one who burned the deed and stayed a rumor. The one who kept running until the river ran out of river. They'd all seemed reasonable once. They'd all been built on the same bad arithmetic—that staying still meant being caught, that being known meant being hurt, that a name was a weight and not a keel.

My father had done the math wrong too, in his way. He'd carried the whole basin on his back alone rather than hand any of it out, because handing it out meant trusting people, and trusting people meant they could be taken from you. So he'd let himself be called a thief instead of a neighbor, and died a rumor instead of a man, and left his daughter a bag, a knife, forty dollars, and a habit of leaving.

But the deed had never really been his to carry either. That was the joke of it. That was the whole beautiful, stupid joke. He'd written *in trust* at the top of the page and then spent his life acting like the word applied to him alone.

"Hartwell Basin Survey," I said.

Elias blinked. "What?"

"The name. For the survey. Hartwell Basin Survey." I tested it in my mouth, expecting it to taste like ash, like exposure, like a target painted on my back. It tasted like none of those things. It tasted like my father's laugh, which I hadn't remembered correctly in fifteen years and now, suddenly, had. "If Crane wants to find me, he can find me at a dock with my name on it. I'm done making it easy for him to pretend I don't exist. And I'm done pretending it too."

For a moment Elias just looked at me, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being surveyed myself—charted, sounded, entered into a journal somewhere with a notation system all his own. Then he grinned, and it transformed his whole narrow, ink-stained face into something younger.

"Hartwell Basin Survey," he repeated. "First order of business: I'll need to hire a crew. Pilots are scarce this season. Difficult temperament, allegedly. Drinks too much coffee. Known to abandon employers without notice."

"Not this one," I said. "This one stays."

We shook on it, formally, absurdly, there on the levee in the dark, and his hand was warm and ink-stained and steady, and I held on maybe a beat longer than a contract required.

Downriver, far off, so faint it might have been thunder settling, I heard—or imagined I heard—the low horn of a heavy launch working its way against the current. Crane's kind of boat. Crane's kind of hour.

I stood at the rail of the levee and listened, and did not flinch, and began, quietly, to count the miles between here and wherever he was—because some habits are worth keeping, and this time, every mile led home.
