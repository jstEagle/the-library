## 22. The Wren, Refloated

The Wren came back to the water on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were working days, unglamorous ones, the kind of day a boat gets reborn rather than launched. Nobody threw champagne. Mama Sena broke a coconut against the hull instead—"for sweetness," she said, "and because champagne is a waste of good money"—and the whole village of Tember's Landing turned out to watch her slide down the ways into the Sable like she'd never left it.

I stood at the tiller and let the river take her weight, and I will admit—I'm admitting it here because there's no one left alive who can use it against me—that I cried a little. Just a little. River water in my eyes. That's my story and I'm keeping it.

She was not the same boat. That was the point. The planking that had rotted out from under me in the flood year—all that cheap timber I'd patched with prayer and denial—was gone, replaced with ironwood and red cedar cut from the high ground above the village, milled at the old Hartwell survey station, which is now called something else, something without my family name on it, because the villages voted on it and decided the station belonged to everyone. The new wood smells like resin when the sun hits her decks. She draws water differently. She rides swells I would have flinched at before. Elias says a rebuilt boat is like a rebuilt person: same lines on the chart, different creature underneath.

Elias says a lot of things. He's had eight months to get comfortable saying them.

* * *

Here is what a supply run looks like now, in the season after everything:

I leave Tember's Landing before dawn, when the mist is still sitting on the water like it owns the place. My manifest is written in Nkechi's careful hand—she does the paperwork now, having discovered that a woman who survived Crane's expedition can survive anything, including bookkeeping. Rice and salt for Kessari Bend. Lamp oil for the flooded-country villages where the wells run brackish half the year. School slates for Upstream Three, where Mama Sena's granddaughter teaches reading to children who'd never seen a slate before this year. Medicine for everywhere, because medicine is always for everywhere.

And mail. There's mail now. Eight months ago these communities were dots on a map nobody updated; now they're stops on a route, and the route has a schedule, and the schedule has my name on it. Captain Yara Hartwell, Sable & Kessari River Service. Elias painted the name on the wheelhouse himself, letter by letter, standing on a ladder and refusing help, and when he finished he climbed down and looked at it a long time and said, "There. Now you can't pretend you're nobody."

"I was never nobody," I told him. "I was somebody with excellent operational security."

He laughed the way he laughs—like a door opening—and went back to his charts.

Because that's the other thing. The maps are changing. Not all at once—the Survey Bureau moves at the speed of a dead snail—but changing. The Kessari Basin appears on the new sheets with its actual villages, actual channels, actual depths, surveyed by Elias Voss with corrections supplied by every pilot, fisherman, and grandmother between the last mapped bend and the headwaters. And in the corner of each sheet, small and official and permanent, sits the notation: *Lands held in common trust per the Hartwell Deed.*

My father's name is on those maps too. Spelled correctly. It took the Bureau three tries to spell it correctly, and I made them do it over twice, and if that seems like a small thing to fight for, then you have never watched your father die under a wrong name.

* * *

The miles still count. I want that understood. Some habits don't break; they just change what they mean.

I used to count sandbars like birthdays—one more year survived, one more bend put behind me, the arithmetic of a woman measuring her life in distance-from-somewhere. Every mile downriver was a mile away from the questions, the name, the past with its teeth out. I could tell you to the furlong how far I'd run from myself, and I did tell people, sometimes, when they asked why a woman so skilled never stayed anywhere. I'd say something wry. I was good at wry. Wry is armor you can wear in public.

Now I count the same miles and they add up different. Eleven miles to the ferry crossing at Low Marsh, where the old woman with the tobacco stall waves both arms like she's signaling a rescue plane. Nineteen to Kessari Bend, where the dockmaster—a boy of maybe sixteen who calls me Captain like it's an ordinary word—has learned to read the river off my face before I say anything. Thirty-one to the flooded country, where the channels move every season and the village pilots come out to meet me in dugouts, trading fresh knowledge for salt and news, because the trust pays for channel markers now and the markers save boats, and boats carry medicine, and medicine keeps children alive. You start seeing the ledger that way after a while. Everything connects to everything, and most of it connects through a dock.

Forty-six miles home.

Home. I say the word now the way you'd test ice with one foot. It holds.

* * *

In the dry season, Elias and I ran the full survey of the upper channel—the stretch beyond the last mapped bend, where my father once took me as a child and pointed at the water and said, *Learn it so well they can't take it from you.* I didn't understand him then. I thought he meant memorize it. He meant belong to it so thoroughly that no deed, no financier, no shame could ever pry it loose. He let himself be called a thief so the villages would keep what was theirs. He hid the truth in a survey station in the hills and carried the lie until it killed him, and his daughter ran from the lie for fifteen years, counting miles.

The upper channel is beautiful the way hard things are beautiful. Narrow water, braided around gravel bars that shift with every flood, banks crowded with fig trees whose roots hold whole sections of shoreline together. Elias mapped it over six weeks while I piloted, and we fought about symbols—about *symbols*, ink on paper—with a passion that Nkechi later said proved we were either married or should be, and we both pretended not to hear her, which Nkechi took, correctly, as confirmation.

One evening up there, anchored below a bluff where the current runs green and deep, Elias asked me the question he'd been circling since the flood.

"Do you forgive him?"

I knew he meant my father. I'd had months to prepare an answer and hadn't prepared one, because some answers you don't draft, you just wait for them to arrive.

"He bought peace with his own reputation," I said finally. "That's not a crime. But he made me inherit the crime instead of the choice. He never gave me the chance to be proud of him or furious at him. He just gave me the shame and left." I watched the current slide past the anchor line. "So no. Not yet. Maybe not ever entirely. But I understand him now, and understanding is a kind of forgiveness with better footing."

Elias nodded slowly, the way he does when he's adding something to a map in his head. "Then that's the entry," he said. "*Understood, not forgiven. Channel deep, holding ground good.*"

"That's not what that notation means."

"It is now. I'm redefining the symbol." He wrote it in the margin of his field book, and it's in the published charts, and every pilot on the river now uses a notation that means, officially, *understood, not forgiven*, and none of them know why, and I love that more than I can say.

* * *

Crane's name comes up less than you'd think. The river settled its account with Odell Crane in one night of broken levee and black water, but the law wanted its share too, and Nkechi's evidence—wrapped in oilcloth, carried through a flooded forest on a dying man's back—was thorough enough to hang a dozen crimes on his estate. His company dissolved. His financiers scattered like minnows. Once a quarter, some journalist comes upriver wanting the story, and I give them the same answer every time: talk to Nkechi Ade, she kept the records. I built a life on not being found. Turns out being found isn't so bad when you get to choose which parts of you they find.

Though I'll confess one vanity. Last month a trader who'd known me in my running days came through Tember's Landing, squinted at the name on my wheelhouse, and said, "Hartwell? Any relation to the thief of the Kessari?" And I looked him dead in the eye and said, "No relation to any thief. But I knew the man they called that, and he was the honestest crook this river ever produced, and the river knows it even if the ledgers took their time." He blinked at me for a long moment and then bought a crate of lamp oil and never mentioned it again. Mama Sena, watching from her porch, laughed until she had to sit down.

"You enjoyed that," she said.

"Immensely."

"Your father would have enjoyed it more."

"My father," I said, "would have done it better."

* * *

The Wren handles the monsoon season now the way an old soldier handles a parade—stiffly, complaining, but with real grace underneath. We don't run the upper channel in flood. Nobody does; the trust posted rules, and I wrote half of them, and rule one is *the river wins arguments*. But the lower runs stay open, and there's something in running loaded through weather that I'd forgotten I loved—not the danger, exactly, but the competence. The feeling of a boat answering your hands like a sentence finishing itself. For years I used that feeling as a hiding place. Now it's just a feeling, and I get to have it in the open, on a named boat, going somewhere on purpose.

Yesterday, coming down from Upstream Three with a load of school slates and one runaway goat (long story; the goat lost), I rounded the last bend before Tember's Landing at dusk and saw the lights. All of them. The village strung along the bank like a constellation someone had dropped and decided to leave where it fell, and at the center of it, Dock Nine—my dock, leased from the trust for a fee I insist on paying because paying is how belonging works, Mama Sena says, and Mama Sena is never wrong about anything except her estimate of how much pepper a dish requires.

My name is on the dock too. Painted smaller than the one on the wheelhouse, but there. *Hartwell Landing.* The villages voted on that as well. I argued against it for two hours at the assembly and lost in four minutes, and Elias counted the minutes out loud, which I have not forgiven, though I have understood.

I brought her in slow, the goat complaining, the slates shifting, the new wood creaking its young creaks, and the dockmaster boy caught my line and tied it off with the quick sure knot I taught him, and the whole operation took ninety seconds because we've done it a hundred times, and that—that right there—is the thing I spent fifteen years running from and didn't know it. Not the dock. The repetition. The same faces, the same knots, the same jokes wearing grooves into the days like water wearing a channel. Miles used to be how I escaped. Now they're how I return.

I stood on my deck in the lamplight with a runaway goat chewing my hat brim, and I counted the day's miles one last time, out loud, the way I always do, and Elias—who appeared at the rail with two cups of tea because he always knows—waited for the number and then said, "And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow's forty-six again," I said. "Upstream. Slates go back empty, mail goes up, and there's a new channel marker to check past the second bend."

"Forty-six miles," he said, "that lead back here."

"Yeah," I said. "Funny how that works."

It isn't funny. It's the whole point. But some things you say funny anyway, because wry is still armor, and even a woman with a dock and a name and a rebuilt boat likes to keep one piece on—for old times' sake, and because the river loves a joke, and so did my father, and so, increasingly, do I.

The goat ate my hat. I let it. Some losses you can afford now.

Tomorrow, forty-six miles. Every one of them leading home.
