## 1. The Debt Collector's Weather

The supercell came up out of the west like something with an opinion about her.

Jude Reyes watched it from the shoulder of Highway 14, one boot on the truck's bumper, eating a gas-station breakfast of peanuts and bad coffee while the sky went the color of a bruise healing wrong. The storm had been building since dawn over the dryland wheat, stacking itself in slow, muscular towers, and now it was beginning its rotation — that first lazy tilt at the top, like a dancer finding her balance before the spin.

"Come on," she murmured to it. "Be beautiful. Be worth it."

Beautiful was the only currency she had left. Three years of chasing for the Vane Syndicate's footage contracts had taught her exactly what they paid for: the money shot, the wall cloud tightening, the funnel dropping against clean light. Not science. Not safety. Beauty, weaponized and watermarked and sold to insurers who used it to deny claims in counties she'd never see.

She swung into the cab, woke the laptop, and let the radar paint the cell's guts in greens and yellows and that hot magenta core where hail was being manufactured like coins in a mint. Her debt sat in a folder on the desktop, too — a spreadsheet named *obligations*, because she'd refused to call it anything softer. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars owed to men who never raised their voices, plus interest that accrued like mold. Every chase this season was a few hundred dollars shaved off the top. At this rate she'd be free sometime after she was dead.

The compass hung from the rearview mirror on a length of parachute cord, turning slowly in the wind through her open window. Brass case gone green at the seams, glass scratched milky, needle swinging without any interest in north whatsoever. Elias Voss had given it to her eleven years ago, the day she'd signed on as his driver, holding it out on his flat palm like something rescued from a fire.

"It doesn't point where you think," he'd said. "That's the point."

He'd been dead two years now. Not dead-dead — no body, no funeral, just a last postcard from a river town she couldn't find on any map, and then nothing. *Gone upstream,* the postcard said, in handwriting that had already started to shake. *If I don't come back, don't follow.* Which was, of course, the only instruction Jude Reyes had ever been constitutionally incapable of obeying. She just hadn't figured out how yet.

The needle swung. It always swung. She'd stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a limp or a debt.

She drove south on the grid roads, dust boiling behind the truck, chasing the hook echo as it curled like a question mark on her screen. The plains here were empty in a way she loved and needed — no towns, no names, nobody to remember her face. Invisibility wasn't a quirk for Jude; it was a discipline. Pay cash. Use the truck's second registration. Never stay anywhere longer than a season. If the Syndicate ever decided she was worth more as an example than as a contractor, she intended to be a rumor by the time they came looking.

The storm agreed to be magnificent. It dropped a funnel at 5:40 p.m. over a fallow section where nothing existed to be destroyed but grass and fence posts, and Jude stood in the ditch with the camera braced against her shoulder and filmed the whole three-minute life of it — the rope-out, the debris glow, the way the light went strange and submarine before it died. It was good work. It was maybe the best work she'd done all year, and she felt the familiar lift in her chest, the one that almost made the arithmetic bearable.

Then her phone rang, and the arithmetic reasserted itself.

"Reyes." The voice belonged to Corben Vane's assistant, a man who sounded like he'd been assembled from voicemail greetings. "Footage confirmation. Upload within the hour or the contract voids."

"Uploading now," she said, and was.

Except she wasn't. Because when she got back to the truck and opened the file transfer, the progress bar crawled to nine percent and died, and the tower bar on her phone went from two bars to none, and the storm — her storm, her paycheck — rolled east fat and satisfied across a cellular dead zone forty miles wide, taking her invoice with it.

She tried the ridge. She tried the highway. She drove twenty minutes north with the laptop open on the passenger seat, watching the upload die at nine percent, then twelve, then zero, until the sun went down and the light in the western sky turned the exact color of the spreadsheet she couldn't pay.

She was sitting in the dark with the engine off, doing the thing where she breathed slowly so the panic stayed a tenant and didn't become a landlord, when the headlights found her.

They came up the grid road without hurry, twin beams sweeping the stubble, and resolved into a black SUV that parked thirty feet off with its lights still burning. Jude knew the vehicle before she could read the plate. There were maybe four people on the plains who drove like that — like the road was a formality.

The driver's door opened, and Marisol Quade stepped out into her own high beams.

Of course. Of course it was Marisol. Rival wasn't even the right word anymore; rivals competed, and Marisol had graduated past competing somewhere around the year she'd sold her archive to Vane Media for enough money to buy the weather itself. She stood there in a field jacket that cost more than Jude's truck, silver hair cut short and severe, looking at Jude the way she looked at cells on radar — assessing rotation.

"Dead zone," Marisol said. Not a question. "I've been sitting on the edge of it for two hours waiting for you to fail. You're predictable, Reyes. It's your best quality."

"I'm not selling to you."

"You're not selling anything. Your upload died at nine percent. By morning, Corben Vane will have declared the contract breached, and your obligations clause kicks in." Marisol walked closer, boots crunching on the drought-cracked ground, and stopped just outside arm's reach, which was a courtesy or a taunt. "Forty-one thousand becomes sixty-two overnight. You know how their penalty schedules work. You helped write half of them."

Jude said nothing. Saying nothing was also a discipline.

"So here's what I came to say." Marisol glanced up at the retreating storm, its last lightning flickering inside the cloud like thoughts behind a skull. "I'm putting together an expedition. Up the Serrano River. To the source."

The words landed strangely. The Serrano was a nothing river — brown, sluggish, famous for nothing except that every map Jude had ever owned simply stopped drawing it above the town of Callow's Bend. Survey blank. Cartographer's shrug. Elias had stared at that blank stretch of map for hours once, in a motel outside Callow's Bend, tapping the paper with one finger like he was trying to wake something sleeping under it.

"No one goes up there," Jude said.

"No one comes back from up there," Marisol corrected. "Different thing. I need someone who can read weather, run water, and keep a crew alive when the maps stop working. That's you. That has always been you, whatever else we've done to each other."

"And the price?"

Marisol smiled, and it was the smile from the old days, before the money, when they'd been two broke chasers splitting fuel and arguing about supercell dynamics in diners at 2 a.m. Seeing it now felt like finding a photograph of someone dead.

"The number is whatever's on your spreadsheet," Marisol said. "Cleared. Gone. Plus a salary that means you never chase for anyone again. All I want is the source, Reyes. Whatever's making that river forget where it comes from — I want it found, documented, and delivered."

Jude looked at the compass hanging in the windshield's reflection, needle drifting through its slow, private orbit. *It doesn't point where you think.*

Elias had gone upstream. Elias had told her not to follow. And the Syndicate's penalty clock was already running, ticking toward sixty-two thousand by sunrise, toward a version of her life where she ran forever and never arrived anywhere.

"How many crew?" she heard herself ask.

"Five. You lead them." Marisol held out a hand, businesslike, final. "We launch from Callow's Bend Thursday. Bring the compass."

The last part slid in smooth and easy, the way the worst hooks do, and Jude took her hand anyway, because running had finally stopped working, and the only direction left was the one the needle had been pointing all along.

* * *

That night, camped in the dead zone with no signal and no upload and no debt collector able to reach her until morning, Jude lay in the truck bed and watched the storm rebuild itself far to the east, flickering over the horizon like a city on fire.

The compass lay on her sternum, rising and falling with her breath. In the dark, without trying, she noticed for the first time in eleven years which way the needle actually pointed.

Not north. Not south.

East-northeast. Upstream. Toward the blank place on the map where her mentor's handwriting had shaken, toward whatever had been pulling at the brass all these years like a tide.

*Don't follow,* Elias had written.

"Too late," Jude said to the dark, and slept, and dreamed of water moving the wrong way.
