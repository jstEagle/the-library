## 2. Upstream of Every Map

The Serrano did not want them there.

Jude felt it in the first hour, the way the current shouldered the raft sideways like a drunk pushing past strangers in a hallway. The river ran fast and glass-green over stones the color of old teeth, and every eddy seemed to have opinions. She stood at the bow with a pole she didn't trust, watching Marisol Quade at the stern with her hands light on the tiller ropes, wearing that expression she'd worn since they were nineteen and racing storm cells across the flats — the expression of someone who had already read the last page.

"Checkpoint at Kilometer Forty," Marisol called over the water's noise. "Or what used to be one."

The checkpoint announced itself as a rusted gantry leaning out over both banks, its chain long since dropped into the shallows, its signboard bleached to a blank white rectangle. No letters survived. Whatever word had forbidden passage here had been eaten by weather or by hands, and Jude found herself wondering which.

Teo crouched under the gear tarp, writing. He wrote constantly — in the rapids, in the rain, balanced on a crate of fuel while the raft bucked — in a waterproof notebook he guarded like scripture. When Jude had asked him about it on the drive to the put-in, he'd said only, "Somebody has to remember this trip," and laughed as if it were a joke.

* * *

> **Teo Vasquez — field journal, day two.**
>
> River is faster than the maps say. Maps are wrong anyway; I checked three editions and none of them agree past the gorge. R. says the cartographers stopped coming up here in the sixties. Asked why. She said "they lost their nerve." Didn't sound like she believed it.
>
> M.Q. runs the crew like she's conducting. Everyone does what she says before she finishes saying it. Even me, and I don't even like her.
>
> Note: my grandmother's name is slipping again. It starts with an M. I'll ask Mom when we get signal.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar beneath cliffs the color of dried blood, and Jude took out the compass because taking it out was a habit older than her debts.

It had been Halvorsen's — Dr. Ines Halvorsen, who had taught her to read a sky the way other people read faces, who had vanished eleven years ago somewhere north of anywhere with a name. The compass was brass, dented, older than either of them, and for eleven years it had done something Jude had never told another living soul: it did not point north.

It pointed upstream.

She'd told herself for a decade it was broken. A bent needle, a local anomaly, some pocket of iron in the plains fooling the magnet. But here, on the Serrano, in the last hour of light, she held it flat on her palm and watched the needle swing — not toward the pole, not toward any metal in camp, but dead upriver, steady as a plumb line, quivering with something that looked almost like eagerness.

"You knew," she said aloud, to no one, to Halvorsen, to the dark water sliding past. "You followed this thing."

And then, colder: *and it followed you back.*

* * *

The second town had a church with no name on it.

That was how Jude thought of the settlements they passed — not by their names, which they did not have, but by their absences. The first town, forty miles down, had still been half-alive: old men on porches who called each other *friend* because they could not call each anything else, children with hand-lettered tags pinned to their shirts reading *I AM ___*, the blanks filled in by visiting teachers in fading ink. The second town was quieter. The third quieter still. As if memory drained downstream like silt, thinning with every mile from the source.

In the second town an old woman waved them ashore and fed them stew nobody could identify, in a kitchen where every jar was labeled with a different handwriting than the one before it. She was warm and funny and generous, and when Teo asked her name she smiled the way you smile at a question in a language you used to speak.

"I had one," she said. "A good one. My mother chose it." She touched her own wrist, where a faded tattoo showed the ghost of letters, lasered away or scrubbed, only the shadow of ink remaining. "It's on the tip of my tongue. It's been on the tip of my tongue for thirty years."

Jude set down her spoon. "Thirty years?"

"Since the storm came to stay." The woman said it lightly, the way you'd mention a leaky roof. Upstream, beyond the ridgeline, thunder muttered — the same low, ceaseless grumble Jude had been hearing since the put-in, the storm that never ended, parked over the source like a dog on a grave. "Before that, everybody knew everything. After, well." She shrugged. "We know each other's faces. We just don't know why we love them."

Teo had gone very still beside her. That night his pen scratched for a long time after the fire died.

* * *

> **Teo Vasquez — field journal, day four.**
>
> The woman in the second town gave us eggs and wouldn't take money. Said money was "for people who keep track."
>
> I asked R. tonight what we're actually going to find up there. She said, "The reason nobody remembers." I asked if she was scared. She looked at her compass for a long time and said, "I'm scared it knows me."
>
> I didn't write that down right away because I wanted to be sure she said it. She said it.
>
> Also: asked M.Q. directly whose money this is. She smiled and said, "Mine, eventually." Thought that was a joke. Watched her face. Not a joke.

* * *

The gorge nearly killed them on day five.

The river narrowed to a throat between walls of black slate, and the water went from fast to furious, standing waves taller than the raft, holes that swallowed the bow and spat it back sideways. They ran it because there was no portage — the canyon walls offered nothing but vertical — and for nine minutes that lasted a season Jude did nothing but react, body ahead of thought, pole jammed against rock, rope burning through her gloves, Marisol's voice cutting clean commands through the roar as if the chaos were an orchestra and she'd rehearsed it.

They shot out the bottom into a wide, slow valley, all of them alive, half the gear soaked, and nobody cheering. The silence after the gorge was enormous. And in it, Jude noticed what she should have noticed days ago: the birds were gone. Not scarce — gone. No swallows over the water, no hawks on the thermals, nothing but the mutter of the distant storm. A river valley with no birds was a valley where something had eaten the sky.

"It's beautiful," Teo whispered, and he was right, and that was the worst part. The valley was heartbreakingly beautiful — wildflowers to the waterline, water clear as breath, the storm-light above gilding everything gold. Belonging here would be easy. That was the trap. Everything about this place reached out and pulled at you like the current, saying *stay, stay, you're home, you've always been home* — while the towns downstream forgot their own mothers' names.

Wonder curdling into unease. Jude stood in the bow and let the compass rest in her open hand and watched the needle strain upstream like a dog at the end of a leash.

"Halvorsen came up here," she said. Marisol glanced over, and Jude realized she'd spoken without deciding to. "Eleven years ago. Her last expedition. She told the university she was mapping headwaters." Jude turned the compass so Marisol could see the needle. "She wasn't mapping anything. She was being *called*. Same as this thing's calling me."

Marisol's face did something complicated and quick — a flicker Jude might have missed if she hadn't spent twenty years reading that face across storm fronts. Recognition. Relief, maybe. Something that looked almost like guilt.

"Then you understand the assignment better than I hoped," Marisol said.

"The assignment was to find the source of the storm."

"It is." Marisol turned back to the tiller, and the light caught the syndicate's pin at her collar, small and bright as a fish scale. "All of it. Every kind of source."

That night, moored below a cliff carved with symbols no one could read — spirals, mostly, spirals within spirals, like water remembering itself — Jude lay awake listening to the storm grow louder. Not closer, exactly. Louder, the way a crowd gets louder, the way a room full of voices gets louder, and somewhere in the middle of the night she sat bolt upright with the certainty sitting on her chest like a stone:

It wasn't thunder.

It had never been thunder. It was too rhythmic, too layered, too *many*. Decades of storm, and underneath it, if you finally listened, a sound like ten thousand people talking at once — murmuring, laughing, calling names.

Calling their own names, over and over, into a place where no one could hear them.

Teo's pen was scratching in the dark. Jude stared upstream, where the compass needle trembled toward whatever waited at the head of the river, and understood that in eleven years of running from debt and memory and Marisol Quade, she had never once been running away.

She had been running *here*.
