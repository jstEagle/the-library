## 3. What the Machine Remembers

The storm had been above them for three days before they found its root.

Jude felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, the same low thrum she'd chased across a thousand miles of prairie, except here it didn't move. It sat over the headwaters like a hand pressed flat on the world, holding everything down. The Serrano narrowed to a channel barely wide enough for the boats, then to rapids they portaged in silence, then to a gorge where the water ran backward — actually backward, curling upstream toward whatever pulled it, and Teo stood at the rail with his journal open and wrote nothing at all.

"There," Marisol said.

Not a mountain. Not a spring. A building.

It rose out of the gorge wall like something the rock had grown rather than borne: pale stone, windowless, vast, its face carved with names in a script none of them could read — or rather, names that refused to be read, sliding off the eye the way water slides off wax. The river entered it through an arch at its base and did not come out. Above the roofline, the unbroken storm turned on itself, a wheel of cloud forty years wide, and lightning crawled down the walls and vanished into the stone without striking anything.

The compass in Jude's pocket was hot enough to feel through canvas. She'd known for two days what it was pointing at. She'd known since the second town, since the woman who'd smiled at her with her own mother's smile and couldn't tell Jude her name because there was nothing left in her to tell it with.

They moored in a shelf of dead calm beneath the arch. The air tasted of ozone and old paper.

"Nobody touches anything," Marisol said, and for once her voice wasn't performance. "We look. We document. Then we decide."

*Decide,* Jude thought. *As if you haven't already.*

* * *

Inside, the corridor sloped down for a quarter mile, lit by veins of light running through the walls themselves, and the humming grew until it wasn't sound anymore but texture, a vibration in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet of the eyes. Doors lined the passage — hundreds of them, thousands, each marked with a brass plate bearing a single word that kept rearranging itself when Jude tried to focus on it. She stopped trying. Her hands were shaking. Not from fear. From recognition, the way you recognize a house you grew up in by the smell alone.

The corridor opened into the vault.

It was a sphere of open space half a mile across, and it was full of weather. Not rain — memory. It hung in luminous drifts like plankton, whole shoals of it turning slowly in the dark: a wedding seen from the back row; a dog's weight against a child's chest; the specific green of a kitchen on a Tuesday afternoon in some year nobody downstream could now name. Every mote was someone's afternoon. Every current was a life's worth of small hours, harvested and hoarded, glowing with the borrowed warmth of belonging.

And at the center, suspended in the storm of everyone's yesterdays, hung the Engine.

It was smaller than Jude expected — a heart-sized lattice of black iron and glass, beating. With each pulse, the drifts brightened, drew inward a fraction, and gave up something more of themselves into the core. Feeding. It had been feeding for decades, and the towns along the Serrano had been forgetting for exactly as long, and somewhere in Jude's chest a grief she'd carried so long it had become architecture suddenly had a shape and an address.

She stepped off the gallery stair onto the lower platform, and the nearest drift reached for her.

It wasn't violent. That was the horror of it. It simply opened, the way a book opens, and Jude was nine years old again, standing in her grandmother's kitchen in a town called — called —

*Amaranth.* The name arrived like a blow. Amaranth, on the Serrano, population four hundred, washed off every map she'd ever owned, and here it was: flour on her grandmother's forearms, the radio reporting weather she'd later chase for money, her mentor Elena Voss sitting at the table with a compass between them, saying, *This doesn't point north, mija. It points home. And home is the one place they can't take from you if you never let them see you love it.*

Elena. Here. In the machine's keeping, or kept out of it — Jude couldn't tell which thought was worse. The last thing Elena had sent her was a single line: *Follow the needle. I'm sorry about what it costs.* Then nothing. Four years of nothing.

"You're crying," Marisol said beside her.

Jude hadn't heard her come down. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist and looked at the drift still hanging open around her, showing her a childhood she'd believed was simply lost — lost the way things get lost, the way people say *I had a happy childhood, I think, I've been told I did* — and understood that she had never been broken. She'd been *harvested*. They'd all been. The emptiness she'd run from her whole life, the reason invisibility had felt less like a choice than a diagnosis, had a machine at the end of it, and the machine was beating.

"It takes from everyone," Jude said. "Even us. Even people who never lived on the river."

"It takes from anyone who comes close," Marisol said. "That's how it defends itself." But she wasn't looking at the drifts. She was looking at the core, and her expression was one Jude had seen a hundred times across a decade of rivalry — at regatta finishes, at grant panels, at the moment before any race was won. It was hunger wearing patience.

"How do we break it?" Jude asked.

Marisol laughed softly. "Break it. God. You always were the romantic." She turned, and in the light of ten thousand stolen afternoons her face was very beautiful and very far away. "Do you know what this is worth? Not money. I mean *worth*. Every debt in the valley is written down somewhere, and every deed, every contract, every grudge — all of it lives in these people's heads, and their heads are empty. Whoever holds this holds the ledger of the entire river. The Syndicate thinks they hired me to find a power source." She smiled. "I'm going to hand them a kingdom and keep the crown."

"You're going to be the keeper."

"I'm going to be the *memory*," Marisol said, and there it was — not madness, worse: clarity. "Someone has to hold it, Jude. Someone responsible. If we release it, it floods back into people who've built whole lives on the holes. Do you know what that does to a person? To a town? I've read the founders' notes. The first attempt to give it back killed eleven people." She gestured at the glowing dark. "This isn't theft. This is triage."

"The woman in the second town," Jude said quietly. "She smiled at me with my grandmother's mouth and she didn't know her own name. That's your triage."

"That's the price of order."

"No," Jude said. "That's the price of *you* deciding you're the one who gets to pay it."

For a long moment neither of them moved, and the Engine beat between them like a held breath. Around them the drifts turned, indifferent and radiant, a galaxy of Tuesdays and weddings and dogs, and somewhere in it Elena Voss's voice was still saying *it points home*, and Jude finally understood what the compass had been for. Not navigation. Inheritance. Elena hadn't gotten lost following it. Elena had gotten *close*, and the machine had taken her the way it took everything — or Elena had given herself to it, deliberately, the way you'd give blood.

Marisol unslung her pack and began setting out instruments, cables, a relay housing stamped with the Vane Syndicate's mark. Businesslike. Final.

"Help me or wait outside," she said. "Either way, in an hour this belongs to someone, and I'd rather it was me than the men who own your boat loan."

And that was the knife of it, turned gently, expertly: Jude's debts, Jude's drowning, Jude's whole precarious invisible life — all of it forgivable, today, with a signature. All Marisol wanted in exchange was for Jude to stand aside while she became the thing the towns had built this vault to escape.

Jude put her hand in her pocket, closed it around the compass, and felt the needle straining toward the core like a finger pointing at a wound.

"Elena left this here for a reason," she said.

"Elena left *herself* here for a reason," Marisol said. "Look how well that worked out."

Above them, through half a mile of stone, the storm that had raged for forty years gathered itself and waited, and Jude Reyes — who had spent her whole life learning how not to be seen — took the compass out of her pocket and held it up to the light of everyone's stolen memories, and made her decision.
