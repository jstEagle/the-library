## 1. Fuel, Rumors, Rain

The rain came in off the coast at four in the morning, the way it always did in the weeks before monsoon, and Silas Pemberton stood under the corrugated overhang of his hangar with a mug of black coffee going cold in his hand and watched it hammer the strip into a brown mirror.

Twelve years of flying this river country had taught him to read weather the way other men read faces. This wasn't monsoon yet. Monsoon was a wall, a solid gray thing that ate the passes for three months and made the interior a rumor. This was only the overture—the sky clearing its throat, testing its range. But it meant the calendar had turned. It meant he had maybe six weeks of reliable flying left, and every hour after that would be a negotiation with the clouds.

He drank the coffee anyway, cold and bitter, because hot coffee was a luxury he'd stopped allowing himself somewhere around year five, when he'd realized nobody was waiting at the far end of any flight to share it with.

The Otter sat on the apron where he'd left her, nose into the wind, rain beading on her wings and running off in silver threads. *Kestrel*, tail number painted fresh last spring because the registry man had complained the old one was illegible. He'd repainted it properly, all four characters, crisp white on blue. A man who wanted to stay invisible didn't repaint his tail number. That was the whole point.

By first light the rain had thinned to a drizzle and the strip had begun to steam, mist rising off the gravel like the ground itself was exhaling. Silas loaded by muscle memory: two crates of penicillin and amoxicillin from the coastal clinic, a box of insulin packed in ice, a sealed carton of sutures, a bale of secondhand clothing someone downriver had donated to the mission at Kettle Bend. He strapped each load and tested each strap twice, because cargo that shifted in turbulence killed pilots faster than engines ever did, and because the ritual of it—hands moving, knots biting, weight settling—was the closest thing he had to prayer.

"You're going up in this?" Old Marta Voss-adjacent nobody—no, that was wrong; her name was simply Marta, the fuel depot woman, seventy if she was a day, who'd run the pumps since before Silas owned an airplane. She came hobbling across the wet gravel with a clipboard under a plastic sheet, squinting up at the sky like it owed her money.

"Going up in worse than this before the season's out," Silas said.

"That's what worries me about you." She signed his fuel slip without looking at it. "You treat bad weather like a personal challenge."

"I treat it like weather." He took the slip, folded it into his breast pocket. "Weather doesn't know I'm here, Marta."

"Everything knows you're here," she said. "That's your trouble."

He laughed, or made the sound that passed for laughing, and climbed the wing root into the cockpit.

* * *

The radio was the first thing to fail, which was traditional.

Silas ran through the checklist while the Kestrel's engine caught on the third blade and settled into its familiar ragged hum—a 1952 airframe held together by safety wire, prayer, and a parts budget that existed mostly in his imagination. He keyed the radio. Static. Keyed it again. Static with a heartbeat of carrier wave underneath, the coastal station's signal arriving like a voice heard through a wall.

*Kestrel One-Seven-Bravo, coastal, say again?*

He said again. They said again. Between them they assembled enough words to confirm departure time, heading, and the fact that the weather satellite feed had been down two days, so everyone flying the interior this week was doing it blind and by memory. Which was how everyone flew the interior every week, if they were honest about it. The maps were forty years old in places. The satellites were somebody else's priority. The government's interest in the interior began and ended at whatever minerals showed up in the survey reports, and even that interest arrived late and left early.

He lifted off into the mist at 6:40, wheels leaving the gravel with a soft double-thump, and the world below him dissolved into green-gray watercolor: river braiding and rebraiding through the floodplain, gallery forest dark along the banks, the occasional tin roof flashing through the canopy where a homestead pretended not to exist.

This was the part of flying nobody paid him for, and the part he'd have done for free. The river unspooled ahead like something being written. At two thousand feet the rain became a ceiling of pearl above him and the air went smooth as glass, and Silas set the trim, loosened his grip on the yoke, and let the airplane fly itself for a while.

Loneliness, at altitude, was almost a form of company. He'd gotten good at it. There was a version of solitude that felt like competence—you alone in the machine, you alone responsible, the whole sky yours to misjudge or manage—and he wore that version like a coat. What he didn't wear, what he kept folded in a drawer at the bottom of himself, was the other kind: coming home to a dark hangar, eating standing up, realizing he hadn't spoken aloud in thirty-one hours except to read back a clearance.

Seven years since anyone had waited for him anywhere.

He did the arithmetic sometimes, against his will. Seven years since Xanthe's last letter, postmarked from a river town that didn't appear on charts, saying she'd found what she was looking for and not to come after her. Six years since the letters stopped entirely. Five years since he'd flown the length of the watershed asking questions and getting the particular polite blankness that meant people knew something and had decided he shouldn't. Four years since he'd stopped asking.

The compass she'd left behind—the cheap brass one he'd given her when they were kids, engraved with an X by some harbor stall engraver who'd charged their father too much—still sat in his flight bag. He never used it. Its needle had gone lazy decades ago. He carried it the way you carry anything you can't put down.

Below him now, the river narrowed into the gorge country, cliffs shouldering up on both sides, and the current turned the color of strong tea. First stop: Kettle Bend, ninety minutes upriver, where the mission nurse would meet him on the beach with a wheelbarrow and complaints about the insulin.

* * *

Kettle Bend smelled of woodsmoke and river mud and, today, of frying fish, because word traveled faster than airplanes in the interior and the medicine run meant market day by unofficial decree. Half the settlement was on the shingle beach when Silas flared in, kids racing the prop wash, and he taxied the Kestrel up onto the sand with the practiced arrogance of a man who'd done it two hundred times and knew exactly how close the wheels could go to the waterline.

Nurse Adaeze met him at the cargo door with her wheelbarrow, took one look at the insulin crate, and said, "You kept it cold."

"I kept it cold."

"The last pilot let it freeze. Whole shipment ruined. Man flew off before I could unpack it."

"I'm not the last pilot. I'm the only pilot."

"You're the only honest one, is what you are." She dug into the crate, checked the vials, nodded once—which from Adaeze was a medal ceremony—and then, as he handed down the sutures, lowered her voice in the way that meant gossip was incoming, and gossip in the interior was currency, more stable than the regional dollar and considerably more liquid.

"They're saying a survey team's gone missing," she said. "Up past the gorge. Government people."

Silas kept unloading. "Which government?"

"Land office, they think. Six of them went up three weeks back to do whatever government people do in the bush, and only the supply boat's come back down. No team." She shrugged elaborately, a gesture that managed to convey both *isn't it terrible* and *what did they expect*. "Boatman says the camp was there but empty. Says the forest was quiet. Says—" she dropped into a theatrical whisper— "says there were no tracks. Not around the tents, not to the water, nowhere. Like they were lifted up."

"Or like the boatman's telling a story to raise his rates."

Adaeze clicked her tongue, conceding the point without abandoning the pleasure of it. "There's talk the office is hiring planes to go look. Big money, they say. Triple rate."

"Talk's free," Silas said. "Triple rate costs somebody something. When the government pays triple, it's because they want something done fast and quiet, and both of those are bad signs."

"You've gotten cynical."

"I've gotten accurate." He lashed the empty crates down. "Missing surveyors are a land-office problem. If they wanted to find people, they'd hire trackers, not bush pilots. Pilots are for when they want to find places."

She looked at him a moment longer than the joke deserved. "You could ask around, though. For the family's sake. Somebody upriver must know something."

"Everybody upriver knows something. That's the problem." He shook her hand, swung up into the cockpit. "Insulin stays cold. Tell your fish-fryers the Kestrel comes back Thursday."

The kids waved. The wheelbarrow squeaked away. And Silas took off from the beach into the building heat with the missing-survey-team story already filed away in the part of his mind where rumors went to decompose—alongside the gold reef that moved location every retelling, the white crocodile of the lower falls, and the government road that had been "starting construction next dry season" for eleven consecutive dry seasons.

* * *

Two stops remained: a church landing with a diesel filter and a case of hymnals, and the homestead at Two Rivers where a diabetic farmer named Okonkwo needed his quarterly check-in and, more urgently, needed someone to tell him his youngest daughter's exam results, which Silas had carried printed from the coastal office because the interior had no mail service worth the name.

It was nearly four in the afternoon by the time he turned the Kestrel back downstream, and the weather had changed its mind about being polite.

The storm cells were stacking up over the western ridgeline—towers of bruised purple, flat-bottomed, already trailing gray skirts of rain that reached all the way to the forest. Not monsoon. Just the overture. But a cell like that could shear a wing off or swallow a valley whole, and between him and the coast lay eighty miles of gorge with no room to turn around.

Silas climbed to clear the ridge line, watching the cells with the professional detachment of long habit, calculating drift and closure rates. The radio crackled with other traffic—two missionary flights, a charter, the coastal station relaying a garbled weather advisory. Then, threaded through the static, a voice he half recognized: Denny Achterberg, another freelance pilot, working the far side of the watershed.

*—anyone copy, anyone—saw lights up the gorge last night, three of them, low, moving slow. Nobody's supposed to be up there. Anybody got eyes—*

Static swallowed the rest. Silas listened for the repeat. It didn't come. Ten minutes later, on the next scheduled check-in, Denny's frequency was silent, and the coastal station marked him *reported operating, no contact* in the bored tone of a clerk ticking a box.

Lights in the gorge. A vanished survey team. A boatman with no tracks to sell. Denny Achterberg, who was careful to the point of cowardice, seeing things at night where nothing should be.

Silas rolled the pieces around and found no shape in them. The interior produced stories the way the river produced silt—constantly, abundantly, and mostly worthless. In twelve years he'd learned that the truth up here was usually smaller and duller than the telling: a party delayed by a broken axle, a boatman embellishing for tips, lights that were phosphorescence or a brush fire or a drunk with a flashlight. The wilderness didn't eat people whole. People got lost, got rained on, got stubborn, and walked out three days later embarrassed.

He told himself all of it, methodically, the way he ran a preflight check, item by item, until the story was cleared for dismissal.

But somewhere over the gorge, with the storm towers burning orange on his left and the river running black beneath him, his hand drifted down to the flight bag on the seat beside him, and rested there, on the canvas, over the small hard circle of the brass compass he hadn't opened in four years.

The needle inside pointed wherever it pleased. It always had. Xanthe used to say that was why she liked it—that a compass that lied to you taught you to navigate by everything else. Stars, current, moss, the lean of trees in the prevailing wind. *You can find true north without anybody's permission,* she'd said, fourteen years old and already halfway out the door of the world.

The radio hissed. The storm built. Silas flew home through the falling dark to an empty hangar, tied down the Kestrel in the last gray light, and stood a long moment in the rain-smell of the evening with his hand still curled around nothing.

Thursday, he'd told Adaeze. Back Thursday with supplies.

He was on the strip Wednesday morning, drinking cold coffee, when the government Land Rover came up the coast road through the drizzle and stopped at the edge of his field with its wipers still going.

A man in a pressed shirt stepped out, holding a folder, and looked at the Kestrel the way men look at tools.

Silas finished his coffee, set the mug down on the wing strut, and went to see what triple rate cost.
