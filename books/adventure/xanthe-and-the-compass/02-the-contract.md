## 2. The Contract

The rain found Silas Pemberton before the government man did.

It came in off the coast the way it always did this time of year—not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the whole of Meridian Bay, turning the harbor water to hammered tin and the airstrip's gravel apron into a long brown wound. Silas stood under the corrugated overhang of his hangar with a mug of black coffee going cold in his hand and watched it come down on the Otter, his plane, his whole crooked livelihood, parked out there like a patient bird that had already given up on flying anywhere dry.

Three days since the medicine run to Kettle Narrows. Three days of sleeping past dawn for the first time in months, and waking anyway at four-thirty because his body didn't know what to do with an empty morning. He'd spent them doing what he always did between contracts: patching, filing, lying to himself about how much money he owed.

The debt sat in a folder in the hangar office, in a drawer he never quite managed to lock. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and change, owed to a bank in the capital that had never once spelled his name right on its letters. The engine overhaul last spring had eaten most of it; the prop strike the autumn before that had started it. There was a version of his life where he'd sold the Otter then, taken a dock job, let the sky go on without him. But a man without a plane in this country was just a man, and Silas had never learned how to be only that.

So when the seaplane tender puttered across the bay at half past nine and nosed up against the strip's little dock, carrying one passenger in a rain coat too clean for the weather, Silas watched him come up the gravel path and felt the particular dread of a man who recognizes trouble by its posture.

The man walked like paperwork. Shoulders squared to some invisible regulation, briefcase held flat against his hip, shoes that had never known mud deeper than a garden bed. He stopped at the edge of the overhang, looked up at the PEMBERTON AIR — CHARTERS & FREIGHT sign with its missing second E, and produced a smile that had been issued to him along with the coat.

"Mr. Pemberton." He extended a hand. "Corliss. Bureau of Interior Survey."

"Nobody flies in this," Silas said, nodding at the rain.

"Actually, I'm not here to fly. I'm here to hire you to fly." Corliss's handshake was dry and brief, a transaction rather than a greeting. "May we talk inside?"

Silas looked at him a moment longer than politeness required. Then he jerked his chin toward the office door and went in ahead of him, moving the coffee-stained chart off the second chair without apology. The office smelled of avgas and old paper and the mildew that lived in the wall behind the radio set no matter what he did about it. He'd built the room himself, mostly, back when building things still seemed like something a person did with a life.

Corliss sat down the way men sit when they've been briefed on you. He didn't look around at the charts pinned to the walls—every river from the Sable Fork to the headwaters of the Vane, annotated in Silas's own hand—or at the photograph of a girl of maybe nineteen standing on the Otter's float, squinting into sun. He looked at Silas, and he opened the briefcase.

"I'll be direct," Corliss said. "A survey team working the upper Vane has missed two scheduled check-ins. Six people. We're mounting a search, and we need someone who knows that stretch of river well enough to fly it low, in deteriorating conditions, before the monsoon closes the passes. That window is—" he glanced at nothing, at some calendar in his head, "—short. Perhaps five days. Possibly less."

"Upper Vane." Silas turned his mug slowly on the desk. "That's gorge country. Weather comes off the icefields sideways. Half my colleagues won't fly it in June."

"Which is why we're not asking your colleagues. We're asking you. You've run that corridor eleven times in three years. Your name came up in every conversation we had on the coast."

"Conversations I wasn't part of."

"Standard diligence." Corliss laid a folder on the desk. It was heavy stock, government gray, and it made a sound against the wood that Silas would remember later—the soft, final slap of a thing being decided. "Triple your standard charter rate. Hazard supplement on top. Fuel and maintenance costs covered separately, invoiced directly to the Bureau. And a completion bonus if you have us on station above the last known camp within seventy-two hours."

Silas didn't touch the folder. Outside, the rain worked at the roof in its patient, endless way, and somewhere down the strip old Marta's dog barked twice at nothing and gave up.

"Six people," Silas said. "What were they surveying?"

"Geology. Mineral potential. Routine work."

"Routine work doesn't miss check-ins." He said it mildly, watching. "Radio's spotty up there, sure. But a team that size carries two sets. Somebody calls in late, not silent."

Corliss's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was itself an answer. "Weather has been severe."

"Weather is always severe. That's not news, that's geography." Silas leaned back. "Here's what I know, Mr. Corliss, free of charge. When a bureau pays triple rate plus hazard plus bonus, it isn't buying a flight. It's buying silence afterward. Whatever those six people walked into, somebody upstairs wants found fast, or found quietly, or both, and they don't much care which. So before I sign anything, tell me plainly: am I flying a rescue or an errand?"

For the first time, something moved behind Corliss's face—not guilt, Silas thought, but recalculation. The look of a man checking which script he'd been authorized to use.

"You're flying a search," Corliss said. "Everything else is above my clearance, and I mean that as a courtesy, not a dodge. I can't tell you what I don't have."

*You have plenty,* Silas thought. *You just don't have permission.*

He should have sent the man back across the bay. Later—in the weeks after, when everything had burned down to ash and consequence—he would replay this hour again and again, looking for the moment he could have stood up, named a polite refusal, and gone back to small honest runs and large dishonest debts. But the truth of it was simpler and uglier: Corliss slid a second sheet across the desk, a payment schedule with numbers that had another zero where Silas's life usually didn't, and fourteen thousand six hundred dollars rose in him like floodwater, drowning every instinct he owned.

Debt doesn't care about instincts. Debt sits in a drawer and waits.

"Passengers?" Silas asked instead.

"One liaison—that's me—and four field personnel. Search specialists."

"Cargo?"

"Equipment. Standard."

"Then you won't mind itemizing it."

Something flickered there, quick as a fish under a boat. Corliss drew a manifest from the folder—a real one, typed, stamped—and laid it beside the payment schedule. Silas read it twice. Radios, tents, medical kit, rations, rope. All accounted for. But yesterday, watching the tender cross the bay, he'd seen the crates loaded on it, and he could count, and the crates on that dock had outnumbered these lines by half again. Two long cases with no listed weight class. A sealed drum, the kind used for things that needed keeping very dry or very dark.

"The tender carried more than this," Silas said, tapping the page.

"The tender also carried personal effects. Not everything requires a line item."

"In my plane, everything requires a line item. Weight and balance isn't bureaucracy, Mr. Corliss. It's the difference between flying and falling." He pushed the manifest back. "I load what's listed. Anything else stays on the dock."

A pause, long enough for the rain to fill it. Then Corliss nodded, once, as though Silas had passed some test whose results were prewritten. "Acceptable."

And that word—*acceptable*, delivered like a stamp on a form—did more to sour Silas's stomach than any number on any page. Men who negotiated didn't say *acceptable*. Men who had already decided said it.

He took the contract folder at last and opened it. The terms were clean enough on their face: charter services, route specified as *upriver per liaison direction*, duration open-ended until released. Standard indemnity clauses. And then, stapled inside the back cover, a single sealed envelope, wax-stamped with the Bureau's crest, labeled in block capitals: **TO BE OPENED ONLY ON STATION ABOVE LAST KNOWN CAMP.**

"What's this?"

"Operational instructions. They're sealed for the protection of the mission."

"My plane doesn't carry envelopes I can't read. If there's a diversion order in there—if you want me hauling cargo somewhere other than where I think I'm going—I need to know now, not at ten thousand feet with the weather coming down."

"It contains coordinates and protocols relevant only once we arrive. Reading it early changes nothing except security."

"Whose security?"

Corliss regarded him with something that might, in a better man, have been respect. "Mr. Pemberton. Six people are missing. In five days, the monsoon seals that valley until October. Whatever you believe about paperwork—and I can see you believe very little—there are six human beings upriver whose odds shrink by the hour while we discuss envelopes. I'm authorized to offer triple rate. I'm not authorized to unseal anything. Those are the facts I have, and the clock is running on all of them."

It was good rhetoric. Silas had heard better lies dressed worse and worse lies dressed better, but rarely a lie dressed this honestly. That was the trick of it, he thought. The best handlers never lied about the true things. Six people missing: probably true. Monsoon closing the passes: certainly true. And wrapped inside all that truth, snug as contraband, whatever it was they actually wanted.

He looked at the payment schedule again. He thought about the bank letter in the drawer, the one addressed to *Mr. S. Pimberton*. He thought about the empty mornings, and the photograph on the wall, and seven years of flying for strangers because there was no one left to fly for.

"Search specialists," he said. "Who leads them?"

"A cartographer. Nia Okafor. She was part of the original survey—grounded with an injury when the team went upriver. She knows the terrain better than anyone alive who isn't currently missing." Corliss paused, and for just a moment the issued smile slipped, and underneath it was something tired and human. "She requested you by name, incidentally. Said you were the only pilot who'd ever landed her on the Vane in fog and apologized for the turbulence."

Silas almost smiled despite himself. He remembered her—a compact woman with ink-stained fingers who'd corrected his river notes mid-flight and been right. "She injured?"

"Ankle. She'll insist it doesn't matter. It will matter."

"And the others?"

"Field men. You'll meet them tomorrow, oh-six-hundred, at the strip." Corliss stood, buttoned his immaculate coat, and set a pen on the contract with the gentle precision of a man laying a trap he expected you to walk into willingly. "I'll leave you to decide. Though I'd note the window shortens with every hour of rain."

He stepped out under the overhang. Silas watched him go down the gravel toward the dock, unhurried, certain, a man who had never once in his life been rained on by accident.

* * *

The office got dark early that day, the storm pressing its face against the single window. Silas lit the lamp and read the contract twice more, then a third time aloud, slowly, the way his father had taught him to read anything that wanted his signature: *listen for the sentence that's shorter than it should be.* He found three. One limited liability in language so smooth it took him a minute to see the hook buried in it. One granted the Bureau "directional authority over routing" — meaning they could point his plane wherever they liked and call it the contract. And one, quiet as a footstep, noted that "all materials observed in the course of operations remain the property of the Crown Bureau and may not be disclosed, described, or retained."

Observed. As in seen. As in everything his eyes did between takeoff and landing belonged to them.

He set the pages down and went and stood in the hangar doorway with the lamp behind him, looking at the Otter in the rain. She was twenty-three years old and ugly as a shoe, and she had never once lied to him. Every honest mile of his life had been flown in her.

"You'd tell me," he said to her. "If you could."

The rain answered, which was to say it said nothing, endlessly.

He thought about the sealed envelope. He thought about the crates that outnumbered the manifest. He thought about six people upriver in the gray teeth of the gorge, and whether they were alive, and whether anyone asking after them was asking in order to help them or in order to close a file. He thought about Xanthe, briefly, the way he did most nights—seven years gone now, chasing rumors of a settlement that didn't exist on any map, leaving him nothing but a postmark and a compass he'd engraved himself. People who vanished up rivers. People the country decided to forget. He had told himself for seven years that it wasn't his business, that neutrality was a kind of safety, that a pilot who flew for everyone served no one and therefore betrayed no one.

But neutrality, he understood suddenly, standing in the lamplight with the pen waiting on the desk, was just a debt of a different kind. Owed to no one. Collectible never. And it kept a man exactly as poor as the other kind.

Silas picked up the pen.

He signed where the X was—his own X, he noticed, the same mark he'd once cut into brass for his sister—and the nib scratched loud in the small room, louder than the rain, and when it was done the contract looked no different and the world felt no different and yet something in him had shifted its weight, quietly, the way ballast shifts in a plane before you feel the nose start to drop.

He sealed the folder. He wrote *oh-six-hundred* on a scrap of paper and weighted it on the desk where he couldn't miss it. Then he dug the bank letter out of the drawer, looked at the misspelled name a long moment, and fed it to the stove, because a man who has just sold a piece of his carefulness might as well stop pretending he intends to buy it back.

Outside, far up the valley of the Vane, beyond the ridgelines drowned in cloud, the rain was already falling on the empty survey camp—on pitched tents and running instruments and whatever else waited there—and on a river running hard toward the sea, carrying, though no map yet showed it, a clearing that had been erased from every chart Nia Okafor ever drew.

Silas slept badly, and at four-thirty he was awake, and the first gray light found him already checking the fuel straws by hand, because instinct was the one creditor he still intended to pay.
