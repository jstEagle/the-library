## 3. Passengers

The ankle was the worst part. Not the pain—Nia Okafor had broken bones before and knew how to hold a conversation through worse—but the way everyone looked at it. The way the doctor at the coastal clinic had wrapped it in a brace and said *grounded* like he was talking about an aircraft, like her body was a machine that had failed inspection and needed to be pulled from service until further notice.

She had been three miles downstream when the others went missing. Three miles, a sprained ankle, and a radio that had chosen that morning to die. She had sat on a gravel bar with her foot elevated on a dry bag and watched the weather build over the gorge, and she had told herself: *they'll be back by dark.* Then: *they're delayed by the crossing at Sable Bend.* Then nothing, because there was nothing left to tell herself, and the river kept saying what rivers say, which is nothing, forever, in a voice you can't argue with.

Now she stood on the strip at Fort Meridian with a duffel over one shoulder and a walking stick cut from river birch, watching a bush plane come in low over the treeline with the particular arrogance of a pilot who knows his aircraft better than the manufacturer does.

Silas Pemberton. She'd heard the name for years before she ever heard it attached to a face. Every settlement upriver knew Pemberton's plane the way coastal towns know the tide schedule—when it came, things arrived; when it didn't, you made do. The liaison's office had contracted him for the search flight, and Nia had put her name on the passenger manifest before the ink on the incident report was dry.

The plane flared, touched down on the gravel with two soft bounces, and rolled to a stop with its nose angled into the wind out of pure habit. The propeller spun down. A man unfolded himself from the cockpit—tall, weathered, somewhere past forty, with the kind of stillness that some people mistook for slowness until they saw his hands move.

He looked at her ankle first. She noticed that. Everyone did.

"You're the cartographer," he said. Not a question. His voice was low and unhurried, the voice of a man who had learned that everything urgent could be said calmly if you said it early enough.

"Nia Okafor."

"Pemberton." He came around the wing, and she watched him do something she recognized immediately: he looked at her the way she looked at terrain. Assessing. Measuring load against capacity. Deciding whether she was cargo or ballast.

"I'm coming," she said, before he could decide out loud.

"Didn't ask."

"You were going to."

His mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might have lived once, before it was repossessed. "Liaison says you're grounded."

"The liaison says a lot of things." She let that sit. "I mapped that stretch of river. Every bend, every bar, every deadfall from the confluence to the headwater canyons. I drew the chart your government is using to look for my team. There are four people on this earth who know where the sandbars shift after every rain, and I'm the only one who isn't currently missing."

"That's three reasons," Pemberton said. "You said four."

"Myself included."

Something shifted behind his eyes—a recalculation, quick as a needle settling. He crouched, uninvited, and looked at her ankle without touching it. "Grade one sprain?"

"Grade two. Six weeks ago. It holds."

"It held," he corrected. "Different verb. Upriver's not a clinic floor. If it gives out on a portage, someone carries you, and someone carries your pack, and that's weight my fuel math doesn't have room for."

"Then I'll carry less." She dug into her duffel and produced her kit, laid it open on the gravel between them like a hand of cards: spare wrap, compression bandage, anti-inflammatories, a second pair of boots with the ankles reinforced. "I've been planning this since the day they carried me out. You think I don't know exactly what I cost?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The wind came down the valley and moved the grass along the strip in a long silver wave, and somewhere beyond the fuel drums a raven made its flat wooden sound. Nia held still and let the silence work. She had learned silence from maps—the blank spaces taught you more than the labeled ones, if you were patient enough to read them.

"All right," Pemberton said finally. "But we do this my way on my aircraft. Preflight starts now. You'll want to see how she's loaded anyway, because if we find your people, we're bringing back six bodies' worth of gear minimum, and every kilo you brought is a kilo of theirs that stays on the ground."

It was, she realized, the closest thing to welcome he was going to offer. It was also, she noticed, the first thing anyone had said to her in nine days that treated her like a professional instead of a casualty.

* * *

They walked the plane together, and it became, without either of them announcing it, an examination.

Not of the aircraft—though the aircraft got examined too, thoroughly, Pemberton's hands moving over fabric and strut and tire with the absent tenderness of a man checking a sleeping child. What they were really doing was circling each other. She could feel it: two animals determining whether the other was predator, prey, or merely another creature trying to survive the same season.

"So," she said, as he drained a fuel sample into a glass tube and held it up to the light, checking for water. "How long have you flown the interior?"

"Long enough."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, and moved on to check the elevator cables.

Nia smiled despite herself. Fine. Two could play. She ran her own inspection while he worked—she always inspected whatever carried her, a habit from a childhood spent on ferries that sank regularly—and let her questions come sideways instead of straight.

"Fuel caches," she said, nodding at the drums stenciled with coordinates. "You've staged these all the way to the source basin. That's a lot of aviation gas sitting in the woods for a man who claims neutrality."

"Neutrality's expensive." He screwed the cap back on the tank. "Caches aren't politics. They're arithmetic. Headwind up the gorge eats range; I stage fuel so I never have to gamble the return leg. Same reason you draw contour lines before anyone asks about elevation. Preparation isn't a position."

"Everything's a position," Nia said. "You just get to choose whether you admit yours."

That stopped him. He turned and looked at her fully for the first time—not at the ankle, not at the manifest, but at her—and she had the strange sensation of being surveyed herself, contour lines drawn across her face by someone deciding what kind of country she was.

"You lost people up there," he said. It wasn't sympathy. It was data collection, and she respected that far more than sympathy would have.

"Five colleagues. Reyes, Adeyemi, Lindqvist, Marsh, and Tomás. Eleven days ago." Her voice stayed level because she had practiced it staying level, alone, in a clinic room, saying their names over and over until they stopped sounding like an accusation. "The official story is flash flood. The river didn't flood. I've read that gauge for six years; it didn't come up eight inches. So either they walked away from a functioning camp—which the state of the camp argues against—or someone took them, and neither possibility is one your government's paperwork seems interested in."

"And you think flying back in with me fixes that."

"I think sitting here fixes nothing, and I've had enough of nothing." She picked up her walking stick. "Your turn. Why does a man stage fuel caches to the forbidden source of a river nobody pays him to visit? Because the liaison's contract covers the survey camp, twelve miles below the gorge narrows. The caches go further. I checked the coordinates."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind his calm like a fish under ice. Then it was gone, and he was checking the pitot tube as though it required his complete devotion.

"Caches are old," he said. "From other runs."

"They're fresh. The drum seals are this season's stamp. I'm a cartographer, Pemberton. Reading marks on things is the entire job."

He set down the tool. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped its pleasantness, and what was underneath was quieter and considerably more honest. "You want the truth? I don't know why I staged them. I told myself it was arithmetic. But when the liaison offered triple pay to fly upriver, I already had the fuel waiting, like part of me signed the contract before the rest of me heard about it." He looked down the strip, toward the grey wall of cloud stacked over the interior like masonry. "I don't fly for people anymore. Haven't for years. Cargo doesn't look at you. Cargo doesn't go missing. And then somebody offers me money to fly toward the one place I've been avoiding for seven years, and I say yes before I've counted the debt I'd be paying off."

Nia stood very still. Seven years. She filed it—not to use against him, but because she understood the architecture of avoided places. She had one too. It was eleven days old and five colleagues deep.

"We're both lying about why we're going," she said.

"Yes."

"Good. Honest liars make better crew than sincere believers." She extended her hand. "Preflight's done. Your aircraft checks out. So do I."

He took it. His grip was careful—measuring her strength rather than testing it—and when he released her, something had settled between them that wasn't trust, not yet, but was at least a shared treaty: neither would handle the other, and neither would pretend otherwise.

* * *

They loaded in the last hour of light.

Pemberton worked like a man solving a puzzle in three dimensions, and Nia found herself genuinely enjoying it—weight forward here, the satellite phone wedged soft-side, her chart case strapped flat where it couldn't warp. He asked her opinion twice and used it both times, and didn't make a ceremony of it, which she appreciated more than she could have explained.

Halfway through, he paused over a crate stenciled with the liaison office's seal and frowned.

"This wasn't on the manifest I was given."

"What's in it?"

"Haven't opened it. Sealed orders travel sealed." He weighed it in his hands, and Nia watched his jaw tighten. "Forty kilos of something the government needs in the wilderness but won't name. You want to know what I've learned about sealed crates in twenty years of flying?"

"That they're heavier on the way home than the way out."

He glanced at her, surprised, then laughed—a short, rusty sound, like an engine starting after a long winter. "Exactly that."

The last crate went in, the tie-downs went taut, and Pemberton stood in the fading light with his checklist, reading items aloud in a murmur while the ravens traded insults in the spruces. Nia climbed into the right seat and buckled in, and the cockpit closed around them—smell of avgas and canvas and old coffee, the instrument faces catching the last orange light like small patient moons.

"One more thing," she said, as he reached for the starter. "When we find them—if we find them—I need you to understand something. I'm not looking for survivors. I'm looking for answers. Those aren't always the same mission, and I need to know now whether that makes us incompatible."

Pemberton's hand rested on the key. Outside, the wind had begun to carry the smell of rain from the high country, wet stone and cold green distance, the monsoon sending its calling card ahead of the storm.

"Seven years ago," he said quietly, "my sister flew out of my life chasing a rumor about a settlement that doesn't exist on any map. I've spent seven years telling myself she chose it, and seven years knowing that's not the whole truth." He turned the key, and the engine caught with a cough and a roar, and the whole airframe shivered alive around them. "So no, Okafor. Answers and survivors aren't the same mission. I've just been pretending mine was only one of them too."

The plane rolled, gathered itself, and lifted off the strip into the thickening dusk, the river glinting below like a wire pulled taut between them and everything neither of them had said.

Two hours upstream, at the survey camp, the instruments were still running. And forty kilometers beyond that, in a clearing that appeared on no chart drawn by human hands, something waited that would change what both of them thought they were searching for.
