## 4. Upstream

*Silas*

The river was the only honest thing in the territory.

Everything else lied to you—altimeters in cold air, compasses near the iron ranges, government men with their sealed envelopes and their triple pay. But the river ran where it ran, cut what it cut, and if you followed it you could not be lost, only delayed. Silas Pemberton had been following it for eleven years, first as a boy in his father's floatplane, then alone, and he knew its moods the way other men knew their wives': the brown sulk of high water, the green whisper of August, the way it went glass-still an hour before weather, like a held breath.

It was holding its breath now.

He glanced left, out over the gorge wall, where the sky had begun to stack itself. The morning had come up clean and blue, the kind of blue that made tourists pay for scenic flights, but by nine o'clock the horizon east of the pass had gone the color of a bruise coming up under skin—a soft gray-violet smudge that hadn't been there at dawn. Storm cells. Building fast. He'd seen monsoons arrive early before, seen them swallow a whole flying season two weeks ahead of the almanac, and something in the set of that cloud line made his hands tighten on the yoke.

Behind him, Nia Okafor sat with her charts on her knees and her sprained ankle propped on a crate of survey instruments, and she had not spoken in forty minutes. He'd decided to take that as a good sign. People who talked constantly on a flight were either frightened or performing; people who went quiet were working. She was working—pencil moving, eyes flicking from window to paper and back, checking the river against her own lines the way a woman checks a stranger's story against what she knows to be true.

"Radio's been dead since the bend," she said finally, without looking up.

"Not dead. Quiet." He keyed the set anyway, thumbed through the frequencies out of habit. The bush band, usually a river of chatter this time of morning—Marta out of Kessel Creek complaining about her carb heat, old Duvalle reading freight manifests aloud to nobody, somebody's kid learning Morse by keying garbage across the whole spectrum. Silence. Not even static-shape, not even the ghost-breath of a carrier wave. Just the clean electronic nothing of a room after everyone has left it.

"Huh," Nia said.

"Yeah."

"That's not normal."

"No," Silas said. "It isn't." He didn't tell her the rest—that in eleven years he could count on one hand the mornings the bush band had gone fully silent, and every one of those mornings had preceded something. A fire year. A flood. The week the survey teams first came north with their sealed orders. Weather did not silence radios. Weather made radios scream. Silence meant nobody was talking, and nobody talking meant everybody was listening, or everybody had decided there was nothing worth saying, or—

Or the storm ahead had already eaten someone.

He climbed two hundred feet without deciding to, some animal part of him wanting altitude between himself and the gorge walls, and the De Havilland groaned up into thinner air and a colder one. Below, the river narrowed and quickened, white water stitching itself down the throat of the canyon, and the walls rose on either side like the pages of a book closing.

"Storm's building faster than the forecast," Nia said. She'd turned in her seat to look back along their track, and her face in the gray light had gone careful. "I've flown this corridor four times. It doesn't do this. You get buildups, sure, afternoon stuff, but—" She stopped. Started again. "This is a wall. That's a wall forming."

"I see it."

"Do we turn around?"

Silas looked at the river ahead, running north toward the source no map admitted to, and thought about a brass compass he wasn't yet letting himself think about, and said: "We keep going until going stops making sense."

Nia looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his profile seemed to satisfy something in her, or maybe disappoint her, or both. She turned back to her charts.

"Then navigate," she said. "I'll watch the sky."

* * *

*Nia*

The clearing appeared at 10:42, and Nia Okafor would spend the rest of her life being certain of the time, because she checked her watch the moment she understood what she was seeing, the way you pinch yourself in a dream.

She had drawn this valley. That was the thing. Four seasons of survey work, three hundred hours of aerial photography, soil cores and stream gauges and a theodolite she'd carried on her own back up slopes that didn't have names because she was the one who named them. Every bend of this river lived in her hand. She could close her eyes and feel the terrain the way you feel your own teeth. And the terrain here—the long meander below the second gorge, the one she'd labeled *Oxbow 7* in her own neat cartographer's script—was forest. Unbroken canopy, old growth, the kind of dark green roof that took two centuries to weave and showed every clearing, every slide, every deadfall from the air. She knew it the way she knew her own palm.

There was no clearing in Oxbow 7.

But there it was below them, maybe a mile off the east bank: a pale wound in the canopy, roughly oval, big enough to land a plane in twice over. And it was not a scar. Scars had texture—blowdown tangles, erosion fans, the gray skeleton of burned timber. This was smooth. Mown, almost. Grass the color of new money, ringed by trees that stood back from it in a perfect unbroken circle, as if they had agreed among themselves to leave the space open.

"Silas." Her voice came out wrong, thin. She cleared her throat. "Do you see—"

"I see it."

"It's not on my maps." She was already flipping through her chart case, hands moving faster than her thoughts, pulling sheets she knew by heart. Oxbow 7, surveyed June, flown July, ground-truthed in August with her own boots on her own transects. Forest. Continuous canopy, density class four, no openings greater than fifty meters. She'd written it herself. She'd *signed* it. And beneath the plane, real and sunlit and utterly impossible, lay an opening half a kilometer across.

"Could be a blowdown," Silas said, but he didn't sound like he believed it, and he banked slightly to bring it abeam, and she loved and hated him a little for checking.

"It's not a blowdown." Blowdowns fell outward, radiating, a starburst of root plates and snapped stems. This circle's edge was clean. "Could be old burn regrowth?"

"You'd see charcoal. Snags." He was quiet a moment. "You'd see it on your photography. Three years of flights, you said."

"Four." Her mouth had gone dry. "Four years of flights. I have imagery of this exact meander from four different seasons and there is *nothing there*, Silas. There is grass and there are trees and there is no gap between them." She heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, breathe, press her palms flat against the chart on her knees. Cartographers did not panic. Cartographers noted discrepancies. But every discipline had its nightmare, and hers was this: the ground disagreeing with the record, the world quietly editing itself behind her back, her life's careful work revealed as a description of a place that no longer existed—or had never existed at all.

And underneath the fear, unwelcome and insistent, another thought, the one she'd been refusing all morning:

*Something down there does not want to be on a map.*

She looked at the clearing again as Silas swung wide around it. From this angle she could see detail now—structures? No. Not structures. Something low, long, pale-roofed, half-swallowed by the tree line, so camouflaged against the canopy that her eye kept sliding off it, refusing to resolve it into buildings. Smoke, maybe. A thread of smoke rising from somewhere near the circle's center, straight and thin in the still air, the smoke of a fire tended by someone who did not want it seen.

"Loggers?" she offered, and heard how hollow it sounded.

"Loggers leave roads." Silas's voice had changed. Lower. She glanced over and found him staring down at the clearing with an expression she couldn't read—not surprise exactly, something older than surprise, something that looked almost like recognition, which made no sense, because Silas Pemberton had never been within a hundred miles of this river's middle reaches before this week. "Loggers leave stumps. Roads. Trash. This leaves nothing. This leaves—" He stopped.

"Leaves what?"

"Nothing," he said again, but his hands had gone very still on the yoke, stiller than they'd been all morning, and Nia had spent enough years reading terrain to know when a man was reading something else entirely.

* * *

*Silas*

He told himself it was coincidence. He was good at telling himself things; he'd had practice.

A clearing off the river. Fine. The interior was full of clearings—old homesteads, forgotten airstrips, places where men had tried and failed and left. Except he knew this stretch. Not from charts—from stories, from the drunk confessions of old pilots in river-town bars, the ones who'd flown the source country before the government closed it. *There's nothing up there worth the fuel,* they'd say, too quickly. *Nothing but trees.* And one of them, Duvalle, ten years ago, three rums deep, had gripped Silas's sleeve and said: *If you ever see a circle of grass where the maps say forest, boy, you put your nose south and you don't look back.*

He'd laughed at the time. He wasn't laughing now.

The radio crackled. Both of them flinched.

"—say again, Kestrel Two-One, say again your—" A voice, distant, shredded by interference, a voice Silas half-knew. Marta, out of Kessel Creek. Or something wearing Marta's frequency. Static rolled through it like water through gravel. "—not on the—repeat, it is *not on the*—"

Kestrel Two-One. That call sign belonged to the government's second survey aircraft. The one that had gone upriver three days ago with six souls aboard and stopped answering.

Silas keyed the transmit. "Station calling Kessel, this is Pemberton, November-Four-Seven. Say your position." Nothing. He tried again, louder, stupidly louder, as if volume could bridge whatever gulf had opened in the signal. "Marta, if that's you, come back."

Static. Then, faint, warped, stretched like taffy: "—going *dark*, they're going—"

And then the band closed. Not faded—closed, the way a door closes, the carrier wave dropping out whole, and the headset filled with that clean dead nothing again, and the silence it left behind was worse than any static, because static at least meant someone was trying.

Beside him, Nia had gone very pale. "That was the survey aircraft's call sign," she said. "That was Reyes's plane. Reyes was flying right seat." Her voice was level the way ice is level. "Silas. They were somewhere on this river three days ago."

"I know."

"And now the radio says—" She stopped. Started over, slower, a cartographer pinning down coordinates. "The radio says they found something that is not on the map, and then they stopped transmitting."

Silas looked down at the clearing, already falling behind them, the pale circle shrinking into the green, the thread of smoke still rising, patient, unhurried. Ahead, the gorge bent north and the river ran on toward its hidden source, and above the far bend the storm had built itself a tower now, blue-black, glittering at the edges with the hard light of hail, a city wall rising across the whole width of the sky. Between the clearing and the storm, the river ran narrow and white and silent, and every instinct he owned—the ones that had kept him alive eleven years of flying freight and medicine and other people's emergencies—every one of them said *turn around, turn around now.*

His hand moved on the throttle. Not back. Forward, a quarter inch, a gesture with no mechanical meaning, just a promise.

"There," Nia said suddenly, pointing.

He looked. On the southern lip of the clearing, small even from eight hundred feet, a figure stood in the grass. Alone. Facing the river. Facing *them*—he felt it rather than saw it, the way you feel eyes between your shoulder blades. The figure did not wave. Did not run. Did not move at all, except once, deliberately, to raise one arm—not waving, he realized, heart beginning to hammer against his ribs like something caged—and point. North. Upstream. Toward the source, toward the storm, toward the place the maps refused.

Then the canopy swallowed the clearing, and the figure, and the smoke, and the radio stayed silent, and the De Havilland carried them on into the narrowing gorge with the storm building black behind the hills like a debt coming due.

Neither of them spoke for a long time. When Nia finally did, her voice was quiet and steady and it scared him more than the radio had.

"Whoever that was," she said, "they wanted us to keep going."

"Yes," Silas said, and pushed the plane north.
