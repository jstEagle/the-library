## 5. Gear and No Tracks

The river bent north around a shoulder of black stone, and there it was.

Silas saw it first from the air, the way you see anything in that country—too late, all at once, a detail resolving out of the green like a photograph developing. The clearing sat on the inside of the bend, a half-acre of cut grass and trampled mud, and in the middle of it stood four tents in a neat row, guy-lines taut, fly-sheets angled to shed rain. A solar panel leaned against a crate at the precise angle the morning sun would demand. A tarp covered something rectangular near the treeline.

It looked, he thought as he brought the plane around for a second pass, exactly like a camp that had been left ten minutes ago.

Except for the silence. Except for the way the grass at the clearing's edge stood waist-high and unbroken, a green moat with no gate in it anywhere.

"There," Nia said beside him. She had her face nearly against the window, one hand pressed flat to the glass as if she could hold the image still. "That's ours. That's—Silas, that's Camp Two. That's where they were supposed to be on the ninth."

He throttled back and let the plane sink along the river's current, reading the wind off the water the way his father had taught him before his father had taught him anything else. The strip—if you could call the mowed margin of the clearing a strip—ran east-west, longer than he needed. He flared, touched, and rolled to a stop with the prop washing a slow circle through the tall grass, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the forest a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, twice, and stopped.

"Your radio's been dead for six days," Silas said. "Their instruments are running."

"I can see that."

"Say what you're thinking, then, because I don't like mine."

Nia unbuckled herself with the deliberate economy of someone who had decided fear was a problem to be scheduled for later. "I'm thinking that camp is maintained. Somebody's cleaning the panels. Somebody's staking those tents. That's not abandonment. That's—" She stopped. He watched her jaw work. "That's housekeeping."

They got out into air that smelled of wet stone and crushed vegetation and something else, faint, underneath—woodsmoke, old and cold, the ghost of a fire days dead. Silas took the flare gun, the machete, and the satellite phone that had shown one bar of nothing since the gorge, and Nia took her survey case and a walking stick she'd cut herself at the last fuel stop, and they crossed the grass moat together, boots sinking into soil so soft it swallowed the sound of their steps.

Up close the camp was worse. Up close it was perfect.

The main tent's flap was tied back with a bowline, dressed and clean, the kind of knot you learn in the first week of fieldwork and never lose. Inside, four sleeping bags were rolled and stowed in their compression sacks, lined up like loaves on a shelf. A mess kit hung from a ridge-line, dry. A pair of reading glasses sat folded on top of a field manual—*Fluvial Geomorphology of the Upper Reaches*, Nia's own name inked inside the cover—and beside them, a paperback novel with a bus ticket for a bookmark, the ticket dated the eighth of the month.

Nobody leaves a book at the good part. Nobody leaves their glasses. Silas had lived alone long enough to know the small archaeology of a person's absence, the things they carried and the things they left, and none of it was here. It was as if four men had stood up from their lives mid-sentence and walked into the trees without disturbing a blade of grass on the way.

The galley crate was worse. Nia opened it and stood looking down into it for a long time without touching anything. Rice in sealed bags. Flour, tins of fish, a brick of tea, sugar in a screw-top jar with the lid on. A bag of oranges, and when she lifted one out it was firm and heavy and the skin gave under her thumb the way a living thing gives.

"Three weeks of provisions," she said quietly. "We always over-provisioned. Marcus used to say the river would eat the radio and the weather would eat the schedule, so the food was the only thing we got to control." She set the orange back down with the care of a woman returning an artifact. "It's all here. It's all *fresh*. Silas, who is feeding a camp that has nobody in it?"

He didn't answer. He was crouched at the fire pit, and the fire pit was the first thing in the whole clearing that told the truth. The ash had been raked flat—raked, deliberately, the way you'd tidy a hearth—but beneath the surface crust the coals were layered, and the layers said *burned hot, burned often, banked and rekindled*, a fire tended across days. Someone had lived here. Someone had cooked here, recently enough that when he pressed two fingers into the ash at the pit's edge he felt warmth like a memory of warmth, or maybe only imagined it, or maybe the ground itself had learned to lie.

"Tracks," Nia said behind him. She'd walked the clearing's perimeter twice, slow, head down, and now she stood at the southern edge where the grass began. "There are no tracks. Not ours—I mean not *theirs*. Not the team's. Not anyone's. The ground here holds a print for days; I've read sign on this river for six years. And there's nothing. Not even bird trails. It's like—" she searched for it, and he watched her not want to find it— "like the camp was set down here from above. Like a stage."

"Stages have audiences."

"Stages have *crews*."

They worked the camp the way professionals work anything, because the work was a handrail and they both needed one. Silas checked the instruments under the tarp: a theodolite on its tripod, dust cover on, a data logger with its little green light pulsing like a slow heartbeat, still writing to its card. A rain gauge, full to the eleven-millimeter line. A wind sock on a pole, faded, hanging dead in the still air. Everything serviced. Everything *tended*.

Nia went for the logbook.

It lived in a green waterproof case clipped inside the main tent, standard survey protocol, and when she carried it out into the open light and opened it, Silas saw her face change in a way he'd only ever seen faces change at funerals—not the grief, exactly, but the moment before it, when the understanding arrives ahead of the feeling and you have to stand there holding it.

"Silas."

He came and stood beside her. The logbook was theirs, official, the survey's daily record, and the handwriting was Nia's—of course it was, she was the team's cartographer, the log was her office. He knew her hand already from the charts she'd shown him in the hangar: quick, forward-leaning, the crossbars on her sevens struck like small acts of defiance. The entries ran through the eighth of the month. *Camp Two established. River gauge set. Weather holding. R. complaining about the tea.*

Then a gap. Then an entry dated the eleventh.

Three days after the team vanished. Three days after Nia, grounded in the coastal town with her sprained ankle, had been a hundred miles and a mountain range away from this clearing.

The entry was in her handwriting. Not similar. *Hers.* The same forward lean, the same defiant sevens, the same way she wrote her *m*s with a little valley in the middle that he'd noticed because he'd spent two days studying her charts and had nothing else to do with his eyes.

It read: *All personnel relocated for their safety. Survey suspended pending review. Do not attempt to locate. Equipment to be maintained in place. —N. Okafor.*

Silas read it three times. The clearing was very quiet around them, the kind of quiet that has weight, that leans on your shoulders.

"That's not your hand," he said, because someone had to say something, and the alternative was silence, and silence in this place felt like agreeing to something.

"It's my hand." Her voice was flat and careful, a voice walking on river ice. "It's my *hand*, Silas. Look at the sevens. Look at—" She stopped. Her thumb moved over the page without touching it, hovering, the way you'd hover over a wound. "I write that way because of a penmanship teacher in secondary school who made us copy maritime charts. Nobody else writes sevens like that. Nobody alive that I've ever met."

"Then who wrote it?"

"I don't know. That's not—" She closed the logbook, opened it again, as if the entry might have reconsidered. "That's not the part that's wrong. The part that's wrong is the *content*. Relocated for their safety. Do not attempt to locate. That's not survey language. That's not *any* language I'd use. That's a—" she looked up at him, and her eyes were bright and furious and frightened all at once— "that's a *message*, Silas. That's someone leaving a note. In my handwriting. In a camp where four men disappeared, telling anyone who comes looking to stop looking."

The wind moved through the treeline and the whole forest sighed, and Silas became aware, in the way you become aware of a draft in a closed room, that the clearing was oriented. The tents faced the river. The instruments faced the river. The fire pit, the tarp, the mowed margin, the neat green moat of grass—it all faced the water, presented, arranged, the way you'd arrange a room for a visitor you expected.

For a visitor like him.

"They knew someone would come," he said.

"Or they wanted someone to come."

"Same thing. Either way—" he turned a slow circle, taking in the tents, the glasses on the manual, the oranges in their bag, the raked fire, the logbook with its forged confession— "either way, this isn't where they vanished. This is where they want us to think about where they vanished. This whole camp is a sentence, and we're standing in the middle of it trying to read it backwards."

Nia sat down slowly on a supply crate, the logbook in her lap, and for the first time since he'd met her she looked small—not weak, never weak, but scaled suddenly and horribly against the thing she was looking at. "I drew every map of this river," she said. "Every contour, every oxbow, every sandbar from the delta to the falls. I *know* this country. And this country has been edited. Somebody came in behind the wilderness and rewrote it, and they used my handwriting to do it, and I don't—" her voice caught and she hated it, he could see her hating it— "I don't know how to be the cartographer of a place that lies."

Silas crouched by the fire pit again. He didn't know why, except that his hands wanted something to do, and the pit was the one dishonest thing in the clearing that had almost told the truth. He raked his fingers gently through the ash crust, and the crust broke, and beneath it—beneath the coals, beneath the layering of burns, pressed flat into the ash at the very bottom of the pit as if it had been burned there on purpose, offered to the fire and then recovered—was a scrap of paper.

Charred at the edges. Folded small. He lifted it out with the tips of two fingers, the way you'd lift a coal, and unfolded it against his knee.

It was a fragment. A corner of a larger page, maybe a quarter of a sheet, the ink smoke-blurred but legible, written in a cramped, hurried hand that belonged to no one he knew. Three lines, and the top line half-eaten by flame, and the bottom line trailing off where the paper had torn:

*—they came at night, no boat sounds, no engine, they were just—*

*—N., if you get this, the valley is real and it's not what—*

*—don't trust the—*

The last word was gone. The fire had taken it, or the hand that fed the fire to the page had taken it, and Silas sat back on his heels with the fragment trembling very slightly in his fingers and felt the whole clearing rearrange itself around him: the neat tents, the tended fire, the forged logbook entry in Nia's hand, the oranges, the glasses, the message to stop looking—all of it suddenly not a stage but a *cover*, a palimpsest, a lie written over a truth that had fought back hard enough to leave one scorched corner of itself behind.

"Silas." Nia was on her feet. She'd read it over his shoulder; he hadn't heard her cross the grass. "The valley. It said *the valley is real*."

"I know what it said."

"And the last line. *Don't trust the—*"

"I know what it said."

They looked at each other across the fire pit, across the ash, across the whole arranged and lying camp, and the wind came down the river gorge ahead of the weather like a courier running ahead of bad news, and in the trees at the clearing's southern edge—just at the limit of what he could swear to, just at the border between seeing and imagining—something moved. Not an animal. Animals moved *through* the world. This moved *with* it, a patch of the forest's own green shifting a half-step left, and then holding still, and then being nothing at all.

Silas closed his hand around the scrap of paper and stood up slowly, and did not reach for the flare gun, and kept his voice at the level of a man commenting on the weather.

"Nia," he said. "Pick up the logbook. We're leaving the camp exactly the way we found it."

"Because—"

"Because whoever wrote your handwriting is very good at knowing when someone's been here." He glanced once, deliberately, toward the southern trees, and let his eyes rest there a beat longer than casual, the way you'd nod to a neighbor across a fence. "And I want them to know we read the part they burned."
