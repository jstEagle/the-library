## 6. The Brass Compass

The camp stove was the first thing Silas checked, because stoves were honest. Tents could be struck and re-struck, instruments could lie with their needles and dials, but a stove told you everything: how long since it had burned, what had been cooked on it, whether the hands that fed it were hurried or calm. He crouched beside it in the doorway of the main survey tent, rain ticking on the canvas above him like a slow telegraph, and ran his thumb along the cold iron lip.

Ash inside. Old ash, gone grey and clumped with damp. Someone had banked this fire properly—raked the coals together, smothered them under ash so they'd hold through the night—and then no one had ever come back to wake them.

"Anything?" Nia called from the instrument table at the far end of the camp, where she stood on her good leg and leaned her weight on a tripod like it owed her money.

"Stove's been cold three days," Silas said. "Maybe four."

"That matches." She didn't look up from the barograph. "Pressure trace stops mid-afternoon, day of the disappearance. Whoever was recording it just... stopped."

He should have moved on then. There was a whole camp to search—six tents, a food cache on stilts, a fuel drum half-sunk in mud—and every hour they spent here was an hour off the clock before the monsoon closed the passes for good. But his eye had caught something under the stove. A gleam in the ash bed, wedged into the gap between the firebox and the iron leg where the ground sloped away, deliberate as a coin slipped into a crack in a doorframe.

Silas reached in.

* * *

Seven years ago, in a different life, he had bought that compass from a pawnbroker in Port Calloway who smelled of diesel and peppermints, and who swore it came off a survey plane lost in the war, which was almost certainly a lie but made a better story than *it fell off a shelf*. Brass, heavy as a knuckle, the lid worn smooth where a hundred thumbs had flipped it open. The needle wobbled drunk until Silas cleaned the pivot with a drop of sewing machine oil, and after that it settled true as scripture.

Xanthe was nineteen that spring and already halfway out the door. She'd been talking for a year about the interior—the unregistered settlements, the families the courts had chewed up and spat into the bush, people whose names had been struck from every list for debts they never owed or crimes that were really just poverty wearing a uniform. Everyone in Port Calloway talked about the interior the way you talk about weather. Xanthe talked about it the way you talk about a person you're in love with.

"You can't navigate out there by rumor," Silas told her, and pressed the compass into her palm, and folded her fingers over it one by one because she was laughing too hard to do it herself. "So take this instead."

She turned it over. On the back plate, old and shallow, someone had scratched a single letter—a crooked X, cut with a knife point by some previous owner whose name neither of them would ever know.

"Look at that," Xanthe said. "It's already mine."

"It's already yours," Silas agreed, and did not say the other thing, the thing sitting in his chest like ballast: *come back*. He'd never been good at saying things like that. He'd flown her to the last strip on the river two days later, watched her shoulder a pack bigger than she was, and she'd turned around on the grass and pointed at him with two fingers, pilot to pilot, and shouted something the propwash ate alive. He'd waved. Then he'd taken off, and the valley had swallowed the strip behind him like water closing over a stone.

For two years she wrote. Letters that took four months to arrive, carried hand to hand downriver by people who didn't exist on paper. Then the letters slowed. Then a letter came back unopened, marked *no such addressee*, and Silas understood that somewhere upstream his sister had done what the people she'd gone looking for had done before her: she had erased herself.

He kept flying. That was the thing about grief nobody warned you—it didn't ground you. It just made the sky very large and very empty, and gave you nothing to do with your hands but hold the yoke.

* * *

His fingers closed around the compass and the world went narrow and ringing, the way the cockpit goes when the engine coughs.

Brass. Heavy. The lid worn smooth.

He drew it out of the ash bed and held it in both hands, and the rain on the canvas was suddenly very loud, and Nia's voice from across the camp seemed to come through water. The hinge was stiff with corrosion but it opened when he thumbed it, that familiar resistance, that small mechanical argument he knew better than his own heartbeat. Inside, under clouded glass, the needle swung once, twice, and settled north with a confidence that felt like mockery.

On the back plate, scratched crooked and deep: **X**.

His hands shook.

Not a tremor—he'd flown through wakes and downdrafts and monsoon gusts that rattled his teeth, and his hands had stayed level because level was the job. This was different. This was the shake that starts in the sternum and works outward, the body's own confession that the mind has just rearranged itself around new information and hasn't finished the paperwork. He stared at his own fingers wrapped around the brass and thought, distantly, *so that's what that feels like*, because in thirty-one years of living and eleven of flying he had never once seen his own hands do it.

"Silas?" Nia's crutch was clicking closer across the mud. "What have you got?"

He couldn't answer. His throat had closed around seven years.

She reached him at the tent door and stopped, and he saw her take it in—the compass, the ash, his face—with the quick cartographer's sweep she gave everything, cataloguing features before deciding which ones mattered. Whatever she saw in his expression made her go still.

"My sister's," he managed.

Nia said nothing. She lowered herself onto the camp stool by the door, bad ankle propped on a supply crate, and waited. It was, he would think later, the most professional thing anyone had ever done for him—not asking, not filling the silence, just making room in it.

"Her name's Xanthe." The word came out rough, like cargo shifted wrong. "I gave her this compass seven years ago. Last time I saw her. She flew—I put her on a boat, actually, upriver, headed for—" He stopped. Laughed, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. "Headed for exactly this. A settlement that isn't on any map. People who'd erased themselves. She wanted to find them. Write about them. She was going to be a journalist, or a historian, or—she was nineteen, she was going to be everything."

"And then?"

"Then the letters stopped. Then the post came back marked *no such addressee*." He turned the compass over. The X caught the flat grey light. "I filed a missing persons report. They laughed me out of the office—literally, there was a sergeant who actually laughed. Said if she didn't want to be found, that wasn't a crime. Which is true. Which is the whole point of places like this." He looked up at Nia. "You mapped this region. Did you ever hear of a woman traveling alone upriver, seven years back? Brown hair, talks too fast, laughs at her own jokes before she finishes them?"

"I heard stories," Nia said carefully. "Everyone who works the interior hears stories. A woman who came up the river and never came down. Some of the river people call her the North Star." A pause. "Because she points people somewhere safe."

The shaking got worse before it got better. Silas set the compass on his knee and pressed both palms flat against his thighs and breathed the way you breathe at altitude when the air is thin and you need your head clear—four counts in, six counts out—until his hands obeyed again.

"She was here," he said. "Or someone who knew her was. Look at where it was placed." He nodded at the stove. "Wedged under the firebox. Not dropped—dropped things land where physics puts them. This was *put* there. In the one place in a camp full of gear where it wouldn't be found by someone doing a casual sweep, but would be found by someone who searched properly. Someone who checked the stove."

"Someone like a pilot," Nia said quietly.

"Someone like family." The word cracked something open in his chest that he'd welded shut years ago. "It's a message, Nia. Not for the survey team. For whoever came looking after. She couldn't know it would be me—but she'd know it would be someone who searches the way I taught her to search. Check the stove. Check the places that keep secrets."

Nia was quiet a moment, working it. Rain drummed. Somewhere downslope, the river ran high and brown and fast, hurrying toward the coast like it knew something they didn't.

"If your sister left this here," she said at last, "then she came to this camp. After the team disappeared—or during. And if she came here, then she knows what happened to them." Her eyes lifted to his, and he saw the same arithmetic running behind them that was running behind his: *the missing are not missing. They are upstream.* "And if she knows, then whoever took her compass and hid it under a stove wants to be followed. Carefully. By the right person."

"Or it's bait," Silas said, because somebody had to say it. "Somebody found the compass, figured out what it meant, and planted it."

"Planted it under a stove? In a camp they'd otherwise stripped of every sign of themselves?" Nia shook her head slowly. "No. That's not how bait works. Bait sits in the open. This is a whisper. This is—" She stopped, and something moved across her face, some private recognition. "This is what my grandmother used to leave in the window when the soldiers came through the village. A candle, unlit. Meaning: *we see you. We are choosing not to run yet. Prove you're worth the risk.*"

Silas looked down at the compass. The needle trembled minutely, the way needles do, alive even in stillness, always negotiating with the earth's great invisible argument.

Seven years he'd imagined finding her. In the early years the imaginings had been cinematic—her walking out of the treeline, older, sun-browned, grinning; the embrace; the airport reunion music of it. Later the imaginings had darkened. He'd imagined finding a grave marker with a name scratched on wood. He'd imagined finding nothing at all, forever, which was somehow worse. He had never once imagined this: proof of life delivered in secret grammar, hidden in cold ash, addressed to a version of himself that might never come.

"Three days cold," he said aloud, thinking with his pilot's part of his brain because the rest of him was too loud. "The stove. If she left this when the team vanished, she's had three days' lead on us. Upstream, in country she knows and we don't. But the monsoon—" He glanced west, where the horizon had gone the color of wet slate all afternoon, a bruise spreading along the sky's edge. "Forty-eight hours, maybe less, before the passes close. After that, nobody flies in or out until March."

"Then we don't waste daylight sitting in a dead camp," Nia said, and pushed herself up off the stool, and winced, and refused the arm he offered with a look that said she'd accept help when help was needed and not one second sooner.

They worked the rest of the camp fast and silent, two professionals reading the same page now without discussing it. Nothing else was out of place. Nothing else was left. The tents held only what a tidy evacuation would leave; the food cache hung untouched; the logbook entry Nia had found yesterday still sat in its impossible handwriting, dated three days after everyone vanished, in her own script, in words she swore she'd never written: *All accounted for. Do not search further.*

"Do not search further," Silas repeated under his breath as he pocketed the compass. It rode against his chest, warm from his hand, heavier than brass had any right to be. "That's either a threat or a favor."

"Could be both," Nia said. "Most true things are."

At the edge of the clearing, where the treeline began its green wall, Silas stopped. The light was failing. Out west, the storm had organized itself into something with intent, a black rampart building along the whole sky, and the first wind of it came down the gorge and moved the treetops in a long rolling wave, like the forest taking a breath.

He touched the compass through his shirt.

"Hold on, Xan," he said, too low for Nia to hear, or maybe not caring if she did. "I'm coming. And this time I'm not letting the river close over anything."

Behind them, in the dead camp, the barograph's pen had frozen mid-stroke three days ago, a tiny silver cliff at the end of a long flat line—the exact moment the pressure began to fall, the exact moment the world upriver had changed shape. Ahead of them, the river ran north into the gathering dark, and somewhere along it, a woman the river people called the North Star was either waiting for him or running from him, and Silas Pemberton found, to his considerable surprise, that he was afraid of both in equal measure—and that for the first time in seven years, he was flying for someone he loved.

He turned back toward the boat. "Nia. How fast can you read water?"

She smiled, and it was not entirely reassuring. "Faster than you can read weather, I'd bet money on it."

"Then let's go collect on both," Silas said, and pushed the bow into the current, and the river took them north.
