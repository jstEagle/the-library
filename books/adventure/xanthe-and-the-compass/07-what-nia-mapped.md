## 7. What Nia Mapped

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Nia finally spoke.

She'd been watching it for an hour, she realized. Watching the coals shift and settle the way she watched contour lines resolve on a fresh sheet—waiting for the shape underneath to declare itself. Across the camp, Silas sat with his back against a supply crate, the brass compass held flat in his palm like something he was afraid to set down and afraid to pick up. He hadn't asked her anything since they'd found it. That was the worst part. He just kept looking at her with those river-gray eyes, patient as water finding a crack, and she could feel the question building in him the way pressure builds ahead of a storm front.

He knew. She could see that he knew. And he was going to let her say it herself, or he was going to sit there until the monsoon came and took them both.

Nia pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. Her sprained ankle throbbed inside its wrapping, a dull metronome keeping time with her pulse. Three days ago she had been a government cartographer with a commission number and a clean record. Tonight she was sitting in a ghost camp her own hands had helped build, next to a man whose sister had vanished into a valley Nia herself had put on a map.

*My map,* she thought. *My lines. My coordinates.*

"Silas." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "You want to know what we were really doing out here."

"I didn't ask."

"I know you didn't." She stared at the fire. "That's why I'm going to tell you."

* * *

The rain started an hour before dawn—not the monsoon, not yet, just a thin gray drizzle that beaded on the tent fly and made the whole forest whisper. Nia sat inside the survey tent with the camp's field ledger open on her lap, and Silas crouched across from her, dripping, saying nothing.

She'd slept maybe two hours. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the logbook page again—the one with her own handwriting on it, dated three days after her team walked into this clearing and never walked out. The letters were hers. The slant of them, the way she crossed her sevens. Someone had studied her hand long enough to forge it, which meant someone had been close enough to study it, which meant everything she thought she understood about this place was wrong.

Or it meant her team had written it themselves, under duress, in a place where duress wore a polite face.

"You said the commission was geological," Silas said at last. His voice was low, careful, the voice of a man handling live ordnance. "Mineral survey. That's what the posting said when I ferried your team up here in the spring."

"It said that because I wrote it." Nia turned the ledger toward him. "Every public document. Every filing. I authored the cover story myself, because I'm the one who understood the terrain well enough to make it believable."

Silas went very still. "Why would you do that?"

"Because the real order came down sealed." She heard her own voice flatten into the clipped register she used for official correspondence, and hated it, and kept talking anyway. "Classified annex to the Interior Commission's mandate. I wasn't supposed to see it. But I process my own flight plans, and someone in the liaison's office got sloppy with a routing slip, and for about ninety seconds the full scope of the operation passed through my hands."

"And?"

"And it wasn't geology." The word tasted like ash. "It was adjudication. Pre-seizure reconnaissance. There's a valley at the head of this river system—unregistered, unmapped, off every census roll for forty years—and the Commission has known about it for two years. They've been waiting for a legal pretext. Something clean enough to take to the courts. A resource claim, a security concern, a humanitarian intervention—any of it. All they needed was a survey team to go in, confirm habitation, and come back with coordinates precise enough to draft eviction writs."

The drizzle thickened against the canvas. Somewhere out in the dark, a bird called once and fell silent, as if it too were listening.

"They needed a cartographer who could read that country," Nia continued, quieter now. "Someone whose work would hold up under legal scrutiny. So they came to me. And I—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I read the sealed annex. I understood exactly what I was being asked to do. And I signed anyway."

"Why?" It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. Silas asked it the way a man asks about weather—as if the answer might simply be true, and truth required no judgment.

"Because they offered me the valley itself." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Not ownership. Access. Exclusive mapping rights, a permanent field station, ten years of funding. Do you know what that means for someone like me? I grew up in flood country, Silas. I've watched three homes wash away and every map I ever drew of them end up in a government archive that nobody's allowed to open. Maps are how power keeps what it takes. And they were offering me the chance to be the one who *kept* something instead. A place no one could redraw without me."

"So you traded other people's erasure for your own permanence."

The words landed like a stone dropped down a well. Nia waited for the splash. It took a long time coming.

"Yes," she said finally. "That's exactly what I did."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old tin, and with it the guilt she'd been holding at arm's length all night finally stepped close enough to touch her.

She made them both tea from the survey team's stores—her hands steady, her mind anything but—and talked while the water heated, because if she stopped talking she was afraid she'd stop entirely.

"There were eleven of us," she said. "Reyes ran geology. Osei handled comms. The Okonkwo sisters did botany and soils. We'd worked together two seasons. Good people. Careful people." She wrapped her fingers around the tin cup without drinking. "We came upriver in May. First pass, we mapped the gorge. Second pass, the tributaries. By June we were working the headwaters, and that's when the data started lying to me."

"Lying how?"

"Gaps." She set the cup down. "I'd plot a traverse and the closure error would be wrong—not instrument-wrong, *pattern*-wrong. Like the terrain itself had been edited. Clearings that should have shown smoke showing none. Trail networks that terminated at nothing. I told myself it was sensor drift. Then Osei picked up a voice fragment on the long-wave band—not a distress call, not chatter, just... singing. A woman's voice, singing something old, in a language our linguistics database couldn't place. For four seconds. Then gone."

Silas's head came up slowly. "Xanthe used to sing."

"I know." Nia met his eyes. "I didn't know that then. But I know it now, and I need you to understand what that means. The settlement isn't hiding from the Commission, Silas. They're hiding *from maps*. From people like me. They've spent forty years learning how to be invisible to exactly the kind of instruments I brought into their country. And I flew in with a commission seal and eleven sets of eyes and started taking them apart."

The fire crackled. Downstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the river rising—monsoon melt already pushing down from the high country, weeks early.

"The team found the valley on the ninth of June," she went on. "Not by accident—I navigated us straight to it, following the gaps in my own data like a blood trail. Reyes wanted to push straight in. I insisted on a perimeter survey first, standard protocol. That insistence bought the settlement three days' warning. I think—I *hope*—it's the only reason your sister's people had time to decide what to do with us instead of just running."

"And then your team walked into that clearing and vanished."

"And then my team walked into that clearing and vanished." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it. "No tracks leaving. No struggle. Tents pitched, instruments running, food untouched. As if the forest swallowed them politely." She looked down at her own hands. "They left me behind because of the ankle. I begged to go. Osei told me to stay off it, keep the radio watch, and they'd be back by dark. That was the last thing anyone said to me. *Stay off it.*"

Silas was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, rougher, stripped of the pilot's professional calm.

"The compass was under the stove."

"Yes."

"That's Xanthe's. I gave it to her seven years ago, the day she—" He stopped himself. Nia watched him rebuild the wall around whatever lived behind his face, brick by brick, and felt something in her chest twist tight.

"She left it deliberately," Nia said. "Under the stove, wedged where only someone searching carefully would find it. Not lost. *Placed.* If your sister is alive in that valley, Silas, then either she left that compass as a message to whoever came looking—or as a message to you specifically, if she ever learned your name was on the flight manifest. Either way, it wasn't abandonment. It was invitation. Or warning."

"Which one?"

"I don't know yet." She held his gaze. "But I intend to find out. Even if the Commission orders me to burn every page I ever drew."

* * *

Midmorning brought the drizzle to a halt and the clouds lower, pressing down on the gorge like a lid. Nia spread her field charts on the crate beside the dead fire, weighting the corners with stones, and did the thing she had sworn she would never do: she showed Silas the real map.

Not the geological fiction filed with the Commission—the true one, the one she'd kept in a waterproof sleeve against her ribs since the day she understood what her signature had purchased. Hand-inked. Annotated in three colors. The hidden valley rendered not as a blank but as a negative space, its boundaries defined by everything that *wasn't* there.

"This is what I mapped," she said. "This is what they'll use to evict them."

Silas bent over the chart for a long time without touching it. When he finally looked up, his expression had settled into something she recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person who has just discovered the exact dimensions of their complicity.

"Hargrove's sealed orders," he said. "The ones he waved at me on the strip. 'Abandon the search, fly out, weather's closing.' You think those orders are about our safety?"

"No." The answer came out of her flat and certain. "I think they're about containment. We've seen the camp. We've seen the compass. Hargrove doesn't need us to find your sister anymore—he needs us to forget her. The search was always the pretext, Silas. Just like the geology was mine. The Commission doesn't rescue people. It acquires jurisdictions."

Something shifted in Silas's face then—not softening, exactly, but a settling, like sediment finding bottom after a storm. He looked at the map, and at the compass in his palm, and at her, and Nia watched him arrive at the same coordinates she had been living in since June: there was no neutral ground left anywhere in this country. There was only the valley, and everyone who wanted it, and the thin line of people willing to stand between.

"Nia." He said her name like a decision. "Your team. If they're alive—if the settlement took them—then they're evidence. Living witnesses to what the Commission actually sent you here to do. And so are you."

"I know."

"You understand what that makes you, if we go upstream? Not a survivor. A defector. Hargrove won't log your ankle, he'll log your name."

"I've been logging my own name since June." She began rolling the chart, slow and precise, sealing her confession inside it like a specimen. "Every line I drew led my friends into that clearing. Every coordinate I plotted handed the Commission a weapon. I'm not choosing a side now, Silas. I'm just finally admitting which side I've been on all along—the wrong one. The only question left is whether I spend the rest of my life paying for it or the rest of my life correcting it."

The wind shifted, carrying the smell of the high country—cold stone, pine resin, and beneath it, impossibly faint, woodsmoke. Real smoke. Someone's fire, burning somewhere upriver in a valley that officially did not exist.

Silas caught it too. His nostrils flared; his eyes went to the ridgeline north of camp, where the morning haze hung thickest.

"You smell that?" he murmured.

"I smell it."

Neither of them moved. The smoke ghosted above the trees, thin and blue and deliberate—a signal, or a lure, or simply the first honest thing this country had shown them. Nia folded the true map into her jacket, against her ribs, where it had lived since June, and felt the weight of it settle against her heart like a second heartbeat.

Somewhere upstream, in a valley erased from every registry, people who had learned to read the intentions of strangers were reading theirs right now.

And somewhere among them, if the compass meant what it seemed to mean, a woman named Xanthe was waiting to learn whether the brother who'd come looking had arrived as family—or as the advance guard of everything she'd spent seven years escaping.

"We'll need the boat," Silas said quietly, eyes still on the ridge. "And we'll need to move before Hargrove realizes we haven't started back."

Nia stood, testing her weight on the wrapped ankle, and found it would hold—for now.

"Then let's give him something to log," she said.
