## 8. Sealed Orders

The rain came in the night like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just a sudden weight on the tent canvas that turned every sound into something owed. Silas lay awake beneath it for a long while, listening to the gorge talk to itself below the camp: water over stone, water over stone, the same sentence repeated until it stopped meaning anything.

The compass was in his breast pocket. He'd told himself he'd only borrowed it back from under the stove, that he'd return it in the morning, that a man didn't steal from an abandoned camp even when the camp had been arranged like a stage set and abandoned by no one. But his hand kept finding the shape of it through the fabric, round and small as a pocket watch, warm now from his own body heat, and every time he touched it the same thought turned over with the needle: *she was here.* Not here-here, maybe not ever within a hundred miles of this river bend—but her compass had traveled where she traveled, and it had ended up wedged beneath a camp stove in a survey site that had been tidied by human hands after the humans left, and there was no version of arithmetic that made that a coincidence.

Seven years. Seven years of flying medicine and mail and the occasional fugitive with cash, of keeping his nose clean and his tail number out of incident reports, all of it built on one quiet bargain with himself: *don't look for her.* Because looking meant finding, and finding meant learning what the world had done to her, and Silas Pemberton could survive anything except knowing.

He'd survived not-knowing just fine. That was the joke of it.

The rain eased toward gray dawn, settling into the steady monsoon-adjacent drizzle that wasn't weather so much as a promise of worse. He crawled out into it, worked the stiffness out of his shoulders, and found Nia already at the fire, her splinted ankle propped on a log, her survey maps spread under a sheet of clear plastic weighted with stones. She looked up when he came near, and he saw that she hadn't slept either. There was a particular look shared by people who had spent a night alone with a bad fact, and they exchanged it without comment—the nod of two witnesses confirming each other's sanity.

"Logbook," she said, before he'd even sat down. "I've been going through it again. Every entry in my hand. Every date correct." She tapped the plastic sheet. "Except the last one. Three days after we lost contact with them. I was in the coastal hospital with my ankle wrapped and my mother reading me crossword clues over the phone. I know where my hands were." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Someone wrote my handwriting better than I do."

Silas crouched by the fire and fed it a split of damp wood. "People who can copy a hand like that are people who've practiced."

"That's what I keep arriving at." She watched him settle across the flames. "You've got the compass."

Not a question. He should have lied. Some old instinct said *lie, deflect, keep your cargo manifest empty*—but Nia Okafor had spent three days in the wilderness with him now, had read water and read him, and lying to her felt like filing a false flight plan: possible, but it would come back on you.

"My sister's," he said. "Xanthe. I gave it to her seven years ago, the week before she disappeared." He took it out and held it up between two fingers so the firelight caught the engraving—a single X struck deep into the brass case, done with a nail gun punch in a hangar in another life. "She went upriver chasing stories. A settlement off the books. People who'd erased themselves." He heard how it sounded, said aloud for the first time in years, and braced for the cartographer's skepticism, the professional's flat stare.

Nia only looked at the compass for a long moment, then at him. "And you signed on to fly a government search party into exactly the country she went looking for."

"The pay was triple."

"The pay was triple," she agreed, in a tone that filed the excuse away without believing a word of it, and Silas found, to his mild horror, that he didn't mind being seen through. It had been a long time since anyone bothered.

* * *

Hargrove came upriver at midmorning, and he came loudly.

Silas heard the outboard first—a big engine, twin-cylinder, government-issue, the kind of motor that announced its owner's budget long before it rounded the bend. Then the boat itself: a black inflatable with a reinforced hull, riding low with gear, and standing in the bow with one hand on the grab line, Deputy Liaison Hargrove, dry under a poncho that probably cost more than Silas's first engine.

He'd met Hargrove twice before—at the contract signing, and once at the fuel depot where the man had watched Silas load cargo with the attentive stillness of someone memorizing a face for later. Hargrove was forty-something and forgettable on purpose: medium height, medium build, a face like a form filled out in pencil. Only the eyes did any work. They were patient eyes, inventory eyes, and they moved now from Silas to Nia to the camp behind them with the unhurried sweep of a man counting everything and showing interest in nothing.

"Pemberton." He stepped onto the bank without waiting for help, boots sinking half an inch into the mud, and glanced at the sky with theatrical distaste. "Weather's turning."

"It's been turning for a week," Silas said.

"And yet here you still are." Hargrove smiled the way a stamp comes down on paper. "Where's the compass?"

The question landed wrong-footed, casual as a handshake, and Silas felt Nia go very still beside him. "Compass?"

"The survey team carried standard nav kits. We're recovering equipment." Hargrove's gaze rested on Silas's breast pocket for exactly one second too long. "Inventory says item forty-one is unaccounted for."

"You want me to search a drowned campsite for a compass," Silas said, "in the rain, while your missing team is still missing."

"I want you to fly out, actually." Hargrove said it lightly, the way a man mentions lunch. "That's why I'm here. The search phase is concluded. Internal review has determined continued operations present unacceptable risk given the seasonal forecast." He produced a folded paper from inside his poncho, dry despite everything, which was its own kind of magic. "Abandonment order. Signed this morning. You're to transport Ms. Okafor and myself downstream to the strip, secure the aircraft, and stand down. Compensation per the original contract, plus a weather delay bonus. The department appreciates flexibility."

Silas took the paper because refusing to take it would have been a statement, and statements were expensive. He read it. It was beautifully vague—phrases like *operational reassessment* and *recovery of assets* doing the work that facts usually did. No mention of the missing six. No mention of a search continuing anywhere else, by anyone else. Just: stop.

"This doesn't say anything about finding them," Nia said.

"The department's concern for the missing personnel is ongoing and sincere," Hargrove said, with the smoothness of a sentence that had been focus-grouped. "But risk management isn't sentiment, Ms. Okafor. You of all people understand what this valley does to plans." His eyes flicked to her ankle, gentle as a knife laid flat on a table. "You were grounded once already. Be grateful it saved your life."

Something moved through Nia's face—fast, buried, furious—and Silas understood suddenly that Hargrove knew more than an abandonment order required. Knew about the sprained ankle, sure, that was in any file. But the way he'd said *saved your life* had the weight of a man who had read the camp, or the reports of the camp, or whatever the people who tidied the camp had chosen to send downstream. And he'd asked about the compass before announcing the order. Item forty-one. As if the compass were the reason for the trip.

"Weather's wrong for a crossing," Silas said.

Hargrove turned to him with polite interest. "Come again?"

"Storm cells stacking up over the pass since yesterday. You flew in under them; you'll have noticed the light." Silas nodded upstream, where the sky above the gorge had gone the color of wet slate, bruised and low, the monsoon's outriders gathering like cattle before a drive. "I don't fly loaded through building cells in a gorge with a quarter-mile ceiling. Ask any pilot on this river. They'll tell you the same thing, if any of them will talk to you."

"They will," Hargrove said mildly. "They're paid to."

"Then ask them anyway. Two days, maybe three, and there's a window. We fly out in the window."

"The window," Hargrove repeated, "is not the schedule." He let the silence sit for a moment, and in that silence Silas heard the real conversation happening underneath, the one conducted entirely in implications: *You are a hired plane. Planes fly when told. Men who own debts do not own weather, but weather makes a fine excuse, and excuses get audited.*

"Deputy," Silas said, folding the order along its existing creases and handing it back, "I'll fly you out when the sky lets me. Until then, we hold here. That's not refusal. That's aviation."

Hargrove took the paper, and for a moment—just a moment—the pencil-face slipped and Silas saw the thing underneath, which was not anger exactly but a kind of recalculation, fast and cold, the look of a man moving an item from one column of a ledger to another. Then the blandness reassembled itself, seamless as river fog.

"Of course," Hargrove said. "Safety first. Nobody respects that more than I do." He turned away, then paused, as if remembering something trivial. "Tail number, Pemberton?"

"You logged it at the contract signing."

"Humor me. Paperwork." He had a little notebook out now, actual paper, actual pencil—an affectation, surely, in a man whose department ran on sealed digital orders, but Silas gave him the number anyway, letter by letter, watching the pencil move. *November-four-seven-two-niner-Kilo.* Each character went down slow and careful, a man carving a name into a tree so he could find it again later.

There it was, then. Silas had watched it happen to other pilots over the years, always from a comfortable distance: the quiet logging, the second meeting, the third, the requests that stopped being requests. Neutrality, he understood now—not as an abstraction but as a physical sensation—was something you held the way you held altitude. You didn't notice it keeping you alive until the moment it started leaving.

"There's a supply barge at the confluence Thursday," Hargrove said, closing the notebook. "Contractor support, resupply, communications relay. If the weather clears, you'll be able to file a proper flight plan with them rather than relying on—" he gestured vaguely at the trees, the river, the entire lawless green world— "improvisation. I'd recommend taking advantage."

"I'll take it under advisement."

"Everyone does." Hargrove smiled again, stamped it, and walked back down to his boat. The big engine started on the second pull and swallowed the gorge, and then the black inflatable swung out into the current and shrank around the bend, and the river closed over the sound of it like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Nia waited until the echo died. Then: "Item forty-one."

"Yeah."

"He asked about the compass before he asked about the team. Six people missing, and his opening question was about a piece of equipment worth maybe thirty dollars."

"Maybe the compass isn't worth thirty dollars to them." Silas stood at the edge of the bank, watching the empty bend where the boat had gone. The drizzle had thickened again, beading on his jacket, running off the brim of his cap. "Maybe it's worth knowing who else has been through this valley. Maybe an X scratched in brass is a signature they recognize."

Nia came to stand beside him, favoring the good leg, and followed his gaze upstream to where the slate sky sat on the ridgelines like a lid. "You're not going to fly out in any window, are you."

It wasn't an accusation. That was the strange part. She said it the way she'd say *the current reverses at the narrows at dusk*—a fact of the river, observed, recorded.

Silas was quiet for a while. Below them the water went on repeating its one sentence, and somewhere in the forest a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and none of it cared about ledgers or tail numbers or the difference between a search and a recovery of assets.

"When I was nineteen," he said finally, "I sold a man's location for two hundred dollars. He owed money to people I also owed money to, and I told myself it was arithmetic, not betrayal. He got out. Barely. Lost everything getting out." He turned the compass over in his fingers, thumb finding the X. "My sister found out. That's the last real conversation we had—her telling me what I was, and me telling her the world runs on what I am, and her walking upriver into the kind of country that disagrees." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Seven years I've been proving her right. Flying clean. Logging nothing. Being nobody's problem and nobody's ally."

"And now?"

"Now some pencil-faced man wants my tail number in his book, and I find I don't care what he writes next to it." Silas pocketed the compass, and the motion felt less like concealment than like loading a round. "Weather holds us here two days, maybe three. Hargrove's barge comes Thursday with contractors and a communications relay, which means Thursday is the last day this valley belongs to itself." He looked at Nia, and saw that she'd already done the math—he could tell by the set of her mouth, the cartographer's habit of solving terrain before it was asked of her. "So we've got two days to go upstream and find out what happened to your team before the people who edited your logbook send professionals to edit the whole valley."

Nia looked upstream for a long moment. Rain dripped from the plastic over her maps. When she spoke, her voice was steady and entirely awake.

"There's an old mining track on the east bank, first four miles. After that, the river's the only road." She was already reaching for her pack. "We'll need the boat, the tarp, and everything edible in that camp—including their food, because as far as I'm concerned, the department can bill me."

They broke camp in the rain, fast and wordless, two people who had discovered they worked well together and saw no reason to examine why. Silas doused the fire with river water and stood a moment over the blackened stones, aware—with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo—that he was walking away from the last neutral ground he would ever stand on. Somewhere downstream, a pencil was moving in a notebook. Somewhere upstream, a sister he'd mourned might be alive, or might be a story he was telling himself to survive the next three days.

The needle in his pocket pointed north, steady as ever, indifferent to storms and ledgers alike.

He picked up his pack and followed it.
