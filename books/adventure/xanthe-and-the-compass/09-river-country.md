## 9. River Country

**NIA**

The boat was a lie of a vessel—flat-bottomed, patched at the seams with something that might once have been roofing tar, and older than either of them by decades. Silas had found it half-sunk in the reeds below the abandoned survey camp, bailed it out, and pronounced it seaworthy in the tone of a man who wanted very much to be believed.

"It floats," he said.

"Barely," Nia said, and stepped in anyway.

They left the camp behind them standing empty and wrong, tents still pitched, instruments still ticking through their programmed cycles like faithful dogs waiting for masters who weren't coming back. She didn't look at it as they pulled away. Looking at it felt like looking at her own handwriting on that logbook page—the one she'd never written, dated three days after her team vanished. The wilderness had been edited. Someone upstream had taken a pencil to the world, and she needed to know whose hand it was.

The river narrowed as they went up. That was the first thing. On the survey charts she'd drawn herself, this stretch read as a broad brown ribbon, lazy and wide. In truth it braided and split around gravel bars, threaded itself through stands of flooded timber, and ran fast enough in the narrows to sing against the hull. The maps were wrong. Not incomplete—*wrong*, deliberately, the way a face is wrong when someone you trusted is wearing a mask of it.

"Your charts put this channel forty meters east," Silas said, poling them off a sandbar with the unhurried competence of a man who'd spent his life making bad equipment behave.

"My charts were made from aerial photos and secondhand reports." She kept her eyes on the water. "I never came up here. Nobody did. That's why they hired us."

"That's why somebody *didn't* want you hired."

She glanced at him. He was watching the current, not her, but there was something underneath the words—not accusation, exactly. Recognition. They had been circling each other for days now, two professionals keeping their instruments calibrated and their hearts unlabeled, and somewhere between the empty camp and the compass under the stove, the circling had stopped being wariness and started becoming something else. Something she didn't have a name for yet and wasn't sure she wanted one.

"Watch the water," she said, because teaching was easier than feeling. "You've been reading it like a road. It's not a road. It's a language."

Silas raised an eyebrow but said nothing, which from him was a kind of courtesy.

"See where the surface goes smooth and glassy over that dark patch?" She pointed with the paddle. "That's depth. The river's telling you where it's deep enough to run. Now look there—" she indicated a line of small standing waves, sharp-crested, evenly spaced, "—that's a riffle. Gravel bar underneath. Fine in a shallow boat if you angle across it. But *that*—" she nodded toward water that looked identical to everything around it except for a faint oily curling, "—that's a hydraulic. Water going back on itself. It'll hold a boat and drown you in six inches of river."

"And you learned all this from drawing pictures of it?"

"I learned it from drowning twice." She said it flatly, and was rewarded by the flicker of genuine respect crossing his weathered face. "Cartography isn't pictures, Pemberton. It's testimony. Every contour line is a place the land did something to somebody."

He was quiet for a while after that, and she watched him actually look at the river—really look, the way he looked at cloud formations, with his whole attention gathered forward like a dog leaning into wind. There was something almost painful about watching a solitary man discover he'd been surrounded by conversation his whole life and never heard it.

* * *

**SILAS**

By midday the rainforest had closed over them like a green fist, and Silas had begun to understand that the river was talking to him too—he just hadn't known how to listen until Nia translated.

She sat in the bow with her sprained ankle propped on a pack, sketching constantly in a waterproof notebook, correcting her own charts in real time. She hadn't slept properly since the camp. He could see it in the fine tremor of her pen hand, the way she blinked too long. But she kept working, because that was what people like the two of them did: they worked instead of grieving, mapped instead of mourning, and called it professionalism when it was really just fear wearing a lanyard.

"Cut saplings," she said suddenly. "Two o'clock."

He saw them before he finished turning: three pale stumps at the waterline, maybe a week old, cut clean and low. Not beaver work. Knife or machete, angled strokes, deliberate.

"Somebody's been building," Nia murmured.

"Or clearing sightlines." He brought the boat alongside the bank, moving slow, and let Nia step out onto the mud while he held the hull. She crouched over the stumps, touched the wood, smelled it.

"Fresh enough to bleed sap still. And look—" she parted the ferns behind the stumps, "—they dragged the brush *uphill*. Away from the water. You don't do that unless you don't want anyone on the river seeing where the trail goes."

"Someone who knows boats come up this river."

"Someone who expects them to."

They stood there a moment in the dripping green silence, and Silas felt the old pilot's instinct reasserting itself—that cold inventory of threat, fuel, distance, options. Except his fuel was a boat and two tired bodies, and every option ended at a woman named Xanthe who had left a compass under a stove like a note pinned to the world's door.

Back on the water, he decided to return her lesson.

"You want to learn weather?" he said. "Real weather, not forecast weather?"

Nia looked up from her notebook. "Is there a difference?"

"All the difference in the interior. Forecasts are written by people who've never been rained on." He tipped his chin at the sky, which was doing its usual afternoon performance: towering white towers building over the eastern ridgeline, flat-based, bright-lit. "Tell me what you see."

"Cumulus. Building vertically. Convective."

"Good. Textbook. Now tell me what they're *doing*." He let the boat drift in an eddy while he pointed. "See how the tops keep collapsing? They build up, go gray, shear off, and start again. That means the air's fighting itself. Cap and instability. Those clouds are trying to become a storm and failing. But—" he moved his finger west, where smaller, raggeder clouds were drifting in a different direction entirely, "—see those? Low scud, moving out of the northwest while the big ones move east. Wind at two levels, opposite directions. That's shear. That's the atmosphere getting organized."

"Organized into what?"

"Into monsoon." He said it simply, the way you'd name a person walking toward you down a road. "Every year it comes up the valley like a train on rails. When those two winds stop fighting and start agreeing, we've got about thirty-six hours before the passes close. And then nobody flies in or out, nobody walks in or out, and this whole country belongs to the rain until October."

Nia wrote it all down. Of course she did. But then she did something he didn't expect—she put the notebook down and just watched the sky with him, bare-eyed, for a long time. Two people in a leaking boat, learning each other's languages.

"My father taught me stars," she said quietly. "Not navigation. Just names. We'd sit on the roof in Lagos and he'd point and tell stories, and I thought knowing the sky meant you'd never be lost." A pause. "Then I grew up and learned the sky doesn't care where you are. It's the ground that keeps secrets."

"The sky keeps plenty," Silas said. "It just tells you first, if you ask politely."

Something happened to her face then—a loosening, brief and quickly corrected, but real. Almost a laugh. He realized with a small internal shock that he'd been trying to earn that expression for days without admitting it, the way you find yourself whistling near someone's tent before you know why.

* * *

**NIA**

They made camp that night on a gravel shelf beneath an overhang, boat hauled well above the waterline, fire small and screened. Nia's ankle throbbed in time with her pulse, and she was too tired to pretend otherwise, so she sat with it elevated and let Silas make coffee without arguing about it, which for her was practically intimacy.

It was full dark when she saw the smoke ghost.

"Silas." She didn't whisper so much as stop breathing. Across the river, high on the far slope, a thread of smoke rose through the canopy gap—thin, steady, and utterly wrong. No campfire burned that straight in this wind. It rose like a plumb line, like a signal, like a sentence being spoken slowly in a language made of gray.

He followed her gaze and went very still. "Somebody wants us to know they're there."

"Or wants us to think somebody's there."

"Either way, they're fluent." He fed another stick to the fire, and its light carved his face into planes and shadows. "You know what strikes me? Everything we've found today—the cut saplings, the drag marks, that smoke—it's all *legible*. Deliberately legible. Whoever's out there isn't hiding from us. They're communicating. Warning us off in complete sentences."

"Or testing whether we can read."

He looked at her then, and in the firelight she saw the thought land in him the same moment it landed in her: that they were being read right back. That somewhere in the dark green country above them, someone skilled and patient was taking their measure—and finding, perhaps, that two strangers who shared coffee and traded knowledge of water and sky might be worth more than a bullet or an arrow.

"We should sleep in shifts," she said.

"We should," he agreed, and neither of them moved to enforce it for a long moment, sitting shoulder-close against the chill, watching the smoke ghost write its patient gray sentence against the stars.

* * *

**SILAS**

Dawn came up the color of tin, and with it, the snare.

Nia found it—or nearly stepped in it, rather, catching herself at the last instant with a hissed word in two languages. It was set on the game trail twenty meters behind their camp, which meant it had been set *after* they arrived, in darkness, within earshot of their fire.

Silas crouched over it and forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

It was beautiful. That was the obscene thing about it. A spring-pole snare, bent sapling under tension, trigger carved from hardwood with a notch geometry so precise it would hold a squirrel's weight and release to nothing else. No cordage visible where it mattered, no scent handling, no human sign anywhere near it. He'd seen trapper snares all his life, clumsy wire nooses that caught anything dumb enough to blunder past. This was different. This was a *sentence*—elegant, economical, and unmistakably addressed to them.

Because it wasn't set on a game run. It was set on the path between their camp and the boat.

"They could have killed us," Nia said behind him. Her voice was level, but he heard the effort in it. "In our sleep. Twice over."

"No." He stood slowly, hands visible, though there was nothing visible to show hands to. "No, they're telling us something else. Look at the placement. If this was hostile, it'd be hidden. It's set where we'd *find* it—where we'd have to walk to reach the boat. It says: we can touch you whenever we want. It also says: we haven't decided to."

"A warning in the grammar of people who've survived raids," Nia said quietly, echoing something she'd said before, and he wondered what history she carried that gave her the phrase.

He looked upriver, where the gorge narrowed to a slot of white water and the forest pressed in from both walls, ancient and total. Somewhere up there was a brass compass's owner. Somewhere up there was a community that edited maps and left messages in smoke and snares, that had erased itself so thoroughly it existed now only as evidence of its own caution.

And somewhere behind them, downstream, a government liaison was logging tail numbers and waiting for weather windows, and Silas understood with perfect clarity that the window was closing in both directions at once.

"We keep going," he said.

Nia looked at him—at the snare, at the river, at him again—and nodded once, and began striking camp.

It was as they pushed off, the current taking the bow, that Nia stiffened and gripped the gunwale. "Silas. The smoke."

He turned. The far slope was empty. The smoke ghost was gone—not dispersed, not drifted, simply *ended*, snuffed between one breath and the next, like a lamp carried away down a hallway.

And on the near bank, directly across the water from where their fire had burned, hung on a cut sapling at eye height, something pale swung gently in the morning breeze.

A strip of bark. Peeled smooth. Marked with a single character, cut deep and deliberate:

**X**

Silas's hands went still on the pole. The river sang against the hull. Upstream, through the gorge, the day waited.

"She knows we're coming," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "She's known since the camp."

Nia said nothing. She only dipped her paddle, and together they turned the boat's nose upriver, into the narrowing light.
