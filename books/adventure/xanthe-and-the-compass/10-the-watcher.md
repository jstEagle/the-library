## 10. The Watcher

Nia Okafor had spent eleven years learning to read the world the way other people read books, and the forest upstream of the survey camp was telling her a story she didn't like.

It started with the light. They'd been on the water since dawn, poling and paddling the long dugout Silas had traded for at a fishing camp two bends below—its owner had taken the money without questions, which told Nia something about how far they were from anywhere that mattered—and the canopy over the river had been doing what canopies did: filtering the sun into coins of green-gold, letting them scatter across the current like spilled treasure. Ordinary. Beautiful, even, if she'd been in any state to notice beauty.

But an hour past the second gorge, the light changed. Not dimmed—*organized*. The shadows along the eastern bank fell in a pattern too regular for chance, as if someone had walked the treeline that morning and disturbed nothing but the angle of every frond that hung over the water. She couldn't have proven it. If she'd said it aloud, Silas would have looked at her the way he looked at his fuel gauge when it read full after a hard flight: hopeful, skeptical, unwilling to commit.

So she said nothing. She watched instead, the way she'd watched terrain through a stereoscope for a decade, letting her eyes go soft until the forest stopped being ten thousand individual trees and became texture, gradient, *pattern*. And the pattern said: someone had passed this way recently, moving with care, keeping to the shadow side of every trunk.

"You're doing the thing," Silas said from the stern.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go quiet and stare at the bank like it owes you money."

"I'm reading." She dipped her paddle, let the current take the stroke. "The river's a text. You should try it sometime."

"I read weather. Weather doesn't lie."

"Neither does water. That's the point." She nodded downstream, subtle, not turning her head. "See the foam line off the point there? It holds a curve instead of breaking straight. There's a submerged log, maybe three days old—the bark's still dark where it scoured. And the eddy behind it is holding debris in a spiral. Anything that falls in that water stays in that water."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning if I dropped a coin off the gunwale right now, I could come back next month and find it in exactly that spot." She let him chew on that. "Rivers remember, Silas. Everything that happens on them gets written down somewhere downstream. You just have to know which page to look at."

He grunted—the sound he made when she was right and he resented it—and for a while the only conversation was the knock of paddles against the gunwales and the river's low continuous muttering over its stones.

The watcher showed itself an hour before noon.

Nia saw it the way you see a face in wallpaper: suddenly, completely, and with the immediate certainty that it had always been there. A shape among the buttress roots of a strangler fig, upslope from the water perhaps forty meters—a stillness so complete it registered first as geology, then, horribly, as intention. A figure in woven fiber the color of leaf-shadow, crouched at the edge of the treeline, watching them pass.

Not aiming anything. Not signaling. Just *watching*, with the patient, evaluative attention of someone counting.

Her heart went off like a dropped pan. She kept her stroke even—some animal discipline deeper than thought held her arms in rhythm—and counted her own breaths until they steadied. Four seconds of contact. Five. The figure didn't move, didn't duck away, didn't do any of the things a hidden person does when discovered. It simply remained, and in remaining made itself unmistakable: *I see you. I am letting you see me.*

Then a bend took the point of land, and the fig tree, and the figure, and the river carried them on into green silence as though nothing had happened.

"Silas." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Don't turn around. Back left, forty meters up—we just passed—"

"I saw it." He hadn't turned. His shoulders had gone rigid under the faded shirt, and his voice had the flat calm she was learning meant fear managed by force. "Two minutes ago. Thought my eyes were playing tricks."

"They weren't."

"No," he agreed grimly. "They weren't." He shipped his paddle across his knees and let the boat drift, and for a moment they both stared upriver at the unfolding gorge as if it might explain itself. "One person?"

"One. Crouching. Fiber clothing, no metal I could see. And here's the part I keep turning over—" she made herself say it plainly, because saying it plainly was the only way she knew to keep fear from metastasizing, "—they *let* us see them. That wasn't a mistake. Nobody who moves like that makes mistakes."

Silas was quiet a long moment. Water chuckled against the hull. Somewhere upslope a bird gave three descending notes and quit.

"When I was flying mail runs out of Kessari," he said finally, "there was a dog on the airstrip road. Half-feral, mean as sin, bit two ground crew. Everyone wanted it shot." He picked up the paddle again. "Old mechanic named Ferro fed it instead. Every day, same time, same spot, never reached for it, never ran from it. Took four months. Then one morning the dog was sleeping under his workbench." He drove the blade into the current. "Point being, that thing back there wasn't hunting us. Hunting looks different. Hunting is quiet and then it isn't. That was a *look*. Like checking your papers at a border."

"A test," Nia said.

"Or an invitation with the details left blank."

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar inside a horseshoe bend, because Silas said open ground beat concealment when you didn't know what you were concealing from—"nothing sneaks across a beach without printing itself"—and Nia, who had once spent nine days mapping a karst field where concealment was the only defense, understood the logic and hated it anyway. Exposure went both ways.

She built the fire small and screened, more habit than hope; whoever was out there already knew exactly where they were. Silas strung their food high and set the rifle he'd brought from the plane within reach of his bedroll, and then, in the way of people who need their hands busy when their minds are loud, he cleaned it though it was clean.

Nia spread her field notebook on her knees by firelight and worked. This was her liturgy: the day's track logged, bearings corrected, the river's course penciled in against her old survey sheets. And it was in the doing of it that the wrongness surfaced, the way wrongness always did for her—not as a shout but as a discrepancy, a number that wouldn't reconcile.

"Silas."

"Mm."

"Where did we make our noon stop? The clay bank with the tapir tracks."

"Left bank, after the second gorge. Why?"

She turned the notebook toward him. On today's page, in her own cramped hand, was a sketch of that clay bank—accurate, detailed, dated. She had no memory of drawing it.

His face did something complicated. "You've been half-asleep paddling all day. Could be you sketched it and forgot."

"I don't forget marks I make. It's how I don't get lost." She traced the pencil lines. The hand was hers—the particular way her sevens crossed, the little hook she put on terminal strokes. But the *pressure* was wrong, lighter, faster, a hand imitating rather than thinking. "Someone copied my handwriting. From the logbook at the camp, probably. And left this in my bag."

"In your bag." He set the rifle down with exaggerated care. "In your bag, which has been sitting beside you all day."

"Yes."

The fire popped. Downstream, the river went on saying whatever rivers say when no one listens.

"That's not a threat," Nia said slowly, working it through, hating every step and needing every step. "Think about it. If they wanted to hurt us, we'd never have seen the watcher at all—that was deliberate. And this—" she tapped the page, "—this took skill and *time*. Someone got close enough to my pack to plant a forgery, close enough to touch our things while we slept or while we paddled, and took nothing. Nothing, Silas. Not the rifle, not the cash in your jacket, not my instruments. Instruments are worth six months' wages downriver."

"So what do you call it, taking nothing and leaving something?"

"A letter." She heard it as she said it, and the hearing chilled her more than the alternative. "A letter written in a language only certain people speak. People who've been raided before learn rules. You prove you *could* have taken everything. You prove you know things about your visitor—their habits, their tools, their handwriting. And you leave a mark that says: we were here, we chose restraint, now choose yours." She closed the notebook. "It's a warning in the grammar of people who survive by not being found. We're being read, Silas. Cover to cover. And whoever's reading hasn't decided yet whether we're worth keeping."

Silas looked into the fire for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere she hadn't heard it go before—past the pilot's flat calm, into something rawer.

"Seven years ago my sister wrote me a letter like that. Not literally. But the same grammar. A message that said *I'm alive, and I can't tell you where, and don't look for me, and please look for me.*" He turned the compass case over in his hands—he'd taken to carrying it since the camp, thumb worrying the worn brass through the leather. "I ignored the rules of it. I answered a letter that was written to be unanswerable, and I lost her twice. Once when she went, and once when I did it wrong." His jaw worked. "Whoever's out there is writing to somebody. And I'd give a year of flying to know whether it's us they're writing to—or her."

Nia reached across the fire and put her hand over his, over the compass. She wasn't a touching person; neither was he; the gesture surprised them both and neither withdrew.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we read carefully."

* * *

She woke in the grey hour before dawn to a silence with edges.

Not the silence of empty forest—which is never silent, which breathes and ticks and murmurs—but a held silence, the acoustic signature of listening things. Nia lay still in her bedroll and catalogued it the way she'd catalogue an anomaly on a contour map: birdless understory, no insect drone from the reed bed, the river running normal, one unnatural absence of movement in the treeline directly opposite the bar.

Then, deliberately, a stick cracked. Twice. Upslope and to the north, well clear of their position, unmistakably intentional.

By the time she was upright, knife in hand, heart hammering its old soldier's rhythm, the sound had become footfalls becoming nothing—someone walking away without hurry, announcing their departure the way they'd announced their presence. *We are leaving. We were never a danger. Notice that we tell you these things.*

Silas sat up, rifle across his knees, eyes wide and fully awake. "How long have you been—"

"Listen."

They listened. The forest exhaled back into its ordinary noises, insect by insect, bird by bird, the reed bed resuming its whisper, until the whole morning sounded like nothing had happened, which was itself a kind of message.

And then Nia saw the boat.

Their dugout sat where they'd beached it, above the high-water line, bow pointed downstream. Exactly as they'd left it—except that they had left it pointed upstream. In the night, without waking either of them, without leaving so much as a print she could find in the pre-dawn grey, someone had turned a heavy dugout end for end on loose gravel and gone.

She walked to it slowly. Silas came beside her. Together they stood looking at the vessel as if it might speak.

"It's not random," she said at last. Her breath smoked in the cold air. "Pointed downstream means *go home*. It's an answer to a question we haven't asked out loud. Turn back, and you'll be allowed to turn back." She crouched, touched the gravel where the hull had pivoted—two faint crescents of disturbance, nothing more. Whoever had done it weighed less than Silas, moved like smoke, and wanted very badly to be understood rather than feared.

"But," Silas said.

"But." She stood. "Yesterday they let us see them. Last night they handled our belongings and returned them. This morning they rearrange our boat and announce their exit. Three contacts, three messages, escalating in intimacy. That's not surveillance anymore." She looked upriver, where the gorge narrowed around the bend into shadow, and felt the strange double pull she'd been feeling since the camp: dread and longing, braided so tight she couldn't tell which was which. "That's a negotiation. And negotiations end one of two ways."

"Acceptance," Silas said quietly.

"Or refusal." She picked up her paddle. "And we're about to find out which, because I don't think whoever lives up that valley intends to let us drift much further without answering for ourselves."

Silas laid his hand flat on the hull, the way a man touches a horse he trusts. Above the treeline, far off, a single thread of smoke rose straight in the windless dawn—thin, pale, deliberate. Too straight to be accidental. Too visible to be anything but a signal.

A marker. A door, standing open.

"Well," Silas said, swinging his pack up. "Somebody just told us where the house is."

Nia looked at the smoke, then at the river running north toward its forbidden source, and pushed the dugout into the current.
