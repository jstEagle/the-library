## 11. Xanthe

The river changed its mind three times in the last mile.

Silas had learned to read water the way Nia taught him—by its temper, not its course—and the source basin announced itself as a mood before it was a place: a hush in the current, a cold coming up off the stones, the kind of silence that wasn't empty but *held*, like a breath behind a door. The gorge walls leaned closer. The canopy knitted overhead until the sky was a green rumor. Somewhere above them, he knew from the contour lines Nia had scratched into the mud of their campfire the night before, the valley opened out—a bowl of land no government map admitted existed.

They left the boat at a gravel shelf and climbed.

The ridge trail was old. That was the thing that kept snagging in him as they went—not wildness but *maintenance*. Footfalls worn into a path too narrow for cattle, too deliberate for game. Saplings cut and stacked with the precision of people who expected to come back for the wood. A stone cairn at a switchback, rebuilt recently enough that the lichen hadn't yet reclaimed the fresh faces of the rocks. Somebody lived here. Somebody had lived here a long time, and had gotten very good at being invisible to everyone except themselves.

Nia climbed ahead of him, favoring her healed ankle on the steep pitches without admitting it, her machete hand resting easy on the handle. Seven days upriver had burned the last of the wariness between them down to something warmer and more useful: trust with calluses. She paused where the trail crested a spine of granite and looked back at him, and the wind coming up the basin lifted the hair off her neck, and she said, quietly, "You feel that?"

"Feel what?"

"Being counted."

He did feel it. He'd felt it since the second switchback—the particular weight of eyes that weren't curious but *assessing*, the way a customs officer looks at your hands. He turned a slow circle. The forest gave him nothing but layered green and the smell of wet granite and some flowering vine whose sweetness sat too heavy in the air, almost medicinal.

"Whoever they are," Nia said, "they've known about us since the snare line."

"Since before that." Silas touched the strap of his pack, where the brass compass rode against his shoulder blade like a second heartbeat. He still hadn't told her everything about it. He'd told her it mattered. He'd told her it was the reason he couldn't turn the boat around when Hargrove's deputy had radioed orders downstream. Some things you say whole or not at all, and this one wasn't ready to be said.

They topped the ridge near midday.

The basin opened below them like a held secret finally exhaled: terraced gardens stitched along the contours, smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys hidden among the trees, a mill wheel turning somewhere out of sight with a sound like slow applause. Houses—real houses, timber and thatch and salvaged tin, built by hands that intended to stay. Fields of something leafy and unfamiliar. Children's voices, faint, carrying up on the thermals. It was beautiful in the way that only forbidden things are beautiful, and Silas stood there with his mouth open like a man seeing the sea for the first time, thinking: *seven years. She was seven years and one valley away.*

He never got to take a step toward it.

The first arrow buried itself in the granite between his boots with a sound like a struck match. The second took his pack strap and pinned it to a sapling, close enough that he felt the shaft's vibration through his collarbone. By the time he'd stopped moving—which was immediately—the forest around the ridge had produced four figures as if the trees had simply decided to stand up.

They were good. That was his first professional assessment, made even as his pulse slammed in his ears. They came out of cover already fanned, already covering each other's angles, bows at full draw without a single wasted motion. Not soldiers exactly. Something older and leaner than soldiers—the posture of people who had learned ambush as a survival skill rather than a trade. Three men and a woman, weathered clothes, faces that gave away nothing because giving things away had probably gotten someone they loved killed.

"Hands," said the nearest man. His accent was lowland, educated, worn smooth by years in the bush. "Slow. Kneel. You came up the river, so you're either lost or you're followed, and either way you'll wait here while we decide which."

"We're not—" Nia started.

"Kneel."

Silas knelt. The stone bit through his trousers. Out of the corner of his eye he watched Nia lower herself beside him, her jaw set in the expression he'd come to know as the one she wore instead of screaming. The archers closed in. One of them began going through Nia's pack with quick, apologetic efficiency, and Silas understood that too—the apology in the hands, the theft done gently, by people who hated doing it and did it anyway because the alternative was graves.

Then the fourth figure stepped out of the treeline, and the world tilted.

She was thinner than he remembered. Harder, the way river stone is harder than quarry stone—same material, different history. Her hair was cut short and practical, streaked early with grey at the temples, tied back with a strip of red cloth. There was a scar through her left eyebrow he didn't recognize, white and old. She carried her bow at rest but her eyes at work, sweeping the ridge, cataloguing threats—and then those eyes came to his face, and stopped, and everything in them went out like a lamp.

The bow arm dropped to her side. For a long moment she just looked at him, and the forest sounds filled the silence—the mill wheel, the birds, the wind in the terrace grasses below—and Silas forgot how to breathe.

"*Xanthe.*"

Her name came out of him broken, half-swallowed, seven years late. He started to rise and an arrowhead swung back to his throat and he didn't care, he was rising anyway, and then her hand came up—not to the archer, but flat, *hold*—and the arrowhead lowered, and she crossed the distance between them in five strides and hit him like a falling tree.

It wasn't an embrace, not at first. It was impact. Her fists closed in his jacket and she shook him once, hard, the way you shake a machine that has failed, and her face was pressed against his chest and he could feel her breathing wrong, ragged, the breathing of someone who has practiced not crying for so long that the body has forgotten the mechanics of it.

"You *idiot,*" she said into his jacket. "You absolute—you flew a government contract up my river. You flew a *survey team* up my river. Do you have any idea—"

"I didn't know." His voice cracked on it. His hands hovered over her back, afraid she'd dissolve, afraid she'd shove him away, certain of nothing except that she was warm and alive and smelled like woodsmoke and some plant he didn't know. "Xanthe, I swear on Mom's grave, I didn't know what they were mapping. I found your compass in their camp and I—"

She pulled back. And there it was—the other thing waiting under the joy, the thing he'd been walking toward for seven years without knowing it. Her eyes were dry now and furious and shining all at once, and she looked at him the way you look at a wound you thought had healed.

"The compass." She said it flatly. "You found the compass."

"In the stove at the survey camp. Wedged under it like—like it was left for someone to find."

"It was left for *you.*" She stepped back out of his arms, and the space she opened between them was a country he didn't know how to cross. "I left it there because I wanted to see if you'd follow it. Because I needed to know, Silas. I needed to know whether you'd come for me, or whether you'd come for the money."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, and her voice shook. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. But you want to talk about fair? I wrote you letters for two years. From Port Meridian, from the coast camps, from every stop on the way north. Did you get them?"

The question landed in his stomach like ballast dropped from altitude. "No."

"Because they were intercepted." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard, joy and grief ground together into something sharp. "Every one. Because the constabulary had a file on me, Silas, and a standing order, and do you know what was in that file? *Family correspondence.* They were reading my mail to find the settlement. And when that didn't work fast enough, they went to the source. They went to my brother, the licensed pilot, the respectable taxpayer, and they asked him questions, and—"

"I never told them anything."

"—and he *answered.*"

The word rang off the granite. Down in the basin, the mill wheel kept its slow applause. One of the archers shifted his weight; Nia had gone very still beside Silas, watching the two of them with the careful attention she gave to fault lines in terrain.

"They came to the strip," Silas said. His voice was low and level because if it wasn't level it would be nothing at all. "Two men, autumn after you left. They asked if I'd heard from you. I said no. They asked if I knew where you'd gone. I said north, because everyone said north, because *you* said north, loud enough for half the tavern to hear. That's all I gave them. North. Which you'd already told the whole room yourself."

"And they believed you?"

"They logged it. They watched me for a year. My fuel purchases, my flight plans, my mail. I lost contracts because of it. I nearly lost my license." He took a step toward her and she didn't retreat, which he chose to read as permission. "I spent a year under surveillance because I wouldn't help them find you, and I never once knew if you were alive, and I never once stopped looking at every passenger manifest and every cargo list and every rumor that came upriver on a bad radio, hoping one of them would have your name in it. So don't stand there and tell me I sold you out. Don't you *dare.*"

The wind moved through the terrace grasses below. Xanthe's jaw worked. The scar through her eyebrow caught the light.

"They told me you cooperated," she said, quieter now, and the fury in it had gone brittle, which meant it was close to breaking in the other direction. "A defector reached us in the third year. Said the pilot brother had given a debrief. Said there were charts with our likely routes marked. We moved the whole settlement because of it, Silas. Upstream. Higher. Two people died on the pass crossing. Old Marta, and Denny Cole's boy. We buried them in frozen ground because of a *rumor* about you."

"And the rumor was wrong."

"And the rumor was *wrong.*" She said it like she was testing whether the words would hold her weight. Her bow hung forgotten in her left hand. Her right hand had come up to her mouth without her seeming to notice, pressing hard against her lips, the way she used to when they were children and she'd broken something and was deciding whether to confess.

"Yeah," Silas said. "It was wrong."

Neither of them apologized. He noticed that, later, and understood it was its own kind of language: neither of them said *I'm sorry* because sorry implied the debt ran one way, and the truth was they'd both spent seven years paying interest on a loan neither had taken out. Instead she looked at him for a long time, and he looked back, and the ridge held its breath around them.

Then her face crumpled and reformed, all in the space of a heartbeat, and she crossed the distance again and this time it was an embrace, properly, her forehead against his shoulder, her fists unclenching in his jacket, and she was shaking, and so was he, and the archers looked everywhere else with the disciplined embarrassment of professionals.

"You got old," she said into his shoulder.

"You got *grey.*"

"That's wisdom."

"That's stress."

A wet laugh escaped her. "Both."

Nia cleared her throat—gently, the way you clear your throat near wildlife—and Xanthe pulled back and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, and turned to Nia with an expression that had rearranged itself into appraisal.

"You're the cartographer. Okafor. The one who sprained her ankle and stayed behind."

Nia blinked. "You know about that?"

"We've read every page your team ever wrote. Including the ones they don't know they wrote." Something flickered across Xanthe's face—there and gone, a shadow under the reunion's light. "Your surveyors are alive, Okafor. All six of them. Fed, sheltered, and extremely unhappy about it. They found what they found in their own data, and we couldn't let them walk out with it. You especially will want to hear how they took that."

"How they took it?" Nia's voice had gone careful.

Xanthe shouldered her bow. Below them, the settlement smoked peacefully in its bowl of concealment, and somewhere a child laughed, and the mill wheel turned.

"There's a disagreement," she said. "About what happens next. About whether people like us survive by staying invisible—or whether it's time somebody finally drew us onto the map." She looked at her brother, and the ghost of the old Xanthe surfaced in her crooked half-smile, the one that had always preceded trouble. "And since you brought a government plane and a government cartographer to my doorstep, big brother, I'd say you've just volunteered to help us settle it."

* * *

They descended into the valley as the light went long and gold.

Xanthe walked ahead, and Silas watched her the way he'd once watched instruments in cloud—constantly, hungrily, confirming she was real. She moved differently than she had at nineteen. She greeted people by touch: a hand on a shoulder, a tap of knuckles against a doorframe, small contacts that said *I see you, I count you* to everyone she passed. The settlement came out to look at the strangers, and Silas saw faces from nowhere and everywhere—faces that had the specific blankness of people whose names exist only where they're planted now. A woman stirring a cauldron who watched them with a soldier's eyes. An old man mending a net who didn't look up at all, which was its own statement.

"Debt-runners," Xanthe said quietly, as they walked. "Wrongly convicted. People the courts broke and then billed for the breaking. A family fled here in '41 because the father testified against a magistrate and suddenly his land deeds developed errors. We don't ask what people did before the valley. We ask what they'll do tomorrow."

"And the survey team?"

"Guests." The word was chosen with visible care. "Involuntary ones. Reyes is the problem. He's a true believer—thinks the survey was honest science and hiding data is a crime worse than what we did to keep it quiet." She stopped at the edge of the common ground, where firelight was beginning to gather, and turned to face them both. "Tomorrow you'll meet them. Tonight you eat, and you sleep, and you—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the ordinary word— "you stay. Both of you. Nobody's shot at you since the ridge, and that was policy, not malice."

But Silas was barely listening, because down the slope, near the longhouse, a figure had risen from a bench and was staring at them—a broad-shouldered man with a surveyor's squint and a week of beard, and beside him, rising more slowly, five more faces he recognized from the missing-persons brief Nia had shown him a hundred river miles ago.

Six surveyors. Alive. Watching the newcomers arrive with expressions ranging from relief to open hostility—and the hostile one, the big man with the squint, was already walking toward them with his jaw set like poured concrete.

"Okafor," the man called, and his voice carried across the common. "Good. Maybe *you* can explain to these people what happens to anyone who obstructs a Crown charter survey."

Xanthe's hand found her brother's sleeve. "That," she murmured, "would be Reyes."
