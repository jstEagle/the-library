## 12. The Valley of the Unlisted

The path down was not a path. That was the first thing I made them understand.

"Walk where I walk," I said, standing at the lip of the ridge with the morning fog still pooled in the basin below like milk someone had forgotten to stir. "Not beside me. Not ahead. Where I walk. The ground lies here."

Silas looked at me the way he used to look at weather coming in off the coast—that flat, measuring stare that pretended it wasn't afraid. Seven years had carved new lines into his face, deepened the creases at his eyes, silvered him at the temples, but the stare was unchanged. It was the stare of a boy who'd taught himself to read cloud banks from the roof of our father's hangar because reading people hurt too much.

Nia Okafor stood behind him, her splinted ankle wrapped in fresh bark-cloth one of my runners had bound on without being asked. She held a notebook against her chest like a shield, and I knew what she was doing behind those careful eyes: mapping. She couldn't help it. Every switchback we descended, every false trail and deadfall we crossed, she was drawing us into the paper prison of her mind, and I hated her a little for it, and understood her completely, because I had been a mapper once too—of debts, of routes, of the constellations of people who owed and were owed.

"The ground lies," I repeated, and stepped off the ledge onto what looked like empty air.

The hidden stair was there, as it had been for eleven years—cut into the limestone by hands before ours, widened by ours, concealed beneath a lattice of living root that my people wove back over it each season like closing a wound. Below my boot, forty feet of nothing. Behind me, Silas's breath caught. Good. Let him be afraid. Fear keeps you honest on the stair.

* * *

We went down through the mountain's skin in silence. The fog took us first, then the rock, then the sound of the river above, until all that remained was the drip of water and the small percussion of our boots on stone and the rasp of Nia's breathing as she favored her ankle on the narrow shelves. Twice I stopped and pressed my palm against the wall, feeling for the cold thread of air that meant an open vent, and twice I found it, and let the darkness breathe around us.

Silas spoke only once, when the passage narrowed and we had to go single file, his hand nearly brushing my shoulder in the black.

"You've done this before," he said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I've lived this before," I answered, and did not turn around, because if I turned around I would see his face in whatever gray light filtered down from the vents, and I was not ready for his face yet. I had not been ready for seven years.

Because here is what I could not say on the stair, what I would have to say in the valley with the whole listening world of my people around me, and even then might not survive saying: I had not left him. I had been taken from him by inches, by paperwork, by the slow grinding arithmetic of a country that decided my life was a rounding error.

It began, as these things always begin, with a debt that wasn't mine.

Our father died owing four hundred and eleven days of labor to the Corvane Timber Combine—a debt contracted in his name by a company clerk who forged his mark on a renewal clause while he lay dying of the lung-rot their own mills gave him. I was nineteen. Silas was flying medicine runs already, gone weeks at a stretch, sending money home that vanished into the Combine's ledger like water into sand. When I went to dispute the debt, they showed me the document. When I showed them Father's actual signature—the shaky, looping scrawl from his last letter—they showed me the magistrate. When the magistrate fined me for filing a fraudulent dispute, they showed me the door, and the door opened onto a warrant, because by then I had missed two scheduled labor days disputing a debt that did not exist, and missed days accrue penalties, and penalties accrue interest, and interest, in the eyes of the law, is a kind of truth no signature can argue with.

I ran. Not bravely, not cleanly. I ran the way animals run, leaving everything, and the law followed me the way laws follow—not fast, but forever. By the time I reached the interior I was not a person anymore. I was a file. A number. A case marked *at large*. And somewhere in the drowned country between the last bridge and the first unnamed tributary, a woman named Odile Ferran pulled me out of a pirogue, fed me fish soup, and told me the words that rebuilt my life:

*"You can't outrun the registry. But you can stop being in it."*

* * *

The passage exhaled us into green.

That is the only way I can describe it, even now, even to myself: the mountain simply opened its hand, and there was the valley, lying in its bowl of basalt walls like something kept safe rather than something kept secret. Terraces stepped down the southern slope, planted with taro and beans and the broad leaves of plants whose names I still didn't know after eleven years, because the people who grew them had brought the seeds from places I'd never seen. Smoke rose thin and straight from a dozen chimneys, unhurried, unhidden—there was no need to hide smoke inside the walls; the walls hid it from the sky. Water ran everywhere, channeled in bamboo flumes that clicked and knocked against each other in the wind like a language of small bones.

Below, along the river's source-lake, the settlement itself: forty roofs, maybe fifty, thatch and salvaged corrugate and planks milled from windfall, arranged with no plan and therefore perfect, the way a forest is perfect. A longhouse with its doors open. A drying rack hung with fish. Children—this undid me, it undid me every time—children running between the houses barefoot, shrieking over some game whose rules existed nowhere on paper.

Behind me, I heard Nia Okafor stop breathing.

I turned. She stood at the mouth of the passage with her notebook half-raised, and her face—her face was the face of a woman watching a cathedral burn in reverse. Everything she had ever believed about the blank spaces on her maps was standing in front of her, growing beans.

"It's not on any survey," she whispered. "I flew the basin perimeter myself. There's nothing here. There's *nothing*—"

"There's nothing here," I agreed, "because there has never been anything permitted to be here. Those are different things. You'll want to learn the difference."

Silas said nothing. He stood with the light of the valley on his face and looked at the smoke, the flumes, the children, and I watched his jaw work around something too large to swallow. He was counting roofs. Of course he was. He counted everything—fuel drums, storm cells, years. I wondered what number he arrived at, and whether it frightened him.

"Come," I said. "They'll want to see you. And you'll want to eat. And then—" I looked at my brother, really looked, for the first time since the arrowpoint on the ridge, since his face had appeared above me like a resurrection I hadn't prayed for. "Then we talk about why you came."

* * *

They received us in the longhouse, which is to say: everyone came, because everyone always comes, because privacy is the first thing you surrender when you erase yourself and the last thing you stop missing.

Odile sat at the head, older now, her hands knotted around a cup of bark tea she wouldn't drink until the talking was done. Around the benches: Reyes and the other surveyors, alive, hollow-eyed, sitting apart under the watchful courtesy of two men with bows leaned within reach—which I had ordered, and which shamed me, and which I would order again. The ferryman Tomas. The Okonkwo family, three generations of them, wrongly convicted en masse of arson they hadn't set, their eldest daughter translating the proceedings into sign for the grandmother who'd stopped speaking the year the constables burned their shop. Debt-runners. Deserters. A midwife whose license had been revoked for serving the unlicensed. People who were, on paper, no one at all.

I introduced Silas and Nia plainly, because plainness was our currency here—we had all spent enough of our lives on euphemism.

"My brother," I said. "And the cartographer who mapped us without knowing it."

That landed hard. I felt it land. Old Amara by the fire drew breath through her teeth. A child near the door stopped fidgeting. Because they all understood what a map is: a map is a claim. A map is the first draft of every eviction notice ever nailed to a door. And here was a woman who had drawn the shape of our erasure without meaning to, holding the only complete picture of our hiding place inside her skull.

"She'll give it up," Silas said quietly. "The map. If you're worried."

"You don't speak for me," Nia said—but not sharp, not angry. Tired. Then she did something I did not expect. She opened her notebook, tore out nothing, closed it again, and laid both hands flat on the cover. "Everything I surveyed in this basin goes into sealed government files. That's what my commission was. Reconnaissance." She swallowed. "For a resettlement action. Eviction. Resource seizure. Your valley is flagged, and I helped flag it, and my team paid for my mistake, and I will burn every page I ever drew before I let that happen. That's what I'm doing here. Not searching anymore. Answering."

The longhouse was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the flumes knocked their bone-language against the wind.

Odile drank her tea at last. "Xanthe," she said, "your brother's plane. Is it known?"

"Logged," I said. "By a man named Hargrove. Tail number and all."

"Then we have less time than I hoped and more than I feared." She set down the cup. "Feed them. House them. And Xanthe—" her eyes, pale as river ice, held mine, "—you owe this house your story. They've heard pieces. Tell it whole. A house that hides together should know together why it hides."

So I told it. Standing, because sitting felt like surrender.

I told them about the forged debt and the fine and the warrant. About the two years of running, sleeping in culverts, learning which constables took bribes and which took trophies. About the night in the flooded timber camp when I finally understood that the law was never going to stop looking, because the law does not need a reason to keep a file open—it needs only the file. About finding the rumor of a place with no name, following it upriver through country so wet my boots rotted off my feet, and about Odile's pirogue rising out of the mist like an answer to a question I'd been too proud to ask.

"The law made me a criminal for surviving," I said. "That's the whole story. That's why I left. Not adventure. Not wandering feet. My brother thinks—I think he thinks—I abandoned him. Maybe I did. But the alternative was standing in a courtroom being converted, line by line, from a person into a liability. And I refused. I refuse still." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "Every person in this room is a refusal. That's what this valley is. Not paradise. A refusal with gardens."

An old man near the fire—Petrov, who'd fled a conviction for a theft his foreman committed—spoke without raising his hand, the way old men do. "Refusals rot if they hide too long, girl. Ask the mushrooms."

"And exposure kills faster," I said. "Ask anyone who's ever been found."

"That," said Petrov, nodding toward the surveyors' bench, toward Reyes, "is the argument we'll be having soon enough. Best sleep first. Arguments go better fed."

* * *

I walked Silas to the guest hut myself, because I wanted the walk, and because I am a coward in the specific ways my childhood trained me to be.

Dusk had come down soft. Lamps burned in windows—real oil lamps, wasteful, extravagant, lit without fear of aerial observation because the basalt walls ate the glow. A woman sang somewhere, tuneless and contented. The smell was woodsmoke and wet earth and frying fish, and underneath it all the clean mineral breath of the river, and I watched my brother try to take it in and fail, the way you fail to hold the whole ocean in one look.

"You built all this?" he asked.

"No. I inherited it. Odile built it. I just—" I searched for the honest word. "I keep it. Like keeping a fire. You don't build fire. You feed it, and you stand between it and the wind."

"Seven years, Xanthe." He stopped walking. In the lamplight his face was all the ages of him at once—the boy at the hangar, the young pilot waving me off at the strip, this stranger with my father's stubborn jaw. "One letter. One message through any of fifty channels. I would have come. You know I would have come."

"To do what? Fight the Combine? Fight the magistrates? Get yourself filed next to me?" I heard my voice rise and hated it. "You think I didn't write letters? I wrote nine. Nine letters, Silas. I burned eight because a letter is a trail, and the ninth—" my throat closed. "The ninth I sent. To the old drop box at Kesswick field. It came back unopened, stamped *addressee relocated, forwarding address unknown*, because while I was running from the law, the law was evicting *you*, and nobody thought to tell either of us where the other had gone."

He stared at me. I watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes—he was counting again, counting the years, counting the letters, arriving at some total that broke something in his face.

"They redeveloped Kesswick," he said finally, very quietly. "The year after you left. Whole strip torn up. Condos." A sound escaped him that was almost a laugh. "Your letter's probably in a landfill outside the city. With mine. I wrote six."

Six. Nine. Fifteen letters between us, all of them dead in the ground, while we each assumed the silence was a choice. I put my hand against the rough timber of the hut's doorframe to steady myself, and for a moment neither of us moved, and the river breathed below us in the dark, and a child laughed somewhere, unregistered, uncounted, free in the only way freedom is ever actually granted—which is to say: taken, and then defended daily.

"We're bad at finding each other," I said.

"We're alive," he said. "We'll work on the rest."

Inside the hut, Nia was already asleep sitting up, notebook open on her knee, pencil still in her hand—and I saw, before Silas eased it gently from her fingers, what she had drawn. Not the valley. Not the terraces or the flumes or the forty roofs.

She had drawn faces. Dozens of them, quick and sure, filling the page: Odile, Petrov, the silent grandmother, the children. A census no government would ever be given. A map of the only territory that mattered.

I left them to their rest and walked up to the watch post on the western rim, where the night sky lay open and enormous above the walls, and I stood my shift as I have stood a thousand shifts, scanning a horizon that had never once, in eleven years, shown me a light that meant rescue.

Tonight, for the first time, I scanned it anyway.

And near midnight, far downstream past the walls' reach, past the drowned country, at the edge of what my eyes could swear to—there was a flicker. Aircraft lights, descending. More than one.

Toward the old survey camp.

I rang the bell.
