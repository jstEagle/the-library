## 13. The Survey Team Found

The descent into the basin took the better part of an hour, and Nia spent every minute of it cataloguing what she saw, because that was what she did—what she had always done. When the world became too much to hold, she held it in measurements instead.

The valley opened below them like a secret told slowly. Terraced gardens stepped down the southern slope in green geometry. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, thin and domestic, threading upward through the canopy of mist that clung to the ridgeline. There was a mill on the river, its wheel turning with the patient patience of something built to outlast its builders. A schoolhouse, or what looked like one—a long low building with a bell tower made from a repurposed propane tank. Children's voices carried up the trail, high and bright, and then cut off all at once when the scouts brought them through the tree line.

Silence, after that. The particular silence of people deciding what to do with strangers.

Nia walked beside Silas, her sprained ankle wrapped tight in a brace Xanthe's people had provided—willow bark and linen, old medicine that worked better than it had any right to. Xanthe moved ahead of them, and Nia watched how the settlement watched *her*: not with warmth exactly, but with recognition. The look of a community seeing its own difficult conscience come home with company.

"They'll want to see you first," Xanthe said, without turning around. "Both of you. Before anyone else talks to you."

"Who?" Nia asked, though she already knew. She had known since the ridge, since the arrowpoint, since the moment her professional mind had started assembling the impossible arithmetic: five missing surveyors, zero bodies, and a hidden valley that appeared on no map she'd ever drawn.

Xanthe stopped at the edge of the compound, where a split-rail fence separated the gardens from the central square. Beyond it, near the well, four figures sat on a bench beneath a tarpaulin awning. They were dressed in the same field clothes they'd vanished in—Nia recognized the sun-faded orange of their survey vests—and they looked, at this distance, like men waiting for a bus that had already left without them.

One of them stood up. He was tall, gray at the temples, with the lean weathered frame of someone who'd spent thirty years carrying instruments up mountains. Even across the distance, even after three weeks, Nia knew the set of his shoulders.

"Oh," she said. It came out very small. "Oh, thank God."

* * *

Dr. Amara Reyes had been her mentor once. Before the commission, before the government contracts, back when Nia was a graduate student who still believed maps were neutral things. Reyes had taught her that a contour line was a promise: *the world is shaped like this, and I am telling you true.* She had also taught her, less formally, that institutions lie, and that the only defense was to be so precise in your own work that their lies couldn't hide inside it.

Now he crossed the square toward her with the stiff, careful gait of a man who had been sitting too long, and stopped two paces away, and looked at her as if she might be a hallucination he'd earned.

"Okafor." His voice was rough. "They said you broke your ankle."

"I did." Her own voice wasn't working properly. "Three weeks ago. I was supposed to be with you."

"We know." Something moved behind his eyes—not quite anger, not quite relief. "We've had a lot of time to talk about it."

He embraced her then, briefly, hard, his chin against the top of her head, and she felt him shaking, just slightly, the tremor of a body that had been holding itself together by routine for twenty-one days. Over his shoulder she could see the others: Vasquez, the geologist, thinner than she remembered; Chen and Osei, the technicians, both unshaven, both watching her with the wary flatness of people who had learned recently that safety was conditional. Okonkwo was missing from the count. Her stomach dropped.

"Reyes. Where's—"

"He's fine." Reyes pulled back, hands still on her shoulders. "He took a fall in the gorge the first day. Busted his knee. They've been setting it here—there's a woman, Adaeze, who knows bones the way you know contours. He's resting. He'll want to see you when he can walk to the door."

The relief hit her like altitude—too much, too fast, a pressure behind the eyes. Five alive. All five alive. Three weeks she had been mapping search grids in her head, calculating drift patterns for wreckage downriver, doing the cold arithmetic of probability that cartographers do when they're trying not to grieve, and the answer had been sitting in a garden valley eating food grown in terraced soil.

"You found us," Reyes said quietly. "Or they let you find us. I'm honestly not sure which anymore."

Behind him, Xanthe had stopped at the fence line, arms folded, watching the reunion with an expression Nia couldn't read. Guarding it, maybe. Or guarding against it.

"Tell me what happened," Nia said. "All of it. From the beginning."

* * *

They talked in the long building—the schoolhouse, though classes had been suspended, children peering through the windows at the outsiders until a woman shooed them away. Someone brought tea that tasted of smoked pine and something bitter. Silas sat near the door, which Nia noted he had done deliberately, angled so he could see both the room and the exit. Old habit, or new caution. Hard to say with him.

"It was day nine," Reyes began. He spoke the way he'd taught her to draw: methodically, leaving nothing out, marking uncertainty where it lived rather than smoothing it over. "We'd finished the upper gorge transects and were working south along the eastern tributary. Standard reconnaissance grid. And then—" he paused, and Nia saw him choose honesty over tidiness—"and then Chen ran the composite. We'd been stitching survey data for eight days, elevation models, vegetation indices, the thermal imagery from the drone passes. And the composite showed us something we hadn't gone looking for."

"The settlement," Nia said.

"The settlement." He nodded slowly. "Heat signatures. Cleared ground. Cultivated soil with a spectral signature you don't get from wild land. A road that isn't a road, a mill dam, terracing. Forty years of habitation, Okafor, sitting in our data like a bruise under skin. And none of it on any map. Not yours, not mine, not the commission's master registry."

Vasquez spoke up from the bench, her voice hoarse. "Reyes wanted to file immediately. Satellite uplink, encrypted burst transmission, done in ten minutes. But we were three days' hike from the last relay point, and the weather window was closing, and—" she glanced at Chen, who said nothing—"and there was a disagreement about whether we should verify on the ground first."

"So you went in."

"So we went in," Reyes confirmed. "And within six hours of entering the basin, we were met. Not attacked. *Met.* Arrows in trees around the camp, notes in three languages telling us to stop and turn around. And when we didn't—" he spread his hands—"they took our radios. All of them. Every transmitter, every beacon, everything that could call the outside world down on this place. And they walked us here, and they've kept us here. Fed us. Treated Okonkwo's knee. Given us shelter, work, a place at the table. And kept us. There's a word for that, and it isn't 'hospitality.'"

"Detention," Nia said.

"Detention," Reyes agreed. "Polite detention. Well-intentioned detention. But ask yourself, Okafor—if your government found a valley full of people who erased themselves from every registry, people who are here because warrants and debts and wrongful convictions followed them like weather—what does the commission do with our data?"

And there it was. The question she had been carrying since the camp, since the logbook written in her own handwriting, since Hargrove's sealed orders. She thought of the liaison's smooth voice on the radio, the manifest with half its cargo unlisted, the way the contract had smelled wrong from the first signature. She thought of what she had confessed to Silas on the riverbank—that the survey was never geology, never hydrology, that the words *resource assessment* in her briefing documents had always been a euphemism wearing a lab coat.

"They evict," she said. "That's what they do. That's what the survey was for."

The room went quiet. Silas looked at her—really looked, the pilot's flat assessing stare that missed nothing—and she held it, because she was done hiding inside her precision.

"You knew," Reyes said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse: it was confirmation-seeking, the tone of a scientist checking a result.

"I suspected. Then I confirmed. Then I got grounded with a busted ankle while my team walked into the middle of it." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "I drew the maps, Amara. My data. My composites. If they build the eviction on a foundation, I'm the foundation."

"No." Reyes leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment he was again the professor who had shaped her, fierce and exacting. "No, Okafor. You're the correction. There's a difference, and it matters more right now than it has ever mattered."

* * *

The argument started an hour later, and Nia would remember afterward that it began over tea.

It was Vasquez who lit the fuse—or maybe it was simply time; three weeks of polite detention had been compressing in all of them like a fault line under strain. Vasquez had lost weight and gained a hardness, and she set down her cup with a click that silenced the room.

"I'm done pretending this is complicated," she said. "We have obligations. We signed contracts. We have a duty of care to the record—to truth, plural, whatever you want to call it. This valley exists. These people exist. Our instruments measured them. The correct action is to report what we found, accurately and completely, and let the process work."

"The process," Xanthe said from the doorway, "evicted my mother."

Nia turned. Xanthe hadn't been invited into the conversation, but she stood there now with the stillness of someone who had learned that doors were for other people. Her voice was level. Almost gentle. That was the frightening part.

"In 2141, the commission surveyed the Kessing Flats. Found 'underutilized land.' Issued relocation notices. My mother's family had farmed those flats for sixty years—no title, because the government that should have granted them one spent that era burning the records of people it didn't want to exist. So they were relocated. To a settlement camp. Two-thirds of them dead within five years—dysentery, exposure, despair, pick your cause of death, they're all listed as 'environmental factors.'" She let that sit. "The process worked, Ms. Vasquez. It worked exactly as designed. That's the problem."

"That was fifteen years ago," Vasquez said. "Different administration."

"Different letterhead. Same mapmakers." Xanthe's eyes flicked to Nia, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "Same instruments. Same beautiful, honest, accurate data, handed to people who will use it as a weapon. You don't get to wash your hands by being precise. Precision is the knife. Someone else swings it."

"And the alternative?" Reyes asked quietly. "What's the alternative to truth? We hide the valley? We falsify the record? We become the thing we're supposed to be measuring against?"

"The alternative is consent," Xanthe said. "You don't report on people like they're ore deposits. You ask them. All of them. And some of them will say no, and that answer is real data too, and your science has no column for it."

Silence. Rain had begun against the roof, soft and steady, the leading breath of the monsoon still days out but making its presence known like a guest arriving early. Nia became aware that the room had filled—settlers in the doorway, in the windows, drawn by the raised voices, watching the argument the way you watch weather. An older man with a carpenter's forearms. A young woman holding a baby. Faces. Dozens of faces, each one attached to a name that existed in no registry anywhere, each one a person the commission would render as a heat signature, a cleared hectare, a line item in a relocation order.

This was what her data pointed at. Not terrain. *People.*

"Here's what I keep coming back to," Reyes said finally, and his voice had dropped, gone tired, gone human. "I'm sixty-one years old. I've spent my whole life believing that the record is sacred—that if we just tell the truth carefully enough, carefully enough, eventually the truth protects people. That's the religion I was raised in, professionally speaking. And I look at this valley and I think: these people hid because the record was used to destroy them. And I think: if we stay silent, we become complicit in the erasure of everyone who comes after, everyone who could have been protected by knowing this place exists. And I think: if we speak, we hand a loaded instrument to people who have already shown us what they do with it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Both sides have justice on their side, Okafor. That's what nobody tells you. Sometimes the deadlock isn't cowardice. Sometimes it's two truths refusing to lie to each other."

Nia looked at her mentor—at the gray in his beard, the tremor in his hands, the thirty years of integrity worn down to this single unbearable knot—and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't grief and wasn't anger but was adjacent to both.

"I know," she said. "I know, Amara. I've been standing in that same knot since the camp."

"And what are you going to do about it?"

She didn't answer. Because the honest answer was *I don't know yet*, and because somewhere beyond the rain, beyond the ridgeline, downstream where the old survey camp stood empty with its instruments still running, a liaison's deputy was logging tail numbers and waiting for weather reports, and the monsoon was forty-eight hours out, and every hour of silence was a choice too.

What she said instead was: "I need to see the original composite. Chen's data. All of it. If there's going to be a decision, it gets made looking at the actual numbers, not at our fears wearing the numbers' clothes."

Reyes almost smiled. "There's my student."

"Don't," she said. "Not yet. I haven't decided whose side of the knot I'm standing on."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the schoolhouse, Chen spreading his printouts across the children's desks—paper printouts, Nia realized, because the settlement allowed no networked devices past the tree line, and the realization settled into her like sediment: these people had built their lives in the gaps between transmissions, and every device she carried was a hole in their wall.

Chen was younger than she remembered—younger than her, maybe, though he carried himself older now. His hands shook slightly as he laid out the sheets, and when Nia touched his shoulder he flinched, then steadied.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I keep thinking—I ran the composite. I flagged the signatures. If I'd just... sat on it. Rounded the thresholds. Called it sensor noise."

"Then you'd be a bad scientist," Nia said, "and the next team wouldn't be. The data doesn't go away, Chen. It just waits for someone less careful."

He nodded, and didn't look comforted, and she understood that nothing she could say would be, not for years.

Outside, the rain thickened. Through the fogged windows the valley turned the color of pewter, the terraces running with silver water, the smoke from the chimneys beaten low. And downriver—downstream, past the gorge, past the empty camp with its tents still pitched and its logbook still lying open in her own handwriting—she could feel the other machinery moving. Hargrove's timeline. The sealed orders. The writs that had surely already been drafted, sitting in some office in the capital, describing in bureaucratic prose the removal of people who had names, and gardens, and a school bell made from a propane tank.

Silas appeared in the doorway, rain streaming off his jacket, and the look on his face stopped her mid-calculation.

"What," she said.

"Boat came up from the watch post on the lower river." He dripped onto the schoolhouse floor, unbothered, voice pitched low enough that only she and Reyes could hear. "Smoke downstream. Engine smoke, not woodsmoke. Big engines. Somebody's flying heavy equipment into the old survey strip, and they're not being quiet about it."

Reyes went pale. "How many?"

"Too many to be a rescue party," Silas said. "And they landed at *our* camp, Nia. Your camp. Which means they read your logbook."

The rain hammered down. Somewhere below, in the settlement, the school bell rang—not for class, Nia realized, but in the pattern she'd heard described that morning, three-and-one, the signal the valley used for strangers on the river.

She looked down at Chen's composite spread across the desks, at the heat signatures glowing like embers in the grayscale, at the valley rendered in all its terrible, beautiful precision. Everything the government needed. Everything she had made.

"Nia," Silas said quietly. "We need to decide. Now."

She was already gathering the printouts into her arms.
