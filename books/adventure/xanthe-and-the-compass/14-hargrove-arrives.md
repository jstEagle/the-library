## 14. Hargrove Arrives

The rain came first as a rumor, the way all bad news did in river country.

Silas stood on the ridge above the source basin at dawn, watching a gray wall build itself over the western passes—slow, patient, enormous, like something that had been walking toward them for a thousand years and saw no reason to hurry. The air had gone thick and sweet with it, that green metallic smell of water waiting to fall. Forty-eight hours, maybe less. He'd flown ahead of storms his whole life. He knew how they thought.

Behind him, down in the valley, smoke rose from cook fires in thin, careful columns—the kind of smoke you made when you'd spent years learning not to make any at all. He could hear children somewhere below, a high bright sound that didn't belong to a place that was supposed to be empty on every map ever drawn. A woman laughed. Someone was arguing about fish.

It sounded like home. That was the thing that kept catching in his chest like a hook. It sounded exactly like the life he'd spent seven years failing to build for himself, and now he was standing on a ridge doing arithmetic about how long they'd have to keep it.

"You're up early."

Nia climbed the last of the slope with her bad ankle wrapped tight, moving with the stubborn economy of someone who refused to let a body part vote. She stopped beside him and followed his eyes west.

"Monsoon," she said.

"Monsoon."

"Forty-eight hours?"

"Forty-six if I'm lucky. Thirty-eight if I'm honest."

She was quiet a moment. Below them the river ran silver through the basin, the same river they'd poled up for six days, reading cut saplings and cold fire rings, following a trail of deliberate invisibility to this place that wasn't anywhere. Nia had drawn maps her whole adult life. Silas watched her look at the valley the way a person looks at a child they've just realized they'd die for.

"Reyes is still talking," she said finally. "About reporting it. About how the law's the law."

"I know. I heard him at breakfast."

"He's not wrong, Silas. That's what makes it ugly. He's not wrong about anything except what the law *is*."

Silas didn't answer. Downriver, past the gorge and the dead survey camp and two days of hard water, an airstrip sat in a clearing where he'd landed a government contract he should never have signed. Where his plane sat under a tarp with half its fuel drained into drums because he hadn't trusted Hargrove's people alone with it. Where, three nights ago, he'd made a promise to Xanthe in the dark: *if they come, we'll have maybe a day's warning.*

He checked his watch out of habit. Then he heard it.

At first he took it for thunder rolling off the pass—but thunder didn't hold its pitch. Thunder didn't come up a river valley in a straight line, growing from a drone to a growl to a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat. Radial engines. Two of them, heavy, running rich.

Nia heard it a beat later. Her head came up like a bird dog's.

"Is that—"

"That's not weather." His mouth had gone dry. "That's a Ford trimotor, or something built by the same man who hated pilots. Coming upriver. Low."

They stood together on the ridge and watched the shapes resolve out of the gray—two aircraft flying wing to wing, boxy and military-green, floats kissing the water, each one wearing the flat gray livery of the Interior Survey Directorate with its small official crest. Not search planes. Search planes flew high and looked down. These were flying the river like a road, like men who already knew the address.

"Xanthe," Silas said, and was already running.

* * *

By the time he reached the settlement the whole valley knew. People stood along the terraces with their faces turned downstream, and the sound of the engines rolled over them again and again, and Silas watched seven years of careful erasure crack open in real time—a woman pulling her son behind her, an old man going very still beside a drying rack, a teenager dropping an armload of firewood and not picking it up.

Xanthe met him at the foot of the council house steps, bow slung across her back, hair tied back, her face doing what his was doing: nothing, absolutely nothing, while everything underneath screamed.

"How many?" she asked.

"Two aircraft. Floats. Directorate colors. Armed, or I'm a missionary." He caught his breath. "They're landing at the old survey camp. That's the deep-water strip. It's the only place on this river they can put down anything that size."

"Then they know the camp. They know our river." Her jaw tightened. "Your liaison. Hargrove."

"Hargrove doesn't fly enforcement," Silas said. "Hargrove files paperwork. Whoever's on those floats brought writs."

Downstream, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that settled the question: the flat crack of a flare pistol, then another. Signal rounds. Somebody wanted to be found.

* * *

The council convened in the longhouse within the hour, and Silas learned what it meant to sit in a room full of people who had built their lives on not being in rooms.

There were perhaps sixty of them in the valley—farmers and foragers, a midwife named Oren who'd done eleven years for a theft she hadn't committed, a former railway engineer called Doss who'd fled a manslaughter charge earned by saving three men instead of five, families born inside the erasure who had never held a document in their lives. Xanthe sat at the head, not presiding so much as holding the room's weight on her shoulders, and Silas saw for the first time how much of her was different—the stillness, the economy, the way everyone looked at her not with love exactly but with the particular attention owed to someone carrying something breakable.

"They'll have eviction writs," Xanthe said. "Paper signed by people who've never seen this valley. They'll read names off a registry we were never on and declare us trespassers on land we've kept alive for twenty years."

"Then we melt into the high forest," said Doss. "We've drilled it. Three days' walk to the winter caves. Let them read their papers to empty houses."

"And come back to what?" Oren asked quietly. "They'll burn the terraces. That's what they do—they can't evict what they can't see, so they make sure there's nothing left to see. We lose everything we built either way. The only question is whether we lose it standing in it."

"The caves are death in monsoon season for anyone young or old," said a lean man near the door—Reyes, the surveyor, the one whose colleagues sat safe and furious in the guest hut. "No offense meant. But I've seen what forty inches of rain does to a karst cave system. You'd drown in the dark."

"Easy for you to say from the dry side of the argument," Doss snapped.

"I'm saying you don't have good options," Reyes said. "I'm saying that honestly, because I'm the only one here who's read the Director's field manual. They don't leave. Once they hold the valley, they hold it. You hide, you starve slower. That's the whole strategy available to you."

The room went ugly after that—voices rising, old grievances surfacing like bodies in floodwater, a woman accusing the scouts of missing the aircraft until the last minute, Doss saying the council had gotten soft since Xanthe started trusting outsiders, and Silas felt the words land on him too, because he *was* the outsider, he and Nia both, the ones who'd led the sound of engines up a river that had spent two decades learning silence.

Xanthe let it burn itself down. When the room finally quieted, she spoke once.

"We decide by morning. Nobody decides scared." She looked around the longhouse. "And nobody decides without knowing the truth, which is this: whatever we choose, some of us pay more than others. That's not failure. That's just the shape of it."

* * *

Silas found Nia at the edge of the clearing afterward, hunched over her field case by lamplight, drawing. He stood over her shoulder and felt his stomach drop.

She was drawing the valley. All of it—the terraces, the water channels, the cave mouths, the defensive sight lines, every feature rendered in her precise cartographer's hand.

"What is that?"

"Leverage." She didn't look up. "Or insurance. Or a noose, depending on the day. I haven't decided." She set down her pen. "Silas, listen to me. Those planes didn't come upriver on a guess. Somebody gave them coordinates. And there's exactly one complete map of this valley in existence, and it's in my head and my case, and the moment those contractors land, I become the most valuable piece of paper in this entire watershed."

"So you burn it."

"I burn it, and if we win, we've got nothing to negotiate recognition *with*. You can't ask a government to recognize a place you can't prove exists." She rubbed her eyes. "You can't fight paper with arrows, Silas. You fight paper with better paper. But right now my better paper is worth more than every life in this valley, and I am *so tired* of being the map."

He crouched beside her. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the lamplight swung, and somewhere below a child cried and was hushed.

"There's a third option," Silas said slowly. The idea had been forming all day, ugly and necessary, like a bruise coming up. "Nobody's said it out loud yet, so I'll say it. Whatever happens in the next two days—negotiation, evacuation, firefight—somebody has to carry the truth out of here. Evidence. Testimony. Names. The fraudulent surveys, the wrongful convictions, all of it. Because if we lose, the only story anyone outside will ever hear is the Directorate's."

"And the only way out," Nia said, "is your plane."

"My plane."

They looked at each other, and Silas watched her do the same math he was doing. One aircraft. Half-fueled, sitting two days downstream at a strip that would be crawling with armed men by nightfall. A storm wall closing the passes behind it. Sixty people who couldn't all fit in it if it flew ten trips.

"It's the only bridge," he said. "Between this valley and the world. And Hargrove knows it. That's why they landed at the camp instead of pushing upriver—they don't need to find us. They need to take the bridge, and wait for the rain to do the rest."

* * *

He went downstream alone that evening, telling himself it was reconnaissance.

The river carried him fast through the gorge, past the cut banks and the snare lines, and he paddled the last stretch in the dark with the engines' echo still living somewhere in his skull. He beached above the survey camp and lay in the wet ferns and looked at what the Directorate had built in a single afternoon.

Lamplight everywhere. A generator droning. The two green floatplanes moored at the bank like sleeping animals. Tents in military rows, and men moving among them with rifles slung loose and easy—the posture of professionals, not panickers. A radio mast already up, antenna wire singing faintly in the wind. At the center of the camp, under the biggest lamp, a folding table had been set with documents weighted under stones against the wind, and a man in a clean khaki uniform stood reading them aloud to a circle of subordinates while they ate.

Eviction writs. Silas could almost hear the syllables from across the water.

He counted thirty-one men. Counted the machine gun mounted on a tripod by the moored planes. Counted the fuel drums lined up on the shore—full ones, dozens, enough to fly anything anywhere—and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that they had brought everything they needed to stay forever, and that the only thing in the entire watershed they hadn't accounted for was him.

His plane sat at the far end of the strip under its tarp, untouched, guarded by a single bored contractor smoking under the wing. Half-fueled. His tail number logged in Hargrove's neat book. Every hour it sat there, it became more theirs.

Silas lay in the ferns until his arms shook, memorizing guard rotations, drum counts, the angle of the moon on the water. Then he paddled back upriver through the dark with the storm wall rising behind him like a second horizon, and by the time he reached the valley he had stopped thinking of the plane as his livelihood.

It was a bridge. Bridges got burned.

* * *

He told Xanthe at midnight, in the council house, with Nia and Doss and Oren listening. He laid it out flat: thirty-one men, one machine gun, two floatplanes, a radio mast, fuel enough to stay a season. Writs to be served at first light, probably. An ultimatum by noon.

"And your plane sits at the end of their guns," Xanthe said.

"For now."

"Silas." She said his name the way she used to say it when they were kids and he'd rigged a rope swing over the quarry—warning and admiration in the same breath. "Whatever you're building in your head, it costs something. Everything costs something now. Say it plain."

He looked at his sister—at the woman she'd become, harder than the girl he'd lost, carrying sixty lives in her stillness—and he said it plain.

"Tomorrow, before they serve those writs, somebody has to get to that strip and take my plane out from under their guns, or destroy it so they can't use it, or use it to carry the evidence out before the rain seals everything. Any one of those choices burns a bridge we might need. And whichever way it goes—" his voice caught, and he hated it, and he went on anyway, "—somebody in this valley pays for it. Maybe everybody. Maybe me. There's no version of tomorrow where nobody bleeds. I just want this council choosing with its eyes open."

The lamp hissed. Rain began, far away, a soft static on the roof that would become a roar in two days.

Doss spoke first. "You're talking about war, pilot."

"I'm talking about arithmetic," Silas said. "Thirty-one men can't hold a valley. But they can hold a plane. And whoever holds the plane when the rain comes owns the only story that gets out."

Xanthe closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was looking at him with something that was grief and pride tangled past separating, and she said:

"Then we decide tonight. All of us. Because come sunrise, there won't be time to be afraid properly."

Outside, the first fat drops hit the leaves, and downriver the generators droned on, and Silas Pemberton sat in a room full of the erased and understood, finally and completely, that the storm they'd been counting hours toward had never been the weather at all.
