## 16. Sabotage and Sacrifice

The rain came downriver ahead of the monsoon like a scout sent to test the gates—thin, cold, needling through the canopy in threads that found every gap in a man's collar. Silas stood at the edge of the ridge above the old survey camp and watched the downstream strip through Nia's binoculars, and what he saw made his stomach fold over on itself with the particular nausea of a man watching vultures land on something he still loved.

Two aircraft sat on the grass strip where his Cessna had spent seven years of mornings. Hargrove's enforcement flight had come in heavy: a twin-engine utility plane with its cargo doors hanging open, contractors unloading crates in the gray light, and beside it a fuel bowser—a trailer-mounted tank, gleaming new, government paint—parked square in the middle of the strip like a throne.

"They're staging for the valley run," Nia said beside him, her voice low even though no one could hear them at this distance over the river's noise. "Resupply point. They'll shuttle fuel and men up to the camp, then push patrols toward the source basin when the weather window opens."

Silas passed the binoculars back without looking at her. He was doing arithmetic he didn't want to do.

The strip was his. Not on paper—the government owned the paper—but in every way that mattered. He'd cut those trees himself the year after Xanthe left, when clearing brush seemed easier than clearing his head. He'd dragged the gravel by hand, graded it with a borrowed blade, built the lean-to shelter at the north end where he kept his tools and his spare parts and, folded into oilcloth beneath the floorboards, three drums of aviation fuel he'd cached against exactly this kind of season: the weeks before the monsoon sealed everything, when a man might need to fly out of trouble without warning.

Three drums. Roughly a hundred and twenty gallons. Enough to get him to the coast twice over. Enough, if it fell into other hands, to keep Hargrove's shuttles running until the valley had been walked, measured, photographed, and emptied.

"You're thinking about the caches," Nia said.

"I'm thinking about a lot of things."

"Name one that isn't the caches."

He almost smiled. Almost. The rain picked at his shoulders like small impatient fingers. Down at the strip, a contractor in a gray poncho strolled the perimeter with a rifle slung casual as a fishing rod, and Silas watched the man pause at the lean-to, try the door, find it latched, and move on—with the unhurried confidence of someone who believed everything here already belonged to him.

"If I burn my reserves," Silas said, "I'm not just costing them fuel. I'm costing me the only margin I've got. Weather closes the pass, engine runs rough, I need one more drum than I planned—" He stopped. Shook his head. "You know what I am, Nia? I'm a courier. Couriers stay neutral. Neutral is how I've eaten for fifteen years. Nobody shoots at the mail."

Nia took the binoculars back and studied the strip for a long moment. Rain beaded on the lenses; she wiped them with her thumb, an absent, practiced gesture, the same motion she used over her maps.

"Hargrove logged your tail number," she said quietly. "Chapter eight of this story, remember? He wrote it down while smiling at you. That means you were never neutral. You were *pending*." She lowered the glasses. "There's a difference, and you're about to learn it the hard way either way. The only question is whether you choose which side of the ledger you're on, or whether they file you under theirs."

* * *

They came down off the ridge in the last hour of light, moving the way Nia had taught him to move through river country—along the water's voice, letting the rapids eat their footsteps, pausing when the forest paused. Silas had spent his life reading sky and now found himself learning ground: the way ferns held their shape after something passed through them, the way birds restarted their gossip in waves behind walking men. It was Xanthe's country, he realized. She'd learned all of this years ago, alone, and he hadn't been there to teach her so much as a single knot.

The thought did not soften him. It sharpened him.

The lean-to smelled the way it always had—engine oil, canvas, the green rot of old wood—and standing inside it in the dark with a wrench in his hand, Silas felt seven years of solitary competence collapse into something smaller and more honest. This shed was the truest home he had. And he was about to burn its heart out.

The floorboards came up quiet. The first drum was full, heavy, cold as a corpse. He rolled it into the open and stood a moment with his palm flat on its top, feeling the fuel shift inside like something sleeping.

"One hundred and twenty gallons," he said to himself. "Fifteen years of margins."

Then he got to work.

Draining fuel is slow, filthy, patient work. He siphoned what he could into jerry cans—fuel the settlement might use, fuel for the plane itself, nothing wasted that love could save—and the rest he vented deliberately across the gravel, across the grass, a dark spreading slick that drank the flashlight's beam and gave back nothing. The smell rose up around him, raw and chemical, so familiar it hurt. His father had smelled like this. Every hangar of his childhood had smelled like this. Fuel was the smell of going somewhere, of leaving, of freedom bought by the gallon.

Tonight it would smell like a wall.

* * *

He heard the patrol before he saw it—not footsteps but a change in the river's audience, the frogs going silent in a moving line along the bank. He killed the flashlight and stood still among the drums, breathing through his mouth, and counted four shapes coming up from the strip with rifles and one handheld light sweeping lazy arcs through the trees.

They weren't searching. They were patrolling, bored, wet, resentful of the weather and the assignment both. The light crossed the lean-to doorway, lingered on the open floorboards, on the two empty spaces where drums had been, and moved on.

Silas let out a breath he'd been holding since the frogs went quiet.

Then the light swung back.

"—here," a voice said, low. "Floor's open. There were crates stacked here yesterday, I marked them."

Boots on gravel. Two men, maybe three, angling toward the shed. Silas's hand closed around the wrench. It was a poor weapon and a worse idea, and he knew both things with perfect clarity, and his body began moving anyway—

A sound cracked through the forest from the east: a branch snapping, deliberate, loud, then another, farther off, as though a large animal were blundering away through the understory. The lights swung toward it. A radio crackled. Somewhere in the dark, Nia—who could barely jog on that ankle, who should have been a hundred yards back at the treeline—was throwing her voice through the woods like a woman who'd grown up hiding from something, and Silas understood in one cold flash that he had never once asked her what she'd survived before the survey team. He filed it away with all the other debts.

The boots receded. The lights chased the phantom eastward and found nothing, because there was nothing, because Nia was already gone, already repositioned, already better at this than he was.

He finished the work fast now, hands steady in the way they only got when the stakes stopped being abstract. The second drum he didn't bother draining. He cracked its bung, tipped it, let it glug out across the slick until the whole clearing reeked of aviation fuel, and then he knelt at the edge of it with a road flare from his emergency kit and understood that the next ten seconds would sort the rest of his life into before and after.

A courier flies for anyone. A courier burns nothing but distance.

His livelihood was soaking into the mud at his knees. Fifteen years of careful neutrality, of flying medicine and mail and the occasional quiet passenger whose paperwork he didn't read, all of it built on the principle that the sky belonged to whoever paid and the ground belonged to whoever held it and Silas Pemberton belonged to neither.

Xanthe's face came to him—not the woman at the ridge with the bow, harder and older, but the girl of eleven holding the brass compass up to the sun, asking him how a needle could know which way was true when everything else lied.

*Because it doesn't decide,* he'd told her. *It just refuses to point anywhere else.*

Silas struck the flare.

It hissed alive, red and furious, spitting sparks, and for one heartbeat he held it above the slick and let himself feel the whole cost at once—the money, the margin, the safety, the self he'd been for fifteen years—and then he dropped it into the fuel and stepped back and the night turned to daylight.

The fire went up with a *whump* that he felt in his teeth, a wall of orange rolling across the strip, and the heat hit him like a hand shoving his chest, and the drums began to sing—that low moaning note heated metal makes just before it fails—and Silas Pemberton stood in the light of his own burning livelihood and felt, beneath the terror and the grief, something he hadn't felt since he was young enough to fly for joy.

Commitment. The terrible, clean kind.

* * *

The strip erupted. Whistles, shouts, the twin-engine's crew scrambling to drag their aircraft clear of the radiant heat, contractors forming a bucket line from the river that was theater more than tactic because aviation fuel does not negotiate with buckets. In the chaos, nobody was watching the trees. That was the plan. The plan worked right up until it didn't.

Silas was forty yards into the forest, moving on Nia's bearing, when the first shot cracked.

Not aimed at him—at movement, a warning, panic dressed as procedure. Then a second shot, and this one had intent in it, and something snapped past his ear close enough to part the air, and he dove behind a fallen log as bark exploded off the trunk above him.

"Nia!" he hissed into the dark. "Nia, talk to me."

Static silence. His heart went from fast to absent. He crawled along the log, listening past the fire's roar, past the shouting at the strip, hunting for her breathing, her swearing, anything—

"Here." Her voice, thirty feet left, strained but level. "Don't come straight. They'll have the angle. Come wide through the ferns."

He came wide through the ferns and found her propped against a tree trunk with her bad ankle stretched out and her hand pressed high on her left arm, above the elbow, blood between her fingers, black in the dark.

"Graze," she said before he could speak. "Through and through the sleeve meat. It's loud and it's shallow and I will be extremely insulted if you make a scene."

"Scene's cancelled." He was already cutting her sleeve open with his knife, hands doing triage while his mind screamed. The wound was exactly what she said—a hot furrow torn along the outer arm, ugly, bleeding freely, missing anything vital by the width of a prayer. He packed it with cloth from his pocket, bound it tight with a strip of tarp, tied it off with his teeth. His hands shook now, now that it was over, now that it was allowed.

"You came back for me," she said. Her voice had gone thin at the edges. Shock arriving on schedule. "At the shed. When they had the light on you. Someone drew them off, and it wasn't a ghost."

"You drew them off me first." He got his shoulder under her good arm. "We're even. Now we're leaving, because in about ninety seconds that fire reaches the bowser trailer, and when a government fuel tank lets go, this entire hillside becomes a headline."

"The bowser's—" She looked back through the trees at the blaze, and even hurt and half-shocked, the cartographer in her did the math instantly. "That's why you drained the drums instead of just torching the shed. You wanted the fire big enough and positioned right to cook their resupply tank. You burned your own caches as *ammunition*."

Silas hauled her upright. Behind them the fire reached the trailer with a sound like the world's largest door slamming, and the shockwave flattened ferns for fifty feet and lifted a column of flame that turned the rain to gold, and every man on that strip stopped being a soldier and became a man running from fire, which was exactly the point.

"That's my livelihood burning," Silas said, into her hair, half carrying her upslope through the smoke. "Fifteen years. Every spare dollar, every careful season, every time I kept my mouth shut and my manifest clean."

"And?"

"And it turns out I'd rather be broke on the right side than solvent in the middle." He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, breathless and disbelieving. "God help me, Nia. I think I just became one of *them*. One of Xanthe's people. An erased man."

"No," Nia said, limping hard against his shoulder, her blood soaking warm through his shirt. "Erased men hide. You just signed your name to something in letters sixty feet high. There's a difference."

* * *

They reached the ridge overlook near midnight and turned to look back, because some sights demand to be witnessed.

The strip below was an inferno contained to its own grave—fuel fires don't spread so much as consume, and the contractors had finally learned to simply stand back and let it eat. The bowser was a black skeleton. The twin-engine sat at the far end, saved, but its crew would not be flying resupply missions anywhere soon, because there was nothing to resupply with and nowhere to stage it from, and the monsoon wall was now visible even at night: a vast darker darkness stacking up over the western passes, flickering internally like something thinking.

Hargrove's operation had just lost its bridge to the outside. For days, maybe a week. Long enough.

Long enough for a council of the erased to decide what they were willing to become.

Silas sat down heavily on the wet stone, pulled Nia down beside him, and dug the last of the coffee tin's contents from his jacket—dry grounds, eaten straight, the pilot's communion. He poured half into her palm without asking.

"My tail number's logged," he said. "My caches are ash. My name is on whatever report Hargrove writes tonight, and it won't say *witness*." He chewed the bitter grounds and swallowed. "When we get back to the valley, Xanthe's going to ask me why. She's going to want to know what changed."

"What will you tell her?"

Silas watched the fire below gutter and shrink, watched the rain begin again in earnest, fine and cold and honest, and found that the answer had been sitting in his chest since a flare hissed red in his fist.

"I'll tell her the compass was right," he said. "I just refused to point anywhere else for fifteen years. Tonight I stopped."

Beside him, Nia laughed softly, winced, pressed her bandaged arm, and laughed again anyway. Then her eyes went past him, upriver, toward the black wall of the storm, and the laughter drained out of her face.

"Silas," she said. "Look at the pass."

He looked. Where the monsoon's leading edge met the peaks, lightning was strobing in long horizontal sheets—not cloud-to-ground but cloud-to-cloud, a silent silver stitching, sewing the sky shut over the only way out.

The weather window was closing faster than anyone had promised.

"We have maybe two days," Nia said quietly. "Maybe less. Whatever the valley decides to do about Hargrove—hide, flee, fight, or Xanthe's fourth path—they'd better decide it tomorrow. Because after that, the only thing getting in or out of that valley is going to be the rain."
