## 17. The Evidence Flight

The rain began at dawn, as if the sky had been waiting for permission.

I stood in the doorway of Xanthe's longhouse and watched it come down the valley in a gray curtain, sheeting off the ridge lines, turning the river from green to the color of wet slate. Forty-eight hours, Silas had said. He'd been generous. The monsoon was early, and it was hungry.

Behind me, the settlement was already moving. Not panicking—these were people who had practiced being invisible for years, and even now, with the decision made and the writs waiting downstream like a held breath, they moved with a kind of grim choreography. Women I'd come to know by their hands—Marta's ink-stained fingers, Odile's scarred knuckles—carried oilcloth bundles down to the drying sheds. Children who had never seen a government form were taught, quietly, what a registry was and why theirs mattered.

Because that was the cargo. That was what we were loading into a bush plane built for medicine runs and fuel drums: paper. The whole weight of seven years of erasure, folded into satchels and waterproof tubes and one dented tin box that had crossed three borders hidden under floorboards.

"Cartographer." Reyes's voice behind me, careful. He'd been avoiding me since the council broke up, since he'd stood in front of everyone and argued for reporting the valley and been answered not with anger but with silence—the worst thing this community knew how to give. "They're asking for you at the strip."

"I know where the strip is, Reyes."

"You also know I was right." He said it without heat. That was the strange thing about him; he could be right the way weather was right, indifferent to whether you wanted it. "Reporting the coordinates doesn't have to mean eviction. There are procedures. Protections."

"There were procedures when my team walked into that valley," I said. "There were protections on paper for every name in that tin box. Paper is exactly the problem, Reyes. We're just choosing whose paper gets read first."

He didn't answer. Down in the clearing, someone struck the forge awake, and the sound rang up through the rain like a bell rung underwater.

* * *

The strip was a meadow pretending to be an airstrip, which was how Silas liked them—short, soft, hemmed by trees tall enough to make every approach a negotiation. The Otter sat at the upper end where the ground firmed to gravel, and around it the loading party worked in the rain, passing bundles hand to hand up into the cabin because the wheels would sink anywhere else.

Silas stood in the cargo door in his oilskins, checking each item against a list he kept in his head, because writing things down was suddenly a liability. He looked older than he had two weeks ago. Or maybe just more legible—like a map that had finally been annotated.

"Documents," he said, taking the first tube from Marta. "Sealed. Dry. If it gets wet, you tell me immediately, because waterlogged evidence is a rumor, and rumors don't hold hearings."

"The tubes are military surplus," Marta said. "They survived a flood in '19."

"Then they'll survive my flying." He took the next bundle—the registry—and handled it differently. Slower. I watched his thumb move across the oilcloth and understood that he wasn't checking the wrapping. He was feeling for the weight of the names inside. Two hundred and eleven people, some of them children born in this valley who did not exist on any government's earth. Some of them old men who had been convicted of crimes committed against themselves.

I carried the tin box myself. Inside it: the recordings. Three weeks of work compressed onto magnetic tape and wax cylinders both, because Xanthe trusted nothing newer than her own paranoia. Hargrove's surveyors, interviewed gently, then less gently, until they admitted what the sealed orders said—that the geological survey was reconnaissance, that the mineral estimates had been inflated fourfold to justify seizure, that the eviction writs predated the discovery of anything worth evicting anyone for. My own voice was on those tapes too, reading my own field notes aloud, confessing in a flat cartographer's monotone that I had drawn the maps that made all of this possible.

I had listened to myself once. Never again.

"Nia." Silas's hand on my shoulder, brief. "You're navigating."

"I'm always navigating."

"Not like this." He nodded at the sky, at the black wall building over the western pass, stacked cloud on cloud like slate shingles torn loose and hurled upward. "Instruments lie in that stuff. Static bends the needle. You're going to fly us out by remembering where the river is when we can't see it. Can you do that?"

I thought about the gorge. Eleven days upstream, reading water with him, learning its grammar—the way it widened before rapids, the way it spoke against stone. I had drawn that river once from satellite imagery, back when I still believed maps belonged to whoever paid for them. Now I knew it from underneath, from a boat, from the sound of it in the dark.

"Yes," I said. "Can you?"

He smiled, and it was the first time I'd seen the smile reach all the way up. "Ask me after."

* * *

We loaded until there was nothing left to load. Fuel drums lashed in the cabin because the external tanks had gone to ash at the downstream strip—his caches, burned by his own hand, and I still caught him sometimes looking at his hands as if they belonged to a stranger. A man who had spent fifteen years keeping his neutrality polished like a windshield had torched his livelihood in one night, and the bill hadn't come due yet, but we both knew it was in the mail.

Xanthe came down through the trees as the last bundle went aboard. She moved differently than she had at the ridge, at arrowpoint—I'd had time to study her these past days, and I'd noticed that she never entered a space without pausing at its edge first, reading it, cataloguing exits. A habit, Silas said, from the year she'd spent running before she found this place. But today she came straight down the slope, straight to the plane, straight to her brother, and the pause she made was only long enough for the rain to run off her chin.

"It's all aboard?" she asked.

"All aboard," Silas said. "Tapes, registry, documents. Plus forty pounds of coffee Marta insisted on, which I'm counting as morale equipment."

"She's right. Morale wins hearings." Xanthe looked past him at the aircraft—at the Otter sitting patient in the rain with its load of other people's lives strapped down in its belly—and something moved across her face that I recognized because I'd worn it myself standing over my own field kit: the look of a person handing over the only copy of something irreplaceable.

She reached into her coat.

I knew the compass before she brought it fully out. I'd seen it wedged under the camp stove, seen what it did to Silas's hands—the first time I'd ever seen them shake. Brass, small enough to disappear in a palm, engraved with a single letter worn nearly smooth: **X**. Seven years it had traveled without him. It had been in a camp it shouldn't have been in, under a stove in a camp where no one should have known to leave it, and I understood now—it had been left for him. A message in the only language the settlement trusted: an object placed deliberately, visible once, then found.

Xanthe held it out flat on her palm between them, rain pooling in the curve of her hand around it.

"This isn't mine," she said. "It never was. You gave it to a girl who thought the world was bigger than it was cruel." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the effort of it, the way you hear current under ice. "I've used it to find north in places north didn't want to be found. It's the only thing I took from home that I don't regret taking."

"Xanthe—"

"Shut up and take it." But she was smiling when she said it, fierce and cracked open at once, and when his hand closed over hers around the compass she didn't let go right away. They stood like that in the rain, brother and sister, holding a piece of brass between them like a treaty.

"You always flew true," she said. "Even when you flew away. Even when I hated you for it. Fly this. Bring it back pointing at me."

Silas's jaw worked. I turned away to give them the moment, busying myself with the tie-downs, listening to the rain hammer the wing above me like a crowd that couldn't decide whether to cheer or mourn.

When I looked back, the compass was in his breast pocket, and Xanthe was stepping down off the wheel strut, and neither of them had said goodbye, because they weren't finished with each other yet. That was the difference. Seven years ago the parting had been an ending. This one was punctuation.

* * *

Engine start in the rain is a ritual of faith. Silas ran it like liturgy—mags checked, mixture set, primer counted—and the nine-cylinder radial coughed twice, caught, and settled into that ragged thunder that I had learned, somewhere in the last two weeks without noticing, to think of as a heartbeat.

The settlement gathered at the meadow's edge. Not all of them—some were already packing for the pass, hedging against our failure, and no one blamed them—but enough. Enough faces under enough oilskins to make a congregation. Marta raised one ink-stained hand, not waving, just open. Odile put her arm around a boy who had never been counted by anyone and lifted him so he could see over the shoulders of adults.

Reyes stood apart from them, near the trees. He met my eyes through the rain and gave me a single nod—not agreement, never agreement, but acknowledgment. *Do it well,* the nod said. *Whatever you think you're doing, do it well.* It was the closest thing to blessing either of us was going to get.

"Straps," Silas said, and I cinched down over the document tubes until the cabin smelled of oilcloth and wet wool and avgas. He ran the engine up, checked the mags again, and swung the nose into the wind that was coming down-valley ahead of the storm, cool and smelling of iron.

"Coastal strip's four hours if the pass behaves," he said. "Six if it doesn't. Journalist's name is Adeyemi—she'll be waiting under the hangar roof with a recorder running. After that it stops being ours."

"After that it belongs to everyone," I said. "That's the point."

"That's the terrifying part." He glanced at the black wall over the pass, and I watched him do the arithmetic pilots do—cloud base, cell spacing, the distance between courage and stupidity measured in minutes. Then his hand went to his breast pocket, briefly, the way a man touches a talisman, and came away.

"River's east of track for the first thirty miles," I said, unfolding the chart I'd redrawn by hand, because the official one was a lie and the true map lived in my head. "Gorge bends south at the twin falls. If the ceiling drops, follow the water noise. It won't lie to us."

"No," Silas said. "Water never does. People do. That's why we're carrying so much of it." And he pushed the throttle forward.

The Otter rolled down the meadow through standing water, tail light, wheels throwing rooster tails, and lifted off with perhaps fifty feet of grass to spare. Below us the settlement fell away—rooftops smoking, the forge ringing, the river unspooling silver toward a world that had spent seven years pretending none of it existed.

Xanthe stood at the meadow's edge with her hand raised, and she did not wave either. She held her palm open toward us, the way you hold it out to catch rain, or prove something falls.

Then the trees took the valley, and the wall of the monsoon rose ahead of us, black and total, and there was nothing left between the truth and daylight but a tired bush plane, a lying compass needle, and the memory of a river.

Silas banked east, toward the pass, toward the storm's edge, and pushed us into the gray.

* * *

The turbulence found us ten miles out, before the rain did—a long slow roll, like the air itself shrugging. I braced my boots against the rudder pedals and spread my hand-drawn chart across my knees, and outside the windshield the world began to come apart.

The cloud wall wasn't a wall. That was the first thing the charts had never told me. It was a country—towering, internally lit, convecting like something alive, its leading edge frayed into streamers that reached down toward the ridgelines like fingers testing a door. Lightning moved inside it, silent at this distance, a pulse of white in the dark like a heart beating behind smoked glass.

"Pass is at eleven thousand," Silas said. His voice was pilot-flat, all the fear filed off it, which somehow made it worse. "Cloud tops are twenty. We go through the notch or not at all."

"Through the notch," I said. "River first. Bend at the twin falls, then northeast up the tributary. Thirty-one miles of gorge before the saddle."

"Thirty-one miles." He rolled the wings level and flew us at the storm.

Rain hit the windshield like thrown gravel. The wiper beat uselessly against it, a metronome for a song nobody wanted to hear. Visibility collapsed to a half mile, then a quarter, and the river appeared below us as a suggestion—a darker seam in the darkening land, running the way I remembered it running.

"Left bend coming," I said. "Falls in two miles. Listen for them."

And we did listen—engine thunder and rain roar and then, underneath, faint through the airframe, the deep continuous note of water going over stone, and Silas banked toward the sound like a man following a voice through a burning house.

Behind us, far downstream, I imagined the old survey camp, the contractors, Hargrove with his writs and his wet gunpowder, stranded by a fire a bush pilot had set himself. Ahead of us: the notch, the coast, a woman with a recorder, and two hundred and eleven names riding in oilcloth over my shoulder.

The lightning came closer. The instruments lied. The river sang.

"Hold on, Xanthe," Silas said—to the compass, to his sister, to the whole erased valley falling away behind us into the rain. "We're coming through."
