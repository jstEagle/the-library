## 18. Through the Storm's Edge

The monsoon did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision.

Silas saw it from the end of the valley strip while they were still lashing the last crate into the cargo bay—a wall of cloud stacked against the northern passes, black at its base and bruise-purple above, so tall it seemed less like a storm front than a country that had decided to move south. Lightning flickered inside it the way thoughts flicker behind a closed door: present, but not yet spoken.

"Forty minutes," Nia said beside him. She had her arm in a sling now, the graze on her shoulder dressed with tape and stubbornness, and she was looking at the same wall he was. "Maybe thirty-five. That leading edge is moving faster than the forecast cells."

"Then we stop talking about it." He cinched the tie-down until the rope bit his palm. "Get in."

The plane was heavier than she'd ever flown—forged survey documents in waterproof tubes, recorded confessions on wire spools, the settlement's registry of wrongful convictions copied out in three careful hands because Xanthe hadn't trusted any single copy to survive. Every pound of it was a name. Silas felt them riding behind him like passengers who couldn't be weighed but could certainly be drowned.

Xanthe stood at the strip's edge, rain just beginning to stipple the dust around her boots. She didn't wave. She held up the brass compass one last time—the needle swinging, settling, swinging—and then pressed it flat against her chest, over her heart, and mouthed something the wind took before it reached him.

He knew what it was anyway. *Fly this.*

He taxied onto the strip with his jaw set hard enough to ache.

* * *

The first ten minutes were almost kind. The river unspooled below them, high and brown with the storm's early runoff, and the light was still ordinary—gray, gusty, the kind of flying Silas had done a thousand times with cargo that mattered far less. Nia rode in the right seat with her good hand braced on the yoke's secondary grip, not steering, just touching it, the way you'd touch a horse's neck to let it know you were there.

"River forks ahead," she said. "Stay left. The right channel narrows under the bluff and there's a rock garden I never mapped because I sprained my ankle the day we were going to fly it."

"Lucky ankle."

"The luckiest of my life." Her voice was steady, but he could hear the counting underneath it. She was doing what cartographers did—measuring the distance between here and the pass, between the pass and the storm, converting geography into time. "We'll make the gorge mouth in twenty-two minutes if the headwind holds at what it is now."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we find out how much airplane this is."

The headwind did not hold.

It came in the way the interior always delivered its bad news—not gradually but all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath and got tired. The plane bucked, dropped fifty feet in a single sickening exhale, and Silas shoved the nose down to keep the airspeed alive while the river swung up toward them like something thrown. Rain hit the windshield sideways, fat drops that exploded rather than ran, and for three seconds the world outside was nothing but water and noise.

"Instruments," Nia said, calm as a woman reading a grocery list.

"I see them."

"They're lying."

They were. The altimeter read eight hundred feet when his eyes told him four hundred; the compass card—the old magnetic one, not Xanthe's brass ghost riding in his breast pocket—was wandering in slow, drunken circles, dragged by iron in the hills or by the storm's electrical temper or by simple malice. The artificial horizon had given up pretending and sat frozen at an angle no horizon in nature had ever held.

"Dead reckoning, then," Silas said. "You're the map."

"I'm the map." Nia leaned forward against her sling, wincing, and put her face to the side window. Below them the river flashed through gaps in the rain—brown ribbon, green bank, brown ribbon. "There—that bend with the gravel bar shaped like a hook. That's mile forty-one. Stay on the river. When we lose sight of it, hold your heading and count seconds. I'll tell you when to turn."

"You realize," Silas shouted over the engine, "that every pilot who ever died in this range died exactly like this. Confidently. With a navigator telling him to count seconds."

"Yes," Nia said. "But they didn't have me."

He laughed—one short, involuntary bark—and the laugh steadied his hands better than any checklist ever had. He brought the wings level. He flew the river.

* * *

The engine coughed at mile sixty-three.

Not died. Coughed—a hiccup of lost power, a stutter in the smooth roar that Silas felt in his teeth before he heard it in his ears. Then again. Then a third time, longer, the propeller sagging audibly, the whole airframe shuddering like a man stumbling on stairs.

"Fuel contamination?" Nia asked.

"Water, maybe. Or the carburetor icing in this humidity." He worked the mixture, the heat, the throttle, hands moving through the emergency flow without consulting his brain. "Or it's just tired. Everything tired today."

The engine caught clean, roared, and then coughed again—softer this time, more like a warning than a failure. Silas did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the strip: full tanks at takeoff, heavy load, headwind stronger than planned, detour wide around the worst cell. The numbers came back ugly. Not impossible. Ugly.

"How far to the pass?"

"Eleven miles. Nine if we cut the dogleg along the ridge line." Nia was quiet a moment. "Cutting the dogleg puts us closer to the edge of that wall."

"It puts us closer to everything. That's what close means." He rolled into the turn. "Tell me what the ridge does."

"Rises to our left for six miles, then a saddle, then the final climb to the pass itself. There's a notch—" She stopped. He watched her go still in a way that frightened him more than the engine did.

"Nia. What's in the notch."

"A downdraft. A big one. I mapped the wind scars on the trees there two years ago—every trunk bent downslope, some snapped. Air comes off that plateau like water off a weir." She turned and looked at him, rain-light sliding across her face. "If we're low when we cross it, it will push us down faster than we can pull up."

"So we cross it high."

"We cross it high, we're deeper into the storm's edge. We cross it low, we might not clear the saddle." She exhaled slowly. "There is no good option, Silas. There's only the one where we're still alive to complain about it."

"That's my favorite kind." He throttled up. The engine answered with a sound like a cough swallowed halfway. "Climb with me. Call the terrain."

"Climbing. Ridge coming up on the left. There—see the saddle? Dead ahead, past the notch."

He saw it. And beyond it, filling half the sky, he saw the rest: the monsoon's leading edge, no longer a wall but a ceiling and a floor at once, cloud boiling down the far slopes like floodwater over a dam. Lightning walked inside it in long horizontal strokes, silent at this distance, patient. Somewhere past that curtain was the coastal airstrip, the journalist, the reckoning. Somewhere behind them was Xanthe, getting smaller every minute.

Between was eleven miles of sky trying to kill them.

"Silas." Nia's voice changed. "Look at the notch."

The downdraft was visible. That was the obscene thing about it—you could actually see it, a sheet of rain and dust pouring off the plateau into the notch like a waterfall made of air, bending the treetops flat in a swath two hundred yards wide. It pulsed. It breathed. And it lay directly across their path like a hand held out flat, palm down, saying *no.*

"Through it or around it?" he asked, though he already knew there was no around.

"Through. High side. Now, while it's breathing out."

He pushed the nose up. The engine screamed its half-coughing scream. The airspeed bled. The stall horn began its thin, idiot whine, and Silas held the nose a breath higher than the horn said he should, because the horn measured the air that was and the waterfall measured the air that was coming.

They crossed the notch at the top of its exhale.

For one second it was fine. For the next second it was not. The downdraft caught them like a fist closing—eight hundred feet a minute, then fifteen hundred, the altimeter unwinding, the treetops on the saddle below swelling up with terrible interest—and Silas did the only thing there was to do, which was nothing graceful: full power, nose down to keep flying, trading altitude for control, letting the mountain have the height back because height was useless if the wings weren't biting air.

The plane dropped through the shear layer and came out the other side of it in rain so hard it sounded like gravel on the fuselage. The saddle slid beneath them. Clearance: he'd never know. Fifty feet. Thirty. The firs whipped in their own wind-bent grammar below, and then the ground fell away as the terrain tipped downhill toward the coast, and they were through—not past the storm, but through the gate of it, inside the antechamber where the monsoon kept its weather like a landlord keeping rent.

"Engine's still coughing," Nia reported. "Worse."

"I know."

"Coastal strip is forty miles. You won't make forty miles on an engine that sounds like that."

"Then we'll make thirty-nine and walk." But his hands were damp inside his gloves, and the brass compass in his breast pocket felt suddenly very small and very cold against his chest. *You always flew true.* Well. The needle was pointing somewhere. He intended to arrive.

* * *

The last twenty miles happened in pieces.

A gap in the cloud, and the coast appearing below—gray sea, gray foam, a fishing village with smoke rising straight up from its chimneys because down here, impossibly, the storm hadn't started yet. They were flying ahead of their own disaster, carrying it with them like a shadow.

The engine quit entirely for nine seconds over the estuary. Silas would remember those nine seconds for the rest of his life: the sudden silence so complete he could hear Nia counting under her breath, the propeller windmilling, the water coming up, the mudflats arranging themselves below with the terrible hospitality of a place that would accept them either way. He pumped the primer. He cursed the carburetor by its full name. And on the eighth second the engine caught with a bang that shook the whole airframe, surged, smoothed, and held.

"There," Nia said, pointing. "Strip. Right of the cannery. See the windsock?"

He saw it—horizontal, rigid, streaming straight out in a crosswind that wanted to eat them. The strip was short, wet, and had a drainage ditch at the far end that might as well have been a canyon.

"Flaps half. I'll put it down on the numbers and we'll use every foot."

"Every foot," she agreed, and put her good hand on the yoke with him—not taking it, sharing it, the way they'd learned to share everything else: risk, silence, blame, hope.

The approach was the ugliest of his career and the best of it. Crosswind shoving the tail, rain strobing the windshield, the engine surging and fading like a heart arguing with itself. He flared too high because the ground rushed wrong, bounced once—hard, the gear slamming, crates shifting behind them with a groan of rope—and then the wheels were down and staying down, and he stood on the brakes while the ditch grew in the windshield with the patience of geology.

They stopped with the propeller blade hanging over the ditch like a finger over a trigger.

Silence. Real silence, except the rain starting to fall properly now, big drops ticking on the aluminum roof of the world. Silas sat with both hands welded to the yoke and discovered he was shaking—not his hands only; his arms, his shoulders, his breath. Seven years of flying and his body had finally found something to be afraid of after the fact.

Nia started laughing. Low at first, then helplessly, wincing around her sling, laughing the way people laugh at funerals when survival catches them off guard.

"We made it," she said. "You horrible, magnificent bush pilot. We actually made it."

"Don't celebrate yet." He nodded through the rain-blurred glass. "Company."

At the strip's edge, under the cannery's corrugated lean-to, a figure stood beside a motorcycle with a camera bag slung across her chest and a press card laminated to a lanyard that swung as she stepped out into the rain. She'd been waiting a long time—you could tell by the way she'd worn a track in the mud. She raised one hand, not waving exactly. Signaling. *I'm here. I've been here. Give it to me.*

"That's Marta Vey," Nia said quietly. "The journalist. Xanthe's contact on the coast." She looked at the cargo bay, at the tubes and spools and hand-copied registries that held the erased names of several hundred human beings. "Everything's in there. Every fraud, every confession, every conviction that never should have happened."

"I know what's in there." Silas unbuckled himself with fingers that still wouldn't quite obey. Through the windshield, the journalist was walking toward them through the rain, and behind her, far inland, the monsoon wall finally swallowed the pass they'd come through—closing it, sealing the valley, cutting Xanthe and everyone she'd built away from the world until the storm spent itself.

He thought of her standing in the rain at the strip's edge, pressing the compass to her heart. He thought of Hargrove suspended, hearings pending, officials already circling with their questions about coordinates. He thought of the registry, and the names, and what would happen in the next seventy-two hours once Marta Vey started reading aloud.

"Well," Silas said, and pushed the door open into the rain. "Let's give the world something to answer for."

Marta Vey reached the wing and shouted over the downpour, already pulling out her recorder: "You'd better come with me. Because half an hour ago, before you even landed—someone in the capital filed an injunction claiming your evidence was stolen government property. They're calling it theft, Mr. Pemberton. They're calling both of you thieves."

Nia met his eyes across the cockpit, rain streaming past her, and smiled the smile of a woman who had already doctored one map and was fully prepared to doctor another.

"Then they'd better prove it," she said.
