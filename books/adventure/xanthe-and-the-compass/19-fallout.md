## 19. Fallout

The story broke on a Tuesday, the way ruinous things often do—on an ordinary day, between the shipping reports and a feature about drought-resistant cassava.

Silas heard it first in the pilot's lounge at Meridian Field, where the radio was tuned to the coastal service and nobody had bothered to change it. He was halfway through a cup of coffee that tasted like scorched aluminum when the announcer's voice shifted register, the way voices do when they stop reading and start *reporting*.

"—documents obtained by this station appear to show that a government survey conducted in the upper Verrone River basin was falsified at source. Geological assessments dated months before any team entered the valley. Mineral claims staked before the survey party's plane ever left the ground—"

Silas set down his cup. His hands were steady now. They had been steady since the storm, since the hard landing at Corvane Strip with the engine coughing its last and Nia laughing—a wild, cracked sound—in the seat beside him. Steadiness, he'd learned, was not the absence of fear. It was fear that had finally been given something to do.

On the radio, a woman's voice he didn't recognize began reading names. Not coordinates. Names. *Marta Ilves, convicted of sedition, 9th year of sentence. Tomas Reyes-Oduya, convicted of fraud—charges later shown to rest on testimony purchased by the magistrate who brought them.* Name after name after name, read slowly, without commentary, the way you'd read a casualty list. The registry Xanthe's people had kept for seven years in ink that didn't fade, on paper sealed against the damp, hidden under floorboards and inside hollowed survey cases—the whole litany of the erased, spoken aloud at last into the public air.

Someone in the lounge turned the volume up. Nobody spoke. A mechanic took off his cap.

Silas looked out the window at his own plane on the apron, tail number 447-Kilo, the one Hargrove had logged in a neat bureaucratic hand back when logging it had meant something menacing. The fuselage still carried a scar of tape over the starboard inspection panel where the storm had tried to peel it open. He thought: *that little airplane just changed the shape of the country,* and then he thought: *no. The people in the valley did. I only drove the truck.*

But even trucks matter, when everything depends on what they carry.

His coffee went cold. He drank it anyway, all of it, like a man taking communion.

* * *

Nia watched the hearings from the third row of the provincial assembly chamber, close enough to see the sweat on the chairman's temples and far enough back to study the room the way she studied terrain—from elevation, looking for the lines water would follow.

She had always believed maps were honest. That had been her religion: the contour line does not lie, the benchmark does not flatter, the datum is indifferent to politics. She had built seven years of professional life on that faith, and she had learned, in a hidden valley full of people the law had made invisible, that the map was never the lie. The lie lived in the hands that commissioned it, in the margins, in what was asked to be left off.

Now the lie was on screens at the front of the chamber, projected large. Her own field data—her *own*, pulled from the survey archive—displayed beside the "final" geological report. The two documents disagreed about everything: soil composition, slope stability, mineral presence. One of them was hers. One of them had been written before her team ever flew upstream, by someone in an office four hundred miles from any river, and signed with the seal of the Department of Lands.

"Cartographer Okafor." The chairman's voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he himself had certified. "You were the senior mapping authority on the Verrone expedition. Can you confirm these are your original field observations?"

"They are."

"And they contradict the department's published findings."

"They don't contradict them." Nia kept her voice level. "They predate them. The published report was composed using my data and then altered. Whoever altered it wanted the valley to look like waste ground—unstable, valueless to anyone but a mining consortium, ripe for clearance. My data showed a living watershed. Someone decided a living watershed was inconvenient."

A murmur moved through the chamber like wind through cane.

In the second row sat Hargrove—not at the witness table, not anymore. He'd been suspended eleven days ago, pending inquiry, and he attended the hearings anyway, every session, sitting perfectly straight in a gray suit with his hands folded, watching the machinery grind with the expression of a man auditing his own autopsy. When the suspension was announced he had not protested. That frightened Nia more than rage would have. Rage meant a man thought he could still win. Hargrove simply watched, and took notes, and once—only once—caught her eye across the aisle and inclined his head slightly, as if confirming an appointment.

At the end of the third day, the chairman leaned toward his microphone and said the words Nia had been waiting for and dreading in equal measure:

"The committee will require accurate geographic documentation of the settlement in question. For the purposes of verification, service provision, and—" a pause, a shuffling of papers, "—future administration of the territory. Cartographer Okafor, as holder of the only complete survey, you will be asked to provide the valley's coordinates under subpoena if necessary."

There it was. The hook inside the gift.

Every eye in the chamber came around to her. Hargrove did not turn. He only smiled, faintly, at his folded hands—as though the whole hearing had been arranged for his benefit, and the trap he'd baited downstream in the bush had simply been re-baited here, in marble and fluorescent light, with legal paper instead of contractors.

Nia looked at the blank space on the wall where a map would go, and said nothing at all.

* * *

The rain reached the valley three days late, which the old settlers said was the mountain's way of giving them time to finish arguing.

Xanthe stood under the eave of the council house and watched it come up the basin—not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the pass, erasing the world above the tree line inch by inch. Behind her, inside, the argument continued without her, because it always did now. Recognition had split her people the way lightning splits a tree: along the grain, but violently.

"You gave them our names," Dara Ilves had said, the night the radio news arrived, her voice shaking with something that wasn't quite anger. "Seven years we taught our children to be unfindable. You put us *in the air*."

"I put us in the record," Xanthe had answered. "There's a difference. Erasure was never safety. It was just a slower kind of disappearance—we were already gone; the writs were only going to make it official."

"Some of us liked being ghosts."

"Ghosts don't get schools," Xanthe said. "Ghosts don't get medicine. Ghosts get bulldozers, eventually, and no one asks where they went."

That had been three weeks ago. Since then: the provisional negotiators' letters, arriving by the same supply flights Silas now flew openly twice a week, tail number known to every child in the settlement, who ran to the strip when they heard the engine the way children elsewhere ran to the ice-cream cart. Since then: hearings, suspensions, a mining consortium's lawyers suddenly very visible and very quiet. And since then, this new letter, which Xanthe held now in her hand while the rain came down and the council argued behind her.

*The committee requires geographic documentation of the settlement for purposes of verification and administration.*

Administration. The word sat in her mouth like a stone. She knew its history. Administration was how it had started for every one of the hundred and forty families in the registry—some clerk deciding where they belonged, some line drawn around their lives by someone who would never smell their woodsmoke.

But there was another word in the letter, smaller, easier to skip past: *provisional*. Self-governing commons, provisional status, subject to review. A door left open rather than shut. And doors, Xanthe had learned, swing both ways—but they also let in light.

Her brother's voice came from behind her. "You're letting the meeting argue without you. That's either wisdom or surrender."

Silas stood at the edge of the eave, rain-jacketed, fresh off the last flight, holding two tin cups of coffee against his chest like offerings. He'd taken to appearing at her elbow at odd hours with coffee, saying nothing much, repairing things neither of them named—one shared cup at a time. Seven years of silence didn't mend in a season. But mortar, she was learning, didn't need to be dramatic. It just needed to keep being applied.

"Wisdom," she said. "They have to hear themselves say it out loud. If I'm in the room, they perform for me." She handed him the letter.

He read it standing close, shoulder almost touching hers, sheltered from the rain. When he finished, he was quiet a moment. "They want coordinates."

"They want coordinates."

"And Nia has the only real map."

"Nia has the only real map," Xanthe agreed. "Which means the next move belongs to a cartographer." She looked out at the rain hammering the landing strip, turning the red clay to the color of old blood, and felt the strange vertigo of a leader whose people had finally stopped hiding—and discovered that visibility was just another kind of exposure, with better lighting. "God help us all."

Silas huffed something that was almost a laugh. "She'll draw them something," he said. "Question is what."

* * *

What she would draw them—that question followed Nia home from the chamber, through the wet streets, up the stairs of her rented room, and sat down across from her at the desk like a guest who refused to leave.

On the desk lay two sheets of drafting film. On the left, the true map: the valley as she had walked it, flown it, loved it in the fierce impersonal way cartographers love places—every bend of the river, every terrace of the settlement, the council house, the school, the long fields, the spring at the source. The most complete picture of those hundred and forty lives that had ever existed. It had cost her team's captivity, her ankle, her commission, and Reyes's friendship, and it was beautiful, and it was dangerous the way a loaded thing is dangerous.

On the right, a blank sheet, waiting.

Because here was the arithmetic no one else in the chamber seemed able to do. If she surrendered the true coordinates, the valley became administrable—which meant fundable, servable, protectable, and also taxable, regulable, visitable, and, the moment any future government's mood soured, evictable. A line on an official map was a handle, and handles existed to be pulled. She had seen what happened to communities with handles.

If she refused the subpoena, she went to jail—contempt, obstruction, whatever charge fit best—and the map passed to someone else anyway, someone with less love and more compliance in them. Refusal bought nothing but her own ruin.

And if she lied—

Lying on an official submission. Falsifying federal geographic data. The offense that had begun this entire catastrophe when someone else committed it in reverse.

Nia picked up her pen. Set it down. Picked it up again.

She thought about the snare they'd found on the riverbank, set by someone skilled, sprung on nothing—a message in the grammar of survivors: *we see you; we choose what you may take.* She thought about Xanthe's face in the firelight saying *visibility beats erasure*, betting her people's future on the open door. She thought about Hargrove's small smile across the aisle, patient as erosion, certain that whatever she chose, he had already mapped it.

Outside, rain began against the window, soft and endless, the coastal rain that erased everything eventually.

Nia drew a single line. Then another. Contours rose under her pen—familiar, confident, wrong. A valley took shape on the film, thirty miles northeast of the truth, plausible in every particular, anchored to real tributaries, dressed with authentic elevations copied from genuine ridges. It was the finest forgery of her career, and she made it with a craftsman's care and a traitor's calm, and somewhere in the middle of it she realized she was crying, quietly, without drama, the way rain falls—because this was the moment her religion finally died and was replaced by something harder and older: the understanding that a map is never neutral, that every line serves somebody, and that the only question a cartographer ever really answers is *whose*.

She finished near dawn. She signed it. She sealed it in the official envelope.

Then she wrote a second letter, short, unsigned except with a single letter—*N*—and addressed it to a settlement that officially did not exist, care of a bush pilot whose tail number everyone knew.

*They will ask me to lead a verification expedition,* she wrote. *When they do, I will resign before I fly them there. Be ready to be found by accident instead. Accidents are harder to prosecute.*

She weighed the envelope in her hand. Victory, she thought, was supposed to feel lighter than this.

Downstairs, the radio in the café was playing the morning news: hearings resuming Monday, the Minister of Lands resigning, the consortium's shares in freefall—and, buried at the end, one line delivered in a bright, unconcerned voice: *the Department has announced it intends to dispatch its own survey team to confirm the settlement's location within the month.*

Nia stood very still in the stairwell, listening to the rain.

Thirty miles, she thought. Thirty miles of wrong map between the government and a hundred and forty families. It would hold—unless someone walked the ground. Unless someone checked.

She put on her coat, and went to find Silas.
