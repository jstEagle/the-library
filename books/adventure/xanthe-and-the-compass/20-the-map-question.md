## 20. The Map Question

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and old paper, and Nia Okafor thought: *this is the smell of a country deciding what it wants to forget.*

She sat at the long witness table with her hands flat on the wood, the way she'd been taught in her first year of survey school—hands visible, posture open, a body that had nothing to hide. Eleven years she'd practiced that posture. Eleven years of being the woman whose maps were trusted, whose instruments were calibrated, whose word was as good as a stamped seal. She had built her whole life into that trust, plank by plank, like a bridge over water too deep to swim.

And now the bridge was the thing on fire.

"Cartographer Okafor." The chairman's voice came through the room's poor acoustics slightly flattened, as if even his authority couldn't quite reach the corners. "You understand the terms of your testimony?"

"I do."

"You've been offered full legal immunity for anything disclosed under this session, provided your disclosures are complete and accurate. The commission requires—requires—the true coordinates of the settlement identified in the fraudulent survey materials."

*Complete and accurate.* The words hung there like weather.

Nia looked down at her own hands. There was ink still caught in the creases of her knuckles from the night before, when she'd sat in her rented room until three in the morning with a straightedge and a bottle of India ink, redrawing a valley.

* * *

She had started with the river.

That was the cartographer's instinct, older than conscience: begin with the water, because water doesn't lie. She drew the gorge exactly as she'd flown it, exactly as Silas had threaded it with his engine coughing and the monsoon wall breathing black behind them. She drew the bends, the gravel bars, the confluence where the tributary came in cold and green from the north. Every line true. That was the discipline of it—that was what made the lie work. A forged map is not a map of lies; it is a map of truths arranged around one absence.

Then she came to the basin.

On her original field chart—the one locked now in the commission's evidence vault, the one Reyes had helped produce before his conscience or his ambition got the better of him—the settlement sat in the source basin like a heart inside a ribcage of ridgelines. Terraces. Smokehouses. The longhouse roofs she'd seen from the ridge, dark cedar silvered by weather, laid out along the creek with the patience of people who intended to stay for generations.

In the new chart, she moved it thirty miles southwest, into a fold of country she knew was wrong—a high, wet plateau of sinkholes and dead spruce, country so miserable that even the government's contractors would take one look at the ground truth and turn back. But they wouldn't turn back immediately. They would fly there first. They would land there, maybe. They would spend days, weeks, slogging through muskeg with their eviction writs and their resource assessments, mapping a valley where no one had ever lived because no one *could* live there—and every day they spent in the sinkholes was a day the real valley had to grow its crops, mend its roofs, teach its children, become a little more permanent, a little more undeniable.

Thirty miles. On a map at survey scale, thirty miles was nothing—a flick of the pen, a rounding error. On the ground, thirty miles of trackless country in monsoon season was a season of someone's life.

She had sat with the pen over that empty space for a long time. Ink is a strange substance, she remembered thinking. It dries fast and stays forever, and there is no instrument precise enough to measure the weight of a single line.

Then she drew the settlement where it didn't belong, and labeled it in her own careful hand, and signed the margin the way she'd signed eleven years of honest charts.

Her hand hadn't shaken. That was the part that frightened her most, afterward. Not guilt—certainty. The needle of her had swung and settled, and it pointed somewhere the law could not follow.

* * *

"Cartographer Okafor?" The chairman again, patient in the way of men who have never once been interrupted. "You were elsewhere."

"I was reviewing my testimony," Nia said. "I'm ready."

They brought out the charts then, unrolling them on easels like trophies: the fraudulent geological survey with its invented mineral assays, the eviction writ with its annexed reconnaissance map, and beneath those, weighted at the corners with brass rods, her own field chart of the interior—the real one, drawn in her own hand, the one that had led her team upriver and into everything.

A senator from the coastal districts leaned forward. "This chart places the settlement—" he checked a note, though he plainly knew it by heart, "—in the upper source basin. Is that correct?"

"It is what I recorded," Nia said.

"And you are prepared to confirm, under immunity, the present location of the settlement for the purposes of this commission's site verification?"

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone already knows what answer they want. She could feel them leaning—not visibly, but the way a river leans toward a cut bank. Somewhere behind her, Hargrove's lawyers sat in a row like crows on a fence. Hargrove himself wasn't there; suspended officials sent proxies, and his proxy was a small neat man who took notes he never looked at.

Nia thought about the settlement. Not abstractly—specifically. Mama Ede's hands dusted with cass flour. The schoolroom with its slates made from split shingles. Xanthe standing at the council fire saying *visibility beats erasure*, betting everything on the idea that a community could stand in daylight and survive it. The children who had never been registered anywhere, who existed in no ledger in the world except the one the settlement kept itself, in its own handwriting, because no one else had ever bothered to write them down.

And she thought about Reyes, testifying yesterday. She'd watched him on the feed in her rented room, watched him give his account with wet eyes and a steady voice—the detention, the moral deadlock, the way he'd finally chosen the truth about the fraud while carefully declining to say where the truth lived. He'd walked the same knife edge she was walking now. He'd kept the coordinates out of his mouth by keeping his testimony narrow, and the commission had let him, because his testimony served them against Hargrove and they weren't greedy. Yet.

But Nia was the cartographer. Her whole testimony *was* the coordinates. They hadn't called her to ask whether the survey was fraudulent—they had Reyes for that, and documents, and Silas's burned fuel caches and the contractor manifests. They called her because she was the only living person who could put a pin in the wilderness, and pins were how governments turned places into things.

"Cartographer Okafor," the chairman said, "the question was—"

"I heard the question." She lifted her head. "Before I answer it, I want to enter something into the record."

"You may make a statement."

"Not a statement. A document." She nodded to the clerk. "I submitted an amended master chart to the commission secretary last night, certified under my professional seal. It supersedes the field chart in your possession. The field chart contains errors—I located and corrected them after further review."

The small neat man with Hargrove's notes stopped writing. Everyone stopped writing.

The chairman frowned. "An amended chart. Amended how?"

"In positions," Nia said. "The river system stands as surveyed. The elevations stand. The fraud documentation stands—it's all in the margins, cross-referenced to the exhibits. But the settlement position was plotted from a single aerial observation under storm conditions, and I have since corrected it against ground triangulation." She paused, and gave them the gift of her calm, because calm was the only weapon left that worked in both directions. "The amended chart is accurate. I'll certify that under oath, under penalty, under whatever you care to attach to it. It is the best map I can make."

It was even true, if you held the words up to the light just so. It was the best map she could make—for the people who needed the map to be wrong.

* * *

They broke for recess, which meant they spent ninety minutes discovering that the amended chart placed the settlement on a plateau of sinkholes, and arguing about it, and sending runners to pull old geological folios of the region, and discovering that the folios agreed with her—which they would, because she'd drawn the amendment to agree with them. Cartography's oldest trick: never contradict the archive. Let the archive do your lying for you.

When they reconvened, the questions came gentler and sharper at once, the way questions do when the questioner suspects the ground has shifted.

"Ms. Okafor." This was a different voice, a woman from the interior districts, one of the few on the commission who had ever actually been past the last telephone wire. "Your original field chart. The one in our vault. You're telling this commission that you, a commissioned surveyor with eleven years' service, produced a chart with a thirty-mile positional error on its primary subject?"

"Yes."

"That seems unlikely."

"It is unlikely," Nia agreed. "Storm conditions, instrument drift, a compromised reference baseline when the survey team's equipment was abandoned. I've documented all of it in the amendment notes. Errors compound. That's why we amend."

"And your team's disappearance? Your colleagues' detention by the settlers? Does the amended chart also explain why the settlement's people detained government surveyors for six weeks?"

"No," Nia said. "But I can tell you why they did." She let the silence stretch until the chairman gestured impatiently. "Because my team found them. Because we arrived with instruments that could erase them—turn a living valley into a coordinate, a coordinate into a writ, a writ into trucks at dawn. They'd survived raids before. Some of them had been convicted of crimes that were later thrown out, and the convictions stayed on their records anyway, and the records followed them like weather. When you have been erased by paperwork, paperwork looks like a weapon. My team was detained because my team was carrying a weapon and didn't know it."

"And you sympathize with that."

"I understand it," Nia said. "Sympathy is optional. Understanding is in the data."

The interior senator almost smiled. Almost.

Then the chairman leaned forward, and Nia saw the trap arrive on schedule, the way weather arrives—you watch it build all day and it still gets you. "Ms. Okafor, the commission accepts your amended chart provisionally. Site verification teams will deploy when the passes reopen. In the meantime, the commission requires your continued cooperation as a serving officer of the Survey Corps. Your commission status gives your certification legal force. You understand that if you remain a serving officer, you are bound to report any further corrections, observations, or discoveries relevant to the settlement's location. Including any contact with its residents."

There it was. Not immunity—a leash. Stay ours, and your signature keeps working for us. Report your friends. Certify their erasure with your honest hands.

She thought of Silas, who had signed a contract he didn't trust because debt doesn't care about instincts, and who had spent his own livelihood in flames to buy strangers a season. He'd told her once, on the river, teaching her to read clouds the way she taught him to read water: *every choice costs, kid. The trick is choosing what you can afford to lose.* She'd laughed and called him an old fatalist. She wasn't laughing now. He'd been drawing her a map the whole time.

"Chairman," Nia said, "may I have a moment to consult my counsel?"

"You have no counsel of record."

"Then a moment to myself."

* * *

She asked for the room's antechamber, a narrow space with a tall window looking out on the capital's administrative quarter: gray stone, gray sky, flags going limp in the rain. Somewhere beyond that skyline, thirty miles of sinkhole country stood between the commission's verification teams and a valley full of people planting a second crop.

She took out her commission card—laminated, hard-edged, eleven years of it. It had gotten her onto flights, into archives, across borders that stopped other people. It had gotten her team upriver. It had weight in the hand, more weight than plastic should carry, because institutions are heavy and everything they touch absorbs some of the mass.

Justice outside the law, she thought. That was the phrase Xanthe had used at the council fire, and Nia had bristled at it then—she was a professional, professionals don't traffic in outside-the-law anything. But sitting here, holding the card, she understood at last that the phrase wasn't lawless. It was arithmetic. The law had produced Hargrove and the writs and the fraudulent surveys, all duly sealed and stamped, every document perfect. The law's instruments had done exactly what they were designed to do. If she wanted a different outcome, she could not use the law's instruments. She had to set them down.

Setting them down cost. That was the part the settlement understood and the capital did not. The settlers had paid in records, in names, in the right to exist on paper. She would pay in smaller coin, but she would pay.

She went back in.

"Chairman. Senators." She laid the commission card on the table, face up, square to the table's edge, the way she squared a chart. "I resign my commission, effective immediately. I'll complete today's testimony as a private citizen. My certifications as a serving officer end with my service. The amended chart stands on its own merits—or it doesn't. I won't be bound to future reports, future corrections, or future cooperation. And I won't be making any."

The room made a sound—not gasp, nothing so dramatic; a rustle, the sound of eleven careers recalculating at once.

"Ms. Okafor," the chairman said slowly, "you understand that resignation during an active inquiry may expose you to charges of obstruction, and forfeits the immunity protections extended to serving officers."

"I understand."

"You understand that without your commission, your amended chart carries no official certification. Any verification team will treat it as the work of a civilian with a demonstrated conflict of interest."

"I understand that too." She folded her hands again, hands visible, nothing to hide—except the one thing, the thirty miles of it, sitting quietly in the vault under brass rods. "But you'll notice, Chairman, that the geological folios agree with my amendment. Check them. I did."

The interior senator was watching her with an expression Nia recognized from mirrors: the look of a person watching someone choose the expensive option and wanting, helplessly, to salute.

"Your resignation is noted," the chairman said at last, heavily. "For the record: Ms. Okafor, do you affirm that the amended chart represents your best professional judgment of the settlement's location?"

Nia Okafor, private citizen, looked at the commission that had offered her immunity in exchange for a valley, and told the truth the only way left to her.

"I affirm it is the best map I can make."

* * *

They let her out into the rain an hour before dusk.

The steps of the commission building were slick and shining, and the city smelled of wet stone and diesel, and Nia stood under the portico for a moment without opening her umbrella, letting the sound of it fill her ears—rain on a capital, rain on a valley, the same water falling everywhere, indifferent to jurisdiction.

Her name was waiting for her at the bottom of the steps. Not shouted—spoken, low, from under a dripping awning where a figure stood with a courier's canvas bag and a pilot's rolling gait, hat pulled down, collar up, doing a poor job of being inconspicuous in a city he clearly despised.

Silas Pemberton pushed off the wall and came up the steps two at a time, rain streaming off his hat brim.

"They said you resigned," he said. "Half the strip heard it by noon. Radio's faster than government." He searched her face. "You all right?"

"No," Nia said. "I committed professional suicide in front of eleven senators and falsified a federal document, and I feel—" She stopped. Tested the feeling the way she'd test a footing on soft ground. "Light. Which worries me more than the alternative."

Silas huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Xanthe says lightness is what it feels like when you stop carrying their stuff and start carrying yours." He hesitated, and then, gruffly, the way he handed over anything that mattered: "She sent word. Council voted last night. Provisional status talks—they're inviting the negotiators in themselves, on their own terms. Xanthe's betting visibility beats erasure." His jaw worked. "She also said—if you're willing—there's a job down there nobody's qualified for. Mapping a place that's about to exist. Properly. Names, boundaries, burial grounds, all of it. Their own registry, their own hand."

Nia looked out past him at the rain coming down on the gray city, on the vaults and the folios and the verification teams that would spend their season in the sinkholes, and felt the needle settle.

"A map of a country that doesn't exist yet," she said. "That's the only kind worth drawing."

She picked up her bag. Together they went down the steps into the rain, toward the airstrip, toward the river, toward the valley that was thirty miles from where the government would spend all next year looking—

—and neither of them noticed, at the top of the steps, the small neat man with Hargrove's notebook, who had stopped taking notes he never read, and was reading this one.
