## 22. Compass Rose

The monsoon ended the way it always did—not with a last storm but with a slow forgetting, the rain thinning day by day until one morning Silas woke and couldn't remember when the sky had last been heavy. The river dropped back into its channel like a dog returning to a familiar bed. The passes opened. And on the first clear Tuesday of the dry season, Silas Pemberton flew into the valley with his tail number painted fresh on the fuselage and no attempt at all to hide it.

That was the strangest part, still, after eight months. For seven years he had flown like a man moving through a house at night—careful of what he knocked over, careful of who might hear him. Now he filed flight plans that said *Valley Commons* in the destination box, and the clerks down in Meridian typed it without comment, because the paperwork had finally caught up to the truth: there were people upriver, four hundred and eleven of them as of the spring census, and they had a name for their home now, and a provisional charter with a government seal on it that Xanthe had signed with a pen she'd borrowed from a negotiator and never given back.

The strip below him had grown since the rains. What had been a meadow with a windsock was now a graded runway with gravel edges and three fuel drums under a tin shelter, and beside it a long low building going up board by board—a hangar, or a hall, or whatever the valley decided it needed most that week. Children waved from the tree line as he came in low over the water. They always waved. In the early months they had watched his plane the way you watch weather, something powerful and indifferent that might turn on you. Now they watched it the way you watch the mail.

He set down soft, taxied to the drums, and killed the engine. The silence after was the old silence, river-sound and bird-sound and wind in the cut grass, but it sat differently on him now. It wasn't the silence of a place where nobody knew his name.

* * *

The coffee was terrible and they both knew it.

Xanthe made it the same way every visit—in a dented pot over a camp stove behind the half-built hangar, grounds measured by eye, water boiled hard enough to strip paint—and every visit Silas drank two cups of it and complained about neither, because complaining would have meant admitting it mattered, and they were both still learning how to admit things mattered.

"You're late," she said, handing him the tin cup. She'd let her hair go longer than he remembered from before, gray coming in at the temples like frost finding the high ground. There was a scar along her forearm he hadn't asked about yet. He was saving it. Some questions you rationed out across years, like supplies.

"Headwind off the coast," he said. "And I stopped at Meridian for parts."

"Parts." She raised an eyebrow. "Or mail?"

"Both." He fished the bundle from his jacket—letters, a newspaper folded around a bolt of canvas, and a small parcel wrapped in brown paper that he set on the crate between them without ceremony. "From the Okafor woman. She says she's finished the atlas."

Xanthe went still the way she went still about everything important, completely, like a heron. Then she took the parcel and turned it over twice before opening it, which was also Xanthe—approaching gifts sideways, as if they might be traps. Seven years of surviving raids taught a person that. Eight months of peace hadn't fully untaught it yet, and Silas had stopped expecting it to.

Inside was a book. Cloth-bound, hand-lettered on the spine: *The River Country: An Atlas of the Unlisted*, and beneath it, smaller, *N. Okafor, Cartographer*.

"She resigned her commission formally in March," Silas said. "They tried to give her a medal instead. She told them where they could put it, more or less politely."

"And the map?" Xanthe asked, and Silas heard the old wariness underneath—the question that had shadowed everything since the hearings began. Where is the valley. Give us the coordinates. The question Nia had answered with a doctored chart thirty miles wrong and a resignation letter that read like a door closing.

"Published," Silas said. "The real one. Every bend, every rapid, the source basin, all of it. She dedicated it 'to the people of the valley, who were here before the maps and will be here after.'" He turned his cup slowly on the crate. "She said if the government wanted to find you now, they could walk up the river like anyone else. Knock like anyone else."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. Down at the strip, two of the Reyes boys were arguing cheerfully about whose turn it was to pump fuel, their voices carrying in the clean post-monsoon air. Somewhere beyond the tree line, a hammer rang against something—construction, always construction, the valley building itself outward now instead of digging itself deeper in.

"Visibility beats erasure," she said finally. It was her own argument, the one she'd made at the council while half the settlement glared at her, the bet she'd placed with all of them as stakes. "I said that. I keep waiting to find out if I was right."

"You're still here," Silas said. "Everyone's still here. That's more than hiding ever bought you."

"It's not the same as being safe."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But nothing is, and at least this way you get to know your neighbors' names before the trouble comes." He nodded toward the parcel. "There's a letter in there too. From her. Read it later—it's mostly about you."

"About me?"

"About how stubborn you are. Professional assessment." He drank the terrible coffee. "I concurred."

* * *

They walked the perimeter of the strip while the morning warmed, because that was the other ritual now, the one that had crept up on them both without either of them proposing it. He inspected his plane; she inspected everything else. The new fence posts. The drainage ditch the community had dug through August's mud. The tin shelter leaning slightly eastward, which Xanthe swore was intentional and Silas swore was not.

It was strange, repairing a relationship. Nobody warned you it was manual labor. There were no grand reconciliations left in them—that had happened at the ridge, furious and tear-streaked, arrowpoint and accusation, and it had spent whatever drama they had in reserve. What remained was slower work. Learning each other's new shapes. She flinched, still, at the sound of a plane she didn't recognize; he had learned to radio ahead with his tail number so the watchers on the ridge wouldn't loose an arrow at a friend. She had learned that when he went quiet it wasn't anger, it was just Silas, thinking, the way he'd always been. He had learned that when she counted things—people, supplies, exits—it wasn't distrust, it was seven years of arithmetic that kept people alive.

"I got a letter from Reyes," Xanthe said as they reached the south end of the strip. "The surveyor. He's teaching geology at a school in Meridian now. He wrote to apologize."

"For what?"

"For arguing. Back then. For saying we should report the valley." She shrugged. "He said he understood it better now. Said he reads the papers and sees what the ministry tries to do to places it knows about, and he thinks about what would've happened if we'd been found the old way."

"What did you write back?"

"That I argued with him because he was right and I hated it." She smiled faintly. "Honesty costs nothing once you're already visible."

The compass sat on his dashboard where it had sat since the night before the evidence flight, when she'd pressed it into his hands and said *you always flew true, fly this*. He'd meant to return it that first week. Then the second. Then he'd stopped meaning to, and started understanding instead that some objects are not possessions but assignments, and the needle pointing north out of the valley every time he flew out was less navigation than promise: *this way back. Always this way back.*

"How's the knee?" she asked, nodding at his left leg. The old injury ached when the pressure changed; she'd learned his weather the way he'd learned hers.

"Fair. Rain coming Thursday, maybe Friday."

"You always did think you were a barometer."

"I am a barometer. Ask any instrument in my cockpit—they're all liars except the compass."

She laughed, short and real, and for a moment they were just two people standing in the sun at the edge of a runway, and the years between them weighed exactly what they weighed, no more.

* * *

In the afternoon he helped unload, because the supply runs weren't ceremonial—canvas for the hall roof, a crate of textbooks, antibiotics, bolts of cloth, a sewing machine for Marta Voss-adjacent nobody, actually for a woman named Odile who ran the mending circle and had once, Silas had learned, been convicted of theft for taking bread during a famine year and had erased herself rather than serve ten. Four hundred and eleven people, and every one of them had a story like that. He knew perhaps sixty of the stories now. He was in no hurry. Stories kept.

The children helped carry the lighter crates, and one of them—a girl of maybe nine, gap-toothed, named Sefi—walked beside him the whole way asking questions about the engine until Xanthe rescued him with the claim that pilots needed to preflight before dusk flying, which was true, and also an excuse, which everyone knew.

At the plane he ran through the checks with the girl watching solemnly from a respectful distance: fuel, control surfaces, the tie-downs, the compass itself, cradled in its bracket. The needle swung as he turned and settled, settled, pointed north. Steady. It had been steady through the storm's edge, through the hearings, through the long months when the whole country argued about the valley over breakfast tables and radio broadcasts, through Hargrove's suspension and the ministry's reshuffling and the slow grinding machinery of a justice that had arrived late but had, at least, arrived.

Sefi pointed at it. "Doesn't it ever get tired?"

"Tired of what?"

"Pointing. Always the same direction."

Silas considered that, crouched at the open door with the evening light going gold on the water. "I used to think that would be boring," he said. "Always north. Never anywhere else." He tapped the glass gently. "Turns out it's not about the direction. It's about having one."

The girl frowned at this, decided it was adult nonsense, and ran off toward the hall, where someone was calling her name. Silas watched her go and thought: *that's the difference.* A year ago a child running toward a stranger's voice in this valley would have scattered like birds. Now they just ran home.

* * *

Dusk came down soft. He could have flown out—he'd done it before, the evening runs, sleeping at the coastal strip and returning in two days—but Xanthe appeared at the wing with a bedroll under her arm and an expression that dared him to object, and he didn't, because eight months had taught him that these overnight visits were the actual repair work, the slow stitching, and you didn't rush stitching.

They ate at the common fire, rice and river fish and a sauce that made his eyes water, surrounded by people who greeted him by name—Pemberton, flyboy, the barometer—and didn't make a ceremony of it, which was the highest compliment the valley paid anyone. Afterward he and Xanthe sat apart from the fire, out where the dark pooled between the strip and the tree line, sharing the last of the terrible coffee from a thermos she'd produced from nowhere.

"Do you remember," she said, "the compass? When you gave it to me?"

"Of course."

"You said, *it doesn't point home, it points north—but if you keep north on your left shoulder and the river on your right, you'll always come back to me.*" She looked into her cup. "I thought about that every single day for seven years. Left shoulder, right side. Come back to him. Even when I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "Even in the worst of it. Even when I believed you'd sold me out. Some part of me was navigating by you the whole time."

Silas looked at the fire, at the sparks climbing, at the silhouettes of people beyond it who had erased themselves from every registry in the country and were now, slowly, writing themselves back in on their own terms. He thought about the man he'd been a year ago—competent, restless, flying medicine and mail for strangers because flying for anyone he loved hurt too much. He thought about loneliness as a kind of altitude, how easy it was to cruise there, how the air thinned.

"I flew for nobody for seven years," he said. "Turns out that's not freedom. It's just cargo that weighs nothing."

Xanthe leaned her shoulder against his, briefly, the way she did—one second of contact, then gone, but real. "You're getting poetic in your old age."

"Altitude sickness."

"Mm."

They sat until the fire burned low and the stars came out hard and close, the way they only did upriver, and somewhere in the dark a nightjar called its two-note question that nobody answered, and Silas felt the ache in his knee that meant weather, and felt, beneath it, steadier than any needle: *home.* Not the coastal strip where he kept a room. Not the cockpit. This—firelight and fish sauce and a sister who rationed her questions same as he did, four hundred and eleven people who knew his tail number and waved anyway.

* * *

He was up before dawn, as always, doing the walkaround in gray light while mist stood on the river in columns. Xanthe came down to see him off, carrying a parcel wrapped in oilcloth.

"Odile sent it. Bread, and don't eat it all before the coast."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would." She held the parcel a moment longer than necessary, then handed it over. "Next run, bring seeds. Tomatoes, if you can get them. The council wants to try a proper garden this year instead of scavenging greens. We voted on it."

"A garden vote."

"We vote on everything now. It's exhausting. Last week we spent two hours debating whether the hall should face east or south." Her mouth twitched. "Reyes would have loved it."

"He'd have hated it and loved hating it."

"Yes." She stepped back from the wing. "Fly safe, Silas."

"Left shoulder," he said, climbing in. "Right side."

She stood on the strip as he ran up the engine, arms crossed against the morning chill, and he lifted off into air so clean it felt newly made, the valley falling away below him—the graded strip, the half-built hall, the smoke of the banked fire, the river unspooling silver toward a country that finally knew it was there.

On the dashboard the compass held its heading, needle steady, patient, certain. North. Always north. And Silas Pemberton, bush pilot, brother, barometer, flew out over the pass with a hold full of empty crates waiting to be filled and a heart full of cargo that weighed nothing at all—

—and then the radio crackled, and a voice he didn't know broke through the dawn static, urgent, clipped, calling his tail number twice, saying there was a message from Meridian, saying it concerned the charter, saying he should land at the coastal strip as soon as he was able.

Silas looked at the compass. The needle didn't waver.

He banked north, toward the coast, toward whatever was coming, and keyed the mic. "Pemberton receiving. Say again—all of it."

Below him the river ran on, and the morning opened like a page.
