## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Marrow woke before I did, the way she always did—hull ticking against the pilings as the current leaned on her, a sound like knuckles on a door. I lay in my bunk under the wheelhouse and listened to the river tell me what kind of day it intended to be. Fast water meant wind upstream somewhere. A hollow knock against the stern meant driftwood coming down from the high country. This morning there was only the low, even press of the current and the gulls arguing over the fish cannery scrap two piers down, which meant the Kestrel was in one of its reasonable moods.

I got up, put the kettle on, and went out onto the aft deck in my socks to look at the sky.

Weather on the Kestrel isn't forecast so much as negotiated. The mountains to the west hold their clouds like cards they haven't decided to play, and by the time you see what they're holding, it's already in your hand. But this morning the light came off the water flat and silver, no green in it, no bruise color over the ridgeline, and the smell off the glacier reach was clean—the cold mineral smell of snowmelt instead of the sourness that comes before a blow. Good enough. Good enough was all the river ever promised anybody.

I drank my coffee standing at the rail and counted the boats at the landing. Twelve slips, nine occupied, and I could have named every hull without looking: the *Petrel*, old man Quist's gillnetter with the patched portside; the fuel barge; the two crab boats that hadn't moved since the season closed because their owner hadn't either. And the Marrow, eighty feet of riveted steel and stubbornness, riding low on empty holds, waiting for me to decide what she'd carry today.

That's the arrangement she and I have. She doesn't ask where we're going. Neither do most people, if I do my job right.

* * *

The manifest came by radio at seven, same as it did three mornings out of five. Freight for the upper landing at Coldbank: diesel drums, a pallet of fence wire, seed potatoes in burlap sacks that breathed like something alive, and a crate marked FRAGILE that I'd learned not to ask about, because the answer was always survey equipment or somebody's idea of a piano. Forty tons, give or take. Six hours upriver through the lower channel, past the Narrows, into the glacial reach where the water ran the color of milk glass and ate propellers for breakfast if you let it tell you who was captain.

I took on fuel first, then freight, working the deck crane alone the way I always did. There's a rhythm to loading a boat by yourself that outsiders mistake for loneliness. It isn't. It's arithmetic. Every drum has a place, every strap a tension, and when the weight sits right the whole boat hums a note you can feel through your boots. I've heard men with crews of six never find that note. They load heavy in the bow and wonder why the Marrow-class hulls porpoise in a following sea. I load stern-heavy by two inches and she runs like she's grateful.

Halvorsen at the fuel dock watched me work while he pumped, the way he does, chewing on whatever he was chewing on.

"Upper landing again," he said. Not a question. Halvorsen doesn't ask questions; he makes statements and waits to see if you'll correct them.

"Coldbank wants their seed in before the ground warms."

"Ground up there won't warm till July."

"Then I'm early." I cinched the last strap. "You got a weather read?"

He squinted west, where the mountains were doing their card trick. "Clean till tomorrow night, maybe. After that—" He shrugged, which from Halvorsen was a full meteorological report. "Ice report out of the hydrology office says the calving's late this year. Reach should be open."

The word *calving* sat in my chest for a second, cold as the water it came from. Up above the Kestrel Narrows, the glacier comes down to the river like a wall, and twice a year it sheds house-sized pieces of itself into the current, and for a few days after, the reach is a demolition site. Everyone upriver plans their lives around it. Farmers, rangers, the few hard-headed souls at Coldbank who think a valley that floods once a generation is worth farming anyway. You count tides, not days. The river keeps its own calendar and it doesn't share.

"Open is open," I said, and cast off.

* * *

The lower channel is forty miles of water that looks like it can't hurt you, which is exactly its trick. Wide, slow, braided around sandbars that move every spring like furniture in a house where nobody's watching. The charts show them where they were three years ago. The charts are liars, or optimists, which amounts to the same thing on a river.

So I read the water instead.

There's a language to it, and I don't mean the pretty kind. A bar shows itself as a smoothness, a place where the chop goes quiet and the current lays down flat like a cat pretending to sleep. Green water means depth. Brown-green means the bottom's close enough to taste your keel. When two currents meet over a buried bar, the seam between them draws a line fine as a hair, and if you cross that line wrong, the river will pick your boat up and set you down somewhere you didn't agree to go. I've seen a loaded scow cross one sideways and come out of it pointing upstream, spun like a coin.

I ran the seams all morning, the Marrow answering her helm with the half-beat lag I know as well as my own pulse, and by noon the banks had narrowed and the water had gone from brown to that pale glacial green that means we were getting close to the melt. Willows gave way to spruce. Spruce gave way to rock. The air came off the ice fields cold enough to make your fillings ache.

At the mouth of the Narrows I slowed, because you always slow there, whether you need to or not. It's less about caution than courtesy. The Narrows is a mile of canyon where the whole river funnels through a gap you could spit across, and the water going through it is not traveling, it's fleeing. Standing waves the size of trucks. Holes that will hold a swimmer until they stop being a swimmer. In the old days, before the dams further south changed the flows, pilots used to line their boats through on ropes rather than run it. Some still do. I run it, but I run it at slack tide, when the difference between the ocean pulling and the river pushing evens out for twenty minutes, and I run it with both hands and no conversation.

Today I wasn't running it yet. Today I just idled at the mouth and looked up at the walls, the way you'd nod to something dangerous you have an understanding with.

The rock above the Narrows is streaked white with bird lime, and halfway up there's a ledge where someone a hundred years ago painted a number—*17*—in letters four feet tall. Nobody knows why. Survey marker, boundary line, a bet between drunk men; the theories change with the beer. I look at it every time. It reminds me that other people have stood where I'm standing, in boats with names nobody remembers, and made it through, and that the river keeps score but doesn't publish it.

I turned back downstream to stage for the slack, and that's when I saw the skiff.

It came around the point below the Narrows, a fourteen-foot aluminum with an outboard, riding light—one person, maybe two—and it was doing something I have only seen done by people who are about to become a story told over drinks. It was crossing the seam above the Graveyard Bar. Crossways. At speed.

I put the wheel over and rang the engine back, though there was nothing I could do from half a mile except watch. The skiff hit the seam and the river took it the way the river takes everything offered carelessly—a hard yaw, a wall of water over the gunwale, and then it was sideways and shipping the rest, and the two figures in it went into the current like dolls.

Except they weren't dolls. One of them surfaced fast, thrashing, and the other caught him—I could see it was a catch, an arm locked across a chest—and then they were both in the water below the bar, in the eddy that forms on the downstream side, the one merciful pocket in that whole stretch. The skiff bobbed away upside down and made for the willows on the far bank at the speed of nothing.

I brought the Marrow around. A boat my size doesn't maneuver like a skiff; she turns like a building, and I had to plan the approach two minutes before I made it. I came down on the eddy with the current quartering, put her stern to the swimmers, and killed the engine, and we drifted together the last fifty feet like it was choreographed, which it was, just not by anyone speaking aloud.

The first one reached the boarding ladder himself—young, maybe twenty-five, government-issue rain gear, glasses gone, terror gone past terror into that gray place where the body just obeys. The second came slower. She got a hand on the ladder and hung there, breathing in great tearing gulps, and I looked down at her and she looked up at me, and I felt the crack run through the day before I understood why.

She had her sister's jaw. That was all. That was everything. Twelve years since I'd seen that jaw, and here it was on a stranger, blue-lipped and shaking on my ladder, and for one full second I was twenty-four years old again and the river was taking something from me that it never gave back.

"Take his hand," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost more than the rescue had. "Get him aboard before the cramping starts."

She hauled him up. I got them into the wheelhouse, into blankets, onto the bench under the heater that I keep for engine parts. The young one shook and stared at nothing. The woman wrung out her hair with hands that wouldn't quite close and said, "Thank you. God. Thank you," and I nodded like a woman who hears that every week, because I do, because people on this river treat the water like a road and the road forgives them until it doesn't.

"You crossed the Graveyard seam," I said.

"We didn't—" The young one found his voice, thin as wire. "The chart showed the channel on the far side."

"The chart's three years old." I said it without heat. Heat would have been a kindness, maybe, but I don't spend kindness I can't account for. "The bar moved in the spring flood. Everything on that sheet east of the willow line is fiction now."

They looked at each other. Government people, I decided. Matching rain shells, matching dry bags, the particular competence of people who are very good indoors. Rangers, surveyors, inspectors—I've carried enough of them as cargo to know the species, though I don't carry them as passengers. Never passengers. Passengers talk, and want things, and remember your name, and names are debts.

"Where were you headed?" I asked.

"The survey camp," the woman said. "Above the Narrows. We're with the hydrology office—we're supposed to relieve the team up there." She hesitated, and something moved behind her eyes that wasn't gratitude and wasn't fear, or was both wearing the same coat. "Captain, could you—"

"No," I said.

She blinked water off her lashes. "I haven't asked yet."

"You were going to ask me to take you up. The answer's no. I run freight. I don't run people." I handed each of them a tin cup of coffee from the thermos, black, hot enough to hurt, which is how you apologize on the Kestrel without saying it. "There's a ranger station eight miles down. I'll put you ashore at their dock. They'll get you a boat that floats and a pilot who likes company."

The woman wrapped both hands around the cup and didn't drink. She was looking at me the way people look at a locked door, and I recognized the expression because I've spent twelve years being one.

"It's important," she said. "The team up there—they're due to report and they haven't. Radio's been silent for six days. If we miss the window before the calving—"

"Then the calving will wait for you," I said. "It's good at waiting. So am I."

Something crossed her face then—not anger. Recalculation. She drank her coffee, and helped me rig a tow line for the drowned skiff, and talked pleasantly about the weather for the eight miles down to the ranger dock, and thanked me again at the ladder with a grip that held one beat longer than thanks requires.

Her name was Voss, she said. Junior something-or-other. I didn't offer mine. I never do. On the radio, boats call each other by hull numbers anyway, and the Marrow's number is the only name I've needed in twelve years.

I watched them walk up the ranger dock, small against the spruce, and I told myself the day was over and the ledger balanced: two strangers alive, one skiff recovered, forty tons of seed potatoes still owed to Coldbank, six hours lost. Miles, not years. That's how I keep my books. A year asks you what you did with it, who you were, whether the thing you buried stayed buried. A mile only asks if you made it.

But that night, anchored below the Narrows waiting for slack, I stood at the rail a long time with the glacier smell coming down the canyon, cold and mineral and older than any grief I own, and I thought about the woman's face when she'd said *the team hasn't reported*. Six days of silence, up there in the reach where the water runs milk-white and the charts are guesses. And I thought about how she'd looked at me like a locked door, and how I'd known exactly what she saw, because I've spent twelve years being one.

The slack came at 2:40 in the morning. I ran the Narrows in the dark by memory and moonlight, both hands on the wheel, the standing waves roaring white on either side of the deep channel like animals held back by a fence they haven't tested lately. The painted *17* slid past overhead, ghost-pale on the rock.

And when I came out the far side into the long glacial reach, with Coldbank still thirty miles up and the ice fields hanging over the water like held breath, I saw what I would spend the rest of this story trying to outrun.

Tents. A survey camp on the gravel bar ahead, set neat and square in the last of the moonlight. Instruments on tripods, still turning. A fire ring with wood stacked beside it, unburned.

And not one soul moving. Not one light burning. Not one track leaving the mud.
