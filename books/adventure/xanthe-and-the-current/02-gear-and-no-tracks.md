## 2. Gear and No Tracks

The ranger came aboard at Greyling Landing before the fog had finished lifting, and Sable knew before he said a word that the money was going to be strange. Strange money had a look to it. It arrived too early in the morning, wearing a uniform that didn't quite fit its posture, carrying questions it hadn't learned how to ask yet.

"You Reyes?" he said. He was young through the shoulders and old through the eyes, the way men got who'd spent too many seasons watching water do what water does.

"Captain Reyes." She kept her hands on the wheel of the *Marrow*, wiping down the brass with a rag that had outlived three engines. "You want cargo moved, I move cargo. You want something else, the answer's probably no."

"Ranger Ostrander." He held up a leather credential wallet the way other men held up empty hands—as if to say, see, nothing hidden here. "Department of Water and Land Survey. I need you to take me and two investigators upriver. Above Kestrel Narrows."

Sable stopped wiping.

Above the Narrows was glacial reach—cold enough to kill a swimming man in four minutes, shallow enough in places to open a hull like a tin can, and due to close entirely when the calving season started sealing the upper channel with ice. Nobody ran it this late. Nobody with sense.

"The survey team," she said. Not a question. The whole river had been chewing on it for six days: eight government people gone up into the reach with sonar gear and generators and a permit signed by somebody important, and then nothing. No radio check-in on schedule. No resupply request. Just silence, spreading downriver like an oil slick.

Ostrander's jaw tightened. "You've heard."

"I've heard everything. That's my job." She set the rag down. "Hearing is free. Going up there isn't."

"I have authorization for hazard pay."

"You have authorization," Sable said, "which means somebody downtown signed a paper saying your death is budgeted. That's not the same as hazard pay. Hazard pay is when I decide what my life costs, and right now I'm still deciding."

The two investigators waiting on the dock were easier to read than the ranger. A woman built like a fence post with a hard case handcuffed to her wrist, and a younger man who kept looking at the *Marrow* the way city men looked at horses—with respect at a distance and no idea what to do up close. They climbed aboard when Ostrander nodded, and the woman put her case down on Sable's chart table without asking, which cost her a look she'd remember.

"Rules," Sable said, untying from the pilings. "Nobody touches the wheel. Nobody touches the throttle. If I say get low, you get low. If I say jump, you'd better already be moving. And nobody asks me why I know things. I just know them."

"That's not standard protocol—" the young one started.

"It's the only protocol on this boat." She fed the throttle in and the *Marrow* slid off the landing, her old diesel settling into that half-beat-off rhythm she'd carried for twelve years, like a heart with a murmur that refused to stop.

* * *

The river above Greyling changed character the way a person changes character after a funeral. Below, it was working water—barges, fish wheels, the brown patient current of a river that had made peace with its banks. Above, past the last homestead and the last fuel dock, it went quiet and green and cold, running fast over gravel bars the color of bone, braiding and unbraiding itself like something trying to remember its own name.

Sable read it the whole way up, and she could feel the investigators watching her do it, not understanding what they were seeing. She wasn't looking at the water. She was looking at everything else—the way the current bent around a snag told her where the deep channel lay; the color of the foam told her how much rock was under it; the birds standing on a bar told her whether that bar had shifted since yesterday, because birds remembered where the safe ground had been and stood on faith until the river corrected them.

"There." She pointed with her chin at a seam of darker water sliding along the north bank. "We'll take that line. Stay off the middle. Middle's lying today."

"Lying," the woman investigator repeated flatly.

"Rivers lie. It's most of what they do. They show you smooth water over a hole that'll swallow a truck. They show you ripples where there's nothing but sand. My job is catching them at it." She eased the wheel over two degrees, no more. "Your survey team would've known that. Government hydrographers usually do."

"They were experienced," Ostrander said, from behind her shoulder.

"Experienced men don't vanish." She glanced back at him. "Something happened to them that their experience didn't cover. Or someone happened."

Nobody answered that, and the engine filled the silence for a while.

They passed the Narrows midafternoon—a throat of gray water between walls of shattered granite, where the river dropped through a chute and came out below looking insulted. Sable took it slow, reading the standing waves, feeling the *Marrow* shudder in the confused water, and the young investigator turned a shade of green that matched the spruce. The woman with the case never blinked. Sable revised her opinion of both accordingly.

Above the Narrows the valley opened and the air changed—colder, sweeter, carrying that mineral smell of glacier melt that got into your teeth. The water here was the pale opaque green of ground glass, thick with rock flour, cold enough that a dropped thermometer would apologize. Somewhere upstream, thirty miles maybe, the ice sat in its white silence, grinding its slow teeth, getting ready to calve.

And somewhere ahead, on a gravel flat where the maps said the survey team had made their base camp, eight people had stopped existing.

* * *

She smelled the camp before she saw it. Woodsmoke, but wrong—old smoke, dead smoke, the ghost of a fire rather than a living one. Then the bend gave them up: four dome tents on the gravel bench above the high-water line, guy lines taut, flys buttoned down against weather that hadn't come. A generator shed of plywood and blue tarp. An antenna mast leaning at a careful angle. And a cook tent with its flap half-open, breathing out that stale smell of abandoned coffee.

Ostrander was over the side before the *Marrow* finished bumping the gravel, and Sable let him go. She stayed aboard five more minutes, doing what she always did first at any landing: reading the bank.

The mud at the waterline was a ledger. Everything that walked wrote in it—moose, fox, gulls, men. She read it slowly, letting her eyes go soft the way you do when you're not hunting a thing but letting it find you.

Here: boot prints, many sets, going up from the landing to the camp. Days old, edges crumbled, rain-softened. Here: the same prints going back down. Normal traffic. A camp that lived its life.

Then she went still.

There were no prints leaving.

Not fewer. None. Eight people did not walk away from this camp, because eight people walking anywhere left a signature in mud this good, and the mud here was perfect—fine glacial silt, damp, holding an edge like wet clay. She could see where a fox had threaded between the tents. She could see the crescent tracks of a heron at the shallows. The river kept accounts, and the account showed eight men arriving and zero men departing by land.

By boat, then. But the camp's skiff was still beached at the landing, hull dry, gas can sitting beside it full enough to slosh.

Sable stood at the rail a long moment, and the cold coming off the water found the gap between her collar and her neck and settled there, and did not leave.

* * *

The investigators worked the camp the way investigators work anything—thoroughly, loudly, and mostly missing the point. The fence-post woman photographed everything twice. The young one knelt by the cook stove and said, "Coffee's cold," with the tone of a man who believed he was contributing.

"Coffee's been cold four days," Sable said from the tent flap. "Cream's curdled but not rotten. Bread's stale but not moldy—it's too dry up here for mold to win quick. Generator's out of fuel, but the tank was filled recently; see the wipe marks around the cap? Somebody topped it off and expected to need it." She nodded at the radio shack. "Their log book ends mid-sentence. You don't stop writing mid-sentence unless something interrupts you—and whatever interrupted them, they weren't scared enough to run, because nobody packed. Every bedroll's still rolled. Every coat's still hung."

"So they left quickly," the woman said.

"No," Sable said. "They left *deliberately*. There's a difference, and the difference is standing right behind you."

She let them turn. She pointed at the instrument table under the tarp awning, where the sonar display unit sat with its screen dark and its cables coiled neat as hair.

"Instruments are running," she said. "Or they were. Sonar rigs like that get shut down proper or they get left logging—you don't just abandon a hundred-thousand-dollar transducer array in a river. But look at the laptop. Lid closed. Power cable plugged in. Battery light was blinking when we walked up—I watched it. Somebody set that machine to keep recording and walked away expecting to come back."

"Expecting to come back," Ostrander repeated slowly.

"People who flee don't expect to come back. People who step out for a minute do." Sable crouched by the mud path between the cook tent and the water. "And people who step out for a minute leave footprints. Look at this. Coming and going, days of it, all normal. Now walk me to the edge of camp in any direction and show me where eight trained field men exited this site. Go on. Find me one line of departure."

They searched for an hour. They found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and by the end of it the fence-post woman had stopped photographing and started simply staring at the mud with an expression Sable recognized from mirrors: the look of a person whose map of the world had developed a hole in it.

* * *

It was near dusk, with the light going amber and long across the water, that Sable found what they'd missed.

She'd been walking the bank downstream of the camp, ostensibly stretching her legs, actually doing what she always did—reading. And forty meters below the landing, where a slough of slack water curled back into a stand of willow, she saw it.

Drag marks. Old ones, rain-softened almost to nothing, but there: two parallel grooves in the silt running from the waterline up into the willows, and willow stems broken at knee height along a line no animal would take. Animals broke willow going *through*. Something had been hauled *along*.

She pushed into the stand, branches whipping back at her, and there it was: a second boat. Aluminum skiff, fourteen feet, government stenciling on the bow worn but legible. Hauled clear up out of the water, turned bottom-down against the brush, and screened with cut willow boughs laid over it like a blanket over a sleeping child.

Somebody had hidden this boat. Carefully. Deliberately. From the water, it was invisible. From the bank, invisible. Only someone walking the exact line of the drag marks—and nobody had, because the investigators had spent all day inside the camp measuring coffee temperatures—would ever find it.

Sable crouched beside it and put her hand flat on the hull. Cold. Rain-wet. But under the gunwale, caught in the seam, she found a thread of orange polypropylene rope, frayed, and a smear of something dark and greasy on the aluminum that her thumb told her was outboard oil mixed with blood. Not much blood. A wiped-up-after amount of blood.

She stayed there while the light died, and she thought.

Eight men. One boat hidden by someone who wanted it not found—which meant someone had left in it, or meant to, or had been made to disappear from it. Instruments left running. Coffee left cooling. Beds left made. A log ending mid-sentence. And not one footprint leaving camp, which meant either eight ghosts or—

Or the rest of them had left by water, in a hurry, in the dark, and the mud by the landing had been washed clean by the wake of their leaving. Except the hidden boat. The hidden boat was the one thing somebody had needed to make sure nobody followed.

Her hand was still on the hull when she heard the willows move behind her, and she came around fast with her knife already open, and it was only Ostrander, hands up, eyes wide.

"Easy. Easy. I followed your tracks." He looked at the boat, and she watched him understand it in real time, his face going through surprise and dread in order, like cars through a tunnel. "That's the team's spare. Why is it hidden?"

"That's the question," Sable said. She closed the knife. "Here's another one. Whoever hid it didn't want the boat found. Which means whoever hid it is either still out there—or doesn't want anyone following where it went."

The wind came down the valley off the ice, and the willows hissed, and somewhere far upstream the glacier spoke—one deep cracking boom rolling down the water like a struck bell, the sound of a mountain deciding to move.

Ostrander flinched. Sable didn't. She'd grown up on that sound, in another life, under another name, and her body remembered it the way bodies remember falls.

"We're done here tonight," she said. "I'm not anchoring in the open. There's a cutbank two miles down with room to swing at anchor, out of the main current. We go back, we eat, and at first light I'm taking this skiff's drag marks further up the slough. Because there's a third possibility you haven't considered, Ranger."

"What's that?"

"That whoever hid this boat wasn't hiding it from us." She looked up the darkening river, toward the ice, toward the silence where eight men had stopped being. "They were hiding it from whoever comes next. And somebody's coming next. Fleet barges don't run this water for science."

"How would you know—"

"Because I know what salvage gear looks like loaded on a barge deck," Sable said, "and I heard the radio chatter at Greyling two nights ago, and none of it sounded like a rescue." She stepped past him, out of the willows, into the last gray light. "Sleep light, Ranger. Tomorrow we find out who's still alive up here—if anyone is—and we find out before the wrong people do."

Behind them the river ran on, keeping its accounts, and the glacier boomed again, closer now, like something counting too.
