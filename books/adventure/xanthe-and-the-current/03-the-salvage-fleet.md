## 3. The Salvage Fleet

They came up the river on a Tuesday, which Sable Reyes would remember later because Tuesdays were when the Marrow took on diesel and the landing was supposed to be quiet.

She heard them before she saw them—three diesels running out of time with each other, a ragged heartbeat rolling down the current. She was elbow-deep in the bilge pump's guts at the time, greased to the wrist, and she stayed that way for a full minute, listening, because her ears had learned to sort river traffic the way other people sorted voices. Fishing boats ran high and light and slapped. Log barges groaned. This was something else: heavy, deliberate, three engines held just off sync like men walking together who didn't trust each other.

She wiped her hands on a rag that was already beyond saving and went up into the light.

The Kestrel Landing sat where the river bent wide and slow below the Narrows—a fuel dock, a gravel ramp, a shed with a rusted sign advertising ice that hadn't been sold there in years. Beyond it the valley narrowed toward the glacial reach, water gone from brown to a pale, chalky green, cold enough to kill a swimming man in four minutes. The mountains above it held their snow into August.

Three barges came around the bend in echelon, and Sable stood very still on the Marrow's deck and looked at them the way you look at weather.

They were industrial in a way this river hadn't seen since the dam surveys died. Flat-decked, steel-hulled, riding low with weight. Cranes folded flat along their rails like sleeping birds. Deck cargo lashed under tarps that bulged with the shapes of machinery nobody ships upriver by accident—winches, spuds, something long and jointed that could only be a dredge head. Men lined the rails. Not many talking. That was the first wrong thing. Rivermen talk; they holler across water at strangers because water is lonely and any voice is company. These men stood shoulder to shoulder and said nothing, and their silence came downstream ahead of them like a smell.

Sable counted. Eleven visible on the lead barge. Hard hands, hard boots, coveralls without names stitched on them. Two of them wore sidearms openly, which on this river meant either they'd come from somewhere lawless or they believed themselves above it.

The lead barge swung for the dock with more authority than grace, and its bow thruster chewed the water brown. A man stepped to the rail before the lines were even thrown—not helping with the lines, just standing, watching the Marrow at her mooring the way an appraiser watches a horse.

He was fifty-something, built like a door, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned. Canvas jacket, no logo. He had the stillness of a man who had never once needed to hurry, because everything hurried toward him instead.

"Captain," he called across the gap while his deck crew made fast. His voice carried without effort. "Corven Hale."

"Sable Reyes." She didn't wave. "You're a long way from anywhere that builds cranes."

"I'm exactly where I intend to be." He crossed the gangway as soon as it touched, uninvited but not quite trespassing—he moved like a man for whom the distinction had never mattered. He stopped at the foot of her deck and looked around the Marrow with frank appraisal: the cargo netting, the coiled lines flaked with a working sailor's carelessness-that-isn't, the chart table under its plastic cover. "Marrow," he read off the transom. "Cargo boat. You run the upper reach?"

"I run what pays."

"The Narrows?"

"The Narrows closes for calving season. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows it closes." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him, because it was warm, and practiced, and arrived a half-second after his eyes had already finished measuring her. "Whether it stays closed is a different question. May I?" He gestured at her deck without waiting, and came aboard anyway.

Sable let him. There were ways to throw a man off your boat and all of them started a war, and wars on water were lost by whoever had less tonnage. She had forty feet of steel and he had three barges of it. Instead she leaned against the wheelhouse door and crossed her arms and let him stand on her deck feeling tall.

"You're the pilot who's been running gear to the ranger station," Hale said. "Two trips last week. Fuel, food, radios." He said it conversationally, like a man remarking on weather, and Sable felt the hair on her forearms lift, because he wasn't guessing. He knew her schedule. Someone had been watching the landing, or someone at the station sold information cheaply, or both.

"Ranger service runs a tab," she said. "I run what pays."

"And now something bigger pays." From inside his jacket he produced a folder, real paper, heavy stock, and held it out. "Salvage contract. Thirty days, exclusive pilot services, your boat leads our traffic through the Narrows and the reach above. We provide escort tonnage, insurance rider, hazard pay at rates that will make you sit down."

She didn't take the folder. "Salvage of what? Nothing's wrecked up there. The survey team's lost, not sunk—the rangers are handling that."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes. It was small and fast, like a fish turning under ice, and then the smile was back in place as if it had never left. "The government loses people in that canyon every few years. When they do, they spend six weeks looking and find nothing, and the families get a letter. We're not the government. We're thorough." He set the folder down on her winch housing, squared its edges to the deck with two fingers, a small precise gesture that told her more about him than the whole speech had. "Thirty days. Think about it tonight."

"I think fast."

"Then think about this." He turned half away, then half back, a man who understood the choreography of exits. "That reach is going to open soon—six tides, maybe eight, before the ice seals it till spring. In that window, somebody is going to run it. If it isn't me, it'll be someone with less equipment and worse manners. I'd rather it was me, with you on the bow calling the water." He paused. "I pay well for good pilots. I've never had to pay badly for one yet."

"That's not a threat," Sable said. "But it walks like one."

Hale laughed—a real laugh, short and genuine, and somehow that was worse. "Captain Reyes. Everything walks like something else. That's what a river teaches you." He stepped back onto the gangway, then stopped, one hand on the cable. "One more thing. Free of charge. The mud at the survey camp—you've seen it, I hear. No tracks leaving. You want to know why a party of six adults walks into the willows and leaves no prints?" He shook his head slowly, sadly, a man mourning a fact. "Because they didn't walk out. Somebody carried them, or somebody followed them in after. Either way—" he spread his hands, "—the river keeps what it keeps. Best not to go asking it for change."

He walked the gangway back to his barge without looking at her again, which was its own kind of message: he had already decided how this would end, and courtesy was just the interest accruing meanwhile.

* * *

She gave herself until dark, which was her habit with decisions—hold them up to the last light and see what shadows they threw.

The landing changed character as evening came. The salvage barges lit their work decks with floods that turned the water below them a hard chemical white, and generators began their drone, and men moved crates with the wordless efficiency of an operation that had drilled until thinking became optional. Sable cooked rice and tinned sausage on the Marrow's little stove and ate standing at the window, watching.

A skiff came alongside at dusk. Two of Hale's men, not Hale himself. They didn't board. One of them held up a waterproof case and said, "Foreman says leave it on your deck if you're in. Contract's got a signature line and an advance." He set it on the dock cleat, not her deck—small courtesy, or a test of whether she'd reach for it—and the skiff backed away into the gloom.

She left the case on the cleat all night. Rain came at midnight, fine and cold, beading on the case's latch. Toward morning a gull landed next to it, considered it, and left, and Sable thought: even the birds have better judgment than Corven Hale's paperwork.

She opened it at first light, because a captain should know exactly what's being offered before she refuses it, and refusing blind is just fear wearing work clothes.

The contract was clean. Too clean. Rates generous, clauses plain, liability structured so that anything that went wrong upriver went wrong to her name and not theirs. Page nine held the interesting part: a confidentiality section so broad it covered *observations*, capitalized and defined—meaning anything she saw, sounded, or salvaged knowledge of belonged to Hale's outfit. Meaning whatever the survey team had found, she'd be legally blind to it while piloting straight over it.

And beneath the signature line, typed in the same neutral font as everything else, a single line that was not boilerplate:

*Pilot to render assistance to fleet operations without regard to third parties present in the operating area.*

Third parties. Present. In the operating area.

She read it three times. Somewhere above the Narrows, in country where the mud kept no tracks, there were—or had been—third parties. And Hale wanted her signature agreeing in advance that whatever happened to them happened outside her concern.

Sable closed the folder. Down the dock, the case sat where the rain had slicked it, and beyond it the three barges idled their generators and waited with the patience of money.

* * *

She found Hale at midmorning on the lead barge's aft deck, drinking coffee from a steel thermos cup, watching the current with binoculars he lowered when she stepped aboard. Uninvited again. She was starting to understand it was a policy, not a habit.

"You slept on it," he said.

"I read it." She put the folder on the rail between them. "Page nine."

"Standard protection of proprietary operations."

"Third parties present in the operating area." She watched his face while she said it, and gave him credit: nothing flickered. A man that controlled deserved a certain professional respect, the way you respect a trap that's been oiled. "Whoever's still up that canyon, your contract asks me to agree I won't see them. I'm a pilot, Mr. Hale. Seeing the water is the entire job."

"The water doesn't have opinions."

"Neither do drowned men. People do." She tapped the folder once. "I'll take your rate for honest salvage. Wreck location, cargo recovery, escort work—I'll call your water and you'll pay my invoice. But I don't sign blindness, and I don't sign around living people."

Hale looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he poured a second cup of coffee and held it out. She didn't take it. He set it on the rail beside the folder anyway, unhurried, and leaned back against the crane housing.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Captain Reyes?" he said. "Nothing. Yet. But I suspect I'm going to." He picked up the folder, squared its edges again—that same precise gesture, a man who kept the world aligned—and slid it inside his jacket. "Declined carefully. That's the phrase, isn't it? You declined me carefully." He smiled, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, and she wished it hadn't. "Careful is a skill. Most people can't afford it. I hope you can."

"It's the only thing I own outright."

"Keep it maintained." He raised his cup to her, a toast to nothing. "Windows close, Captain. Tides turn. And boats are fragile things—lines chafe, tanks leak, moorings fail. A woman alone on a river has so many small vulnerabilities." He drank. "My offer stands until the ice does. After that, we won't need a pilot. We'll need a witness, and those are cheaper."

Sable stepped back off his barge onto the dock planks, and only when her boots were on her own territory did she answer.

"Then hire cheap," she said. "This river charges everyone the same tuition. Some of us already paid."

She walked the length of the dock without hurrying, past the floodlights and the silent men and the tarped machinery whose shapes she catalogued as she went—winch, spud, dredge head, and something crate-long that might have been a cutting arm—past the case on the cleat, which she now kicked into the river without breaking stride, and up the gangway to the Marrow, where she cast off her own lines and moved her boat to the far mooring, upstream of the barges, facing the Narrows.

Behind her the generators droned. Ahead, the chalk-green water slid down from the glacier, cold and patient, keeping everything it kept.

It was while she stood her afternoon watch, logging the current against the piling marks, that she saw the second thing—the thing that turned unease into arithmetic. A helicopter, high and northbound, running the canyon line without lights, without markings, too high to hail. It slowed over the reach above the Narrows. It hung there a moment, a black tick against the icefield.

Then it turned and came back south, and passed directly over the landing, low enough now that she could see the camera mount under its belly, and it orbited the Marrow twice before climbing away into the cloud.

Sable stood at her wheel and wrote the time in the logbook in her small square hand, and underneath it, because logs should tell the truth even when captains can't yet:

*Aircraft, unidentified, 2 orbits, vessel Marrow. Reason unknown.*

She knew the reason. She just didn't know yet that it had a name, or that the name was twelve years old and buried in a file she'd paid good money to seal.

The river slid past, green and cold, carrying the smell of ice down from the calving fields, counting down tides no one could see.
