## 4. Six Tides

The dispatch came upriver the way all official news comes upriver: late, damp, and smelling faintly of someone else's coffee.

Sable found it wedged in the Marrow's mail slot at Grayling Landing, a manila envelope gone soft at one corner where rain had gotten at it, stamped with the blocky seal of the Territorial Hydrology Office. She stood on the dock with it a long moment before opening it, because envelopes from that office had a way of changing the shape of a season, and she had been enjoying the shape of this one.

Around her, Grayling did its gray morning work. A fuel truck idled. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings. Two hundred yards downstream, moored in a line like animals at a trough, sat the salvage fleet—three barges, low and black-hulled, their crane arms folded against the sky like sleeping insects. Their generators had run all night. She'd heard them through the hull of the Marrow, a sound she could not stop cataloguing: diesel heartbeat, sixty cycles, patient as a pulse.

She opened the envelope with her thumbnail.

*TO ALL LICENSED PILOTS, UPPER KESTREL REACH,* it began, in the cheerful bureaucratic voice of someone who had never been cold in his life.

*BE ADVISED: SEISMIC MONITORING STATION 9 (UPPER GLACIER) HAS RECORDED ACCELERATING CREEP RATES CONSISTENT WITH IMMINENT CALVING EVENT AT THE KESTREL NARROWS HEADWALL. CALVING IS EXPECTED TO BREACH THE SEASONAL ICE DAM AND OPEN NAVIGABLE WATER ABOVE THE NARROWS FOR AN ESTIMATED PERIOD OF SIX (6) TIDAL CYCLES, PLUS OR MINUS ONE CYCLE.*

*FOLLOWING THIS WINDOW, ICE DISCHARGE WILL RENDER THE UPPER REACH IMPASSABLE UNTIL SPRING THAW. ALL VESSELS ENTERING THE NARROWS DURING THE CLOSED PERIOD DO SO AT THEIR OWN RISK AND BEYOND THE SCOPE OF ANY RESCUE GUARANTEE.*

*THE OFFICE THANKS YOU FOR YOUR ATTENTION TO SAFETY.*

Sable read it twice, standing in the rain, while the gulls went on arguing and the fuel truck went on idling and somewhere behind her the salvage barges breathed their electric breath.

Six tides.

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Twelve days, if you were generous. More likely ten. The window would open when the ice dam let go—a violence first, then a strange calm, the river running high and cold and clean between walls of rotting floe—and then, cycle by cycle, the glacier would shed its weight into the reach and close the door behind whoever was inside it.

Whoever reached the wreck site first held it. That was what the dispatch didn't say but meant. Water above the Narrows was going to be a room with one entrance, and the entrance was going to shut.

She looked down the line of barges again. Three of them. She'd counted cranes, winches, deck crew. She'd watched men in company jackets take soundings off their stern with equipment that cost more than the Marrow entire. They weren't here for cargo. Nobody ran three barges and a crane spread two hundred miles past the last honest port for cargo.

They were here for whatever the survey team had found. And now they had a schedule.

* * *

Back aboard, Sable spread her charts on the saloon table and weighted the corners with a brass cup, a spool of wire, and the hydrology dispatch itself, which seemed only fair.

The Marrow was forty-one feet of pre-war steel with a Gardner engine older than she was, honest and slow and utterly without vanity. Fully loaded she made seven knots on flat water and hated every one of them. Light, with the current under her, she could touch nine and a half if she flattered the engine, which Sable did the way other people prayed—regularly, privately, without much expectation.

The lead barge—the one they called *Iron Meridian*, though nobody aboard ever said the name aloud where she could hear it—she'd watched come up from the delta. Twin screws, modern diesels, a hull built to push ice, not negotiate with it. Twelve knots easy. Fourteen if they pushed, and men who answered no questions were men who pushed.

She laid dividers on the chart. Grayling Landing to the Narrows mouth: thirty-eight miles of winding channel, sandbars shifting weekly, two reaches where the fairway narrowed enough that a barge that wide would have to slow to thread it. Narrows mouth to the upper pool where the survey camp had stood: another twenty-two, mostly glacial water, deep and fast and treacherous in ways the charts admitted with little dotted lines and the word *unsurveyed*, which on a chart is a polite way of saying *we don't know and we're sorry*.

Sixty miles total. At nine and a half knots, running light, running hard, running lucky: six hours and twenty minutes, give or take the river's mood.

At fourteen, the *Meridian* would do it in four and a half, and she wouldn't even be breathing hard.

Sable sat back and looked at the numbers the way you look at a hand of cards you've already lost. There was no version of this where she outran them. Even if she slipped her lines tonight, in the dark, ahead of the calving, ahead of the window—they didn't need the window open to catch her. They needed it open to get where they were going, same as her, and they'd simply run her down in the middle reach and call it an accident of traffic. Or worse, they'd follow her in, let her find the channel, and take it off her wake like crumbs off a tablecloth.

Which was, she realized, exactly what they wanted her to do.

That was the thought that turned the cold clarity on, the gambler's clarity, the one that arrived not as excitement but as a kind of terrible quiet. Hale hadn't offered her the contract out of courtesy. He'd offered it because he intended to have her services either way. Sign it, and she piloted them in for money. Refuse it, and they'd simply sit on her wake at the Narrows and follow her through, letting her read the water so their hulls wouldn't have to. Her eyes for their engines. Her neck for their payroll.

Either way, she was the pilot. Either way, she did the work.

Unless.

She pulled the chart closer and looked at it differently—not as a route but as a text, the way she actually read rivers, which was not lines on paper but memory stacked on memory. Thirty years of pilots had worked this reach before the charts were drawn, and half of what they knew had never been written down because writing it down meant sharing it, and sharing water knowledge on the Kestrel was how you ended up poor or drowned.

There was the cutoff below Raven Island that the charts showed as marsh but ran four fathoms in spring melt—you couldn't take a loaded barge through it, but you could take the *Marrow*, and more importantly, you could take her where a following vessel couldn't see which way you'd gone. There was the old log-drive chute on the east bank, blasted by lumber crews in her grandfather's era, silted to nothing on paper but scoured back open every high-water year; she'd sounded it herself two springs ago out of boredom and found eleven feet over a gravel floor. And there was the thing she kept coming back to, the thing that had been sitting in the back of her skull since the day she'd read the investigators' report and seen the word *sonar*:

The survey team had been mapping the drowned town. Which meant they'd been mapping the bottom. Which meant somewhere in their instruments, in their data, in whatever they'd carried out of that camp or failed to carry, was a picture of the upper reach more accurate than anything the Hydrology Office had printed in fifty years.

A channel the charts didn't know. Deep water where the paper showed shoals. If the old dam had changed the river's habits—and a dam always changed a river's habits—then every chart above the Narrows was a lie told slowly, and the truth was sitting in a dead man's sonar file.

She couldn't outrun the fleet. But she didn't have to. She had to arrive knowing things they didn't, in water they couldn't read, and let the six tides do the rest. Let them burn their hours sounding the honest channels while she took the dishonest ones. Let the ice close behind all of them and sort out whose hull belonged inside.

It wasn't a plan yet. It was a shape of a plan, the way a bar is a shape of an island—true at this hour, false at the next, fatal if you trusted it too far.

But it was hers, and it was the only hand at the table worth betting.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing unglamorous arithmetic of a different kind. Fuel: the tanks were at seventy percent, enough for the round trip with margin if she didn't idle, and she never idled. Water, food, the spare impeller, the box of engine parts she kept for the Gardner the way some people keep medicine for a sick relative. She checked the bilge, checked the shaft packing, checked the anchor rode for chafe, working through the boat methodically, and the work steadied her the way it always did. Hands busy, mind free to run cold.

Twice she caught herself looking downstream at the barges.

Once, Corven Hale was standing on the *Meridian*'s deck rail, hands in his pockets, looking back at her. Even at two hundred yards she could feel the quality of his attention—not hostile, not friendly, just thorough, a man taking inventory. He raised one hand, a small unhurried acknowledgment, the gesture of someone who considered the outcome already settled and was being gracious about the interval.

Sable did not wave back. She turned to her engine and let him watch her back, which was its own kind of message, though she suspected he'd read it correctly anyway: *I see you too.*

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it did this far north—not a sunset so much as a long negotiation between light and mountain. The rain quit. The air went glassy and cold enough to taste iron. Upstream, beyond the tree line, the glacier sat in its cirque like something asleep that everyone in the valley pretended not to hear breathing.

Sable took a mug of tea out to the wheelhouse roof and sat against the stack for its warmth, and let herself think about the part of the calculation she'd been avoiding.

Because the numbers weren't really about knots. They were about names.

If she ran the Narrows alone, ahead of the fleet, into unsurveyed water, on a route stitched together from rumor and memory and a dead surveyor's instruments—she would have to be good. Not competent-good, not *runs-cargo-in-weather* good. She would have to be the pilot she'd been twelve years ago, under the other name, the one whose license had died in a hearing room full of men reading findings that had been written before the hearings began. That pilot had been famous for exactly this kind of water. That pilot was also the reason her sister was at the bottom of a different river, and the last time Sable had steered toward that memory instead of away from it, it had cost everything she'd had.

The river above the Narrows was the same river. That was the thing nobody said about grief, how patient it was, how it didn't chase you—it just bought land along your route and waited.

She drank her tea and watched the first stars come out over the headwall, and felt the gambler's clarity settle fully into place, cold and level and strangely comforting, because at least it was honest. The odds were bad. The stakes were a woman's life waiting somewhere upstream in a camp full of cold coffee, and a truth somebody with three barges and a crane wanted buried under a season of ice, and—if she was truthful with herself, and the clarity demanded truthfulness—her own name, which she had buried so deep she sometimes forgot it had ever been spoken aloud.

Ten days. Six tides. One boat that couldn't win a race but might still win a river.

Downstream, the generators hummed. Upstream, the glacier groaned once, long and low, a sound she felt through the deck plates before she heard it through the air—a sound like a door being tested on its hinges.

Sable set down the mug, went below, and began laying out the charts she'd sworn she'd never look at again: the old ones, the hand-drawn ones, the routes from a life under a different name.

If she was going to bet on water nobody else knew, she'd need to remember everything the other woman had known.
