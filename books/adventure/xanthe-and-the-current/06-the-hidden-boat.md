## 6. The Hidden Boat

The drag marks started where the willows thinned, and they were wrong in a way that took me half a mile of walking to name.

I'd left the Marrow anchored below the survey camp with the ranger's investigators still poking through cold coffee and running instruments nobody had turned off, and I'd told them I was going to walk the bank and stretch my legs, which was true the way a knife is true when it's pointed at something. They were map men. They read paper. I read mud, and the mud had been talking since dawn.

The skiff had come out of the water here—no question. The drag marks ran from the gravel shelf up into the willow stand, two shallow furrows with a keel-line between them, and the willows were bent back along the path in a way that said somebody had hauled a boat through them more than once, or taken their time doing it once. But here was what was wrong: the marks didn't fade. A boat dragged in haste leaves a bright scar that heals in a day of wind. These scars were old at the top of the bank and fresh near the water, which meant the boat had gone up dry and come down wet—or up wet and come down wetter—and either way it meant somebody had used this landing twice, quietly, on a river where quiet is a currency most people can't afford.

And there was this: no footprints leaving. The investigators had spent an hour photographing the camp's churned ground, the absence of tracks leading away from those standing tents, and they'd written it down as mystery, as strangeness, as something for a report. But I'd stood where they stood and looked at what they looked at, and I'd seen it differently. The mud around the camp wasn't empty of prints. It was *swept*. Pine boughs dragged in long arcs, the way you'd erase a trail if you knew how trails are read—if you'd grown up reading them, or hiding from someone who did. Somebody hadn't vanished from that camp. Somebody had been erased, or had erased themselves, and had done it with craft.

The skiff in the willows was part of that craft. Hidden, but findable—findable by someone who thought like the hider. Which meant the hider wanted to be found, eventually, by one particular kind of someone, and wanted to be missed by every other kind.

I crouched at the keel-line and put my palm flat in the furrow. Damp came up through my glove. Recent. Within a day, maybe less. The river had risen a finger's width overnight—I'd checked the gauge on the pilothouse post with my coffee—and a rise like that wicks backward into soft ground for a few hours before the sun takes it back down. The damp said last night. The dark said last night too: whoever dragged this boat had done it under cover, working blind, trusting their hands.

I looked upriver toward the Narrows, where the glacier sat behind its shoulder of fog like something sleeping with one eye open, and I thought about six tides. Then I thought about three salvage barges full of crane gear and men who didn't answer questions, sitting at the landing downstream with their generators humming like a hive that hadn't decided yet whether to sting.

Somebody on this river was hiding from those barges.

I went up into the willows following the drag marks, and I kept my boots off the keel-line out of a habit I couldn't have explained to anyone, including myself. Respect, maybe. Or the superstition of pilots, who are all of us half pagan about water and the things water keeps.

* * *

The fish camp announced itself by smell before sight—old diesel soaked into the ground, fish scales silvering the boards of a drying rack that hadn't held fish in years, the sweet-rot breath of a place abandoned mid-life. It sat in a fold of the bank where a creek mouth made a pocket of slack water, screened by alders, invisible from the channel unless you knew to look. I knew to look because I'd run cargo past places like it for twelve years and had learned that the river's real settlements are the ones the charts don't show.

Three structures: a drying shed with its door hanging by one hinge, a bunkhouse sunk to its sills in moss, and a smokehouse built of split cedar gone gray as driftwood. Net floats hung in the alders like pale fruit. A woodstove pipe rose from the smokehouse roof, and—

Smoke. Thin, blue, deliberate. Not enough to warm a room. Enough to boil water.

I stood among the net floats for a long moment and let the arithmetic finish itself. A hidden boat, used twice. A swept trail. A fire small enough to hide. Someone alive, hurt or close to it, holed up in a ruin, watching the river for the wrong boats and waiting—for what, I couldn't say—for the right one.

Every sensible thing in me said go back to the Marrow, cast off, and let the rangers and the salvage fleet and the whole tangled mess of the upper river sort itself without me. Sensible had kept me alive for twelve years. Sensible had a name, actually—it was called never taking passengers, never taking sides, never taking on anyone else's weather—and it had served me the way a good hull serves: invisibly, until you needed it not to fail.

But I'd counted miles instead of years for a long time now, and counting miles means you never have to stop moving, and stopping was exactly what my feet refused to do. They carried me across the creek mouth on the stones, quiet as the person who'd swept that trail, and up to the smokehouse door.

I knocked. Two knuckles, twice, on cedar. Loud enough to be heard. Soft enough to be a choice.

Nothing. The smoke kept rising.

"I'm not with the barges," I said. "I'm not with the rangers. I run a boat called the Marrow, and I found your skiff in the willows, and I know your trail's swept, which means you're better at this than most people, so you already know I could've brought company and didn't." I paused. The river moved behind me, patient. "Whoever you're hiding from is downstream, and they've got cranes."

A long silence. Long enough that I started composing my retreat. Then, from inside, a voice like paper being torn slowly: "How many tides?"

The question caught me sideways. Not *who are you*. Not *are they coming*. Tides—the currency of the glacial reach, the countdown everyone above the Narrows lived by once the hydrology dispatch went out. Whoever was in there had heard the same numbers I had, or had worked them out alone, feverish, from the sound of the ice.

"Six," I said. "Five and a half now."

Another silence, shorter. Then: "The door sticks. Pull left."

* * *

She was curled in the corner farthest from the door, wrapped in a survey-team thermal blanket with the agency crest printed along the border, and she had a fish-gutting knife in her hand—not raised, just held, the way you hold a thing when letting go of it isn't an option anymore. Young. Mid-twenties maybe, though fever had scraped years off her face and left it planed down to angles. Brown hair matted flat. Lips cracked. Eyes that tracked me with a terrible, expensive clarity—the clarity of someone who has spent her remaining strength deciding exactly how much to trust, and has arrived at a number barely above zero.

"You're the captain," she said. "The cargo runner. Marrow."

"News travels."

"The rangers hired you. Everyone at the landing talked about the woman who wouldn't take passengers." She tried to laugh and it became a cough that bent her double, and I saw then how bad it was—the shivering that wasn't from cold, the flush riding high on her cheekbones, the way her right ankle was propped on a rolled blanket and swollen to twice its size, the skin above it streaked with red lines climbing toward the knee.

Blood poisoning. Or headed there. On this river, that was a clock with a faster hand than the glacier's.

"Ilsa Voss," she managed, when the cough released her. "Junior surveyor. Hydrographic." She said it like reciting her own headstone. "You're going to want to know what happened to the others."

"I'm going to want you drinking water first." There was a dented kettle on the stove, and a canvas bag of melted snow beside it. I filled a tin cup from the bag—cold, but clean—and crouched to put it in her hands, keeping well outside the arc of that knife, which she hadn't lowered and which I respected completely. "Slow. Small sips."

She drank like it was a task assigned to her, obedient and mechanical, and I understood she'd been rationing herself hard—there was plenty of snow-melt, and she'd been drinking almost none of it, which told me she hadn't trusted herself to stay conscious while she fetched it. Alone, hurt, in a ruin, listening to the river for engines.

"How long have you been here?"

"Two nights. Three?" Her brow folded. "The fever makes it slippery. I came down from the camp the first night, after—" She stopped. The knife shifted in her grip. "After I decided staying there would kill me faster than the walk."

"The camp was swept. Pine boughs over the ground. That was you?"

"And Ansen. Dr. Ansen taught us all trail-craft his way—said a survey team that can't hide its own tracks has no business finding anyone else's." Something moved through her face at his name, some current too fast and deep for me to read, and then it was gone under the fever again. "We split. That's the short version. The team split, and I took the skiff, and I hid the skiff, and I walked until my ankle—" She gestured at it with her chin, economical even in sickness. "Then I crawled. Then I stopped."

"What split the team?"

Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, and for a moment the fever burned away entirely and I was looking at something level and frightened and absolutely certain, and I recognized it, God help me, because I'd worn that exact expression for twelve years in mirrors.

"The sonar," she said. "What the sonar found. Half the team wanted to publish it and half of them—" Her voice dropped, went rough. "Half of them stopped wanting to publish it after certain people came upriver and made calls. And then it stopped being about publishing and started being about who was allowed to leave the valley knowing it. And then Ansen disappeared into the hills with the data drives, and Rook and Chen sided with the people making calls, and I ran, because I'm not brave, Captain, I'm just—" the cough took her again, wracked her, left her gray-lipped and shaking— "I'm just hard to kill, apparently."

I sat back on my heels on the filthy floorboards of a dead man's smokehouse and felt the shape of it settle over me like weather. A survey team broken from the inside. Data carried into the hills. Men downstream with cranes and unanswered questions, and a foreman whose offer had the texture of a threat. Six tides. And between me and all of it, a fevered girl with a gutting knife who wouldn't say who she was hiding from, only *what*—and the what was big enough that she'd chosen a rotting fish camp over her own camp beds.

"Who's making the calls?" I asked. "Names."

"No." Flat. Immediate. Even feverish, she was quick. "You'll take it to the rangers, or worse, you'll take it to Hale, and I can't—" She pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth. "Please. I didn't ask you to come in here. You knocked. I need water and I need my ankle wrapped and I need a boat out of this valley before the ice closes, and I will pay you, the agency covers charter fees, I'll sign anything—but I'm not saying names to anyone until I'm somewhere with walls and a lawyer."

It was, I had to admit, the most sensible thing anyone had said to me in weeks.

"I don't take passengers," I said.

"I know." A ghost of a smile, cracked lips stretching painfully. "Everyone knows. That's why I flagged you in my head, three days ago, when we were still a team and I was still planning exits. The woman who doesn't take passengers doesn't sell them either."

* * *

I wrapped the ankle because there was no one else to do it and because watching sepsis set in on a living person is a thing I've done once in my life and sworn off. I boiled water over her objection—she worried about the smoke, and she was right to worry, and I built the fire down to coals anyway and kept the kettle low. I found sulfamethoxazole in the survey kit she'd carried out of camp in a waterproof bag, along with a field notebook wrapped in oilcloth that she watched me notice and then watched me not ask about, and I gave her the pills with the tin cup and made her eat half a tin of the smoked salmon still hanging in the racks, old but sound, the last ghost of the family that had worked this camp.

Through all of it she talked in fragments, fever-loosened, catching herself and going silent, then leaking again. Fragments only. A town under the water—that much she gave me, staring at the smokehouse wall as if the grain of the cedar were a chart. *Kettle Landing*, she said once, and then pressed her lips white and said nothing more for ten minutes. Sonar returns that shouldn't exist. A lead surveyor named Ansen who'd gone up into the hills with everything. And underneath it all, threaded through every fragment like wire through cloth: fear. Not of the river. Of people.

I should have left her there. Every rule I owned said leave her there—call it in anonymously from the next landing, let the rangers come up with a medevac, keep the Marrow light and fast and answerable to no one. Six tides. A salvage fleet that wanted whatever was in that sonar data buried badly enough to make calls. A survivor who was the only loose thread, sitting in a ruin with a knife, and me kneeling next to her with my name—my safe, buried, rebuilt name—known to everyone at the landing.

If Hale's people found her first, she wouldn't reach a hospital. I knew that the way I knew sandbars: without proof, without argument, in the marrow. Some knowledge isn't reasoning. It's just the river telling you what's under the surface, and after enough years you stop doubting it.

"Here's what happens," I said finally, crouching to gather the wrapping roll. "I carry you down to the Marrow tonight, after dark, using your own swept trail so we don't cut a new one. You ride to the ranger station at Milepost 40, you see a doctor, and you tell the rangers whatever you're willing to tell them. Then you're off my boat and it never happened."

"That's not what happens," she said.

I looked at her.

"That's what you're offering because you want to believe you can put me down at Milepost 40 and wash your hands." Her voice was thin but it didn't waver, and I hated her a little for the clarity. "Rook and Chen are still out there. The people making calls are still downstream. If I surface at a ranger station with a story and no evidence, I'm a feverish junior surveyor with heatstroke and a concussion, and by the time anyone believes me, the ice will have closed and the barges will have done whatever barges do with cranes over drowned towns." She pulled the blanket tighter. "You found my skiff, Captain. Nobody finds that skiff unless they read water the way I needed them to. I didn't hide that boat for the rangers."

The smokehouse was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the creek talked to itself over the stones, and farther off, the big river moved with that sound it makes at night, like a crowd murmuring in another room.

"Six tides," I said.

"Five and a half."

"And you can't walk on that ankle."

"No."

"And I don't take passengers."

"No," she agreed softly. "But you already broke the rule. You just haven't signed the paperwork." She held out the tin cup for more water, and her hand shook, and her eyes didn't. "Ansen's up there in the hills with the only copy of the truth, the ice is closing from the top, and the men with cranes are coming up from the bottom. Whatever you decide, Captain, decide it fast. The river doesn't wait for people who are counting miles."

I took the cup. I filled it. And out on the darkening water, the Marrow swung gently at her anchor, riding light and free and answerable to no one—for a few more hours, anyway.
