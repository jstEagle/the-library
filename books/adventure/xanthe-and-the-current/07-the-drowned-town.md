## 7. The Drowned Town

The fever came in tides.

That was the first thing Ilsa Voss understood about it, lying in the fish camp with a wool blanket that smelled of diesel and old salmon: the fever did not rise and fall so much as arrive and withdraw, like water against a shingle beach, and each time it withdrew it took something with her—her certainty about which year it was, the name of the ranger who had hired them, once, horribly, her mother's face. And each time it returned, it brought the river.

Not the river as it was now. The river as the sonar had drawn it.

She had spent eleven nights in the survey boat's cabin watching that image build itself on the screen, pixel by patient pixel, while Dr. Ansen stood behind her chair breathing through his nose in the particular rhythm he used when he was trying not to hope. The transducer sang its thin song into the green-black water below the Narrows, and the water answered, and the answer assembled itself on the screen in lines of gold and shadow: a street grid. A grid, at forty meters, under sixty years of silt and current, laid out neat as a hymnal.

"That's not a wreck field," Ansen had said. "Wreck fields don't have sidewalks."

Ilsa had zoomed until the resolution broke apart, then backed off one increment, and there it was again—the long straight line of what had been Front Street, the square shadows of foundations set shoulder to shoulder, the pale signature of a bridge abutment, and rising from all of it, impossibly, stubbornly, the hard vertical echo that could only be masonry. A tower. A church steeple still standing after six decades underwater, because whoever built it had built it to outlast the congregation.

"Kettle Landing," she said, before she knew she was going to say it.

Ansen went very quiet. Then he laughed—one short, unguarded bark of a laugh, the kind she'd never heard from him—and said, "You know your history."

"I know my grandmother's stories." Her grandmother had been born two valleys over, but the old women traded grievances the way they traded seed potatoes, and Kettle Landing's grievance was the biggest one in the basin. *They drowned it,* her grandmother used to say, stirring the pot, not looking at anyone. *Drowned it with the bells in the tower.* Ilsa had assumed, the way children assume, that this was a figure of speech. That the town had emptied first. That everyone had been paid, or persuaded, or moved along, the way people always were.

The sonar said otherwise. Not yet—not on that eleventh night—but soon.

* * *

The fever took her back there now, in the fish camp, under the blanket, with rain starting on the tin roof in a rhythm like fingers testing a lock.

In the dream-memory she was back in the cabin, and the screen was building the town again, but this time the pixels were slow and deliberate, assembling one house at a time, and each house came with a sound—the fever supplied sounds—a door closing, a kettle set on a stove, a child counting skip-stones. She watched a schoolhouse resolve out of static. She watched a dance hall. She watched, with the terrible clarity that fever grants, the moment Ansen pulled the sealed-file printout from his waterproof case and spread it across the chart table under the lamp, and the lamp light fell across the red stamp, and she read it upside down the way you learn to read documents when you work for a government hydrology office:

**RECLASSIFIED — HYDROLOGIC NECESSITY — DO NOT CIRCULATE — BY ORDER OF THE BASIN AUTHORITY, 1961.**

"Basin Authority doesn't exist anymore," she'd said.

"The Basin Authority never existed," Ansen said. "That's the point. It was created to sign this order and dissolved within the year. Look at the signatures. Two of the men who signed this went on to run the dam commission for thirty years. One of them has a reservoir named after him."

The paper was brittle at the folds. Someone had folded it small enough to fit in a coat pocket and carried it that way for a long time—through years, through careers, through whatever private weather a man walks through when he carries a thing like that. Ansen smoothed it flat with both hands, gently, the way you'd smooth a wound dressing.

"There were holdouts," he said. "You've heard the stories. Everyone's heard the stories, and everyone assumes they're stories. Evacuation completed ahead of schedule. Compensation disbursed. Townsite cleared." He tapped the sonar screen, where the ghost of Front Street lay in its gold lines. "Cleared means cleared of structures. It does not say cleared of people. And here—" his finger moved to a line item near the bottom, an expenditure entry, dry as dust, "—here is a line item for 'post-clearance site security,' billed monthly, for fourteen months after the official evacuation date."

Ilsa remembered how the cabin had felt then. The little heater ticking. The river pressing on the hull like a hand. Fourteen months. She had done the arithmetic without wanting to: fourteen months of security patrols on a townsite that officially contained nothing worth securing.

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I requested the original evacuation manifests under a routine records audit, and the request came back denied in nine days by an office that normally takes nine weeks, and the denial cited a statute that was repealed in 1974." Ansen looked at her, and his eyes behind the salt-cracked glasses were not the eyes of a careful bureaucrat anymore. They were lit from somewhere below, like the water over the town. "Someone is still watching this file, Ms. Voss. Sixty years later, someone is still standing on the lid."

* * *

The fever rose. The rain on the roof became hail, became the drumming of rotor wash, became—no, that wasn't memory, that was invention, the fever inventing helicopters that had never come. Or had they? In the last week at the survey camp there had been a plane overhead twice, high, circling, and Ansen had stood outside looking up at it with his hands in his pockets and said nothing, and his saying nothing had frightened her more than any words.

Between the tides of heat she surfaced in the fish camp and took inventory the way Ansen had taught her: water, yes; the dented thermos, half full; the window, boarded except for one slat showing gray river-light; her ankle, swollen and hot where the rocks had taken it during the run upstream. Somewhere out there was the Marrow, and the pilot woman with the gray eyes who had found her drag marks in the mud and followed them like a sentence to its end. Sable Reyes. Ilsa held onto the name the way you hold onto a rail in rough water. The woman had not asked enough questions. That either meant she was safe, or that her questions were coming later, with interest.

Then the tide came back and took her under again, and she was in the survey cabin on the worst night, the night everything turned.

Ansen had called a meeting of the full team. Eight of them around the chart table, the sonar running on standby, its screen glowing like a hearth. He had spent three days drafting the disclosure package—the sonar mosaic, the sealed order, the expenditure records, the manifest denials—addressed to the provincial archive, the university, and two newspapers whose editors he had known since graduate school. He laid it out page by page. He was not a dramatic man. He presented evidence the way other men dealt cards, flat and quick, and when he finished he said only: "We go public Friday. I wanted you to hear it from me first, and I wanted witnesses to the chain of custody. Sign the log if you're willing to attest."

And Rennick—she would remember Rennick's face as long as she lived, fever or no fever, maybe longer—Rennick, the senior tech, the one who taught her to calibrate the transducer, Rennick didn't reach for the pen. He reached for his coffee, and drank, and set the cup down with a small precise click, and said, "Tommy. Walk with me a minute."

They walked. She wasn't supposed to hear it, but the cabin was small and the generator was loud in the wrong places, and voices carry over water the way grief carries over years. She heard Rennick say, low and level, "There's a man at the landing offering triple rates for salvage support. Corven Hale. You know who's behind Hale? Because I do, Tommy. I did the diligence when we took the contract money, same as you should have. Wray Odell. Assistant administrator, Basin Authority, 1961. Signature number three on that order you've been carrying around in your breast pocket like a love letter."

Silence. The generator ticked.

"He's not sending lawyers, Tommy. Lawyers are what he sends when he wants something contested. This is the other thing. This is the thing he sends when he wants something *finished*. Your disclosure goes out Friday, and by Friday night every copy is ash and every person who attested to it is a liability, and I am telling you this because I like you, and because I have a daughter, and because I am asking you—as a friend—to let the town stay drowned."

Another silence, longer. When Ansen finally spoke, his voice had gone somewhere she'd never heard it go: down past anger, past fear, into a flat cold country underneath both.

"You've met him."

A pause that lasted a year.

"Rennick. You've already met him."

"We had coffee," Rennick said. "Two weeks ago, at the fuel dock. He was very reasonable."

* * *

The fever gave her the rest in fragments, the way a river gives up a wreck in pieces after a flood—here a door, there a window frame, never the whole house at once.

Fragment: Ansen burning nothing. Ansen instead copying everything, three sets, hiding them apart, murmuring to himself the way he did when he was working a problem—"redundancy, redundancy"—and pressing the field notebook with its coded marginalia into her hands because she was the youngest and the fastest and, he said, the only one of them Rennick didn't watch.

Fragment: the crew splitting like ice in spring, silently, along a seam nobody marked. Marsh and Okonkwo suddenly taking their meals at the far tent. Deverell packing his gear a day early with a story about a sick mother that no one believed and no one challenged. The camp getting quieter and quieter until the silence itself was a kind of alarm.

Fragment: waking at 3 a.m. to the sound of the survey boat's engine turning over—someone leaving in the night, or someone positioning the boat, she never learned which—and looking out of her tent to see a lantern moving along the bank where no lantern should be, moving in a search pattern, slow and methodical, sweeping the willow line.

Fragment: running. God, the running. The drag marks she'd left hauling the skiff into the willows, hating herself for every meter of mud she churned, knowing that a hidden boat was a confession, that a clean disappearance might have been survivable and a hunted one was not—but she couldn't leave the skiff on the open bank like a signpost, and she couldn't take it, because the river above the Narrows was already beginning to speak in the voice it speaks before it closes, that deep grinding mutter of ice shifting in its sleep, and she was not the pilot Sable Reyes was. She knew that even then. She had watched Reyes handle a boat exactly once, a year ago, at the fuel dock, in wind that had every other captain tied off, and she had thought: *there is a person who has made peace with the water.* She hadn't known then that the water had also made demands of that peace. Everyone's peace had a price tag. Hers did too, now.

Fragment, the last one, the one the fever returned to most often because it was the one she could not put down: Ansen's hand on her shoulder at the willow line, his glasses fogged, his voice steady in a way she understood now was not calm but decision worn so long it had gone smooth. "The cache is on the gravel bar below the twin snags. Notebook's the key. If I don't come, the work comes. Promise me the work comes." And her, crying without dignity, saying yes, yes, and him pushing the skiff off into the current and standing there in the reeds getting smaller, a man alone on a bank with a drowned town beneath him and a living secret above, and the fog closing over both.

He had said *if I don't come.* Not *if I don't make it.* Ansen did not waste words, and she had lain in the dark of the fish camp for six nights turning that verb over, and the fever had finally done her the mercy—or the cruelty—of showing her why he'd chosen it: because Ansen had already decided that if the choice came between coming back and staying with the proof, he would stay with the proof. He was not planning to survive Friday. He was planning to be right.

* * *

She surfaced near dawn, soaked in sweat, the blanket heavy as wet rope, and for a long time she lay still and listened to the river.

It sounded different today. Lower, somehow, and more urgent—the voice of water being drawn down toward some appointment. Calving season. She'd grown up on these rivers; she knew what that grind-and-hush meant. The glacier above the Narrows was clearing its throat, and when it finished, the route would open for a handful of tides and then seal like a scar.

Which meant the fleet would move. Which meant Hale would move. Which meant that somewhere downriver, right now, a man who had once had coffee with Rennick was deciding whether a feverish junior surveyor in a fish camp was worth a detour.

Ilsa pushed herself up on one elbow, and the camp swam, and steadied. Through the gap in the boards, the river ran gray-green and fast, carrying ice-cream clouds reflected from a sky she couldn't see. Somewhere downstream was the Marrow. Somewhere downstream was a woman who had looked at her drag marks and understood them instantly for what they were—not accident, but message. *Someone hid this boat. Someone is hiding.*

Her grandmother's voice rose out of the fever's last retreat, out of a kitchen sixty years and two valleys away, stirring the pot: *Drowned it with the bells in the tower.* And beneath it, newer, colder, Ansen's voice: *the work comes.*

The notebook was still against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her body, heavier than paper had any right to be. Every coded line in it pointed downriver—to the gravel bar, to the cache, to the truth sitting in its waterproof case like a coffin waiting for a funeral that sixty years of powerful men had refused to hold.

Ilsa got her boots on. It took a long time and cost more than she had, but she got them on, because the tide was going out and the river was opening, and whatever came next, the work came next.

Outside, far downstream, faint under the water-sound, she heard it: the deep-throated hum of a big diesel, steady and unhurried, coming upriver.

Not the Marrow. The Marrow she would have known.

The fever had one more gift to give her, and it saved it for last: the sudden, total, ice-water certainty that the engine she was hearing belonged to a boat that had come looking for her specifically—and that the woman with the gray eyes was going to walk back into this camp at exactly the wrong moment, or exactly the right one, and Ilsa was going to have to decide, with a quarter-mile of water and a lifetime of silence between them, how much of the drowned town to raise.
