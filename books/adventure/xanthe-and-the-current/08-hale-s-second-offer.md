## 8. Hale's Second Offer

The fuel dock at Grayling Landing smelled of diesel and creosote and the sweet rot of willow leaves caught in the pilings. Sable had been topping off the Marrow since before dawn, working the hose alone, wiping the nozzle with a rag gone stiff from a hundred such mornings. The river ran low and glassy under the first real cold, carrying down a scum of glacial flour so fine it looked like milk stirred into tea, and the far bank stood in a haze that might have been fog or might have been the glacier's breath reaching all the way down to her.

She liked fuel days. They were simple arithmetic: gallons in, miles out, the boat heavier and happier, her ledger squared. She was halfway through that arithmetic, pencil tucked behind her ear, when she heard boots on the planking behind her and knew before she turned that they were not ranger boots or fisherman boots. They were the boots of a man who had never once hurried and never once needed to.

"Captain Reyes."

Corven Hale stood at the head of the dock with his hands in the pockets of a canvas coat too clean for the country, watching her the way a man watches water he intends to own. Behind him, out at the landing, the three salvage barges sat heavy on their lines, cranes folded like sleeping insects, their generators muttering under the hull-noise of the river. Men moved on the decks without urgency. They didn't need urgency. The river was doing their waiting for them.

"Mr. Hale." She went back to the hose. "Fuel dock's for customers."

"I'm told you're the only pilot on this stretch who's run the Narrows above the Kestrel and lived to file a report." He came closer, unhurried, his reflection sliding along the Marrow's black hull. "That's a rare commodity. Rare things get priced."

"Rare things also get left alone."

"Sometimes." He smiled, and it was a good smile, built for boardrooms and hearings and men who shook hands with cameras in the room. "I'd like to buy your wheel for six tides. Pilot my fleet through the Narrows to the survey site and back. Name your number, and I'll write it on a line and never blink."

Sable shut the valve and coiled the hose with the slow economy of a woman buying time. The diesel smell rose around her, warm and chemical, and beneath it the river's cold mineral breath, and between those two smells she thought clearly, the way she always thought clearly when her body was busy.

"You've got barges that draw six feet," she said. "The Narrows at calving season draws whatever it feels like drawing that day. You don't need a pilot. You need a priest."

"I need someone who reads water the way you do." He crouched then, unhurried, so that they were nearly level, and his voice dropped into something softer and far worse than the smile. "I did my research, Captain. I make a practice of it. Before I spend money, I like to know exactly what I'm buying."

"You're not buying anything."

"Eleven years ago," Hale said, "there was a licensed river pilot on this system named Sabine Reyes."

The name went through her like a fishhook through the hand—small entry, terrible depth. She kept her face still. She had practiced that stillness for twelve years, on landings and in hearing rooms and in the long dark of the Marrow's cabin, and she had thought it was armor. It was not armor. It was just the habit of a drowning woman pretending to float.

"Common name," she said.

"Common enough." He let the silence do its work. "That pilot lost her license on a falsified incident report. A survey run above the Kestrel. A drowning. The findings said pilot error—reckless navigation in known ice conditions. The findings were written by a hydrology board that had already decided what they needed them to say, and the pilot was too broken to fight it. She walked off the river, changed the spelling of her name, and bought a cargo boat, and for twelve years she has kept no fixed address, taken no passengers, and filed no route, because a woman with no route leaves no trail."

The pencil behind Sable's ear felt suddenly loud, as if he could hear it. She took it down and put it in her pocket, a small motion, the only one she'd allow him.

"You've been busy," she said.

"I'm thorough." Hale stood. "Here's what I know that the board didn't. The incident report was falsified from the top—someone needed a pilot to blame, and your sister's survey was inconvenient to a man with a career built on the old dam. You weren't reckless, Captain. You were sacrificed. And you've spent twelve years paying for a crime that was committed against you."

The river made its small sounds against the pilings. Somewhere up the bank a raven said one flat word and thought better of a second. Sable looked at Hale's clean coat, his clean hands, and felt the old anger rise in her the way silt rises when a hull grounds—not dramatic, not sudden, just the whole riverbed lifting at once into the water.

"And if I believe you've done your homework," she said, "what's the offer? You'll make it vanish. That's the shape of this, isn't it. The report, the license, the name. All of it, gone, if I drive your barges through the Narrows."

"I can have the original findings unsealed and expunged inside a month. I know the people who hold them. I can restore your license under your true name—Sabine Reyes, master pilot, cleared of all findings. I can give you back the river you lost." He said it gently, the way a man offers a rope to someone in the water, and like a rope it could pull you out or pull you under, depending on who held the other end. "All I want is six tides of your skill. That's the whole price. Six tides, and the twelve years stop mattering."

* * *

She made herself walk the dock. It was the only way to keep her hands from shaking—give them work. She checked the Marrow's mooring lines, though she'd tied them herself an hour ago and the knots were good. She checked the fuel caps. She watched the water slide past the pilings, brown-green and patient, carrying the glacier's milk down toward a sea that would never taste it.

Twelve years. She had built a life the way you build a weir in a flood current—stick by stick, in the eddy behind her own silence. No passengers. No route. No name that could be looked up. She had told herself it was freedom, and some mornings it almost was. She had counted miles because miles asked nothing of her; a mile was a mile, spent and gone. A year demanded an accounting, and the accounting had a line in it she could not bear to read: *the year the river took Maren, and the river was not what took her.*

And here was a man on a fuel dock offering to hand her the accounting, closed and stamped, in exchange for six tides.

That was the trap, and she saw it with the same cold clarity she used on a bar at low water: the offer wasn't the license. The offer was the name. *Sabine.* A woman who had drowned so completely that even Sable Reyes sometimes doubted she'd ever existed. Hale wasn't buying her wheel. He was buying the proof that she had something left to lose—and then holding it over her forever, because a man like Corven Hale did not make debts vanish. He refinanced them.

She thought about Ilsa Voss, feverish in the fish camp upriver, hiding from men whose boat had been hauled into the willows by hands that knew how to hide things. She thought about the cold coffee in the survey tents, the instruments still running, the mud with no footprints leaving it. Six people. She thought about the sonar, whatever it had found, that had made a survey crew turn on its own lead.

"You know what I did after the hearing?" she said, not turning around. "I ran cargo. Twelve years of cargo. Seed sacks and fence posts and fuel drums and once an entire wedding party, though I made them ride on deck. And every spring, when the ice went out, I'd stand at the wheel and watch the Narrows open and think: *this year I'll run it. This year I'll go up and see what's there.* And every year I'd turn south instead, because going up meant going back, and I promised myself the river could have everything except the past."

Hale said nothing. He was good at that too.

"You're not offering me my name back," she said. "You're offering to sell it to me. And the price goes up every time I look at you. I've known men like you on the water, Mr. Hale. You don't buy pilots. You buy the moment a pilot needs you, and you own the wheel ever after."

"The offer stands until the tide window closes." His voice had cooled by a single degree, the way a river cools a foot below the surface where the sun doesn't reach. "After that, I find another way through, and the offer dies, and whatever happens at that wreck site happens without anyone clean watching. You should think about who's up there, Captain. Think about what a crew does when it splits over a find. Think about who walked out of that camp, and who didn't, and what kind of man it takes to make six people disappear off a river with no footprints."

"I think about that every day since I saw the tents."

"Then think about this." He stepped closer, and now there was nothing soft in him at all; the smile was gone and what was underneath was smooth and gray as ballast stone. "I know where you moor at night. I know your boat draws three foot two and burns forty gallons a day and that you carry a flare gun with two shells left. I know you've been seen at the fish camp, and I know the girl is alive, and I know you've been feeding her. That's not an accusation, Captain. That's just what thoroughness looks like from the other side."

The dock seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way it did when a heavy wake came through—except there was no wake. The water was glass. It was only the ground of her moving, the twelve-year ground, the whole quiet architecture of her hidden life being read aloud by a stranger like a chart he'd had drawn without her knowing.

"Leave the girl out of this," Sable said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, roughened at the bottom like a hull that had kissed gravel.

"There she is," Hale said quietly. "There's the pilot they falsified the report about. That's not a cargo captain's voice. That's a woman standing at a wheel in ice." He straightened his coat. "Six tides, Captain Reyes. Sabine. Whichever name you answer to. My fleet runs the Narrows, you're a licensed pilot with a clean record and a bank account that doesn't fit in a boat. You don't, and I run it anyway, and you spend the rest of your life knowing what came out of that valley because you wanted to keep your little boat and your little secrets."

He turned and walked back up the dock, unhurried, his boots sounding the planks like slow clockwork, and the barges out on the water sat exactly where they'd sat, patient as ice.

* * *

She did not go into the cabin. She stayed on the dock until his launch carried him back across the channel, and then she stayed longer, because her legs had opinions and none of them were trustworthy. The cold came up through the planking and into her boots, honest cold, and she was grateful for it. Honest things helped.

The falsified report. She had not said that phrase aloud in twelve years, and hearing it in Hale's mouth had been like hearing a dead woman's name called in a market—every head turning, every face wrong. The board had read their findings in a room with bad coffee and worse light, and she had sat there with her hands flat on the table because if she didn't hold them down they'd go for someone's throat. *Pilot error. Reckless navigation in known ice conditions.* Maren had gone into the water off the starboard bow where the chart said eight feet and the water had four, and Sable had been at the wheel, and that was the only true sentence in the whole document. Everything else had been written before she walked in.

She had been twenty-six. She had appealed, and the appeal had gone into a drawer, and the drawer had belonged to a hydrology office whose director's career was built on the old dam, though she hadn't known that then—she'd only known that doors kept closing with a smoothness that felt less like bureaucracy than like weather. So she had done what the river taught her to do in flood: stop fighting the current, find the eddy, hold.

Twelve years in the eddy.

And now a man had come along who knew the eddy was there, and was offering, for a price, to let her back into the current.

The terrible thing—the thing she turned over and over as the light went long and copper on the water—was that some part of her wanted it. Not the license. Not the money. The name. *Sabine.* To hear it said in a room and have it mean a pilot instead of a verdict. To have the finding torn up, the sentence commuted, the twelve years retroactively changed from exile into something she'd merely survived. She wanted it the way a drowned town wants the water to drop. And that wanting was exactly what Hale had bought and paid for before he ever walked onto her dock, because men like Hale did not offer you things you wanted. They offered you things you *needed*, and then they kept the receipt.

She looked up at the Narrows, far up the valley, where the glacier hung above the river like a held breath. Six tides, maybe less. The hydrology dispatch had said it plainly: calving season, the window opening and then slamming shut until spring. Whoever held the wreck site when the ice came down would hold it alone.

If she took his offer, she'd pilot the fleet to Ilsa. To Ansen, if Ansen lived. She'd deliver the wolves to the door and call it a fare.

If she refused, he'd run the Narrows without her, or find another pilot, or do whatever men did who could make six people vanish from a riverbank—and she'd spend the rest of her life in the eddy, safe and named nothing, knowing.

There was a third thing, of course. There was always a third thing on a river; the charts only ever showed two channels, and the river always had a third. But the third thing would cost her everything she'd built to hide behind, and she stood on the fuel dock a long time, watching the glacier's milk slide past her hull toward the sea, measuring that cost the way she measured a bar: not by whether it could be crossed, but by what it would take off her keel.

Behind her, in the Marrow's cabin, the radio hissed and muttered to itself, and somewhere upriver a feverish surveyor was waiting on her, and the light was going.

She picked up the pencil from her pocket and, in the ledger, in the column where the day's miles went, she wrote nothing at all. She just held it, the way you hold a wheel when you've decided, and the decision hadn't reached your hands yet but was coming down your arms like current.

Across the channel, on the lead barge, a crane unfolded one slow joint, testing, like something waking up.

They weren't going to wait for her answer. And from the way the deck crews had begun to move—no urgency, never any urgency, but all of them moving now, all in the same direction—she understood that Corven Hale had not come to the fuel dock to make her an offer at all.

He'd come to tell her how much time she had left.
