## 9. Refusal at Low Water

The fuel dock at Grayling Landing sat on pilings that had been straight when Sable first tied up there, seven years ago, and had been leaning ever since, like old men listening to a story they didn't believe. She came down the Marrow's gangplank with her hands in her coat pockets and the diesel receipt folded in her breast pocket, and Corven Hale was waiting for her under the tin awning, alone, which was itself an answer. Men like Hale did not stand alone in the open unless they wanted you to know they weren't afraid of you.

"Captain Reyes." He said it pleasantly. He was holding two paper cups of coffee from the landing's one diner, and he offered her one, and she took it, because refusing small courtesies was how you told a man like him where your flinches lived. The coffee was terrible and scalding and she drank it anyway.

"You've had time to consider my offer," he said.

"I've had time to run cargo," she said. "Consideration takes longer."

"Not this time." He turned his cup slowly, watching the surface. Behind him the river ran low and glassy-brown, the gravel bars showing their spines, the water holding its breath before the glacier gave. "Six tides, Captain. You know it as well as I do. After that, the Narrows seals until spring, and whatever's up there stays up there, and everyone goes home having wasted a season." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were patient and gray and entirely without warmth. "I'd rather not waste the season. I'm offering you more money for six days' work than this boat grosses in two years. All I want is your hands on the wheel."

"My hands are spoken for."

"By what?"

"By me."

He smiled at that, a small closed smile, the kind a man wears when he's setting down a piece on a board. "You know," he said, "when I took this contract, I did my homework. That's the job—homework. And homework on the Kestrel is interesting reading. There was a pilot once, years back, ran survey work above the Narrows. Lost a boat. Lost a crew member. Lost her license over it, per the findings." He let that sit between them like a stone dropped into still water. "The findings were thorough. Signed, stamped, filed. Very official."

Sable kept her face still. Somewhere behind her ribs, twelve years old and drowning, something turned over.

"Here's the thing about findings," Hale went on, conversational now, almost gentle. "They can be revisited. Reopened. A woman named Reyes applies for reinstatement, and some clerk pulls the old file, and who knows what comes out of it. Or—" he spread his free hand, palm up, an open door, "—a woman pilots three barges through the Narrows, does honest work, gets paid honestly, and nobody anywhere has any reason to go digging through twelve-year-old paperwork. Files stay shut. Names stay buried. Everybody keeps exactly what they've got." He sipped his coffee. "It's a good offer, Captain. It's the only offer. And it expires when the tide does."

The river said nothing. The river never said anything; that was why she trusted it. People filled silence with their own wants. Water just moved.

"You're threatening me," she said.

"I'm *contextualizing* you." He set his cup down on the piling, precise as a surgeon. "Threats are crude. I don't make threats. I describe situations, and reasonable people respond to situations." He stepped closer, close enough that she could smell the coffee on him and under it something industrial, machine oil and cold metal. "You're going to say no right now. I can see it coming, the way you'd see a snag under flat water. So let me tell you what happens after no. Nothing. Nothing happens. No men come to your boat. No phone calls. Your lines hold, your fuel holds, your engine turns over tomorrow morning like it always does. And if any of those things *fail* to be true—if anything goes wrong with the Marrow, anything at all—it'll be wear, or weather, or vandals, or the river being the river. Rivers do things, Captain. Everyone knows that." He picked up his cup again. "But they won't. Because you'll have thought it through by then. You'll have realized that six tides is not enough time to fight me and reach the wreck, and a smart captain picks her water."

"And if I don't pick yours?"

Hale looked past her, at the Marrow riding at her moorings in the last of the light, her hull dark against the darkening water, and he looked at her the way a man looks at a tool he intends to use and then put away.

"Then you're cargo," he said, "and cargo waits."

He walked away up the dock without hurrying, and the boards barely creaked under him, and Sable stood a long time with the bad coffee going cold in her hand, watching the current slide past the pilings, low and slow and full of silt, the river telling her nothing she didn't already know: that the water was dropping, the window was opening, and every hour she spent standing still was an hour given away.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She told herself it was the tide tables, and she did work them, twice, by lamplight in the wheelhouse, but the truth was simpler and uglier: she was listening. Every hour or so she came out onto the deck with the lantern hooded and stood in the cold and listened to the landing breathe—the generator hum from the salvage barges across the channel, steady as a hive; the lap of water against her own hull; once, far off, a loon crying its broken-hinge cry. Nothing else. No footsteps on her deck. No boat crossing the black water toward her lines.

That was the worst of it. Not the menace but the patience of it. Hale hadn't needed to send anyone tonight, because he'd already sent the only thing he needed: the sentence, installed in her skull, running on its own power. *Anything that goes wrong will be the river.* She lay in her bunk staring at the overhead and understood that she had been boarded after all—not her decks, but her judgment. He'd gotten inside the wheelhouse of her head and put his hand on her throttle, and every sound in the night now arrived pre-accused.

Toward morning she finally slept, an hour, maybe two, and dreamed of green water and a church steeple and woke with her sister's name in her mouth like a stone.

She was on deck with the first gray light, coffee-less, moving through her pre-departure ritual with the mechanical calm she'd built over twenty years of mornings—and it was Ilsa Voss, still half-feverish, wrapped in a blanket in the wheelhouse doorway, who saw her stop.

"What?" Ilsa said. Her voice was rough. "What is it?"

Sable didn't answer. She was looking at the bow line.

The bow line was still there. It was still cleated off, still led fair through the chock, still bearing against the piling the way a mooring line bears when a boat sits quiet at rest. It was perfect. It was *too* perfect, and Sable's eye, trained on twenty years of reading water and wood and rope, caught what a stranger would never see: the line bore wrong. It hung with a slackness in it that wasn't right, a softness, and when she crossed the deck and took it in her hand and pulled, the fibers parted in her fist with hardly any resistance at all, like wet bread.

Someone had cut it nearly through—three-quarters of the way, deep in the shadow of the chock where no casual eye would find it—and then whipped the cut ends back together with fine waxed twine, stitched and served so neatly that from even ten feet the line looked whole. It would have held through a calm night. It would have held until she cast off into the current with the engine turning and the stern line doing all the work, and then it would have parted, and the Marrow would have swung beam-on into the channel, into the fleet's anchorage or the gravel shallows, and it would have been the river. Rivers do things.

"Sable." Ilsa's voice, sharper now. "What happened."

"Stern line," Sable said, already moving. "Check the stern line."

Ilsa went. Sable heard her boots on the deck, heard her stop, heard the long silence, and then Ilsa came back holding the stern line's end in both hands like something dead. This one hadn't been stitched. This one had simply been cut, clean, sometime in the night, and the loose end had been coiled and hung back on its cleat with a seaman's neatness. Professional. Deniable. Quiet.

And the fuel.

She smelled it before she opened the deck plate—that sweet sharp reek of gasoline where there should have been only diesel—and when she lifted the hatch and looked down into the tank bay, her stomach went cold and stayed there. There was water in the fuel. Not much. A few gallons, poured in slow through the fill cap, sitting in a lens at the bottom of the tank where the pickup line would find it, right around the time she was committed in the middle reach with the current pushing and no margin left. The engine would cough, surge, die. And it would be the river. Contaminated fuel, bad luck, careless handling—everyone knew rivers did things.

Sable stood over the open tank with her hands braced on the coaming and let the humiliation arrive, because there was no sense refusing it passage. It came up through her like floodwater, hot behind the eyes, a taste like copper. He had done it while she listened for footsteps. He had done it with her own rituals as cover, her own predictability as his tool, and he had done it so well that if she reported it—if she called the ranger, filed the complaint, waited for anyone at all to come and look—she would lose the window. Six tides. The first tide was already building somewhere upriver in the long green throat of the Narrows, and by the time a deputy drove out from Grayling and took photographs and wrote a report that would die in a drawer, the ice would be calving and the route would be sealed and Ansen's evidence would sit under forty feet of closing water until spring, if it survived the spring, if the salvage fleet didn't come back with better equipment and finish the erasure.

Report it, and lose everything. Say nothing, and swallow it.

Or—

She looked at the cut line in her hand. Then at the tank. Then at the sky, going from gray to the color of old pewter, the light strengthening by degrees, the river running low and fast and clear over its bars.

Or fix it herself, and run.

* * *

"We need four hours," Sable said.

Isla blinked at her from the wheelhouse doorway, blanket clutched at her throat, fever still burning in her cheeks but her eyes clear underneath it, clear and quick and getting quicker by the day. "For what?"

"Fuel first. I carry Racor filters and spare elements, and there's a hand pump and a drum of clean diesel lashed in the lazarette. We drain the tank low, pump off the water lens, change both filter elements, bleed the lines. Three hours if we're lucky, four if we're honest." Sable was already pulling the lazarette hatch, hauling out the drum, talking with her hands as much as her mouth because the plan was assembling itself faster than sentences could carry it. "The bow line I can serve myself—I keep marline and waxed twine aboard, and whoever cut it used a proper whipping, which means I can put a proper one back. It'll hold. It'll hold better than it held last night."

"And then?"

"Then we run the Narrows. You and me. First tide."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. The fever made her sway slightly, and she gripped the doorframe. "They'll follow you. Hale. He'll put his barges on your wake the moment you show."

"He'll try." Sable straightened, wiped her palms on her thighs. "But here's what he doesn't have, Ilsa. He doesn't have the survey data. He doesn't have the channel the charts don't record. And he doesn't have me." She heard how it sounded—pride, vanity—and found she didn't care. Let it be pride. Pride was fuel too, and hers was clean. "He thinks he bought my fear last night. He thinks I'll either report it and waste my window, or slink off and wait for spring. Those were his two doors. I'm going through the wall."

"The tank first," Ilsa said, and pushed off the doorframe, and rolled up her sleeves. "Tell me what to hold."

They worked through the morning with the sun climbing thin and white behind high cloud, and the work was ugly and exact. They pumped the contaminated fuel into buckets and jugs, gallon by gallon, the reek of it soaking into their skin, and Ilsa knelt on the deck plates with her arm down inside the tank bay wiping the water lens with rags while Sable fed her instructions. They changed both filter elements and found the primaries already fouled, gummed pink with the water-and-diesel emulsion, and Sable felt her jaw tighten at the thoroughness of it—every detail handled, every trace deniable—and bled the injection lines until clean fuel ran clear and the air was out. Ilsa's fever broke somewhere in the second hour, sweat-soaked and shaking and grinning fiercely, holding the bleed wrench aloft like a trophy, and Sable surprised herself by laughing—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh in days, wrung out of her like water from a rag.

The bow line took forty minutes. She stripped the enemy's beautiful whipping, cut back to sound fiber, and served the splice with her own marline, tight and tarred, working the twine with her fingers until they burned. When it was done she held it up to the light and it was, if anything, better than new. *Let it part now,* she thought. *Let anything try.*

Across the channel the salvage barges sat on their anchors, generators humming, cranes folded like sleeping insects. Once, mid-morning, she saw movement on Hale's lead barge—a figure at the rail, binoculars raised, watching the Marrow. Watching her pump fuel and mend rope and do nothing, apparently, but housekeeping. She imagined what he saw: a stubborn woman wasting her window on chores. She raised one hand and waved, slow and deliberate, and the figure lowered the binoculars and did not wave back.

At noon she stood in the wheelhouse with the engine idling below her feet, a clean, strong, even idle, the best sound in the world, and Ilsa beside her with the survey charts spread on the chart table, her finger on the deep unrecorded channel threading the lower Narrows.

"Tide turns in ninety minutes," Ilsa said.

Sable looked downriver, toward the Kestrel, toward the green throat where the water was already beginning to lift and quicken, toward the drowned town and the steeple and the sister-shaped hole in her past that she was, at last, sailing directly toward instead of away from. Hale had taken her lines, her fuel, her night's sleep, and very nearly her name. He had left her exactly one thing, and he'd left it because he couldn't see it, because to a man who bought pilots it was invisible:

He had left her furious. And fury, properly trimmed and steered, was the finest working breeze a captain ever got.

"Cast off the stern," Sable said, and put her hands on the wheel.
