## 10. Night Work

### Sable

The Marrow sat low and wrong in the water, the way a wounded animal sits—still, but with everything inside rearranged. Sable knelt in the engine bay with a lantern hooked on a pipe overhead and listened to the silence where her engine's heartbeat should have been. Diesel in the bilge. A fuel line opened and left to bleed. The intake screen packed with river mud, packed deliberately, by hands that had wanted it to look like neglect.

Corven Hale's work. She'd known before she climbed down the ladder.

Across the channel, the salvage fleet glowed like a small insolent town. Three barges lashed bow to stern, generators humming a chord she felt in her teeth through the water, floodlights burning off the river fog. They weren't trying to hide. Men who cut mooring lines and packed mud into intakes didn't need to hide. They only needed her delayed.

She wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being clean years ago and climbed into the night.

Ilsa Voss was sitting on the cargo hatch with her knees drawn up, wrapped in Sable's spare blanket, watching the lights across the water with the fixed attention of a woman watching a fire she'd escaped from. The fever had broken that afternoon but it had left her hollowed, cheekbones standing out like keel timbers, eyes too bright.

"They'll come at dawn," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question. She'd been listening to the generators too.

"They'll sound the lower Narrows at dawn," Sable corrected. "They'll come for us whenever it's convenient. That's worse."

"Then we should be gone before they're convenient."

Sable looked at her. The girl—woman, she was maybe twenty-six—had a voice like river gravel now that the fever had scraped the softness out of it. Two days ago she'd been delirious in a fish camp, calling for a doctor who wasn't coming. Tonight she was talking about being gone before dawn like it was a thing a person could simply decide.

"The fleet's got fuel," Sable said. "I've got a tank with a hole in it and a bilge full of my own diesel. I've got a fuel line that's been opened and a mud-clogged intake, and I've got maybe—" she did the arithmetic she'd been doing since sundown and hated the answer, "—eight hours of work if nothing goes sideways, and six tides of window, and one of those tides is tomorrow morning."

"Then we'd better start," Ilsa said, and stood up, and the blanket slid off her shoulders, and she didn't reach for it.

Sable had run alone for twelve years. She'd built a whole life with the load-bearing walls on the inside, where nobody could see them, and she had told herself—right up until the moment she'd hauled Ilsa Voss out of an abandoned fish camp and broken her own oldest rule—that the life worked.

Now there were two of them on the deck of the Marrow, and the fleet's generators were humming across the water, and the night was going to be long.

"Fuel first," Sable said. "Then the line. Then the intake. Then, if the river loves me, sleep."

"The river doesn't love anyone," Ilsa said. "I've read its reports."

* * *

### Ilsa

The siphon hose tasted of diesel and cold. Ilsa knelt on the dock planks with the hose in her mouth and the fuel drum's bung open beside her, and when the diesel finally came up the line in a black ribbon she spat the hose into the drum and knelt back on her heels, wiping her mouth with the back of her wrist, eyes watering.

"You get used to it," Sable said, without sympathy, from the engine hatch.

"I don't intend to get used to it."

"Nobody intends anything on this river. They just do it until it's a habit."

Ilsa fed the hose and watched the drum fill and tried not to think about how the drum was the difference between escape and capture, and how a woman she'd known for three days was trusting her with it. The diesel caught the lantern light and threw it back up at her, orange and slow-moving, like something alive under glass.

She had spent the last six weeks being useful in the way surveyors are useful: precise, quiet, replaceable. She had watched Dr. Ansen—Vera Ansen, who signed her field notes *V.A.* in letters like fence posts—carry the sonar printouts out of the survey tent at Kettle Landing and stand there in the wind with her face doing something Ilsa had no word for. She had watched the crew split the way a log splits, along a grain nobody could see until the axe fell. She had run. She was not proud of the running. She was alive, which was a different thing, and she was learning—sitting on a dock in the dark, siphoning stolen-adjacent diesel for a stranger—that alive was the prerequisite for everything else.

The drum filled. Sable capped it and hefted it like it was empty, which it was not, and carried it up the dock to the Marrow, and Ilsa followed with the second drum and the funnel, and they worked in a rhythm that surprised her. Sable would hold, Ilsa would pour. Sable would say *tighter*, and Ilsa's hands would know what tighter meant without being told. It was like the survey crews at their best, before the split—two people and a task and no room in the task for doubt.

Except that doubt was all around them, humming across the channel in three-part harmony.

"They'll have a watch posted," Ilsa said quietly, as they worked. "On the lead barge. There's always a watch."

"There's always a watch," Sable agreed. "And the watch always looks at the water, because the water's what they're afraid of. Nobody looks at a dark dock with a dead boat on it."

"You're betting your boat on their boredom."

"I'm betting on twelve years of watching people not look at things." Sable set the empty drum down with a click that sounded, in the night quiet, like a gunshot. "It pays better than you'd think."

* * *

### Sable

The fuel line was the easy part. Sable had spliced lines in worse light, in worse weather, with worse company, though she had to admit the company was growing on her in the specific way that a splinter grows toward the surface. Ilsa Voss held the lantern exactly where Sable needed it without being asked twice, and asked questions that were the right questions—*how do you know the line wasn't just fatigued, how do you know the mud wasn't silt*—questions that weren't doubt, exactly. Questions that were the fever of a careful mind.

"Because fatigue opens along the grain," Sable said, running her thumb along the cut edge of the hose. "This is cut across it. Clean. And silt settles in layers. This is packed. Somebody packed it with a stick." She said it flat, and watched Ilsa's face do the arithmetic, and saw her arrive at the same place Sable had been arriving at all day: Hale hadn't just wanted to strand her. He'd wanted it to look like her own neglect, so that when the Marrow was found dead at her mooring, the story would write itself—a careless captain, a boat that finally quit, a woman who'd been warned.

"Twelve years ago," Ilsa said, and then stopped, because some doors you don't open in the dark on someone else's behalf.

"Twelve years ago what?" Sable said, because she'd rather walk through the door herself than have it standing open behind her all night.

"Nothing. It's not—"

"Say it. We're going to be running water together at dawn. I'd rather know what's in your mouth now than have it come up halfway through the Narrows."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. The lantern hissed. Across the channel the generators hummed their idiot chord.

"There's a name in the old incident file," Ilsa said. "The Kestrel survey run. The one where the pilot lost her license. The name in the file isn't Sable Reyes."

The river moved under the dock, patient, enormous. Sable tightened the last clamp on the fuel line, tested it with both hands, and found that her hands were steady, which surprised her, because nothing else was.

"No," she said. "It isn't."

"Are you going to tell me?"

"Are you going to tell me why a junior surveyor knows the incident file by heart?"

Ilsa's jaw set. In the lantern light she looked, for a second, very much like someone else Sable had once known—someone with the same stubborn architecture of face, and Sable put that thought down the way you put down a live wire.

"Because the file is wrong," Ilsa said. "And wrong files are how things like Kettle Landing stay buried. I read everything, Captain. It's the only weapon they gave me that they don't know about."

Sable stood up out of the engine bay, joints reporting in one at a time, and looked at this woman—fever-scraped, diesel-mouthed, holding a lantern like it was a torch she intended to carry somewhere—and felt something shift in her chest that she had spent twelve years keeping locked.

"Intake next," she said. "Then you can show me what you've got."

* * *

### Ilsa

The intake screen came clear in ten minutes of filthy work, mud packed in layers like a wasp nest, and after that Sable bled the lines and said the word *maybe* about the engine, and *maybe*, Ilsa was learning, was the most optimistic word in the captain's vocabulary.

Then there was nothing to do but wait for the engine to prove itself, and Ilsa sat in the wheelhouse with the survey data spread across the chart table, and did the thing she was actually for.

She'd kept the data drive sewn into her jacket lining since the night she ran. Six weeks of sleeping in culverts and riding gravel trucks and finally, stupidly, drinking bad water at a fish camp, and the drive had never left her. Now she unzipped the lining with fingers that still didn't work right in the cold and laid the printouts out beside the river charts, and the two documents lay there together like two witnesses who'd never been allowed in the same room.

"Here," she said. "The charts say the channel through the lower Narrows runs east of the marker shoal. Twelve meters at low water, narrowing to eight. Every pilot on this river has learned it east of the shoal, because the charts say so, because the charts have said so since the dam went in."

Sable leaned over the table. She read charts the way Ilsa imagined other people read letters from home—fast, and with her whole face.

"And the sonar says?"

"The sonar says the river moved." Ilsa put her finger on the printout, on the long blue tongue of deep water that the survey had mapped in September. "The flood two years ago re-cut the channel. There's a new throat through the Narrows, west of the shoal. Fourteen meters at low water. It's not on any chart. It's not on the fleet's charts either, because this—" she tapped the drive, "—is the only copy, and they want it destroyed more than they want anything else on this river."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Ilsa watched her do the thing she'd watched her do all day—take a fact, weigh it, turn it, check it against the river in her head. She could almost see the water moving behind the woman's eyes.

"West of the shoal," Sable said finally. "That's the side with the drowned timber. The old logging boom anchors. Every pilot I know gives that side a berth wide enough to drive a barge through sideways."

"Because the charts say it's eight meters of gravel."

"Because the charts say it's eight meters of gravel." Sable straightened up. Something had changed in her face—not softening exactly. More like a lock turning. "The fleet will sound the east channel at dawn. They'll take hours doing it, because that's what you do with a channel you don't trust. And we'll be through the west throat before they've finished their first sounding line, and they'll follow our wake because they'll have no other choice, and they will *hate* it."

"You sound like you'll enjoy that."

"I'll enjoy it the way you enjoy pulling a splinter." Sable looked at the printouts again, and her voice dropped into something less armored. "This is Ansen's data."

"Dr. Ansen's, yes."

"You trust it?"

Ilsa thought about Vera Ansen standing in the wind at Kettle Landing with the printouts in her hands. Thought about the last thing she'd said to Ilsa, pressing the drive into her jacket lining with fingers that shook: *If I don't come out of this, it's because someone made sure I didn't. Take it to water. Water remembers.*

"I trust it with my life," Ilsa said. "I already have."

* * *

### Sable

At two in the morning Sable turned the engine over and it caught on the third crank and settled into its old heartbeat, and she stood in the engine bay with her hand flat on the block and felt like a woman whose own pulse had come back after she'd stopped believing in it.

She came up into the wheelhouse and found Ilsa asleep at the chart table, cheek on the sonar printouts, one hand still resting on the drive as if even in sleep she was keeping it from being taken. The lantern had burned low. Across the channel the fleet's floodlights still burned, and the generators still hummed, and somewhere over there a watch was looking at the water and not looking at the dark dock where a dead boat sat with a live engine and a full tank and a course already laid in.

Sable stood in the doorway a while, not waking her. She thought about the incident file. She thought about a name that wasn't Sable Reyes, and about how the girl at her chart table had read that file the way she read everything—looking for the wrongness, the seam, the place where the official story had been cut across the grain and packed with mud and called neglect.

Twelve years ago someone had falsified a report and taken her license and her sister, and she had answered by burying herself so deep that even she had trouble finding the body.

Tonight she had a boat, a channel no chart recorded, a witness with a drive sewn into her jacket, and six tides.

She reached over and shook Ilsa's shoulder, gently, the way you wake someone you need and don't want to frighten.

"Voss. Up. We cast off in twenty minutes, on the dark of the tide, before their watch gets relieved and gets curious." Sable looked out the wheelhouse window at the black water, at the Narrows waiting upriver like a held breath. "And while we run, you're going to tell me everything that's on that drive. Starting with why a junior surveyor knows a twelve-year-old incident file better than her own birthday."

Ilsa sat up, eyes clearing fast, hand going straight to the drive. Outside, the first thin gray suggestion of dawn was still hours away, and the river was theirs, and the fleet was humming, and Sable Reyes took the wheel of the Marrow and felt the old name rising in her throat like water finding its level.
