## 11. The First Tide

The Narrows at first light were the color of a bruise going green.

Sable stood at the Marrow's wheel with her coffee untouched on the chart shelf beside her, watching the water do what water does when it has been held back all night and is finally allowed to speak. The glacial reach ran milky where the meltwater carried its load of stone flour down from the icefield, and in that milk the current wrote its opinions in long gray script—eddies curling off the point like signatures, a boil line above the sunken ledge that looked, from this distance, almost gentle.

It was not gentle. Nothing above Kestrel was gentle before calving season. But it was *legible*, and legible was worth more than gentle. She had been reading this reach since she was nineteen years old, under a name that no longer existed, and the river had not forgotten her any more than she had forgotten it.

Behind her, across the black water of the lower pool, the salvage fleet's running lights burned in a neat triangle. Three barges riding heavy, their deck cranes folded like sleeping insects, generators muttering through the hull of the dawn. They had not moved all night. They would not move until they sounded the channel ahead of themselves—every fathom of it, every bar and ledge and shifting throat—because men who worked for Corven Hale did not take chances with other people's rivers.

That was the whole arithmetic of the morning, and Sable had done it three times since midnight and gotten the same answer each time: they could not follow her until they had measured what she was about to run, and by the time they finished measuring, the window would be half spent. Six tides. The hydrology dispatch had said six tides before the ice closed the upper route until spring, and one of them was already bleeding out behind her in the dark.

So she would go first. And they would watch her go, and they would write down every turn she made, and when they came—and they would come—they would come down her wake like hounds on a drag line.

She had understood this last night, lying awake in her bunk while Ilsa Voss slept feverish in the wheelhouse settee, and she had understood something else along with it, something that sat in her chest like ballast taken on wrong: that there was no way to run the Narrows without teaching the river to the men hunting her. Every trick she used to survive would be a lesson delivered. Every line she threaded would be a line drawn on their charts.

*You can't outrun them,* she told herself, *so you'll have to lead them somewhere the lesson kills.*

* * *

Ilsa came up the wheelhouse stairs at grey light, moving carefully, one hand on the rail. The fever had broken sometime in the small hours but had left her looking like something the river had returned—pale, wrinkled, not entirely convinced it wanted to be back. She had wrapped herself in Sable's spare oilskin, which fit her the way a tarp fits a fence post, and she stood at the chart table without being invited, because surveyors did not know how to stand anywhere except over charts.

"You're going now." Not a question. Her voice was still rough from the fish camp, from days of hiding and cold and whatever it was she wasn't saying yet.

"First slack," Sable said. "Water's lowest and slowest for about forty minutes. After that the melt comes down and everything I'm about to show you gets louder."

"And they'll see."

"They'll see." Sable took up her coffee and drank it cold; it tasted like iron and decisions. "They've got lights, radar, and probably a man with binoculars who's better at his job than he'd like to be. What they don't have is anyone who's run this water. So they'll copy me. Turn for turn, throttle for throttle. That's the price of going first."

Ilsa looked at her for a moment. In the grey light her eyes were the flat pale color of the glacier water, and there was something in them that Sable recognized from mirrors—the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"You could wait," Ilsa said. "Let them sound it. Take the long way around the point, lose a day—"

"Lose a day is lose the window. And if they get to Ansen first—" She stopped herself. She didn't actually know what Hale would do to Dr. Ansen, only that a man who cut mooring lines in the night and put sugar in a fuel filter did not climb into bell towers to have conversations. "No. We go now."

Ilsa nodded slowly, then did something Sable hadn't expected: she picked up a grease pencil and began marking the chart. Quick, sure strokes. "If you're leading them anyway," she said, "lead them badly. There's a stretch below the second bend where the sonar shows two channels—one deep and honest, one shallow and mean, separated by maybe thirty meters. On their instruments they'll look identical until you're committed. If you take the honest one, they'll see it. If you take the mean one—" she glanced up, "—you'd ground us."

"I know that stretch." Sable heard her own voice go quiet. "I've known it since before your sonar existed. The mean channel is called the Comb. My—" *my sister mapped it by hand*, she almost said, and didn't. Twelve years of not saying it, and the words still lived in her mouth like a splinter too deep to dig out. "Someone mapped it by hand once. Pole and lead line. It shifts every season, but the bones don't move."

"Then here's what I'd do," Ilsa said, and started drawing again, and Sable let her, because the girl had spent three days running for her life through country that wanted her dead and had come out of it still thinking in channels, and Sable found, to her surprise, that she trusted that more than she trusted most things.

They planned the run together while the light came up the valley wall by degrees, the way water fills a lock.

* * *

At 0540 by the Marrow's old brass clock, Sable rang the engine telegraph to slow ahead and took her boat into the Narrows.

The entrance was a gate of gray stone with the river pouring through it in a single broad tongue, and the tongue was lying this morning—it always lied at slack, smoothing itself flat as poured glass—but Sable knew where the lies were thickest. She brought the Marrow in on the starboard side of the tongue, close enough to the rock that she could have read the lichen, because the current there was dead water sliding backward against the stone, free speed if you knew how to borrow it. The boat ghosted through the gate with the engine barely breathing.

In the wheelhouse mirror she watched the fleet's lights wake up. One after another, searchlights snapped on across the pool, white blades sweeping the water, finding her, holding her. A radio crackled—a voice she didn't recognize, clipped and professional, asking a question she couldn't make out—and another voice answered, and she didn't need to make that one out either. She knew the cadence. Calm, unhurried, interested. Corven Hale, watching his problem solve itself by sailing away and leaving him a map.

*Good*, she thought, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

Below the gate the river bent hard to port around a bluff the old-timers had named the Anvil, and here was the first real test, the place where the fleet's sounding gear would earn its keep or fail it. The channel split. To seaward lay the honest water, wide and deep and dull. Inshore, hugging the bluff's shadow, lay the Comb—a quarter mile of staggered gravel teeth with a snake of deep water threading between them, water that existed only at low slack and only for those who already knew where it lived.

Sable took the Comb.

She took it at four knots with both hands on the wheel and her whole body listening through the soles of her boots. The teeth showed as faint bronze smears under the surface, each one trailed by a V of disturbed water, and between them the deep lane curled like a whip mid-crack. She worked the Marrow through the first gap with ten feet to spare on either bow, swung hard, straightened, took the second gap almost dead astern of the first, and felt the boat's hull shiver once as an eddy tried to swing her tail onto the third tooth. She gave the engine a burst, killed the swing, and was through.

Behind her, in the mirror, the searchlights tracked every degree of it.

"There," Ilsa said softly. She had wedged herself at the aft window with binoculars, and her voice had a strange quality in it—not fear exactly. Witness. "The lead barge is moving. They're following your line. Exactly your line."

"Of course they are." Sable kept her eyes forward. The river below the Comb opened into a long riffle run, and she needed the next ninety seconds to think about the riffle run, not about the fact that she had just drawn a treasure map for the man who'd crippled her boat. "A pilot who won't trust another pilot's wake deserves what he gets. And Hale trusts me the way a loan shark trusts collateral."

"That's not trust."

"No," Sable said. "It isn't."

* * *

The middle miles went easy, and easy made her nervous.

The river widened between timbered slopes still holding their morning fog, and the Marrow ran clean through water she could have run blindfolded, and twice she caught herself checking the mirror more often than the channel ahead, which was exactly backwards, and she corrected it, and then checked the mirror anyway.

Because the thing about being followed was that it changed the shape of the day. She had left the landing intending to win a race. Somewhere in the dark that had turned into something else—into being the tip of someone else's spear. Every fathom she gained, she gained for both of them. The window was six tides for her and six tides for them, minus whatever head start she could carve out of knowledge, and knowledge, she was discovering, was a currency that spent the moment you used it.

"Tell me about the town," she said, mostly to keep her mind off the mirror.

Ilsa lowered the binoculars. For a while Sable thought she wouldn't answer. Then, quietly, watching the fog slide off the trees: "Kettle Landing had nine hundred people when the government decided it was going to be a reservoir. That was before my time. Before yours. The dam company bought out everyone who'd sell, and most did, because the offer was decent and the company was patient. But some wouldn't. Some had been there four generations." She paused. "The official record says the last holdouts relocated by agreement in the spring before the gates closed."

"The official record," Sable said.

"The sonar doesn't lie the way records do." Ilsa's knuckles whitened slightly on the binoculars. "There are structures down there that were never vacated. Furniture. Fences. A church with its bell still hung. You don't leave a bell hanging if you're leaving on purpose." She looked at Sable. "Dr. Ansen found the original survey from the flooding year. The one that was sealed. He found it because the sealed version and the published version disagree by eleven families."

The wheelhouse was very quiet except for the engine and the water talking against the hull. Eleven families. Sable turned the number over and felt its weight, the way you feel a swamped skiff's weight when you're deciding whether to try to right it.

"And Hale's fleet is going down there to—"

"I don't know what they're going to do. Salvage, they say. But salvage crews bring cranes and cutting torches, and nobody needs a cutting torch to pick up treasure." Ilsa lifted the binoculars again. "Ansen said the river is going to give the town back. The glacier retreat is changing the flow regime—in twenty years, maybe thirty, the reservoir bed will be exposed at low water for the first time since the flood. Everything down there will be visible. Everything." Her voice dropped. "Including what's in the church."

Ahead, the river narrowed toward the second bend, and Sable put the conversation away in the part of her where she kept things that were too heavy to carry and too important to drop.

* * *

The second bend was where the river got personal.

It came around a shoulder of blue-gray rock in a long leaning curve, and the current, compressed against the outside of the bend, dug itself a hole and then threw the hole's contents back across the channel in a standing wave train that rolled upstream against the logic of the world. Old charts marked it as the Washer. Old pilots marked it as a place to be elsewhere.

But the Washer had a seam. At low slack, a ribbon of smoother water ran along the very lip of the inside bank, close enough that the Marrow's rail nearly brushed the alders, a ribbon no wider than the boat herself, and the seam was only there if the water was low enough and only where the submerged stump field allowed—and the stump field moved, winter by winter, log by log, so that knowing the seam last year was worth nothing this year except as a starting guess.

Sable slowed to bare steerage and read the water the way she'd learned to read it before she could legally drink: not the surface but the shadows under it, the places where the boil lines kinked, the little dimples that meant a snag's wake pushing up from twelve feet down. She built the seam in her head out of those signs, laid it on the water, and drove the Marrow down it like a thread through a needle's eye.

Halfway through, the thread moved.

A boil opened off the port bow where no boil should be—a new snag, or an old one risen—and the seam kinked hard to starboard, toward the wave train. Sable did the math in the time it takes to say a name: hard over and risk the tail swinging into the waves, or ease off and kiss the snag. She went hard over, gave the engine everything, felt the stern step out, caught it, stepped it back, and came out the far end of the Washer sideways and alive, straightening in the calm water beyond with her heart going like a bilge pump.

In the mirror: the lead barge slowing at the top of the bend. Lights playing on the water. Men on the foredeck with a sounding pole, measuring the seam she'd just proven.

"They stopped," Ilsa reported unnecessarily.

"They'll measure for twenty minutes, then follow." Sable wiped her palms on her thighs, one at a time, keeping the other hand on the wheel. "And they'll find the kink. And they'll mark it. And by tomorrow my whole route will be on their chart with their pencil on it."

"Does that bother you?"

Sable considered the question honestly, which was more than she usually gave it. What bothered her wasn't the copying. It was the shape of the morning itself—that she had woken up free and was spending the day as a lead car in a convoy she despised, that the only way to use her skill against these men was to demonstrate it to them, that the river she loved was being translated, through her own wake, into a language its hunters could read.

What bothered her was that it was working. That was the bitter joke underneath everything: the copying was also costing them. Twenty minutes at the Washer. Forty at the Comb, if they even dared it. Every hour they spent transcribing her was an hour the tide took from them, and the tide did not negotiate.

"It bothers me," Sable said, "the way a scar bothers you. Reminds you what happened. Doesn't stop you walking." She nodded forward, down the long green run of the lower reach, where the water flattened and the fog was burning off and the mountains on either side had begun to show their glaciers—white tongues hanging in the cirques, patient as creditors. "Besides. I'm not showing them the whole route. I'm showing them the route I want them to believe in. There's a difference, and it starts mattering about ten miles from here."

Ilsa looked at her. "There's another channel."

"There's a decision," Sable said, "that I haven't made yet. Ask me at the fork."

* * *

They cleared the lower Narrows at 0715, and Sable anchored the Marrow in the lee of a gravel island to wait out the tide turn, because the upper reach could not be entered on a falling gauge unless you hated your hull.

From the island's shingle, in the strengthening light, she could see back down the way they'd come. The lead barge appeared at the mouth of the Narrows at 0802, riding high and ugly, its crane silhouetted against the brightening water. It came down the channel at half speed, cautious, exact—turning where she had turned, holding the line she had held, its bow wave laying down a carbon copy of hers from five hours earlier. Behind it, the second barge. Behind that, the third, and on the third barge's roofdeck, small with distance, a man stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the Marrow at anchor the way a chess player watches a piece that has just moved somewhere interesting.

Corven Hale. Even at a mile, even through river haze, she could feel him looking.

"He's not angry," Ilsa said, coming to stand beside her on the shingle. She had the binoculars but wasn't using them. "Look at him. He's pleased."

"He should be." Sable shoved her hands in her coat pockets against the morning cold. "He's getting exactly what he wants. A guided tour."

"Then why does he look like that?"

Sable studied the distant figure. Hale stood motionless in the wind off the water, and even at this distance there was something in the stillness that didn't match pleased. Pleased men shifted their weight. Pleased men talked to their crews. This man stood like a man reading a document he'd expected to be shorter.

"Because he's counting," she said slowly. "Tides. Hours. He knows what I know—that every minute he spends following me is a minute off his clock. He's pleased with the map." She turned back toward the upper reach, where the river gathered itself between walls of granite and the first white shoulders of the icefield showed above the mist. "He's just not sure yet what the map costs him. And he's right to wonder."

Above the Narrows, far up in the blue shadow under the glacier's snout, something boomed. Once. A deep, flat concussion rolling down the valley like a door closing in an empty house.

Both of them went still.

"Calving?" Ilsa asked.

"Settling," Sable said. "Maybe. Or the ice clearing its throat." She looked at the mountain a long moment, at the white tongue hanging there with its crevasses like blue scars, and felt the six tides shrink in her chest to something smaller. "Either way, we're not waiting for the next slack to decide anything. Come inside. Show me that fork on your sonar sheets."

They went up the boarding ladder together, and behind them, downriver, the three barges formed up in column and began to move, following her wake exactly, faithfully, obediently—her students, her shadows, her hunters—while overhead the glacier went on counting in a language older than any of them, and getting louder.
