## 12. Ansen's Cache

The fever broke sometime in the gray hour before dawn, and Ilsa Voss woke to the sound of the river breathing.

That was how she had come to think of it, these last days aboard the *Marrow*—the water as something alive and lunging, drawing breath against the hull, exhaling in long hisses along the gravel shallows. She lay in the narrow bunk beneath the wheelhouse with a wool blanket pulled to her chin and listened, and for the first time since she'd run from the survey camp with Ansen's notebook sewn inside her coat, the listening did not feel like hiding. It felt like waiting.

Her body still ached. The fever had burned through her like a brushfire, leaving her thin and shaky, her hands unsteady on anything smaller than a coffee mug. But her mind was clear—clearer than it had been in weeks—and clarity was its own kind of grief. She remembered everything now without the fever's softening blur: the sonar screen in the survey tent, the shape rising out of the green static, Dr. Ansen's hand pressed flat against the monitor as if he could touch the rooftops through it. She remembered the argument that night, voices low and vicious around the camp stove. She remembered the sound of the skiff's engine cutting out behind her in the fog, and the men's voices, and the way she'd dragged the boat into the willows and run until her lungs tore.

She sat up. Above her, through the deck boards, she could hear Sable Reyes moving—boots on steel, the creak of the wheel, the low murmur of a woman talking to her boat the way other people talked to God.

Ilsa dressed, took the oilcloth-wrapped notebook from under her mattress, and climbed into the cold morning.

* * *

The *Marrow* lay anchored in a back eddy below a long gravel bar, the kind of place Sable Reyes found the way other people found their own kitchens—by instinct, without looking. Mist stood on the water in columns. Across the channel, far downstream now but not far enough, the salvage fleet's generators droned like wasps in a bottle. They'd made up distance in the night. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it.

Sable stood at the rail with two tin mugs, and handed one over without ceremony. Steam curled off it. "You look less dead," she said.

"I feel more dead. That's usually the sign I'm getting better." Ilsa wrapped both hands around the mug. Her fingers were still unreliable; she watched them tremble and hated them a little. "I need to show you something."

Sable said nothing, which Ilsa was learning was her way of saying *go on*.

The notebook came out of the oilcloth stiff with damp at the corners, though the pages inside were dry—Ilsa had checked them a hundred times in the fish camp, by candlelight, with the fever roaring in her ears, checking and rechecking as if the words might rot like everything else. She opened it to the last third, where the handwriting changed. Where it stopped being survey data and became something else.

"Dr. Ansen kept two sets of notes," she said. "The official log, which went in the strongbox with the team. And this." She turned the pages toward the gray light. "He wrote in code when he thought the camp might be searched. He taught me the cipher my second week—said every surveyor should know one, because maps get confiscated and people don't. He thought it was a joke. A game." Her throat tightened. "It wasn't a joke in the end."

Sable took the notebook. She held it the way she seemed to hold everything—carefully, but without reverence, as if reverence were a luxury the river didn't stock. Her eyes moved down the page. Columns of letters, numbers, small marginal diagrams: contour lines, depth readings, a sketch of a church steeple with a measurement beside it that made no sense unless you understood it was drawn from memory, from above, from a time before the water came.

"This is Kettle Landing," Sable said quietly.

"You know the name."

"Everyone who works this river knows the name. It's the town that isn't there anymore." Sable looked up. "What I want to know is why a government survey team was mapping what's under the reservoir reach, and why half of them are missing, and why you won't say who you're hiding from."

Ilsa looked out at the mist. The truth had been sitting in her chest for weeks like swallowed glass; the fever had nearly been a mercy, because pain left no room for it. Now there was room.

"The sonar found the town intact," she said. "Not ruins. Intact. Streets, foundations, the church, the school—the cold water preserved everything. And the survey found something else." She turned to a page near the end, where Ansen's cipher gave way to plain text, written fast, the pen pressed so hard it had torn through in places. "Records. His own family's records—he's from there. Or he was. The dam project didn't relocate everyone. There were holdouts. People who refused the buyouts, refused to leave. And the project didn't wait for them."

She heard Sable set her mug down on the rail. Slowly. The way you put something down when your hands have gone strange.

"How many?" Sable asked.

"We don't know. Ansen thinks—thought—dozens. The official record says the last residents were evacuated in March of the final year. Ansen found requisition logs, payroll entries for 'site security' after that date, and a gap in the flood timeline where nothing was written down at all. Three weeks of nothing, right before they closed the gates." Ilsa's voice came out flat, because if she let it do anything else she would not be able to finish. "He was going public. Full report, full sonar archive, names. And then—" She stopped.

"And then his own crew turned," Sable said. Not a question. "The ones who aren't missing."

"Some of them were bought. Some of them were scared. I don't know which is worse." Ilsa hugged the blanket tighter around her shoulders even though she wasn't cold anymore—or was cold, but somewhere the blanket couldn't reach. "Ansen told me to take the notebook and go. He said he'd cache the archive himself, somewhere only the cipher could find, and meet me downriver. He never came." She made herself say the next part, because Sable Reyes deserved it straight, whatever it cost. "The fleet isn't salvaging. There's no wreck worth money down there. They're erasing. Every instrument on those barges is for demolition or recovery-and-disposal. They're going to tear the town apart before the river shifts and gives it to the world."

For a long moment Sable Reyes said nothing at all. She looked downstream, toward the Narrows, toward the drowned streets lying under all that green meltwater, and her face did something complicated and old—grief and fury passing over it like weather fronts.

"My sister worked a survey run on this water," she said finally. "Twelve years ago. It ended badly." She picked her mug back up. "Get your coat. If that notebook says where the cache is, we're going to find it before the fleet does."

* * *

The cipher resolved itself in pieces through the morning, Ilsa working at the chart table while the *Marrow* ran easy up the slackening current, Sable at the wheel calling depths in a voice like a metronome. It was good work, real work, the first Ilsa had done since the camp, and her hands steadied as she went. Ansen's code was elegant—field-practical, built from river terms. *Heron* meant north. *Gravel* meant bar. *Cold storage* meant the cache. Numbers keyed to landmarks on the old pre-dam charts, which Sable happened to own, because Sable Reyes apparently owned every chart of this river ever printed, including three that were supposed to have been destroyed.

"It's the bar below the Split Rock," Ilsa said at last, laying her finger on the page. "He wrote it as a riddle. 'Where the heron stands on gravel and the cold keeps what the fire lost.' Gravel bar, north bank, below a landmark he calls the Standing Stone." She looked up. "Is there a Standing Stone?"

"There's a rock the size of a barn that the old-timers swore a glacier stood on end. Charts call it Split Rock. Ansen's people called it the Standing Stone." Sable's mouth curved, just barely. "Surveyors. Always renaming things like the river cares."

They came to it in early afternoon: a long pale tongue of gravel thrust out from the north bank, the water sliding off it in clean braided sheets, and beyond it, upstream, the dark shoulder of Split Rock standing against the treeline like something that had waded out of the hills and decided to stay. No sign of the fleet. Yet. Sable brought the *Marrow* alongside the bar's tail with a gentleness that seemed impossible for a boat her size, and dropped the bow anchor into the shallows with a single muffled splash.

"Ten minutes," Sable said. "If anyone comes around that point, we're taking on water and fixing a pump. Understood?"

"Understood."

They walked the bar together. The gravel spoke under their boots—a sound Ilsa associated with every field season of her adult life, that dry clatter and shift, the ground refusing to hold a footprint longer than a moment. Which was the point, she realized. Ansen had chosen it the way he chose everything: practically, unsentimentally. Nothing here would keep a track. Nothing here would tell the fleet where to dig.

The cipher's numbers counted paces from the stone. Forty-two, bearing off the notch in Split Rock's profile. Ilsa walked them twice, counting under her breath, and stopped where the bar widened into a shallow bowl of coarse gravel above the high-water line.

There was nothing to see. That was the first test, she understood now—Ansen would never mark it. She knelt and began moving stones, working outward in a spiral, and it was Sable who saw it: a fist-sized rock sitting wrong, its underside dusty-dry in a wet field of stones, settled into a depression instead of resting on top.

Beneath it, a canvas-wrapped case, army-green, waterproofed with tar and wax until it looked like something grown rather than made. Beside it, a smaller bundle in oilcloth, tied with wire.

Ilsa's hands shook again, but differently now.

"Open it here or on the boat?" Sable asked, scanning the tree line, the water, the empty sky.

"Here. If it's not what he said it was, I want to know before I carry it another mile."

The tar peeled away in long black ribbons. Inside, packed in wax paper and wool: a hard drive in a shock case, labeled in Ansen's blocky hand—*FULL SURVEY ARCHIVE, KETTLE LANDING REACH, DO NOT DUPLICATE UNTIL PUBLICATION*. Beneath it, a second drive marked *RAW SONAR*. And beneath that, wrapped separately, a leather journal with a cracked spine, its cover stamped with a university crest twenty years out of date.

Ilsa opened the journal right there on the gravel, kneeling, the wind pulling at her hair, and read.

* * *

The first pages were ordinary—an academic's field journal, observations on flow rates, sediment cores, the tedium of grant accounting. Then, dated eleven months ago, the tone changed. Ansen wrote the way drowning men talk: fast, certain, running out of air.

*The archive confirms it. The evacuation order for the upper Landing was falsified retroactively. Residents remained past the closure date. Site security payroll continues four months past the official "last departure." I have names. I have the gate logs.*

And then, further in, the name itself, written once and underlined twice, as if he needed to convince himself:

*Wray Odell. Odell was assistant administrator of the Kettle Valley Dam Authority during the final phase. Every document that vanished, vanished under his signature or his office's stamp. Afterward—promotion, commendation, a career. He sits on the regional development board now. He chairs the trust that funds half the hydrology research in this basin, including, God forgive me, mine. He built everything he is on the fact that no one ever counted the people who stayed.*

*I requested the sealed relocation records through legal channels six months ago. Yesterday my request was denied, and today a man I've never seen visited our sponsor's office, and my sponsor has stopped returning my calls. I understand now what this is. This is not negligence being covered up. This is a life's work being protected, and I am standing on it.*

The wind came down the bar and riffled the pages. Ilsa became aware that Sable had crouched beside her, reading over her shoulder, and that her breathing had gone very quiet and very controlled, the way it did, Ilsa was learning, when Sable Reyes was angriest.

"He funded the survey," Sable said. "Odell. Your own expedition."

"Through the Meridian Trust. Ansen figured it out too late—that the money was never about science. It was about knowing exactly what was down there, and where, and how much time the river would give them before it exposed it." Ilsa turned a page. "The fleet's schedule matches the hydrology reports. They're not racing calving season to save the site. They're racing it to destroy it while the ice cover keeps anyone from seeing."

She read on. The last entry was eight days old, written in a hand gone ragged:

*Crew divided. Two have taken money—I won't write names here, they'll know themselves and God knows them. I have moved the archive. If you are reading this, Ilsa, then you got out, and you are braver than I ever told you, and I am sorry for the way I used you. Take it to people who cannot be bought. Take it to daylight. Whatever happens to me, the town waited sixty years. It can wait a little longer—but not forever. Neither can the truth.*

Ilsa closed the journal. For a moment she couldn't move. The gravel pressed cold through her knees. Somewhere upstream a glacier was groaning its slow tectonic syllables, and the river slid past the bar in its braided silver sheets, indifferent, patient, keeping everything.

She had imagined this moment in the fish camp, feverish, half-delirious: finding the proof, holding the proof, feeling the proof make things *right*. Instead she felt the weight of it settle onto her like ballast. Proof was not a weapon. Proof was a debt. Every name in that journal—every resident who'd stayed, every security payroll line, every silenced request—was now hers to carry, and carrying it meant walking into the open with it, into hearings and courts and the sightlines of men like Wray Odell, who had demonstrated already what he did to people who stood on his secret.

"It costs something," she said aloud, not meaning to. "Having it. I thought it would feel like winning."

Sable was silent a moment. When she answered, her voice was low and rough, like water over a hidden bar.

"It always costs. That's how you know it's true." She stood, and offered Ilsa a hand up, and her grip was iron and warm. "My sister died on a lie somebody signed. Twelve years I've run from the paperwork of it. You're holding the paperwork of sixty." She looked down at the journal, then at Ilsa, and something passed between them that had no comfortable name. "We get it downriver. We get it to people who can't be bought. And we survive the delivery. All three of those, or none."

They wrapped the drives back in wax paper and wool. Ilsa carried the journal inside her coat, against her chest, where Ansen had told her to keep the cipher notebook a lifetime ago in a classroom with a view of a parking lot. The case went into the *Marrow*'s bilge locker, beneath the spare impeller and a coiled anchor chain, and Sable locked it and pocketed the key without discussion.

They were casting off when Ilsa saw it—just a flicker at the edge of vision, downstream, where the channel bent around Split Rock's shadow. A masthead light. Then another. Running without generator noise, engines throttled low, hugging the bank's quiet water the way a hunter hugs cover.

Three barges' worth of lights, closing the gap.

"Sable," she said.

"I see them." The captain's hand was already on the wheel, her eyes doing that rapid arithmetic Ilsa had watched her perform on sandbars and current seams, except now the numbers included lives and evidence and a journal pressed against Ilsa's ribs. "They skipped the sounding stations. They're running blind water at speed, which means Hale's stopped being careful." The *Marrow*'s engine rose beneath them, a deepening note. "Which means, Ms. Voss, that the polite part of this river is over."

Ahead, the channel narrowed toward the middle reach, where the old charts and Ansen's new survey disagreed—and behind them, patient as winter, the fleet came on.
