## 13. The Middle Reach

The river changed its mind overnight.

Sable felt it before she saw it—the way the Marrow's hull answered the current a half-beat late, the way the wake off the bow came back wrong, muffled, as if the water were speaking through a blanket. She was at the wheel before dawn with coffee she hadn't tasted and Ilsa Voss asleep in the deckhouse behind her, still feverish but sleeping like something that had finally been allowed to stop running.

The Middle Reach opened ahead of her in the gray light: a long trough of water between gravel bluffs, wide enough to look generous, which was exactly how it killed people. The old charts called it honest. The old charts had been drawn forty years ago, before the dam went in upstream of everything, before the glacier above began shedding its weight into the river and rearranging the bottom like a man turning over in his sleep.

She throttled down and studied the reach the way she'd been taught to study anything that might kill her: patiently, without flattery.

The water told her plenty. There was a seam running down the middle of the channel where two currents met and argued—a line of small standing waves, no bigger than a hand's width, that shouldn't have been there if the channel ran deep and true beneath them. Downstream, maybe six hundred meters, the current bent left where every chart she owned said it should bend right. The river was being pushed. Something under the surface was big enough to shove a whole channel sideways.

"Bar," she said aloud, to nobody, because saying it made it real. "New bar. Right where nobody bothered to look."

Ilsa appeared in the doorway wrapped in Sable's spare blanket, hair flat on one side, eyes too bright with fever. She carried the survey folio against her chest like a child. "You're talking to the boat again."

"The boat listens better than most people." Sable nodded at the seam. "Your survey. The deep-channel trace Ansen mapped—where does it run through this reach?"

Ilsa set the folio on the chart table and opened it with the care of someone handling scripture or evidence, which, Sable supposed, it was both. Her finger traced the sonar line. "Here. Deep water hugs the south bluff through the reach, then swings hard right about—" she squinted at the scale, "—two-thirds down. Why?"

"Because the old charts say the swing is left."

They looked at each other across the table, and Sable watched the understanding land in the younger woman's face the way cold lands: gradually, then all at once.

"That can't be," Ilsa said. "Ansen sounded it in June."

"And the glacier's been calving since June." Sable tapped the window, where the pale shoulder of ice above the Narrows was visible between the bluffs, closer every day, shedding its melt into the river like an animal bleeding out. "Every ton of meltwater carries silt. Silt settles where the current slows, and the current slows wherever the old channel used to be deepest. The river's building your bar exactly on top of Ansen's deep water. It doesn't care what the sonar says. It never has."

* * *

She could have gone around. That was the thought that sat with her while she brought the Marrow down to bare steerage way and let the current carry her toward the seam. South of the new bar there was a secondary channel—shallow, braided, mean—but passable for a boat drawing what the Marrow drew, probably, mostly, if the morning was kind. The fleet behind her would take the main channel, sound it carefully, and lose three hours doing it.

Three hours. She'd been living on margins like that for two days, spending minutes the way a drowning woman spends air.

But going around meant trusting the braid, and the braid shifted by the hour this time of year. And there was another arithmetic underneath the first one, the one she kept trying not to do and did anyway: Hale's barges drew too much water for the braid. If she went around, she'd be safe—and slow—and the gap would hold steady instead of growing. If she ran the main channel over the top of a bar that hadn't existed when any chart was drawn, she either gained three hours or she put the Marrow on the gravel with the fleet bearing down on her.

Behind her, faint across the water, she could hear them. Generators. The diesel thrum of a crane engine being tested, tested again, tested a third time, the way men test things when they're nervous. They'd cleared the lower Narrows on her wake and they'd been gaining ever since, because barges don't tire and rivers don't favor sentiment.

"You could wait for them to sound it first," Ilsa said quietly. She'd learned enough in two days aboard to know what Sable was weighing. "Let them find the bar the hard way."

"They won't find it the hard way. They'll find it the careful way, with sounding lines and patience, and then they'll dredge a path through it if they have to, and we'll have spent our lead buying nothing." Sable turned the wheel a spoke. "No. We go over it. High water slack, light load, and I keep her bow pointed at whatever tries to eat us."

"That's not a plan, that's a prayer."

"I've had good luck with prayers shaped like throttle settings."

Ilsa almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in a day and a half, and Sable counted it the way she counted everything now: as fuel.

* * *

The seam came up to meet them.

Sable took the Marrow into it at full flood tide push, bow square to the standing waves, engine at a growl just short of complaint. The hull began to shudder—that particular tremor she knew from twenty years of reading boats, the one that meant the water under the keel was getting personal. Six feet. Five. The depth gauge was a liar in silty water and she knew it, so she stopped watching it and watched the river instead: the color change where the shallows began, tea-dark water going to milk-coffee; the little V of disturbed current downstream that marked the bar's crest like a scar.

"There," she said. "Hold on to something that's bolted down."

The Marrow struck at 9:14 by the deckhouse clock, though Sable would remember it as lasting much longer than the half-second it actually took. A shudder ran the length of the boat, stem to stern, a grinding crunch like a giant biting through celery, and then the river dropped out from under them and the Marrow settled onto the bar with a groan that Sable felt in her teeth.

Silence, except for the current hissing past the hull and the engine idling against nothing.

"Well," Ilsa said from the floor, where holding on to bolted-down things had deposited her. "That's the prayer answered, then."

"Answered *no*." But Sable was already moving, already reading, because a grounded boat is a problem and problems are just information wearing armor. She went out onto the foredeck in the cold spray and looked at the water around them.

It wasn't bad. That was the first thing. The bar had caught them amidships, port side, and the current was pushing them *onto* it rather than off—which was bad—but the crest was soft gravel and sand, not the boulder garden it might have been, and the downstream face fell away into what looked like honest water. The Marrow was a cargo boat, heavy-boned, built by a shipwright who'd assumed his customer would abuse her. She could take a grounding. What she couldn't take was ten minutes of it, because the current would slew her stern around, and a boat lying broadside on a bar in a rising tide is a boat preparing to become wreckage.

"Spars," she called back. "And the anchor. Move."

They worked. This was the part Sable loved—not the running, not the narrow escapes, but this: the clean violence of a problem met with hands. Ilsa hauled the kedge anchor aft along the deck while Sable rigged a spar as a lever under the starboard rail. The plan was old as rivers: plant the anchor in the deep water astern, winch the stern around until the bow swung free, let the current finish the job. Simple. Everything simple, once you'd done it a hundred times, and only impossible the first time, in the cold, with the tide coming back.

The anchor went out with a splash and a rattle of chain. Sable put the winch in gear and the Marrow's stern swung a degree, two degrees, the chain coming taut with a sound like a struck cable. The hull ground on the gravel—a horrible sound, a sound she felt responsible for personally, as if the boat were a creature whose pain she'd caused.

"Come on, old girl," she murmured. "You've been stuck in worse company."

On the third pull, with the tide beginning its turn and the water lifting a hand's width, the Marrow slid free.

The river took her like a hand closing around a wrist. Sable had the wheel and the engine before the stern finished swinging, and they came off the bar backwards-and-sideways in a confusion of current that would have swamped a lesser boat, and then the bow came around, and they were through, and the deep water of the lower reach opened ahead of them, green and cold and theirs.

Sable realized she was laughing. Short, ugly, relieved laughter, the kind you don't decide to do. Ilsa was gripping the rail beside her, soaked to the elbows, staring at her.

"You're laughing."

"I'm counting." Sable wiped spray off her face. "We just bought back the three hours. Cost us twenty minutes and some paint."

* * *

They made the lower end of the Middle Reach by mid-morning, and that was where the advantage died.

Sable saw it first as a smudge of dust against the south bluff—a vehicle track, fresh, running down from the ridge road to the waterline. Then the thing the track existed to serve: a man on the gravel bar at the reach's mouth, standing beside a tripod-mounted instrument, a surveyor's glass or a signal lamp, and beyond him, tied at the bar's edge, a shallow-draft skiff with an outboard.

Someone was marking the channel. Someone was standing at the far end of the reach, looking back up it—at the exact line Sable had just run—and recording it.

She cut speed and drifted, letting the current carry them quiet, and raised the binoculars.

The man at the instrument wasn't salvage crew. He wore no orange, no company gear. He wore a canvas coat and rubber boots and he handled the instrument with the bored competence of a man who'd done this a thousand times in a thousand reaches, and when he turned briefly to check the skiff, the binoculars found his face, and Sable's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the glacial air.

She knew him. Not well—she'd never wanted to know him well—but twelve years ago he'd run deck on boats out of the same landings she had, and he'd been there, on the dock, the season her license died. Tomas Reyes' crowd. No—Tomas was dead; she'd heard that years ago, and good riddance. But this man had run with him. He'd drunk at the same tables where her name had been taken apart and reassembled into something false, and he'd heard the stories, and now he stood at the mouth of the Middle Reach with a lens pointed at her wake.

He wasn't surveying the channel for science. He was surveying it for Hale.

"He's marking your line," Ilsa said. She'd come up beside Sable without Sable hearing her, which was itself a small alarm—Sable didn't miss people on her own deck. "For the barges."

"For the barges." Sable lowered the glasses. Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "He's not even guessing. He watched me take the seam, he watched me ground, he watched me kedge off, and now he's going to hand Hale the whole route drawn in my own handwriting."

"Can you outrun them to the next reach?"

"No." She said it plainly because lying to herself was a luxury she'd given up along with her name. "They'll follow the marked line now. No sounding, no caution, full power. And they burn more fuel than I do, which means they'll run harder to make up the difference. By the Kestrel Narrows proper, the gap will be—" she did the arithmetic and hated it, "—an hour. Maybe less."

The wind came down the reach and pushed at the Marrow's hull, and somewhere above them, distant as artillery, the glacier spoke—a long grinding report that rolled down the valley and made the water shiver. Both women looked up. The ice field above the Narrows had visibly shrunk since yesterday, its shoulder slumped, its face riddled with the blue-black wounds that meant it was deciding something.

"It's going early," Ilsa said.

"Everything's going early." Sable turned the wheel and brought the Marrow back up to speed, and the marked channel, the man with the instrument, the skiff all fell away behind them—but not really, not in any way that mattered. She could feel the fleet now the way you feel weather coming: pressure behind the ears, a change in the quality of the silence. Somewhere back downriver, three barges were lining up on a path she'd cut, following her like wolves following blood on snow, and at their wheelhouse windows stood men who answered no questions, and somewhere among them—

She thought of Dree Callas and made herself stop thinking of Dree Callas.

There was one card left, and it wasn't speed. Speed was spent. The card was knowledge—the deep, buried, unwritten knowledge of water that had never been charted because no one sane had ever needed it. Twelve years ago, running under another name, she'd known every back channel and dead slough between here and the Narrows. Some of those channels were gone. Some had moved. But one of them, if it still lived, ran where no barge could follow and came out above the worst of the ice.

It also ran through country she'd sworn never to see again.

Sable stood at the wheel with the river pulling her north and the fleet pulling at her heels, and understood, with the gambler's cold clarity that was the closest thing she had left to faith, that the river had been herding her the whole time. Every mile north was a mile toward the water where her sister had drowned. The charts disagreed with the survey; the survey disagreed with the river; and underneath all of it, patient as gravel, lay the one route that could save them, and it ran straight through the past.

"Get me Ansen's notes," she said to Ilsa. "All of them. There's a channel called the Cut-off, above the Middle Reach, and if it still exists, we're taking it."

Ilsa hesitated. "What's the Cut-off?"

"A shortcut," Sable said, and fed the engine fuel, and did not add: *and a grave.*
