## 14. The Pilot Who Knew Her

The Middle Reach had cost me a night and most of my pride. The Marrow sat on the gravel bar for forty minutes while I ran her engines against the current like a woman arguing with a locked door, and when she finally came free—stern swinging, propeller chewing silt into brown ribbons—I didn't let myself feel relief. Relief was a luxury you bought after. I just put her nose downriver toward the Narrows and watched the water for what it was telling me.

What it told me, an hour later, was worse than any bar.

The fleet had closed the gap.

I saw them first as sound: the deep industrial thrum of three diesel generators carrying across the water the way thunder carries over flat country, arriving before anything you can name. Then the barges themselves came around the bend below Miller's Point, riding low with crane gear, their running lights burning even in daylight because men who work salvage don't trust the sun to do its job. Two of them. The third—the lead barge that had taken ice in the calving—was gone, and I didn't waste grief on it. Hale's remaining hulls moved with the patience of things that know they are heavier than everything around them.

But it wasn't the barges that stopped my breath. It was the pilot boat running ahead of them, small and quick, cutting the current at an angle no stranger would dare.

I knew that angle. I'd taught it.

"Dree," I said, though there was no one aboard to hear me but Ilsa, hunched over Ansen's field notes in the wheelhouse with a blanket around her shoulders like a shawl she hadn't asked for.

She looked up. "Who?"

"Someone I used to be." I took the glasses off the hook and found her: Dree Callas standing at the wheel of a survey skiff rigged with a fleet radio antenna, dark hair cut short since the last time I'd seen her, one hand on the throttle, the other resting on the rail the way she always rested it—fingers spread, reading vibration through her palm instead of trusting her eyes. She'd learned that from me too. Everything she knew about water, she'd learned from me, which meant everything she knew about water was now pointed at my stern.

The only person alive who knew my old routes. Every shortcut I'd ever cut through the Kestrel system under another name, every seam between the bars that never made it onto a chart because I'd never written them down—they lived in her hands now, and Hale had bought them.

"How much does she know?" Ilsa asked. Her fever had broken sometime in the night but left her voice scraped thin, a voice speaking up from the bottom of a well.

"Everything." I set the glasses down before my hands could shake enough to make it obvious. "Every trick I ever used. Where the deep channel runs behind Gull Island. How to read the foam line off the Narrows' east wall. Which eddies will hold a boat and which will eat one."

"And she's using them?"

"She's using them." I watched the skiff take the seam above Miller's Point—a seam I'd found twelve years ago, running cargo I shouldn't have been running, under a name I'd drowned—and come through clean, gaining on us by half a length. "That's my line. That's mine."

"You gave it to her."

"I taught it to her. Giving is different." But even as I said it I heard how thin the distinction sounded, out here where the river kept no records and neither did I.

* * *

We ran the afternoon that way, the Marrow and the skiff trading water like two knives circling. I held what advantage the river allowed—my draft was shallower, my boat lighter, and I knew this stretch of the Current the way you know your own pulse—but Dree flew the fleet's colors close enough behind that I could see the men on the barge decks. Cranes folded for transit. Winch cables coiled. And once, when the channel narrowed past the old ferry landing, a man with a rifle slung across his back, standing at the rail not even pretending to be crew.

Hale wasn't hiding anymore. The calving had cracked something open in him besides the glacier.

At the narrows above Split Rock the channel pinched to less than a boat-length of margin between the bar shoal and the standing wave off the point, and I took it the way I'd taken it a hundred times—throttle down to nothing, letting the current carry us through sideways, the Marrow drifting like a leaf in a gutter until the water widened and I could give her power again. It was a move that looked like madness and was actually arithmetic. I'd shown it to exactly one person, on a gray morning six years ago, saying *never use this unless you have to, because if you get it wrong there's no getting it right.*

Through the glasses I watched Dree bring the skiff to the same pinch, kill her engine, and drift through sideways, fingers spread on the rail.

She came out the other side and did something I hadn't seen in six years.

She raised her arm and swung it in a slow, deliberate arc—port to starboard, twice—the old deckhand's signal we'd used between boats when radios were dead or untrustworthy. *Come alongside.* Then she pointed, twice, at the gravel shelf below Split Rock where the current went slack and two boats could lie together without grinding.

Ilsa saw it too. "Is that a trap?"

"It's a signal." I kept the glasses up. "Whether it's honest is a different question."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is whether I'm still a fool." I thought about it longer than I wanted to. Behind Dree's skiff, the barges ground on, patient as debt. If I ran, they'd follow my wake through the pinch and be on me by dark. If I stopped, I was stopping next to the only pilot in the world who could run me down blindfolded. Either way, Dree Callas was in my future. The only choice I had left was whether to meet her on my terms or hers.

"Take the wheel," I told Ilsa. "Hold her in the slack below the shelf. If anyone but that skiff comes near, run for the east channel and don't wait for me."

"Where are you going?"

"To find out what loyalty costs these days." I was already pulling on my coat. "I'm told the price has gone up."

* * *

The slack water below Split Rock was green and glass-still, a pocket the river forgot twice a day, and I brought the skiff alongside the Marrow with a fender's width to spare—Dree handled her lines the same as ever, quick and economical, making fast without being told. For a moment we just stood there, ten feet of cold air between us, two women who had shared a wheelhouse through three seasons and a silence of six years.

She looked older. Not worn—*settled*, the way silt settles, layer on layer, until you can't tell what's river and what's bank. Her eyes went over the Marrow the way they always did, cataloging, and then they came to my face and stayed there.

"Sable Reyes," she said. Testing it.

"That's the name on the registration."

"It's not the name I signed on under." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "Six years I worked for a woman called Merrin Vale. Best captain on the upper Current. Never lost cargo, never lost a hand, never filed a route in her life. Then one day she sold me her boat manual, paid me triple, and vanished so completely I figured her for dead." Her jaw tightened. "Turns out she was just hiding."

"Dree—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and I saw it wasn't steady. That surprised me more than anything else she'd done. Dree Callas had hands like mooring cleats; I'd never seen them tremble. "You don't get to explain yet. First you're going to listen, because I've got maybe ten minutes before Hale sends someone to see why I'm not closing the gap, and I didn't risk my neck coming alongside to hear apologies."

I shut my mouth. Old habit, from the years when listening was survival.

"Hale hired me four days ago. Cash, double rate, no questions—which should have been the first warning, because nobody pays double for no questions unless the questions are worth triple." She glanced back at the barges, distant now below the point, then lowered her voice. "He told me he needed a pilot who knew the Kestrel system above the Narrows. I told him I knew one route: yours. He smiled like I'd handed him a gift and said, 'Then you know where she'll run.'"

"He's been hunting me with my own map."

"He's been hunting you with your own *name*. He knows, Sable. About the license. About the report. About—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide whether to say it, "—about your sister. He read me the file himself, first night, sitting across a table with a lamp in my eyes like this was some cheap interrogation drama. He wanted to see if I'd flinch."

"Did you?"

"No." Her chin came up. "Because I don't believe it. Twelve years I've heard stories about the Vale wreck—every dock rat on the Current has—and none of them ever matched the woman I worked for. The story says a careless captain ran a survey boat into floodwater with a storm coming. The woman I know wouldn't run a dinghy into a wet parking lot without checking three forecasts and the mood of the moon." She stepped closer, boots ringing on the Marrow's deck. "So here's what I need from you, Captain, and I need it straight, because I've got a barge full of armed men behind me and a paycheck I haven't earned yet and a conscience that's been keeping me up nights since the calving."

"Ask."

"Tell me what happened on the water twelve years ago. Not the report version. Yours." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "And then tell me what's in that boat behind you that's worth three barges and a small army, because I've done the arithmetic, Sable, and nobody spends this much money chasing a drowned town out of sentiment."

* * *

I told her.

Not all of it—not the parts that belonged to my sister alone, the parts I keep in a sealed compartment below my own waterline—but the shape of it. The survey run. The storm the forecast missed. The findings that were true and the report that was falsified, and whose signature sat on the falsification, and how a young pilot with no money and no family and a revoked license learns very quickly that the river doesn't ask for references. I told her about buying the Marrow with the last of the insurance money I'd hated every bill of, and renaming myself the way you rename a boat: scraping the old letters off, painting new ones, pretending the hull doesn't remember.

Dree listened the way she read water—with her whole body, still, absorbing.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment. The current sighed against the hull. Somewhere upstream, ice ticked and groaned in the Narrows, counting down hours I couldn't afford.

"The sonar data," she said finally. "The drowned town. That's what the fleet's really after."

"They're not after it. They're after its *nonexistence*. There's a man named Odell who built a career on that flood, and there's evidence on the bottom that would end him, and Hale's job is to make sure the river never gives it up." I nodded toward the wheelhouse, where Ilsa's silhouette bent over Ansen's notes. "There's a survivor aboard who can testify to all of it, and a cache of proof waiting on a gravel bar, and about thirty-six hours of open water before the ice closes the door on all of us."

"So you're running it downriver. Through the Narrows. In closing ice." Dree exhaled through her teeth. "With a fleet behind you."

"With a fleet behind me that now knows every route I know."

"Not every route." Something shifted in her face—something I hadn't seen in six years, and had almost forgotten the weight of. "You held one back. You always hold one back. You told me once: never give away the last card, because the day you need it, it's the only one that matters." Her eyes searched mine. "There's a channel through the lower Narrows that isn't on any chart and isn't in any survey. You found it the year we ran timber, the year of the low water. You showed it to me from the rail and swore me to silence and never once wrote it down. Tell me I'm right."

My heart was doing something complicated and painful. "You're right."

"Does Hale know about it?"

"He knows I know things he doesn't. That's why he needs you."

"Then here's my price." Dree planted her spread fingers on the rail between us, the old gesture, reading the vibration of whatever passed beneath. "I don't work for free and I don't work for gratitude—gratitude doesn't clear in a bank. I need paying, Sable. Real money, enough to disappear on, because when this is over, whether you win or sink, I can't ever take a contract on this river again. Hale doesn't forgive, and Odell's kind doesn't forget faces." She swallowed. "I'm choosing a side with my eyes open. But I'm not choosing it for love. Love doesn't pay for a new name, and God knows you'd know."

It should have stung. Instead it felt like the first honest currency anyone had offered me in weeks. Loyalty with a price tag attached was the only kind I trusted anymore—it was the kind that held in weather.

"Half now," I said. "Half when we clear the Narrows. There's a strongbox under the Marrow's floorboards, port side, under the fish hold. It's not a fortune, but it's a new name and a bus ticket and six months of rent anywhere south of the border."

"You keep a strongbox under the fish hold."

"I keep a lot of things under floors. It's a hobby."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died young. Then she straightened, and the pilot came back into her posture, the deckhand's looseness folding away into something harder. "Ten minutes are up. I'll run the fleet slow through the middle reach—engine trouble, injectors fouling, whatever Hale will swallow. That buys you the night. At dawn he'll push hard for the church shallows, because the ice is closing faster than his charts say and he knows it. When he commits to the gap—" she met my eyes, "—that's when you run. And Sable? When you run, don't look back at me. Whatever happens on that barge tonight, don't look back. Looking back is how captains die."

"Dree." I caught her wrist before she turned. Her pulse was going like a winch under load. "Why? Six years of silence, and you cross open water to sell yourself to the losing side. Why?"

She looked down at my hand on her wrist, then up, and for a second she was twenty-three again, standing my watch in the rain, asking questions I didn't answer then either.

"Because when they read me that file," she said quietly, "the part that convinced me wasn't the falsified report. It was the date. The storm hit on a Tuesday. The report was filed Monday morning—signed, stamped, certified *before the water even rose*." Her jaw set. "Somebody wrote that ending in advance, Captain. Somebody decided how it ended before it happened. And I've spent six years working for men who write endings in advance, and I am so tired of their handwriting."

She stepped back onto her skiff, cast off the lines without ceremony, and pushed out into the current. At the edge of the slack she raised one hand—not the signal, not this time, just a hand, held up briefly against the gray sky—and then the skiff swung downstream toward the barges, toward the cranes and the rifles and the man with the lamp, carrying my last secret in her hands like something breakable.

I stood at the Marrow's rail until the river took her.

Behind me, Ilsa appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, blanket still around her shoulders. "Well?"

"Well," I said, "we just gained a night, a route, and a traitor. The only question left is whose."

Upstream, deep in the Narrows, the ice answered first—a crack like a rifle shot rolling down the valley, long and splitting, followed by a groan that went on and on, the sound of a door that had been open for six tides beginning, at last, to swing shut.
