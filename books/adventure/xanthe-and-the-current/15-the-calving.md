## 15. The Calving

The glacier spoke before it broke.

I'd been awake since the small hours anyway—captains of shallow-draft boats sleep the way cats do, one ear always on the water—and so I heard it first as a sound beneath sound, a groan rolling down the valley like a door swinging on hinges the size of church towers. The Marrow lay at anchor in a back eddy below the last bend before the upper reach, and the sound came through the hull itself, up through the keel and into the deck plates and into the soles of my feet where I stood at the rail with a mug of coffee going cold in my hands.

Ilsa came out of the wheelhouse wrapped in my spare blanket, her fever finally broken but her eyes still carrying that bruised, hunted look she hadn't quite shed. She didn't ask what the noise was. She'd been above the Narrows long enough to know.

"That's not normal settling," she said.

"No," I said. "That's a glacier deciding something."

The morning was the color of pewter, the river running high and green with rock flour, cold enough that the air over the water had a glassy quality, every sound arriving sharp-edged. Above us, past the shoulder of the ridge, the Kestrel Glacier hung in its valley like held breath. From down here you couldn't see it—you could only feel it, the way you feel weather coming through an old scar.

The hydrology dispatch had given us six tides. That was two days ago. We were working on tide four, and everything about the water said we were spending them faster than the office had promised. The current had come up half a knot overnight. The temperature had dropped. And now the mountain was talking.

"We should run while we can," Ilsa said.

"We should eat first." I handed her the mug. "You can't outrun ice on an empty stomach, and neither can I."

She drank because her body wanted it even if her judgment didn't, and I went below and fried eggs and tinned potatoes and toast that burned on one side because my attention kept climbing the valley wall like a man checking a fire line. Ilsa ate standing up, which told me more about her nerves than any question would have. Twelve years ago I'd have eaten the same way. Some lessons the river teaches only once, but it teaches them permanently.

* * *

We weighed anchor at first light proper, and the upper reach opened ahead of us like a throat.

The Narrows at this height were nothing like the lower water I'd threaded two days before. Here the river compressed between walls of gray granite streaked white with mineral bleed, and the current ran fast and smooth and deeply treacherous—the kind of water that looks like silk and behaves like a belt sander. The drowned town lay somewhere below all this, under sixty feet of green meltwater, its steeple I'd seen breaking the surface still miles downstream. Up here there was only speed and stone and the glacier's voice getting louder with every mile.

I ran it the way I run everything: reading the surface the way other people read print. A V of smooth water meant current stacking against a ledge. A boil line meant a boulder doing its work unseen. The pale seams in the green told me where meltwater entered cold and unmixed. And everywhere, along the banks, the signs I liked least: ice. Not floes yet, not the grinding pack that would seal the route—but slush lines, brash drifting down from above in long windrows, each piece small as a fist, gathering where the current slowed along the eddy margins.

"Look at the willows," Ilsa said suddenly, pointing at the left bank.

I looked. The willow stands that lined the lower benches were trimmed—sheared off clean at the same height for fifty yards, like a barber had passed through. Debris lines on the bank stones sat higher than yesterday's high-water mark by a good three feet.

"The glacier's pond is backing up," I said. "It's damming itself. When it lets go—" I didn't finish it. She could do the arithmetic. Everyone who worked this river could do the arithmetic; it was why the calving season emptied the reach of everything that floated.

Behind us, faint but unmistakable across the water, came the diesel throb of heavy engines. I didn't need to look. Hale's remaining barges had been following my wake since the lower Narrows, close enough that I could set my watch by their generators. Two barges now instead of three. The lead one—I'd heard it on the radio band the night before, a mayday cut short by static and then nothing—had met ice somewhere in the middle reach and lost the argument. Hale had lost a hundred tons of crane gear and most of his margin, and men who lose margin stop being careful.

"Radio," Ilsa said. "You should hear this."

I'd been monitoring the working channels out of habit. She'd been monitoring them out of terror, which was better motivation. I climbed into the wheelhouse and turned the gain up, and the voice that came through was Hale's foreman channel, flat and stripped of all its dockside charm.

"—no more waiting on soundings. Callas says she can hold our line to the church shallows before the pack sets. We take the site today or the river takes it for us."

A pause, crackle, then another voice—Hale himself, and the difference in him was audible even through the compression of the radio. The measured, reasonable tone was gone. What remained was quick and thin and hungry, a blade drawn rather than displayed.

"Then we're done being delicate. The survey woman is the loose end. Odell wants no testimony reaching the hearing, and I want no witnesses contradicting my salvage report. You have your orders. Take the woman. Sink the boat. Whatever flag she's flying, whatever registry—it's unregistered salvage operating in a closed reach, far as anyone needs to know. Drown the story with the rest of it."

Static. Then the other voice again, younger, hesitant in a way that made me file his name away if I ever got it: "And the captain? Reyes?"

Another pause, longer. When Hale answered, he sounded almost regretful, which was worse than anything else he'd said.

"Reyes was never here. Nobody's ever here. That's the whole point of a river, son. It carries things away."

I stood with my hand on the volume knob and let the channel hiss itself empty. Beside me, Ilsa had gone very still, the blanket clutched at her throat.

"He said sink the boat," she whispered. "He said it like—like ordering lunch."

"He's stopped pretending." I killed the radio. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, something had gone very quiet and very clear, the way it does when a situation stops being complicated. For days there had been a race, and races have rules—lanes, marks, a finish line, a chance that better seamanship wins. This wasn't a race anymore. A race has two boats trying to arrive. A hunt has one boat trying to arrive and another boat trying to make sure the first one doesn't.

"They'll come up behind us," Ilsa said. "Callas knows your water."

"Dree knows some of my water." I heard how my voice flattened on the name and didn't apologize for it. "She knows the routes I taught her. She doesn't know what I learned after she left." Which was true, and thinner than it sounded, because what I'd learned after Dree left was mostly how to be alone, and that doesn't draw a channel on any chart.

But there was more truth underneath it, and I made myself look at it straight while the Marrow pushed north into the narrowing light. Dree Callas had been the best deckhand I ever shipped, quick and loyal and funny in bad weather, and she'd walked off my boat—or off the boat I'd been then, under the name I'd been then—because the scandal took everything it touched, and she had a life to build. Now she was standing a wheel on a wrecking barge for a man who ordered drownings over an open channel, and the only reason I could find for it that didn't break my heart was money, and the only reason that didn't break it worse was that maybe she didn't know yet what she was steering for.

Either way, she was behind me, gaining, holding my wake exactly the way Hale's helmsmen had learned to do—because my wake was proof the water was survivable, and they trusted proof more than charts.

I had led my hunters through the lower Narrows. If I didn't change something, I'd lead them all the way to Ansen's bell tower.

* * *

The glacier gave its second speech at noon.

We were through the worst of the upper compression and into the long lake-like stretch below the ice face—a mile of open water where the river widened and slowed and the mountains fell back enough to show sky, and that sky was where I saw it first: a plume of snow-smoke rising off the glacier's tongue, hanging in the still air like a signal only pilots read.

"Hold on to something," I said, and cut our speed, because what was coming couldn't be outrun and didn't need to be—only survived.

The sound arrived a full minute after the plume, which is how you know the ice is big: light beats sound, and the gap between them measures the size of the end of the world. It started as thunder and became something with no name I know—a cracking, a booming, a long tearing roar like the valley itself splitting—and then the front of the glacier calved, a wall of ice the height of a ten-story building letting go all at once and falling into its own meltpond, and the displacement wave came down the valley toward us wearing a crown of spray.

I put the Marrow's bow into it. There was nowhere else to put her; the banks were cliff and scree, and a beam sea kills small vessels the way a hand kills flies. The wave reached us as a moving hill of green water, twenty feet of it, steep and glassy and littered with brash, and I fed the engine just enough to keep steerage and rode the face of it up and over, the whole boat groaning, cargo straps singing, Ilsa white-knuckled on the wheelhouse rail beside me with her eyes wide and fixed forward because looking sideways at a wave that size teaches you things you don't need to know.

Then it was behind us, and the water was chaos—ice everywhere, the entire surface of the reach carpeted with bergs from the size of cars to the size of houses, grinding and groaning and turning, the current pushing the whole churning mass downriver toward the Narrows like a hand sweeping crumbs off a table.

I brought us into the lee of the largest berg within reach—an island of old blue ice taller than the wheelhouse—and let the Marrow ride out the aftermath in its shadow, engine ticking, both of us breathing like we'd run the distance ourselves.

"That was early," Ilsa said finally. Her voice shook and she let it. "The dispatch said six tides. That was—that was the season starting, wasn't it? Not ending. Starting."

"It was the dam breaking," I said. "The glacier's pond. Everything above us is coming down now, and it's coming down faster than any schedule." I looked upriver, where the ice field already stretched bank to bank in the middle distance, closing like a door. "The route isn't going to close from the bottom, Ilsa. It's closing from the top. Every hour, the window moves downriver toward us—and toward the church shallows—and when it reaches them, Kettle Landing is sealed under new ice until spring, and everything Ansen carried dies with it."

"And Hale?"

I glanced astern. Far down the reach, past the drifting ice, the two barges had hove to at the edge of the wave's wreckage, alive and intact, their cranes swaying. Even from here I could see activity on deck—lines, boats, motion. They'd survived it too. Men like Hale survived everything; that was the whole trouble with them.

"Hale just watched his schedule die," I said. "Which means he stops calculating and starts grabbing. He'll run the ice while it's still soft, take losses, take risks—he'll spend anything to get to that steeple before the pack sets. Including us."

I turned back to the wheel. The plan assembled itself in my head with the cold click of parts fitting, and I hated every part of it, and there wasn't another one.

The drowned town lay two hours south, at slack water, if slack water still existed anywhere in the world. The ice coming down would reach it in four, maybe five. Hale would reach it in three if he drove his barges through the brash and didn't care what the ice did to his hulls. And between us and the steeple lay the middle reach, where the river bent around the shoulder of Mount Kestrel and narrowed again, and where a certain pilot with a certain memory of my old routes would be pushing two hundred tons of crane barge at speeds that would make a sane man weep.

"Ilsa," I said. "Get below and strap everything twice. Then get the survey data up here—all of it, Ansen's notes, the sonar overlays, everything. If there's water between here and that steeple that isn't on any chart, I want to know about it in the next hour."

"Why?"

"Because we're about to do the stupidest thing either of us has ever done." I rang the engine telegraph to full ahead and felt the Marrow answer, the old girl leaning into the current like a dog into wind. "We're going to run the closing ice. Downriver, through the pack, ahead of it, inside it—whatever it takes to beat Hale to that bell tower. And when he comes after us—"

I looked at the radar screen, where his barges were already moving, two hard returns driving south through the drift.

"—when he comes after us, he won't be racing anymore. So neither will I. From here on, I'm not trying to arrive first. I'm trying to make sure he never arrives at all."

Ilsa looked at me for a long moment, fever-thin and steady-eyed, and then she went below without another word, and the sound of her boots on the ladder was the sound of a crew answering orders, and I realized I had a passenger no longer.

What I had was a hunt of my own.
