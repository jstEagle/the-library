## 16. Kettle Landing

The river told us we were close before anything else did.

Ilsa felt it through the deck plates first—a change in the Marrow's voice, the engine's labor smoothing into something almost like ease, as if the boat had passed from one current into another and the new one remembered her. Then the water itself changed color. All morning it had been the gray-green of glacier melt, opaque as ground glass, but now a paler thread ran through it, a vein of blue-white cold that spread from the banks inward like frost climbing a window.

"Slack water," Sable said, and her voice was strange—flat and careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut with her shoulder. "We've got maybe forty minutes before the tide turns and this whole reach starts moving again. Forty minutes to look at what your doctor found."

Ilsa stood at the rail with Ansen's field notes open against her chest, the pages soft as cloth from handling, and watched the water go pale.

She had imagined this moment a hundred times since the fish camp, and in every imagining she had been alone with it—just her and the sonar images glowing on the laptop screen, the ghost-geometry of streets and foundations rendered in amber lines. She had thought she understood what they were coming to. She had been wrong by an entire order of magnitude. The sonar had shown her data. The river was showing her a place.

It came around a bend all at once, the way grief comes, the way bad news comes: no approach, no warning, just there.

The town lay under the water like a body under a sheet.

Not deep—the flood had never been deep here, that was the horror of it, that was the thing the sealed records would not say. Twelve feet at the deepest corner, eight over most of the streets. The green ice-melt was clear enough in the slack water that Ilsa could see down through it as if through the glass bottom of a boat ride at a county fair, except that below her was not fish and gravel but rooftops. Whole rooftops, ranked along invisible streets, their shingles furred with silt, their chimneys standing like periscopes. A wagon sat in what had been a dooryard, its wheels still round, its bed full of sixty years of quiet. A fence line ran off into the murk, posts upright, wire long since gone, marking a property line for people who were no longer anywhere.

"Oh," Ilsa said, and the word came out of her small and involuntary, the sound a person makes when they touch something hot.

Sable brought the Marrow in slow, off-throttle, letting the current's last breath carry them, and did not answer. Her hands moved on the wheel with a tenderness Ilsa had not seen in her before—not the competent grip of the pilot but something closer to how you'd handle a sleeping child. The boat slid between two submerged rooflines with yards to spare on either side, and Ilsa looked down and saw, directly beneath the hull, a garden trellis collapsed against a porch rail, and a child's tricycle lying on its side in the street, its handlebar tassels rotted to black threads.

They had flooded it with people still in it. That was what Ansen's notes said, in his cramped hand, in the sentence he'd underlined twice: *They ran the water in March. The holdouts were still in Kettle Landing in March.* And the government's own survey—the one Ansen had been sent to bury, the one he'd turned instead—had proven it with soil cores and foundation marks and the high-water stains inside attics that should never have been underwater at all if the town had been empty when the gates closed.

Ilsa had read that sentence forty times. Reading it and seeing the tricycle were different things entirely.

* * *

The steeple broke the surface a quarter mile up the reach, and it drew them the way a lighthouse draws them, except inverted—everything about it wrong, a marker standing where no channel should be.

The church rose out of the water at an angle, leaning perhaps five degrees eastward, its white clapboard gone gray and soft-edged with decades of submersion, but the steeple itself still proud, still vertical where it mattered, the belfry's louvred openings dark above the waterline. Water stained the wood in a tide-line band six feet up, the record of every spring flood since the drowning, and above that band the paint survived in flakes, so that the steeple wore the years visibly, like rings in a tree. Below the band: drowned. Above it: merely abandoned.

"There," Ilsa said, though Sable was already looking.

"Light's wrong," Sable murmured. "Somebody's got a lamp up there."

And then, faintly, across the flat water, across the roofs of the drowned town, a bell rang.

One stroke. It was not a clean stroke—the tone was cracked, or the rope-hand was weak, or both—and the sound came out muffled and mournful, rolling over the slack water and dropping into the flooded streets, and Ilsa felt it in her sternum more than her ears. It died away and left a silence so complete she could hear the tick of the Marrow's cooling engine and the small lap of water against the hull and, underneath everything, the distant groan and crackle of the glacier miles upstream, the sound of the route closing behind them like a door.

"He's alive," Ilsa said. Her eyes were burning. She hadn't decided to cry; her body had simply made the decision without consulting her. "He rang the bell. He's been ringing the bell."

"For whom?" Sable said quietly. But she was already turning the wheel, already easing the Marrow toward the churchyard, which was not underwater—which had been built, sensibly, on the one knoll high enough to stay dry, and was now an island of alder and dead grass no bigger than a tennis court, ringed by the drowned streets of the town it had once served.

* * *

Moor-ing at the knoll was a maneuver Sable performed with a precision that Ilsa, even in her state, registered as extraordinary—two lines to dead alder trunks, the bow nudged into a shelf of old riprap, the engine backed down to silence. The moment the boat kissed stone, Ilsa was over the rail.

"Ilsa—" Sable said, sharp.

But the ground held, gravel and root, solid as any ground, and Ilsa was climbing the knoll through the alders with Ansen's notes crushed against her ribs, and the church was rising in front of her, getting larger, getting realer, its shadow falling across her in the low northern light.

Up close the building was worse and better than it had seemed from the water. Worse, because the rot was everywhere—in the sill logs, in the steps, in the great front doors hanging open on hinges that had surrendered years ago. Better, because it was unmistakably a church, unmistakably loved once: someone had carved a fish into the lintel, and someone else had scratched initials into the doorframe at the height of a child, and the brass plate beside the doors still read, in letters worn nearly smooth, KETTLE LANDING UNION CHURCH — ALL ARE WELCOME.

All are welcome. Ilsa put her hand flat against the doorframe and stood there a moment, breathing.

Inside, the nave was a ruin of light. The roof had holes in it, and the afternoon came down through them in slanted columns, and dust hung in the columns, and the pews stood in their rows with their seats buckled by wet and dry and wet again, sixty springs of snowmelt finding its way in. Bird droppings whitened the altar rail. But someone had been living here—Ilsa saw it immediately, saw it the way she'd learned to see camps in her three years of fieldwork: a bedroll in the corner behind the organ, tinned food stacked with obsessive neatness, a rain barrel under the best of the roof leaks, a camp stove, and everywhere, everywhere, paper. Notebooks, printouts, maps, pages torn and annotated and pinned to the walls between the hymn boards, so that the whole nave had become a single enormous document, a mind made visible, Dr. Elias Ansen's proof spread out on the walls of the last building the drowned town had left him.

Footsteps on the tower stairs. Slow, uneven, deliberate.

Ilsa turned, and Dr. Ansen came down into the nave.

She had prepared herself for a corpse and for a stranger, and he was neither. He was thinner than the man in the personnel photos—thinner by thirty pounds, easily—and his beard had gone fully white though he couldn't have been more than fifty-eight, and he moved like a man who had been alone so long that other bodies had become a category of weather, something to be assessed rather than greeted. He stopped four steps from the bottom and looked at her for a long time.

"You're Voss," he said. His voice was rusted. "Junior hydrographer. You ran the north transects." He frowned, and the frown was terrible, because it was lucid. "You ran. They told me you ran."

"I hid," Ilsa said. Her throat was tight. "In the fish camp. I'm sorry. I didn't know what else—"

"No." He raised a hand, and the hand shook. "No, running was correct. Running was the only correct move available. I told Marta to run and she wouldn't and—" He stopped himself. Closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet and furious. "Who else is with you?"

"Captain Reyes. Of the Marrow."

Something crossed Ansen's face at the name—recognition, or the ghost of it, some file pulled from a drawer deep in him. "Reyes," he repeated. "That name was on the manifest of the boat that took us upriver twelve years ago. Before my time on this project. Or—" he paused, and his head tilted, birdlike, "—or after. Time is difficult up here. The bell helps. I ring it at slack water so the town knows the hour. Towns should know the hour."

"The town is underwater," Ilsa said gently.

"Yes," Ansen agreed pleasantly. "That is the entire point."

* * *

Sable came up the aisle while Ansen was showing Ilsa the wall maps, and the change in him was immediate and unsettling: he straightened, he smoothed his shirt, he became, in patches, the professional the photographs remembered. He shook her hand with both of his. He said, "Captain. You came through the lower Narrows at first light—I heard your engine. Nobody runs the lower Narrows at first light."

"Somebody does," Sable said.

Ansen laughed, a short cracked bark, and then pressed his knuckles to his mouth as if to keep anything else from escaping.

He led them through the evidence because he could not stop leading them through the evidence; Ilsa understood within minutes that this was the shape his survival had taken, that the truth was the only thing keeping him upright, and that he had carried it alone in this bell tower for eleven months the way a man carries a beam. The soil cores. The foundation surveys. The parish register, wrapped in oilcloth, listing baptisms and burials through the final winter. The government memo, photocopied in 1987 by a clerk whose conscience had cost him his pension, ordering the reservoir filled on schedule despite the documented presence of forty-one residents who had refused relocation and whose removal had been assigned, in a single chilling passive construction, *to local authorities.*

"To local authorities," Sable read aloud, and her voice had gone very quiet and very hard. "Which meant Odell."

"Wray Odell signed the fill order," Ansen said. "Wray Odell's first directorship was funded by the men who profited from filling it early. Wray Odell has spent sixty years making sure this valley stayed quiet, and now the river is going to shift and give the town back, and he has hired Corven Hale to make sure that when it gives the town back, there is nothing left in it but water." He gestured at the walls, at the whole paper cathedral of it. "This is why the fleet is coming. Not treasure. Us. The records. Me. The truth has a location, Captain, and locations can be sunk."

Outside, far downstream, faint as a headache, came the sound of diesel generators.

Ilsa looked at Sable. Sable was already looking at the door, doing the arithmetic Ilsa could see her doing—the tide, the distance, the barges, the closing ice—and Ilsa watched her arrive at the same number Ilsa had arrived at days ago in the fish camp, feverish, listening to engines across the channel.

"They're coming for him," Ilsa said. "For all of this."

"Then we don't be here when they arrive," Sable said. But even as she said it, the light through the roof holes was dying, the slack water was ending, the river was beginning to move again—and Ansen had gone to the tower stairs and was pulling the bell rope with both hands, ringing the hour for a town that could no longer hear it, and Ilsa understood that they could not simply take him out the way you take a survivor out. Whatever they carried down from this steeple, they would have to carry all of it.

Below them, through the clear green water, the tricycle lay in the street, waiting to be seen by someone, anyone, at last.
