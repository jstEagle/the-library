## 17. The Man Who Flooded It

The bell tower smelled of gull lime and old varnish, and Dr. Elias Ansen sat in it like a man who had been sitting there so long he had begun to take on the qualities of the wood.

He was thinner than the photographs in the survey file had suggested—Sable had read that file twice on the run upriver, by lantern, while Ilsa slept—and his beard had gone white in patches, as though frost had claimed him one finger at a time. He wore a surveyor's jacket with the insignia cut off. His hands, when he lifted them to show them they were empty, trembled not from fear but from something older and more settled than fear. The tremor of a man who has rehearsed a confession for years against the day someone would finally come to hear it.

"You came up the Narrows," he said. His voice was rusted from disuse, from whispering. "In what? A cargo boat?"

"The *Marrow*," Sable said.

Ansen's eyes went past her, out the louvred opening where the bell hung silent above the drowned street, and something moved through his face like weather. "I watched your wake come around the point. I thought—I thought they'd sent someone at last. Or that Hale had found me." He laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "I've been watching wakes for eleven days. You start to see meanings in them."

Ilsa was crouched at the top of the ladder, keeping watch on the water below, but her head turned at his voice. "Dr. Ansen."

"Voss." He said her name gently, the way you'd touch a bruise. "You made it. I told them to run. I told all of them to scatter and I never believed—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You're feverish."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, but we'll come back to that." He looked at Sable again, and now she saw what was underneath the ruin of him: a precise man, a careful man, still in there somewhere, hoarding his facts like matches in a wet country. "You're the pilot Reyes hired. The one who was supposed to bring us out."

"I'm the pilot," Sable said. "Reyes is dead."

"I know." He closed his eyes. "I know what happened to Reyes. I need you to understand that I know all of it, because no one else does, and if I go into that water—" he nodded at the green flood below the tower, at the steeple shadow moving on it, "—then it goes with me. So I'm going to tell you. All of it. And you're going to want me to stop, and hurry, and get to the part about the money or the salvage or whatever brought you here, and I'm asking you to let an old man do this in order."

Sable leaned against the bell frame. Below them, through gaps in the tower floor, the flooded town lay in its long green light: rooflines soft with silt, a fence row standing in eight feet of water, the schoolhouse chimney leaning like a drunk against nothing. Slack water held everything still. Somewhere under that glass-flat surface were streets with names, and doors, and the small domestic archaeology of four hundred lives.

"Start anywhere," she said. "We don't have much slack."

* * *

Ansen started, as it turned out, with a map.

He drew it on the back of a sonar printout with a carpenter's pencil worn to a stub, kneeling, using the tower floor for a table, and his hands steadied as soon as they had work. That was the thing about him, Sable thought, watching. He wasn't mad. He was a man who had carried a load too heavy for one spine, and the carrying had bent him, but the load itself was intact.

"Kettle Landing," he said. "Population, last census before the flood: four hundred and six. Not a ghost town, not an abandoned site—a living town, with a church, a cannery, a school with forty-one children enrolled. The Kestrel Dam project needed the valley. The government offered relocation terms. Most people took them." The pencil moved. "Sixty-one households refused."

"I've heard the story," Sable said. "Holdouts. Everyone's heard it. They gave interviews until the water came, then the story died."

"The story didn't die." Ansen's pencil stopped. "It was killed. There's a difference, Captain, and the difference has a name, and the name is Wray Odell."

Ilsa had come down off the ladder. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself, fever-bright, listening the way starving people look at food.

"Odell was the assistant administrator for relocations," Ansen said. "A career man. Ambitious, meticulous, and—this is the part the histories leave out—present. He didn't manage the Kettle Landing removals from an office two hundred miles away. He came. He sat in kitchens. He drank coffee with holdouts and explained, patiently, over months, that the water was coming whether they signed or not, and that signing meant compensation and dignity, and not signing meant—" the pencil tapped the map, "—meant what? Everyone assumed it meant losing the compensation. Losing the fight. Being dragged out by constables at the end, humiliated, filmed, forgotten.

"That's what I assumed too, when I took the commission. Eleven years ago. The historical survey of the inundation zone—the one they finally authorized once the dam was decommissioned and the drawdown began. My job was to document what remained. Foundations, wells, the church, the cannery. Heritage mapping. A quiet retirement project for a quiet old academic." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Sable had seen in a long time. "They gave it to me because I was harmless. Because my findings would go into an archive. Because nobody reads archives.

"But I'm a thorough man. And the drawdown exposed things."

The light through the louvres had gone the color of weak tea. Downriver, faint, came the sound Sable had been dreading since the glacier's first groan at dawn: ice, working. Not calving yet. Negotiating.

"What things?" Ilsa asked.

"Bones," Ansen said simply. "The drawdown dropped the waterline nine meters over three years. Shorelines that hadn't seen air since the flood started coming up out of the green like—like a body surfacing. And along the old county road, which ran the high side of the valley, we started finding people."

* * *

He told it in order, as he'd asked, and the order was the horror of it. Not a massacre—one night, one mob, one mass grave, which could be named and mourned and fenced. It was slower than that, and administrative, and that was what made it unforgivable.

"They didn't drown in the flood," Ansen said. "That's the official record and it's almost true, which is how the best lies are built. Some did—the very last holdouts, the ones who rode their rooftops. But most of the sixty-one households left before the water. Left early. Left in boats, in trucks, in the night. And the record shows they arrived at resettlement camps, or shows nothing at all, which amounts to the same thing in an archive. Unaccounted. Dispersed. Assimilated elsewhere.

"Except they didn't disperse. Odell ran a second operation alongside the official one. Resisters were offered passage out of the valley—free transport, guaranteed placement, no questions. There was even a boat. A converted fish packer called the *Meridian*, running the valley road of the river twice a week through the last autumn before closure. People queued for it. They handed over their papers for processing—that was the procedure—and they boarded, and the *Meridian* went downriver.

"And the *Meridian* never reached the camps."

Sable heard her own pulse. The tower creaked around them, settling, patient.

"It went as far as the lower gorge," Ansen continued, "where the channel narrows and the current runs hard even in low water. There was a landing there—Odell's people built it, a private loading dock, logged as a construction supply point. The passengers were taken off at night. Papers burned in a stove on the dock—you can still find the stove, it's in the drawdown zone, I have it marked. And then—" the pencil drew a short, brutal line across the map, again, and again, "—they went into the water. Some shot. Some weighted. Some just put over the side in the dark in a current that does the rest. The gorge takes everything down and holds it. Scour holes sixty feet deep. Nothing surfaces. Nothing ever surfaced.

"Four hundred and six people in the town. Sixty-one refusing households—call it two hundred souls, counting children. The official record accounts for perhaps thirty deaths in the flooding itself. My survey, extrapolating from remains along the old road, from the *Meridian*'s manifest fragments, from the camp intake ledgers that show arrivals that never happened—" his voice cracked and he mastered it, "—my survey puts the number killed between a hundred and sixty and a hundred and ninety. Men, women, and children, murdered administratively, by a man who then wrote the final report on the relocation program, received a commendation, and spent the next fifty years becoming Wray Odell, elder statesman, the engineer whose vision tamed the Kestrel."

Ilsa had sat down at some point without seeming to decide to. Her face was gray under the fever flush. "The sealed records," she whispered. "The reason our commission's access was restricted. The reason the original flood surveys were classified—"

"Were mine," Ansen said. "Or rather, Odell's. When the decommissioning hearings started, he understood what the drawdown would do. Water goes down, truth comes up. So he had the old surveys reclassified—not destroyed, he was too careful for that, destruction leaves questions—sealed. Restricted access. And when the heritage commission finally forced a new survey through, he made sure it was funded through channels he controlled, staffed through channels he controlled, and slow. Glacially slow, if you'll forgive the word. He intended to run out the clock. Let the river finish the job. In twenty years the scour would collapse the banks, the remains would shift and scatter and become unattributable, and Kettle Landing would be a sad story about a flood, full stop.

"And then my sonar team found the *Meridian*."

* * *

The wind had come up while he talked. Sable felt it before she identified it—a shiver across the flat water below, the first texture on a surface that had been holding its breath. Slack water dying. She should be thinking about the channel, the closing gap, the fleet behind them. She was. But she stood still and let the old man finish, because some things you carry out of a place or you deserve what follows you home.

"Eleven days ago," Ansen said, "we ran the deep grid over the lower gorge, and there she sat. Intact. Cold water preserves; you know this. Hull sound. Cargo hold full of what the manifests say should have been crates of cannery equipment and was not. And scattered down-current from her, in the scour fan, a distribution pattern of—" he closed his eyes, "—of material I will not describe to a feverish young woman in detail. Enough. Enough to prove everything. Names matched to effects matched to positions. The *Meridian* is not a wreck, Captain. It is a grave with a hull around it, and it is legible. For the first time in fifty years, the crime had a shape.

"I did what a surveyor does. I documented. Full sonar mosaic, dive plan, chain of custody, the works. And I told my crew what we'd found, because a finding like that isn't yours—it belongs to the dead, and to the living, and I am only the instrument.

"I forgot that instruments can be bought."

He looked at Ilsa. "Your colleagues. Brandt, Okonkwo, the twins, Reyes. Good people. I'd have trusted any of them with my life. Odell's people got to three of them inside forty-eight hours—not with money, mostly. With fear. Families, debts, careers. One of them I believe they simply owned already; I've wondered for years why our funding kept flowing despite the delays. And the three of them decided, in the way that decent people decide terrible things, quietly, over one evening, that the finding was too dangerous to release. That it would die with the archive. That *I* might have to die with it, if I wouldn't agree.

"Reyes refused them. Whatever they offered, Reyes said no, and warned me, and that's why Reyes is dead—they were followed from the moment they turned. I sent everyone to scatter and I stayed with the data, because data can be re-hidden but a witness who talks is a witness who can be disproven, discredited, buried the way the report was buried. I cached the mosaic and the journal on the gravel bar—Voss knows the code—and I came here." He gestured, taking in the tower, the town, the whole drowned congregation of it. "Because if they're going to erase this place, they'll have to do it while I sit in its steeple. I confess the symbolism mattered to me. At my age you take your comforts where you find them."

"Why the salvage fleet?" Sable asked. "If the evidence is a wreck in the gorge, why spend millions on cranes and barges up here?"

Ansen's face changed, and Sable understood she had finally asked the question he'd been waiting for, the one that made the trembling stop.

"Because the river is turning," he said. "The dam is gone. The drawdown is done. And next spring, with the glacier retreating the way it is, the Kestrel is going to cut a new channel through the old townsite. I've modeled it. The scour fans will destabilize, the banks will slump, and everything the water has kept for fifty years—the graves along the road, the *Meridian*, the stove with the paper ash—is going to wash downriver into the light. Piece by piece, over a season or two, but inevitably. Odell's own engineers know it; that model is why the decommissioning timeline was rushed, why the salvage permits were issued so fast, why three industrial barges are sitting in the Narrows in calving season when every sensible boat is tied up.

"They're not salvaging, Captain. Salvage recovers value. This is the opposite of salvage. They're going to break the *Meridian* up on the bottom, drag the scour fan, net and sift every fragment, and burn what they lift. Erasure. Total, professional, deniable erasure—before the river does what rivers do, which is give back everything it's given."

The wind strengthened. Below them the green surface wrinkled, and the steeple's reflection broke apart and reformed, broke apart and reformed, like something trying to remember its own face.

"He's eighty-one years old," Ansen said quietly. "Wray Odell. Dying, they say—there are photographs from the hearing last year, and the man is a husk. You'd think a dying man would want to make peace. But men like Odell don't repent. They *complete*. The flood was the work of his life and the murders were its foundation and he will not leave this earth with the foundation showing. Everything he has—the fleet, Hale, the sealed records, fifty years of careful men being careful—he has pointed it all at this valley, at this window of low water, because after this season the river takes the choice out of his hands forever.

"So it's now. It's these six tides. Us, or him."

* * *

Sable moved to the louvre opening and looked downriver, and what she saw took the breath she had been rationing.

The light on the water had gone strange—yellow-green, submarine, the color of the deep ice in the glacier face. Far upstream, above the Narrows, the white wall of the serac field had shifted, and even at this distance she could see it was no longer negotiating. Mists of spray stood off the ice front where nothing had stood an hour ago. The sound arrived late, rolling down the valley like a door slamming in a house the size of the sky.

"That's not the evening wind," she said.

"No," Ansen agreed, rising stiffly, gathering his maps with the tenderness of a man collecting children. "That's the beginning. We have less time than anyone thinks—including, I suspect, Mr. Hale, whose schedule was built by accountants and not by glaciers."

Isla was already at the ladder, and Sable saw her sway, saw her grip the rail, saw her refuse with her whole rigid body to fall. Fever, exhaustion, and now this: the knowledge that the dead of Kettle Landing had names, and that those names were in a cache on a gravel bar that three barges full of men with cranes were steaming toward.

"We get the cache first," Sable said. "Then we run."

"Downriver?" Ansen asked. "Through the Narrows? In this?"

"Downriver," Sable said, and heard in her own voice the pilot she had spent twelve years pretending not to be. "The river's giving it all back, Doctor. Every piece. Our job is to make sure it lands in daylight."

Above the Narrows, the ice groaned again—longer this time, deeper, a sound like the valley clearing its throat—and the first white birds of spray rose off the glacier front, catching the yellow-green light, beautiful and terrible, like the town below them shaking off fifty years of silence to say *remember me*.
