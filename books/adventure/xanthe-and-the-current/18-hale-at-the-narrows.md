## 18. Hale at the Narrows

The ice came down like a verdict.

Sable heard it before she saw it—a sound she had no honest name for, somewhere between a rifle shot and a mountain clearing its throat, rolling down the Narrows in echoes that doubled and redoubled off the granite until the whole gorge seemed to be speaking. Then the first berg came around the bend above the drowned town, house-sized, blue as a bruise, dragging a wake that slapped against the Marrow's hull even at a hundred yards.

"Ansen!" she shouted. "Get below! Ilsa—tie everything down that isn't already tied!"

They were at the church shallows, holding position in the eddy behind the steeple's broken shoulder, where the current split around the drowned bell tower and ran slack for thirty feet of merciful water. It was the only holding water left. Above them the Narrows was closing like a fist; below them the run downriver waited, a throat of white water no chart had ever blessed. Ansen stood at the rail with the journal clutched to his chest like a hymnal, staring up at the glacier's shoulder through the ragged mist, and for a moment Sable almost pitied him—a man looking at the thing that had drowned his life's work and finding it beautiful.

"Below," she said again, gentler. "You've carried it this far. Don't let a piece of ice carry it away."

He went. Ilsa stayed at the rail a heartbeat longer, her fever-bright eyes tracking the bergs, and Sable saw her doing the same arithmetic Sable was doing: how many tides left, how much river, how many ways to die between here and the delta.

Then the radio crackled, and the arithmetic got worse.

"Marrow." Corven Hale's voice, calm as a man reading a grocery list. "You're holding the only slack water in the Narrows, and my barge draws nine feet loaded. I'm coming to that eddy. You can share it or you can lose it. Your call, Captain."

Sable keyed the mic. "There's room in hell, Hale. Try there."

A pause. She could picture him on the wheelhouse of the *Iron Meridian*, his remaining barge, the one that had come through the calving with its crane arm folded and its hull scarred but whole. The other two were behind him now—one pinned against the east wall by a floe the size of a barn, one gone entirely, swallowed in the first minute of the calving with eleven men aboard whose names Sable would carry whether she wanted to or not. Hale had not mentioned them. Hale did not mention costs.

"I know you have Ansen," he said. "I know you have the survey. And I know, Sable—*Reyes*—that you're out of river. The lower Narrows is a rumor. Nobody's ever run it. You'll kill four people and lose the evidence, and I'll still get paid to drag the pieces out. Or you can come alongside, hand over the old man and the papers, and I'll let the Marrow through the gap behind me before the ice closes. That's not a threat. That's the last fair offer this river's going to make."

Behind her, Ilsa said quietly, "He's lying about the gap."

"I know."

"Ansen's journal says the Meridian's draft—"

"Ilsa." Sable turned. The girl looked terrible and steady, which Sable was learning was her natural state. "Get below with Ansen. Lock the wheelhouse door behind you. If I go in the water, the Marrow's yours—there's a key in the chart drawer, and the throttle is the lever on the left, not the right. Don't ask why. Just remember it."

Ilsa's throat worked. "You're coming back."

"That's an order, not a promise. Go."

The girl went. Sable stood alone in the wheelhouse with the engine idling below her feet like a held breath, and looked at the channel ahead.

Hale was right about one thing: the lower Narrows was a rumor. She'd read the water for three days now, the way it coiled and stacked against the walls, the way the calving had changed its voice. There was a line through it—she could almost see it, a thread of green water threading white—but *almost* was a word that drowned people. And she couldn't run it anyway, not with Hale's barge behind her, not with the ice closing from above. The gap he'd offered was real for another twenty minutes, maybe thirty. It would close. Everything closed.

The *Iron Meridian* came around the steeple's shoulder at half ahead, and the eddy shuddered.

She was bigger than Sable remembered from the landing—crane folded flat, deck crowded with men in orange, fenders hanging off her port side like a row of black teeth. Hale stood at her rail with a megaphone he didn't need; the Narrows carried a whisper like a shout.

"Come alongside, Captain. Port side to. Nobody has to get wet."

*Nobody has to get wet.* The oldest lie on any river. Sable put the wheel over and brought the Marrow in, gentle as a courtship, her port fenders kissing the barge's steel with a sound like a handshake. Lines went across—she let them, because fighting it here meant both vessels grinding against each other in the last slack water in the gorge, and she needed the eddy intact for what came next.

Men came aboard. Four of them, then six, moving with the unhurried competence of men who'd done ugly work before and been paid on time. They didn't rush her. That was the worst of it—the courtesy of it. Hale crossed last, stepping over the rail like a man visiting a neighbor, his rain gear beaded with meltwater, his face composed into something almost kind.

"The papers," he said. "And the old man. Then we're done, you and I. You get your river. I get my silence. Everybody floats."

"Ansen's not cargo."

"Everything's cargo. That's the first thing this river teaches." Hale glanced around her wheelhouse—the chart drawer, the lantern still swinging from the last night's work, the thermocouple of ordinary life—and his eyes came back to her with something that might have been respect in a better man. "You're good, Reyes. You were good twelve years ago, too, under the other name. I read that report. Both of them. The true one and the one they let stand." He smiled without warmth. "I'm the only man alive who can tell you the true one is still in a filing cabinet in Odell's basement. Think about that. Think about what it's worth to you."

"It's worth nothing," Sable said. "I stopped needing it."

"No," Hale said. "You stopped *wanting* it. Different animal."

One of his men moved toward the wheelhouse door. Sable didn't look at him. She kept her eyes on Hale and her hand on the throttle, and she did the last of the arithmetic.

The eddy behind the steeple was thirty feet of slack. The Meridian, port side to, occupied twenty of them. The current outside the eddy was already stiffening as the ice crowded the channel above, stacking the flow, and the bergs coming down were choosing their lines with the indifference of geology. A barge that size, in that eddy, with its crane arm folded high and catching wind—

"You should know something, Hale," she said. "The Marrow's engine is running. Has been since you hailed. And I've got my hand on the throttle."

Hale's composure didn't crack, but something behind it shifted, a man hearing water where he'd expected stone. "You'd take us both into the current? With your passengers below?"

"You keep saying *passengers* like it's a weakness. They're crew." She let the throttle forward one inch. The Marrow's hull trembled against the barge's, steel worrying steel. "Last chance to step back aboard."

"Take her wheel," Hale said quietly.

The man at the door lunged. Sable threw the throttle full ahead and put the wheel hard to starboard, and the world became noise.

* * *

The Marrow came off the Meridian's side like a knife leaving a sheath. The boarding line snapped taut and parted; a man went into the gap between the hulls and was gone—Sable heard the cry, saw the orange flicker, and the river took him the way it took everything, without malice and without mercy. The wheelhouse door burst open behind her and two of Hale's men filled it, and she had time for one thought—*Ilsa, the door, I told her to lock it*—before the first one reached her.

She was not a fighter. She was a pilot, and she fought the way pilots fight: with the boat. She dropped her shoulder into the wheel, swung the Marrow's bow hard, and the deck heeled; the man's lunge became a stumble, and she put the brass bell stand between them, then the chart table, then the doorframe, giving ground by inches and buying it with bruises. The second man got a hand in her collar and she drove her elbow back into whatever it found and heard him grunt and let go.

"Captain!" Ilsa's voice, from below, from the engine hatch—then the hatch banged open and Ilsa came up it with a fire axe in both hands, feverish, ferocious, holding it the way she'd probably held nothing in her life before this week. The men in the doorway looked at the axe, and at the girl behind it, and at the wheelhouse where the captain had the throttle wide open and the river ahead, and something in their arithmetic changed. Professional men. They knew a losing position when they stood in one.

Hale knew it too. He'd made it back over the rail to the Meridian's deck in the first seconds of chaos—Sable saw him there, megaphone forgotten, shouting orders she couldn't hear over the engine and the river. The Meridian's engines came up to full astern, trying to tear free of the eddy before the Marrow dragged her into the current, and the two hulls ground apart with a shriek that Sable felt in her teeth.

And that was when Dree Callas turned her wheel hard.

Sable saw it happen and didn't believe it, and then believed it completely, the way you believe a thing you've been afraid to hope for. The Meridian's bow was swinging out of the eddy, swinging downriver, swinging to run the Marrow down in the throat of the lower Narrows—and then it wasn't. Dree had the wheel over hard to port, the full opposite of everything the maneuver required, and the big barge wallowed, her stern slewing across the channel, her folded crane arm swinging out like a scythe. The barge's hull came around and grounded with a grinding, groaning impact on the gravel tongue below the church shallows—the one shoal, the one place in thirty miles of river where a hull that size could be stopped—and there she stuck, athwart the channel, her stern plugging the gap like a cork in a bottle.

Behind her, the ice came down.

Sable stood at her wheel with blood in her mouth—she'd bitten her tongue somewhere, or maybe her cheek, she'd inventory later—and watched the Meridian's deck dissolve into chaos. Men running. Hale at the rail, and even across the water she could see his composure finally, finally crack, the careful menace burned away, a man shouting at a river that had stopped listening. Dree stood at the Meridian's wheel with her hands flat on it, holding her course into the gravel, holding the channel shut, and she looked across the water at Sable and did not wave and did not smile. She just nodded once, the smallest nod on any river, and turned back to her wheel as the first berg came down on the Meridian's starboard quarter and struck like a hammer.

The hull boomed. The crane arm buckled. The barge ground deeper onto the shoal, and the channel behind her filled with white.

"Sable!" Ilsa was beside her, axe abandoned, hands on the rail. "The gap—below the shoal—there's still water, I can see it, but it's closing—"

"I see it."

The lower Narrows opened ahead of her like a held secret: a thread of green water along the west wall, maybe forty feet wide, maybe less, bracketed by standing waves and the sweep of the current and the ice coming down to meet it. No one had ever run it. No chart blessed it. The Marrow drew four and a half feet and Sable Reyes had spent her whole life learning what water kept from the people who only looked at it.

Ansen came up the hatch, white-faced, journal strapped to his chest under his coat with what looked like survey tape. He looked at the Meridian, aground and groaning, and at the ice burying the channel behind her, and he said nothing at all. There was nothing in any language for it.

"Hold on to something," Sable said, to all of them, to the boat, to the river, to the dead sister whose name she was finally done running from. "We're going through."

She put the Marrow's bow into the green thread of water, and the lower Narrows took her.

* * *

It was not running so much as being read aloud to. The river shouted its lines and she answered, wheel and throttle, the hull singing under her feet in frequencies she felt in her spine. The standing wave at the first bend lifted the bow and set her down soft—there was a cushion of water there, a pillow the charts would never know about, and she'd have wept if she'd had the time. The second bend was a lie: the water looked like it wanted the east wall, but the boil along the wall said rock just under, and she held west, west, trusting the boil, trusting the twelve years of miles she'd counted instead of years. Gravel hissed under the keel once, a long terrible whisper, and then the channel deepened and the whisper became a song and the song became the open river.

Behind her, through the wheelhouse glass, the Narrows closed. The last bergs came down into the gap and did not come out the other side. The Meridian was a shape, then a suggestion, then gone—Dree with it, or off it, she couldn't know, wouldn't know, and Sable put the grief in the same hold where she kept her sister and kept her hands on the wheel.

Ahead, the river widened. The light came down gold through the gorge mouth. In the chart drawer, under the spare key, Ilsa had left the sonar plates and Ansen's journal, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where Sable could reach them.

They had it. They had all of it. And sixty miles of open water between here and the delta, and a man named Wray Odell who would very soon learn that his silence had gone down with the ice.

Sable Reyes, born under another name she'd finally stopped flinching from, stood at the wheel of the Marrow and ran for the light.
