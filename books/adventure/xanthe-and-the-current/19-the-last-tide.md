## 19. The Last Tide

The glacier spoke first.

Not a crack this time—a groan, deep and structural, the sound a cathedral makes when it decides to become rubble. It rolled down the Narrows and through the hull of the *Marrow* and up through the soles of my boots into my teeth, and every hair on my arms stood up under my oilskin like the river itself had put its hand on me.

"Ansen," I said. "Get below. Ilsa, lash that case to the table—double line."

Dr. Ansen didn't move. He stood at the rail with his ruined face turned up toward the icefield above us, toward the white wall of the glacier's terminus two miles upstream and closing, and he was laughing. A thin, cracked sound, half sob, the laugh of a man who has carried a corpse of truth for thirty years and finally feels it kick.

"It's coming down," he said. "It's actually—it's giving it back. The river is giving it all back."

"The river doesn't give anything back," I told him, and took the wheel.

Behind us, wedged against the church shallows where Dree Callas had planted her barge across the channel like a cork in a bottle, Hale's remaining vessel was screaming. Not the men—the steel. I could hear the bow plates flexing as the current piled against her beam-on hull, hear the mooring lines sing, hear the crane boom groan on its slewing ring as the whole clumsy mass of her fought to turn in water too narrow and too fast for turning. Dree's barge lay athwart the channel behind her, and between them they had made a dam of themselves, and the Narrows does not forgive dams.

I threw the *Marrow*'s engines full ahead and swung her nose downstream.

* * *

You have to understand what running the lower Narrows means when the calving starts. On an ordinary day it's a canyon of green water with a floor of drowned town, tight enough in places that you could spit from one wall to the other, and the current runs four knots where it runs kind and seven where it doesn't. When the glacier calves, everything changes. The ice comes off the face in bergs the size of houses, hits the water like artillery, and throws up displacement waves that run downstream faster than any boat I've ever steered. Then the bergs themselves catch the current and come hunting. They don't drift, exactly. They *choose*. The river sorts them, sends the small ones to the eddies and the big ones down the throat of the channel, and if you're in the throat when they arrive, you are a thing the river has decided to be rid of.

I had one advantage, and it wasn't courage. It was that Ilsa Voss had spent three feverish days teaching me the survey data by lantern light, and the survey data held something no chart had ever recorded: a side channel, left of the main throat, that had opened when the old dam failed and never been sounded because no one thought it navigable. Six feet at low water, the sonar said. Eight now, with the tide pushing. Narrow as a coffin and twice as long, walled with rock that the flood had scoured smooth as glass.

No one had ever run it. No one had ever wanted to. That was the whole point.

"Hold on," I said, and put the *Marrow* into the gap.

* * *

The walls came at us close enough to read the stone. Old flood-marks striped the rock like tree rings—brown, brown, brown, then a band of pale mineral where the dam's impoundment had sat for decades, then brown again—and we slid past them at eight knots with a hand's width of daylight between my rub rail and the cliff. Ansen had gone below after all; I could hear him down there reciting names. Not praying. Reciting. Kettle Landing's dead, a roll call he'd built from sealed records, and he was saying them out loud into the belly of my boat as though the water needed to hear them before it would let us pass.

Ilsa came up beside me, wet-haired, gray-faced, both hands locked on the wheel housing. She'd tied Ansen's case to herself with a strap, the way you'd carry a child through fire. The sonar printouts were inside it. The journal. The photographs Odell had paid three salvage fleets and God knew how many men to keep at the bottom of a lake.

"How far?" she shouted over the engine noise.

"Half a mile of this," I shouted back. "Then it spits us out below the Narrows proper, above the old ferry bar. After that it's open water and we run."

"And them?"

I looked back once. Through the gap behind us, framed in rock, I could see the top of Hale's barge superstructure, still pinned sideways in the main channel, and beyond it the glacier's face, and the glacier's face was *moving*. Not calving yet—shedding. Whole acres of it sloughing forward like a man shrugging out of a coat, and the sound that came off it was not a sound but a pressure, a fist of air shoved down the canyon ahead of the ice.

"They're out of time," I said. "So are we. Watch the starboard wall—I can't see the bend."

She watched. I steered. The *Marrow* ran.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next four minutes, and I have gone over it so many times since that I no longer trust the order of things, only the facts:

A wave found us in the narrows—reflected off a bend, doubled back on itself, a standing shoulder of green water taller than my mast—and the *Marrow* climbed it the way a deer climbs a fence, all bow and prayer, and hung there at the top with her propeller singing in air, and Ilsa's hand found mine on the wheel and gripped it, and we came down the far side straight and true because I had put the rudder over at the crest and the river let me.

A berg the size of a barn entered the side channel behind us. I heard it before I saw it—that grinding, planetary scrape of ice on rock—and I did not look back again, because looking back is how pilots die, and I had done enough dying for one lifetime already.

And somewhere in the middle of it, in the worst of it, with spray hitting the wheelhouse windows like gravel and the engine temperature climbing and Ansen's voice below decks saying *Marta Oduya, ferry keeper, Marta Oduya*, I understood that I was not running from Hale anymore. I was running the river. Just the river. The way I had run it at nineteen, at twenty-five, at every age before I learned to be afraid of my own name. My hands knew what to do. They had always known. Twelve years of cargo and sandbars and no fixed address had not been hiding. They had been practice.

The channel spat us out.

One moment: rock walls, roar, white water. The next: open sky, the broad drowned valley below the Narrows spreading out ahead of us, calm as a held breath, the church steeple breaking the surface a quarter mile astern with the water sliding off it in sheets. Behind the steeple, the mouth of the side channel discharged its freight—foam, spray, and then the berg, emerging from the rock like a whale surfacing, huge and white and slow, riding the last of the flood out into open water.

And behind the berg, filling the entire head of the valley, the glacier's face came down.

* * *

I have seen buildings fall. I have seen a bridge let go in high water, seen a grain elevator fold like paper. Nothing prepared me for watching a glacier die. It did not collapse so much as *kneel*—the whole terminus dropping forward in stages, each stage bigger than the last, each impact throwing a shockwave across the water that hit the *Marrow* like a series of fists. Ice rained. Actual rain of it, shards and slabs and powder, hissing into the river around us for a hundred yards in every direction. I got the wheelhouse shut and put us behind the steeple, using the old church as a shield the way you'd use a wall in a gunfight, and we sat there in the lee of a drowned town's last standing bone while the world came apart upstream.

Through the spray and the whiteout I saw the fleet's barge make its choice.

Hale had gotten her turned—God knows what it cost him, or maybe I do know, maybe that's why there were no more radio calls from Dree's position—and she came down the main channel under full power, running the same water I'd refused to run, chasing the wake of a boat she could no longer see. For one moment she looked almost majestic, that much steel moving that fast through that much violence, bow up, wash boiling, the crane boom lashed down and streaming spray like a banner.

Then the river sorted her.

The first berg took her bow off. I heard it even over the calving—a crunch like the earth biting down—and the barge slewed, and the second berg, the big one from the side channel, caught her amidships and simply did not stop. Steel folded. The superstructure went flat. The crane boom cartwheeled end over end and vanished, and then the river took the rest, rolling her under with a gulp of foam and debris, and the water closed over the place where Corven Hale's ambition had been with the terrible indifference of water, which is the only eulogy the Kestrel ever gives anyone.

I waited for grief. It didn't come. What came instead was colder and older: the memory of every pilot who ever went down in these waters believing the river owed them a living. The river owes nothing. It keeps accounts, but it never pays out. Hale had spent a year trying to buy the Kestrel, and the Kestrel had accepted the payment and given nothing back, and some part of me that I am not proud of noted that the ledger balanced.

"Sable." Ilsa's hand on my arm. Her voice very quiet. "Dree."

I looked at the shallows where Dree Callas had blocked the channel. Her barge was still there—or most of it was, grounded hard on the church bar, canted, taking water over the stern, but alive, and on her aft deck small figures were moving, hauling a skiff off its davits, getting people off. One figure stood apart from the work, at the rail, facing us. Even at that distance I knew the set of those shoulders. Dree raised one arm—not waving, signaling, the old deckhand signal, two fingers then a fist: *clear water behind me. Go.*

Every debt called in, I thought. And one paid forward.

I rang up full ahead and took the *Marrow* out from behind the steeple, downriver, into the open valley, carrying the dead town's testimony in a canvas case strapped to a fever-burned surveyor's chest, carrying a mad old man who was still reciting names, carrying twelve years of my own buried name up into the daylight where it could finally drown or breathe.

* * *

We ran until dark.

Below the Narrows the Kestrel gentles into braids and bars, and I threaded them from memory while the ice field behind us ground itself into silence and the displacement waves flattened one by one, and by the time the sun went down behind the western ridge the river was just a river again, brown and patient, carrying silt and secrets with equal disinterest. Ilsa slept sitting up against the wheelhouse door with the case still strapped to her, one hand on it even in sleep. Ansen came up at dusk and stood beside me without speaking for a long time, and then he said, "You could have run the other way. At any point. You could have taken your boat and your name and gone."

"I don't have another name," I said. "That's the point. I never did. I just kept the real one in a drawer for twelve years and called the drawer a life."

He nodded slowly, the way old men nod when a young person says something they'd worked out decades ago and paid for worse. "Tomorrow," he said, "there will be hearings. Men in rooms. They will want you to say less than you know."

"They'll get more," I said. "All of it. Under my own name. Sable Reyes, master of the *Marrow*, license number whatever-the-hell-it-was before they took it." I heard myself say it out loud for the first time in twelve years—my name, the whole of it, unburied—and the river carried the sound away downstream the way it carries everything, which is to say completely, which is to say forever.

Ansen smiled with half his ruined face. "Good," he said. "The dead will want it read properly."

He went below. I stayed at the wheel.

* * *

Near midnight, with the moon up and the water glass-calm, I saw the light.

A skiff, running dark except for a single hand lantern, working down the braided shallows with the careful, economical strokes of someone who knows the water. I watched it come. I didn't hail it. I stood at the rail of the *Marrow* with my hands in my pockets and let it approach, and when it drew alongside, the lantern rose and lit a face I'd last seen across a channel of killing water, and Dree Callas looked up at me and said:

"Heard you were hiring."

I looked at her a long moment—this woman who had turned her wheel against a fortune to buy me forty seconds, who had lost her berth and nearly her life for it, who was sitting in a leaking skiff at midnight with nothing but a lantern and a grin.

"Deckhand pay," I said. "Bad food. Worse hours."

"Same as always, then." She tossed me a line, and I caught it, and that was the whole ceremony.

I tied her off, and the *Marrow* rode easy on her anchor in the black water, and downstream—miles downstream, days downstream—there were hearing rooms and men in suits and a name, Wray Odell, that was about to stop being a shadow and start being a target. Ilsa stirred in her sleep. Ansen's voice murmured below decks, still reading his roll of the dead, softer now, almost finished.

I put my hand on the wheel I wouldn't need until morning, and I counted—not miles, for the first time in twelve years, but days. Two days to the capital. Three to the hearing. Four, maybe five, until I stood in a room and said my name out loud and made it mean something.

The current carried us on. I let it.
