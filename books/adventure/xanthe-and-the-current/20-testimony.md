## 20. Testimony

The pen they gave me was cheap—a government ballpoint, translucent barrel gone yellow with age, ink that skipped if you held it wrong. I turned it over in my fingers while the clerk waited, and I thought about all the pens I'd signed things with in twelve years. Fuel manifests at landings where nobody looked twice at me. A lease on a storage shed in Grayling Bend, under a name I'd invented on a bus bench. The bill of sale for the *Marrow*, which was the only signature in all of them that had ever felt like mine.

"Sable?" Ilsa said softly, from the chair beside mine.

I hadn't told her yet. Neither of them. Dree was out in the corridor with her cap in her hands, and Ansen was two rooms down giving his own account to a stenographer who kept stopping to ask him to repeat things, because Dr. Elias Ansen had spent six weeks alone in a bell tower and had lost whatever talent he'd once had for speaking in straight lines.

But this part—the name—that was mine to give.

"The name on your license," the clerk said. He was young, patient, bored in the way of men who process other people's catastrophes by the ream. "We need the legal name matching your certification records."

"My certification records are the problem," I said.

He blinked. Ilsa leaned forward. Outside the tall windows of the provincial building, the Kestrel ran high and brown with melt, carrying the last of the calving season down toward the sea. Somewhere upriver, behind a boom line and a no-entry order signed three days ago, the rooftops of Kettle Landing sat in their green water, waiting to be seen.

I set the pen down without writing anything.

"Before I sign," I said, "you should pull the file. Twelve years back. The survey run out of Alder Fork—the one where the vessel *Corriedale* was lost with one soul aboard, and the pilot's license was revoked on findings of negligent navigation." My voice came out level. I'd rehearsed it against the engine noise for two hundred miles, and it held. "Pull that file. Because the findings were falsified, and the pilot didn't lose that boat. She lost her sister. And then she lost her name, because it was the only thing left anyone could take."

The clerk's boredom fell off him like a coat.

"What was the name?" he asked.

I looked at the window a moment—at the water, doing what water does, going on—and then I said it out loud for the first time since the hearing that broke me.

"Yarrow. Sable Yarrow."

Saying it was like pulling a splinter that had been in so long the flesh had grown around it. There was relief in it, and there was blood too. Ilsa made a small sound beside me—not surprise exactly. She'd read Ansen's journal. She'd known for days, and had the grace not to say.

"Yarrow," the clerk repeated, already typing. The letters went into the record and became permanent, and nothing in the room changed, and everything did.

* * *

They gave us three days before the hearing, which was two more than I wanted and one fewer than I needed. I spent them aboard the *Marrow* at the government moorage, under orders not to leave the district, which suited me fine because I didn't want to go anywhere. For the first time in twelve years, staying put didn't feel like a trap. It felt like an anchor holding.

Dree found me on the roof of the wheelhouse the second evening, where I'd gone to count barges out of habit. The salvage fleet was impounded downstream—all three hulls, or what remained of them. Two were scrap. The third, Hale's, sat low at its lines with the ice damage stitched over in weld bead, evidence now, every scar catalogued.

"You could still run," Dree said, settling beside me with the ease of a woman who'd spent years sitting beside me on that same roof, back when she was my deckhand and I was someone else. "Hearing's not for two days. You've got a fast boat and I know the watch rotations down at the boom."

"I know you do," I said. "You told them yourself, didn't you. That's why they posted the extra man."

She grinned, unashamed. "Told them the second day. Figured if you were going to run, better somebody official be watching close enough to appreciate it." Her grin faded into something older and more careful. "You're really going to sit in that room and say it all out loud. Under Yarrow."

"That's the whole point of the room."

"It'll cost you the *Marrow*." She said it flat, the way you call out a bar depth. "Revoked license gets reinstated retroactive or it doesn't, but either way, the moment your name's public, every creditor, every reporter, every cousin of every man who ever lost cargo on your runs—they'll come looking. Reyes was clean. Reyes had twelve spotless years. Yarrow's got a drowned boat and a falsified report, even if they overturn it. Mud sticks."

I watched the water carry a length of glacial ice past the pilings, white turning gray as it took on the river.

"Dree," I said, "do you know why I named her the *Marrow*?"

"Always figured it was a joke about bones."

"It's because marrow's the part of you that makes new blood." I said it before I could decide whether it was too honest. Too late. Honest was the whole discipline now; I was practicing it like scales. "Reyes was skin. Good skin—I grew it myself, wore it well. But it wasn't making anything. I've been running cargo up and down this river for twelve years keeping the wound closed, and you know what I figured out, sitting in that bell tower watching Ansen try to remember how sentences work?"

"What?"

"The wound doesn't close from hiding. It closes from air." I shrugged, feeling foolish and steady at once. "Odell flooded a town with people in it and buried the proof for fifty years because he couldn't stand the air either. I'm not going to be him. Not even a small version of him, not even just about me."

Dree was quiet a long moment. Then she knocked her knuckles twice on the wheelhouse roof, the old signal—*all secure, make way*—and stood.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I never believed the report. Even at nineteen, even hating you for leaving without a word. The woman who taught me to read water off a boil didn't misread a channel." She paused at the ladder. "And for what it's worth twice over—if they give you back the license, I know a captain hiring."

She went down the ladder before I could answer, which was her way of being kind about it.

* * *

The hearing room had been a courtroom once and still wore the trappings: high wainscoting, a judge's dais repurposed for a panel of three, brass fittings gone dull. But there was no jury and no gallery—just the panel, a court reporter, a hydrologist with a laser pointer he kept clicking nervously, and a long table where Ilsa sat with boxes of Ansen's field notes squared in front of her like ammunition.

I testified in the morning.

They'd offered me a statement to read—one of the investigators had drafted it, clean and chronological, names spelled right, dates verified. I read the first paragraph and stopped.

"With respect," I said to the panel, "I'd rather tell it myself."

The chairman—a dry woman named Okafor with reading glasses on a chain—considered me over the top of them. "In your own words, Captain."

So I started at the beginning, which was not the Narrows and not the survey camp. It was twelve years earlier, the *Corriedale* riding light on the spring freshet, my sister Wren in the bow with the sounding pole, laughing at me because I wouldn't run the shortcut past Alder Fork and she thought I was being careful when really I was being proud—we were going to take the long safe route and show the commission how thorough we were, and the flood that killed her came down the long safe route instead, a wall of water off a slide nobody had any reason to expect, and I lived because I was aft, near the wheel, and she died because she was forward, doing the job I gave her.

I told them about the investigation. About the surveyor named Brandt who measured the debris line wrong—or didn't measure it wrong, but wrote it wrong, because Wray Odell's office had a finding it preferred and Brandt had a mortgage. About the hearing where I was too gutted to fight, where my lawyer was a public defender who'd never seen a river chart, where the falsified numbers went into the record and my license went into the shredder and the whole machinery closed over the whole thing like water over a stone.

"And you understood, at the time, that the findings were false?" Okafor asked.

"No." I said it plainly. "That's the part I need on the record. I didn't understand it. I believed it. For four years I believed I had killed my sister through negligence, and I built a life on the bottom of that belief. It wasn't until Ilsa Voss put Ansen's journal in front of me—until I saw Odell's signature routing the original debris measurements to a private archive—that I understood the report wasn't a mistake. It was a purchase."

The court reporter's keys clattered. Nobody moved.

"Why does that distinction matter?" the third panelist asked. He was younger, and his question had an edge of genuine puzzlement rather than procedure.

"Because Odell's whole method was letting people believe true-feeling lies," I said. "He let the town believe the relocation was generous. He let the resisters believe they'd be compensated. He let the commission believe the flooding was orderly. And he let me believe I was guilty, because a guilty woman keeps quiet, and a quiet woman changes her name and stops asking questions about the water." I looked down at my hands on the table. They were steady. Twelve years of reading current will teach your hands things your heart takes longer to learn. "He built a whole career on the assumption that people would rather carry a false weight than set it down in public. I carried mine for twelve years. I'm setting it down now, and I'm telling this panel that everything I testify to afterward should be weighed knowing I spent twelve years testifying to a lie against myself. If I could be fooled that thoroughly, so could your archives. Check everything. That's my professional recommendation."

Okafor almost smiled. Almost.

"Noted, Captain Yarrow."

Hearing her say it—*Captain Yarrow*, in that flat procedural voice, entered into the record of a government proceeding—I felt something shift in my chest like ballast settling. Not lighter, exactly. Truer. A boat rides different when the load is stowed right.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the others, and I learned what it costs to sit and listen to your own history read aloud by strangers.

The hydrologist presented the sonar survey—Ansen's survey, recovered from the cache on the gravel bar, water-spotted but legible—and walked the panel through the bathymetry of Kettle Landing. The town rose off the screen in contour lines: the grid of Front Street, the mill foundation, the church with its steeple still standing above the silt line. And threaded through it, the thing the survey team had split over, the thing Ilsa had run from her own crew to protect: the anomaly clusters. Human remains, unrecorded, unrelocated, present in the flooded streets in numbers the official relocation ledger could not survive.

"Fifty-one residents were listed as successfully relocated," the hydrologist said. "The sonar data is consistent with a significantly higher number of individuals remaining in the town at the time of inundation."

A silence followed that had texture, weight, temperature. In the back of the room a man cleared his throat and stopped himself.

Then Ilsa testified.

She was fever-thin still, moving carefully, but her voice was clear as cold water. She walked them through the night the team split—through Ansen announcing he was going public, through the crew members whose travel and families had been quietly arranged-for by consultants with Odell Foundation letterheads, through the argument on the bank and the shot fired over the skiff, through six weeks alone in the willows eating what the fish camp yielded, listening for engines. She never dramatized. She laid it down fact by fact like a woman laying planking, each piece fitted to the next, until the panel could walk across on it.

When she finished, the chairman asked her the question I'd been dreading for her.

"Ms. Voss. Your lead surveyor, Dr. Ansen, has given testimony today that is detailed but—as his attending physician notes—complicated by his ordeal. Your account and his corroborate. Captain Yarrow's account establishes motive and mechanism for the original suppression. Is there anything in the documentary record that ties Administrator Odell directly to the destruction of the original debris analysis? Anything beyond inference?"

Ilsa looked at me. I looked at the table.

"There's the journal," she said. "Ansen kept duplicates. He told us—he told *me*, the night before—" Her voice caught, steadied. "He said he always mailed a copy somewhere the Foundation would never think to look. We assumed the cache on the gravel bar was it. It wasn't. The cache was the survey only."

"Where is the duplicate?"

Ilsa took a breath, and I realized she was smiling, very slightly, the way you smile at a river that finally shows you its channel.

"He mailed it to the one place in the district guaranteed to have a fixed address, a resident of twelve years' standing, and no connection to the dam administration whatsoever." She turned fully toward me, and the whole room turned with her. "It's been sitting in the *Marrow*'s mail hold at the Grayling Bend landing since before any of us met. Addressed to Sable Reyes. Ansen picked the name off the cargo manifests years ago—he said it was the only boat on the upper river that never once ran a charter for the Foundation."

Every face in the room came around to me. Okafor removed her glasses entirely.

"Captain," she said. "Do you have this document?"

I had found it the night before, in the mail hold, wedged behind two years of catalogs—a fat waterproof envelope, postmarked eleven months back, addressed in a surveyor's cramped hand. I'd sat with it on my bunk for an hour before opening it, and inside had found Ansen's duplicate journal, complete, with the correspondence attached: Odell's own letters, on administrator's stationery, instructing that the debris analysis be "resolved administratively" and the originals "archived beyond routine access." Signed. Dated. The kind of paper that ends careers and opens graves.

I'd slept well afterward, for the first time in twelve years, with it locked in the *Marrow*'s strongbox ten feet away.

"I do," I said. "It's aboard my vessel at the government moorage, under guard. I can have it here within the hour."

"Do so," Okafor said. Then, quieter, almost to herself, she added: "Twelve years you kept that name clean enough that a hunted man trusted it with the truth."

"Yes, ma'am," I said. "Best cargo I ever carried and didn't know it."

* * *

They reconvened the findings two days later, and I stood in the hearing room with Dree on one side and Ilsa on the other and heard the panel read them out in order, like depths off a sounding line.

The 12-year-old findings on the loss of the *Corriedale*: vacated. My license: reinstated, retroactive, the revocation struck from the record as procured by fraud. Wray Odell: referred for prosecution, his Foundation's contracts frozen, his name—which had been on buildings and bursaries and a plaque at the dam overlook—already coming down faster than any crew could be hired to take it down, because shame travels faster than cranes. The salvage operation: dissolved, its remaining assets seized. And Kettle Landing itself: recommended for immediate protective designation, pending memorial survey, entry forbidden, the river ordered to keep what it holds until it can be held properly.

Afterward, in the corridor, the young clerk from the first day handed me the certified copy of the amended record. My name sat at the top of the page. *Yarrow, Sable.* Both names, actually—the record noted the alias formally, *also operating as Sable Reyes*, and I found I didn't mind. Reyes had done good work. Reyes had kept the light on. She deserved to exist in the record too, the way a river deserves its old channels even after it cuts a new one.

Dree read it over my shoulder and whistled. "Captain Yarrow," she said, trying it out. "Got a ring. Though fair warning—the boys at the fuel dock are going to want the whole story, and the story's got a bell tower and a ghost town in it. You'll never buy a drink again."

"That sounds expensive," I said.

"You'll manage." She clapped my shoulder, hard, the deckhand's blessing, and went to go sign papers of her own.

Ilsa lingered. She had the amended record too, folded into the pocket of a jacket that still smelled faintly of woodsmoke and fish camp. For a moment neither of us spoke, and the corridor filled up with the sound of the building breathing—doors, footsteps, the distant murmur of the river against the seawall below the windows.

"They're offering me the memorial survey," she said. "Lead position. Mapping the town properly, respectfully, for the families. It'll take years."

"You'll be good at it," I said. "You read water."

"I read *paper*," she said. "You read water. Which is why I'm telling you the survey needs a pilot who knows the reach, and the posting comes with a government contract, and—" she hesitated, and for the first time since the fish camp, the junior surveyor looked genuinely uncertain, "—the applicant pool is apparently one person deep, if she wants it."

I looked out the window. The Kestrel ran high and bright with the last of the melt, and far upstream, behind the boom line, a church steeple stood in green water over a town that had waited fifty years for someone to say its name out loud. Below the seawall, tied snug at the government moorage with her lines doubled, the *Marrow* rode the current the way she always had—patient, ready, needing only a heading.

Twelve years I'd measured my life in miles, because miles don't ask questions. Standing there with my real name on government paper and my sister's report vacated and a drowned town finally surfacing into the light, I understood that the miles had been carrying me somewhere after all. Rivers do that. Every mile is also a year, if you let it be.

"I'll want to see the terms," I said, and Ilsa's whole face changed, and outside the gulls wheeled over the water, and the current went on bearing everything—ice, silt, truth—steadily down toward the sea.
