## 21. The Wreck Stays

The letter came in April, when the ice on the Marrow's rail had stopped being a threat and started being an ornament.

Ilsa Voss read it three times on the aft deck, her breath steaming, the river running high and brown with snowmelt below her. The government seal was embossed heavy enough to catch the light. *Department of Waterways and Historical Survey. Re: Site K-7, Kettle Landing. Designation: Protected Submerged Cultural Resource.* Below that, a paragraph of bureaucratic language that took her a moment to translate into plain speech: no salvage. No dredging. No cranes dragging the town apart for scrap and secrets. Kettle Landing would stay where it was, under the green water, whole.

She sat down on the cargo hatch because her knees told her to.

Twelve years of sealed records. A survey team that had gone upriver and come back as a rumor. Ansen's journal in evidence lockers now, photocopied into a hundred hands, his handwriting steady until the last pages where it wasn't. And at the end of all of it, this: a paragraph saying the drowned would be allowed to keep their town.

"You're smiling," Sable said from the wheelhouse door. She had a mug in each hand and the particular way of standing she'd developed since the hearings—like a woman who had set down something heavy and hadn't yet learned where to put her hands.

"I'm reading."

"You're smiling at government paperwork. That's either a miracle or a symptom." She handed over the coffee anyway. It was terrible, scorched, made by Dree, who believed that coffee was fuel and should taste like it.

"They're protecting it," Ilsa said. "The site. Officially. There's a buffer zone, a permit process—" Her voice did something embarrassing on the word *permit*, went soft with disbelief, and she covered it by drinking.

Sable leaned on the rail beside her and looked downriver, toward the Narrows, which were open again and would be until October. From here you couldn't see them. You could see the water going toward them, though, brown and patient, carrying everything it carried.

"Good," Sable said. Just that. But she said it the way she read sandbars—one word, and underneath it a whole chart of things she wasn't going to say out loud.

* * *

The second page of the letter was the one Ilsa kept turning back to.

*The Department further proposes the establishment of a Memorial Survey under the direction of a qualified lead surveyor, to document the site, identify remains where recoverable, and compile a register of the residents of Kettle Landing as of the date of inundation.*

*As of the date of inundation.* As of the night in 1949 when the gates closed and the water came up Main Street while some people were still packing, and some people were still refusing, and some people—the ones whose names Odell's people had worked so hard to keep off every list—were still in their houses.

They wanted her to do it. That was what the careful passive voice meant. *Under the direction of a qualified lead surveyor.* They had interviewed her twice already, in rooms with bad coffee and better intentions, and she had told them everything she knew about sonar grids and sediment layers and how to find a church steeple in sixty feet of green meltwater. She had thought they were evaluating whether she was competent.

They had been evaluating whether she could carry it.

She thought about Dr. Ansen, who had carried it alone for thirty years, hiding in a bell tower above his own drowned conscience, half-mad and entirely right. She thought about the fever, that week in the fish camp, when the dreams had been so vivid she'd walked the flooded streets in her sleep—past the hardware store, past the schoolhouse, past the houses with their doors still shut—and woken with river mud on her bare feet and Sable watching her from the wheelhouse doorway with an expression that said, without judgment, *I know exactly what water does to a person.*

"I'll take it," she said aloud, to the river, to the letter, to nobody.

Sable raised an eyebrow.

"The memorial survey. They offered me the lead." Ilsa folded the letter along its creases, then unfolded it, unable to stop handling it. "I'm going to say yes."

"You already said yes." Sable nodded at the letter. "You've got the yes-face. Same face you had when you siphoned diesel out of Hale's tender at two in the morning."

"That was theft."

"That was initiative." Sable drank her terrible coffee. "Say yes properly, then. Out loud. Some things want witnesses."

So Ilsa stood up straight on the deck of the *Marrow*, with the spring current pulling hard beneath her and gulls arguing over the pilings, and said it again like a vow: "I'm taking the job."

Somewhere downriver, sixty feet under green water, a town full of names waited to be counted. She was going to count them. Every one. Even the ones that would make important people flinch. *Especially* those.

* * *

Dree Callas came aboard on a Tuesday, carrying one duffel bag and wearing the expression of a woman arriving at a decision she'd made months ago and was only now admitting to.

"I heard you need a deckhand," she said to Sable, in the tone of someone stating a fact about weather.

"I heard you have a job," Sable said.

"Had." Dree dropped the duffel on the hatch cover. "The ferry company pays fine and the work is boring and every time we pass the Narrows I look up there and think about a barge going sideways in the ice with a man on it who deserved it, and I've decided I'd rather work for somebody interesting."

"Interesting is expensive," Sable said.

"So I hear. What are you paying?"

They haggled. Ilsa watched from the rail, arms crossed against the cold, and understood that the haggling was the ceremony—that Dree needed to be paid fairly because being paid fairly meant she wasn't a favor or a debt or a guilt, she was crew; and that Sable knew this, and was therefore fighting hardest over the numbers that mattered least. In the end they shook on a wage that made Dree snort and Sable shrug, which apparently meant both of them had won.

Then Dree turned to Ilsa and stuck out her hand, callused and chapped at the knuckles.

"Heard you're going to be a government woman," she said. "Lead surveyor. Fancy."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what? Like you spent a winter eating fish camp beans and reading a dead man's field notes by lantern light until you knew them better than he did?" Dree grinned. "Like you earned it? Fine. I won't say it like that."

Ilsa shook her hand. Dree's grip was honest and too strong, the grip of a woman who had once turned a wheel hard enough to block a channel behind a man with a gun, and had never once, in all the hearings afterward, pretended it had been anything but a choice.

"Fair warning," Dree added, jerking her chin at Sable. "She counts miles. Ask her sometime why."

"Dree," Sable said, in a voice that had a warning in it and, underneath the warning, something easier than a warning had any right to be.

"What? It's good luck now. She measures in years these days. Everybody knows." Dree picked up her duffel and headed for the fo'c'sle like she owned it, which, Ilsa suspected, within a month she would.

* * *

The stone went up in June.

There was a fight about it first, of course—there had to be, because nothing true about Kettle Landing had ever arrived without a fight. The Department wanted the memorial on the overlook above the Narrows, tasteful, granite, facing away from the water. The descendants' association—Ilsa had found them, or they had found her, letters forwarded through three offices until one day a woman named Marta Okonkwo was standing on the *Marrow*'s deck with photographs of a house that used to stand on Alder Street—wanted it at the waterline. Facing the water. Facing the town.

Ilsa testified for the waterline. It was the first time she'd spoken in a hearing room without her hands shaking, and she noticed that, and the noticing steadied her. "You don't put a headstone where the mourners can't reach it," she said. "You put it where they can put their hands on it. The water is the grave. Let them stand at the grave."

They put it at the waterline.

It was a single slab of gray basalt, quarried forty miles downriver, set into a concrete pad on the gravel shelf just above high water, close enough that in June flood the current licked its base. On its face, cut deep and filled with dark enamel so the letters would read against the glare:

**KETTLE LANDING**
*Inundated November 14, 1949*
*Here lived 312 souls.*
*Here lie 41 who refused to leave.*
*Their town remains below. Their names remain above.*

Forty-one. Not the eleven in the official record. Not the nineteen in Ansen's first accounting. Forty-one, confirmed by the memorial survey's first season—by Ilsa's divers, by her side-scan imagery, by the house-by-house census she had built from parish ledgers and school registers and the memory of old women in kitchen chairs who had been children on Alder Street when the sirens started. Forty-one names, each one argued over, each one verified, each one cut into stone where the water couldn't take it and neither could a man like Wray Odell, who was currently awaiting trial in a facility where the only thing he surveyed was the ceiling of his cell.

The dedication was small. The Department sent a deputy administrator who said appropriate things. A pastor from downriver said a psalm. Marta Okonkwo, who had lost a great-grandmother to the water and a grandfather to the silence afterward, pulled the cord on the canvas cover herself, and when the stone came into the light there was a sound from the crowd—not a gasp, not a cheer, something lower and more human, the sound a congregation makes.

Ilsa stood at the edge of it, in her good coat, and let the sound go through her.

Ansen would have hated the ceremony and loved the stone. She was sure of both. He had spent thirty years believing that the truth, once loose, would do its own work, and he had been mostly wrong—it had taken testimony, and hearings, and a riverboat captain willing to sign her real name to a falsified report twelve years cold—but he had been right about this part: that once it was loose, it could not be put back. The town stayed. The names stayed. The record stayed. Whatever else the river did now, it did it in the open.

Afterward, people drifted down to the waterline in twos and threes. Marta Okonkwo pressed her palm flat against the basalt and held it there a long time. An old man in a fishing vest found the name HALLORAN, E., age 34, and laughed and cried at once, and his daughter held his elbow. Children ran fingers along the carved letters like braille.

Sable stood apart, as she always stood apart, near the *Marrow*'s landing line. But she was looking at the stone, and her hand was in her coat pocket, and Ilsa—by now fluent in the captain's small languages—would have bet the survey budget that inside that pocket, her thumb was moving over something. A coin. A river-worn bead. Something of her sister's. The sister whose name was on the overturned report, whose death had finally been re-examined, re-heard, re-decided: *no fault of the pilot.* Twelve years late. True, at last, on paper.

"She'd have liked it," Ilsa said, coming to stand beside her. "Your sister. The stone."

"She'd have complained about the font," Sable said. Her voice was rough at the edges. "Then she'd have memorized every name on it in an afternoon, because she never met a list she didn't want to beat." She exhaled slowly, watching the current comb the stone's base. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one."

"And three hundred and twelve." Sable nodded at the top line. "That's the number that matters. The living ones. People forget that. The dead don't need the town remembered. It's for everybody who came after and wasn't told."

Ilsa looked at her. "Is that what you tell yourself?"

"No," Sable said. "That's what you taught me. I just quote my betters now." And before Ilsa could decide whether she'd been complimented or teased—probably both—Sable pushed off the rail. "Tide's turning. We're running cargo to the fish camps tomorrow and Dree wants to re-tar the deck seams tonight, and she will lecture us both if we're idle."

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it did in June—late, gold, unhurried. Ilsa stayed at the waterline after the others left, after the deputy administrator's car ground away up the gravel road, after Marta Okonkwo hugged her hard enough to creak ribs and whispered *thank you* in a voice that cracked in the middle.

The stone caught the last light and held it. The water moved against its base, gentle now, slack-water soft, as if the river itself had come to pay respects.

Ilsa crouched and touched the letters. Cold basalt, warm day, the enamel smooth under her fingertip. She traced the line she had fought hardest for, the one the Department's drafters had tried to cut in revision: *Their town remains below.*

It remained. She had seen it that morning, actually seen it, on the monitor in the survey skiff—the side-scan sweeping the streets like a lighthouse beam, the grid of foundations sharp and orderly under the silt-fog, the church with its steeple fallen but its walls standing, the schoolhouse roof collapsed inward around a hundred small desks that would never be sat in again. Protected now. Buffer zones and permits and a law with a number. A town that had been a secret, then a crime scene, then an embarrassment, and was now simply what it had always been: a place where people lived, until the water came, and they were not forgotten.

Grief, she thought, rising stiffly to her feet, was supposed to be formless. Nobody told you it could be given a shape—a slab, a census, a season of diving, a list of names argued into permanence. Nobody told you that the shape didn't make the grief smaller. It made it *bearable*, which was different, and better, and enough.

Downstream, the *Marrow* lay at her mooring with her lights coming on one by one. Dree's silhouette moved along the deck, tar brush in hand, swearing at a seam with real affection. Sable stood at the wheelhouse window, and even at this distance Ilsa could see her doing it—looking downriver, toward the Narrows, toward the water she had finally stopped running from.

Tomorrow they'd load cargo. Next week, Ilsa's team would begin the second survey season, mapping the outlying farms, the millrace, the cemetery that had been moved in '48—or mostly moved; she had questions about plot 40. All summer the work would go on, patient as sediment.

She turned once more to the stone before walking back to the boat. The light was nearly gone. The names glowed faintly anyway, dark enamel holding the dusk.

And that was when she saw it—low on the slab, at the waterline itself, where June flood could reach it: a fresh mark in the basalt's base, thin and deliberate, cut by no chisel anyone had ordered. Someone had scratched a small sign into the stone below the last carved line, down where the river touched. A boat. A tiny, crude, unmistakable outline of a riverboat, hull and wheelhouse and wheel, drawn by a hand that knew boats.

Ilsa stared at it for a long moment. Then she looked up at the wheelhouse window, where Sable Reyes stood in the lamplight, watching the water, measuring—in years now, in years—and Ilsa smiled, and said nothing, and let the river keep its secrets and give up its dead, both, in its own time.
