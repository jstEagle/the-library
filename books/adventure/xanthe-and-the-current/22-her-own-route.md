## 22. Her Own Route

The ice went out of the Kestrel on a Tuesday, with a sound like the whole river clearing its throat.

Sable stood on the Marrow's foredeck at first light and listened to it—the groan and shudder of the reach letting go, plates of ice grinding against each other below the Narrows, the water rising brown and muscular under her hull. Twelve springs she had heard that sound from various landings, always from the outside, always as a spectator to a river she refused to belong to. This spring she heard it from her own mooring.

Her own mooring. She still tested the words like a deck plate that might give way.

The berth at Miller's Landing was nothing much—a stretch of piling she'd leased in February, when the ice was still thick enough to walk on and the clerk at the harbormaster's office had looked at her twice, as if checking whether the woman signing the paperwork was the same one from the hearing broadcasts. Sable Reyes, the form said. Not Sable Vane, not the name she'd bought secondhand in a fuel dock office twelve years ago along with a clean license history and a story about coming up from the coast. Her real name, in ink, on a document filed where anyone could find it.

She had signed it without her hand shaking, which surprised her more than anything else that winter.

* * *

The Marrow came off the lines easy, the way an old dog rises when it hears the leash. Sable walked her engine room before dawn out of habit, though Dree had been through it twice the evening before and left everything gleaming. Her new—old—deckhand worked like a woman paying off a debt she'd never been asked to acknowledge: fuel filters changed, bilge dry, the spare propeller lashed down amidships where it had always ridden. Dree Callas did not talk about the church shallows or the closing gap or the sound Hale's barge made when the ice took it. She just showed up early and worked until there was nothing left to work on, and then she sat in the wheelhouse drinking coffee and looking at the river like it owed her something.

"Route's filed," she said now, handing Sable the carbon copy. "Landing to Kettle Narrows and back, cargo manifest attached. The harbormaster's girl asked if you wanted it posted public."

"It is public. That's the point."

Dree shrugged, but Sable caught the look that crossed her face—not disapproval exactly, more the expression of someone watching another person handle a live wire barehanded and deciding not to say so. Dree had known her under the old name. Dree was the only one who remembered what hiding had cost, because she'd watched the cost get paid by everyone around her.

"Manifest says general freight," Dree said. "You want to tell me what's actually in those crates marked 'survey equipment'?"

"Crates for Ilsa. Memorial survey gear. She'll meet us at the landing."

"The government's paying you to haul government crates to a drowned town." Dree turned her cup slowly. "Twelve years ago you wouldn't have taken a fare if they paid in gold."

"Twelve years ago I wasn't trying to be found."

Dree looked at her a long moment over the rim of the cup. Then she nodded once, the way she used to nod before a hard run, and went forward to cast off the bow line, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

The lower reach ran high and fast with snowmelt, carrying willow branches and fence rails and one entire chicken coop downstream like offerings. Sable took the Marrow up the channel she'd run a hundred times blind, but this morning she read it differently. The sandbars had all moved; the flood always rearranged the river's furniture, and a pilot who trusted last year's memory was a pilot who would spend this summer on a bar. So she read it fresh, slow, eyes moving from the water's texture to the willow line to the way the current folded around the point above Teller's Eddy—and she found herself narrating it aloud, half to Dree, half to herself.

"Bar's built out six feet past the old marker stake. See how the ripple line bends? That's the deep side running close under the bluff now. Come August that'll shift again."

"You're going to write that down," Dree said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to write it down." Sable tapped the chart table, where a fresh survey sheet lay clipped flat—Ilsa's preliminary bathymetry of the lower reach, drawn from the memorial project's first soundings, with Sable's own pencil annotations already crowding the margins. "New charts. Real ones. Everything I know goes on paper where the next pilot can use it."

"Everything?"

Sable understood the question underneath it. Everything—including the routes she'd kept secret for twelve years, the tricks, the private channels, the knowledge that had once been the only property she owned. Knowledge hoarded is armor. Knowledge shared is a map with your house marked on it.

"Everything," she said. "Turns out armor gets heavy."

They ran the middle reach where the old charts and Ansen's survey disagreed, and Sable took the disagreement seriously this time instead of trusting her gut. Gut had grounded her here once already, in the bad autumn, and she'd spent four cold hours kedging off the bar while the fleet closed behind her. Now the water lay different—higher, faster—but the survey's contour lines held true, and she threaded the Marrow down the deep lane with two feet to spare under the keel and felt no triumph in it, only a quiet satisfaction, the feeling of a tool working the way a tool should.

Above the Narrows, the river widened into the long green reach where the drowned town slept.

She slowed there. She always slowed there now; the preservation order required it, five knots through the protected zone, no anchoring on the town site, no wake over the rooftops. But the truth was she would have slowed anyway. The steeple broke the surface ahead, black timber furred with new algae, leaning a little further each season as the current undermined it. Around it the water lay glassy and deep, and on clear days—you could see them now, since the ice-melt dropped the turbidity—the rooflines showed faintly below, a geometry of gables and streets laid out under forty feet of green water like a thing pressed between the pages of a book.

Kettle Landing. Three hundred and eleven names on the stone above the landing now, cut in granite the county had hauled in by barge, because granite was the only honest material for the job. Sable had stood in front of that stone in November with her hat off, reading names, and found the ones she'd come for: the resisters who never left, the ones Odell's crews had reported as "relocated" in files that Ansen's journal had finally unsealed. She had read every name aloud. It had taken most of an afternoon. Dree had waited by the boat without hurrying her, and afterward neither of them had spoken for a mile of river, and that silence had been the right kind.

Now she rang the bell once—a single stroke, tradition, the pilots' greeting to the town—and the sound rolled out across the flat water and came back off the bluffs softened, like an answer.

At the head of the reach, the memorial camp had grown since fall: three canvas shelters, a generator hut, a floating dock where a small survey launch lay tied. And standing on the dock in a coat too thin for the morning, waving with both arms like a woman signaling a rescue, Ilsa Voss.

* * *

Ilsa had filled out over the winter. The fever had burned weight off her in that fish camp, and the months after—testimony, hearings, the endless interviews—had kept her hollow-eyed and sharp-edged. But the memorial survey had given her back something. She came up the Marrow's boarding ladder talking before her boots hit the deck.

"The north channel opened early. Two weeks early. Dr. Okafor thinks the calving changed the outflow pattern permanently—we're going to have to redraw the seasonal assumptions for the whole upper basin." She dumped a duffel on the deck and started lashing it down herself, out of old habit from her one trip as Sable's passenger. "Also I brought you something."

"Ilsa—"

"It's not a gift, it's a delivery, don't make it weird." She pulled a flat parcel from the duffel, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with survey twine. "From the archive. They finished restoring it."

Sable knew what it was before she untied the knot. She'd known since Ilsa mentioned the restoration request in January, and she'd said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

Inside the oilcloth lay a photograph, large format, mounted on board: the original crew portrait of the Kestrel Basin Survey, taken the spring before everything. Eleven people squinting into the sun on the deck of a government launch. And in the back row, slightly out of focus because she'd been turning toward the camera a half-second late, a young woman in a canvas jacket with her hand resting on the shoulder of the woman beside her—Dr. Maren Ansen, lead surveyor, the sister whose death Sable had spent twelve years outrunning and whose name she had finally, in a hearing room in the capital, said out loud for the record.

Maren's hand on her shoulder. Sable had forgotten that gesture. Standing in the wheelhouse with the photo in her hands, she felt it again anyway, the phantom weight of it, warm and certain.

"She'd have liked how it ended," Ilsa said quietly. "Not the dying. The ending. The testimony. The stone. She believed records could be forced to tell the truth if you put enough pressure on them." A crooked smile. "She used to say the river keeps everything, it's only the people who lose track."

"The river keeps everything," Sable agreed. She set the photograph carefully against the wheelhouse bulkhead, where the light from the forward windows fell on it, and did not trust her voice for a moment longer than that.

Ilsa watched her with the directness that had survived everything the winter had done to her. "You could come work the survey, you know. Okafor would take you tomorrow. Pilot consultant, your own terms."

"No."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it all winter." Sable checked the engine telegraph, needlessly; the Marrow idled sweet and steady beneath her feet. "But the survey's about the past. Somebody has to run the present. Cargo still moves, Ilsa. Mail still moves. People upstream still need fuel and flour and somebody willing to carry their letters out. That's not nothing. That's—" She stopped, surprised by the word that arrived. "That's the job. It was always the job. I just forgot, for a while, that the job was mine."

Ilsa studied her, then nodded, and hugged her quickly and hard, the way she did everything, and went aft to help Dree with the crates, and the Marrow turned her bow downriver toward home.

* * *

Summer came on the way summers do, gradually and then all at once. The Marrow ran the route twice a week, then three times when the upper landings petitioned for service. Sable hired a part-time deckhand out of Miller's Landing, a silent boy named Tam whose father had drowned on the Kestrel and who wanted, more than anything, to learn water. She taught him the way Maren had once taught her: not by telling, but by making him watch until he saw it himself. Watch the ripple line. Watch the birds working the seam. Watch where the deadheads ride. The river will tell you everything if you stop assuming you already know.

She filed her route every Monday. She answered her radio. When people wrote to her—and they did now, letters forwarded through the harbormaster's office, some from strangers who'd followed the hearings, some from other pilots asking about channels, one extraordinary envelope from a hydrology student asking permission to cite her Narrows run in a thesis—she answered them. It cost her something every time, that small act of being locatable. Some mornings she stood at the rail and felt the old reflex rise, the itch to cast off unannounced, to be a rumor again, a wake and no name. She let the feeling pass through her like weather. It came less often as the summer went on.

In August she took her vacation—not a disappearance, a vacation, announced in advance to three separate people—and spent it doing nothing but sitting on the Marrow's roof at anchor above Teller's Eddy with a ledger open on her knees.

It was Dree who'd suggested it, in her blunt way. "You count miles," Dree had said one night in June, watching Sable log the day's distance. "Always have. You know what your mileage was every season since you bought this boat, but I'd bet money you can't tell me how long you've had her."

"Eleven years in October," Sable had said, and then heard herself, and gone quiet.

So: the ledger. Not a logbook—a different animal entirely. In it, that week at anchor, she wrote down years instead of miles. Eleven years owning the Marrow. Twelve since Maren. One since the hearing. She wrote down the things the miles had let her avoid: that grief doesn't sink, it just rides low in the hull, and you can either pump it out or learn to trim for it. That the falsified report was overturned, but the twelve years were still hers—they belonged to her now, all of them, even the hidden ones, even the cowardly ones, and pretending otherwise would just be a smaller version of the same lie. That she was forty-six years old and had spent twenty-two of them on this river and intended to spend the rest the same way, in the open, where anyone could find her.

The accounting hurt. She'd expected that. What she hadn't expected was that it was survivable—that you could sit with the whole sum laid out on your knees, every debt and credit, and find that the total was not zero, was maybe not even negative, was possibly, if you counted honestly, something like ahead.

On the last night of her vacation, a floatplane went over heading upriver, and she raised her coffee mug to it out of pure foolishness, and the pilot dipped a wing, and she laughed alone on the roof of her boat like a woman with nothing to hide, because she was.

* * *

Frost came early that year, silvering the willows in the last week of September, and the Marrow made her final scheduled run of the season on a morning so clear the mountains stood up out of the horizon like they'd been waiting all summer for the haze to lift.

Cargo down: winter stores for the upper landings, drums of stove oil, mail sacks, and one crate of books for the school at Miller's Landing that Sable had ordered herself and entered on the manifest as "general freight," fooling nobody. Cargo up: furs, ore samples, letters, and Ilsa Voss riding as a declared passenger—declared, ticketed, logged in the book in Sable's own handwriting, because rules you make are rules you keep.

They ran the Narrows at midday, the ice already forming lace along the eddy lines, and passed the stone with its three hundred and eleven names, and Sable rang the bell twice this time—once for the town, once, newly, for the season's end, a farewell until the water opens again.

At Miller's Landing she tied up at her own mooring as the light went amber, and Dree went ashore to file the season-close report, and Tam carried the mail sack up the ramp whistling, and Sable stayed aboard a moment in the quiet, listening to the river move under the hull.

It would freeze soon. It always froze. And in the spring it would open again, groaning and shuddering, rearranging its furniture, keeping everything it kept. She would be here for it. That was the whole difference, she thought—the difference between the woman she'd been and the woman standing at this rail. Not braver, not wiser, not healed, whatever healed meant. Just here. Findable. Filed.

Downstream, far off where the reach bent toward the lowlands, she saw lights she didn't recognize—two of them, moving together against the current, too steady for driftwood, too slow for a patrol boat. She watched them for a long minute, the old instinct rising to name every vessel on her water.

Then she picked up the radio, keyed it, and spoke her name and her position to whoever might be listening, because that was who she was now, and waited to see who would answer.
