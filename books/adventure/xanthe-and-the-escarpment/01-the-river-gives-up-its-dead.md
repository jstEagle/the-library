## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been eating the valley for three days, and on the fourth morning it started giving things back.

Beatrix Brightwater stood at the edge of the floodplain with her boots sunk to the ankle in gray silt, watching the water perform its slow, indifferent accounting. A fence post. Half a door, still swinging on hinges that belonged to a house somewhere downstream. A sheep, bloated and wrong, turning in an eddy like it was deciding whether to come ashore. The Karst Escarpment loomed behind all of it, white-shouldered and indifferent, wearing its spring melt like a man wearing everything he owns at once.

"Guide." Doss's voice, from the mule line. "We're burning light."

Doss was the smuggler who paid her, though *smuggler* was generous. He moved salt, lamp oil, and occasionally people across the low passes of the Escarpment, and he moved them badly, and he paid Beatrix to make sure his badness didn't kill anyone. She'd been doing it eleven months. It was the only work left to someone whose name had been struck from two countries' registers of guides — struck so thoroughly that the ink might as well have been blood.

"I see the light," she said.

She did not move yet. That was the thing about her that clients either trusted or hated: she would stand still as long as standing still was the correct action, even when every animal instinct said go. The river was dropping. You could read it in the mud lines on the stones, each one a tide mark, each one lower than the last. Another hour and the ford would show its spine. Go now and you drowned; wait too long and you lost the day. Somewhere between those two failures was her entire profession.

The cargo this trip was twelve crates of lamp oil, four bales of wool, and a family of three from the border provinces who paid extra not to be asked questions. The family sat on a rock upriver, wrapped in oilcloth, watching the water with the particular patience of people who had already fled something worse than a flood.

Beatrix crouched and put two fingers in the current. Cold enough to hurt. Snowmelt, straight off the high faces. In three weeks the passes would start closing at the top even while the valleys drowned down here. Spring on the Escarpment was a season that tried to kill you from both directions at once.

Something knocked against her boot.

Not driftwood — she knew the knock of driftwood, hollow and light. This was dense, deliberate, a soft wooden thud against leather. She looked down.

A corner of dark oak stood out of the silt like a shoulder blade.

She should have stepped away. Later, she would think about how easy it would have been — one step back, let the river take it, tell no one, because telling no one was the oldest skill she had. But her hand was already in the water before the thought finished forming, fingers finding iron fittings crusted brown, wood gone soft at the edges but sound underneath. Heavy. Waterlogged, but heavy beyond what water alone explained.

"Doss," she called. "Rope."

He came down the bank grumbling, a small man built like a knot, and stopped when he saw what she was hauling. "That's not ours."

"No."

"We're not taking other people's—"

"It's not anybody's. It's the river's. And the river's giving it up." She got a loop under it. "Help me or watch."

They hauled it onto the mud together, and it came free of the shallows with a sucking sound, like something reluctant to be found. An iron-banded chest, long as a coffin lid, dented along one edge where it had clearly traveled hard — through rapids, maybe over a fall. Seaweed in the fittings, which meant it hadn't come from upstream at all. There was no upstream big enough to explain seaweed. It had come from the lake country, or beyond it, from places the maps went vague about.

And on the lid, set into the oak beneath a plate of tarnished brass, was a seal.

Beatrix wiped the mud away with her thumb and then stopped wiping, because her thumb had stopped working.

A crowned tower above crossed keys. Stamped deep, official, wax-blackened in the old way.

The arms of Vellmark.

Nobody stamped anything with Vellmark's arms. Vellmark had been a country once, a wedge of mountains and river valleys east of the Escarpment, until twenty-two years ago when three treaties and one short war dissolved it into its neighbors the way sugar dissolves into tea — officially welcomed, officially settled, officially never spoken of again. The atlas in the loft Beatrix rented had been printed after the annexation, and in it the space where Vellmark had been simply had other names, as if the land itself had agreed to be renamed.

People from there existed. Exiles, mostly, scattered thin, keeping their language alive in kitchens and funeral rites. But their seal? Their seal was contraband older than some of the soldiers who'd enforced the ban.

Doss had gone very still beside her. Stillness was how he showed fear; noise was how he showed everything else, and right now he wasn't making any.

"That's not possible," he said finally.

"It's wood and iron. Possible enough."

"You know what I mean. That crest—" He glanced upriver, downriver, at the family on the rock, at the mules. Paranoia, performed openly. "You know what happens to people who are caught carrying Vellish anything. Confiscation first. Then questions. Then—" He made a gesture that could have meant imprisonment or could have meant nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

"I know what happens."

"So we put it back."

The word landed in her chest with a strange weight, because she had been thinking exactly that, and hearing it aloud made it sound obscene. Put it back. Give it to the river. Let the current finish the job someone upstream had presumably failed to do — because a chest doesn't fall off a boat by accident, not one sealed like this, not one that travels that far. Someone had wanted it gone. Someone else had probably died making sure it stayed gone.

*Put it back* was the smart answer. Put it back was the answer of a woman whose name lived nowhere official, who crossed borders by walking at night, who had spent eleven months being so careful that carefulness had become the shape of her personality. Beatrix Brightwater, erased twice over — once by the guilds, once by the registries, once more unofficially by everyone who remembered the glacier and preferred not to speak of it. Invisible people survived by not picking up interesting objects.

Her hands were still on the chest anyway.

It was the curiosity she couldn't kill. Eleven months of erasure and it was the one thing that had refused to be scrubbed out of her, sitting in her chest like a coal that wouldn't die down. Every guide she'd ever admired had it. Her mentor, Halvard, used to say the mountains rewarded exactly two qualities: patience and an inability to leave a question alone. Patience kept you alive. The other thing got you up the mountain. He'd also said you needed both or you had neither, and he'd died proving some third thing entirely, but the saying stuck.

"Ten minutes," she said.

"Beatrix—"

"Ten minutes to look at what it is. Then we decide. If it's somebody's household goods, we sink it ourselves and never speak of it. If it's—" She didn't know how to finish that sentence, so she didn't.

"If it's what?"

"If it's trouble, I need to know what kind before it finds us. Trouble always finds us, Doss. I'd rather meet it informed."

He looked at her the way he looked at weather reports he didn't believe. Then he sighed, which in Doss's economy was agreement. "Ten minutes. And we open it away from the road. And if anyone comes down that track, we're sinking it and swimming after it if we have to."

They carried it up the bank together, grunting, the mules watching with the flat judgment of their kind. Behind them the river kept lowering, patient, having delivered what it delivered.

* * *

They opened it in the lee of a granite shelf half a mile from the ford, out of sight of the track, with the family and the mules left under Tomas—the eldest son of the paying family—holding the lead rope. Doss insisted on the distance. Beatrix insisted on the crowbar, because the lock was a good one, salt-welded shut, and her knife wasn't going to argue with it.

The iron gave with a crack that echoed off the shelf. Both of them flinched. Somewhere upslope a bird startled out of the scrub.

Inside, packed in wool that had soaked and dried and soaked again until it felt like felt, were papers.

Dozens of them. Bundles tied with cord gone brittle. Vellum, good vellum, the expensive kind that survives water better than paper — whoever packed this chest had known what they were doing, had planned for exactly this kind of abuse. Beatrix lifted a bundle out with both hands, the way you lift something injured.

The top document was a treaty draft. She could read enough of the formal script to get the shape of it: parties, territories, guarantees. The header named the signatories. One of them was Vellmark.

The other was the Consortium of Free Riverine Interests — the merchant combine that now effectively owned the eastern valleys, that set tolls and tariffs and, rumor said, kept garrisons of its own dressed as caravan guards. The consortium whose caravans she sometimes guided and whose agents she always avoided.

Below the header, in a different hand, in the margins, someone had written notes. Corrections. Small, precise annotations in Vellish shorthand, the kind archivists use.

One of them, translated loosely into the trade-tongue by a woman who'd worked enough contracts to recognize drafting language, said: *This clause is fabricated. The boundary described does not exist in any survey.*

Another said: *Date is impossible. The fort cited was not built until six years after this signature.*

Forged. The words arrived in her mind quietly, without drama, the way important things do. Forged treaties. Drafts of forged treaties, annotated by someone who had caught the forgery and preserved the evidence instead of destroying it.

Doss had backed up a step. "What is it. What does it say."

"It says the annexation was a lie," Beatrix heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "It says somebody wrote these treaties, and somebody signed them, and none of it was real."

"That's—" He laughed, one dry note. "That's not a thing. Countries don't get un-made by paperwork."

"They get made by paperwork. Why not the reverse?" She was turning pages now, faster, the coal in her chest roaring. Maps. A fragment of a map, cipher-marked along its edges, showing high passes — high passes, her territory, her country of illegitimate knowledge — with a symbol repeated at one point near the glacier line that she didn't recognize and couldn't stop looking at. Ledgers. Lists of names. Payments recorded in neat columns, sums that would buy a regiment, paid to officials whose titles she half recognized from gazetteers.

And then a page near the bottom, loose, different stock, older.

A list of names. Rescue personnel, it said at the top. Certified guides and mountain wardens, Vellmark border service.

Fourteen names. Four of them struck through with a single line each — dead, she thought, or dismissed. The rest marked with a notation she understood immediately, because the same notation appeared in her own file, in the copy she'd once stolen a look at before they burned her access: *discharged, record sealed, ineligible for re-registration.*

Scapegoats. Guides and wardens who had been on the border the year the treaties were finalized, who had seen the crossings, who had known the terrain where things were moved and hidden — and who had been quietly stripped of their livelihoods so they'd have no standing, no platform, no voice, when the paperwork became history.

Her own name was not on the list. But the list was the same species of thing that had happened to her. The same machinery. The same quiet pen.

"Beatrix." Doss's voice, careful. "Your ten minutes are long past. And I want you to hear me very clearly: whatever this is, we are not the people for it. We move oil and wool. We are small. Small is how we're alive."

She looked up at him. The morning had brightened while they worked, and the light came off the snowfields above and fell into the crate, onto the papers, and she thought: *someone carried this through all of that to give it to the river, and the river refused it.* The river had brought it to her feet. To her boot, specifically. Out of all the miles of shoreline.

Or maybe that was just what a curious mind told itself. Maybe rivers delivered things at random and meaning was a story people told afterward. She'd spent eleven months believing exactly that — that nothing meant anything, that survival was arithmetic, that the safest life was the one where nothing attached to you.

But her hands wouldn't put the lid down.

"Close it," Doss said. "Please. Bury it, sink it, burn it, I don't care. Just close it."

She closed it. She picked up the crowbar and bent the hasp back into place as best she could, and stood there with her palm flat on the wet oak, feeling the water drain out of the wood grain against her skin.

"The family," she said. "Where are they from?"

"Border provinces. Why?"

"Because the mother watched us pull it out of the water, and she hasn't taken her eyes off this shelf since. Because when I said the name Vellmark just now, your face went white, and hers will too, but hers will also do something else." She turned to look down the slope toward the ford, where the woman sat wrapped in oilcloth, small and still, watching them with an expression Beatrix recognized from funerals. "Grief, Doss. I've seen it on rope teams and I've seen it at gravesides. That woman knows what this seal is, and she's been waiting eleven days of travel to find out whether we're going to throw it back."

Doss said nothing for a long moment. The river spoke for both of them, low and constant, already forgetting what it had delivered.

"Ten minutes," he said finally, "is going to be the death of us."

"Probably," Beatrix said. She hoisted one end of the chest. "Take the other end. We're not leaving it here, and we're not sinking it. Not yet."

"And when the consortium's men ask why our mules are carrying a Vellish seal through the low passes?"

"Then we'll be very glad," she said, "that I know a route where nobody asks questions at all."

She started up the slope toward the ford, toward the family, toward the day's work, the chest swaying between them like something newly alive. Above the valley the Escarpment stood in its spring whites, and somewhere up in those high passes — she couldn't have said how she knew, only that the map fragment had burned the shape of it into her — something waited that had been waiting a long time.

The coal in her chest didn't die down. It spread.
