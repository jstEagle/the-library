## 2. What the Chest Would Not Say

The loft cost Beatrix four coppers a night and smelled of tallow and old fish, which was fair, because the man below her rendered both. It had one window facing the river, one facing the alley, and a floor that announced every footstep to the whole building — which she'd have hated in any other mood but tonight counted as a feature. Nobody could creep up on her here without the boards filing a report.

She set the chest on the table and sat across from it for a long time.

Outside, Lowbridge was doing what Lowbridge did after a flood: hauling silt, arguing with insurers, dragging drowned furniture into the street to dry in the sun like a row of sad flags. The river had given back everything it took this spring and then some. Somewhere down the embankment a woman was weeping over a mattress. Somewhere else, someone laughed at her.

Beatrix turned the chest around so the seal faced the wall.

It didn't help. She knew what was on the other side of that oak lid the way you know a scar's shape through your shirt. A crowned bridge over three waves, struck deep in green wax. Vellmark. A country that had been a country for six hundred years and then, eleven years ago, simply stopped being one — absorbed by its neighbors in a treaty everyone signed and nobody explained, the way you'd absorb a bruise.

Eleven years ago. Two years before the glacier. Before the registry men came to her door in Karsthold with their ledgers and their soft voices and took her name off the rolls of two nations in an afternoon.

*You were never here,* the registries said now, if they said anything at all. *There is no guide named Beatrix Brightwater. There never was.*

She'd spent nine years learning to be nobody, and she'd gotten good at it. Good enough that when the chest came up out of the shallows tangled in flood-brush, her first instinct — her honest, bone-deep instinct — had been to let it sink back down. Let the river keep its secrets. Rivers were better at keeping them than governments were.

But Halvard the smuggler had already seen the crest, and Halvard talked in his sleep, and so the chest had come home with her, wrapped in oilcloth, stinking of river, sitting on a table in a rented loft like a guest who intended to stay.

"You're nothing," she told it. "You're driftwood."

The chest did not agree.

* * *

The lock was good work — Vellish work, which meant old work, which meant it had opinions about being forced. Beatrix didn't force it. She'd learned locks from a smuggler's crew in the years after the blacklist, back when picking them was the only trade open to a person with no name, and she went at this one the way you go at a stubborn knot: patiently, respectfully, listening for where it wanted to give.

It gave on the third try, with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then the lid was up and the smell of the river rolled out and under it, faintly, something older. Paper. Wax. Cedar shavings gone gray with age.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had once been white: a bundle of documents tied with cord. A leather folio, swollen with damp but intact. And beneath those, flat as a slate, a sheet of heavy paper covered in fine lines and symbols that made no sense at all until she tilted it toward the window light and saw the second layer of marks between the first — tiny, precise characters inked in a paler shade, visible only at an angle.

A cipher-keyed map. Or part of one. The fragment showed contour lines climbing toward a high pass, a glacier drawn in the old style with hatched blue strokes, and along the margin a column of ciphered notations. The corner was torn away. Whatever lay beyond the glacier's edge was somewhere else entirely.

Beatrix set it down carefully, as if it might bite, and untied the cord on the document bundle.

The top page was a treaty draft. Not the treaty — a draft, worked over in at least three hands, with crossings-out and marginal arguments in a cramped clerk's script. The header read *Articles of Accession Between the Free State of Vellmark and the Karst Confederacy*, and the date at the bottom was eleven years and four months old.

She read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third time was when she saw it.

The clauses didn't match. Anyone who'd ever filed a border permit knew the published treaty — it had been reprinted everywhere, taught in schools, cited in every land dispute since. The published version said Vellmark's passes were ceded *in perpetuity* to joint administration. This draft said the same thing in the main text. But in the margins, in the clerk's hand, in the space where amendments got recorded before signature:

*Clause 9 amended per instruction — see annex. Perpetuity struck. Term: 30 years, renewable at sole discretion of the Confederacy.*

And beside that, in a different ink, angrier: *This is NOT what was agreed. Who authorized this?*

Someone had asked. Someone had asked, and the question had been answered not with an explanation but with a signature anyway, because the final page carried the seals — and Beatrix recognized enough about wax to know that these seals were wrong. Too crisp. Too uniform. Real seals get pressed by tired hands on uneven days; these looked stamped from a single die, all six of them, as if they'd been born together rather than agreed upon separately.

Forged. Or worse than forged — real signatures gathered under false pretenses, the way you get a man to sign a bill of lading by telling him it's a receipt.

Her hands had gone cold. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather.

She kept reading.

* * *

The bundle held seven drafts, each further along than the last, each with more of the truth shaved off it. By the fourth version the marginal objections had stopped. By the sixth, the term-of-years clause had vanished entirely, folded into language so smooth a reader would need the earlier drafts to know anything had ever been there. The seventh was nearly identical to the treaty the world had accepted.

But the folio — the folio was different.

It wasn't drafts. It was correspondence, or copies of it, and the names in it were names Beatrix knew from newspapers and customs-house walls. Consortium men. The Founding Partners of the Trans-Karst Development Consortium, whose crests hung in every tollhouse from here to the coast, whose money had built the new roads and the new registries and, presumably, the new version of history.

One letter discussed payment schedules. One discussed "the Vellish archivist problem" in terms that made Beatrix's skin crawl — *the records must be consolidated; consolidation requires relocation; relocation is best accomplished before winter.* One, dated four months after the annexation, was a single paragraph long and thanked an unnamed official for his discretion, promising that "the matter of the northern guides will be handled in accordance with our agreement."

*The matter of the northern guides.*

Beatrix put the letter down. Picked it up again. Read the sentence five times, because the first four times her eyes slid off it like rain off waxed canvas.

Northern guides. The phrase meant nothing to anyone else in the world. To her it meant a specific afternoon, a specific rope team, a specific glacier that had eaten three people while the world watched and asked questions it had already decided the answers to.

Two years after Vellmark vanished, a commercial party had gone onto the Serac Glacier above the old Vellmark border with a guide named Osric Vane leading and Beatrix Brightwater second-rope. Weather closed in from the south — fast, ugly, the kind of front that makes liars of forecasts. The party split in the whiteout against Beatrix's explicit advice. Three clients and Vane went high. Beatrix and two others went down to raise alarm.

Vane's rope team never came off the ice. When the weather broke, the search found anchors cut — cut, the inquiry said, though every guide who read the report knew that serac fall can shear a rope clean and leave edges that look like knife work to a man with a ledger instead of an axe. Somebody had to be responsible. The consortium's insurance arm held the liability. The consortium's friends held the inquiry. And the two surviving guides — the ones who'd argued against the split, who'd gone for help, who'd done everything the mountain teaches you to do — became the most convenient guilty parties in two countries' memory.

Osric Vane died the following winter. Officially of exposure, alone, drunk, on a slope he had no business on. Unofficially — well. There was no unofficially. That was the point of being erased. Nothing you did afterward could be proven to have happened at all.

Beatrix had run south, changed her name twice, taken work under other people's names, guided smugglers' cargo because smugglers didn't check registries. Nine years of invisibility, and she'd told herself it was survival. It was. But reading that single line — *the matter of the northern guides will be handled in accordance with our agreement* — she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the blacklist hadn't been an aftermath of the disaster.

It had been a line item. Scheduled. Paid for. Decided in a room full of consortium men four months before the glacier ever broke anyone's rope.

They hadn't punished her for the rescue going wrong. They'd needed the rescue to go wrong, and needed someone blameless-and-erasable to carry it, and she and Vane had been selected the way you select timber.

The dread that had lived in her chest since the river didn't break. It hardened. She could feel it happening — fear cooling into something with edges, something you could hold. Something you could use.

* * *

She worked until the light went. Made a careful inventory: seven treaty drafts, fourteen letters, a ledger page listing disbursements to officials in three governments with initials instead of names, and the map fragment. She copied nothing — copying took time and left evidence — but she read everything twice, building the architecture of it in her head the way she'd build a route: what connected to what, where the load-bearing points were, where it would all collapse if you pulled the wrong piece.

The map fragment bothered her most. Cipher notation, old-style glacier hatching, a pass drawn where no pass appeared on any chart she'd ever been licensed for — and she'd been licensed for charts in two countries, once. The notation system wasn't any cipher she knew, but the *structure* of it was familiar. Marginal keys keyed to landmarks. It was how the old Vellish survey teams had marked restricted routes, according to a lecture she'd half-heard at a guild dinner a lifetime ago, back when she still got invited to dinners.

Restricted routes. Hidden archive. *Consolidation requires relocation.*

Somebody in Vellmark had known the treaties were false, had collected the proof, had hidden it — and had hidden it well enough that the consortium's letters spoke of relocating records rather than destroying them, which meant even they hadn't found it all. Eleven years, and somewhere in the high passes above the dead country, the original documents were still sitting in the dark, waiting for someone with the key.

Or waiting for the river to deliver a chest to exactly the wrong person.

She was repacking the folio when the stairs creaked.

Not the ordinary creaks. She knew those — the fish-renderer's tread, heavy and slow; the widow on the second floor, quick and light. These were deliberate. Paused. The particular rhythm of someone standing on a landing, deciding.

Beatrix killed the lamp with her palm and stood still, and the loft's floorboards — her sentries — reported the rest: a step closer to her door. A stop. The soft sound of someone testing the handle, finding it locked, and withdrawing without hurry.

Footsteps descended. The street door opened and shut.

She waited a hundred heartbeats, then crossed to the window overlooking the alley. Below, in the last gray light, a man in a drab coat was lighting a pipe he never smoked, standing just long enough to look up at the row of windows, and then walking away at the pace of a man with nowhere particular to be.

Consortium watchers looked exactly like that. She'd seen them outside registry offices, years ago, before she understood what she was seeing. Patient men in drab coats, smoking pipes they didn't want.

He hadn't broken in. He hadn't called the constables. He'd *looked*, and left, which meant he hadn't been sure yet — and it also meant that by tomorrow, someone would be sure, because men like him wrote reports and reports traveled faster than rivers.

Beatrix turned back to the chest. Four hours ago she'd been trying to decide whether to push it back into the current. Now the question wasn't whether the papers were dangerous. The question was what to do with dangerous things when hiding them had just stopped working.

She thought about the marginal hand on the first draft — *This is NOT what was agreed. Who authorized this?* Somebody had asked. Somebody had written the question down and kept the draft anyway, kept all of them, packed them in cedar and sealed them in wax and committed them to a river rather than let them burn. That person was probably dead. The chest had come downstream like a coffin.

But coffins float toward people, sometimes. Toward the one person in Lowbridge who could read a Vellish survey notation and a forged seal and a consortium threat in the same hour.

Which raised a question she couldn't answer alone, and the answer to which might be the most dangerous thing in the entire chest: *who else in this valley could read that map fragment?*

Because whoever forged the treaties had needed experts. And whoever hid the originals had needed experts too. And experts in dead countries' paperwork did not grow on trees — they survived, the way she survived, under other names, in rented rooms, keeping their heads down.

Somewhere out there was another erased person who knew what these symbols meant.

Beatrix wrapped the documents back in their oilcloth, rebundled the cord, and did not relock the chest. There didn't seem to be much point anymore. Instead she took the map fragment and the first draft — the one with the angry marginal hand — and folded them into the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, where the important things rode.

Then she sat by the dark window with her boots on and her pack half-made, watching the alley, and let the dread finish hardening into resolve.

In the morning, she would start asking quiet questions. Ferry-men remembered faces. Archivists remembered handwriting. And there was one ferry-man in particular, down at the low crossing, who had looked at her a moment too long when she'd paid her passage last month — a small, neat old woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that catalogued everything, who had said *safe travels, guide* in Vellish when Beatrix's papers said she was Karsti-born and nameless besides.

*Ilsa,* the ferry ledger had said. Just Ilsa. No family name, no origin mark, in a country that demanded both.

Beatrix watched the alley until full dark, and the name turned over in her mind like a stone in a current, catching light.
