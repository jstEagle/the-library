## 3. Two Countries' Worth of Enemies

The loft smelled of wet wool and lamp oil, and Beatrix had not slept in it for three nights running.

They told themselves it was the papers. A person could hardly be blamed for losing sleep over a chest full of forged treaties and a map written in cipher. Any sensible person would lie awake cataloguing what they'd found, weighing what to burn and what to keep. That was diligence, not fear.

But Beatrix knew the difference between diligence and dread, because they had lived with dread so long it had worn a groove in them, the way a boot wears a path across scree. And this was dread.

* * *

Six years ago, on the Vantrel Glacier, the weather had turned in eleven minutes.

Beatrix remembered the number because they'd counted. Eleven minutes between the first smell of snow on the wind — that cold-mineral scent like struck flint — and the whiteout closing over the icefall like a lid. Eleven minutes to get four clients and two guides off the exposed traverse and into the lee of the serac wall.

They got five of the six down-rope before the visibility went. The sixth was a banker named Ostrava Helt, heavy man, bad boots, worse attitude, who had spent three days complaining about the pace and then frozen at the fixed line when the wind hit. Beatrix went back for him. That was the job. You went back.

What happened after that had been told so many times, in so many courtrooms and registry offices and taverns where Beatrix wasn't welcome, that the true version had been buried under all the others like a body under an avalanche. In the official version — the one in the Karst registries and, later, when the story crossed the border, the one in the Vellish annexation files — Guide Brightwater abandoned a client on the glacier to save their own skin. In the official version, Ostrava Helt died alone in the storm while his guide descended without him.

In the true version, Beatrix reached him, clipped him to their own harness, and the two of them moved together through a whiteout so total that Beatrix navigated by counting steps and probing with the axe, by the slope's lean against their boots, by memory. They were four hundred meters from shelter when the cornice let go.

Beatrix remembered the sound. Not loud. A sigh, almost. The mountain exhaling.

Then the world tilted, and there was rope, and there was Helt's weight tearing at their harness, and there was the choice every guide dreads and trains for and prays never to face: cut the rope or go over.

Beatrix did not cut the rope. They went over.

They survived because the debris field caught them in a pocket against the serac wall, and because Helt's body landed above theirs and took the worst of it, and because Beatrix dug out with frostbitten hands through six hours of night. Helt was dead when they reached him. Had probably been dead before they stopped falling. Beatrix carried the knowledge of that down the mountain along with the man's effects, and stood in front of the inquiry board with blackened fingers, and told the truth.

The truth did not fit the shape of the hole the consortium needed filled. There had been money involved — there was always money involved — a consortium insurance structure, a liability question, a client list that included men whose names appeared, Beatrix would learn years later, in ledgers very much like the ones now sitting in the chest in the corner of this rented loft. Someone had to be at fault. The dead man couldn't be. The consortium wouldn't be.

So it was the guide. It was always going to be the guide.

* * *

Presently, in the loft above the chandler's shop in Lowmark's ferry quarter, Beatrix sat cross-legged on the floor with the treaty drafts spread around them like a hand of cards they didn't know how to play.

Morning light came through the east window in a dirty yellow bar. Below, the river ran high and brown with snowmelt, carrying its spring cargo of branches and fence posts and once, memorably, a drowned cow. The town moved around its business. Carts on the ferry road. Gulls fighting over fish guts at the quay.

Beatrix read the drafts again. They had read them eleven times now, and each time the same detail rose to the surface like a drowned thing: the marginalia. Whoever had drafted these forgeries — and they were forgeries, no doubt of it anymore; the ink analysis Ilsa hadn't done yet but Beatrix's own eyes had, the anachronistic paper stock, the seal applied wet over dry text — whoever had made them had worked carelessly in the margins. Dates corrected. Names swapped. And in the third draft, the annexation instrument itself, a note in a clerk's cramped hand:

*H. confirms payment schedule satisfactory. B. matter closed to mutual satisfaction.*

*B.* 

Brightwater.

Beatrix had stared at those two letters for a long time the first night, feeling something move in their chest that they had thought was dead. Not grief exactly. Grief was the old country. This was something colder and more useful: confirmation. The blacklist hadn't been an accident of bureaucratic spite. It had been a transaction. Their name had been sold, the same as Vellmark's borders had been sold, the same as everything else in these documents had been sold, and the same pen had signed both purchases.

*Matter closed to mutual satisfaction.*

Well. It wasn't closed anymore.

* * *

Another memory, unbidden, arriving the way memories do when you're tired: the registry office in Karsthold, three weeks after the inquiry. Beatrix standing at the counter with their hat in their hands — they'd worn a hat then, tried to look like someone who belonged indoors — while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs drew a line through their name.

"You understand," the clerk said, not unkindly, "that this is administrative. Your certification is suspended pending review. You may reapply."

"When?"

The clerk had looked at the ledger, and then at Beatrix, and then at the ledger again, and said nothing, and the nothing had lasted long enough to be an answer. Six years of nothing. Two countries' worth of nothing. Because the story had traveled — the consortium made sure of that, planting it in the Vellish trade gazettes before Beatrix even crossed the border — and by the time Beatrix understood that the Karst review would never come, the Vellmark registries had already struck them too. Erased in the country of their birth, erased in the country next door. A guide with no registration could not lead clients, could not buy route permits, could not legally set foot on half the high passes. Could work smugglers' trails for cash, if they kept their head down and their mouth shut.

Which is what Beatrix had done. For six years. Head down, mouth shut, invisible.

Running, they had told themselves, was freedom. It had taken a waterlogged chest floating out of a flood to teach them otherwise.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Beatrix went down to the quay to buy coffee and look at the river, which is what they told themselves, though part of them knew better. Part of them had been watching the street since dawn.

Lowmark's ferry quarter woke slowly. The chandler's boys hauled barrels. A woman argued with a fishmonger about the price of eels with the fluency of long practice. Two drovers drank coffee standing up outside the tollhouse, their ponies stamping at the hitching rail.

And across the square, under the awning of the map-seller's shop, stood a man who was not buying maps.

He was dressed well enough to pass — traveling tweed, good boots, the kind of coat that cost more than the chandler's whole inventory — but he had the stillness wrong. Townspeople in Lowmark moved with the loose economy of people who had nowhere to be and all day to be nowhere. This man stood like a post. He'd been standing like a post, Beatrix realized, since before the chandler's boys started work, and he held his hands in a way that riders hold them, thumbs forward, weight even, the habit of men who spend their lives near horses and want to be able to mount fast.

Consortium. The word arrived in Beatrix's mind without drama, the way you notice a change in the wind. Not proof. Just the shape of it.

Beatrix bought their coffee from the quay cart, paid with small coin, and did not hurry. Hurrying was a tell. They drank standing up, watching the river, letting the cup warm their hands, and used the reflection in the cart's brass fittings — a trick old Sera Voss, their mentor, had taught them, *the metal sees more than your eyes do, girl, boy, whatever you are today* — to keep the map-seller's awning in view.

The man had a companion. Of course he did. Watchers worked in pairs; one watched, one walked. The second was harder to spot because the second was supposed to be hard to spot: a woman in a gray shawl who had passed the chandler's door twice in ten minutes, each time pausing to adjust her boot, and who looked, when her face turned toward the light, like she carried herself the way soldiers carry themselves, or couriers, or hunters.

Two of them. In Lowmark. Four days after a flood delivered a sealed chest bearing the crest of a country that officially did not exist.

Coincidence was possible. Beatrix had built six years on the assumption that coincidence was possible, that nobody looked for ghosts, that invisibility was armor. But the chest had gone into the loft past neighbors, past the chandler's boys, past the ferryman who'd helped drag it ashore and gotten a look at the seal before Beatrix could throw a tarpaulin over it. Word moved down rivers faster than water did.

And there was another possibility, one Beatrix liked even less: that the watchers weren't here for the chest at all. That someone had finally decided a ghost was worth exhuming. That the *B. matter*, closed to mutual satisfaction six years ago, had reopened somewhere in a marble office, and a clerk with clean fingernails had said, *find out if the guide is still alive, and if so, how alive.*

Beatrix set the empty cup on the cart rail and walked back toward the chandler's at a market-day amble, counting doors, noting sightlines, doing the arithmetic they'd sworn off years ago. Back door to the yard. Yard wall to the cooper's lane. Cooper's lane to the towpath, towpath north to the ferry landing. If it went badly, they could be on the water in ninety seconds with nothing but the clothes they wore, and the thought of leaving the chest — the treaties, the map fragment, the two letters that spelled out their own name in a dead clerk's hand — sat in their stomach like swallowed stone.

Because here was the admission, the one that had been working its way up through six years of sediment and finally broke the surface this morning, standing in a square full of ordinary people buying ordinary things:

Running had never been the same as being free.

They had traded the mountains for the margins. They had traded their name for their neck. And the whole time, the people who had taken both had simply gone on signing things, comfortable in the knowledge that the one witness who mattered had been successfully converted into nobody at all. The chest hadn't ruined that arrangement. The chest had just shown Beatrix the price tag.

They climbed the stairs to the loft, and stopped halfway up, because the neighbor was there.

Her name was Petra Ilves, a widow who mended nets on the landing below and had the eyes of a woman who missed nothing that happened on her stretch of river. She was standing at the top of the stairs with a basket of washing on her hip, and she was looking at Beatrix with an expression that was not hostile and not friendly and was, in fact, the most dangerous expression available: recognition.

"Flood brought you more than driftwood," Petra said. Not a question.

Beatrix said nothing. Silence was a tool; they'd learned its edges long ago.

"Man's been asking after you." Petra shifted the basket. "Down at the quay. Well-dressed fellow. Wanted to know if anyone new had rooms above the chandler's. Said he was a cousin." Her mouth did something dry at the corner. "I told him the chandler keeps no lodgers. Which is true, near enough, seeing as you pay in cash and I see you come and go at hours cousins don't keep."

The river roared faintly below the floorboards. Somewhere in the loft behind Beatrix, the chest sat with its forged treaties and its cipher map and its two letters, patient as a grave.

"Why?" Beatrix asked finally. "Why tell me?"

Petra looked out the stairwell window at the water, at the brown flood carrying whatever the mountains had decided to give up this year.

"My husband fished these banks forty years," she said. "River takes, river gives. Only rule I ever learned is you don't hand back what the flood delivers to your door. Somebody upstream threw that chest away for a reason, and somebody downstream wants it bad enough to send cousins. Means it matters." She started down the stairs past Beatrix, then paused, close enough that Beatrix could smell the lye soap on her sleeves. "There's a woman asking after you too. Down at the ferry landing. Old. Sharp. Been there since dawn, and she don't strike me as a cousin either."

She went down and out into the morning, and Beatrix stood alone on the stairs with their heart going hard, understanding that the quiet six years were finished, that the flood had ended more than one drought, and that whichever direction they moved next — up the stairs to the chest, or down to the landing, or out the back to the cooper's lane and away — they would be moving for the first time in six years as someone with something to lose.

They went down to the ferry landing.
