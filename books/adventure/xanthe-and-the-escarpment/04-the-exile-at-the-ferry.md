## 4. The Exile at the Ferry

The ferryman's name was Osk and he had never once asked Ilsa Venn her business, which was why she had ridden his rope-ferry across the Greywater twice a week for eleven years under the name Marta Kell. A woman who bought lamp oil, mended nets, and kept to herself was a woman no one remembered. That was the whole architecture of a life like hers: build it out of things too dull to recall.

She was carrying a basket of dyed wool she did not need when she saw the guide on the far landing.

Ilsa knew faces the way other people knew scripture. She had spent thirty years in the Vellmark State Archive memorizing the features of men who signed things they should not have — ministers, consortium brokers, the polite clerks of annexation — and afterward, in exile, she had turned the same discipline outward, cataloguing everyone who might be looking for her. So it took her only three heartbeats to place this one, though the face belonged to no archive.

Beatrix Brightwater. The guide who wasn't.

Ilsa had read the file years ago, before she burned her own copies of everything that could burn. Two registries struck. One glacier, one storm, three climbers dead and a fourth carried down alive by hands the commissions called reckless. The language in both reports had that particular bureaucratic music Ilsa recognized from treaty forgery — the same careful arrangement of facts around a conclusion already chosen. She had never met the guide. She had simply noted, in the private ledger of her mind where she kept such things, that here was another person the paper world had decided did not exist.

And now the person who did not exist was standing at the ferry landing with a chest under one arm, and the chest was dripping river water onto the boards, and the crest stamped into its lead seal was a crowned bridge over three stars.

Vellmark.

Ilsa's basket handle bit into her palm. She kept walking. Eleven years of practice said keep walking, buy your oil, be Marta Kell until the shaking stopped. But her eyes had already done what her training could not undo: they had read the seal, and beneath the seal they had read the guide's posture — the way Beatrix stood with one boot on the chest's lid, casual as a man resting a foot on a crate, while every line of their body screamed *this is mine and I will not let go of it*.

Someone else was watching too.

He was across the landing, leaning against the customs shed with the studied idleness of a man paid to be idle. Grey coat, good boots, town-cut hair gone two weeks unbarbered — a traveler, or pretending to be one. He had been there when Ilsa crossed an hour ago. He had been reading a broadsheet then. The broadsheet was still open to the same page.

Ilsa turned toward the wool merchant's stall, adjusted nothing in her basket, and watched him in the reflection off a copper kettle. When Beatrix moved toward the ferry rail, the grey-coated man moved too — not following, just drifting, the way smoke drifts, always keeping the guide in the corner of his vision.

So. The chest had already drawn eyes. Which meant whoever owned its contents had already guessed where those contents had washed ashore.

Ilsa bought her oil. She paid with exact change, because Marta Kell always did, and she walked back to the landing with her heart going like a mill wheel and her face arranged into the mild vacancy of an old woman thinking about supper.

The ferry was loading. Four farmers, a peddler with a handcart, two goats, and Beatrix Brightwater standing at the bow with the wet chest between their boots.

Ilsa stepped aboard.

* * *

The Greywater ran high with snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, and the ferry crossed on a cable strung bank to bank, the current shouldering the hull sideways so that the crossing was less a passage than a negotiation. Ilsa stood near the stern, close enough to speak without raising her voice, far enough to seem accidental.

"You're taking the low road east," she said.

Beatrix didn't look at her. "I'm taking whatever road I take."

"The low road east passes the tollhouse at Karst Bridge." Ilsa watched the water. "There's a man at the tollhouse today in a grey coat who was at the landing ten minutes ago, and he'll be at the tollhouse after you, because he was watching you, not the road."

That got her a look. Beatrix's eyes were the pale grey of glacial ice, and they went through Ilsa the way a probe goes through snow — testing for the hollow underneath. "Who are you?"

"Marta Kell," Ilsa said. "I mend nets."

"Then mend them." Beatrix looked away again.

Ilsa had planned, in the long walk from the wool stall, to be patient. To wait for the far bank, some quiet stretch of lane, an approach built over days if necessary. Patience was the first rule of hiding and she had followed it for eleven years without once breaking it.

But the chest sat there between the guide's boots, water still weeping from its seams, and the crowned bridge stared up at her through the drying mud, and Ilsa Venn found that patience had a limit and she had reached it somewhere around the moment she'd seen three stars and a bridge rendered in lead.

"That is a Vellish diplomatic courier chest," she said quietly. "Pattern 4, issued between '61 and '69 to envoys carrying material too sensitive for the diplomatic pouch. The lead seal means the contents were registered under the Second Archive Act — which means someone inside the foreign ministry wanted them recoverable but deniable." Her voice stayed level, conversational, a woman discussing net-mending. "I would know. I helped write the registry notation."

The ferry swayed. A goat complained. Beatrix had gone very still, the stillness of a person recalculating everything.

"You're Vellish," Beatrix said at last. Not a question.

"I'm nobody," Ilsa said. "That's rather the point of me. As I understand it, that's rather the point of you as well."

Something moved behind the ice-grey eyes — anger, or its exhausted cousin. "You've been reading about me."

"I read everything. It's a disease of my former profession." Ilsa shifted her basket to her other arm. "Two registries. One commission of inquiry that interviewed forty-one people and managed to quote none of the four survivors of your rope team. A finding of gross negligence written in language so carefully symmetrical it could only have been drafted before the inquiry began." She paused. "I know what manufactured conclusions look like, guide. I spent thirty years filing the genuine article next to the forged ones, and learning to tell them apart, and then learning, much too late, that telling them apart was not the same as stopping them."

The far bank came up slow and green. The ferryman threw his hook; the hull bumped the pilings; the farmers began shoving their goats down the ramp. Ordinary life, resuming around them like water closing over a stone.

Beatrix hadn't moved. "Say what you want to say," they said. "At the tollhouse, in the open, with watchers about? No."

"Then say it here, in the middle of a river, where no one can approach us and every word drowns behind us." Ilsa nodded at the chest. "Where did it come from?"

"Flood debris. Upstream of the old quarry bend."

"Which means it came out of the Karst gorge, which means it came from higher up, which means—" Ilsa stopped herself. Thirty years of archival instinct were clawing at her throat. "Open question. What's inside?"

"Paper."

"What paper?"

"The kind that gets people erased." Beatrix's mouth twisted. "You'd know something about that too, apparently."

The ferryman called the crossing fee. Beatrix paid for both of them before Ilsa could move — a small thing, done absently, the reflex of someone used to covering costs for clients — and then they stepped onto the bank and stood there in the mud and the noise of goats, and Ilsa understood that the guide was deciding, right now, whether to walk away from her forever.

She made it easy to decide the other way, because she had nothing left except the truth and no time left to spend it slowly.

"My name is Ilsa Venn," she said. "Until nine years ago I was Keeper of the Treaty Registry in the Vellmark State Archive. I authenticated documents for the ministry for three decades. Including, in the final years, the instruments by which Vellmark consented to annexation." She heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice she used for cataloguing. "I fled the week before the archives were sealed. I have lived here as Marta Kell since. I dye wool. I mend nets. And for nine years I have told myself that preservation was enough — that keeping the notation systems alive in my own head, keeping the true history alive in one skull, was a form of resistance." She looked at the chest. "It isn't. It's hiding with better furniture."

Beatrix was staring at her. Not with suspicion now — suspicion had been replaced by something more dangerous, recognition.

"You've been running as long as I have," Beatrix said.

"Longer. You lost a name. I lost a country." Ilsa lifted her chin toward the road, where the grey-coated man had appeared, walking unhurried along the fence line, matching their pace from fifty yards. "And unless I am very much mistaken, we are both about to lose our afternoon. Walk with me. There's a lane behind the ferryman's cottage that doesn't meet the main road for half a mile. He thinks I use it to visit a lover. Let him."

* * *

The lane was rutted and overhung with alder, and the sound of the river filled it wall to wall, drowning conversation for anyone not walking shoulder to shoulder. Ilsa talked fast and low.

"The consortium has field agents in every market town on the escarpment's south flank. They don't wear uniforms. They wear grey coats and read broadsheets. They pay innkeepers, ferrymen, and at least one customs clerk whose name I will give you when I trust you, which is not yet." She glanced back. The grey coat had not followed into the lane. Yet. "Whatever is in that chest, they believe it exists, and they believe you have it. How do I know? Because they've been in this valley for a month asking after flood salvage. Quietly. Through middlemen. Offering good money for 'recovered goods of foreign manufacture.' Someone upstream knows what went into the water."

"Then they don't know I have it specifically."

"They know someone does, and you are the only person in forty miles who pulled anything out of that river and didn't sell it." Ilsa shook her head. "Guide. Listen to me. I have spent nine years being invisible and I can tell you exactly what it costs, because I have paid the full price. Invisibility keeps you alive. It does not keep you *safe*. There is a difference, and the difference is that safety ends someday and invisibility just continues. You will grow old in rented rooms. You will die under a false name. And the truth in that chest will rot with you, and the men who forged the treaties will die honored, and nothing — nothing — will ever have been different."

Beatrix walked in silence for twenty paces. The alders rattled. Somewhere behind them, faintly, the ferry cable groaned.

"You don't know what's in it," Beatrix said finally.

"No."

"Then hear it, and understand what you're asking to carry." They stopped, set the chest down on a flat stone, and worked the lock with a thin knife — the lock was ruined anyway, swollen with river water, and gave up with a grunt of corroded iron. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work heroically: bundles of paper, ink blurred at the edges but legible, and a folded sheet stiff with wax sizing.

Ilsa knelt. Her hands, which had not shaken in nine years, shook now.

Treaty drafts. Not the treaties themselves — drafts, working papers, the scaffolding of the crime. She read the header of the topmost page and felt the world tilt gently, the way it tilts when a floor turns out to be a trapdoor.

"This is the Annexation Instrument of '68," she whispered. "The final text was published. I filed it. This draft is *different*. The clause on border garrisons — in the published version Vellmark retains garrison rights for ten years. Here the clause reads differently, and look—" her finger trembled above the margin, "—initials. Initials of the drafting committee, and dates, and this annotation in the corner is a registry cipher, and I know this cipher, I *invented* the second permutation of this cipher—"

She stopped. Breathed. Made herself be the archivist, because the archivist was the strongest thing left in her.

"There's a map fragment," Beatrix said. "Cipher-keyed. I can't read it."

"Give it to me."

Beatrix hesitated — one full second, Ilsa saw the whole war of it cross the guide's face, the reflex to stay invisible warring with something older and hungrier — and then handed it over.

Ilsa unfolded it with the care of a woman handling a relic. It was a fragment, torn raggedly, showing contour lines and pass elevations in the old Survey style, annotated in cipher notation she had last seen on documents sealed under the Second Archive Act. Her lips moved as she worked. The letters resolved.

"It's a route," she breathed. "A route into the high passes. And these annotations—" she laughed, a small broken sound, "—these are archive access protocols. This is a map to a *repository*. A hidden archive. They built one. During the annexation negotiations, someone in the ministry hid the original instruments — the real treaties, the unaltered record — somewhere in the Escarpment, and keyed the location in cipher, and put the key in a courier chest, and the chest was supposed to reach—" she frowned, turning the fragment, "—someone. An ally. And it never arrived. It went into the river instead, and the river held it for however many years, and then the river gave it to *you*."

The lane was quiet. The river roared on, indifferent, having delivered its cargo at last.

"Nine years," Ilsa said softly. "Nine years I thought the originals were burned. I mourned them like graves. And they've been sitting in the ice above us the whole time, waiting for a flood and a disgraced guide." She looked up at Beatrix, and the old woman's mildness fell away entirely, and what was underneath was the Keeper of the Treaty Registry, thirty years deep in the paper war, still standing. "I want the chest."

"No."

"I want—"

"You want to carry it, and study it, and you'd do it bravely and you'd probably do it well," Beatrix said. "But I've read enough of these pages to know they name names. People who were scapegoated. People who were paid. And I'm one of the names, Venn. Whatever's in here touches the rescue, touches the commissions, touches the two registries that struck me. Which means the consortium doesn't just want the paper. They want the paper *and* anyone who's read it." They picked the chest back up. "You've been invisible nine years. Why would you trade that?"

"Because invisibility is just a slower way of dying," Ilsa said. "And because I owe a debt to the truth that nine years of wool-dyeing hasn't touched." She drew a breath. "But you're right about one thing. Neither of us can do this alone. You know the mountains. I know the cipher, the notation, the people, the history. And there are others — others like us, scattered along this valley like seeds the wind dropped. A boy who can forge any seal in the old empire. A surveyor who mapped these passes before his charts were confiscated. A man named Davor who—"

She stopped. Something cold moved through her, an archivist's instinct, the sense of a document that doesn't quite fit its folder.

"Davor who what?" Beatrix asked.

"Who has been useful to me," Ilsa said, and heard how the sentence sounded even as she said it, and filed the doubt away where she filed all doubts — carefully, to be examined later, when there was time. "There are others. And there is a route over the Escarpment before the snows close, and only you know it. So here is my offer, guide, and I will make it only once. Help me reach that archive. Help me bring the originals down into the light. In exchange, I will help you clear your name — the inquiries, the registries, all of it. The same paper that buried you can dig you out."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you go back to being no one, and I go back to dyeing wool, and we both get what we deserve."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. On the main road beyond the alders, the grey coat passed the gap in the hedge without turning his head — but he was walking toward the tollhouse, and the tollhouse stood on the low road east, and the low road east was the only road.

"Two countries' worth of enemies," Beatrix said quietly. "You understand that's what you're signing for."

"I've had one country's worth for nine years," Ilsa said. "I find I miss the variety."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the guide's face — there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Back room," Beatrix said. "Tonight. The tavern with the green door, the one with the crooked sign. Bring your others. We'll talk terms."

They hoisted the chest and walked on ahead, and Ilsa Venn stood alone in the alder lane with her basket of unnecessary wool, listening to the river, feeling nine years of Marta Kell sloughing off her like wet cloth.

She had demanded the chest and been refused. She had offered everything she had left and been given only a meeting. And yet she walked back toward the village lighter than she had walked anywhere in a decade — because for the first time since the archives were sealed, someone else in the world was carrying the same weight, and had not run from her, and had not handed her over, and had said *tonight* instead of *never*.

Two people built for hiding, recognizing each other across a ferry deck. It was, Ilsa thought, the closest thing to a homecoming she would ever get.

She only wished she could shake the image of Davor's face when she'd mentioned the chest — the half-second pause, the too-quick nod — and the cold little voice in her head, the archivist's voice, that never forgot a discrepancy:

*This document does not match its folder.*
