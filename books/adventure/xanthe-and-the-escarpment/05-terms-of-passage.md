## 5. Terms of Passage

Ilsa Venn's back room smelled of lamp oil, dried lavender, and the particular staleness of paper that has been hidden a long time. Beatrix counted the exits before she counted the people: a door to the ferry yard, a window painted shut, a floor hatch half-hidden under a braided rug that almost certainly went down to a root cellar. Old habits. The room had one lamp, turned low, and four people in it besides herself, and every one of them was watching her the way people watch weather.

Ilsa sat at the head of a scarred table with her hands folded, an old woman's posture, deliberate as a signature. To her left, a broad-shouldered man in his fifties with river-gray eyes and the stillness of someone who had once carried things for a living — heavy things, at night. To her right, a woman perhaps thirty, wiry, with burn-scarred fingers and the coiled patience of a falconer. And at the far end, half in shadow, a young man who kept touching the edge of the table as if to confirm it was real.

"You said three," Beatrix said.

"I said three," Ilsa said. "You've met none of them, and you're counting the doors. Good. Davor Ostrevic." She nodded to the broad man. "He moved contraband across the Karst for eleven years before the border closed. He knows the low valleys the way you know the high ones. Petra Sollen." The falconer woman inclined her head a fraction. "She was a conservator at the Vellmark state archive. What she can do with damaged paper, no one in two countries can match. And Tomas Rell." The young man lifted his chin. "A forger's son. He can make you a document, a seal, or a signature, and he can read one faster than you can read a road."

"They're not the cargo," Beatrix said. "I don't haul people."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "You haul them over the Escarpment before the snows close. That's the entire proposition, and we both know you're going to say yes, so let's not waste the lamp oil pretending otherwise."

Beatrix leaned back in her chair. The loft where she'd pried open the chest was two miles and a lifetime away; the neighbor who'd noticed too much had already been questioned by men with clean boots, she'd learned that much from the ferryman. There was no version of this where she walked away and stayed invisible. The chest had ended that. All that remained was to set the price.

"Terms," she said.

"Terms," Ilsa said, and unfolded her hands.

* * *

They negotiated the way old soldiers fight — economically, with an understanding that most of the ground was already decided and only the edges were in question.

"I know the archive's location," Ilsa began.

"You know a notation on a map fragment," Beatrix corrected. "You know a tradition. I know the mountain. There's a corridor through the Escarpment that appears on no chart printed in the last forty years. It was never surveyed because the surveyors were never allowed up there — it crosses three borders and a watershed, and every government involved preferred it unmapped. The smugglers kept it alive. I kept it alive after the smugglers were done. It's the only way over before snowfall that the consortium won't have sealed, because they don't know it exists, and the men they'd ask don't talk to men like them."

"And the archive sits at the far end of it."

"The archive sits under the Whitethroat glacier, if your notation means what I think it means. Which means the last third of the route is glacier travel, and the last mile is a door that may or may not still be there, because glaciers move and forty years is a long time."

"Six weeks," Petra Sollen said. Her voice was dry and quick. "The high passes historically close in the first week of the last month of autumn. We have six weeks, perhaps seven."

"Five," Beatrix said. "The barometer's been falling wrong for a week. This is an early-winter year. I've watched the larches turn from the top down too fast. We move in three days or we don't move at all."

"Three days," Davor said, and something in his voice suggested he'd been ready to move for eleven years.

Ilsa raised a hand, and the room quieted. "Then let us be precise about what each of us brings, so there are no surprises on the ice. I bring the knowledge — the archive's history, the cipher key's origins, the authentication protocols that will make the documents credible when they surface. I am old, I am slow on a mountain, and I will not pretend otherwise. I will slow you down."

"You'll have a rope on you the entire time," Beatrix said. "And a second rope in my pack."

"I assumed as much." Ilsa's mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile. "Davor brings valley craft, contacts on the far side, and the muscle to move the chest — because we are bringing the chest, Beatrix. The original documents are the proof. Copies can be dismissed as forgeries." She glanced at Tomas. "No offense to your trade."

"None taken," Tomas said. "Forgeries are how we got into this. It seems fitting."

"Petra brings conservation — the documents are water-damaged and will get worse before they get better, and every day of handling costs us credibility. And Tomas—" Ilsa paused, and Beatrix watched the old woman choose her next words with care. "Tomas brings the ability to make the consortium's own seals work for us, if it comes to that. Passes. Letters of passage. A lie with the right seal has opened more doors in this country than any truth ever has."

"And what do you bring, guide?" Davor asked. Not hostile. Testing.

Beatrix let the silence sit for a moment. "I bring the route. I bring the only rope team experience on this table. And I bring the fact that the consortium has been hunting me since spring and hasn't found me, which means I know how they move, what they pay their informants, and how they think, because I've spent four months being exactly what they think a hunted person looks like."

"You were blacklisted," Petra said. It wasn't a question. "Both registries."

"I was erased," Beatrix said. "There's a difference. Blacklisted means they remember you. Erased means they've decided you were never there. My certifications were struck, my rescue record was sealed, and the one rescue that mattered — the one where I did everything right — became the story of how two guides killed three climbers through negligence. That was eleven years ago. I've been a ghost since."

The young man — Tomas — had gone very still. Beatrix caught it, the way he was looking at her, and recognized the look. She'd seen it before, in young guides who'd heard the outlaw version of her story, the version told in low taverns where the truth had been sanded down into something shinier. She didn't like it. It put a weight on her that wasn't hers to carry.

"Whatever you've heard," she said to him, "it's wrong, or it's half-wrong, which is worse. I'm not a legend. I'm a guide who lost people, and then lost everything else, and got very good at not being found. That's the whole of it."

"That's not what my father said," Tomas said quietly.

"Your father," Beatrix said, "made documents for people who needed to disappear. I imagine he said a lot of things."

"He said you were the only guide who ever came to him for papers and paid in full, up front, and never asked him to make you into someone new. Just someone unfindable." Tomas's eyes were steady. "He said that told him everything he needed to know about what the registries had done to you."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. Rell. She'd never asked the forger's surname. She'd paid in coin, taken the papers, and walked out of that back room in a border town she'd never returned to, because that was the discipline of being unfindable — you never let the same door open twice.

"Your father is alive?" she asked.

"No," Tomas said.

"I'm sorry."

"He'd have liked this," Tomas said, and gestured at the table, the lamp, the chest sitting in the corner with its Vellmark crest catching the lamplight. "He spent his life making false papers for a country that stopped existing. He'd have liked being in the room where the true ones came back."

* * *

The negotiation's hard edges came at midnight, over the question of command.

"The route is mine," Beatrix said. "On the mountain, my word is the last word. Not negotiable. Not discussable. If I say we turn back, we turn back. If I say we leave the chest, we leave the chest. If I say we leave someone—"

"You'll never say that," Davor said. "Not you. I've heard what kind of guide you were."

"You don't know what kind of guide I am now," Beatrix said. "Eleven years of guiding no one but myself makes a different animal. But you're right — I won't leave someone. That's exactly why the rule exists. Because I know myself, and I know that the moment I want to keep going is the moment I'm most dangerous to everyone on my rope. So when I say turn, you turn. All of you. Including her." She nodded at Ilsa. "Especially her. She's the payload. The documents are the payload. I'm just the road."

"And if we refuse to turn?" Petra asked.

"Then I tie you to a rock and collect you on the way down." Beatrix said it flatly, without humor, and watched them decide whether she was joking. She let them decide. "I've done it before. The man I tied to a rock is alive today and still angry about it, and I can live with both."

"And off the mountain?" Ilsa asked. "In the towns, at the ferry crossings, in the low valleys where Davor's word is law and mine is rumor. Who commands there?"

"You do," Beatrix said. "I don't know your world. I don't know which innkeepers are yours and which are theirs. Below the treeline, I follow you. Above it, you follow me. And at the boundary —" she tapped the table — "we talk. Fast. Before we cross it."

Ilsa studied her for a long moment. The lamplight made the old woman's face into a map of itself, every line a road she'd walked. "You've done this before," she said. "Commanded a mixed company."

"I've done rope teams," Beatrix said. "Strangers tied together, learning each other's weight. It's the same thing. The rope doesn't care if you like the person at the other end. It only cares if you trust them, and trust is a thing you can build in three days if you're honest about your weaknesses."

"What are yours?" Davor asked.

It was a fair question, and Beatrix respected him for asking it, and hated him for it, and answered it anyway, because that was the deal being made in this room.

"I freeze at decisions that look like the glacier," she said. "Eleven years ago I had a rope team on a serac slope and I made the call to cross late in the day because the clients were impatient and I was young and I wanted the summit more than I wanted to be safe. The mountain punished that. Three people died. Since then, when a choice looks like that choice — late light, bad angle, pressure to push on — I hesitate. Not always. Not visibly. But inside, there's a half-second where I'm not deciding, I'm remembering. A good enemy could use that half-second. Corvane—" she saw them react to the name — "yes, I know who hunts us, I've read his file the way he's read mine — Corvane is a good enemy. So now you know. That's the weakness. There's the price of admission."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of people recalculating.

"And your strengths?" Petra asked.

"I read terrain faster than anyone alive. I can find water, shelter, and a way through where other guides see a wall. I don't panic, and when I do, I panic quietly. And I have never, in eleven years of being hunted, been caught — which is not luck. It's craft."

"Then we have a company," Ilsa said. "Not a friendship. A company."

"Company," Beatrix agreed. "Friendships get people killed on rope teams. People who like each other hesitate to give hard orders. People who respect each other don't."

* * *

They argued about the chest for another hour. Ilsa wanted it carried whole; Beatrix wanted it broken down, the documents distributed across packs, waterproofed in oilcloth and wax, so that no single disaster — a crevasse, a river crossing, a betrayal — could take them all.

"You're talking about splitting the proof of a nation's murder into five pieces," Ilsa said.

"I'm talking about not letting one crevasse eat your country's whole history," Beatrix said. "The chest stays behind. It's too heavy, too recognizable, and it's the only thing about us that tells Corvane what we're carrying. A sealed chest with a Vellmark crest is a confession. Five packs of oilcloth are laundry."

"She's right," Petra said quietly. "And I can make the oilcloth bundles look like nothing at all. Specimen rolls. Botanical collectors' wraps. No one searches a botanist's packs."

"Then we're agreed," Beatrix said. "The chest goes in the river."

"The chest," Ilsa said, with the finality of a woman closing a ledger, "goes in my root cellar, under the floor, until this is over. I did not spend forty years preserving a nation's memory to drown it in a ferry crossing on a whim."

Beatrix looked at her, and for the first time that night, something like respect moved through her, unwelcome and warm. "The root cellar," she conceded. "But if the consortium comes here, you burn it. You don't let them have it. Swear it."

Ilsa held out her hand, and they shook on it, an old woman's dry grip and a guide's calloused one, and that was the closest thing to a vow the room would see.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when the terms were done. Davor and Petra had gone to sleep in the loft above; Tomas lingered, rolling a cigarette he didn't light, and Beatrix found herself standing at the window with Ilsa, looking out at the ferry yard where the river ran black and quick under a moon going in and out of cloud.

"Ask it," Ilsa said. "You've been circling it all night."

"Why me?" Beatrix said. "You've got Davor's contacts, Petra's hands, Tomas's seals. You could have found any guide. Half the blacklisted ones would have jumped at this. Why the one whose name was struck from two registries?"

Ilsa was quiet for a while. Down in the yard, a mooring rope creaked against its post.

"Because I read the rescue file," she said at last. "Before it was sealed. I was working in the records office in those years — under another name, in another capacity. I read the testimony of the survivors, the guides' statements, the weather logs. I read what they did to you, Beatrix. And I knew, when the chest came up out of the river and the story started moving through the low towns like blood through a body, that if I wanted this done, I needed the one guide in the Karst who had already been destroyed by these people and was still standing." She turned. "I needed someone with nothing left to lose who had chosen, anyway, to keep living carefully. That's rarer than courage. Courage is cheap. Careful living after everything's taken — that's the expensive thing."

"You could have just asked."

"I did just ask. You're here."

Beatrix almost smiled. Out on the river, the current carried a branch past the pilings, quick and indifferent, the same water that had brought the chest to her feet four months ago. She thought about the moment on the bank — her hands on the wet wood, the Vellmark seal under her thumbs, the old reflex saying *push it back, let the river have it, stay invisible* — and how she hadn't. How she'd never once, in eleven years, been glad of the invisibility. Only safe in it. There was a difference, and she was only now learning its size.

"Three days," she said. "Everyone at the north ferry yard at first light, packs weighed, boots inspected. Anyone whose gear isn't right stays behind, and that's not cruelty, it's arithmetic. The mountain doesn't grade on effort."

"The mountain," Ilsa said, "and Corvane."

"Corvane's just weather with a budget," Beatrix said. "Weather I can read."

She shouldered her pack at the door and paused, hand on the latch. Behind her, the lamp threw Ilsa's shadow long across the floor, over the rug that hid the root cellar, over the chest that held a country's stolen name.

"Get some sleep, Archivist," Beatrix said. "You're about to become a very slow mountaineer."

"And you," Ilsa said, "are about to become a very public ghost."

Beatrix went out into the dark, and the river said nothing, and the mountain to the north stood against the stars with its passes still open — five weeks of open, maybe less — waiting to find out what kind of company she'd just tied herself to.
