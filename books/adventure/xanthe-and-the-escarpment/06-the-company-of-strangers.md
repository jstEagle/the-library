## 6. The Company of Strangers

My father taught me that every forgery begins with a true thing. You take the real seal, the real hand, the real weight of paper, and you bend it just enough to make it lie. He said the best liars in Vellmark were all honest men at heart — they simply understood how truth was built.

I thought about him the whole first morning on the trail, because Beatrix Brightwater turned out to be nothing like the stories.

The stories had Beatrix six feet tall with a scar from jaw to collarbone and a voice like gravel sliding off a cart. The person standing at the trailhead above Greywater Ford was shorter than me, broad through the shoulders, with a face weathered to the color of old rope and eyes that went over each of us the way my father used to check a bill for flaws — quickly, thoroughly, and without any affection for what they found.

"Names," Beatrix said.

We'd already given names. Twice. Ilsa had insisted on it in her back room, over tea that tasted of tannin and secrets. But Beatrix wanted them again, out here, where the river ran loud enough to cover a confession.

"Tomas Rell," I said, third in line.

"You're the forger's son."

It wasn't a question. My stomach dropped anyway. "I can—"

"I don't care what you can do. Can you walk?" Beatrix's gaze moved past me before I answered. "Davor. You've got new boots."

Davor Kess looked down at his boots as if seeing them for the first time. "Bought them yesterday."

"With what?"

"The money Ilsa gave us for provisions." Davor's chin came up. He was the oldest of us exiles, a customs man once, back when there was a Vellmark to have customs in, and he carried himself like a man who'd been demoted by history rather than by anyone's fault. "Wet feet kill more climbers than falls. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone who's read about climbing," Beatrix said. "Break them in tonight or carry them. Blisters on day one become infections by day four, and I don't pack enough rope to haul two of you." The eyes moved on. "Petra."

Petra Solm, who'd been a surveyor's assistant before the annexation and a smuggler after it, and who hadn't spoken more than ten words since I'd met her: "Here."

"You'll be second rope behind me. Davor, you're behind Petra. Tomas, anchor position. If we go down a slope, you're the last one over the edge, which means you need to trust everyone ahead of you more than they deserve." A pause. "Questions?"

I had about forty. None of them survived the look on Beatrix's face.

* * *

Ilsa came up from the ferry road an hour later, riding a mule she clearly distrusted, and the whole dynamic of the company changed in a way I didn't understand until much later. Beatrix, who had treated the four of us like inventory, treated Ilsa like weather — something to be planned around, respected, and never argued with.

"You didn't wait," Ilsa said, dismounting badly.

"We're losing daylight."

"You waited eleven years to open that chest. You could wait an hour for an old woman to say goodbye to her own kitchen."

Beatrix said nothing to that, which from what I'd seen was the loudest argument available.

We made maybe nine miles the first day, along the low valley track, and I learned three things. One: Petra counted her steps under her breath and got visibly angry when anyone broke the count. Two: Davor talked constantly — about the old ferries, the old tariffs, the old flag, the old everything — and nobody listened, and he kept talking anyway, the way a man rings a bell in fog because the alternative is admitting the fog has swallowed him. Three: Beatrix Brightwater walked at the front and never once looked back, and somehow always knew exactly when one of us stumbled.

That last one unsettled me most. It was like watching my father work — the illusion of effortlessness built on top of a thousand hours nobody saw.

At the first camp, under a leaning shelf of granite above the river, Beatrix distributed tasks without discussion. Petra gathered wood. Davor took water. Ilsa sat by the fire with the map fragment wrapped in oilcloth on her knees, doing whatever archivists do, which as far as I could tell was staring at things until they confessed. That left me.

"What do you want me to do?" I asked.

Beatrix looked up from the stove. "What are you good at?"

"Paperwork," I said. "Ink. Seals. I can copy a hand so well my father used to—" I stopped, because Beatrix's expression had gone flat in a way I recognized. It was the way people looked at my father right before the gendarmes came.

"Rope," Beatrix said. "Coil the spare lines. Then sleep."

So I coiled rope in the dark while the others settled, and I listened to Davor tell Ilsa about the ferry schedules of 1889, and I thought: this is the legendary outlaw company that's going to shake three capitals. A step-counter, a bell-ringer, a woman who stares at paper, and a forger's son holding rope wrong.

Somewhere beyond the firelight, Beatrix laughed — short, surprised, like it had escaped custody.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." But the laugh was still there, banked down. "You coil rope like a clerk."

"My father said—"

"Your father folded paper for a living. This is different." Beatrix crossed over, took the coil from my hands, and rebuilt it in about eight seconds, loops falling into place like soldiers answering roll. "Over-under. Watch." Did it again, slower. "Now you."

I did it wrong twice. The third time it held its shape, and Beatrix nodded once and went back to the stove, and I sat there feeling absurdly pleased with myself over a length of hemp, which is probably the most embarrassing thing I'll ever admit to writing down.

* * *

The second day we left the valley track and started up, and that's when the company of strangers began to come apart in the small, ordinary ways that I suspect every rope team comes apart before it holds.

It started with the pace. Beatrix set a rhythm I could keep but barely, and Davor couldn't keep at all. By mid-morning he was thirty yards back, and by noon he was making his case.

"We should rest."

"We rested at the stream," Beatrix said, not turning around.

"That was twenty minutes ago."

"Yes."

"My lungs aren't what—"

"They are what they are. We move while the light moves." Beatrix finally stopped and let him catch up, and I watched something pass between them — not kindness exactly, more like an accounting. "You want to make the tree line before dark. Below it, we camp warm. Above it, we camp alive. Your choice which kind of night you'd rather have."

Davor chose the tree line, muttering about guides who thought altitude was a religion.

Then it was Petra's turn. She'd been silent for six hours, which I gathered was normal, but at the first steep pitch she refused the rope.

"I don't tie in on ground," she said.

"It's not ground. It's a forty-degree slab with wet grass on it."

"I've crossed worse alone."

"And now you're not alone. Tie in."

They stood facing each other on the slope, and I swear neither of them blinked, and Ilsa called up from below in a voice dry as old vellum: "Petra. The guide is right. Take the rope."

Something in the way she said it — the authority of a woman who had spent decades being believed — did what Beatrix's glare couldn't. Petra tied in, furiously, and then climbed the slab so fast she nearly pulled Beatrix off balance, and when we regrouped at the top she said, "Told you I didn't need it," and Beatrix said, "You needed it. You just needed someone else to say so," and Petra almost smiled, which I later learned was the equivalent of another woman weeping with joy.

Me, I fell. Not far — a slip on scree, a skinned palm, the particular humiliation of going down in front of people. But I went down hard enough that my pack spilled, and among the things that tumbled out onto the rocks was a folded square of paper I'd been carrying since the ferry.

Beatrix picked it up before I could.

It was a practice sheet. A page of handwriting exercises — the same sentence written twelve times in twelve hands, one of them a very good imitation of a Vellish customs stamp authorization. My father's training drills. I'd kept the page the way other people keep a saint's medal.

Beatrix read it upside down, which told me something, and then read it right side up, which told me more.

"This is your father's hand?"

"The top line. The rest are mine."

"How old were you when you wrote these?"

"Eleven."

A long pause. The wind came up the valley and pushed at our backs like a hand between the shoulder blades.

"When we get to the archive," Beatrix said, handing the page back carefully, "if there are seals on those documents that need reading — really reading, not just looking at — I may have work for you." And then, before I could ruin the moment by beaming: "Pick up your pack. Anchor position means you watch the ground, not your boots."

I watched the ground. Mostly.

* * *

That night, above the tree line's last ragged pines, the comedy of us finally became visible even to Davor, who was usually the last man in any room to notice a joke at his own expense.

It happened over dinner. Ilsa produced a lump of Vellish honey-cake from somewhere in her baggage — actual honey-cake, baked in the old style, dense enough to stop a bullet — and cut it into five pieces with the gravity of a woman dividing an estate. We ate it in silence. Then Davor, mouth full, said, "My mother made this with caraway," and Petra said, "With caraway it's funeral cake, you absolute relic," and Davor said, "It is not funeral cake, it's festival cake, we served it at the Assumption fair every year of my childhood," and Petra said, "Because everyone died at the fair, apparently," and Ilsa closed her eyes and said, "Both of you have been wrong about this since before the annexation and I refuse to referee it at four thousand feet," and Beatrix — Beatrix laughed.

Not the banked laugh from the first night. A real one, startled out of them, head tipped back toward the first stars, and for about three seconds the whole mountain seemed lighter for it.

Then Beatrix caught us looking and put the laugh away like contraband.

"Sleep," Beatrix said. "First rope tomorrow. Real rope. Ice at the col by afternoon if the cold holds."

We rolled into our blankets. I lay awake a while, listening to the company breathe — Petra's slow metronome, Davor's occasional muttering in a dialect I only half knew, Ilsa's silence, which somehow had a sound anyway — and I thought about what my father would say if he could see me here. He'd say I'd fallen in with criminals. He'd be right. He'd also, I think, have liked them, because they were all, in their various ways, honest about the lies the world had told them.

The stars were very bright. Somewhere north of us, over the shoulders of the Escarpment, the high snowfields caught their light and threw it back faintly, like paper held up to a lamp.

I was almost asleep when Beatrix spoke from the edge of camp, quietly, to no one in particular or possibly to the mountain:

"There's a fire down-valley."

I propped up on an elbow. Far below, back along the track we'd climbed, a point of orange flickered against the black — too steady for a campfire, too small for a farmhouse.

"One fire," I said.

"One fire," Beatrix agreed, "where there shouldn't be anyone." A pause. "Or several someones, moving together."

Nobody else woke. Ilsa's breathing didn't change, though I'd have sworn she was listening. Beatrix stood watch a long time, a shape against the stars, and in the morning the fire was gone and the pace doubled, and nobody complained — not even Davor — because strangers or not, we were roped together now, and the mountain had begun telling us what that meant.
