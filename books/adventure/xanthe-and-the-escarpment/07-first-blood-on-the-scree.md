## 7. First Blood on the Scree

The scree fields began where the pines gave up, a gray ocean of broken stone sloping down from the shoulder of the Escarpment like something the mountain had coughed out and never bothered to clean up. Beatrix stopped at the treeline and let the others catch up, which took longer than she liked.

They were four days out from Ilsa's back room, and they were still learning to be a rope team instead of five strangers who happened to walk in the same direction. Davor moved well — too well, she'd noted, for a man who claimed to have spent twenty years behind a desk in Ferrenholt. Tomas moved badly but cheerfully, his pack straps always wrong, his grin permanent. The Venn woman — old Ilsa herself, because she had refused to be left behind and nobody had been fool enough to argue — climbed with a stubborn economy that reminded Beatrix of her mentor, Halvard, may he rest under whatever glacier had finally taken him. And Petra, the quiet one, the one who'd been a ferry-wright before the annexation made her a widow and an exile in the same week, said nothing and noticed everything.

"Rest," Beatrix said. "Ten minutes. Then we cross the scree before the sun softens anything."

"Why does the sun matter?" Tomas asked, dropping onto a boulder with the enthusiasm of a man twice his age.

"Because frozen scree holds together like a bad marriage, and thawed scree lets go." She shaded her eyes and looked up at the field. It rose six hundred meters to a rib of gray rock, and beyond the rib, if her reading was right, the smugglers' corridor cut west toward the high passes. "We move fast, we move light, we don't bunch up. If one person goes down, the stone takes whoever's standing below them."

"And if two go down?" Davor asked.

"Then I've failed," Beatrix said, and drank her water.

She had crossed this field eleven times. She knew it the way you know a face you've seen crying and laughing both. There was a line through it — not a path, paths were for maps and maps were for people with registries — a *line*, a grammar of stable blocks and loose aprons that she could read the way Ilsa read treaty marginalia. You went up along the tongue of gray where the frost heave hadn't chewed through yet. You avoided the fan below the red-streaked cliff, because that fan moved every spring and would move again. You kept your feet on the big blocks, the ones too heavy to lie about their intentions.

She was about to call the ten minutes when Petra stiffened beside her.

"Riders," Petra said. "South. In the trees."

Beatrix didn't look. Looking is a gift you give people; she'd learned that from watching Halvard die. Instead she listened: the creak of saddle leather, a horse shifting its weight, the particular silence of men trying not to make noise and failing at it. Four riders, maybe five, holding back at the treeline the way wolves hold back when they haven't decided yet.

"How many?" Ilsa asked quietly. The old woman had come up beside her without a sound, which should have frightened Beatrix more than the riders did.

"Four or five. They're waiting."

"For what?"

"For us to commit to the scree. It's a killing ground if they're above us, and a trap if they're behind. They want us halfway across when they come out." Beatrix turned and looked at them all now, casually, as if checking packs. "So we don't give them halfway."

Davor's hand had gone inside his coat. "I can shoot from here—"

"You can shoot from here and then explain to me how we outrun their friends across open stone for three days." She shook her head once. "Nobody shoots. Not first. That's not what this is today."

"What is it today?" Tomas whispered.

"Today it's a lesson in reading." She looked back at the scree field, really looked, letting her eyes do the work her hands would do in a minute. The morning sun was climbing the eastern wall. By midday it would hit the upper field and start prying the frost out of everything. Below the red-streaked cliff, the fan sat in shadow until afternoon.

There was another way. There was almost always another way, if you were willing to take one that no sane guide would write down.

"The gully," she said. "West edge. Where the field meets the rock."

Petra followed her gaze and frowned. "That's not a route. That's a chute."

"It's a chute with a floor. The blocks are keyed in — you can see the interlocking from here, the way they're stacked. Somebody built that floor, deliberately, years ago. Smugglers, probably. It'll hold four people walking soft." She paused. "It will not hold horses."

Understanding moved around the group like weather. Ilsa got there first, because of course she did. "You mean to draw them onto the field itself."

"I mean to let them choose it," Beatrix said. "They think the scree is their weapon. We're going to hand it to them and step aside."

* * *

The trick of moving across scree quietly is that you don't fight the stone; you agree with it. Every block has a neighbor it leans on, and if you find the lean, you can put your weight where the mountain already expects weight to be. Beatrix went first, setting the rhythm — heel, roll, toe, listen — and the others copied her the way you copy someone dancing. Tomas was loud. Davor was quieter than he'd been on any previous day, which she filed away. Petra was nearly silent. Ilsa climbed like a woman who had done harder things than this in worse shoes.

Behind them, at the treeline, the riders waited. Let them wait. Every minute the sun climbed, the upper field got softer, and every minute the riders' arithmetic got worse without them knowing it.

Halfway across, Beatrix called the halt she'd planned to call, loudly enough to carry. She pointed up-slope toward the obvious line — the broad, reasonable-looking ramp that any competent pursuer would read as the route. It wasn't. It traversed beneath the red-streaked cliff, and its surface was armored with flat plates of stone sitting on marl, and by noon the marl would be soup. But a man on a horse looking up from below wouldn't see marl. He'd see a road.

"There," she said, pitching her voice to carry. "That's our line. We'll rest at the top of the ramp."

Tomas, bless him, understood immediately and made a show of collapsing against a boulder, groaning theatrically. Petra unslung her pack and rummaged in it with the exaggerated weariness of a tired courier. Ilsa simply sat, folded her hands, and became an old woman resting, which required no acting at all.

Then Beatrix led them, low and fast, into the western gully.

The chute was exactly as she remembered it: a slot between the living rock and the field, floored with interlocked blocks laid by somebody patient and desperate decades ago. It held them. It even hid them — from below, the gully read as shadow and broken stone, nothing worth a second glance. From inside it, they could see the whole field spread out below like a map of other people's mistakes.

They didn't have long. The riders came out of the trees within the quarter hour, five of them, riding easy, fanning wide the way men do when they think the work is already done. Their leader was a heavyset man on a dun mare, and even at this distance Beatrix recognized the cut of his coat. Consortium grey, but tailored. This wasn't hired muscle. This was management.

"They're taking the ramp," Petra breathed.

"Of course they are. It's the only line that looks like a line." Beatrix watched the lead rider bring his horse to the base of the field and hesitate — good instinct, wasted — and then follow his own confidence up onto the plates.

For a while, nothing. The riders picked their way upward, spreading out, and the scree accepted them the way it accepted everyone: patiently, without comment. Beatrix found she was holding her breath anyway, and made herself stop. This was the part she hadn't thought through carefully enough, back at the treeline, back when the plan had felt like geometry instead of consequences. The plan required the field to do what fields do. Fields slide. That was the whole point.

The sun cleared the eastern wall and touched the upper field, and the marl began to wake up.

It started small — a hiss of settling stone, a plate tilting by a finger's width. The lead rider steadied his mare and kept coming, because turning back on a slope is harder than going on, and every man knows that in his knees even when his head won't admit it. Behind him, the second rider chose a line slightly off the ramp, onto the looser apron, and that was the mistake. His horse's hind hoof punched through a crust, and the crust let go, and the letting-go traveled.

Scree avalanches aren't dramatic at the start. That's what makes them terrible. A patch of slope the size of a table begins to pour, and the pouring undermines the block above it, and the block above that, and within ten seconds a hundred tons of the mountainside is in motion with the calm inevitability of water finding its level.

"Down," Beatrix said, though they were already low in the gully, already safe, already nothing but witnesses.

The second rider fought uphill for two strides and then did the right thing, the only thing: he bailed off his horse and threw himself flat behind a boulder the size of a cart. The horse screamed — horses scream, people forget that — and went down the slope sideways in a spray of stone, and the slide took it, and the field closed over it like water over a dropped coin. The rider behind the boulder lived because the boulder lived. The lead rider on the ramp might have lived too, if the slide had stayed in the apron. But slides don't respect lines drawn by men. The edge of it licked across the lower ramp, and his mare lurched, and he went off her, and for one long moment he was sliding on his back down a field of moving stone with nothing in the world to hold.

He caught a block. Beatrix saw it happen — saw his arm snap out and his hand close on the corner of a fixed slab, saw the whole weight of his body jerk against his shoulder, saw him hold. Below him the slide poured past and thinned and died, and the field settled into its new shape, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"He's alive," Tomas whispered. "He's hanging on. He's — we have to—"

"No," Beatrix said.

The word came out of her flat and cold, and she heard it as if someone else had said it, and hated it instantly.

"We have to help him," Tomas said. "He's going to lose his grip. Look at him — his feet are swinging, there's nothing under him, he's just *hanging*—"

"I know what he's doing. I taught half this range to hang like that." She was watching the rider's boots kick against the smooth face below the slab, searching for purchase that wasn't there. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, and his grip would go. The drop below him was forty meters of fresh-run scree, and fresh-run scree is a lie of stability over a throat that swallows.

"We can't reach him anyway," Davor said. "Not without crossing the run-out. He's lost."

"He's not lost," Beatrix heard herself say, and then, before the sensible part of her could object, she was moving.

* * *

Later, she couldn't fully reconstruct the decision. She knew the pieces: the rider's face, gray with effort, younger than his coat. The mare's hindquarters still showing above the stone, which meant the slide had buried it shallow, which meant the run-out was cooling, which meant — her eye insisted on doing this math whether she wanted it or not — there was a line. There was always a line. Up the gully to its head, out onto the keyed blocks above the slide scar, down the spine of fixed slabs to a point ten meters above the hanging man. A rope. An anchor. A lowering.

A rescue.

The last time she had performed those exact calculations, on the Vesper Glacier nine years ago, the math had been perfect and the outcome had been a coffin and two blacklists and a career erased from every registry in two countries. She had sworn — not aloud, never aloud, but sworn in the place where oaths actually live — that she would never again spend her skill on other people's lives. Skill spent on escape only. Skill spent on getting herself and whatever contraband paid the rent safely over stone. That was the bargain she'd made with the mountain, and the mountain had honored it for nine years.

And here she was, rigging a loop around a horn of granite with hands that remembered exactly how to do it.

"Give me the rope," she said. Nobody argued. Petra fed it out; Ilsa anchored the tail around a block with a tensionless hitch that made Beatrix look at her twice. Even Davor helped, bracing her hips as she backed over the lip of the scar.

The descent was thirty meters of controlled horror. The fresh-run stone shifted under her boots with every step, whispering, testing her weight, deciding. She talked to it under her breath the way Halvard used to — *hold, hold, I'm light, I'm nothing* — and it mostly believed her. Above the hanging rider she stopped, set her heels, and paid out the last of the rope.

"Hands on the rope," she called down. "Don't grab. Wrap. Wrap it twice and lock it with your boot. Can you lock it?"

The rider looked up at her. Up close he was maybe thirty, with consortium grey gone dark with sweat and a face that had stopped pretending to be a hunter's face and become simply a young man's face, terrified, human.

"Yes," he said.

"Then wrap it. Now."

He wrapped it. She took the shock of his weight through her harness and her hips and her teeth, and for a moment the whole apparatus — her body, the rope, the anchor, the mountain's grudging permission — hung in balance above forty meters of hungry stone. Then it held. Of course it held. She had built it to hold. That was the obscene thing about skill: it worked whether you wanted it to or not.

Getting him up took everything the five of them had, and ten minutes none of them had. When they finally hauled him over the lip onto the keyed blocks of the gully floor, he lay there gasping like landed fish, and Tomas knelt beside him and gave him water from his own flask, and Beatrix stood apart with her hands shaking in a way they hadn't shaken during the descent.

"You should have let me fall," the rider said. It wasn't gratitude. It was confusion, raw and total, the confusion of a man whose employers had told him exactly what she was.

"Probably," Beatrix said.

"Corvane says you're the one who killed the Vesper party. That you cut the rope."

"Corvane says a lot of things." She crouched so their eyes were level. "Here's what you tell him. Tell him we went up the west gully. Tell him the field did what fields do. And tell him—" she paused, because the next part surprised her as it left her mouth, "—tell him the guide he sent to kill us owes her a rope."

They took his knife, his whistle, and his horse's empty saddlebags, and left him on the blocks with water enough to reach the treeline. He watched them go with the expression of a man re-reading a book he'd thought he knew.

* * *

They climbed the rest of the day without speaking much, up out of the scree and onto the gray rib, and camped at dusk in a hollow below the corridor's mouth. The fire was small. The wind was not.

Beatrix sat apart, cleaning grit out of her rope coils, and the work didn't stop the thinking, which was the problem with work.

Nine years. Nine years of sharpening herself into a tool with one edge — the escaping edge, the vanishing edge, the edge that kept her alive and cost her nothing but company. And today, given a free choice, with no flood chest forcing her hand and no exile band depending on her, she had gone down a slide run on a rope for a man sent to kill her, and her hands had known every knot without asking her permission.

Halvard would have laughed at her. *You don't get to choose what you are, Astrid,* he'd have said, using the name nobody used anymore. *You only get to choose what you do with it.* And then he'd have charged her for the advice, because he was like that.

But it wasn't quite that either, and lying to yourself is the one luxury a guide can't afford on a mountain. The truth was uglier and simpler: some part of her had wanted the rider to die. Had stood in that gully and watched him swing over the void and felt the cold, clean relief of a problem solving itself. The skill had saved him. The wanting had not. Both things were true, and she carried both down the rope, and only one of them showed.

"You knew that gully would hold," Petra said, settling beside her with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had decided Beatrix was worth talking to. "Before you ever saw it up close. How?"

"Somebody built it. People who build things leave signatures." She coiled the rope. "Same as forgers."

Petra glanced back toward the fire, where Ilsa sat with the map fragment's replacement notes spread on her knees, reconstructing by lamplight what Davor's theft — no. What the river had taken. Beatrix corrected herself, and noted that she'd had to correct herself, and filed that away too, in the drawer where she kept things she wasn't ready to open.

"Tomorrow we enter the corridor," Petra said. "The one that isn't on maps."

"The one that isn't on maps," Beatrix agreed.

Below them, far down the valley they'd left, a light appeared. Then a second. Then a string of them, patient and orderly, moving up the ramp like beads on a wire.

Corvane wasn't sending five riders anymore.

Beatrix counted the lights twice, then looked west, where the corridor's mouth waited in the dark like a held breath, and reached for her pack.
