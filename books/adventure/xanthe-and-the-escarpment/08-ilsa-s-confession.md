## 8. Ilsa's Confession

The fire was small on purpose. Ilsa Venn had spent forty years learning how much light a person could afford, and the answer, most nights, was almost none. She fed the flames one dry branch at a time, watching each catch and gutter, while above them the Escarpment's shoulder rose black against a sky salted with stars.

They had made nine miles since dawn and camped in a hollow below the tree line, a fold of stone that Beatrix had found without slowing down, as if the mountain had whispered it to her in passing. The others were settling: Tomas coiling rope with the exaggerated care of a boy showing off for his hero; Sera checking the document case for the fourth time since supper; Davor at the edge of the firelight, cleaning his boots with more attention than they deserved.

Ilsa watched Davor's hands and told herself she was only watching because old archivists noticed everything. It was what she was for.

"You're doing the thing," Sera said without looking up from the case. "Where you count us."

"I'm not counting you."

"You're counting us. You do it when you're worried."

"I'm always worried. It's cheaper than prayer." Ilsa set another branch on the fire. "And tonight I have something to say, and I keep hoping someone will talk over me so I don't have to say it."

Beatrix looked up from the map she was studying by firelight — the route ahead, the col they'd cross tomorrow, all of it drawn from memory and smugglers' gossip because no honest chart would carry it. "Then say it," Beatrix said. "Nobody here talks over anybody. It's the worst-behaved ambush party I've ever led."

Tomas laughed. Even Davor's boot-brushing paused.

Ilsa folded her hands in her lap, the way she used to fold them before opening a sealed folio, and found that the gesture did nothing for her now. Her hands wanted to shake. She let them.

"When I was twenty-six," she said, "I authenticated a treaty."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale.

"Not a copy. Not a transcription. The original instrument itself — the Treaty of Meridian Vale, signed between the Crown of Vellmark and the Consortium of Northern Interests, the document that formalized the annexation protocols. My hands touched it. My seal went under it. I was the third-ranked authenticator in the Royal Archive, and I certified that the signatures, the paper stock, the wax, and the chain of custody were genuine." She heard her own voice go flat and official, the register she'd worn for thirty years, and made herself drop it. "It wasn't. Any of it."

Sera sat very still. Tomas had stopped fidgeting entirely.

"I know what you're thinking," Ilsa said. "You're thinking: we knew this. That's why we're walking over a glacier carrying these papers. Yes. But there's knowing and there's knowing, and I've been carrying the second kind for a long time, and I've never once said it out loud to people who had the right to hear it. So let me say the whole thing, badly, and then you can decide what I'm worth."

Beatrix closed the map. "Go on."

* * *

"I was young and I was proud and I was terrified," Ilsa said, "which is a combination that has built most of the world's disasters. Do you know what the Archive was, in those years? Not a library. A fortress. We were the last institution of the old kingdom still functioning after the annexation, and everyone told us that made us sacred. Custodians of continuity. If Vellmark as a state was gone, the record would remain, and someday — everyone said someday — the record would restore what politics had taken. I believed that the way you believe in weather.

"The treaty came up for authentication six years after the annexation. It arrived under Consortium seal, escorted, with a request for certification so it could be lodged in the international registries. Standard procedure. Except." She paused. "Except I had seen the annexation papers when they first came through, years earlier, and something about them had itched. Nothing provable. The ink was right, the wax was right, but the *handwriting rhythm* — the way a forger's letters sit too evenly on the line, like soldiers at inspection — I'd noticed it and filed the doubt away, because filing doubts away is what a junior archivist learns to do if she wants to keep her post.

"So when the treaty came to my desk, I looked for the itch. And I found it. Same hand. The same too-even soldier letters, in a document supposedly written four years apart by different secretaries in different cities. Two documents, one ghostwriter. I ran the tests anyway, properly, because if I was going to destroy a peace settlement I was going to do it beyond argument. Paper fibers: consistent across both documents, inconsistent with either claimed provenance. Wax: a compound blend that didn't exist in the royal chancery's inventory until after the annexation. And the chain-of-custody seals —" she laughed, a small bitter sound — "the seals were perfect. That was the tell. Real documents accumulate damage. These had none. They had been born already archived.

"I wrote my report. I wrote it three times. The first draft said *forged*. The second draft said *irregularities warranting further study*. The third draft — the one I submitted — said *authenticated, with minor procedural observations noted*. 

"You want to know why. Everyone always wants to know why, and the answer is never interesting enough for them. Here it is: a man named Chancellor Ostrava came to my office the night before I filed. He was the Archive's director, and he was kind to me, which should have warned me. He said: Ilsa, if this treaty fails certification, the Consortium declares the annexation void ab initio, which sounds like victory, except they hold every garrison and every customs house, and a void annexation means they stop pretending to be administrators and start being occupiers openly. There will be war. Vellmark has eleven thousand men under arms and they have sixty thousand. He said: your certification lets us negotiate from inside the fiction. Time. Autonomy guarantees. A generation to outlast them. He said: you are not certifying a lie, Ilsa, you are certifying a bridge, and bridges are how people survive.

"And I was twenty-six, and he was the most powerful man who had ever been kind to me, and I wanted so badly for him to be right that I made him right. I filed the third draft. The treaty entered the registries under my seal. The autonomy guarantees lasted nineteen months. Then the Consortium stopped needing the fiction, and the fiction dissolved, and Vellmark dissolved with it, quietly, the way ice goes out of a river — no single day you can point to, just one morning the channel runs empty.

"They gave me a commendation. Did you know that? A commendation, for service to regional stability. I kept it for thirty years and I could never decide whether keeping it was penance or vanity, and I burned it the night I left the country, and I regretted burning it, because destroying evidence is apparently the one habit I can't break."

She stopped. The fire had burned low and she fed it again, grateful for the task, for hands with something to do.

* * *

"The worst part," she said, when she trusted her voice again, "isn't the forgery. Forgers I can forgive; they're craftsmen, and craft is amoral. The worst part is that I was good. My doubt was correct, my evidence was sound, my report — the first draft — would have held up in any court on the continent. The truth was in my hands, fully formed, and I chose the story that let me sleep. And then I spent thirty years telling myself I'd been deceived, when the plain fact is that I deceived myself first and merely let Ostrava finish the job.

"That's the confession. Not that I failed to see the forgery. That I saw it."

Silence. The wind came down off the col and stirred the smoke sideways, and for a moment the firelight swung across all their faces at once, and Ilsa thought: whatever happens next happens now.

It was Sera who spoke first, and Ilsa braced herself, because Sera had lost the most — a brother in the border purges, a name on a list Sera carried in oilcloth against her heart.

"My brother's unit surrendered under the terms of that treaty," Sera said slowly. "The autonomy guarantees. They laid down arms because the document said they'd be treated as domestic police under Vellish command." She looked into the fire. "You certified the paper that disarmed them."

"Yes."

"Six weeks later the security directorates reclassified them. Enemy combatants retroactively. You know the number."

"I know the number," Ilsa said. "I've read the lists. I've read them for thirty years. I read them the way other people pray."

Sera nodded, once, twice, working something through. "I'm not going to tell you it's fine," she said. "It isn't fine. But I'll tell you what I decided somewhere around mile forty today, watching you read that scree slope better than anyone I've ever walked with. If I put you outside the circle of the guilty, I have to put myself somewhere, and the place I'd be standing is *innocent*, and I know exactly what I did to stay innocent. I kept my head down. I changed my name. I taught school to exile children and told myself teaching was resistance. It wasn't. It was hiding with a curriculum." She looked up. "So no. It isn't fine. And you're walking with us anyway, because the alternative is a company of pure people, and pure people have never once gotten anything back from the Consortium except killed."

Tomas shifted forward, knees cracking, looking younger and older at once. "My father forged papers," he said. "Exit visas. Marriage certificates. Whole new lives, forty crowns apiece. He always said the difference between a forger and an honest man is that the forger admits the world runs on paper." He shrugged, embarrassed. "I don't have anything wise. I just — I'm glad you said it. Out loud. My father died never having confessed to anything, and it made him small. You saying it makes you bigger. That's all I've got."

"It's more than enough," Ilsa said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much she meant it.

Davor spoke from the edge of the light, and Ilsa realized she had been waiting for his voice the way you wait for a tooth to ache. "Ostrava," he said. "He lived well, I take it."

"He died in his bed," Ilsa said. "At eighty-one. State funeral."

"Mm." Davor set down his boots. "And the man who forged the documents? The ghostwriter?"

"Never identified. The hand appears on eleven documents across nine years. Someone in the Consortium chancery, or close to it. Whoever they were, they were very good, and they were never caught, and I have thought about them more often than I've thought about Ostrava. Because Ostrava needed a liar. Somebody *made* the lie. Somebody sat at a desk and practiced a dead secretary's letterforms until they were perfect, knowing what the paper would disarm." She shook her head. "I've hated them for thirty years, and lately I've noticed the hatred has a familiar shape. It points both directions."

Davor considered this. "Honest, at least," he said, and said nothing else, and turned back to his boots, and Ilsa told herself again that noticing everything was simply what she was for.

* * *

Beatrix had listened the way Beatrix did everything: without wasted motion. Now the guide stirred the fire with a stick, and the sparks went up toward the stars and died.

"I erased two countries' worth of registry entries," Beatrix said. "You certified a lie. Between us we've got a full set of crimes against paperwork." A ghost of the guide's dry humor, but underneath it something else, and Ilsa recognized it, because she'd spent her life among documents and knew a marginal note when she saw one — the thing written beside the main text, quieter, truer. "Here's what I keep coming back to. You said the first draft said *forged*. Where is it?"

Isla blinked. "What?"

"The first draft. Three drafts, you said. The third went into the file. What happened to the first two?"

For a moment Ilsa couldn't speak. Thirty years of carrying the weight, and she had never once — not once — thought of it as anything but ash.

"Ostrava instructed me to destroy all working drafts," she said finally. "Standard procedure for sensitive certifications. And I —" She stopped. Started again. "I destroyed the second draft. The middle one, the coward's version. But the first one — the one that said *forged*, with the full evidentiary apparatus, fiber analysis, wax compound, the handwriting comparison plates — I took it home. I told myself I was keeping it as insurance. I told myself that for thirty years. Insurance implies you intend to use it. I think I just couldn't burn the only honest thing I ever wrote."

"Where?" Beatrix asked. Not unkindly. The way you ask a patient where it hurts.

"Burned. With the commendation. The night I fled." Ilsa pressed her palms flat against her knees. "Everything I just confessed to, I confessed to ashes. There's no first draft anymore. There's only the drafts the Consortium kept, and the copies in the archive we're walking toward, and —"

She stopped.

Because she had stopped walking, in her mind, at the same place she'd stopped speaking, and the thought arrived the way the best archival discoveries always did: not as a flash but as a slow, terrible, wonderful click, like a lock whose key had been in her pocket for decades.

"What," Beatrix said.

"The marginalia." Ilsa was on her feet without deciding to stand. "The treaty drafts we pulled from the chest — the ones Beatrix fished out of the river — the Consortium's internal working copies. I read them on the ferry, I read them again last night, and I catalogued the annotations without thinking about what I was reading, because cataloguing is what my hands do while my mind is elsewhere. There are corrections in the margins. Handwriting corrections. Someone went through the forged instruments and adjusted details — dates, seal placements, a chancery inventory reference — to fix errors, to make the forgeries *better*. Eleven documents across nine years, all improved by the same correcting hand." She was talking fast now, the old archive cadence, and she didn't care. "I assumed it was the ghostwriter refining their work. But ghostwriters don't leave correction trails on documents destined for the secure files. Correction trails are left by *reviewers*. By the person who checked the work. And I know that hand, Beatrix. I've spent thirty years hating it. I've compared it against my own memory ten thousand times. It's the hand that corrected the Treaty of Meridian Vale's companion instruments — the ones I authenticated alongside it, the ones where the same too-even letters appear."

Sera stood too. "Whose hand?"

"A chancery reviewer," Ilsa said. "Someone inside the Consortium with access to the originals *and* the forgeries, which means someone who could prove the whole scheme from the inside, and chose instead to perfect it." She looked around the fire, at each of them, at the dark wall of the Escarpment rising behind. "The chest we're carrying doesn't just contain the forgeries. It contains the forger's homework. Corrections, revisions, practice strokes — a complete graphological profile of the person who built the lie that erased a country. If any archive in the world holds a matching hand — and the Vellmark Archive will, because whoever they were, they worked from materials that passed through it — then we don't just prove the treaties were forged. We prove *who forged them*. A name. The one name thirty years of lists never gave me."

Tomas whistled softly. "The chest is better than we thought."

"The chest was always better than we thought," Ilsa said. "That's the nature of chests. It's the people who open them late." She sat back down, suddenly exhausted, the adrenaline draining out through her boots into the cold ground. "I'm sorry. I had a confession prepared and I've replaced it with a research finding. Old habits."

"No," Beatrix said. The guide was looking at her with an expression Ilsa hadn't seen before — not suspicion, not warmth, but the particular attention of one professional recognizing another's competence in the field. "You had a confession, and then you did the thing that matters more. You turned guilt into intelligence. Purity's useless on a mountain. Guilt with a direction of travel — that I can lead with." She picked up the map again. "Tomorrow we cross the col. Day after, the high corridor. Which means tonight, Archivist, you're going to show me every marginal correction in those drafts, and you're going to teach me to recognize that hand, because if the Consortium understands what we're carrying even half as well as you just did, they won't be sending riders anymore."

"They'll be sending Corvane," Sera said quietly.

At the edge of the firelight, Davor's boot-brushing had stopped altogether.

Ilsa opened the document case with steady hands — steadier than they'd been all evening, which struck her as the night's final irony: confessing had shaken her, but the work calmed her, the work had always calmed her, and perhaps that was the truest confession of all. She spread the treaty drafts on a flat stone, weighted the corners, and set the fire's small light to work.

"Page by page," she said. "Watch the descenders. Watch how evenly they sit, like soldiers at inspection."

Above the hollow, far up the black shoulder of the Escarpment, something moved against the stars — or didn't; at this distance, at this hour, no one could swear to it. Only Davor looked up, and only for a moment, and then he leaned in with the rest of them to study the handwriting of a ghost.
