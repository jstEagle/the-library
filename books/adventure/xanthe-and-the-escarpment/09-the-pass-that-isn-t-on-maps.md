## 9. The Pass That Isn't on Maps

They left the widow's track before dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridgeline they were already on ground that did not exist.

Beatrix felt it in her boots first — the particular give of a trail maintained by no one, worn only by feet that had reasons to be unrecorded. The smugglers' corridor began as a sheep path above Maren's summer pasture, then became a ledge, then became nothing at all: a traverse across a face where the rock had been stacked in slabs like badly dealt cards. On any official chart, from either country, this was blank space. The cartographers had drawn a glacier and a cliff and left the space between them white, as if whiteness were the same as emptiness.

It wasn't. Whiteness was just paper's way of saying *we don't want to know*.

"Where are we?" Tomas asked. He had the map fragment's memory copied into a notebook he kept inside his coat, against his chest, and Beatrix had watched him check it three times since the col, as if Davor might come back for it even now.

"Nowhere," Beatrix said. "That's the point."

They climbed. The company strung out behind her in the order she'd set at first light: Ilsa next, because the old woman's knees were honest but her pride wasn't, and she'd kill herself trying to keep up rather than ask for a rope; then Sera and Pellin with the chest's contents redistributed between their packs; then the two younger exiles whose names Beatrix still stumbled over; then Tomas, who insisted on the rear because rear-guard was what heroes did, and who was learning, slowly, that rear-guard was mostly what tired people did.

The ledge narrowed to a hand's width. Below them the valley dropped away in green terraces, and beyond the terraces, far to the south, a thread of smoke rose where the consortium riders had been two days ago. Beatrix didn't point it out. Everyone had seen it. Everyone had decided not to say anything about it, which was its own kind of discipline.

"Hand on the wall," she said instead. "Weight on your feet. The rock doesn't care about your fear, so stop offering it."

Ilsa laughed — a dry, cracked sound, like a book opening after years shut. "You sound like Halvard."

Beatrix's hands tightened on the stone. Halvard. Her mentor. The man whose notes had led them toward an archive under a glacier, whose name had been struck from the same registers hers had, who had died poor and drunk and certain that the mountains remembered what the governments chose to forget.

"You knew him," Beatrix said. Not a question.

"I authenticated his field reports once." Ilsa's voice came level from behind her. "Before I understood what authenticating meant. Before I understood what anything meant." A pause, breath drawn thin at altitude. "He wrote that the best routes were the ones no one would pay you to know. I thought it was bitterness. It was prophecy."

Beatrix said nothing. But she climbed a little straighter, and the ledge forgave her.

* * *

By midday they reached the place Beatrix thought of as the Doorway — a cleft in the escarpment's shoulder where two walls leaned together like conspirators, leaving a slot of sky maybe three meters wide. Water had cut it over ten thousand years, and smugglers had widened it over two hundred, chipping footholds into the walls, driving iron pins that were older than either nation currently claiming this land.

She'd found it at twenty-three, blacklisted eight months, broke, following a mule train she had no business following because the mule driver owed her money and she intended to collect. He'd taken her up through the Doorway instead of paying, taught her the corridor in a single crossing, and told her: *This is what they took your name for. Now it's yours.* She'd never known if it was generosity or recruitment. It hadn't mattered. Knowledge was the only currency that couldn't be confiscated.

"In here, single file," she said. "Touch nothing that isn't rock. Some of these pins are decorative."

"Decorative," Sera repeated flatly.

"They're holding the mountain together out of habit. So are we. Move."

One by one they went through — Ilsa first, stubbornly refusing Beatrix's offered shoulder until the third pin, when her knee buckled and she took the shoulder without comment and without thanks, which Beatrix respected more than gratitude. Then the others, packs scraping the walls, breath loud in the narrow dark. Last came Tomas, and halfway through he stopped.

"There's writing here," he said.

Beatrix eased back beside him. On the wall, low where a climber's hand would fall naturally, someone had scratched letters into the stone with a knife or a nail. Old letters. Vellish letters.

*HOLD THE LINE AND THE LINE HOLDS YOU.*

Below it, smaller: a date forty years old, and initials she didn't recognize.

Tomas read it twice, his lips moving. When he looked up his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "My father used to say that. Before—" He stopped. "Everyone said it. In Vellmark. Before there wasn't a Vellmark to say it in."

"Somebody carried it up here," Beatrix said. "Forty years ago. Carried it over ground that isn't on maps, into a country that erased theirs, and put it where only people like us would ever find it." She touched the letters. Cold stone, warm meaning. "That's not graffiti. That's a relay station."

Tomas wiped his face with the back of his glove, embarrassed, and finished the climb. But Beatrix stayed a moment longer, her palm flat against the scratch of dead Vellish steel, and felt something she hadn't let herself feel since the river gave up its chest:

Not curiosity. Not dread.

Company.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow the charts insisted was uninhabitable — a wind scoop behind a rib of limestone, sheltered enough for a fire if you knew how to build one low and feed it slow. Beatrix built it the way the mule driver had taught her, and the smoke rose thin and blue and vanished into the rock before it could become a signal.

Pellin cooked. The exiles ate. And somewhere between the barley and the tea, the talk started — the kind of talk that happens when strangers have shared enough danger to run out of caution.

"The consortium's bounty," Sera said. "Maren said doubled. What does that make you worth now, guide? A house? A village?"

"A rumor," Beatrix said. "Rumors hold their value better."

Laughter went around the fire, and Beatrix let it, and realized she was smiling, and realized further that she couldn't remember the last time. Two countries' registries had struck her name. Two governments considered her a liability. Every legitimate pass, every licensed route, every honest wage — closed. And yet here she sat, on ground that existed nowhere officially, feeding people who existed nowhere officially, and the fire was warm and the route was holding and nobody had died.

There was a word for what she felt and it took her a moment to find it, because it wasn't a word she'd ever been permitted.

*Pride.*

Not the pride of the summit register, the signed name, the witnessed achievement. The other kind. The kind that grows in the dark, unwatered, unwitnessed — the knowledge that everything she knew about mountains, everything that kept six people alive tonight, had come to her precisely *because* she'd been cast out. The legitimized guides learned the mapped passes and the posted conditions and the safe seasons. She had learned the rest. The erased corridors. The unread weather. The hundred small betrayals the mountains commit against anyone foolish enough to trust the official version of them.

They had taken her name and given her, in exchange, the truth. She was only now deciding it had been a fair trade.

"You're doing it again," Ilsa said quietly, beside her. The old woman had a way of speaking without moving her lips much, a lifetime's habit of not being overheard.

"Doing what?"

"Deciding something alone. I can see it. Your jaw sets on the left side."

Beatrix almost laughed. "You've known me nine days."

"I've known people who carry things alone for sixty years. You have the look." Ilsa fed a twig to the fire. "Whatever you're deciding — decide it out loud. That's what a company is. Otherwise we're just six people walking in the same direction, and that's not the same thing at all."

Beatrix looked at the fire. At the exiles arguing softly about ration weights. At Tomas showing one of the younger ones how to read the stars for latitude, his hands moving with a forger's precision, tracing constellations in the air like signatures.

"When we cross the high shoulder tomorrow," she heard herself say, "we leave the last charted water source behind us. After that, everything depends on snowmelt and my memory. If my memory's wrong, we die politely, in order, roped together." She paused. "I wanted you all to hear me choose it. Out loud. Since apparently that's what a company is."

Ilsa nodded slowly, satisfied, and said nothing else, which was its own kind of approval.

But later, when the fire had burned to coals and the watch rotation was set, Beatrix sat up alone a while longer, looking north along the corridor they'd climbed, back the way they'd come. Somewhere down there was the ferry, the loft, the rented life. Somewhere south were the riders and the smoke and the doubled bounty. And ahead, higher than anything on any map she'd ever been paid to walk, was a glacier that had eaten a country's memory and kept it cold.

Every official road was closed to her. Every unofficial one was open.

She found, lying in her bedroll under stars that belonged to no registry, that she preferred it that way — and that this preference frightened her more than Corvane's riders ever had. Because a person who prefers the erased roads is a person who might never come down. And some part of her, the part that had survived the glacier, the blacklist, the long gray years of invisibility, whispered that coming down was exactly what she'd sworn never to do again.

Sleep took her mid-argument with herself.

* * *

Dawn came wrong.

Beatrix knew it before she opened her eyes — the quality of light through her lids too bright, too white, the air too still. She rolled out of the bedroll and stood, and the eastern horizon confirmed it: a cloud bank building along the high peaks, fat and dirty-white, stacked in layers like plates on a shelf.

Cirrus at dawn, thickening from the west. Her mentor's voice, decades dead: *Mare's tails mean the sky is telling you a story. Listen early or listen sorry.*

"Tomas," she said. "Barometer."

He had the instrument out before she finished — a good brass one, looted from a consortium supply cache in another life. He tapped it, squinted. "Falling. Fast. It's dropped since midnight."

"How fast?"

"Like the bottom's gone out of it."

Beatrix studied the cloud bank. Studied the route ahead — the high shoulder, the exposed traverse, the glacier approach. Three days of walking, if the weather held. One day, if it turned, and the difference between those numbers was the difference between a company and a casualty list.

The snows weren't supposed to come for another two weeks. The snows were supposed to follow the almanac, the way passes were supposed to appear on maps and guides were supposed to appear in registries. She should have known better than anyone: the world did not keep its promises to people like them.

"Pack up," she said. "All of it. We move in ten minutes, and we don't stop until the shoulder."

Sera was already rolling her kit. "How bad?"

Beatrix looked at the clouds again, and this time she let herself read them honestly, the way no licensed guide would dare to, the way only the erased were ever forced to learn.

"Worse than bad," she said. "That's not a storm front. That's winter, arriving early — and it knows exactly where we are."
