## 10. Storm Warning

Tomas Rell had learned to read weather the way other boys learned to read faces — out of necessity, in a forger's shop where a wrong guess about tomorrow meant ink ruined and paper warped and his father's temper turned ugly. So when he woke on the fourth morning above the tree line and saw the lenticular cloud stacked over the western peaks like a plate balanced on a bottle, he knew before anyone said a word.

He checked the barometer anyway. Beatrix carried one, a brass thing older than any of them, and had taken to letting him wind it each evening — a small trust, or maybe just a chore offloaded. The needle had been sliding all night. It sat now at a number Tomas had only ever seen in the margins of his father's almanacs, next to annotations like *stay home*.

"Beatrix," he called across the camp. "It's dropped again. Half an inch since midnight."

Beatrix was already standing, facing west, hands loose at their sides. They didn't turn around. "I know."

"You know how?"

"The cloud. The smell." Beatrix finally looked back at him, and there was something in their face that Tomas had never seen there before — not fear exactly, but a kind of grim accounting. "Snow's coming early. Days early. Maybe tonight."

Behind them, Ilsa Venn emerged from her shelter with the map fragment's replacement — the reconstruction she'd been penciling every night by firelight, her memory doing what the river had drowned. Davor was gone; the fragment was gone; but Ilsa's head held things no rider could steal, and she'd spent three nights transferring them to paper while the rest of them slept.

"Then we move faster," she said simply.

"We move *differently*," Beatrix corrected. "Faster is how people die up here when the weather turns. We go to the cache at Hollow Saddle, resupply, and then we make a decision about the high route versus the low traverse."

Tomas heard it before he understood it: the word *cache*. His stomach did something complicated. "The cache at Hollow Saddle," he repeated. "That's the one Davor knew about."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to.

---

They broke camp fast, and the mountain helped them hurry without meaning to. The cold had teeth now, a raw edge that hadn't been there two days ago, and the light through the thinning cloud had gone the flat pewter color that made distances impossible to judge. Tomas climbed with his hood up and his mind running the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the col: Davor had been feeding their position to the consortium since the ferry. Which meant the consortium knew their route. Which meant the consortium knew about the cache.

Which meant either the cache was bait, or it was already ash.

He kept this to himself through the morning's climb, because saying it aloud would make it real, and because the group had finally stopped sniping at each other and he didn't want to be the one who started it again. That was the strange thing about the last two days. Before the col, they'd been five strangers tied together by rope and resentment — Ilsa correcting everyone's pronunciation of Vellish place names, Marta and Oskar bickering about load distribution like an old married couple who'd never actually married, Beatrix tolerating all of it with the patience of a stone. After Davor, something had burned away. Not trust — you couldn't manufacture trust in forty-eight hours — but a kind of shared flinch. A common wound. When Marta stumbled on the moraine yesterday, three hands had reached for her before Beatrix's command came.

Fear, Tomas thought, was a terrible glue. But it held.

---

They crested the shoulder below Hollow Saddle in the early afternoon, and Tomas smelled it before he saw it.

Burnt oil. Burnt canvas. Underneath both, the sweet char of sugar — the hardtack and dried fruit they'd been counting on, cooked into carbon.

The cache site sat in a hollow between two slabs of granite, a natural locker that generations of smugglers had used and that Beatrix had described to them in detail two nights ago: sealed drums, waxed crates, enough food for six people for ten days, cached by a friend of Beatrix's mentor years ago and never touched. Tomas had dreamed about that food. He wasn't proud of it, but he had.

What remained was a black circle of scorched earth, split drum staves, and a half-melted tin badge pinned under a rock where they couldn't miss it. The crest of the consortium. A message written without words.

Oskar crouched at the edge of the burn and touched nothing. "Two days old, maybe three. They came up from the south side." He glanced at Beatrix. "They knew."

"They knew," Beatrix agreed. Their voice was level, which somehow made it worse.

"So we're done." Marta's voice cracked on the second word. She was the oldest of them after Ilsa, a former customs clerk with bad knees and a talent for seeing the worst outcome first. "No food, storm coming, hunters behind us and now *ahead* of us. This is where it ends. We should go down. Surrender the papers, tell them we were coerced—"

"With what leverage?" Ilsa asked quietly. "Davor already told them everything he knew. Our surrender buys them the one thing they still want: certainty that the archive stays buried. They don't need us alive for that, Marta. They need us *silent*."

Marta opened her mouth. Closed it. Sat down heavily on a rock and put her face in her hands.

Tomas watched Beatrix, because watching Beatrix had become his private education. They stood at the edge of the burnt circle for a long time, reading the ground the way Ilsa read documents — every scuff, every ash pattern, every choice the scouts had made. Then they walked a slow circuit around the hollow, wider and wider, and came back with an expression that had shifted from grim to something almost like interest.

"They burned the food," Beatrix said. "But they didn't take the rope."

"What rope?" Oskar asked.

Beatrix pointed. Wedged under a slab at the hollow's far edge, half-hidden by snow dust, lay a coil of climbing line — old, but sound. Next to it, a small iron box. Beatrix pried it open and held up its contents: a spool of waxed cord, a barometer tablet, a stub of pencil, and a folded note in handwriting Tomas recognized from the mentor's journal pages Ilsa had shown them.

*If you're reading this, Astrid never told you about my second cache. Good. Means she still doesn't trust easily. Supplies are at the glacier mouth, under the third cairn from the left as you face the ice. Take what you need. Leave the rest for whoever comes after. — E.*

Silence, except for the wind beginning to rise in the saddle above them.

"That's—" Tomas started.

"Elian Voss," Beatrix said. "My mentor's teacher. Dead eleven years. And a cache he never mentioned to me, in a spot I've walked past maybe thirty times." Something moved across their face — grief, or its long echo. "He hid it from me too. Or from everyone. Hard to say with Elian."

"Can we reach it before the storm?" Ilsa asked.

Beatrix looked west. The lenticular cloud had grown. It had edges now, sharp as cut paper, and beneath it the sky was filling with the gray smear of approaching snow. "Maybe. If we push hard and don't break for a proper meal. If we're slow, we spend the night somewhere very cold with nothing to eat, and then we keep going anyway, because the alternative is staying here to starve politely."

"Then let's not be slow," Oskar said.

---

They weren't slow. They were also not fast enough, and Tomas learned the difference over the next four hours in a series of small humiliations: a boot that soaked through at a stream crossing, a pack strap that froze stiff, fingers too numb to work a buckle until Marta, of all people, took his hand and worked it herself without comment, her own joints crackling audibly.

The snow started an hour before dusk. Not a blizzard — not yet — just fat, deliberate flakes falling straight down, the kind that meant the sky above was full and heavy and waiting. By the time they reached the shelf of rock where Beatrix decided they'd camp, there were two inches on everything, and the world had gone quiet in that particular way that Tomas hated most: the silence of being watched by weather.

They got the tarp up. They got the stove lit inside the lee of a boulder. They ate the last real food — a handful of rice each, a scrape of fat, half a sausage divided into fifths with a knife and a solemnity usually reserved for funerals. And then, because there was nothing else to do and nowhere to hide from each other, they talked.

"I was eight," Marta said, apropos of nothing, staring into the stove flame, "when the annexation finished. My father worked the border customs house in Drava. One day the seals changed. Same desk, same ledgers, different crest. He stamped papers for the new government for twenty years and never once said out loud that it was theft. He died still stamping." She looked around the circle. "I used to think he was a coward. Now I think he was just tired, and scared, and alone with it. That's what they count on. Being alone with it."

"I'm not alone with it," Oskar said. It came out gruffer than intended, and he cleared his throat. "That's all I meant to say."

Tomas found himself speaking before he'd decided to. "My father forged Vellish exit permits. Did you know that? Real ones, copied from stolen blanks. He charged exiles everything they had, and sometimes he charged them nothing, and I never knew which until later. He'd come home some nights and drink, and some nights he wouldn't, and the difference was whether someone had paid." He stared at his boots. "When they arrested him, the consortium prosecutors called him a parasite. A man who profited off desperation. And the worst part is they weren't entirely wrong. But the *reason* there was desperation to profit off — that was theirs. They built the market my father died supplying." He looked up. Beatrix was watching him with an expression he couldn't name. "I used to be ashamed of him. I'm not anymore. I'm ashamed of them."

The fire popped. Snow ticked against the tarp.

"He'd have liked you," Ilsa said to Tomas quietly. "Your father. The good nights, at least. Archivists and forgers are cousins — we both keep the truth alive by making copies." She smiled, thin and tired. "Though only one of us goes to prison for it."

That got a laugh — a real one, ragged at the edges, from everyone including Marta. And Tomas felt something shift in his chest that he couldn't have described if you'd paid him: the bickering and the grudges and the cold-war silences of the last week dissolving into something simpler. Not friendship. Not yet, maybe not ever fully. But a rope-team feeling. The knowledge that tomorrow, when the pitch steepened, these specific people would be holding his life on the other end of a line, and he'd be holding theirs, and that this fact had quietly become the most important fact in the world.

Beatrix let the talk run another few minutes, then doused the stove.

"Sleep in shifts," they said. "Storm's going to build overnight. Tomorrow we cross open ground to the glacier approach, and I want everyone fresh enough to move fast when it breaks." They paused at the edge of the firelight, and for once they didn't look like armor wearing a person. They looked tired, and human, and young in a way Tomas had never noticed. "You did well today. All of you. Burning a cache is what they do to people they think are already beaten. We're still moving. That's the whole war right now — still moving."

One by one they rolled into their shelters. Tomas took first watch, sitting with his back to the boulder, the barometer in his lap. The needle had fallen again. Outside the tarp's glow, the snow came down thicker, erasing the trail behind them inch by inch, and somewhere out in that white dark, he knew, riders were doing the same arithmetic he was — food, distance, days — and reaching conclusions that ended with them finding the party frozen, or finding them at the glacier, or finding nothing at all.

He wound the brass mechanism, set the instrument down, and pulled his hood tight.

Below the shelf, faint under the wind, he heard it: a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Long, deep, rolling. An avalanche, distant, on a slope he couldn't see.

The storm wasn't coming anymore. It was here.
