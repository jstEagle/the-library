## 11. The Widow of High Camp

The storm gave them four hours of warning, and Beatrix spent all of them wishing she had more.

She read it first in the light — that flat, pewter quality that turns the high meadows colorless — then in the wind, which had swung from the west to the north-northwest and gone quiet in the way that quiet means trouble. The barometer Ilsa carried in a leather case against her breastbone, like a relic, confirmed it: the needle had fallen a full finger's width since morning.

"Snow tonight," Beatrix said. "Heavy. Maybe two days."

Tomas looked up at a sky that was, to his eye, still blue at the edges. "Two days?"

"Two days. Maybe three if we're lucky, and we haven't been lucky since the ferry." She turned to the others. Davor stood apart as always, arms folded, watching the valley below with an expression Beatrix had stopped trying to read. Ilsa was already repacking. The two older exiles — Petra and the quiet one called Anselm — began cinching straps without being told, which was the only reason Beatrix hadn't yet turned them around and sent them home.

"Is there shelter?" Ilsa asked.

Beatrix looked north, up the long shoulder of the Escarpment where the meadows gave out into stone. "There's a camp. A herder's camp. High Camp, we used to call it." She hesitated. The name tasted like a decade she didn't want to chew. "The woman who keeps it is named Maren. If she's still there, she'll take us in."

"If?" Tomas said.

"Her husband died eight years ago. The sheep went with him, most of them. Some people stay after a thing like that and some don't." Beatrix shouldered her pack. "Move. We want to be behind her walls before the light goes flat for good."

They climbed. The trail was a herder's trail, which meant it was no trail at all, just a line of cairns that Beatrix's eyes found the way other people found faces in crowds — instantly, and without knowing why. The meadows rose in slow switchbacks, gold going gray, and behind them the southern valleys lay in a haze that was no longer entirely haze. Somewhere down in that haze were riders. Somewhere down there was Corvane, or Corvane's money moving other men's legs, and the arithmetic of the bounty Beatrix carried on her head.

She didn't say any of that. She set a pace that ate the switchbacks and let the altitude do the talking.

They came over the last shoulder at dusk, and there it was: a low stone bothy with a sod roof, a byre attached, a drying rack stripped bare for winter, and a thread of woodsmoke standing perfectly straight in the dead air. Beatrix felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched.

"She's here," she said, and heard her own voice go strange on the second word.

They came down to the camp in the last light, and the door opened before Beatrix knocked — a small woman, sixty or better, gray hair cut short and practical, a wooden spoon in one hand and a look on her face that moved from alarm to recognition to something older and more complicated than either.

"Well," Maren said. "The river sends me fish and the mountain sends me ghosts."

"Maren."

"Beatrix Galloway." She said the whole name slowly, like she was checking it against a list she kept somewhere behind her eyes. "Ten years, and you still walk like him. Get inside, all of you, before the sky finishes deciding."

* * *

The bothy was one room, stone-floored, warm from a stove that had been fed all day in anticipation of exactly this weather. Maren fed them in shifts because the room was small: barley soup with mutton in it, dark bread, cheese hard enough to require a knife and patience. She fed them the way she did everything — without ceremony, without questions, watching the door more than her guests.

The exiles ate like people who had been afraid for a long time. Tomas ate like a boy, which he still was under the beard he'd started growing on the ferry. Ilsa ate slowly and kept looking at the walls, at the bundles of dried herbs hanging from the rafters, at the single shelf of books — six books, spines cracked — and Beatrix watched her cataloguing them the way she'd catalogued the map fragment, hungry and hiding it.

Davor ate with his back to the wall and his eyes on the door. Beatrix noted it. She had been noting it since the ferry, and the noting had begun to feel like a stone she carried in her pocket, always there when she reached in.

"You knew my mentor," Beatrix said, when the first shift had eaten and the second was eating. "Halvard. You guided with him."

Maren didn't stop stirring the pot. "I guided with him for eleven years. Then he went and got himself a reputation, and reputations are heavy cargo." She glanced over. "You got his. Or he got yours. Hard to say which way that kind of thing travels."

"The glacier rescue wasn't his fault."

"No," Maren said. "It wasn't." She let that stand, then ladled more soup into Tomas's bowl without asking. "It wasn't yours either. I've had ten years to think about it up here, and I've concluded it wasn't anyone's fault except the mountain, and the mountain doesn't do apologies. The registries in two countries decided otherwise. Registries are written by people who were nowhere near the ice." She set the ladle down. "Eat. Argue after."

But Beatrix didn't want to argue. She wanted — and this surprised her, sitting in the warmth with her people fed and the storm arriving on schedule — she wanted to talk. Ten years of silence had built up behind her teeth like meltwater behind a moraine.

"He taught me route-reading," she said. "Everyone thinks it's about the mountain. It's not. It's about the light. The mountain tells you what it's going to do six hours before it does it, if you know how to read the light. He could stand on a col at noon and tell you what the weather was doing at midnight."

"He could," Maren said. "And the last time he did it, two countries' worth of officials decided he'd read it wrong." She wiped her hands on her apron. "He read it right. The cornice went early because the client wouldn't wait, and everyone knew it, and the client's family had money, and money writes registries." She looked at Beatrix straight on. "I know why you're up here, girl. Word travels faster than weather in these hills. There are men in the low valleys offering more silver than I've seen in my whole life for the people sitting in my kitchen."

The room went quiet. Tomas's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"How much more?" Ilsa asked.

"Double," Maren said. "Doubled three days ago. A guide and a party of Vellish exiles, last seen on the Karst, heading high. The word I heard said the guide alone was worth more than this whole valley." Her eyes didn't leave Beatrix. "And the men offering it aren't from here. They ride consortium horses and they've been buying up the low towns — grain, loyalty, whatever's for sale. Which in a hard year is everything."

Beatrix felt the old reflex rise in her chest, the one that had kept her alive for a decade: *leave tonight, leave now, don't sleep under a roof where your name has been spoken aloud.* She had built her whole life on that reflex. It had kept her breathing and it had kept her alone, and sitting in Maren's kitchen she understood, with the clarity that only warmth and soup and exhaustion can deliver, that the reflex and the prison were the same thing.

"We can go," she said. "Tonight. In this." She nodded at the window, where the first flakes had begun to tick against the glass like fingernails.

"You can," Maren agreed. "And by dawn the mountain will have decided for you, and not politely." She stood, took the pot off the stove, and set it down with a click that ended the discussion. "You'll sleep in the byre loft. There's hay and it holds heat. You'll leave when the weather breaks, and not before, and you'll do it because I'm telling you, and I've been reading this mountain's light since before you were born."

* * *

Later, when the exiles were bedded down in the loft — Tomas already asleep mid-sentence, Ilsa reading one of Maren's six books by candle stub, Davor a shadow in the far corner who was either sleeping or doing a very good imitation of it — Beatrix sat with Maren at the table while the storm found its voice outside.

Maren poured something from a clay jug. It was plum brandy, fierce and homemade, and it hit like a small avalanche.

"Halvard came through here," Maren said, "the year before they struck him from the rolls. Before the glacier. He was already worried. He said the consortium men had been asking about the high routes — not the trade passes, the *other* ones. The ones that aren't on charts." She turned her cup. "He said a man from Vellmark had come to him with papers. Old papers. He wouldn't say more, and then the glacier happened, and then he was dead within two years — the cold, the drink, take your pick — and I stayed up here and kept sheep and told myself the world down there wasn't my business anymore."

"Do you believe that?" Beatrix asked. "That it's not your business?"

Maren was quiet for a while. The stove ticked. The wind rose and fell against the sod roof like something breathing.

"I believe," she said finally, "that the people who broke your life and his life are the same people breaking Vellmark, and that they'd break this valley too if there was profit in it. And I believe that when they came through last week buying loyalty, old Sella at the ford took their silver and old Bren took theirs, and I told them both what I thought of it, and now I'm the only one up here who'll shelter a stranger." She smiled, and it was a hard smile. "So no. I don't believe it's not my business. I just believe it took me eight years and a dead husband to admit whose side I'm on."

"Why refuse the money?" It came out of Beatrix blunter than she intended. "Doubled. You said yourself it's more than this valley's worth. One word to the ford and you'd never herd again. You could—"

"Could what?" Maren said. "Buy a life? Girl, I *have* a life. It's small and it's high and it's mine, and it's the only thing I've got that those men can't forge." She reached across the table and gripped Beatrix's wrist, her hand dry and strong as a root. "You've been invisible ten years. I can see it on you — you sit like a woman who's practiced not sitting. Invisible keeps you alive. It doesn't keep you *human*. Somebody has to feed you like a person or you'll forget you are one." She released the wrist and stood. "That's why I refuse the money. Now sleep. The loft's warm and the storm's honest, at least. It only wants to kill you. It's not lying about anything."

* * *

Beatrix climbed to the loft and lay in the hay and did not sleep.

Above her, through the sod roof, the storm worked. She listened to it the way she'd been taught — wind direction, weight of the flakes on the thatch, the deep note of it — and her professional mind made its calculations while the rest of her lay still. Two days, maybe three. Enough. Barely enough. The pass they needed was two hard days beyond this camp, and the snows would close it behind them, which was the plan, and the plan had a flaw she kept not looking at directly: a plan that only works if nothing goes wrong is not a plan. It's a prayer.

She heard a floorboard below and went still.

Davor's corner of the loft was empty.

Beatrix lay in the hay and breathed and did not move, and told herself the reasonable things: a man getting up in the night, a man going down to the byre, a man who doesn't sleep well in strange lofts. She had been noting him since the ferry — the way he watched the valleys instead of the peaks, the way his questions were always about the route *down*, the way he'd been the first to know, back at the burned cache, exactly which direction the scouts had come from.

The reasonable things didn't fit. She knew how it felt when a rope team had a bad strand in it. She'd felt it on the glacier, ten years ago, and she'd ignored it then too, because the client was a paying client and paying clients don't get doubted.

Below her, the outer door opened and closed. Softly. The kind of soft that takes practice.

Beatrix rose without a sound, took her boots in her hand, and went down the ladder into Maren's dark kitchen, where the widow sat awake by the banked stove, and saw the question on Beatrix's face and answered it with her own:

"Your Vellish man," Maren said quietly. "He's been standing at the byre window for the last hour. Facing south. And there's a light down in the valley that's been answering him."
