## 12. Betrayal at the Col

Ilsa Venn had spent forty years learning to read people the way she read documents: not for what they said, but for what they were trying not to say. It was how she'd survived the annexation, how she'd survived the registries, how she'd survived three decades under a name that wasn't hers in a valley town where nobody asked questions because asking questions was how you ended up in a ledger.

And she had read Davor Melik wrong.

She knew it the way you know a forgery — not all at once, but in a slow accumulation of wrongness that the eye keeps forgiving until it can't. The way he volunteered for water duty. The way his boots were always dry. The way he asked Beatrix, casually, twice, how far it was to the col, and whether the weather would hold, and whether the consortium riders would bother coming up this high with the snows so close.

*Casual questions.* In her experience, casual questions were never casual. They were the questions a man asked when he already knew the answer and wanted to see if you knew it too.

She sat up in her bedroll in the black hours before dawn, beneath the overhang Beatrix had chosen below the col, and let the truth assemble itself in her chest like a confession she was reading aloud to herself.

Davor was gone.

Not gone the way a man goes to relieve himself in the night. Gone the way a man goes when he has packed before sleeping, when his bedroll is rolled tight and left neat — too neat — when his share of the rope is coiled and set on a stone like an apology.

She was on her feet before she finished thinking. Her knee, sixty-one years old and honest about it, cracked like a branch. She didn't care.

"Beatrix." She kept her voice low. A camp that woke screaming was a camp that scattered. "Beatrix. Wake up."

The guide came awake the way Ilsa imagined she did everything — completely, instantly, one hand already finding the ice axe beside her. In the grey pre-dawn her eyes were the only thing with any color in them, a pale sharp grey like the sky over the escarpment before weather.

"Davor's pack is gone," Ilsa said. "His rope's left behind. His boots — the wet ones, the ones he complained about — he took the dry pair and left these."

Beatrix was already moving. She didn't waste breath on questions; she read the camp the way Ilsa read documents, and Ilsa watched her do it, watched her crouch at the edge of the fire pit, touch the ash — cold, hours cold — and then stand very still, looking north, toward the col.

"He's been gone since midnight," Beatrix said. "Maybe earlier."

"He's been gone since the ferry," Ilsa said, and heard her own voice go flat and old. "I just didn't know it yet."

* * *

They woke the others. Tomas first — the boy came up swinging, all elbows, and Beatrix caught his wrist and said one word, *riders*, and he went still as a held breath. Then Sera, who asked nothing and started lacing her boots. Then Petri, who asked everything, and got no answers, which told him more than answers would have.

By the time the sky went from iron to dirty silver, they stood in a knot at the edge of camp, and Ilsa made herself say it plainly, because a lie told slowly was still a lie.

"Davor has taken the map fragment. He's gone down toward the valley. He has been in contact with the consortium since before we crossed the river — I believe since the ferry, since the first day. I vouched for him. I told you he was Vellish to the bone." She stopped. Her throat worked. "I was wrong."

The silence that followed was the worst kind — the kind with weather in it.

"He ate with us," Sera said at last. Not angry. Worse than angry. Confused, the way a person is confused when the arithmetic of their own life comes out wrong.

"He slept ten feet from me," Petri said. "I told him about my father. I told him about the *Ledger of Names*, Ilsa. I told him things I haven't said aloud in eleven years."

"I know," Ilsa said. "I know."

And she did know, and that was the wound of it. Not that Davor had betrayed the expedition. That he had betrayed *her*, and she had handed him the knife herself, had vouched for him with the only currency she had left, which was the credibility of a woman who had spent her whole life making sure her word was the one thing about her that couldn't be forged.

Beatrix had not spoken. She stood at the edge of the overhang, looking down the long scarp of the descent, and Ilsa watched her read the ground the way she'd read the flood-swollen river on the first day, when the chest came out of the water like a corpse with a secret.

"He's on the east flank," Beatrix said finally. "There's only one line down from here that doesn't kill you, and he knows I know it, so he'll take it fast and sloppy. Scree to the shoulder, then the goat track to the col. Four hours if he's careful. Three if he's frightened." She turned. "Frightened men are fast and dead. We can catch him at the col."

"And then what?" Petri asked. "What do we do when we catch him?"

Nobody answered. Ilsa noticed that Beatrix didn't answer, and noticed the shape of the not-answering, and understood that the guide had already done the arithmetic and didn't like the sum.

* * *

They climbed. Of course they climbed — there was no other direction that meant anything. The col sat above them like a notch cut by a careless god, and the wind came through it in long cold gusts that smelled of snow from basins they couldn't see. Beatrix set the pace, and it was the pace she used on bad ground: steady, unsentimental, the rhythm of a body that had decided fear was a luxury billed later.

Ilsa climbed behind her and thought about Davor.

She thought about the ferry, three weeks and a lifetime ago, when she'd seen the map fragment's notation in Beatrix's hand and felt her whole buried life crack open like river ice. She thought about how Davor had stood at her shoulder on the ferry deck, and how he'd said, softly, *you knew my mother's people, didn't you, from the archive at Sarn?* — and how she had been so starved for the sound of her own country in someone else's mouth that she'd answered everything he asked and thanked him for asking.

She had vouched for him to Beatrix with the full weight of her name — the false one, the true one, all of it. *He's one of ours,* she'd said. *I'd stake the archive on it.*

And the terrible thing, the thing she kept turning over like a document with a smudged seal, was that she might still be right. That was the sophistication of it. Davor *was* Vellish. His grief was real — she'd watched him weep at the fire the night Ilsa confessed her own part in authenticating the forged treaty, and his tears had not been performed; she would stake her life on that too, and possibly already had. He had simply decided, somewhere on the road, that grief was not a plan, and that the consortium's money was, and that a man who had lost a country was entitled to sell the pieces.

Or he was frightened. That was the other document she kept reading. Fear made forgers out of honest men; she'd authenticated enough coerced signatures to know. Somewhere down in the valleys, someone had found Davor's real name, his sister's address, his father's unquiet grave, and had made him an offer shaped like a threat. She could build that case. She wanted to build that case. She caught herself building it, brick by brick, on the climb — and made herself stop, because a scholar who argued only one side was no scholar at all, and the fact remained that her map fragment was in his coat and the consortium's riders were in the valley and the snows were coming whether anyone's motives were pure or not.

"You're blaming yourself," Beatrix said, without turning around. The guide's voice carried flat over the wind, neither kind nor unkind. "Don't waste the altitude on it."

"He was my responsibility."

"He was a grown man with his own reasons. You gave him trust. That's not a crime up here. It's the only thing that isn't."

Ilsa wanted to say something bitter and precise, the kind of sentence she'd spent a career sharpening. What came out instead was quieter. "I spent thirty years trusting no one. It kept me alive and it made me nothing. So when I finally trusted someone—" She stopped. The wind took the rest.

Beatrix climbed on. After a while she said, "Trust isn't the thing that failed. He failed. Those are different failures, and only one of them is yours to carry."

It was the closest thing to comfort the guide had offered since the river, and Ilsa took it the way you take a rope on bad ground: without grace, without thanks, because you need it.

* * *

They reached the shoulder at midday and saw him.

He was a dark figure on the goat track below, moving fast, the way frightened men move — too fast for the ground, his stride breaking on the loose stone, once stumbling to a knee and rising without stopping. The col lay a half-mile beyond him, a saddle of grey rock with the first dirty rime of the season already striping its lee side, and beyond the col the ground fell away in long folds toward the valley, toward the riders, toward whatever bargain he'd struck.

Beatrix went down the scree like water finding a channel — no hesitation, no wasted motion, choosing each step before her foot committed, the whole slope an open book to her in a way it would never be to any of them. Tomas matched her as well as he could, young and furious, shouting things that the wind shredded. Sera and Petri came behind with Ilsa in the middle, the old archivist's lungs burning, her knees filing their complaints, refusing to be left at the shoulder because this was hers, this reckoning, and she would arrive at it on her own feet or not at all.

They gained on him. Frightened men are fast and dead, Beatrix had said, and Davor was proving it — he took a line too far left, onto stone that rang hollow, and Beatrix shouted something Ilsa didn't catch and Davor didn't hear, and a whole shelf of scree let go beneath him and carried him forty yards down the track in a hissing grey avalanche of small stones. When it stopped he was up again, limping now, and the col was close, so close, and Ilsa understood with a scholar's cold clarity that he was going to make it. That the arithmetic of legs and fear was on his side.

Then he stopped.

He stopped at the lip of the col, and he did not go over it. He stood very still, a dark cutout against the white sky beyond, and Ilsa, twenty yards below and closing, saw what he saw.

Riders.

Three of them, maybe four, small in the distance on the valley side, but unmistakable — the consortium's men moved with a particular economy, no wandering, no hunting posture, just the straight confident lines of men who had been *told* where to be. Told by a man on a goat track who was now standing at the top of the world, holding a scrap of cipher-keyed paper, discovering that the people he'd sold them to had not needed him as much as he'd needed them.

Or — and this thought arrived in Ilsa like a blade between the ribs — discovering that they had needed him exactly as much as he'd feared, and that his usefulness ended at the col.

Davor turned. He looked back up the track at them — at her, she was sure, though the distance was too great for eyes; she felt it anyway, the way you feel a reader's attention through a page. And then he did the thing that Ilsa would think about for the rest of her life, in the way you think about a sentence you should have written differently.

He took the map fragment out of his coat.

He held it up. And the wind, the great westing wind that Beatrix had been reading all morning and muttering about, chose that moment to come through the col in a long hard gust, and the paper — a single sheet, cipher-annotated, irreplaceable, the only key between them and the archive — lifted out of Davor's hand like a bird released, tumbled once white against the grey sky, and was gone over the far side, down into the valley folds, into the riders' country, into the wind's keeping.

Davor stood looking at his empty hand.

Then he sat down on the rock at the lip of the col, all at once, like a man whose strings had been cut, and did not move.

* * *

They reached him as the first flakes came down — small, dry, experimental flakes, the snow testing its voice before the aria. Beatrix got there first and did not touch him. She stood over him with the wind pulling at her coat and said, "Are you hurt?"

It was, Ilsa thought, the most Beatrix sentence imaginable. A man had sold them all, and the guide's first question was whether the mountain had broken him.

"Ankle," Davor said. His voice was a ruin. "Maybe. I don't— " He looked up at Ilsa, and his face did the thing faces do when the performance is over and there is nothing left but the person underneath, who is smaller than the performance and colder. "Ilsa. I didn't— they had my sister's name. They had where she lives. I thought I could feed them enough to keep them off us and keep her safe, I thought—"

"You thought," Ilsa said, "and the thinking was the forgery."

He flinched as if struck.

She crouched in front of him, knees screaming, and made herself look at him — at the boy she had adopted on a ferry deck because he had his mother's vowels, at the man who had wept at her confession and sold their route the same week. Both of those men were real. That was the whole horror of the human document; it was never one text. It was always an original with a forgery bound into the same cover, and no archivist alive could always tell them apart.

"I vouched for you," she said. "I put my name under yours. In my country, that was the oldest contract there was — older than treaties, older than the consortium's paper. When I put my name under a thing, it stood." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "You broke something that isn't mine to forgive and isn't yours to sell. Do you understand what you've cost us? Not the paper. The paper I can maybe rebuild. You've cost us the believing."

"I know." He was crying now, openly, ugly, the tears freezing in the wind. "I know. I'm sorry. Ilsa, I'm sorry."

"Sorry is a word," she said. "I've authenticated ten thousand of them."

Beatrix crouched beside her. The snow was thickening, the flakes coming fatter now, and beyond the col the riders had stopped too — Ilsa could see them, small and patient, waiting out the weather or waiting for them, and there was no telling which and no time to learn.

"We can't go down that way and we can't stay here," Beatrix said. Her voice had gone into its calm register, the armor-voice, the one Ilsa had learned to hear the fear underneath. "The col's closed to us with riders below it. The map fragment is in the wind somewhere on the north face. And the snow is coming early." She looked at Davor a long moment. Then: "You'll come with us, tied, or you'll stay here and the mountain can have your apology. Choose now."

Davor looked at the riders. He looked at the snow. He looked at Ilsa, and whatever he saw in her face — and she made sure it was nothing, nothing at all, the flat blank of a closed archive — he chose the rope.

They tied him. Tomas did the knots, and his hands shook with something that wasn't cold, and nobody said anything about it.

They turned their backs on the col and on the riders and on the lost paper, and Beatrix led them up — up, because down was owned now, and up was the only direction that still belonged to nobody. Behind them the storm came down the valley like a door closing, and Ilsa walked into it with an empty map in her head and one name in her mouth that she didn't say aloud, because saying it would have been admitting she was already composing the reconstruction: the archive's location, the cipher's logic, the marginalia in the treaty drafts, everything she had memorized across thirty years of hiding and was now going to have to remember perfectly, in a storm, with a traitor on a rope behind her and no paper left to trust but the paper in her own skull.

*Beatrix,* she wanted to say. *I can find it. I think I can find it without the map.* But the wind was a shout now, and the snow was a wall, and there would be time to say it around a fire that they first had to survive.

The white took the col. The white took the riders. The white took everything, and they climbed into it.
