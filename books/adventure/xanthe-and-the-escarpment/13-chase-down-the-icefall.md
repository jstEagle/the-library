## 13. Chase Down the Icefall

Davor was a hundred meters ahead and gaining when Beatrix came off the col.

She'd heard the rope go slack behind her, turned, and seen the empty space where he'd been tied in — just the cut end of the line whipping in the wind, sheared clean with a knife. He'd been carrying that blade since the ferry. She'd watched him sharpen it on a whetstone outside Maren's widow's hut and thought nothing of it. That was the thing about betrayal: it never announced itself. It packed its own gear, shared your fire, complained about the state of its boots.

"Tomas!" She didn't look back. "With me!"

He caught up at the edge of the icefall, breathing hard, his crampons still half-buckled from the descent. Below them the glacier broke apart into a chaos of blue seracs and leaning towers, the whole mass groaning under the morning's sun like an old ship working loose its seams. Davor was already into it, small against the white, moving fast for a man who'd spent twenty years forging customs stamps in a cellar.

"He's heading for the lower route," Tomas gasped. "The one you said—"

"I know what I said." Beatrix spat, pulled her goggles down. "The one I said no sane person crosses after nine o'clock."

The sun had cleared the eastern ridge an hour ago. Every minute they hesitated, the meltwater worked deeper into the cracks between the ice towers, and every minute Davor got further away with the map fragment sewn into his jacket lining — the fragment that was the only key to the archive, the only reason any of this had ever mattered.

"Beatrix." Tomas grabbed her arm. His face was pale under the windburn. "If we go in there after him—"

"Then stay here."

She stepped onto the ice.

* * *

The icefall was a city that hated visitors. Beatrix moved through it the way she'd learned to move through all illegitimate places — by reading what the official maps refused to say. The seracs leaned east; so the safe line ran west. The meltwater sang in the crevasses; where it sang loudest, the bridges were thinnest. She'd crossed this glacier twice before, years ago, running cargo for men who paid in cash and asked no names, and both times she'd promised herself never again. The mountain kept a ledger too, and it always collected.

"Davor!" Her voice cracked off the ice walls and came back to her doubled, tripled, as if the glacier itself were mocking the pursuit. "You'll die down here and the paper dies with you!"

He didn't answer. He didn't need to. She could read his line from the tracks — good prints, deliberate, a man who'd listened during six days of travel even while pretending not to care. That was the worst part. He'd been competent. He'd carried his share. He'd taken the middle position on the rope without complaint. And the whole time he'd been memorizing their route for the consortium's riders, learning Ilsa's cipher-talk, waiting for the one morning when the terrain would give him his chance.

Tomas was thirty meters back, keeping to her line, and Beatrix found herself adjusting her pace without deciding to — slowing just enough that the boy wouldn't fall behind, wouldn't take a risk she hadn't vetted first. Old habit. Guide's habit. The habit that had gotten her erased from two registries, because once, on a glacier much like this one, she'd gone back for someone the arithmetic said was already lost.

Ahead, Davor reached the first real obstacle: a crevasse spanned by a bridge of snow the color of old bone. He tested it with one axe, then committed.

Beatrix saw the bridge flex. Saw the far lip sag a hand's width under him. Saw him make the far side anyway, stumbling, catching himself, standing up straighter than he needed to — the posture of a man telling himself the worst was over.

"Don't follow my line," she called back to Tomas. "Traverse left. Twenty meters. There's a solid rib of blue ice."

"You're going across?"

"I'm going across."

She hit the bridge at a run, because the only thing worse than a bad snow bridge was a bad snow bridge carrying a thoughtful weight. It held. It held the way rotten things hold — grudgingly, with sound effects — and then she was on the far side and closing the gap, forty meters, thirty, and Davor glanced over his shoulder for the first time.

What she saw in his face wasn't fear. It was apology. Which was somehow more frightening.

"They have my daughter," he shouted, and the words came out ragged, torn loose by altitude and guilt. "Corvane has my daughter, Beatrix. Since the ferry. Since before the ferry. What would you have done?"

She didn't answer. There was no honest answer. She thought of the glacier, twelve years ago, and the man she'd gone back for, and the two careers and one country that had died with him. She thought: *I would have told us. On the first night. Around the fire.* And she thought: *No. You'd have told us exactly when it served you.*

The mountain, meanwhile, made its own argument.

It started as a sound — a deep, structural groan somewhere above and to the right, in the cluster of towers the guides called the Cathedral because of how the light came through the gaps between them. Beatrix knew the sound. Every guide knows the sound. It is the sound of the mountain revising its opinion.

"Davor! MOVE!"

He moved. They both moved. For ten seconds the icefall contained nothing but three human beings sprinting across a frozen waterfall while the sun did its slow work overhead, and then the Cathedral let go.

Not all of it. A single serac, the size of a chapel, calved from the upper tier and came down the slope in a long, silent, almost graceful slide — the silence itself obscene, because something that size should roar. The roar came after, when it hit the lower ice and shattered, and the shockwave rolled through the fall like a drumbeat through a cathedral's bones.

Beatrix was at the base of a leaning tower when it passed. She felt the ground shudder, threw herself flat, and covered her head while the air filled with ice-dust, white and choking, erasing the world.

When it cleared, she was alive. Tomas was alive — she could see him thirty meters back, on the blue rib, waving frantically, pointing.

Pointing at the place where Davor had been.

* * *

The serac had come down directly on the route. Where Davor had stood there was now a field of broken blocks, some the size of tables, some the size of houses, jumbled at angles that would take hours to search and might shift again at any moment. No blood. No cry. Just absence.

"Davor!" Beatrix climbed onto the nearest block, boots ringing on the ice. "DAVOR!"

Nothing. Then — a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Not a voice.

Coughing.

She found him under an overhanging slab near the debris field's edge, pinned from the knees down, his pack crushed beside him, his face gray with dust and shock. Alive. Genuinely, improbably alive — the serac had split around a buried rib of older ice and spared the exact patch of ground where he'd thrown himself.

"The map," he rasped, before she'd even reached him. "Take the map. Take it and go. Please. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I—"

"Shut up." She was already digging. Hands, then axe, then hands again, levering blocks off his legs with the economy of someone who had done this before, who had done exactly this before, on another glacier, in another life. "Where's the fragment?"

His eyes went wide. He patted his jacket, and she watched the understanding arrive — watched him feel along the seam where the lining should have been, where the oilcloth packet should have been, and find nothing but a torn flap and empty cloth.

"It was—" His voice climbed toward panic. "It was in the lining, I sewed it myself, I—"

The impact. The slide. Somewhere in those seconds, the packet had torn free. Somewhere in that white avalanche of broken ice, a piece of oilcloth no bigger than a prayer book was buried under a thousand tons of fresh collapse.

Beatrix sat back on her heels and looked at the debris field, and the debris field looked back, vast and indifferent and glittering.

"No," Davor whispered. "No, no, no—"

"Your legs are broken or they're not," Beatrix said. Her voice came out flat, guide-flat, the voice she used for weather and rockfall and other facts beyond negotiation. "We're getting you out. That's the whole plan. Everything else is weather."

"We can dig—" Tomas had arrived, chest heaving, taking in the scene. "We can dig, right? We've got hours before—"

"We don't have hours." Beatrix nodded at the sun, now well past the Cathedral's western spire. "This whole section cooks every afternoon. You want to be standing in a debris field when the next tier lets go? Look at it. It's already leaning."

They dug anyway. Of course they dug. They dug for forty minutes and recovered a boot, half a rope coil, and a growing certainty. Beatrix organized it like a rescue, because organizing was the only way she knew to keep the screaming in her chest quiet — grid lines, rotation, hydration, Tomas on the shovel work because his back was younger. A proper search. The kind she used to be certified for, back when certifications meant anything.

They found the oilcloth at minute fifty-one. Torn. Empty. Whatever had been inside had shaken loose deeper in the slide, or been pulped, or simply ceased to exist as a recoverable object.

Beatrix held the scrap of cloth and felt the entire expedition tilt beneath her. Six days of travel. Two betrayals survived. A storm coming that Maren had promised would be early and vicious. And the only map to the archive, gone into the belly of a glacier that did not care about Vellmark, or justice, or any of them.

Davor was crying now, quietly, the tears cutting channels through the dust on his face. "Ilsa," he kept saying. "I have to tell Ilsa. I have to—"

"You'll tell her yourself." Beatrix hauled the last block off his shin and began splinting it with an axe shaft and the remains of his cut rope. "That's not mercy. That's logistics. I'm not carrying your confession down this icefall for you."

* * *

Getting a man with a shattered leg across an icefall is a problem with exactly one solution and no alternatives: you carry him.

Beatrix rigged a carry harness from the spare rope, the way her mentor had taught her thirty years ago — the same mentor whose notes had started all of this, whose ghost seemed to be supervising every miserable step of the journey. She took the front. Tomas took the back. Davor, semi-conscious, moaned apologies into the space between them like a man praying in a language he'd forgotten.

Halfway across, on the last snow bridge before the lateral moraine, the bridge cracked.

It was a small sound. A knuckle-crack of a sound. But Beatrix felt it through her boots and knew it instantly, the way you know a rope about to part — and she did the thing she'd sworn, twelve years ago, never to do again. She stopped. On the bad ground. With a survivor on her back.

"Hold," she said. "Weight low. Don't move."

The crack widened with a sound like tearing linen. Beneath her boots, through the snow, she could see the void — black water moving fast under thin ice, the glacier's secret plumbing.

"Beatrix." Tomas's voice was very small. "Beatrix, the bridge—"

"I know."

Twelve years ago there had been a bridge like this, and a climber named Aldric Voss hanging below it, and a choice: cut the rope and save the team, or hold and lose everything. She'd held. Everyone had said holding was wrong. The consortium had said it loudest, in tribunals and newspapers, until the word *Galloway* became shorthand for a guide who kills her clients. She'd held because going back was the only thing she knew how to do, and they had erased her for it, and she had spent twelve years being proud of her invisibility, which is another way of saying she had spent twelve years lying to herself.

The bridge groaned. Held. Groaned again.

"On my count," she said. "Three steps. Fast and flat. If it goes, I drop the harness ring and you grab Tomas. Understood?"

Neither of them answered, which meant yes.

"One. Two. Three."

Three steps. The bridge screamed, sagged to her knees, and let them go — deposited them on the moraine like something coughed up, and collapsed behind them into the grey water with a slap that echoed off the valley walls.

Beatrix lay on the rocks with Davor's weight pinning her and laughed. Once. A short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman who has just understood something about herself that she will never be able to explain to anyone.

"You okay?" Tomas crawled over, white-faced.

"No." She sat up, checked Davor's pulse, checked the splint, checked the sky. "But everyone's breathing, which is more than the mountain wanted. Get the tent up. We're not moving him today."

* * *

They made camp on the moraine with the icefall glittering behind them like a graveyard full of chandeliers. Davor drifted in and out of consciousness, morphine-less, whimpering fragments of a daughter's name — Lise, her name was Lise — and Beatrix sat outside the tent with the torn oilcloth scrap in her hand and watched the light die on the seracs.

Tomas came and sat beside her. He didn't say anything for a long time. That was one of the things she'd begun to tolerate about him: he knew when silence was the correct tool.

"We lost the map," he said finally.

"We lost the map."

"So what do we do? Ilsa can't—"

"I don't know." She turned the scrap over. Oilcloth, wax-sealed corner, nothing else. "Twelve years ago I carried a man off a glacier and it cost me everything, and I told myself I'd never do it again. Today I did it for a traitor, and it cost us the only thing we had left to trade." She looked at the boy. "Tell me why that doesn't feel like losing."

Tomas considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, chewing his lip.

"Because Corvane doesn't have it either," he said. "Nobody has it. It's just... gone. Into the ice." He shrugged. "Seems fair, kind of. The mountain took it back."

Beatrix almost smiled. Almost.

"Get some sleep. First light, we carry him down to Ilsa. And Tomas—" she paused, hearing herself, hating it, unable to stop, "—good work on the rope today. Your anchor at the bridge saved us thirty seconds. Thirty seconds was the difference."

He glowed. He tried not to, and failed, and went into the tent glowing.

Beatrix stayed out a while longer. Below, far down the valley, she could see a thread of smoke rising from the tree line — too steady, too deliberate to be a herder's fire. Riders. Corvane closing the net, exactly as Maren had warned. And somewhere between here and there, an old archivist waiting for a map that no longer existed, believing in a truth that now had no address.

Unless.

Beatrix turned the scrap over one last time. Wax seal. Marginalia. Ilsa's voice in her memory: *the drafts carry their own annotations, child, the forgers were vain, vanity leaves fingerprints.*

The map was gone. But the forgers' vanity was still folded in Beatrix's pack, in sixty pages of treaty drafts, and Ilsa Venn had spent forty years reading other people's fingerprints.

It wasn't a plan. It was a prayer with frostbite. But it was forward, and forward was the only direction Beatrix Galloway had ever been able to walk.

She banked the fire and went to sleep holding the axe.
