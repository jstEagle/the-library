## 14. Reading the Cipher by Starlight

The map fragment was gone. Davor was gone. And somewhere below them in the dark, the consortium knew exactly where they were going.

Ilsa Venn sat with the treaty drafts spread across her knees and refused to panic.

Panic was a luxury she had surrendered forty years ago, the night she'd burned her own name out of the Vellish civil rolls and become a woman with a borrowed face and a borrowed trade. Panic made people fast, and fast people were sloppy, and sloppy people died on mountains or in archives or — most often — in the small hours of the night when they convinced themselves that doing something, anything, was better than thinking.

She had spent her whole second life thinking.

Around her, the camp was coming apart quietly. Beatrix had returned from the icefall carrying Tomas over one shoulder like a sack of grain, and since then the young man had not spoken more than six words. He sat at the edge of the firelight now, his hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from, staring at the glacier as if it might apologize. Davor's rope still hung coiled by the cook fire where someone had left it. Nobody had moved it. Nobody wanted to be the one who moved it.

Petra sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Halden was asleep, or pretending to be, curled against a boulder with his coat over his head. And Beatrix stood apart, at the lip of the moraine, watching the dark valley below with the particular stillness of a woman counting enemies she couldn't see.

They had nothing left to trade. That was the truth of it. The chest's papers were copies without their key; the map fragment was buried under forty tons of collapsed serac; Davor had carried their destination down the mountain in his head and handed it to men who would burn the archive rather than let it be read.

Unless.

Ilsa turned the topmost treaty draft toward the firelight and looked again at the marginalia.

* * *

She had noticed the marks weeks ago, in her back room above the ferry, when Beatrix first laid the documents out on her table and asked, in the flat voice of someone expecting disappointment, whether any of it could be authenticated.

The drafts themselves were elegant forgeries — she'd known that within minutes, had felt the old shame rise in her throat like bile, because she recognized the hand. Not Davor's hand. An older one. A hand she had helped train, back when Vellmark still existed and the Ministry of Records still believed its own seals meant something. The forger had reproduced the treaty conventions perfectly: the chancery abbreviations, the witness formulas, even the deliberate irregularities that authenticators like Ilsa had been taught to expect in genuine documents. It was forgery built on scholarship, which was the only kind that ever worked.

But whoever had drafted these had worked quickly, and quickly meant annotations. In the margins, in a cramped cipher shorthand, were notes to self. Corrections. Cross-references. And in three places — Ilsa had marked them with slivers of charcoal that first night — references to a location.

Not an address. Nothing so crude. A notation system.

She closed her eyes and let herself go back.

The year Vellmark died, she had been thirty-one years old and the youngest senior archivist in the Ministry of Records, and she had believed — this was the wound she carried, the confession she'd offered to the fire two nights ago — that preserving documents was the same as preserving truth. When the annexation commissioners came with their requisition orders, she had catalogued. She had boxed. She had labeled. She had watched cart after cart of Vellish state paper roll south toward consortium warehouses and told herself that paper survived regimes; that someday, someone would open those boxes and know.

What she had not known — what she learned only later, too late, from a drunk clerk who wept into his beer about it — was that certain materials never went to the warehouses at all. There had been a removal order, signed by three names she still remembered, authorizing the transfer of "materials of transitional sensitivity" to a secure site in the high passes. The clerk had helped load the carts. He said the crates went up the Vetterl road and then off it, onto a track that wasn't on any survey, under escort of men who wore no insignia.

He said the crates were numbered in a system he'd never seen before or since.

Ilsa opened her eyes. Her hands were steady. She was almost proud of them.

"Tomas," she said.

The boy didn't move. Beatrix turned from the moraine first, crossing the camp in four long strides, and crouched beside Ilsa with the unhurried economy that Ilsa had come to read as the guide's whole character: everything conserved, nothing wasted, including attention.

"He's not hearing anything tonight," Beatrix said quietly.

"He doesn't have to hear. He has to hold things." Ilsa held up the treaty draft. "Your young man is a forger's son. Ask him what a forger does when she can't risk writing a place-name."

Beatrix studied her for a moment. Then, without a word, she crossed to Tomas, took the untouched cup out of his hands, replaced it with the document, and said something low that Ilsa couldn't catch. Tomas blinked at the page. His lips moved. And slowly, the dead look in his eyes gave way to something else — the thing Ilsa had seen flicker in him since the ferry, the hunger of someone raised on ink and deception confronting real work.

"He reads cipher shorthand," Tomas said at last, his voice scraped raw. "My father used it. For client instructions." He swallowed. "This isn't standard. It's Ministry shorthand — pre-annexation. My father had a reference manual. Half-burned. I copied it when I was twelve because I thought it was beautiful."

"Can you read it?" Petra asked from across the fire, knife stilled.

"Some of it." Tomas traced the margin with one finger. "Numbers are straightforward. The letter substitutions follow the old ministry phonetic key — I know maybe sixty percent of the values. But look—" he tapped a cluster of marks near the bottom edge, "—these aren't letters at all. They're crate marks. My father called them ledger-marks. Archivists used them for inventory. Each symbol is a class of material."

"And?" Beatrix said.

"And there are seven of them here, in sequence, followed by a bearing figure and a word I can't resolve." Tomas looked up, and there was something terrible and young in his face. "It's a shipping manifest. Whoever wrote this note was telling himself what he'd put in the crates and where he'd sent them."

* * *

They worked through the night.

There was no question of sleeping; the loss of Davor had seen to that, and besides, every hour the storm held off was an hour the consortium's riders gained. So they built the fire high, posted Petra at the moraine with the spyglass, and turned the loft of rock behind them into a scriptorium.

Ilsa directed. It was strange, being directed-at again after decades of being nobody's anything, but her hands remembered how to command a table of documents. She had Tomas transcribe every legible mark from all eleven drafts onto a single sheet, arranged by date. She had Beatrix — who protested that she was a mountain, not a scholar, and was ignored — recopy the bearing figures in a column. She herself took the hardest task: reconstructing the phonetic key from memory.

That was the work that cost her. Because the key lived in the Ministry of Records, and the Ministry of Records lived in her, and going into that building in her mind meant passing through every room she had left behind. The reading room with its green lamps. The smell of size and dust. Director Halven's office, where she had sat with the forged treaty spread between them while he explained, gently, why authentication required discretion, why some questions were better left unasked, why a young woman with her future should sign the certificate and go home.

She had signed. That was the fact underneath everything. Whatever she told the fire, whatever penance she performed with false names and borrowed lives, she had signed the certificate, and her signature had helped make a lie into a country's death warrant.

"Ilsa." Beatrix's voice, quiet. "You've stopped writing."

"I'm remembering," Ilsa said. "It's slower than writing."

She bent back over the sheet. The phonetic key assigned cipher values to ministry terms — *requisition*, *transfer*, *deed*, *seal* — and she reconstructed it term by term, testing each against the marginalia until the drafts began, mark by mark, to speak.

By the third hour, they had a sentence.

Tomas read it aloud in a whisper, as if the words might bruise: "*Materials of transitional sensitivity, seven classes, conveyed by the Vetterl track to the door under the ice, keyed to the surveyor's error.*"

Nobody spoke. Above them the stars wheeled, hard and cold, and the glacier groaned somewhere in its sleep — a long settling sound, like a house shifting on its foundations.

"The door under the ice," Petra repeated. "That's a place?"

"It's a phrase," Ilsa said. "Which is worse. Phrases are meant to be resolved by insiders." She pulled the bearing column toward her. "But we have bearings. Three of them, taken from three different points along the drafts' supposed journey — the forger annotated each draft with the position from which he wrote it, which was either arrogance or caution, and either way it saves us. Triangulate three bearings and you don't need a map. You need arithmetic and a fixed point."

"What's the fixed point?" Beatrix asked.

And Ilsa smiled — a thin, cold thing, but a smile. "The surveyor's error."

* * *

Here is what she told them, sitting forward with her hands wrapped around her own knees like a woman holding onto a ledge:

In the last survey of the high passes before the annexation — the great imperial survey, the one every subsequent map was built on — there was a mistake. A single triangulation station, Station Nine, had been plotted four hundred meters east of its true position. Every official map since had inherited the error, because maps are copied from maps, and no one re-surveys what has already been surveyed. Ilsa knew this because the error was famous in the Ministry — a scandal in miniature, hushed up, corrected in internal gazetteers but never in the published sheets. Anyone navigating by official chart in the high passes near Station Nine would find their positions drifting east by four hundred meters.

Anyone hiding something would hide it in that drift.

"You're saying," Beatrix said slowly, "that the archive sits in the gap between the real mountain and the mapped one."

"I'm saying the phrase *keyed to the surveyor's error* means the archive's true position only resolves if you correct the charts. To anyone following an official map, the coordinates land on impossible ground — a cliff face, a crevasse field, something that says *no one is here*. But apply the correction, shift west four hundred meters—" she tapped the bearing figures, "—and these three lines cross."

"Where do they cross?" Tomas whispered.

Ilsa did the arithmetic in her head, the way she had once done it daily across forty years of catalogues and registers, and found that her mind, at least, had never been exiled. The numbers came cleanly. Almost insultingly cleanly.

"They cross at the terminus of the glacier," she said. "Below the eastern col. Where the ice has pulled back."

Beatrix went very still. Ilsa watched the guide's face do something complicated — recognition, resistance, and beneath both, a kind of grief.

"There's a lateral moraine there," Beatrix said finally. "Old ice, retreating for decades. My mentor had notes on that ground. He always said the maps lied about the eastern approaches, and everyone laughed at him." A pause. "He was blacklisted two years before I was. They said he lost a client on the Vetterl road. They said a lot of things."

"Your mentor surveyed it," Ilsa said. It wasn't a guess. She had watched Beatrix navigate for nine days; she had seen whose habits lived in the woman's hands. "Or walked it often enough to carry it. Which means the route to the archive may exist in the one map that was never written down — yours."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, wind moved through the valley like a long exhale.

* * *

Petra came down from the moraine an hour before dawn and crouched by the ashes.

"Lanterns," she said. "Three of them. Far side of the valley floor, moving north along the river line. They're not searching the slopes. They're heading somewhere."

"The Vetterl road," Ilsa said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The track off the Vetterl road. They have the same manifest we do — Davor gave them the fragment, but they'll want readers. Men who can resolve the shorthand." She heard her own voice flatten, the way it did when the archivist took over from the woman. "We have perhaps a day. Less, if they travel light."

"Then we leave now," Beatrix said, and stood, and the camp became motion around her — bedrolls cinched, rope shouldered, the dead man's coil finally picked up, by Beatrix herself, and added to the load without ceremony.

But Tomas didn't move. He was looking at the transcription sheet, at the sentence they'd dug out of the margins, and his face had changed again — past grief into something harder.

"They'll burn it," he said. "If we're slow, if we take the safe route, if we lose a day — they'll burn it. Seven classes of materials. Original treaties. Ledgers. Names." He looked up at Beatrix, and his voice cracked but held. "Everything my father forged copies of. Everything Ilsa signed away. It's all sitting under a rock in the dark waiting for someone to carry it out or light a match."

"We know," Beatrix said.

"So we don't take the safe route." Tomas stood. His legs shook; Ilsa saw it, and saw him lock them anyway. "Whatever you were saving. Whatever route you kept in reserve because it was too dangerous even for smugglers. We take that one."

Silence, except the wind.

Then Beatrix looked at Ilsa, and Ilsa understood that she was being asked — not as the keeper of the cipher, but as the oldest person in the company, the one who had the most life left to lose and the least excuse for wasting it.

"We have nothing left to trade," Ilsa said. "No fragment. No leverage. No anonymity worth the name — Davor sold whatever was left of it. All we have is what we know and what we're willing to spend." She folded the transcription sheet with the precise movements of a woman sealing a state document, and handed it to Beatrix. "Spend me first. I'm the one who helped build the lie. I'll walk the worst route on this mountain to help bury it."

Beatrix took the sheet, weighed it in her hand as if it were rope, and nodded once.

"Eastern col," she said. "Under the glacier. We move in twenty minutes, single file, and nobody speaks above a whisper once we're on the ice." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and for just a moment the armor slipped and Ilsa saw the tired woman underneath, the one who had been running for ten years. "My mentor's notes described a gully above the moraine. He called it the throat. He also wrote that no party should attempt it loaded and late in the season."

"And?" Petra asked.

"And we're loaded, and it's late in the season," Beatrix said. "So we do it anyway. Wake Halden."

Above them the sky was turning the color of old pewter, and the wind had begun, very faintly, to carry the smell of snow.
