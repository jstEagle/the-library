## 15. The Consortium Closes the Valley

Beatrix saw the smoke first.

It rose from the valley floor in three thin columns, straight and unhurried, the way smoke rises when nobody is in a hurry to put it out. She had been watching the southern approaches since dawn, lying flat on the ridge with her elbows sunk into old snow, the spyglass pressed so hard to her eye socket that the bone ached. The columns told her everything she needed to know before the glass confirmed it.

Fires meant camps. Camps meant riders. Riders meant the consortium had stopped chasing them and started waiting for them.

"Let me see." Ilsa's voice came from somewhere near her left boot, muffled by wind and wool. The old woman had crawled up beside her on her belly like a soldier, which was absurd and also not absurd at all — Ilsa Venn had been hiding from governments longer than Beatrix had been alive, and she moved through concealment the way other people moved through their own kitchens.

Beatrix passed the glass without taking her eyes off the valley. Below them, the southern valleys unspooled in a long gray-green braid — the Threlkeld, the Marrow, the low road between them where the river ran wide and shallow and every crossing was visible from the heights. Along that road, small dark shapes were moving with purpose. Not searching. *Positioning.* She could tell the difference now after weeks of being hunted; searchers wandered, but these men walked like fence posts being driven into the ground, one after another, until the line was complete.

"They're closing it," she said. "All of it."

Ilsa lowered the glass slowly. For a moment she said nothing, and Beatrix heard only the wind coming up the glacier tongue behind them, the sound it always made at this altitude — a long exhale over stone, patient as anything that has been doing its work for ten thousand years.

"The ferry crossings," Ilsa said finally.

"Burned or held. Both, probably. Look at the bridge at Marrow Ford." Beatrix took the glass back. The bridge was still standing, which was worse than if it had burned. A burned bridge was an obstacle. A standing bridge with six horses tethered at either end and men lounging against the rails was an invitation you weren't supposed to accept. "They want us to come down. They want us to try."

Behind them, further up the shoulder of the ridge, the rest of the party waited in the lee of a granite fin — Tomas, Maren's nephew Piet who had joined them at High Camp, and what remained of their supplies wrapped in oilcloth. They had lost Davor to the icefall. They had lost the map fragment with him. They had gained nothing on paper except the certainty that Ilsa's memory was good enough to find the archive without it, and the knowledge — cold, heavy, still settling into all of them — that one of their own had sold them for consortium coin.

And now this.

Beatrix lay back from the edge and let the sky take her weight. It was the middle of the afternoon and the light had already gone flat, that particular flatness of late autumn at altitude when the sun gives up trying to warm anything and settles for illuminating the fact that everything is about to get much worse. To the north and west, over the high passes they would need to cross, the clouds were building in the wrong way — stacked, bruised, organized. Weather with intent.

She did the arithmetic because she couldn't stop herself. It was the same arithmetic she'd done at every decision point since the chest came out of the river, and every time the numbers came out worse.

South: closed. The consortium had spent two days and, by the look of those camps, forty riders sealing valleys that had been open ground since before either of their countries existed. Corvane was down there somewhere. She hadn't seen him, but she could feel him the way you feel a storm cell building past a ridgeline — pressure before evidence.

East: the lowlands, the towns, the hearings Ilsa kept talking about, the capitals where the truth might actually land. Also closed, because east meant descending through exactly the country the riders now owned.

West: the border marches, another nation's patrols, and no guarantee the people who'd erased Beatrix from their registry would treat her any better than the people hunting her.

North: the archive. And above the archive, the high passes. And above the high passes—

She looked at the clouds again. The weather no sane guide would enter.

"There's a version of this where we go down anyway," Tomas said. He had crept forward while they talked, young and quiet on his belly the way he'd learned to be in his father's workshop, forging papers under a floorboard while inspectors drank tea upstairs. "Bluff our way through. I can make papers that would pass. Papers that would pass *me*, if that makes sense."

"It makes sense," Beatrix said. "And it fails."

"How do you know?"

"Because Corvane isn't checking papers." She sat up, brushing snowmelt from her front. "He's checking faces. He's had my description since the scree fields, he's had yours since the ferry, and Ilsa's face is on a warrant older than both of us put together. We don't walk through a checkpoint, Tomas. We walk into a net."

Tomas looked at the valley for a long moment. She watched him take it in — the riders, the bridges, the tidy geometry of the trap — and she watched something happen in his face that she recognized from her own mirror, years ago. The moment when a person understands that the clever solution doesn't exist, and the only solutions left are the expensive ones.

"So we go up," he said.

"We go up."

* * *

They held council in the lee of the granite fin, huddled close enough that their breath made one shared cloud. Beatrix laid it out the way she laid out routes, plainly, without decoration, because decoration was for people who had something to sell.

"Here's what we know," she said. "The archive sits behind the glacier's retreat, north of the col, three days' hard travel if the weather holds." Nobody laughed, because there was nothing funny in it. "The weather will not hold. That—" she pointed her chin at the western sky, where the cloud deck had thickened visibly even in the last hour, "—is an early winter. Not a storm. A season arriving ahead of schedule."

"How far ahead?" Piet asked. He was a herder's boy, twenty maybe, with a herder's read of sky. He'd earned his place on the rope twice over since High Camp.

"A week early. Maybe two. Which means we have perhaps a day of margin, and then whatever falls falls on us wherever we are."

"And behind us?" Ilsa asked, though Beatrix could see she already knew.

"Behind us is Corvane, and Corvane does not care about weather. He cares about ledgers. He'll spend riders the way a miser spends nothing else — carefully, but he'll spend them. He knows we won't go south. He knows the only door left is the high route. So he'll push a picket up the lower approach and wait for the mountain to soften us, then walk up and collect what's left."

"That's assuming the mountain softens us," Tomas said.

"The mountain will try," Beatrix said. "That part isn't in question. The question is whether we're still a party when it finishes trying."

Silence, except the wind. Beatrix looked at each of them in turn. Ilsa, seventy-some years old, whose hands shook unless she was holding a document, in which case they were steady as a vise. Tomas, who had never in his life been above the tree line until this month and had climbed like he'd been born to it, which frightened Beatrix more than she let show, because people with talent and no scar tissue took risks that killed rope teams. Piet, who knew snow the way farmers know soil, and who had already said, quietly, that he'd rather die going forward than going back to a valley full of consortium riders.

Three people. One archivist, one forger's son, one herder. And her.

A sane guide would turn them around. A sane guide would find some third option — hole up, wait out the season, come down in spring when the hunt had cooled. But spring meant the documents aged, meant the consortium had months to burn archives and bury witnesses, meant Ilsa's memory had time to fail. And it meant sitting still in country where stillness was the one thing that got you found.

There was no third option. There was only the door that everyone else had the sense not to use.

"I'm going to say the thing nobody wants said," Ilsa said. "So we've said it and can move past it. If we go up into that weather, some of us may not come down. Possibly none of us. An old woman, a boy, a herder, and a blacklisted guide, walking into an early winter, pursued, undersupplied, following the memory of a map that was stolen from us by a dead traitor." She folded her hands. "I've said it. Now someone argue with me, because I would very much like to be argued with."

Nobody argued with her. Piet looked at his boots. Tomas looked at the sky. Beatrix looked at the north, where the col waited, white and indifferent.

"I'll tell you what I know instead," Beatrix said. "I know that route. Not the official route — the real one. The smugglers' corridor. It's not on any chart because charts get confiscated and the men who drew it got erased, same as me. My mentor mapped it. I climbed it four times before I was twenty-five. It's steep, it's exposed, and in fresh snow it will try to kill us every hour of every day." She paused. "But it exists. It's real. And right now it is the only piece of ground in this entire range that Corvane cannot predict, because the kind of man who closes valleys with forty riders is the kind of man who believes maps. All of them. Even the ones that lie."

"And the weather?" Ilsa asked.

"The weather is honest," Beatrix said. "That's the whole argument for it. Everything else in this story lies — treaties, registries, bounty notices, friends. The weather just tells you what it's going to do and then does it. You can negotiate with weather. You can't negotiate with Corvane."

* * *

They broke camp in the last gray light and started north along the ridge, single file, roped at thirty feet because the wind had begun to gust hard enough to matter. Beatrix led. Behind her came Piet, then Ilsa, then Tomas on anchor, and the little column moved up the shoulder of the world like a stitch pulled through heavy cloth.

Below and behind, the fires of the southern camps glowed through the dusk, and Beatrix made herself look at them once, properly, the way you look at a debt you intend never to pay. Forty riders. Warm tents. Hot food. Every advantage that legitimacy could buy — because that was the joke at the bottom of everything, wasn't it? The consortium's money was built on forged paper, their authority on forged treaties, and yet they rode with the weight of two nations behind them while the people carrying the *proof* of the forgery climbed away into the dark with half a sack of flour and a prayer shaped like a route plan.

Legitimacy, she thought, was just a forgery that had won.

The thought should have made her angry. It made her calm instead, and the calm frightened her a little, because she knew what it was. It was the calm she'd worn since the glacier, since the rescue that went wrong, since two registries struck her name and she'd learned that invisibility was a skill you could practice until it became a personality. Terror, transmuted. Fear with nowhere to go, beaten into something flat and functional and wearable, like armor hammered from the shield you broke.

She had worn it for eleven years. She knew its weight better than her own heartbeat.

But somewhere between the river and the ridge, the armor had changed. It had grown hinges. Because for eleven years the calm had been *for her alone* — calm so she could vanish, calm so she could survive, calm in service of nothing. And now it was for the three people breathing hard behind her on the rope, and for a country that no longer existed, and for a truth sealed under a glacier that deserved daylight. Armor you wear for yourself is just a shell. Armor you wear for others is a promise.

"Guide." Piet's voice, tight. "Look west."

She looked. The cloud deck had come down off the peaks while they climbed — not drifting in but *arriving*, the way a verdict arrives, whole and final. The first flakes came with it, sparse and dry, ticking against her hood like fingers counting.

"Snow?" Tomas called up the rope.

"Not yet," Beatrix said. "That's the advance guard. The main body comes tonight or tomorrow morning." She turned north again, toward the col, toward the corridor her mentor had drawn in a dead man's hand on a chart that officially had never existed. "Which means we climb through the night and reach the entrance to the corridor before dawn, and we do not stop, and we do not discuss it, because stopping is how parties die and discussing is how parties decide to stop."

"You have a gift for morale," Ilsa said dryly, and Beatrix heard the smile in it, and answered it with the ghost of her own.

They climbed. The snow thickened by degrees, from ticks to whispers to a steady dry hiss, and the world narrowed to the circle of Beatrix's headlamp and the rhythm of the rope. Somewhere below, Corvane's pickets were lighting their cook fires in the mouths of the valleys, certain that the mountain would do their work for them, certain that no guide alive would take four people up into the teeth of an early winter.

Beatrix smiled into the snow, and it was not a nice smile.

No sane guide would. That was true.

But the registry had struck her name, and the charts had struck her route, and the world had spent eleven years teaching her exactly one lesson: that the erased see doors the registered never learn to find.

Ahead, invisible in the dark and the falling snow, the entrance to the corridor waited — and Beatrix Galloway, who no longer officially existed, began the climb toward it.
