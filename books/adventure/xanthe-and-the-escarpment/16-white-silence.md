## 16. White Silence

The barometer had been falling for two days, and by the time we crossed the shoulder below the Widow's Tooth it fell so fast that Beatrix stopped checking it. She just looked at the sky the way a doctor looks at a wound that's gone bad.

"Storm tonight," she said. "A real one."

Ilsa squinted up at the clouds stacking themselves over the western peaks like grey plates on a shelf. "How long?"

"Until it's done." Beatrix was already moving. "We need walls before dark. Not shelter — walls. There's a difference."

There was a difference. I'd learned that much in nine days on the rope with her. Shelter was what you found when you were lucky. Walls were what you built when you weren't.

* * *

She found us a drift bank against the lee side of a granite rib, thirty feet of wind-packed snow that the storms of ten winters had welded into something close to stone. We dug into it with shovels and ice axes and our hands, four people who hadn't slept properly in three days, and the storm arrived while we were still excavating, arriving not as weather but as a fact — a white wall that erased the mountain, the sky, the other members of your own rope team if they strayed more than an arm's length away.

Beatrix ran rope between the digging site and the cave mouth before the whiteout closed. "Nobody moves without the line," she shouted over the wind. "Nobody. I don't care if you can see your own boots. You can't."

Davor would have argued. Davor wasn't there anymore. That arithmetic sat in all of us, unspoken: three exiles had started from the ferry, and two walked now, and the map fragment was somewhere under the icefall in a dead man's pocket, and the only reason any of us were alive to grieve it was the woman shouting rope discipline into a hurricane.

I held the line while Ilsa crawled inside first. Then me. Then Beatrix, last, coiling the rope with hands that didn't shake even though mine did.

The cave was maybe eight feet deep, a dome we'd hollowed out of the drift, floored with packs and rope coils and everything spare we owned. Beatrix punched a ventilation hole through the roof with her axe shaft, checked it twice, then checked it again. "That hole is your life," she told us. "If it clogs, you clear it. Even half asleep. Especially half asleep."

Then there was nothing to do but wait out the night, and the storm, and ourselves.

* * *

It was Ilsa who slept first, which surprised me. She was sixty-something and built out of paper and stubbornness, and she'd been reconstructing the archive route from memory every evening by firelight, muttering marginalia like prayers. But she curled against her pack and was gone in minutes, breathing thin and even, a woman who had learned decades ago how to sleep anywhere because home was never safe.

Which left me and Beatrix and the wind.

The wind was the whole world. It came through the snow around us as a sound more felt than heard, a pressure, a vast animal breathing. Snow sifted down through the vent hole in fine grains that caught what little light our single candle threw. The candle flame leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered, like it was practicing for something.

I couldn't stop my hands. I'd been carrying them clenched since the icefall, and now, in the quiet — the relative quiet, the muffled roaring quiet — they started going. Shaking. I watched them like they belonged to someone else.

"You should sleep," Beatrix said.

"I'm fine."

"You're not." She said it without judgment, the way you'd note the weather. "Your hands are shaking and you keep looking at the entrance."

"I keep thinking about Davor." My voice came out wrong, too high. "Not even — I keep thinking about the sound. When the serac went. And then the quiet after. And I keep thinking that he was reaching for the map, and I was reaching for him, and I chose—"

"You chose right."

"I chose the map!" It burst out of me, ugly and loud, and Ilsa stirred but didn't wake. "No. That's not — I reached for him, I swear I did, but my body — some part of me grabbed the strap of his pack instead of his wrist, because the pack had the fragment in it, and I've been telling myself I don't know if that's true but I know it's true, Beatrix. Some part of me did the math. He was already gone and the papers weren't, and I did the math, and I hate—" My breath hitched. "I hate that I'm good at math like that."

Beatrix didn't answer right away. She fed the candle a little wax, adjusted the flame. Outside, the storm threw itself against our eight feet of snow like something enormous trying to get in, or maybe trying to tell us something.

"When I was seventeen," she said finally, "I worked a glacier rescue. Up on the Havel Spire. You'll have heard the story — everyone's heard the story. The one where the guide froze and six people died."

"The blacklist story."

"That's the one." Her face in the candlelight was very still. "Except the story everyone heard isn't the true one, and I've never told anyone the true one, and I think maybe tonight is the night, because you're doing exactly what I did for twenty years, which is punishing yourself for being competent in a place where competence looks like murder."

I didn't say anything. I don't think I could have.

"There were seven of us on the ice," she said. "Two guides — me and my mentor, Aldric Voss. Five clients. A crevasse bridge collapsed at midday. Three clients went down. Voss went down after them on a running belay, solo, because there wasn't time to build an anchor and he knew it and he went anyway." She paused. "He got two of them clipped to him. The third was too far. He was hauling them up when the anchor — a snow bollard I'd cut — failed."

"You cut the anchor."

"I cut the anchor. Seventeen years old, first season, and my mentor trusted me with the anchor, and it failed, and five people died including him." The candle flame leaned; she watched it. "That's the story. Here's the true one. The bollard didn't fail because I cut it wrong. I cut it perfectly. I've re-cut that anchor in my head ten thousand times and it was perfect. It failed because the temperature dropped twelve degrees in an hour and the snow bridge sublimated from underneath — a hollowing nobody could see, nobody could predict, a thing the mountain did. The inquiry knew that. The consortium's engineers knew that. They wrote reports saying it. And then the consortium needed someone to blame, because their own client vetting had put five underprepared tourists on a glaciated peak in a warming week, and blaming the mountain doesn't sell insurance or protect contracts. So they blamed the seventeen-year-old and the dead man who couldn't defend himself, and they struck us both from the registries, and twenty years later here I am, digging holes in the snow for smugglers' cargo."

The wind howled. I realized my hands had stopped shaking.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because on the icefall, you made a choice in half a second, and the choice was correct, and you're going to spend the rest of your life deciding whether to be destroyed by that or to understand it." She looked at me straight on. "The math you did — the pack versus the wrist — that's not cruelty. That's training. Every hour I've spent teaching you rope work, every drill, every 'again, faster' — I built that reflex into you on purpose, because on a mountain, the person who hesitates to do the math kills the whole team. You did the thing I trained you to do. What you do with that knowledge afterward — whether you let it make you careful or make you cruel — that's the only part that's yours."

"And which are you?"

She almost smiled. Almost. "Still deciding. Ask me in spring."

* * *

Ilsa woke near midnight, or what we guessed was midnight, and took her turn clearing the vent hole, and then it was my turn, and then Beatrix's, and the storm went on and on like it had forgotten anything existed besides itself. At some point Ilsa, wrapped in her blanket, said quietly into the dark:

"In Vellmark we had a word for nights like this. *Hvidstille.* White silence. The old archives used it to describe gaps in the record — periods where nothing was written down because no one could travel, no one could speak, the country simply held its breath." A dry rustle that might have been a laugh. "I spent forty years terrified of the gaps. Turns out they're where you survive."

"Turns out," Beatrix agreed.

And I lay there between them, in a hole dug out of a mountain by four strangers who weren't strangers anymore, listening to the storm try and fail to erase us, and understood that this — the rope, the vent hole, the terrible honest stories told by candlelight — this was the archive too. The one nobody could burn.

* * *

Near dawn the wind changed pitch. Lower. Tired. Beatrix crawled to the entrance, chipped away the ice glaze, and put her head out into the grey.

"It's easing," she called back. "Six hours, maybe less. We move when it stops — the fresh snow will be unstable, so we go roped, tight spacing, and we go *now*, before the next system stacks up behind this one." She turned back to us, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before. Not hope exactly. Direction.

"Because Ilsa's memory says the archive mouth is one valley over," she said, "and Voss's old notes say the same thing, and I just realized — I've been carrying both maps my whole life without knowing it. His notes and her notation. The erased guide and the exiled archivist." She shouldered her pack. "The storm gave us a day. The consortium won't give us two. So we walk."

Ilsa was already folding her blanket with archival precision. I checked the rope, checked my hands — steady, steady enough — and followed them out into the white silence, toward the door under the glacier.
