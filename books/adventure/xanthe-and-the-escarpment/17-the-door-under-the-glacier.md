## 17. The Door Under the Glacier

The storm spent itself sometime before dawn, and what it left behind was a world remade in white.

Beatrix crawled out of the snow cave first, the way she always did, and stood up into a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving. The sky was that hard, rinsed blue that comes after three days of wind — no cloud anywhere from horizon to horizon, and the sun already climbing, throwing light off every surface like the whole range had been dipped in glass. Somewhere below them, the icefall they'd crossed two days ago would be unrecognizable now. Somewhere behind them, Corvane's riders were either buried or digging out, and Beatrix found she didn't much care which.

"Beatrix." Tomas's voice, muffled through the cave mouth. "Ilsa's asking if we move today."

"We move today."

They were six now, and they looked it. Davor gone into the ice with the map fragment. Maren's son's old coat on Ilsa's shoulders because the archivist had left hers frozen solid against a rock at the col and hadn't complained once, which Beatrix had come to understand was simply how Ilsa Venn did everything. The other exiles — Petra and the brothers Anselm and Rurik — hollow-eyed, frost-nipped, but upright. And Tomas, who had stopped talking about legends somewhere around the second day of the whiteout and had started, quietly, doing the work of a mountaineer instead of an admirer. That was better. Beatrix trusted people more when they stopped being impressed by her.

She got them moving by first light proper, roped in pairs across the glacier's upper tongue, following a line only she could see — the shallow compression ridges under the fresh snow, the places where the ice groaned differently underfoot. Her mentor had taught her to read a glacier the way a fisherman reads water: not the surface, but the tensions underneath. Halvard Voss. Twenty years dead, disgraced for the same rescue that had ruined her, his name struck from the same registers. She carried his notes in a waxed packet against her chest, next to the treaty drafts, and had read them so many times in the last weeks that she could recite passages whole.

*The old door lies where the ice gives back what it took,* he had written, in the cramped hand of a man writing fast and hiding the page often. *Glaciers retreat. Stone does not. When the snout pulls back past the moraine of '61, look for the twin boulders leaning like conspirators, and the seam between them.*

Everyone had assumed he meant some smuggler's cache. A weapons store, maybe. Coin. Beatrix had assumed it too, right up until the night she'd sat in Ilsa's back room with the cipher-keyed map fragment glowing under lamplight and realized the notation matched Voss's marginalia exactly — the same reference marks, the same obsessive little crosses. Whatever was under that glacier, Voss had known about it. And Voss had died poor, blacklisted, and silent about it, which told Beatrix more than any document could have.

The morning was brutal and beautiful. They climbed the glacier's spine as the sun warmed the surface into a thin squeaking crust, then crossed onto the lateral moraine where the going was honest — rock and rubble, no hidden crevasses, just the endless labor of putting one boot above the other. By midday they could see the snout of the ice ahead, a gray-blue wall forty feet high, weeping meltwater down its face in a hundred bright threads.

And beyond it, where the ice ended, a valley of bare stone that no map showed.

"There," Ilsa said.

Beatrix had already seen it. Twin boulders, each the size of a cottage, leaning toward one another at the top like two old men sharing a secret. Exactly as Voss had drawn them, sixty years ago, when the ice had reached nearly to their bases. Now the glacier had pulled back half a mile, and the boulders stood alone in a amphitheater of raw stone, exposed to the light for the first time in who knew how long.

Nobody spoke as they came down off the moraine. Even the wind seemed to hold off. Beatrix unroped at the base of the boulders and stood between them, looking at the seam.

It was there. A vertical crack in the living rock, maybe four feet wide, running up into shadow between the leaning stones. It had been packed with debris — old scree, gravel washed down by centuries of melt — but at the bottom, where the glacier's runoff had scoured clean, something else showed. Cut stone. Dressed blocks, fitted tight, with a channel carved down their center where a door had once seated.

"It's real," Rurik said, and his voice cracked on it. He was the oldest of the brothers, a quarryman before the annexation, and he put his palm flat against the dressed stone the way a man touches a grave. "This is Vellish work. I know this facing. This is cut the old way, the way my grandfather cut."

"The archive," Ilsa whispered. She had taken off her glove without seeming to notice she'd done it, and her bare hand hovered over the stone without touching, as if the rock might be hot, or holy. "Sixty years I've been telling myself it was destroyed. Burned with the rest of it, scattered, sold off piece by piece to private collectors who'd lock it away forever. And it's been here. Under the ice. The whole time."

"They didn't destroy it," Beatrix said quietly. "Somebody hid it. Somebody got it out before the consortium came, hauled it up here, sealed it behind a door, and let the glacier keep the secret." She thought of Voss again — young Voss, before the disgrace, before whatever had broken him. *A guide who knew the high passes.* "Maybe somebody like us."

Petra had the tools — they'd carried crowbars and a small forge-hammer since the ferry, on Ilsa's insistence, though at the time nobody had known why. Now it was obvious. The door's channel was packed with ice and grit, and the seal, whatever mechanism the old builders had used, had long since surrendered to frost. It took them two hours of prying, hammering, and hauling broken stone, working in shifts while the others kept watch on the valley mouth, and it was Tomas who found the trick of it — a counterweight slot behind the left-hand block, choked with gravel, which Rurik cleared with his hands until the whole left leaf of the door groaned and gave six inches, then a foot, then swung inward on hinges that shrieked like something being murdered.

Cold air breathed out of the dark. Old air, dry as paper, smelling of stone dust and lamp oil and something faintly sweet — wax, maybe, sealing wax, decades of it.

Nobody moved.

"Well," Beatrix said. "We didn't cross the Escarpment in a blizzard to stand in the doorway."

She went in first. Of course she did. That was the job.

* * *

Inside, the passage ran straight back into the mountain for perhaps thirty paces, and then opened.

Tomas lit the lanterns — they had three, hoarded since High Camp — and hung them on iron brackets that someone had thoughtfully set into the walls a century or more ago, and the light climbed the room and found the shelves.

Shelves. Floor to ceiling, cut into the rock itself, running away into wings the lanterns couldn't reach. And on them, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas and stacked in careful, numbered rows: documents. Ledgers with brass clasps. Scroll cases of cedar. Boxes marked in Vellish script that Ilsa read aloud in a shaking voice — *Treaties, 1802–1819. Correspondence, Ministry of Exterior. Ledger of Disbursements, Confidential.* Maps. Seals. The physical memory of a country, packed away by hands that had loved it enough to carry it up a glacier.

"Oh," Ilsa said. Just that. She walked forward slowly, both hands out now, touching nothing, and Beatrix watched sixty years of careful disguise fall off the old woman's face all at once, like snow sliding from a roof. What was underneath was just grief. Clean, simple grief, and underneath the grief, something that looked unbearably like hope.

Rurik sank down onto the stone floor right where he stood, a quarryman kneeling in a quarry, and began to laugh — or cry; from the sound, it was both. His brother put a hand on his shoulder and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything to say.

Beatrix stood near the entrance and let them have it.

This was the part she hadn't expected — that finding the thing would feel like this. Not triumph. Not even relief, exactly. More like standing in a room where a conversation had been interrupted mid-sentence sixty years ago, and everyone who'd been speaking was dead, and the words were still hanging in the air waiting for someone to finish them. Voss had known. Whoever had packed these shelves had known. They had done the impossible thing — gotten the truth out, gotten it up the mountain, gotten it behind stone and ice — and then they had died, or scattered, or been silenced, and the truth had sat here in the dark doing nothing at all.

Waiting. That was the word. The whole place felt like an open hand, held out across sixty years.

*Take it,* the hand said. *Finish it.*

"Beatrix." Ilsa's voice, from deep in the left wing. "Come see this."

She followed the lantern light. Ilsa stood before an open case, and inside it, under a layer of oiled silk, lay a ledger bound in green leather, its spine stamped with the Vellmark crest — the same crest that had stared up at Beatrix from the flood-borne chest a season and a lifetime ago. Beside it, a bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone brittle as straw.

Ilsa lifted the top letter with the care of a woman handling a sleeping child. Read the first lines. Then read them again.

"This is signed by Aldous Renner-Coyne," she said. "The founder. The man whose grandson chairs the consortium today." Her finger moved down the page. "He's instructing the treaty commission on which clauses to alter. In his own hand. Before the treaties were ever presented as authentic." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and terrible. "Beatrix. It's not evidence that the forgery happened. It's the forgery happening. Draft by draft, instruction by instruction. Sixty years of it, in order."

"And the names?"

"Every name." Isla turned pages with increasing speed. "The officials who took the bribes. The ministers who signed. The border commissioners. The ones who buried the inquiry afterward." She stopped. Went still. "And here. The inquiry itself. Who was blamed for the failure of the investigation." She read the name aloud, and it was not Beatrix's, and it was not Voss's, and somehow that was worse — because it meant the machine that had crushed them was the same machine, running the same play, on whoever stood in its way, generation after generation.

Beatrix looked at the shelves. Hundreds of cases. Thousands of pages. Enough truth to burn the consortium down to its foundations in every capital that had ever recognized the annexation — if it could be gotten out, copied, published, spread faster than Corvane's riders and the consortium's lawyers and every official whose name was written in that green ledger could smother it.

That was the next problem, and it was enormous, and for one long moment Beatrix let herself not think about it. She just stood in the cold dry dark, in a room no living person had entered in sixty years, and felt the strangest thing settle over her: not peace, exactly. Recognition. She had spent her whole adult life learning routes that weren't on maps, keeping skills that had no legitimate use, being competent in the cracks of the world. And all along, up here above the ice, the world's cracks had a room like this in them. Full of other people's erased work. Other people's blacklisted truths.

The illegitimate had been keeping the records all along.

"Tomas," she said. "How many blank notebooks do we have left?"

"Four. And pencil stubs enough for maybe two of them."

"Then we start copying tonight. Everything Ilsa flags. Treaties first, then the Renner-Coyne letters, then the ledger names. We don't take the originals out — too heavy, too fragile, and if we're caught on the descent, the originals die with us and the truth dies too. Copies travel light." She looked around at their faces, six exhausted people in a hole in a mountain, and heard her own voice go flat and certain, the guide's voice, the one that didn't ask. "We work until the light fails, sleep here where it's warm, and move at dawn. Down the north side, the route I showed you at the col. Four days to the low town if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?" Petra asked.

"Then five days," Beatrix said, "and a worse story to tell."

They laughed — actually laughed, all of them, the ragged laughter of people too tired to be afraid — and set to work. Lanterns were distributed. Ilsa moved down the shelves calling out designations like a woman reading a roll of honor, and Tomas wrote, and Rurik's brother sharpened pencils with a knife, and the scratch of graphite on paper became the only sound in the mountain.

Beatrix took the last shift of the night, copying alone by one lantern while the others slept among the shelves like refugees in a temple. Near dawn, her pencil paused over a name in the disbursement ledger — a payment authorized, dated thirty years ago, to a mountain guide of the Karst district, for services rendered in surveying the high passes. The amount was modest. The signature was Renner-Coyne's.

The guide's name was H. Voss.

She sat very still for a long time, the lantern flame steady in the dead air. So he'd been paid. Once. Early — before the annexation was finished, before he understood what he'd helped map. And everything after — the silence, the disgrace, the notes hidden in a waxed packet, the obsession with a door under a glacier — everything after was a man spending the rest of his life trying to give the money back.

"You knew," she said softly, to the dark, to him. "You knew, and you couldn't say it, so you drew me a map instead."

The lantern flickered. The mountain said nothing, the way mountains do.

When the others woke, Beatrix had the copies bundled, the originals reshelved, the door propped open to the blue morning light — and she was standing outside between the twin boulders, looking south along the glacier's course, toward the low valleys, toward the towns, toward the hearings that didn't exist yet and had to.

Which was when Tomas, scrambling up the moraine from the watch post, stopped dead and pointed.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, signal-smoke, rising from the valley mouth to the south. One column, then a second, answering it from farther off.

Corvane hadn't given up. Corvane had found the valley.

"Get Ilsa," Beatrix said. "Tell her we leave in ten minutes, and tell her to bring every copy we made. We're done being invisible — but we're not done yet."
