## 18. The Vellmark Archive

The door was not a door until Beatrix put her shoulder to it.

Ilsa had imagined it for forty years — the archive of Vellmark, the vault her professors whispered about, the room where her country kept its conscience. In her imagination it opened easily, grandly, the way truth was supposed to. In fact it opened with a groan of stone grinding on stone and a breath of air so cold it seemed to come from another century, and then they were through, five exhausted people staggering into darkness that smelled of ice and paper and time.

Tomas found the lanterns first. There were racks of them by the entrance, wicks trimmed, oil still good after all these years — Vellish thoroughness, Ilsa thought, feeling something crack open in her chest. Even in defeat, even in hiding, someone had come down here and trimmed the wicks so that whoever came after would be able to see.

"Light," she said, and her voice came out strange, thin.

Beatrix struck the flint. The first lantern caught, then the second, then two more, and the archive assembled itself out of the dark the way a photograph develops: shelves cut into the living rock, row upon row of them, rising beyond the reach of the light; document cases of oiled leather stacked like bricks; a long reading table with eight chairs pushed neatly beneath it, as if the archivists had only stepped out for their supper and might return at any moment.

Nobody spoke. Davor's absence stood among them like a sixth chair.

It was Beatrix who moved first, because Beatrix always moved first when standing still became unbearable. She walked to the nearest case, lifted the lid, and drew out a bundle wrapped in waxed linen. She set it on the table and looked at Ilsa.

"You're the one who can read this," she said. "So read it."

Ilsa's hands were shaking. She told herself it was the cold. She untied the linen with fingers that would not quite obey her, and inside were treaties — the treaties, the originals, the ones every schoolchild in three nations had been taught to revere as the legal bedrock of the annexation. *The Concordat of Meridian Fields.* *The Act of Accession.* She had spent her whole adult life studying copies of these documents. She had authenticated reproductions of them. She had, God forgive her, helped authenticate one of them.

She unrolled the Act of Accession across the table and the lantern light fell on it and she knew within ten seconds.

"This is real," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "This is the original. The seal matrix marks are right. The paper is right. The ink has oxidized exactly as it should." She looked up at Beatrix. "Do you understand what this means? Every copy in every chancellery in the world is a copy of *this*. And this says—"

She stopped. Because she had known. Of course she had known since the ferry, since the cipher map, since the drafts in the chest. Knowing a thing in the abstract and seeing it in the original are different animals entirely. One is an argument. The other is a wound.

*The Act of Accession*, in the hand of the Vellmark chancellery's own second secretary, bore a marginal note in the chancellor's own ink: *Signed under duress. Signatories held at Kesselhof. No lawful quorum. This instrument is void.*

"They wrote it down," Tomas said behind her. "They just — wrote it down."

"They wrote everything down," Ilsa said. "That's what archivists do. That's what we were *for*." She laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "We thought we were preserving a nation. We were building the evidence locker."

She worked through the night. They all did, in shifts, though Ilsa barely registered the others anymore. Case after case. The Concordat of Meridian Fields, genuine, annotated, void. The private correspondence of the annexation commissioners, discussing payments before the signatures were dry. Ledgers — ledgers upon ledgers — in a merchant's cramped cipher that Ilsa could read because she had spent three years of her exile teaching herself dead mercantile hands to keep from going mad in a border town where no one knew her name.

The ledgers had names. That was the thing about ledgers; they were honest in a way people never were. Sums received. Sums disbursed. Purpose of payment, entered without embarrassment, because no one had ever expected these books to be read.

*To Deputy Minister Halloway — 4,000 crowns — consideration rendered, treaty matter concluded.*

Halloway. The man who had signed Beatrix Brightwater off the registries of two nations. Whose signature sat on the blacklist notice Ilsa had seen once, years ago, folded in a smuggler's papers, and had thought nothing of at the time.

She set that ledger down and looked across the table at Beatrix, who was sitting very still with her hands flat on the wood, watching her.

"It's him," Ilsa said. "Your blacklist. It's in here. Page forty-one. He was paid to bury the Kesselhof inquiry, and you—" she stopped, recalculated, started again. "You weren't erased because your rescue failed. You were erased because your rescue *succeeded*, and the man who survived it was carrying proof of what happened at Kesselhof. You've been paying for other people's crime for eleven years."

Beatrix didn't say anything. Ilsa had learned, over the passes, to read her silences the way she read manuscripts — for pressure, for erasure, for what had been scraped away and written over. This silence said: *I suspected. Suspecting is not knowing. Thank you and damn you.*

Ilsa turned back to the ledgers because turning back to the ledgers was easier than looking at Beatrix Brightwater learning the name of the man who had stolen her life, and finding that his price had been four thousand crowns. Less than a good horse and a season's wages. A life, wholesale.

* * *

Near dawn, or what Ilsa's body insisted was dawn though no dawn had ever reached this far under the glacier, they found the founders' file.

It was Maret who pulled it — Maret the ferryman's daughter, who had said perhaps forty words in the last six days and who now read aloud in a clear, flat voice while the others went quiet around her. The consortium had not been born in smoke-filled rooms, whatever the pamphlets claimed. It had been born in this archive's own shadow, in the final weeks before the annexation, when nine men — merchants, magistrates, one bishop — had met to divide the assets of a country that had not yet finished dying. Their names were all there. Their shares were all there. The founding charter named the purpose plainly: *to administer, in perpetuity, the territories and revenues formerly of the Kingdom of Vellmark, and to preserve the legality of their acquisition.*

*Preserve the legality.* Ilsa read those words four times. Not *establish* it. Preserve it. They had known, from the first hour, that the legality was a garment that needed mending forever, and they had built an institution whose true business was the endless repair of a lie.

"They're all dead," Tomas said. "The founders. Aren't they?"

"The institution isn't," Ilsa said. "Institutions don't die of old age. They die of exposure." She tapped the charter. "Every seat on the consortium's ruling council traces to one of these nine names. Every one. Corvane himself holds the share of the man called the Ferryman — look, here, the ninth signature. It's been passed down like a title."

"So we have them," Maret said. "We have everything. Why does it feel like this?"

Because, Ilsa wanted to say, because vindication is not what the songs promise. Because she had carried this moment through forty years of false names and rented rooms and had imagined it as warmth, as sunlight, as the weight lifting. Instead it was cold and heavy, like a stone recovered from deep water — valuable, undeniable, and freezing to hold. The truth did not lift anything. It simply lay there, in its thousands of pages, asking to be carried the rest of the way.

What she said aloud was: "Because we're tired. Copy nothing yet. Rest. We'll need steady hands for the copying, and we leave nothing original behind if we can help it — but we can't carry it all, so the choosing matters more than the taking."

Beatrix nodded slowly. "How long to choose?"

"A day. Two if the weather gives us it."

"Weather won't," Beatrix said. "Take one."

* * *

They slept in the archive itself, among the shelves, wrapped in everything warm they owned. Ilsa lay awake longer than the others, listening to the glacier tick and groan overhead like an old house settling, and got up twice to walk the rows with a lantern, touching the spines of cases she would never have time to read. Whole histories in there. Poetry, probably. Tax rolls. Love letters between chancellery clerks. A country's entire interior life, filed and shelved by people who had believed someone would come.

Someone had come. Forty years late, half-frozen, hunted, reduced to five people and three working lanterns. But someone had come.

On her second circuit she found Beatrix awake too, sitting at the reading table with page forty-one of the bribery ledger open under the lantern, staring at the name Halloway the way a person stares at a scar they'd forgotten the origin of.

"You should sleep," Ilsa said.

"So should you."

"I'm old. Old people sleep badly and apologize for it." Ilsa sat down across from her. For a while neither spoke. Then Ilsa said, quietly: "When we get down, they'll ask you to testify. Both capitals, maybe three. hearings, tribunals, men in good coats asking you to say your name into a record."

"I know."

"And you'll do it. You'll trade the invisibility you bled eleven years to build." Ilsa leaned forward. "I want you to hear from someone who's lived under a false name for four decades: it will cost you more than you think, and it will be worth less than you hope, and you should do it anyway. That's not a contradiction. That's just the arithmetic of truth."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. "You sound like you've given this speech before."

"To myself. Many times. It never took." Ilsa smiled faintly. "Maybe it takes better spoken to a guide."

Outside — above them, rather, through a hundred fathoms of ice and stone — the wind had changed. Ilsa heard it before Beatrix did, or perhaps Beatrix heard it and simply went still in the particular way she went still, the stillness Ilsa had learned meant calculation. The glacier's ticking changed pitch. Somewhere far off, snow moved against snow.

Beatrix rose, crossed to the tunnel mouth, and stood listening with her head tilted, a woman reading weather through solid rock.

"That's not storm wind," she said at last. "Storms moan. That's a whistle. That's wind coming *through* something." She turned back, and the lantern light caught her face, and Ilsa saw the calm settle over it like armor being buckled on. "There's another way into this valley. Someone's using it. Get everyone up."

Ilsa closed the ledger gently, on page forty-one, on the name Halloway, on four thousand crowns, and reached for the lantern.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"If it's who I think it is," Beatrix said, "less than an hour."
