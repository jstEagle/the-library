## 19. Riders at the Moraine

Beatrix felt them before she saw them—the way you feel a change in the mountain's weight, a pressure behind the ears that has nothing to do with altitude. She stopped at the lip of the moraine and held up a fist, and behind her the column went quiet in a way that would have been impossible a month ago. They were a rope team now. Strangers no longer, but not yet friends. Something better than either: people who had learned to trust each other's hands.

"There," Tomas breathed.

Riders. Eight of them, maybe nine, spread across the glacier tongue below the moraine like stones someone had thrown and let lie where they fell. Dark against the blue ice. Horses blowing steam. And at their center, apart from the others, a single figure on foot, walking up the moraine's spine with the unhurried gait of a man arriving at an appointment he'd scheduled himself.

Corvane.

Beatrix had never seen him. She knew him the way you know a storm by its name—reports from Maren, whispers from Ilsa's contacts in the low towns, the shape of his methods inferred from what he left behind: a burned cache, a doubled bounty, Davor's body carried off the icefall by men who hadn't wanted to look at it too long. She'd built him out of evidence, the way she built any route she hadn't climbed. Now the route was here, and it looked nothing like the map and everything like itself.

He was older than she'd imagined. Grey at the temples, a lean face weathered into something almost kind, the way certain cliffs are almost gentle right up until they shed ten tons of rock onto your head. He wore no consortium colors. He didn't need to. The eight riders behind him wore enough of it for everyone.

"Keep moving," Beatrix said quietly. "Slow. Toward the col."

"The passes are closed," Ilsa said. "You said so yourself this morning."

"I know what I said." Beatrix watched Corvane come. "I'm checking whether he knows it."

The wind came down off the high snowfields and flattened the grass between the moraine boulders, carrying the smell of ice and horse. Behind her, the exiles shifted—Tomas with his hand near the rope coil, Ilsa clutching the document satchel against her chest like a child, old Vessik leaning on his staff and breathing hard from the morning's climb. They'd come out of the archive two days ago with the truth of a murdered country in their packs, and every step since had been a race against winter and against the riders they'd known were coming.

Now winter had won the race. And the riders had too.

Corvane stopped twenty paces away. Close enough to talk without shouting. Far enough that neither of them could pretend this was anything but what it was.

"Beatrix Brightwater," he said. His voice was mild. That was the worst part. She'd expected iron or venom, and instead she got the tone of a man discussing freight rates. "I've read your file. Both registries. It's thinner than you'd think, for someone who's caused me this much trouble."

"Your file's thicker," Beatrix said. "I've only read part of it. The bribes. The ledgers. The names of the men who signed my blacklist as a favor to your founders." She let that sit. "It's all in the satchel. Copies went out from High Camp with Maren's nephew three days ago. You can do the arithmetic on whether killing us helps you."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—not doubt exactly, but the flicker of a man recalculating. Good. Let him recalculate. Every second he spent doing it was a second the truth traveled further down the valley.

"You're bluffing," he said.

"I'm a guide," Beatrix said. "Bluffing is for card tables. On a mountain you either read the terrain honestly or you die believing your own lie. So believe me when I tell you what the terrain says." She turned, slowly, deliberately, and pointed—not at the col, not at the southern valleys, but at the sky.

The clouds had been building since dawn, stacked over the western peaks like slate waiting for the hammer. The barometer in her pack had been falling for thirty hours. The snows weren't coming. The snows were here; they just hadn't started falling yet.

"That front arrives tonight," she said. "Full storm, three days minimum, maybe four. Your horses won't survive it on open ice. Your men won't either, if they're honest about their supplies—and I watched them come up the tongue, and they're carrying what they carried out of the valley, which wasn't enough then." She turned back to face him. "So here's where we actually stand, Corvane. Not where you planned to stand. Where the mountain has put both of us."

* * *

She'd learned bargaining on the Escarpment, and the first lesson was that you never bargain from where you wish you stood. You bargain from the truth. Her mentor—Halvard, dead eleven years now, disgraced beside her—used to say it with a rope in his hands: *The mountain doesn't care what you promised anyone. Tell the truth about the terrain or the terrain will tell it for you.*

So she told the truth.

"Option one," she said. "You take your men down the southern valley trail today, fast, ahead of the front. You lose nothing but the chase. We go north over the shoulder—it's a bad route, worse in weather, and some of us may not make it, but that's our affair, not yours. The documents reach the capitals regardless, because copies already have. You go home, you report failure, and you live."

"And option two?"

"Option two is you try to take the satchel here, on this moraine, in the last hours before a storm that kills everyone who isn't under stone by dark." She met his eyes. "And I want to be clear about something, because I think you're a man who respects clarity. If it comes to violence, we don't win. Nine armed riders against six tired climbers—we don't win. But we don't need to win. We need to make it expensive. A fight on scree above a glacier, in building wind, with horses that will bolt the moment the first shot cracks the air—your men know what that costs. Ask them. Look at their faces. They've been climbing behind you for four days and they can read that sky as well as I can."

She saw it happen. Not in Corvane—he was too well-built for it, too many years of keeping his face a closed door—but in the riders. A shifting in the saddles. One man glanced west at the clouds and then made himself stop glancing. Another looked at his horse's legs, at the ice under them, at the long blue sweep of glacier between here and anywhere warm.

Arithmetic. That was all any of this was. Corvane had brought force, and force was a number, and numbers changed with the weather.

"They're criminals," Corvane said, but he said it to his men as much as to her, and Beatrix heard the weakness in it. "Thieves and forgers. The woman helped forge the very treaties she's carrying proof of."

"She did," Beatrix said, before Ilsa could speak. "And she confessed it to us around a fire, and we judged her for it, and then we decided the truth mattered more than purity. Which is more than your consortium ever did with its own history." She took one step forward, onto the moraine's crest, where the wind pulled at her coat. "Here's my offer, hunter to hunter. Safe conduct down. All of us, together, on the southern trail—I'll lead it myself. My name's still struck from two registries, but there isn't a guide alive who reads ice better than I do, and your men know it, because they've spent four days following our sign and losing us. You get your whole company home alive. In exchange, we walk. No arrests, no seizures, no knives in the night at the ferry crossing. You write whatever report saves your own skin—I don't care. The documents are already beyond you."

"And why," Corvane said softly, "would I trade a chance at everything for a guarantee of nothing?"

"Because it's not a chance at everything," Beatrix said. "That's the arithmetic you're refusing to do. It's a small chance at a satchel, paid for with a large chance at nine graves—including yours. And even if you took the satchel, you'd be carrying paper into a storm, and the paper has already been copied, and the story has already left your hands. You're not hunting documents anymore, Corvane. You're hunting a rumor, and rumors don't bleed."

The wind rose. Somewhere up-valley, a serac calved with a sound like a door slamming in a giant house, and every horse sidestepped, and every rider fought his mount, and for a moment the consortium's power looked exactly like what it was: eight cold men on frightened animals, a long way from home, under a sky turning the color of iron.

* * *

Later—much later, in the white silence of the descent, when there was time to think—Beatrix would replay those minutes on the moraine and understand how close it had come. But standing there, she felt nothing but a strange calm, the calm she'd worn so long it had grown to fit her like her own skin. It was the calm of the guide at the moment of commitment: the point past which retreat costs more than the climb. She had passed that point weeks ago, in a flooded river, hauling a chest out of the shallows when every instinct screamed to let the current take it.

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled—a thin, tired thing, almost human.

"My file on you said 'resourceful,'" he said. "It undersold."

"Files usually do. Yours called the annexation 'a lawful consolidation.' Mine calls it forgery and bribery, and mine has signatures."

He turned and looked at his men. Beatrix watched him do it and understood that the decision had already left his hands—that perhaps it had left his hands the moment the copies left High Camp, or the moment the storm front stacked itself over the western peaks, or the moment Davor fell into the crevasse with a map fragment in his coat and the consortium's plan half-buried with him. Leaders liked to believe they decided things. Mostly they ratified what the terrain had already decided.

"Sergeant Hale," Corvane said.

A rider dismounted. Broad, grey-bearded, the one who'd looked at the clouds and made himself stop. He came up the moraine and stood at Corvane's shoulder, and Beatrix marked how he stood—weight low, eyes on the ice, a man who'd done mountain work and hated it.

"Hale led the party that burned the cache at Stony Fork," Corvane said. "He'll confirm the supply situation. Sergeant—how many days of feed do we have for the horses?"

Hale didn't hesitate, and Beatrix gave him credit for that. "Two. Maybe three if we cut grain and pray. And the southern trail's a day and a half in good conditions." He looked west. "Those aren't good conditions coming."

"If we take them by force," Corvane said, "how many of ours do you bring home?"

Hale was quiet for a moment. Beatrix watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done aloud, and she watched him refuse to lie about it, because men like Hale lied about many things but not about mountains.

"On scree, with those horses, in that wind?" He shook his head once. "I wouldn't guarantee you me, sir."

The word hung in the cold air. *Sir.* Spoken like a verdict.

Corvane closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he looked older, and Beatrix thought: there it is. The moment the number comes back wrong. Every hunter meets it eventually—the instant when the quarry stops being prey and becomes a cost, and the man whose job is pursuit has to decide what pursuit is worth.

"Your terms," Corvane said. "Say them precisely. I'll want them witnessed."

* * *

They spoke the terms plainly, on the moraine, in voices pitched to carry, because Beatrix wanted every rider to hear them and every exile too. Safe conduct for all six—herself, Ilsa Venn, Tomas Rell, old Vessik, Petra and Anselm, the quiet ones who'd barely spoken in three weeks. Down the southern trail, led by Beatrix, starting within the hour, ahead of the front. No seizure of persons or papers. No reprisals against Maren at High Camp, against the ferryman, against anyone who'd fed or sheltered them. Corvane would give his parole before witnesses, and Beatrix would give hers: that her people would raise no hand unless raised against, and that she would bring his company down alive if the mountain allowed anyone to be brought down at all.

"And if the storm takes some of us," Corvane said, "on your trail, under your lead?"

"Then it takes some of us," Beatrix said. "That's the bargain nobody gets to negotiate. I can promise you my skill and my honesty. I can't promise you the weather. Any guide who promises you the weather is selling you something."

Tomas spoke up from behind her, young voice cracking slightly with the cold and the enormity of it: "Witnessed. I witness it." And Ilsa: "Witnessed." And Vessik, lifting his staff like a judge's rod: "Witnessed, by a clerk of the Vellmark archive, God keep its memory." Even Petra and Anselm murmured it, and Beatrix felt the strange weight of it settle over the group—they were witnessing each other into existence, making themselves real in front of the men sent to erase them.

Corvane gave his parole in a flat, careful voice, naming himself and his command, and Hale repeated it after him like a man reading terms of surrender, and the other riders murmured assent because their sergeant had spoken and the sky had spoken and the arithmetic had spoken.

Then Corvane did something Beatrix hadn't expected. He walked the last twenty paces alone and stood in front of her, close, and lowered his voice so only she could hear.

"The men who blacklisted you," he said. "Their names are in those ledgers?"

"Three of them signed the order personally. Two took payments the same season."

He nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis. "I wondered why the registry purge was so thorough. It always seemed like excess. Burning a guide's name twice." His mouth twisted. "They weren't erasing a mistake. They were erasing a witness."

"Now you know," Beatrix said. "Welcome to the truth. It's heavier than it looks. Most people can't carry it far."

"No," Corvane agreed. "But some of us have been carrying its opposite for thirty years, and my shoulders know the difference." He stepped back, and his voice returned to parade-ground mildness. "Sergeant Hale will ride at your side. For safety," he added, dryly, "everyone's."

* * *

They moved within the hour. Beatrix set the pace herself, fast but honest, testing the snow bridges with her pole and calling the hazards back down the line in the clipped grammar of the rope: *ice—step left—short rope—breathe.* Behind her came her five, roped and steady, and behind them the riders leading their horses along the moraine's edge where the footing was worst but the ground was true, and somewhere in that long strung-out column the shape of the world had quietly changed: hunters and hunted descending the same mountain under the same bargain, bound together by a truth too heavy for any of them to carry alone and too public now for any of them to bury.

The first flakes came at midafternoon, fat and slow, spinning down through the grey like the mountain deciding, at last, to speak.

Hale rode up beside her and squinted into it. "How long?"

"Until it's serious? Two hours. Until it owns us? Four." She glanced at him. "Can your horses hold the pace to the tree line?"

Hale looked back down his column—eight riders, eight exhausted animals, six climbers who moved across the ice like they'd been born to it—and then at the wall of white gathering in the west.

"They'll hold," he said, "if you get us there. That's the deal, isn't it? We follow you down, and nobody owns the descent."

"Nobody ever owned it," Beatrix said, and turned into the snow, and led.
