## 20. The Descent Nobody Owns

Tomas had imagined this moment a hundred times on the climb up — the moment they came down again with the truth in their packs and the world changed by it. In his imaginings there was always cheering, or at least torchlight, or at minimum a road.

What he got instead was Corvane's hunters standing in a ragged line at the moraine with their breath smoking, looking at the ice below them the way men look at a grave they've been asked to dig themselves.

The storm had passed in the night, but it had taken the light with it. The sky over the southern valleys was the color of dirty wool, and the glacier below the archive's mouth ran down toward the col in a series of blue steps and broken seracs that no one in either party would have crossed for money. Not for any money.

"Nobody owns this descent," Beatrix said quietly, beside him. "That's the point of it."

* * *

It had started an hour after dawn, when Corvane made his offer and Beatrix made hers, and Tomas — pressed against a boulder with Ilsa's elbow in his ribs — understood that he was watching two people negotiate over the lives of eleven others as calmly as if they were haggling over wool.

Corvane had come up alone to the talking distance. That itself told Tomas something. A man confident of killing doesn't walk forward alone; a man who needs something walks forward alone.

"You have what you came for," Corvane had said. His voice carried flat across the ice, worn down by wind and years of giving orders. "Give me the documents and safe passage down, and the bounty dies here. All of it. You walk into Vellmark's successor state as free people."

"And when we're over the border," Beatrix had called back, "and your consortium's lawyers are done explaining to three capitals why their founding charters are forgeries?"

"I don't answer for lawyers."

"No," Beatrix had agreed. "You answer for the men you burned out of High Camp. For the cache. For Davor." A pause, long enough that the wind filled it. "You can't buy back what we're carrying, Corvane. It stopped being paper the day you started hunting us. Now it's testimony."

Tomas had watched Corvane's jaw work. He'd watched the man glance back at his own line of riders — eight of them, maybe nine, half of them young, all of them looking not at Beatrix but at the glacier.

That was when Tomas understood how this would end. Not with a bargain. With arithmetic.

Because every man on that ridge could read ice. Every one of them knew what the storm had left behind: fresh snow loaded on steep blue slopes, bridges over crevasses softened until they'd take a mule and nothing heavier, the whole face of the lower glacier rearranged like furniture in a house where nobody lived. And every one of them knew that the only person on this mountain who had ever descended this route — who had ever descended it at all, in any season, alive — was standing on the wrong side of the argument.

"You want us to follow you down that?" one of the younger riders had shouted. Not at Beatrix. At Corvane.

"I want you to do your duty," Corvane had said, and even from forty meters Tomas heard the hollow in it.

"Duty's a warm fire and a paymaster who exists," another rider said. "You want to arrest them, arrest them. You want to follow them onto that ice because they're the only ones who know the way — then say so."

And then the strangest thing happened, the thing Tomas would tell for the rest of his life in low towns and high camps both: the riders did not move. They stood in their ragged line, and they looked at Corvane, and they waited, and the waiting itself was a mutiny more complete than any drawn knife.

Corvane looked at his men. He looked at the glacier. He looked, last of all, at Beatrix Brightwater, blacklisted in two countries, erased from every register, standing on the ice with a rope coil over one shoulder like it had grown there.

"Well, guide?" he said. "You claim to know the way down. Prove it. Take all of us or none of us."

Beatrix didn't smile. Tomas had learned that about them on the climb — that the legends had it wrong, that Beatrix Brightwater never smiled at moments like this, that the calm they wore was not confidence but armor, hammered flat over something that hurt to carry.

"Rope teams of four," Beatrix said, loud enough for everyone. "I lead first team. My word is law on the ice — anyone who argues with me on a crossing gets cut loose from the plan, not the rope, but they walk behind and they don't get a vote. We move now, while the cold holds the snow bridges. By midday the sun starts eating them, and then nobody goes down, and everybody explains to God why they stayed."

A silence. Then the youngest rider — a boy with frost in his eyebrows — unclipped his rifle sling and slung it across his back, muzzle down, and stepped forward to be counted.

* * *

They roped up in the blue shadow of the moraine, and Tomas found himself tied into the second team with Ilsa and two of Corvane's men, which felt like being lashed to wolves, except the wolves kept checking each other's knots with a care that embarrassed him.

"Front or back?" one of them asked Ilsa. An older man, gray at the temples, hands like tree roots.

"Back," Ilsa said. "I'm cargo. I'm the most valuable cargo on this mountain, so put me where nothing falls on me."

The man almost smiled. "What is it you're carrying, exactly?"

"The truth," Ilsa said. "Original treaties. Ledgers. Names. Yours may be in there — I haven't finished reading."

The man went quiet. But he checked her knot twice anyway, and pulled it tight, and said, "Then I'll keep you dry."

Tomas watched Beatrix set the pace ahead, and thought about everything the outlaw ballads got wrong. The ballads had Beatrix Brightwater as a ghost, a trickster, a name whispered in smugglers' dens. Nobody sang about this: the way Beatrix read the glacier, ten meters at a time, probing snow bridges with the axe shaft before trusting weight to them, calling back in a flat carrying voice — *bridge holds, come on, wide step left, don't look down, looking down is how people fall* — and how thirty strangers moved when that voice spoke, exiles and hunters alike, because competence does not care whose side you signed on with.

By the third hour they were a column. Not friends. Not allies. A column — which on ice is its own kind of country, with its own laws, and Beatrix Brightwater was its entire government.

* * *

The trouble came at the icefall, where the glacier poured through a gap in the moraine like batter through a funnel, breaking into towers the height of churches.

Beatrix stopped the column in the shadow of the first serac and studied the route for a long time without speaking. Tomas had learned to read those silences. This one was bad.

"Sun's on the upper wall," Beatrix said finally, coming back along the rope. "Those towers shed through the afternoon. We cross the funnel fast, in single file, no talking, no stopping. If I shout *run*, you run to the far moraine and you do not look back and you do not wait for your rope partner, because the rope will be coming with them or it won't. Everyone understand?"

One of Corvane's men — the skeptical one, the one who'd shouted about duty — raised a hand like a schoolboy. "And if we refuse?"

"Then stand here," Beatrix said. "The mountain will settle the question inside an hour."

He went. They all went.

The funnel crossing was the worst hour of Tomas's life, and he had buried his father under a false name and forged his own travel papers at fifteen. The ice groaned around them constantly, a deep tectonic complaint, like the mountain clearing its throat. Meltwater ticked somewhere beneath the snow under his boots, and every tick sounded personal. Ahead of him Beatrix moved with a speed that looked casual and wasn't, placing each step exactly where the previous step had proven the ice honest, and behind him came nine armed men of the consortium walking in the footsteps of the woman they'd been paid to destroy, because the alternative was standing still under falling mountains.

Halfway across, the mountain cleared its throat for real.

The crack came from above and to the right, a sound like the sky tearing linen, and Beatrix's voice went through the column like a whip: "*Run.*"

Tomas ran. He did not look back, because he'd been told not to and because obedience on ice is love in its plainest form. The far moraine came up at him in a rush of scree and shadow, and he threw himself behind the first boulder big enough to matter, and then the world ended in white noise.

When the powder settled, he counted heads. Ilsa, gray-faced, gripping her satchel of documents like a mother with a child. The gray-handed rider. The boy with frost in his eyebrows. All nine hunters. All five exiles. Beatrix, standing upright in the settling snow-dust, counting too, lips moving.

"Everyone," Beatrix said. "Everyone's here."

Behind them, where the middle of the funnel had been, a serac the size of a chapel lay shattered across the route they'd just walked. Where the rope had stretched between Tomas and the man behind him, the rope was gone — sheared clean, its ends whipping loose in the wind.

The man who'd been tied behind him stood staring at the severed rope, then at Tomas, then at Beatrix.

"That was my rope," he said. "My rope partner cut me loose and ran."

"He followed orders," Beatrix said. "So did you. You're alive. Both of you."

"My partner—"

"Is right behind you," said the partner, emerging from the snow-dust, pale and swearing. "And cutting you loose was the hardest thing I've done in eleven years of this work, and I'll thank you to buy me a drink when we're down."

A laugh went through the column — shaky, disbelieving, but real. Even Ilsa laughed, a small cracked sound, and Tomas realized he was laughing too, and that the laughter was the sound of nine hunters and six exiles becoming, for one irreverent moment, a single rope team.

Corvane stood apart from it, arms folded, watching Beatrix count his men a second time.

"You could have left us at the icefall," he said. "Told us the route, walked away, let the mountain do your work. No witnesses, no bounty, no trial. Who would ever know?"

"Me," Beatrix said. "I'd know." They shouldered the rope coil. "Two teams. We lose the light in three hours. Move."

* * *

They came off the ice as the sun went down behind the western peaks, and the glacier's last blue light turned the whole lower slope the color of deep water. Below them, impossibly close after everything, lay the valley: woodsmoke rising straight in the still air, a bell ringing vespers in the low town, lamplight starting in windows one by one like the valley lighting candles for them.

Tomas stood at the edge of the ice and let his legs shake. Around him the column broke apart into small human clusters — hunters checking each other for frostbite, exiles sharing water, Ilsa sitting down hard on a rock with the document satchel in her lap, holding it with both hands, breathing.

Corvane's men had formed up loosely near their commander, and Tomas watched the youngest one — the boy with the frost in his eyebrows — hesitate, then walk over to where Beatrix stood coiling rope in the failing light.

"They'll ask us to lie," the boy said. "When we report. About the icefall. About who led whom."

"Yes," Beatrix said.

"I won't."

"I know," Beatrix said. "That's why I counted you twice."

The boy nodded, once, and went back to his line, and Tomas saw Corvane watching the exchange with an expression Tomas couldn't name — not defeat, exactly. Something more like a man reading a map of a country he'd believed didn't exist.

Ilsa came to stand beside Tomas. Below, the bell rang the last of vespers, and the lamplight spread, window joining window.

"We did it," Tomas said. "We actually — the documents, the descent, all of it. Tomorrow we're in the town, and by next week—"

"Tomorrow," Ilsa said, "we find out whether paper is stronger than fear. The mountain was the easy part, Tomas. Mountains only want to kill you. Towns want to decide what you were." She squeezed his shoulder. "But tonight — tonight we're down, and everyone's alive, and the truth is in this bag. Let that be enough until morning."

Tomas looked back up the valley one last time. High above, almost lost in the dusk, the glacier's mouth glowed faintly blue, like a door left open behind them.

Then the first lamp of the low town flickered brighter, and a second bell began to ring — not vespers, faster, urgent — and figures came out of the houses below, moving toward the road, moving toward them, and Tomas could not tell from this distance whether they were coming to welcome them or to stop them.
