## 21. Testimony in the Low Town

The town was called Brasten Hollow, and it had one printing press, two churches, and a ferry landing that smelled of tar and drowned wood. Ilsa Venn had lived in places like it for forty years — places where the news arrived late and left even later, where a stranger's face was remembered longer than her name. She had chosen such places deliberately. Invisibility, she had learned, was not the absence of being seen. It was the art of being seen as something unremarkable.

She did not feel unremarkable now.

She stood in the back room of the ferryman's house with Beatrix Brightwater's chest open on the table between them, and the documents inside were fanned out like the hand of a card player who had already won. Original treaties, signed in Vellish ink by men whose names Ilsa had once copied out in a careful archivist's script. Ledgers of payments, dated to the year of annexation, itemized in a consortium clerk's tidy hand. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp of the glacier, the master register: every official who had taken consortium money, every magistrate who had stamped the forged papers, every name on the blacklist that had erased Beatrix Brightwater from two nations' rolls.

Ilsa's own name was not in it. She had never been important enough to bribe. She had only been useful enough to deceive.

"You're doing that thing again," Tomas Rell said from the doorway. He had a bandage on his forearm from the icefall and a way of standing that made him look taller than he was. "The thing where you read a page like it's going to apologize."

"Documents don't apologize," Ilsa said. "That's rather the point of them."

Downstairs, the ferryman's wife was arguing with someone about the price of lamp oil. The sound came up through the floorboards, ordinary and domestic, and Ilsa found herself listening to it the way a drowning woman listens to weather. Forty years of hiding had taught her that ordinary sounds were the last things you lost. When they stopped, the world had changed.

The world had changed three days ago, when they had come down off the Escarpment with frostbitten fingers and a mixed column of exiles and former hunters, and Beatrix Brightwater had walked into the low town's single tavern and set the chest on the bar like a woman paying a debt.

* * *

It began, as most dangerous things do, quietly.

Beatrix did not make a speech. Ilsa had expected one — she had spent the descent composing speeches herself, drafting the words she would say when the moment came, the archivist's instinct for the formal declaration still alive in her after all these years. But Beatrix simply bought a round, waited until the room had settled into its evening murmur, and said, in the flat voice of someone reporting trail conditions:

"There are people in this valley whose grandparents were Vellish. Some of you know it. Some of you have forgotten on purpose. Either way, there's something in this chest you should see."

Then she opened it and let the papers speak.

Ilsa watched the room instead of the documents. She watched a ferryman's hands stop moving over his tankard. She watched an old woman lean forward and mouth the words of the treaty preamble — the old Vellish oath of borders, the one children used to recite before the annexation made reciting it a crime. She watched a young man in consortium gray, a clerk or a spy or both, slip out the back door so fast his chair scraped like a scream.

"He'll report us," Davor would have said. Davor was dead under the icefall, and no one said it, but everyone thought it. The consortium would know within hours. Riders would come. Papers would burn.

"They'll come," Beatrix said, reading the room the way she read scree fields. "So we don't give them anything to burn."

* * *

What followed was the strangest forty-eight hours of Ilsa Venn's long, hidden life, and she had once spent six weeks in a root cellar during a purge.

Beatrix divided the documents the way she divided rope on a glacier — into equal loads, each carried by different hands, each following a different route down. No single copy complete. No single courier knowing what the others carried. It was smuggling logic, exile logic, the logic of people who had survived by never letting the whole of anything rest in one place.

Ilsa took the ledgers herself. She argued for it and won, because she was the only one who could read the consortium's cipher notation in the margins, and because at seventy-one she had decided she was done letting younger braver people walk toward danger while she catalogued it from behind.

"The press first," she told Beatrix in the ferryman's back room, tracing the route on a map gone soft at the folds. "Brasten Hollow has a printer — Oskar Lindt, he sets the weekly gazette and does job work for anyone who pays. His grandfather printed Vellish primers before the ban. He'll do it."

"He might be afraid."

"Of course he's afraid. Fear is not the same as refusal." Ilsa looked up. "You taught me that on the mountain, though I don't think you knew you were teaching it."

Beatrix almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, and Ilsa hoarded it.

They went to Lindt's shop at dawn, when the ink smell was thickest and the printer was alone with his compositing stick. He was a heavy man with ink-stained knuckles and his father's jaw, and when Ilsa laid the ledger pages on his stone counter he went very still, the way stone goes still.

"I know these figures," he said at last. "My father kept books for the Vellish trade office. Same column style. Consortium clerks copied it — did you know that? They stole our bookkeeping along with everything else."

"I didn't know that," Ilsa said, and felt the strange vertigo of scholarship meeting life, the archive breathing.

Lindt read for a long time. Outside, cart wheels passed; a dog barked at nothing; the town conducted its morning as if the century had not just turned over in a rented loft and a flooded river and a chest pulled from the shallows by a disgraced guide.

Then Lindt rolled up his sleeves. "How many copies?"

"As many as your press can bear before they arrive."

"And when they arrive?"

Isla met his eyes. "Then you were never here, and neither were we, and the gazette this week is about turnip prices."

Lindt laughed — a short, startled bark — and reached for his type case. "Turnip prices," he said. "God, I haven't set a boring line in twenty years. Give me the pages."

* * *

The pulpit was harder, and Ilsa insisted on doing it herself.

There were two churches in Brasten Hollow. The low church belonged to the valley congregation, and its minister was a cautious man named Aldous Renner who had spent thirty years preaching obedience in a language just vague enough to keep the consortium's censors bored. The high church up the hill was older, half-abandoned, its congregation reduced to a dozen grandmothers who sang in Vellish when they thought no one was listening.

Ilsa went to the high church first, because she was a coward in exactly one direction and always had been: she could not bear to watch hope fail slowly.

The grandmothers were singing when she came in. The hymn was one her mother had sung, and Ilsa stood in the doorway with snowmelt dripping off her boots and let the sound go through her like a blade finding an old scar. Forty years of false names, and the first true thing anyone had said to her in a decade was a hymn in a dying language sung by women too old to care who heard.

The eldest of them, a tiny woman with hands like birch bark, stopped mid-verse and looked at her.

"You're Vellish," the woman said. Not a question. "From the old country accent. Before the border schools beat it out of everyone."

"I'm from the archive," Ilsa said. "Which is nearly the same thing."

She told them. All of it — the forged treaties, the bribes, the annexation engineered on paper by men who had never stood on Vellish soil. She read aloud from the master register, and when she reached the name of the magistrate who had ordered the Vellish primers burned, the birch-bark woman crossed herself with a motion so old it predated the gesture's official meaning.

"What do you want from us?" the woman asked.

"Nothing," Ilsa said. "Only that you sing louder. Word travels faster than riders when it travels in a hymn. Every market day, every wedding, every funeral — sing the old verses. Let the whole valley wonder why. Wonder is a courier the consortium can't shoot."

The grandmothers looked at one another. Then the birch-bark woman turned back to her sisters, lifted her chin, and started the verse again — louder, slower, in the full old cadence that the border schools had spent a generation trying to kill.

Ilsa stood in the empty church and listened to a language refuse to die, and understood, finally, why Beatrix had opened the chest instead of pushing it back into the river.

Some things only need one person to stop hiding.

* * *

Minister Renner of the low church required more finesse, and that task fell to Beatrix, which surprised Ilsa until she saw how it worked.

Renner received them in his study, a narrow room smelling of lamp oil and caution. He listened to their account with his hands folded and his face arranged into the expression of a man preparing to decline politely. When Beatrix finished, he said:

"I sympathize, truly. But I preach to farmers and ferrymen. If I take up a political matter from the pulpit, the consortium will have my church registered as a dissident hall by Friday, and my congregation scattered by Sunday. You ask me to spend thirty years of careful work in an afternoon."

"I do," Beatrix said.

Ilsa held her breath. This was the moment she had dreaded — the reasonable man, the sensible refusal, the door closing with regret rather than malice.

But Beatrix leaned forward, and Ilsa saw her put down the guide's calm and pick up something rawer.

"Minister, I was erased from two registries for a rescue that failed through no fault of mine. I know what it is to have careful work taken away in an afternoon. So let me tell you what your afternoon buys." She laid the treaty drafts on his desk, one by one, like cards. "This page says your valley was sold. This one names the men who sold it. This one proves the sale was dressed up as law. Your congregation's grandparents were citizens of a country that never legally stopped existing. You've been preaching obedience to a forgery."

Renner stared at the pages. His folded hands came apart. One of them — Ilsa watched it happen, the small treason of the body — one of his hands moved toward the oldest draft and touched the Vellish seal as if checking whether it was warm.

"My grandmother," he said quietly, "used to sing something at the washline. I never knew what it was. The neighbors complained once and she stopped."

"It was probably the border hymn," Ilsa said. "The high church still sings it."

Renner sat for a long time. Then he stood, took his sermon notes for Sunday — a careful, censored, thirty-years-safe meditation on stewardship — and fed them page by page into his stove.

"I'll want the documents read aloud," he said. "All of them. Slowly. My congregation has bad hearing and long memories."

* * *

The couriers left that night, four of them, on four roads.

Tomas Rell carried the copies bound for the capital of Karst itself — the nation that had absorbed Vellmark and inherited its lies. He volunteered before anyone asked, and Beatrix looked at him for a long moment and then gave him the packet, and Ilsa understood that this was the closest thing to a blessing either of them knew how to perform.

Maren's nephew took the eastern road with the second set. A wool merchant who owed Maren a debt took the southern. And the fourth set went by river, sewn into a bale of hides, addressed to a newspaper editor in the port city whose name Ilsa had found in the marginalia of a thirty-year-old letter — a journalist who had once tried to print the truth about the annexation and been ruined for it, and who, Ilsa gambled, had spent the years since waiting for exactly this.

"Four roads," Beatrix said, watching the last rider disappear into the dark. "Even if they catch two, two get through."

"Even if they catch three," Ilsa said, "the grandmothers are singing."

* * *

The consortium came, of course. They came the next morning, eight riders in gray, led by a lieutenant with a list and a warrant signed by a magistrate whose name was in the register. They searched the tavern, the ferry house, Lindt's shop. They found turnip prices.

By noon, the lieutenant stood in the town square looking at a crowd that had gathered without being called — farmers, ferrymen, the birch-bark woman and her sisters sitting on the church steps with their hymnals open like shields. The lieutenant announced that seditious materials had entered the valley and that any person possessing them would be arrested.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. And then, from the steps of the high church, thin and clear and absolutely unafraid, the grandmothers began to sing.

The lieutenant's horse shifted under him. He looked at the crowd, at the hymnals, at the faces — and Ilsa, watching from the tavern window, saw him do the arithmetic that Corvane's men had done on the moraine, the arithmetic of men unwilling to die for forged paper. Eight riders could arrest twelve old women. Eight riders could not arrest a town that had decided, all at once and without discussion, to remember.

They rode out before dusk. They took nothing.

* * *

The reply from the port city came on the fourth day, by the fastest river boat, carried by a boy who ran the whole length of the landing with the envelope held above his head like a torch.

Ilsa opened it with hands that shook, and read it twice, and then read it aloud in the tavern because her voice needed witnesses.

The editor wrote: *Received your material. Verified the seal impressions against our 1897 files within the hour. We go to press tonight with ten thousand copies. Three capitals will have it by week's end. Enclosed find a letter of introduction to counsel in each — lawyers who have waited twenty years for cause.*

And beneath that, in a different, fiercer hand: *To the archivist who sent this — whoever you are, whatever name you're using — you have given my career its reason. I intend to spend it recklessly.*

The tavern was silent. Then the ferryman's wife, who had argued about lamp oil through all of it, said what everyone was thinking:

"Hearings. They'll have to hold hearings. In the capitals. In the open."

"In three capitals," Ilsa said. Her voice cracked on the number, and she let it. "Vellmark existed on paper for one fraudulent day. Now it exists in ten thousand copies, in a hymn, in a sermon, in a printer's type case. Paper can be burned. That—" she gestured at the room, at the town, at the whole improbable conspiracy of the ordinary— "that has to be answered."

Beatrix stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the road south, and Ilsa saw that she was not celebrating. She was counting.

"What is it?" Ilsa asked.

"Corvane got away with eleven riders," Beatrix said. "He's had four days. He knows what we've done, and he knows hearings mean testimony, and testimony means witnesses." She turned from the window, and her face had the calm Ilsa had learned to fear and trust in equal measure — the calm of a guide who has read the weather and doesn't like the reading. "The consortium's only move left is to make sure no witness reaches a hearing alive. Which means every one of us is now evidence."

Tomas looked up from rewrapping his bandage. "So what do we do?"

"We do what evidence does," Beatrix said. "We survive until we're read."

Outside, the grandmothers had begun the evening hymn, and the sound came up the street like water finding its level, and somewhere far to the north, in three capitals that did not yet know their own histories, typesetters were reaching for their cases.
