## 22. A Name on the Register

The letter came in April, when the escarpment was still wearing its winter collar and the river below the low town ran the color of old glass.

Beatrix read it standing in the doorway of the loft they'd rented two winters ago — the same loft where a sealed chest had been pried open with a smuggler's cold chisel, where a neighbor had noticed too much and turned out to be the first friend instead of the first threat. The paper was official stock, heavy enough to have weight, with the struck-through registry number of two nations side by side and a single line of fresh ink beneath:

*Entry revoked. Name restored. Effective upon receipt.*

Ilsa Venn stood at the window behind them, hands folded, watching the street the way she still watched every street, out of habit older than the war that had made her a ghost. "Well?" she said.

"They spelled my name right." Beatrix set the letter on the table, weighted it under the tin cup that had survived the whole journey and half the storm. "First time anyone's bothered."

Tomas Rell came up the stairs three at a time, the way he did everything now, seventeen going on thirty with a forger's quick hands and a guide's new calluses. He'd been carrying a crate of survey stakes from the ferry landing and he dropped it in the doorway without apology. "Is that it? Is that the letter?"

"It's the letter."

He crossed the room and read it twice, lips moving on the second pass, as if he wanted to memorize the exact wording of a thing he'd only ever heard about secondhand — the blacklist, the glacier, the rescue that went wrong and the rescuers who paid for it. To Tomas, Beatrix Galloway had been a legend before she'd ever been a person. She was still not entirely sure which version he preferred, or whether it mattered anymore.

"So what do you do now?" he asked.

Beatrix looked at the letter. At the window, where the high passes were just visible above the rooflines, white on white, holding their snow like a held breath. At Ilsa, who had stopped watching the street and was watching her instead, with the particular patience of an archivist waiting for a document to finish being read.

"Now," Beatrix said, "I buy rope."

* * *

They bought rope, and pitons, and a secondhand theodolite from a surveyor retiring to the coast who threw in his leather case for free because, he said, he'd once been guided over the Col by a woman named Maren and had never forgotten what it cost her to be kind. They bought a stove and a chalkboard and forty feet of good oak for shelving. The building they took was nothing — a stone barn at the edge of the low town with a sagging roof and a door that opened toward the mountain, which Beatrix considered the only real qualification a building needed.

The sign went up on a gray morning in May. Tomas had painted it himself, and if you looked closely you could see the forger's son in the lettering: each stroke confident, each curve deliberate, a hand trained to make false things true now making a true thing permanent.

**GALLOWAY & VENN
MOUNTAIN GUIDES — ALL ROUTES — ALL COMERS**

"All comers," Ilsa said, reading it. "That's ambitious."

"That's the point."

Below *ALL COMERS*, in smaller letters, Tomas had added a line Beatrix hadn't asked for and didn't remove:

*No one turned away for their papers.*

She stood in the road looking at that line for a long time. The consortium's hearings were still grinding through three capitals; the forged treaties were public property now, reproduced in pamphlets and preached from pulpits, and the men whose fortunes rested on them had discovered, as men always do, that paper burns faster than reputations. Corvane had retired somewhere south, or fled, or been quietly absorbed into some other machine that ate people — no one could say, and Beatrix found she no longer cared to ask. What mattered was that the valleys were open. What mattered was that the passes belonged to whoever could walk them.

What mattered was that a name on a register was finally hers again, and she had learned, over two winters and one impossible crossing, the difference between a name given back and a name taken up.

* * *

The first clients came before the shelves were finished.

A geologist and his two students, wanting the limestone terraces above the scree fields. A widow from the ferry settlement walking the route her son had died on, alone except for a guide, because she said grief was lighter carried uphill. A pair of Vellish exiles who arrived at the door speaking their own language among themselves and switched to the trade tongue only when Beatrix greeted them, out of habit so deep it looked like shame — until Beatrix answered in Vellish, badly, with Ilsa's patient corrections ringing in memory, and watched both of them go still as if a locked room had opened.

Word traveled the way word does in mountain country: slowly, then all at once. By midsummer there was a waiting list. By late summer there were two guides besides Beatrix and Ilsa — Davor's younger sister, who had come down from the high camps to ask, flatly, whether the offer of work extended to traitors' kin, and who stayed after Beatrix said the mountains don't check bloodlines; and a former consortium tracker named Pell who showed up one morning with his gear on his back and his eyes on the ground and said he'd rather carry rope for honest wages than hunt people for dishonest ones. Beatrix hired him on a trial basis. He never gave her a reason to end it.

Maren came down from High Camp twice, both times refusing payment, both times leaving with her pack heavier than she'd arrived — salt pork, wool socks, a jar of the fierce plum spirit she distilled at altitude. The second time she stood in the barn doorway surveying the racks of rope and the chalkboard covered in route notes and said, "Your old mentor would have hated the paperwork and loved everything else," and Beatrix had laughed harder than she had in years, because it was true.

Even the teaching was going well, though teaching was not a word Beatrix would have chosen. *Passing it on,* maybe. Every client who could afford it got more than they paid for: how to read a slope's grain, how to listen to a serac field the way you'd listen to a sleeping animal, how to know when the mountain is offering you a way through and when it's offering you a way to die. And every season, four places in each party went free — to kids from the low town, to exiles with no papers, to anyone the mountains would have and the registries wouldn't.

"You're training your own competition," Pell observed once, watching a ferry boy learn prusik knots with the fierce concentration of someone learning to fly.

"I'm training the range's next guides," Beatrix said. "Competition implies we're rivals. We're colleagues they haven't met yet."

* * *

In September, on the anniversary of the day they'd pulled the chest from the floodwater, Beatrix climbed alone to the ridge above the icefall.

It was a rest day, officially. Unofficially, there were things a person needed to say to a mountain, and some of them couldn't be said in front of students.

The route they'd taken in the storm year was visible from here in pieces — the moraine where Corvane had made his arithmetic offer, the white slope where they'd buried themselves alive for a night while Tomas shook and Beatrix talked, telling the true story of the glacier rescue at last, the whole ugly weight of it, and finding that confession held warmth better than any cave. Davor lay somewhere below the icefall still, under moving ice, unclaimed by either nation that had erased him. Beatrix had stopped feeling guilty about not grieving him properly. Some debts you pay by remembering them accurately, and no more.

"I used to think running was freedom," she told the ridge, because the ridge was a good listener. "Turns out it was just running."

The wind came up the valley carrying the smell of stone and distant rain, and somewhere below, faintly, a bell — the ferry, or a goat, or one of the students playing games with the camp tackle. The escarpment spread out north and south, indifferent and magnificent, holding its secrets now in the open: the archive behind the glacier, catalogued and guarded and visited by scholars who wrote letters thanking *the guides of the Galloway & Venn service* for their indispensable assistance. Ilsa had gone back to work there three days a week, cataloguing a country that no longer existed so that no one could ever again claim it had never existed at all. She came home in the evenings with ink on her fingers and arguments in her mouth and, Beatrix noticed, without checking the street first. That habit had died sometime over the winter, quietly, the way bad habits die when they're no longer load-bearing.

Two countries had erased her. One country had stopped existing rather than admit what it had done. And here she stood, on the spine of the world, employed.

Not restored. Not forgiven — there was nothing left to forgive; the hearings had seen to that, coldly and thoroughly. Just *employed*. Registered. Findable. A name in a book that anyone could look up, attached to a door that anyone could knock on.

Belonging, she had learned, wasn't granted. It was chosen, daily, like a route: you picked your line, you committed, and you lived with the mountain's opinion of your judgment.

* * *

She came down to find the barn full of noise.

Tomas was arguing with the geologist's students about the correct way to coil wet rope, with the passionate pedantry of the recently converted. Davor's sister — Sela, her name was Sela, and Beatrix made herself use it — was patching a pack strap and correcting Tomas's argument without looking up. Pell had a kettle on. Ilsa sat at the big table with the autumn schedule spread out before her, making notations in three languages, and when Beatrix came in she pushed a page across without ceremony.

"Booking for October. A party of six, mixed experience. They want the high corridor."

"The corridor's closed by October."

"They know. They want to go anyway. They're Vellish." Ilsa's voice was dry, but her eyes weren't. "Third generation born in exile. They want to walk the route their grandparents' country died on. They want to see the archive door."

Beatrix took the page. Read it. Set it down.

"Six is too many for the corridor that late."

"Then take eight and split the rope teams. Or tell them no." Ilsa leaned back. "You're the guide. It's your name on the register."

And that was the thing, Beatrix thought — the whole strange, hard-won, ordinary miracle of it. It was her name. On a sign, in a ledger, in a government book with its blacklists struck through. A name that could be trusted with other people's lives because it had been tested against exactly the forces that had spent years trying to erase it.

"Tell them yes," she said. "But they climb by our rules, and if the weather turns, we turn. No exceptions, not even for ghosts."

"Ghosts are the most rule-abiding clients we'll ever have," Ilsa said. "They've already lost everything once."

Tomas had gone quiet at the rope rack, listening, and when Beatrix glanced over he was grinning at her with the expression he still wore sometimes — the outlaw-legend grin, the one that saw her as something larger than life. But it faded as she met his eyes, replaced by something better: the look of a colleague. Of a rope partner checking in before a pitch.

"October corridor," he said. "You'll lead?"

"I'll lead."

Outside, the light was going gold on the escarpment, and the first snow of the season was already falling somewhere above the high camps, patient and unhurried, waiting for them the way it always waited — not as an enemy, not as an escape, but as the oldest colleague of all, keeping the only register that had never once lied.

Beatrix stepped out the door that faced the mountain and stood a moment in the cold coming down, letting the range look at her, letting herself be seen.

Then she went back inside, picked up the chalk, and wrote the October party's names on the board — six strangers, one route, all comers — and began, in her own plain hand, under her own true name, to plan the way up.
