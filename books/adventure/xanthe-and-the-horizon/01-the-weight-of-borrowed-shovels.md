## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Shovels

The trench was four feet deep and the color of old tea, and by noon Jude Quintana had stopped noticing the smell.

That was the thing about the lowlands in the dry season — everything smelled like everything else. Rot and river and diesel from Harken's generator, all of it cooked together into a single thick air that sat on the back of the tongue like a coin. You worked in it. You sweated it out through your shirt and drank it back in with the water from the canvas bag, water that tasted faintly of rubber, and after a while your nose gave up filing complaints and you just dug.

He dug well. That was the one thing he could say for himself without flinching. Give him a trowel and a section wall and he'd read the soil like a man reading a letter from home — where the fill had been turned, where the ash lens ran, where a pit had been cut and re-cut across generations of people who had never once thought about what they were leaving behind. He'd learned it young, at his father's knee, before the name meant anything but dinner-table stories about potsherds and dead kings. He'd learned it so thoroughly that even now, eleven years and three continents later, his hands did the work without asking permission from the rest of him.

"Quintana." Harken's voice came down the trench from the shade of the survey tent, where the crew boss sat with his boots up on an ammunition crate and a ledger open across his knees. "You're reading that wall wrong. The midden's older than you're cutting it."

"The midden's exactly as old as I'm cutting it," Jude said, not looking up. "The shell layer dips east. It follows the old channel."

Harken grunted, which from Harken was agreement. He was a wide man gone soft at the edges, with a face like a dropped brick and small eyes that had learned arithmetic before they'd learned kindness. He ran contract digs for private buyers — collectors who wanted provenance without paperwork — and he hired men like Jude because men like Jude worked cheap when they owed money, and Jude always owed money.

Everyone in this trade owed money. That was the design.

Jude set his trowel down and lifted out a sherd the size of his palm, turning it in the flat light. Painted. Fine-lined, deliberate, a fragment of something that had once been beautiful to somebody. He held it a moment longer than the job required. Then he bagged it, tagged it, and passed it up the ladder to the boy who ran buckets, because the job required that too, and the difference between what a thing deserved and what a thing required was a luxury he'd stopped affording years ago.

* * *

It happened at half past three, when the light went long and amber and everyone's tempers went short with it.

The collector himself had come out that morning — a Mr. Aldous Vey, silver-haired, linen-suited, sweating elegantly under a parasol held by a local boy who couldn't have been more than fourteen. Vey walked the site the way a man walks a garden he owns, pausing here and there to admire a cleared floor or a line of postholes, and Jude kept his head down and his shoulders working, because collectors were weather: you endured them, you didn't engage them.

But Vey stopped at the trench edge. And Vey looked down. And Vey said, in the pleasant carrying voice of a man who has never once needed to be quiet:

"You there. The one reading the stratigraphy. You've done this professionally."

"He reads walls better than men I've paid triple," Harken said, coming over with his ledger tucked under his arm like a weapon he hadn't decided to draw yet.

"Where did you train?" Vey asked.

Jude wiped his hands on his thighs. "Nowhere worth naming."

"Everyone trains somewhere." Vey crouched at the edge of the trench, parasol tilting, and peered at the section wall with genuine interest. "That's a clean profile. That's university work. Come now — a name."

And here is the thing about names: you can go years without saying yours. You can introduce yourself as Jude, as J., as nothing at all, and the world will mostly let you get away with it, because the world is busy. But put a man in front of you who collects things — who collects *people*, really, the way other men collect pots — and he will dig for the name the way you dig for a burial, patiently, politely, with a little brush, until something surfaces.

"Quintana," Harken said, before Jude could shape a lie. "Off the Quintana dig. The Peruvian one. The one that—"

"I know the one," Vey said softly.

The air changed. Jude felt it the way you feel a storm front — pressure first, then the animals going quiet. Up and down the trench, the other diggers had stopped pretending to work. The bucket boy stood frozen with a full pail. Somewhere beyond the site fence, a bird kept calling, indifferent, obscene.

Vey straightened. He looked at Jude the way he'd looked at the postholes, with appreciation curdling into appraisal. "Aldous Vey," he said, extending a hand down into the trench, which Jude did not take. "I attended a lecture of your father's, once. Vienna. Before. He was brilliant. Genuinely brilliant — that was what made the rest of it so unforgivable."

"There's no rest of it," Jude said. "There's just me, and I'm digging your trench."

"Oh, there's always a rest of it." Vey smiled, and it was almost kind, which made it worse. "Forged provenance. Sold collections twice over. A foundation that turned out to be a mailbox in Zurich. Your father didn't just disgrace himself, young man — he disgraced the discipline. Every institution that ever trusted him closed a door afterward, and some of those doors are still closed. Do you know how many acquisitions I've lost to committees citing *the Quintana precedent*?" He shook his head, marveling, as if Jude were a natural phenomenon rather than a man standing in a hole. "And here you are. In the dirt. Doing it beautifully. It's in the blood, isn't it? Like a good hound."

"I'm nothing like him."

"No," Vey agreed pleasantly. "You're worse. He at least had the decency to be original."

Harken laughed. One short bark, quickly smothered, but Jude heard it, and heard what lived underneath it — relief, almost. Because Harken had known the name since the day he'd hired him. Had known it and said nothing, filed it away in that ledger behind his eyes, waiting for the moment it might be useful. Now it was useful. Now he had a collector standing on his site realizing he'd gotten university-grade labor at ditch-digger rates, which meant either a raise for Jude or a problem for Harken, and Harken had never once in his life chosen the option that cost him money.

"I want him off my site," Vey said. "Nothing personal. But if word travels that I'm employing a Quintana, every legitimate dealer I know will assume the worst about my own provenance. You understand. Reputation is the only currency in our business that can't be forged."

"It can be bought," Jude said.

Vey's smile widened. "Yes. By people like me. Good afternoon."

* * *

They fired him at sundown, formally, in front of the crew, because Harken believed humiliation was a management tool and cheaper than wages.

"Two weeks' pay, forfeit," Harken said, ticking items off in the ledger with a pencil stub. "You broke your contract. Site's compromised. That's the terms."

"The site isn't compromised. Vey's vanity is compromised. Those aren't the same thing."

"They are today." Harken closed the book. He seemed almost regretful, in the way a lock is regretful. "You still owe me for the advance. Boat passage, tools, three months' board. Call it ninety dollars. Call it a hundred, since you're feeling talkative."

Jude stared at him. Ninety had become a hundred between one sentence and the next, and they both knew it, and both knew there was nothing to be done about it, because the ledger was Harken's, the camp was Harken's, and the nearest town was two days' walk through country that ate strangers.

"I'll work it off," Jude said. "Another month."

"On what site? Vey owns the concession. Vey wants you gone. Word goes downriver tonight on the mail boat, and by Friday every crew boss from here to the delta will know there's a Quintana looking for a shovel." Harken picked dirt from under a fingernail, considering. "Although."

Jude waited. He hated himself for waiting. Waiting was how men like Harken farmed men like him.

"Vey'll pay a finder's fee," Harken said. "For anything pre-contact, intact, out past the flooded districts. Old riverbeds. Temples the water took. There's a whole drowned country out there nobody's surveyed properly." He tapped the ledger against his palm. "You bring me something Vey wants, I knock fifty off your account. Bring me something Vey *loves*, I zero it out, and I'll even throw in passage downstream on the next boat, no questions."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you owe me a hundred dollars in a country where nobody will hire you, and I'm a patient man, but my patience has interest." Harken smiled with all the warmth of a fish. "Sleep on it."

* * *

Jude didn't sleep. He sat on the levee behind the camp with his father's compass open in his hand, watching the moon come up over the flooded districts — a vast silver sheet of water to the east, broken by the black crowns of drowned trees, hiding whatever it hid.

The compass was brass, dented, older than he was. His father had left it to him along with nothing else — no letter, no explanation, just the instrument itself, mailed from nowhere in particular two years after the scandal, two years after the last time they'd spoken, with a customs form declaring its value at one dollar. Jude had carried it through six countries and sold everything else he owned at least twice, but never the compass. He told himself it was because it was useful. He told himself a lot of things.

He turned it now in the moonlight. On the reverse, scratched deep into the brass in his father's cramped, impatient hand, was a string of characters he'd never been able to make sense of — coordinates, maybe, or a cipher, or just the idle scratching of a man who destroyed everything he touched, including his son's ability to trust a single sentence anyone ever said to him.

*Brilliant,* Vey had called him. *Genuinely brilliant.*

*Original,* Vey had called him, meaning fraudulent, meaning liar, meaning the worst thing a man in this trade could be.

Both things were true. That was the trap of it. His father had been the best field archaeologist of his generation and a thief, a visionary and a fraud, and Jude had inherited the talent and the name and none of the explanation, and somewhere out there under that silver flood was a drowned temple that could buy his way clear of Harken's ledger — or drown him properly, which would at least settle the account.

He closed the compass. Out on the water, something moved — a boat, maybe, or a log, or nothing. The bird that had been calling all evening finally quit.

Ninety dollars. A hundred by morning, probably.

He'd need a boat, and he'd need luck, and tomorrow he'd start with whichever one he could find.

Downriver, at the port where the mail boats docked, there was a tavern called the Anchor Line where prospectors drank themselves blind on their stake money, and where a man with a hundred-dollar debt and a ruined name might hear, over the noise, the kind of story that dying men tell strangers — about vaults, and glaciers, and promises.

It was as good a direction as any. Everything was, when you had nothing left to lose except the name you'd never asked for.

Jude pocketed the compass and walked north along the levee, toward the port lights, while behind him the floodwaters rose silently over the drowned country, keeping its secrets the way his father had kept his — completely, and without apology.
