## 2. A Map for a Promise

The Salt Anchor smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who had run out of river. Jude came in off the levee with his boots squelching and his collar up, and the tavern's heat hit him like a hand pressed flat against his chest — thick, close, smelling of fried fish and cheap arrack. He counted what was left of Harken's advance against what a room would cost and decided he could afford either dinner or a bed, but not both.

Dinner won. Beds were for men with somewhere to be in the morning.

He took the corner table by the window because it had a wall at his back and a view of the door, habits learned in trench camps where shovels doubled as arguments. The rain had followed him up from the lowlands, arriving in long gray sheets that turned the harbor lights into smears of gold on black water. Somewhere out there, Harken's crew was probably drinking on his money, telling the story of how Quintana's boy got thrown off the site — *the Quintana boy*, as if the name were a brand burned into him at birth rather than something he'd spent thirty years trying to scrape off.

He ordered stew and arrack from a girl who looked twelve and moved like she'd been working the room since she was six. While he waited, he laid out his possessions on the scarred wood like a man taking inventory of a life: the compass, its brass case warm from his pocket; eleven coins; a folding knife with a broken tip; and the crumpled chit from Harken's bookkeeper showing a debt of forty-two days' wages, minus what had been docked for the shovel Jude had allegedly thrown into the trench.

He hadn't thrown it. He'd set it down carefully. But accuracy had never been one of Harken's virtues.

The compass he turned over once, twice, the way he did every night without deciding to. His father's compass. The only thing Aldous Quintana had ever given him that hadn't come wrapped in disappointment or an apology that explained nothing. The needle swung lazily, found north, held it. Even now, even drowned in tavern light and rain-hum, it pointed true while everything else in Jude's life wandered.

*You'll understand when you're older.* That was the whole inheritance, really. A compass and a sentence that never finished.

The stew arrived. So did the coughing.

It started from the dark end of the room, behind the stacked crates where the tavern kept its failures — men too sick or too broke to rent even a corner of floor. At first Jude took it for the building settling, some wet timber groaning. Then the cough broke again, deeper, wetter, the kind of sound that comes from a chest filling up like a bilge, and a woman at the bar crossed herself without looking up, which told Jude everything: this was not new, and nobody expected it to end well.

He ate. He told himself he'd eat. But the coughing kept coming, rhythmic now, terrible, like surf against a hull, and between the waves of it Jude could hear another sound — a thin, cracked voice muttering numbers. Coordinates. He knew the cadence of it. Every digger who'd ever worked a site muttered coordinates in his sleep eventually; the numbers got into you like sand.

Jude ate half the stew. Then he picked up his arrack, crossed the room, and looked into the crate-shadow where the sound was coming from.

The man was old the way driftwood is old — bleached, split, hardened past softness. His beard was white and matted, his hands lay open on his knees like dropped tools, and his eyes, when they found Jude's, were burning with the particular fever-brightness that Jude had seen take three men on the Kessel dig alone. High fever. Wet lung. Days, maybe less.

"You're staring," the old man said. His voice was gravel rolled in honey gone to sugar.

"Heard you counting."

"Counting's free." A smile cracked through the beard. "Everything else costs."

Jude crouched, bringing himself level. Up close the man smelled of camphor and river mud, and there were marks on his forearms — old rope burns layered over old rope burns, the signature of a man who'd spent decades hauling himself up ice and rock by whatever line would hold. Not a lowland man. Not a river man. An ice man, far south of any ice.

"Elias Vane," the old man said, and offered a hand that trembled but gripped like a trap closing.

"Jude." He didn't give the surname. He'd stopped giving the surname two years ago, when it became cheaper than explaining.

"Jude." Elias tasted it, nodded slowly, as if confirming something on a list only he could see. "You've got digging hands. I can always tell. Calluses here—" he tapped the base of his own fingers, "—from the mattock, not the oar. You work other men's holes."

"Sometimes my own."

"No." Elias's fever-bright eyes didn't waver. "Men who work their own holes don't drink arrack they can't afford in a port they're passing through. Men who work their own holes are already there." He coughed then, long and awful, folding forward until Jude instinctively put a hand on his shoulder and felt the architecture of the man — ribs like barrel staves, a body that had been magnificent and was being dismantled from the inside out.

When the fit passed, Elias leaned back and studied Jude with an expression that had shifted into something sharper. Appraising. The look of a man pricing goods.

"I'm dying," he said, conversationally, as if reporting weather.

"So am I. Slower."

That got a laugh that turned into more coughing. When Elias surfaced again, there were tears in the fever-bright eyes, whether from the coughing or something else Jude couldn't say. "Good. Good. A man with nothing to lose makes the best kind of promise, because he doesn't know yet how much promises weigh." He fumbled at his neck, at a cord there, greasy with sweat and age. "I need a courier. Not a thief. Not a priest. A courier."

"I'm not in the delivery trade."

"You're in the debt trade. Everyone in this room is in the debt trade." Elias pulled the cord over his head with shaking hands. On it hung an oilcloth packet, flat, rectangular, tied with waxed string gone gray with handling. He held it against his chest like a man holding a wound closed. "I've been carrying this for nineteen years. Carried it up rivers, across passes, through two wars and one marriage. It's the only thing I own that's worth anything, and I want you to understand—" his grip tightened, knuckles bloodless, "—it is worth *everything*. There are men who would peel the skin off my corpse for it. There is a man in this very town, probably drinking in a better tavern than this, who has been looking for it for nine years."

Jude should have stood up then. He knew that later, turning it over the way he turned the compass over every night. A dying stranger in a port tavern offering treasure — it was the oldest story on the river, older than the river, and it ended the same way every time: with a fool in a hole in the ground, or a fool at the bottom of it.

But he didn't stand. Because the old man's hands weren't trembling like a swindler's. They were trembling like a man holding something heavier than paper, and his eyes — fever or no fever — were clear in a way that sober men's eyes rarely managed.

"What is it?"

"A map." Elias said it simply, and the simplicity was the strange part. No theater, no lowered voice, no glance at the door. Just a fact, laid down like a stone. "A map to the Sunken Vault of Kesh-Tamar."

The name landed in Jude's chest before his mind caught it. Kesh-Tamar. He knew it the way you know a word from a language you thought you'd forgotten — from fragments, from a childhood of overhearing his father argue with colleagues who thought the Sunken Vault was a legend invented by monks to fill their donation boxes. *Kesh-Tamar, the vault beneath the glacier, the treasury that drowned when the mountain moved.* His father had said the word once, drunk, in a voice Jude had never heard from him before or since. A voice like a man describing a wound.

"That's a story," Jude said.

"Most true things start as stories." Elias worked at the waxed string with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. "Nineteen years ago I found the approach. Not the vault — the approach. The gate in the ice. I was younger than you, dumber than you, and I had a partner who talked me out of going in alone, which is the only reason I'm alive to die in this tavern." His mouth twisted. "We agreed the find belonged to someone else. To people who'd fed us when we were starving on the icefield. A village called Tsering, under the glacier's shoulder. We swore it. Both of us. And then my partner went back on it, and I—" the cough took him again, shorter this time, angrier, "—and I have spent nineteen years trying to decide which of us was worse."

Jude sat down fully on the crate. The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way water pulls back before a wave. "Your partner."

"Gone now. Dead or disappeared, same thing at our age." Elias finally got the knot loose and let the oilcloth fall open just enough to show the edge of what was inside: heavy paper, hand-drawn lines, ink gone brown. "Here's my arithmetic, boy. I can't climb anymore. I can't even walk to the harbor. In three days I'll be dead, and this map will either rot with me or fall into the hands of the first clever vulture who goes through my pockets. Unless I give it away first." He looked up, and the fever-light made his face into something ancient, a mask from a dig, half-eroded. "Give it to someone who'll carry it where it needs to go. Before the calving season opens the route. After that, the ice closes for another year, and after enough years, forever."

"Why me? You've known me ten minutes."

"Nineteen minutes. And because you flinched." Elias tapped his own chest. "When I said Kesh-Tamar. Right here. Most people hear a fairy tale. You heard a name somebody said in your house once, in a voice you remember." He smiled, terrible and gentle at once. "Whoever said it taught you that some words are doors. I need a man who knows doors exist."

Jude's throat was dry. He reached for the arrack and found he'd left it at the table. "Even if any of this were real — and I'm not saying it is — why would I haul your map up a glacier for strangers? What do I get?"

"The question of an honest man." Elias nodded approval. "You get the climb. You get to stand in front of the greatest find in four hundred years and know you put it where it belongs instead of where it could be sold. And you get this—" he raised one crooked finger, "—you get to stop working other men's holes. Because a promise kept is the one possession nobody can repossess. Believe me. I know the price of the opposite."

"And Tsering gets the vault."

"Tsering gets the vault. All of it. Whatever's in there — gold, relics, the bones of kings — belongs to the village. Not to collectors. Not to museums. Not to you." Elias's grip closed on Jude's wrist, shockingly strong, fever-hot. "Swear it. Swear it on whatever you still believe in, and if that's nothing, swear it on the fact that you'll have to live with yourself longer than I'll have to die."

The rain hammered the windows. Behind the bar, the girl was stacking bowls, and a pair of boatmen argued about tide times, and the world went on being ordinary while an old man dying of fever held a dying prospector's whole life out in his open hands.

Jude thought of Harken's ledger. Forty-two days. He thought of the trench, the thrown-shovel-that-wasn't, the name he couldn't scrape off. He thought: *this is exactly the kind of story that ends with me in a hole.*

And underneath that, quieter, in a voice he barely recognized as his own: *but what if it isn't.*

"I swear," Jude said. "Whatever's found goes to Tsering. Not to collectors. Not to me. On—" he hesitated, then touched the compass through his jacket, feeling its brass shape like a heartbeat, "—on this. It's the only honest thing I own."

Something went out of Elias then — not strength, but weight, twenty years of it leaving his shoulders all at once. He pressed the oilcloth packet into Jude's hands and folded Jude's fingers over it, both his hands around them, a benediction or a burial.

"It matches a compass," Elias whispered, urgent now, the fever climbing back into his voice. "There's a compass that goes with it. Markings on the bezel — star, notch, three lines. If you ever see such a compass, trust it. Trust it more than you trust me." His eyes searched Jude's face. "Promise me you'll go before the ice closes. Promise me the village sees it done."

"I promised once already."

"Promises are like shovels." Elias slumped back against the wall, smiling, his eyes drifting toward some middle distance where perhaps a glacier stood white under a hard blue sky. "They're only good if you swing them."

Jude sat with him while the fever climbed. Toward midnight the muttering started again — coordinates, always coordinates, and one phrase repeated like a bell buoy in fog: *"the village keeps it, the village keeps it."* The tavern girl brought water and said the priest had been sent for, which meant the priest had been sent for too late. Jude paid for the old man's room with three of his eleven coins, which was either charity or the first installment on a debt he didn't yet understand he'd taken on.

Before dawn, Elias Vane died with his hand resting on Jude's sleeve, and the last thing he said clearly enough to keep was:

"Check the markings, boy. Check the markings."

* * *

Jude walked out into the gray beginning of morning with the oilcloth packet inside his jacket, against his ribs, where it felt less like a possession than a coal. The rain had slackened to a fine drifting mist. Harbor gulls fought over fish guts on the levee, and the river ran high and brown with the first melt-swollen push of the season, hurrying somewhere with the impatience of all water.

In the lee of a customs shed, out of the wind, he untied the waxed string at last and opened the packet.

The map was drawn on sailcloth-backed paper, dense with a dead man's certainty: contour lines like fingerprints, a river system inked in confident strokes, and there — northwest, high, where the mountains crowded together — a glacier rendered in careful hatching, and at its shoulder a symbol like a gate. Around the border of the sheet, in a margin cramped as a prayer, ran a sequence of small marks repeated over and over, like a refrain.

A star. A notch. Three lines.

Jude's hands went cold in the warm morning air.

Slowly, watching the harbor traffic and not watching it, he drew out the compass. His father's compass. The brass worn bright along one edge where a thumb had worried it for years. He turned it over. He had turned it over ten thousand times and never once looked properly, the way you can live beside a door your whole life and never notice it opens.

On the underside of the bezel, cut shallow and old, were the markings.

A star. A notch. Three lines.

The needle swung, steadied, and pointed north — and beyond north, if the map was true, toward a glacier, a gate, and a village waiting under the ice for a promise that had taken nineteen years and one dying stranger to reach it.

Behind him, up the levee road, came the sound Jude had been dreading since the tavern: boot heels, several pairs, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly whom they were looking for.

Harken's men had found the port.
