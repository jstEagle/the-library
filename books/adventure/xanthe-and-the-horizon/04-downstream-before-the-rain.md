## 4. Downstream Before the Rain

The boat was called *Charity*, which Jude took as an omen of the bad kind.

She sat low in the brown water at Sallow Quay, tied to a post that leaned like a drunk against his friend, her hull streaked with green to the waterline and patched in at least four places with tin sheeting hammered over older rot. Her deck planks had the soft give of fruit past its season. A single mast, a stub of jib, a cabin you could stand in only if you were built like a heron.

"She floats," said the broker, a wide man named Doss whose shirt had once been white and whose smile had never been anything but yellow. "That's more than most can say this time of year."

"Does she steer?"

"She goes where the river wants," Doss said, "and mostly where you want. That's a marriage, that is."

Jude walked her length twice, pressing his thumb into the hull below the waterline until the wood dimpled and wept a bead of damp. He'd spent six years digging other men's trenches for other men's fortunes, and he knew rot the way a priest knows sin — intimately, and without much surprise anymore. The *Charity* was rotten in the way of things that had been loved badly: patched where it showed, neglected where it didn't.

He named a price that was half what Doss asked. Doss laughed. They settled somewhere between, paid in coin that had come from Elias Vane's oilcloth roll, and by the time the sun cleared the smoke haze over the quay, Jude owned a boat that would probably drown him.

Probably. Not certainly. He'd worked with worse odds.

He loaded her in the grey light before anyone could ask questions: rice in sealed tins, a coil of new rope, pitch and canvas for patching, a hand bilge pump whose leather gasket he replaced himself on the dock with a knife and a strip cut from his own boot. The map rode inside his shirt, folded small, against the skin over his heart, which was either sentiment or superstition or both. The compass rode beside it. His father's compass, its brass case worn bright along one edge where a thumb had worried it for thirty years.

*The markings match.* He still hadn't decided what to do with that knowledge. It sat in him like a swallowed stone.

"You're leaving ahead of the rain." Doss had drifted back, hands in pockets, watching the loading with the idle interest of a man who sold to desperate people often enough to recognize the species. "Monsoon's early this year. River pilots won't take contracts past Kiln Landing after tomorrow."

"I'm not hiring a pilot."

"No," Doss agreed pleasantly. "You don't look like a man who trusts anybody with his boat. Or his cargo." His eyes flicked, just once, toward Jude's chest, where the map lay hidden, and Jude felt the look land like a finger laid on bare skin.

"I'm carrying rice," Jude said.

"And I sell fish," said Doss, "fresh from the sea." He touched two fingers to his hat brim and wandered off, whistling, and Jude stood in the rising heat and understood that whatever Doss knew or guessed would be sold within the day, because everything in Sallow Quay was for sale except the rain, and the rain came free to everyone.

Good, then. Let it be sold. He'd be gone before the buyer arrived.

* * *

Harken found him anyway, because Harken always found him. That was the nature of debts; they had better legs than debtors.

Jude was three miles downstream when he heard the engine — a flat, ugly knocking under the birdsong, the sound of money idling. He looked back and saw the gunboat come around the bend with the morning sun flashing off its windshield, and standing in the bow with one hand on the rail and the other holding his hat against the wind, broad and patient as a courthouse, was Crew Boss Harken.

Jude swore, once, comprehensively, and opened the throttle.

The *Charity* responded the way an old dog responds to a whistle — with effort, and complaint, and not much speed. She wallowed up onto something resembling a plane, her bow throwing spray that immediately began finding its way through seams that had no business admitting daylight, let alone river. Water ticked into the bilge. Jude set the tiller with his knee, grabbed the hand pump, and pumped while he steered, which was exactly as graceful as it sounds.

The gunboat gained. Of course it gained. Harken's boat cost more than every vessel at Sallow Quay combined, and it carried forty horsepower and a crew of four, and behind the wheel sat a man who had once chased a runaway porter across nine miles of flooded paddy field purely on principle.

"HARKEN—" Jude shouted uselessly over the engine, because there was nothing useful to say.

The gunboat closed to fifty yards. Thirty. Close enough now to see faces: Harken at the rail, flanked by two of his trench men, Ferro and the boy they called Weevil, both looking deeply unhappy about the spray and the speed and the general direction their lives had taken. Harken raised a megaphone. Of course he had a megaphone. Harken believed in equipment.

"QUINTANA!" The name boomed off the water, off the drowned trees along the banks, off the egrets lifting in indignant clouds. "YOU OWE ME FOUR MONTHS' WAGES AND THE TRENCH GEAR YOU WALKED OFF WITH!"

"THE GEAR'S IN THE TRENCH!" Jude shouted back, which was true, and also the entire reason he'd left it there.

"THE MAP ISN'T!"

There it was. Jude's stomach dropped through the bilge and into the river. So Doss hadn't even needed a full day. Word traveled faster than boats in this country; word traveled faster than light, if the price was right.

"IT'S RICE!" Jude bellowed. "IT'S ALL RICE!"

Harken lowered the megaphone and did not answer, which was worse than any threat. He simply stood there at the rail, big and unhurried, letting the gunboat eat the distance while the *Charity* labored and leaked, and Jude understood with cold clarity that this was not a chase that would end at the next bend. Harken did not spend fuel he didn't intend to recover. He would follow downriver all day, all night if it came to that, until the leaky boat failed or the fugitive tired, and then he would board, and he would search, and he would find the oilcloth packet, and he would take it as payment, and Elias Vane's promise would die in a collector's strongbox in some city Jude had never seen.

Unless the river got wider first. Unless the rain got there first.

Jude looked upstream, past the pursuing gunboat, at the sky over the southern hills. It had gone the color of a bruise. The monsoon had been promised for a week, and everyone from Doss to the ferry women had said *tomorrow, tomorrow,* the way people say it about death and deliveries, and now the horizon wore the particular green-black shine that meant *today.*

A mad idea, then. The only kind he had.

He hauled the tiller hard to port.

The *Charity* swung her blunt nose off the main channel and into the cut — a side passage marked on no chart worth owning, a shortcut through the drowned forest that the ferry pilots used only in low water, when the snags showed themselves. In high water it was a slot machine that ate boats. But high water was coming whether he liked it or not, and right now, with the river still hours from flood, the snags were visible: black fingers breaking the surface, marking the channel like the bones of some enormous animal.

Behind him, the megaphone crackled with outrage. Then the engine note changed — Harken following, because of course he was following, because Harken had never in his life declined an invitation to make something worse.

* * *

The drowned forest was cathedral-quiet after the open river, and treacherous as a liar's memory. Jude steered by snag-shadows and instinct, throttle down to a crawl, the gunboat's heavier draft forcing it to hang back a hundred yards and pick its way. For twenty minutes neither boat spoke. The only sounds were water sliding along the hull, the tick of the bilge filling, and somewhere deep in the green, birds doing arithmetic.

Then the first squall line hit.

It came without ceremony — a wall of wind out of the south that flattened the reeds and turned the forest channels into moving rope. Rain arrived not in drops but in sheets, in volumes, as though the sky had been holding a grudge all season and had finally been given permission. Visibility went to nothing. The compass on its lanyard spun and steadied and spun again, sulking in the storm's magnetic tantrum, and Jude steered by feel and prayer, the two instruments available to a man in his position.

The *Charity* shipped water over the bow in solid green loads. The bilge rose past his ankles. He pumped with one hand, steered with the other, and shouted navigation instructions to God, who did not appear to be taking notes.

Something struck the hull — a log, a snag, a submerged church roof, he'd never know — with a blow that shuddered the whole boat and knocked him to his knees in the sloshing bilge. By the time he got upright, the gunboat's engine noise had changed character: no longer the flat patient knock of pursuit but a ragged, coughing stutter, and then, faintly through the rain, shouting. Human shouting. The kind that isn't about maps.

Jude hung there in the maelstrom, one hand on the tiller, and made the calculation that would define him for years afterward: he could run. The storm was his ally now; the cut forked ahead into three dark passages, and Harken's swamped gunboat would be lucky to keep its own bow pointed downstream. No one would blame him. Harken had chased him into a monsoon over a debt; whatever happened next was arithmetic, not mercy.

But Weevil was seventeen. And Ferro couldn't swim — everyone on the trench crews knew Ferro couldn't swim, it was the standing joke that wasn't funny.

Jude swore at length, at volume, at the universe in general, and turned the *Charity* back into the rain.

He found them by sound. The gunboat lay heeled hard against a snag island, her stern already kissing the waterline, her crew bailing with hats and buckets while the river poured in through a stove plank with the cheerful efficiency of all disasters. Harken stood waist-deep in his own sinking fortune, still wearing his hat, still somehow commanding, roaring orders at men who were beyond orders.

"TAKE MY LINE!" Jude bellowed, and threw the new rope coil across the gap.

What followed was fifteen minutes of the least dignified rescue in riverine history. Ferro went first, white-faced and silent, hauled through the rain and dumped gasping on the *Charity*'s deck. Then Weevil, who cried without embarrassment and apologized for it in the same breath. Harken came last, because Harken would, wading across the rope hand over hand with the gunboat's ledger box held above the water like a relic, and stepped aboard Jude's leaking tub as though boarding a flagship.

For a moment the four of them stood in the flooding bilge of the *Charity*, rain hammering the cabin roof, the gunboat settling behind them with a long sigh of surrender, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing that covered it.

Harken removed his hat, emptied a cup of river water from it, and put it back on.

"You sank my boat," he said.

"The river sank your boat. I offered you a rope."

"You owe me four months' wages."

"Ferro owes me his life. Call it a wash and bail."

And here was the thing Jude would turn over later, in the dark, in the months after: Harken actually considered it. Jude watched him consider it — watched the man's eyes move from the rising water to the bailing bucket to the two dripping men already hauling at the bilge, and something almost like reason crossed the broad weathered face. Almost.

Then Harken's gaze dropped to Jude's chest, to the shape beneath the wet shirt, and reason packed its bags.

"The map," Harken said quietly, under the rain's roar, so the others wouldn't hear. "You've got Vane's map. Everyone from here to the delta knows it. You dug my trench for four months and never once told me your surname until the collectors said it out loud, and now you're running downriver ahead of the flood with a dead man's treasure chart sewn to your ribs." He shook his head slowly, almost sadly. "I liked you, Quintana. That was my mistake, and I'll correct it. Give me the map and I'll put you ashore at Kiln Landing with your debts cleared and my mouth shut. Keep it, and I'll follow you to the ice line itself. I've got money. Money buys boats, and boats buy rivers."

The *Charity* groaned beneath them, shipping another load over the bow. The bilge was at mid-shin. Somewhere ahead, through the rain-curtain, the cut would empty into the lower river, and the lower river would be rising fast, and every hour of daylight was an hour the vault route stayed open and the calving window stayed shut.

Jude thought about Elias Vane dying in a tavern bunk, pressing an oilcloth packet into his hands with fingers like old roots. *Not to collectors. To the village. Swear it, boy, swear it on something you haven't lost yet.*

He'd sworn on the compass. On the one thing his father had left him that wasn't a name.

"No," Jude said.

Harken nodded, unsurprised, and picked up a bailing bucket, and for the rest of that endless afternoon the four of them bailed the *Charity* down the flooded cut together — debtor and creditor, thief and rescuer, each keeping his own private ledger — while the monsoon came down on all of them equally, indifferent as gravity, generous as ruin.

They reached the lower river at dusk, riding a current gone from brown to boiling, and the last Jude saw of the drowned forest was the gunboat's mast tip disappearing under a snag island with a sound like a coffin lid.

Harken watched it go with an expression Jude couldn't read. Then he looked at Jude, and the expression resolved into something perfectly legible after all.

"Kiln Landing has pilots," Harken said, over the roar of the flood. "Pilots have memories. You'll need one, going upriver into the ice country, and every pilot on this water owes somebody money. Guess who's been buying paper all week."

Jude's hands went cold on the tiller, colder than the rain could account for.

Ahead, through the failing light, the lights of Kiln Landing were just beginning to show — a scatter of lamps along a black levee, warm and unreachable as stars — and somewhere among them, he would learn much later, a river pilot named Mara Oduya was watching the flood come down and deciding, for reasons of her own, not to close her door.
