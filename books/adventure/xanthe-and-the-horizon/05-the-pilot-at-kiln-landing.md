## 5. The Pilot at Kiln Landing

Mara Oduya could smell a lie before it finished walking up the gangplank, and the man coming toward her now wore one like cheap cologne.

She sat on the rail of the *Sable Tern*, her feet hooked through the stanchions, mending a net she had no intention of using. Kiln Landing smelled the way it always did in the last week before the rains — woodsmoke and wet rope and the sweet rot of fruit nobody would buy — and every season it produced the same species of passenger: the kind with more money than sense, or less money than nerve. This one was trying to pass for neither.

He came down the plank with a canvas duffel over one shoulder and a rolled oilskin under his arm, and he stopped three meters short of her boat as if measuring the distance to a cliff edge. Tall, lean in the way of men who forgot to eat when they worked, hands scarred in the pattern she'd learned to read years ago on riverbanks and dig sites — rope burns across the palms, old cuts from stone, a splinter of something dark still lodged under the nail of his left index finger. A digger's hands. A hired shovel.

"You're Oduya," he said. Not a question. "They said you run the upper river."

"They say a lot of things." She didn't look up from the net. "Mostly wrong."

"I need to go upstream. Past the confluence. As far as you'll take me."

"Nobody goes past the confluence this time of year."

"I'll pay for as far as you'll take me."

That was the tell, right there. Men who led with money were men who thought everything ended in money, and Mara had spent eleven years learning what the river did to people who believed that. She let the net fall into her lap and finally looked at him properly. Younger than his hands. Eyes the color of tea left too long in the pot, and tired behind them in a way that sleep didn't fix.

"Where past the confluence?" she asked.

"Tsering."

The name landed on the deck between them like a dropped tool. Mara kept her face still, but something behind her ribs shifted its weight. Tsering. The glacier village. The place Elias Vane had limped out of two winters ago with frostbitten fingers and a story he told only once, drunk, about a vault under the ice that swallowed treasure and men alike. The place her brother's grave was a day's walk below.

"Nobody goes to Tsering," she said. "The ice road won't open for weeks, and the river above the falls is suicide in pre-monsoon water."

"The map says otherwise."

He unrolled the oilskin without waiting for permission, and there it was — a hand-drawn chart, ink gone brown with age, rivers rendered in confident strokes that no surveyor would have approved and no pilot would have argued with. She knew the hand. She'd watched it draw charts just like this one on the deck of another boat, in another life, while rain hammered the canopy and a man with her father's laugh traced routes nobody else dared.

Mara stood up slowly. "Who gave you this?"

"A dying man named Vane. Why?"

"Because I've seen this handwriting before." She tapped the margin, where someone had annotated the river's course in a smaller, tighter script: *ferry crossing unreliable after third week — take east channel, trust the kilns' smoke.* "Eleven years ago. On a boat called the *Meridian*. With a man who owed me—"

She stopped herself. He was watching her now with an expression she recognized too well, because she'd worn it herself for a decade: the look of someone bracing for a blow they'd been expecting since childhood.

"He was my father," the man said quietly.

The word hung there. *Father.* Mara had imagined this meeting a hundred times over eleven years — usually at night, usually angry — and in none of those imaginings had the son looked so much like him around the eyes. Same hollows. Same way of holding still when the world got loud, as if stillness were armor.

"Jude Quintana," he said, and offered a hand she didn't take.

"Mara Oduya." She stepped back onto her deck, putting the gunwale between them. "And no."

"No?"

"No, I won't take your fare, Mr. Quintana. Not upstream, not downstream, not across this landing to save your life." She began coiling the net with sharp, deliberate motions. "Find another boat."

"There are no other boats. Every pilot on this stretch has already pulled their hulls for the rains."

"Then walk."

"It's four hundred kilometers to the highlands."

"Good exercise."

He didn't argue. That was the second thing she noticed about him — the first being the eyes — because most men argued. Most men would have leaned on the name, or the money, or the simple arithmetic of desperation. Instead Jude Quintana stood on the mud-slick planks of Kiln Landing and studied her the way he'd probably study a trench wall: patiently, looking for the seam.

"You knew him," he said at last. "My father."

"I knew a man by that name. Whether he was your father is between you and whatever letters he never sent."

Something flickered across his face — pain, quickly filed away behind competence. She'd seen that too, on her own reflection, for years.

"He's dead," Jude said. "Two years now. Buried in a pauper's field outside Meridian City, if you can call that burial. I didn't go. I was digging in the Karst Delta and I told myself the fare wasn't worth it." His jaw tightened. "So if you have things to say to him, you're talking to the wrong Quintana. But if you know the river above the falls — really know it, not the way the maps know it — then you're the only person on this landing who does, and I'm asking. Not hiring. Asking."

Mara tied off the net and looked at the sky instead of him. Low cloud moving in from the southwest, fat and gray, dragging its belly across the ridgeline. Three days, maybe four, until the first real rain. After that the river would rise two meters and turn brown and hungry, and anything floating on it would be gambling.

She should say no. Every instinct built over eleven years of piloting said no — no passengers with dead fathers' maps, no cargo that made her chest tight, no routes that ended at glaciers where her brother lay under a cairn she'd never visited because visiting meant admitting the search was over.

But the compass.

She hadn't seen it yet. She was almost sure he carried it, because Elias Vane had described it to her once, drunk again, weeping again — *the boy has the compass, the old man's compass, brass case, cracked crystal, needle that doesn't point north* — and if that compass was in Jude Quintana's pocket, then this wasn't a job anymore. It was a debt coming due.

"Show me your kit," she said abruptly.

"What?"

"Your gear. Everything you're carrying. If I'm going to consider drowning with you, I want to know what you'll be dragging down with us."

He hesitated — a half-second too long, the hesitation of a man deciding whether to trust her — and then set the duffel on the deck and opened it. Rope, pitons, a coil of wire, dried food wrapped in waxed cloth, a folding saw, a tin of matches that had gotten wet once and survived. And at the bottom, wrapped in a leather rag, something round.

Mara's breath went shallow.

He unwrapped it without understanding why she'd asked, and held it up to the flat gray light: a brass compass, heavy, old, the crystal cracked in a spiderweb from its center outward. The needle swung lazily, settled, and pointed — not north. Northeast, and slightly down, as if the thing were trying to burrow into the earth itself.

"Do you know why it doesn't point north?" she asked.

"I assumed it was broken."

"It's not broken." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "It's a lodestone housing. Someone rebuilt it — counterweighted the needle, added a mass underneath. It points at iron. At a specific deposit of iron, buried deep, somewhere northeast of here." She looked up at him. "Your father built this. I watched him build it. We were anchored off Serpent Bend and he worked three nights straight, filing the counterweight out of a broken engine valve, swearing at it in two languages, and when it finally settled he laughed and said—" 

Her throat closed.

"Said what?" Jude asked.

"That he'd finally built something that couldn't lie to anyone." Mara turned away, busying her hands with the mooring line, because if she kept looking at that compass she was going to say things she hadn't decided to say. "I ran the river with your father for two seasons, Mr. Quintana. Survey work, salvage, one job I still don't talk about. He was the best field man I ever shipped with, and then one morning at Kiln Landing — this landing — he was gone. Left his share of the boat, left his gear, left me a note that said *don't follow* and owed me answers I've been collecting interest on for eleven years."

"And now?"

"Now you show up with his compass and a dead prospector's map, headed for the exact stretch of river where my brother drowned looking for the same thing your father walked away from." She faced him. "So understand what I'm telling you when I say yes. I'm not taking your fare. I'm taking a debt. Yours and his both, and I intend to collect."

Jude Quintana looked at her for a long moment. Then he repacked the duffel, carefully, the way careful men do when their hands need something honest to hold, and said, "What do you need from me?"

"Three days of work before we cast off. Caulk, pitch, new planking on the port side — she's rotten and you'll learn her seams if you're going to sail her. You cook. You bail. You don't touch the wheel, you don't ask about my brother, and when I give an order on the water, your body obeys before your mouth does." She stuck out her hand at last, rough palm up. "Those are my terms."

He took it. His grip was dry and strong, and for a moment neither of them let go, each recognizing in the other the same stubborn architecture of grief.

"One question," he said as they broke apart. "Why hide it? If you knew him — if you have history — why spend ten minutes pretending you'd refuse any fare?"

Mara looked past him, upriver, where the clouds were stacking themselves against the peaks like a debt coming due.

"Because if I'd told you the truth on the gangplank," she said, "you'd have known I needed this trip more than you do. And a pilot who needs a voyage is a pilot who makes bad decisions." She turned and started forward, tossing the words over her shoulder. "Now get aboard, Quintana. We've got three days, a rotten hull, and a monsoon that doesn't care whose father we're chasing."

* * *

They worked the next three days like people outrunning something.

Mara taught him the *Sable Tern* seam by seam — which planks sang when the hull flexed, which ones had gone soft enough to press a thumbnail into, where the bilge pump lived and how many strokes per minute it needed to stay ahead of the river's patience. Jude learned fast, silently, the way she suspected he learned everything: as though knowledge were rations to be hoarded. By the second day he could caulk a seam without being shown twice. By the third he'd re-planked a meter of the port bow and only swore twice.

She caught herself watching him. The angle of his head when he concentrated was wrong — too much like the other Quintana — and she made herself stop looking.

On the second evening, rain arrived early as a rumor: a warm drizzle that turned the landing's lanterns into smudges. They sat under the boat's canvas awning, sharing a pot of rice and dried fish, and Jude asked the question she'd been waiting for since the gangplank.

"My father," he said. "On the river. What was he like?"

Mara fed a stick into the small brazier and watched it catch. "Careful," she said. "You mean that as a trap for me, but it's mostly a trap for you."

"Probably."

"He was brilliant and he was a coward, and the two weren't separate things. He could read water better than any man I've sailed with — could look at a riffle and tell you what the bottom looked like fifty years ago. But when it came time to stand still and be known, he'd rather run." She poked the fire. "You have his hands. Let's find out if you have his feet."

"And my brother?" Jude asked quietly. "You said he drowned up there."

Mara was silent long enough that the rain filled it. "Tomas went up the icefields six years ago with a survey team. Looking for the same vault, or something like it — he never said exactly, and I never asked hard enough. The river brought back two of the five." She turned the stick in the fire. "I signed on to this trip to finish his route. That's all you need to know, and it's more than I planned to tell you."

"Why tell me at all, then?"

"Because you'll hear it eventually from someone in Tsering, and I'd rather you heard it from me than make up a kinder version." She met his eyes across the brazier. "We're not friends yet, Quintana. We're two people on the same boat with the same ghosts. Don't confuse the two."

He nodded slowly, and she saw him file it away — the same patient archiving she'd already learned to expect from him.

"Fair enough," he said. "But for the record — I'm not chasing my father. I'm chasing a promise I made to a dying man. Whatever my father wanted from that vault, he can keep wanting it underground."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. "We'll see," she said, and doused the fire.

* * *

They cast off on the fourth morning, ahead of the rain by half a day, and the river took them like it takes everything — without malice and without mercy.

Kiln Landing fell away behind them in a smear of smoke and gray water, and Mara stood at the wheel feeling the *Sable Tern* settle into the current, testing her seams, listening to the hull with her whole body the way a doctor listens to a chest. One plank aft of the new work ticked faintly. Manageable. For now.

Jude stood at the bow with the compass open in his palm, letting the needle drag its invisible thread northeast, toward iron, toward ice, toward everything either of them had lost.

"Don't navigate by that thing," she called forward. "It points at graves."

"So do I," he said, without turning around, "these days."

Mara gripped the wheel and said nothing, because the current was picking up, the sky was lowering, and somewhere behind them — she could feel it the way you feel weather in a scar — trouble was coming downriver too, in a faster boat, wearing a crew boss's anger.

She opened the throttle. The *Sable Tern* answered.

Ahead, the first drops of the monsoon struck the water like fingers drumming on a coffin lid.
