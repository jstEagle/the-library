## 6. Monsoon Rising

The sky had been lying to them all morning.

Jude stood at the tiller of the *Kestrel* — a name painted on her stern by some optimistic previous owner, because the boat was less a kestrel than a wounded duck — and watched the clouds stack themselves over the western hills like slate being loaded onto a cart. They were the color of bruises. The river had gone strange beneath them, flat and oily, the current slowing as if it too were holding its breath.

"That's not rain," Mara said from the bow, where she'd spent an hour re-lashing their cargo under oilcloth. "That's a wall."

"How long?"

She didn't answer at once. She was looking upstream, past him, reading the water the way he read strata — for what it concealed, for what it admitted despite itself. A line of white foam was moving downriver toward them, impossibly straight, like a seam being ripped open across the whole width of the channel.

"An hour," she said. "Maybe less. That's the front edge." She turned and pointed at a break in the treeline on the left bank — a mudflat, gray and uninviting, backed by jungle so dense it looked solid. "We can beach there and wait it out."

"And lose half a day."

"Yes."

Jude looked at the foam line again. He thought about Harken's gunboat somewhere behind them, about the debt that grew interest every hour like mold in the wet season, about Elias Vane's map folded in the oilskin packet against his chest. He thought about how his father had always said the difference between a fool and a hero was mostly timing.

"We run ahead of it," Jude said. "There's a bend coming up — you said the channel narrows there. If we're through before it hits—"

"If we're not through when it hits," Mara said, "we are in the worst water on this river with zero visibility and a hull that leaks when it's *calm*."

"I patched the leaks."

"You patched two of nine."

The first drop hit the deck between them, fat and warm as a flung coin. Then another. Then the sky simply opened, all at once, the way a sack opens when the bottom gives out, and the world went white with noise.

Mara didn't argue further. She moved — fast, economical, no wasted motion — hauling the jib down while Jude fought the tiller to keep the bow into the building chop. The wind arrived a breath behind the rain and hit the sailcloth even furled like a fist against a door. The *Kestrel* lurched, took green water over the port rail, and Jude heard, beneath the roar, the sound he had been dreading since Kiln Landing: water finding its way below, unhurried, patient, certain.

"The bilge!" Mara shouted, and he couldn't hear her, but he could read her mouth, and he went.

* * *

Below decks was a different country. The cabin was four feet of headroom lit by a single lamp that swung on its hook like a pendulum marking the end of something. Water ran along the seams of the planking in bright threads, a dozen of them, two dozen, converging in the bilge where maybe six inches had already collected and sloshed against the frames with every roll.

Jude found the bail bucket lashed to the mast partner and started throwing water up through the companionway into the storm, which accepted each offering and immediately returned more.

It was, he thought wildly, the most honest transaction of his life.

He bailed. The water rose anyway. He could measure the losing race by the lamp: each time the boat rolled, the light swung wide and caught the shine of the bilge surface a little higher on the frames. Six inches became eight. His shoulders burned. Rain hammered the deck above his head like a crowd demanding entry.

Then Mara came down the ladder, soaked through, hair plastered to her skull, and without a word took the second bucket from under the thwart, and they bailed together.

They worked in rhythm because they had to — one filling while the other threw, passing the buckets hand to hand in the dark, and the rhythm required trust the way a bridge requires piers. You could not hesitate. You could not check whether the other person was keeping their side. You just kept yours and believed.

It was Mara who broke the silence, shouting over the storm the way people shout things they might not say otherwise.

"My brother's name was Tomas!"

Jude nearly missed the bucket. "What?"

"Tomas! You asked me at Kiln Landing why I knew your father's compass and I said it wasn't my story. It's *his*. Was." She threw a full bucket up the companionway; the wind threw half of it back. "Your father carried him forty miles down a mountain with his leg opened to the bone. Carried him. Fed him his own rations. Sat up three nights digging the infection out of that wound with a knife heated in a fire. And then one morning he was gone, and he never sent word, and Tomas died two years later still waiting for him to come back and explain!"

The boat rolled hard. The lamp swung. In its arc of light Jude saw her face — furious and wet and younger than she let herself look on the river.

"Why are you telling me this now?" he shouted back.

"Because if we sink tonight I am not going to drown holding it!" She thrust the bucket at him. "Bail!"

He bailed. The secrets came up with the water, bucket by bucket, as if the storm had reached into both of them and decided that anything not nailed down was going overboard.

* * *

Near midnight the lightning stopped being weather and became architecture. Every few seconds the whole world was drawn in white lines — the river a silver road running uphill, the banks gone, the rain a curtain of beads — and then erased again. In one of those flashes Jude saw the bilge and understood with cold clarity that they were going to lose.

Eight inches had become ten. The pump — a ancient brass thing Mara had sworn worked — had choked on grit an hour ago and refused everything since. His hands were shredded from the bucket handle. Mara's lips had gone gray. They were bailing the ocean with a thimble, and the ocean knew it.

"We can't keep this up!" he yelled.

"No!" she agreed, cheerfully, horribly.

"What do we do?"

"Same thing! Until we think of better!"

And that — God help him — steadied him. Because it was such a stupid plan, and she offered it with such complete conviction, that it circled around through absurdity and came out the other side as faith. He laughed, actually laughed, a wild bark of a sound torn away by the wind, and kept bailing.

Somewhere in the next hour — he would never be able to reconstruct exactly when — the arithmetic changed. Not because they bailed faster. Because the storm, having spent its fury on the open reach, began to spend itself on land instead, and the wind dropped from a scream to a shout, and the shout to a mutter, and the rain from a wall to a sheet to a steady drumming. The river settled from chaos into merely violent. And the *Kestrel*, ten inches of water in her belly, rode it like a tired animal that has decided, for tonight, not to die.

They bailed until the bilge showed bare frames. Then they bailed a little more, because neither of them quite trusted it. Then Mara sat down on the thwart, put her head back against the mast, and slept — instantly, completely, mid-sentence, like a lamp blown out.

Jude sat in the dripping dark and listened to the boat live.

* * *

Dawn came gray and washed-out, the river high and brown and full of traveling islands — whole trees sailing past with their roots waving. The storm had rearranged the world overnight: new sandbars where channels had been, channels where sandbars had been. Mara stood at the bow with her sounding pole, reading the changed water, and did not speak for a long time.

When she finally did, her voice was level again, the night's confession packed away somewhere below her waterline.

"There's a flooded church two bends down. Old mission, abandoned fifty years. Stone walls — the roof's gone but the floor's above flood level. We can haul out, dry the boat, patch her properly." She glanced back at him. "Properly means all nine leaks, not your two."

"All nine," Jude agreed.

They worked the *Kestrel* downriver through the debris-strewn current, and the storm's wreckage told its own stories as they passed — a roof floating intact like a raft, a dead cow wedged in a tree twenty feet up, a child's doll riding a log. Jude found himself thinking about Elias Vane, dying in a port tavern, trading away the only treasure he had left: not the map, but the promise. Making someone else carry it. *Is that what my father did too?* he wondered. *Hand off the weight and vanish?*

"You're doing your brooding face," Mara said without turning around.

"I don't have a brooding face."

"You have a face like a man reading a bill he can't pay. You've worn it since Kiln Landing." She planted the pole and swung the bow. "Whatever you're carrying — the map, the debt, the father — it'll keep until the church. Storms have a way of sorting cargo. What matters floats. What doesn't, sinks. We'll see which is which."

The bend opened, and there it was: a stone tower rising out of the flooded forest, green with moss, its bell arch empty, the nave around it transformed into a lagoon where the water lay mirror-still among drowned columns. It looked less like a ruin than like a held breath.

Mara brought the boat alongside the old landing steps, and as Jude made fast, she said quietly:

"When we're patched and moving again, I'll tell you the rest about Tomas. All of it. Even the parts that won't make your father look better."

"Why?"

"Because last night you bailed until your hands bled and you never once complained, and you never once tried to take the tiller from me." She stepped onto the stones and looked up at the tower. "That's more than your father ever gave anyone. Time someone in your family paid into the account instead of drawing it down."

Jude stood on the landing steps beside her, the ruined church rising silent out of its lagoon, and felt the words land somewhere deeper than argument could reach.

Then Mara stiffened. Her eyes had gone downstream, past the church, to the far bend — where, small and hard and unmistakable in the clearing haze, a gunboat was coming upriver against the current, flying no flag at all.

"Harken," she said. "He didn't wait out the storm either."
