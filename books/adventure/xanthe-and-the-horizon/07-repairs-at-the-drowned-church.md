## 7. Repairs at the Drowned Church

The river told them where to stop before either of them said a word.

Jude saw it first — a pale geometry rising out the brown water on the left bank, like the ribs of something enormous surfacing to breathe. Then the current itself changed its mind, curling back on itself in a long slow eddy behind the ruin, and the boat, which had fought them for two days, all but steered itself into the calm.

"Church," Mara said, cutting the engine. The sudden silence rang in Jude's ears. "Or what the river left of one."

She let the hull drift against the flooded stone and Jude threw the line around a pillar that must have been a doorpost once, its carvings worn to suggestion — a figure, maybe, or a flower, or just the river's own handwriting. Water lapped at the lintel. Beyond it, the nave stretched back into shadow, flooded to knee-depth, its vaulted ceiling half-collapsed, open to a sky the color of wet tin.

"How far to the next landing?" Jude asked.

"Two days, if the hull holds. Three if it doesn't." Mara was already hauling their gear onto the highest dry ledge, moving with the economy of someone who had made this calculation a hundred times. "It doesn't. You've been bailing since dawn."

"I've been bailing since Kiln Landing."

"Then you know the arithmetic." She crouched at the gunwale and ran her palm along the seam below the waterline, and when she brought her hand back it was shining. "She's opened up. We beach her, we patch her, or we swim."

The rain, which had been threatening since morning, arrived without ceremony — not the monsoon proper, not yet, but its advance scouts, a fine gray drizzle that made the river steam. Jude looked at the drowned church, at its patient stones, at the water standing in its aisles like a congregation that had never left.

"Cozy," he said.

"It's dry enough, high enough, and hidden enough." Mara swung her pack down. "Harken's boats draw too much water to come in past the shallows. If he's behind us, this is the last place he'll look."

*If he's behind us.* Jude didn't say what they were both thinking — that Harken wasn't *if*, he was *when*. The crew boss had a gunboat and a grudge and a ledger in his head where Jude's debt lived, compounding daily. Two days out of Kiln Landing, and Jude had spent every one of them listening for engines.

They beached the boat on a shelf of stone inside the ruin itself, where the vaulted roof still held, and Jude stood for a moment in the strange light — river-light, filtered through water and broken masonry, green and gold and moving. Fish moved through the doorway in small silver schools, unbothered by the architecture. Somewhere above, a bird had built a nest in the eye of a fallen dome.

"People worshipped here," he said.

"People worshipped everywhere, once. Before the river took the valley." Mara had already found what they needed — a shelf of old pitch, gone hard as glass in a clay jar some mason had left behind centuries ago, and a cache of bamboo strips in a corner that would do for splints. She set to work without reverence, and Jude understood: for her, the church wasn't a ruin. It was a workshop the river happened to furnish.

He stripped to the waist and waded in to tip the hull, and the cold took his breath. Working by feel, they found the wound — a seam split along two feet of planking, the caulking rotted out, the wood underneath soft as bread. Bad. Not fatal, but bad.

"Patch it or replace it," Mara said. "We don't have wood to replace it."

"Then we patch it."

"Then you hold and I pitch, and neither of us complains about the cold."

They worked through the afternoon while the rain thickened. Jude held the splints against the hull's belly with his arms half-numb, and Mara heated the old pitch over a spirit stove until it ran like honey, and the smell of it — tar and smoke and something older, mineral, the church's own breath — filled the flooded nave. She worked fast and precisely, and he found himself watching her hands: brown, scarred across the knuckles, absolutely sure of themselves.

"You've done this before," he said.

"I've done everything before. That's what the river does. It makes you a generalist or a corpse."

"Poetic."

"I'm a pilot, not a poet." But something almost like a smile crossed her face, there and gone, like a fish under the surface. "My brother was the poet."

The words landed somewhere in Jude's chest and stayed there. He held the splint. He said nothing. The river said everything, lapping at the pillars, patient as a creditor.

* * *

By dusk the patch was holding and the rain had settled into a steady drumming on the ruined roof, and Mara built a small fire on the highest ledge, under the one intact corner of vaulting, where the smoke could find its way out through the broken dome. They ate rice and dried fish and drank river water boiled with a twist of some piney leaf she'd gathered from the banks, and the church filled up around them with firelight and shadow.

Jude had been turning something over all afternoon, and the fire finally loosened it out of him.

"At Kiln Landing," he said. "When you saw the compass. You looked at it like you'd been slapped."

Mara fed a stick to the fire. She didn't answer right away, and Jude had learned in two days on the water that her silences were not empty — they were load-bearing.

"You know whose it is," he said. "You knew before I told you."

"Your father's name was Aldous Quintana." She said it flatly, the way you'd read a name off a cargo manifest. "Yes. I knew."

"Then you know what he was."

"I know what they *say* he was." She looked up, and the firelight made her eyes hard to read. "The fraud. The forger. The man who sold a dozen collectors fake provenance and vanished with the money. That's the story on the river, in the port towns, in the dig camps. That's the story you've been carrying like a stone in your pocket since you were old enough to carry anything."

Jude's jaw tightened. "You have a different one?"

"I have my own." Mara turned the stick in the fire. "I had a brother. Tomas. Younger than me by four years, smarter than me by twice that, and reckless the way only clever people are reckless. He ran cargo on the upper river — not the worst kind, not the best. One monsoon season, maybe twelve years ago, his boat went down in the Kess Gorge. Swamped. He and two others clinging to the wreck through the night, water rising."

The rain filled the pause. Jude found he was holding his breath.

"There was a man camped on the gorge wall that season. A scholar-type, alone, surveying something in the cliffs — everyone said he was mad, up there in the wet with his notebooks. When the word came downriver about the wreck, he didn't wait for the rescue barges. He went up the gorge in the dark, in the rain, with rope he'd bought off fishermen, and he went down the cliff face to the wreck twice. Twice." She held up two fingers, and the firelight shivered across them. "First time he brought up one of the crew, half-drowned. Second time, Tomas. The third time he went down, the rock gave way, and he nearly went with them. They pulled him out on the end of his own rope, broken-armed, and the first thing he asked was whether everyone was up."

Jude's hands had gone still in his lap.

"Twelve years ago," Mara said. "I was twenty. I sat with him while they splinted his arm — he wouldn't go to the clinic, said he had to get back up the cliff to his camp before the weather closed. And I asked him why. Why go down for strangers, twice, three times, in the dark. And he laughed — this quiet laugh, like he was embarrassed — and he said, 'Because I know what it is to be written off. Nobody should drown in a story other people tell about them.'"

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the flooded nave, water shifted against stone with a sound like a long exhale.

"I owed him," Mara said. "Tomas owed him his life. I told him so. I told him: whatever you're doing up in those cliffs, whatever you're looking for, when you come back down, come find me. I'll run you anywhere on this river, free, for the rest of my life. And he said he'd remember." Her jaw worked. "He shook my hand on it. Aldous Quintana shook my hand on it."

"And then?"

"Then he vanished. Not that season — the next. The story broke about the forgeries, the collectors, the whole disgrace, and Aldous Quintana went upriver and off the face of the earth, and I never saw him again." She looked at Jude, and her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "I spent twelve years hearing what a fraud he was and knowing what he did on that cliff, and the two stories wouldn't fit in the same skull. So when a half-drowned stranger walked up my gangplank with his compass in his pocket — *his* compass, the one with the Kesh-Tamar markings, the one he never let out of his sight even with a broken arm — I needed to know which story was true before I decided what I owed you."

Jude stared into the fire. The heat of it didn't seem to reach him.

"That doesn't sound like him," he said finally, and hated how young his voice came out. "The man on the cliff. The man who — " He stopped. Started again. "My father taught me to read a contour map before I could read words. He'd take me out to the lowland digs and show me how the ground lied, how it looked flat and wasn't, how you had to learn to see through it. He was — " The word caught. "He was *good*. That's what I can't get past, all these years. He wasn't some bungler who got caught. He was the best field archaeologist I ever saw, and he threw it away, and he let everyone — he let *me* — believe the worst."

"Did you?" Mara asked. "Believe the worst?"

"I built a life on it." Jude laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know why I dig other people's trenches? Because I won't touch anything with my name on it. Because the last thing Aldous Quintana gave me was a name that burns, and a compass, and a note that said *I'll explain when I'm back* — and he never came back. So I decided the explanation was: he was exactly what they said. It was simpler. It hurt less than hoping."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a drowned church listening to a woman tell me he pulled her brother off a wreck with a broken rope and a broken arm, and I don't know what to do with it." He looked at her. "Why are you telling me this? You could have kept it. You could have let me go on thinking — "

"Because you bailed all night in the storm and never once asked whose fault the leak was." Mara's voice softened by a degree, no more. "Because you held that splint for four hours in river water without complaining. And because I've spent twelve years wanting to ask Aldous Quintana a question, and he's not here to answer it. So I'm asking you instead. Not the son. The man on the boat."

"What's the question?"

She leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her face like water.

"Your father went upriver looking for something. Everyone says it was gold, or glory, or a way to pay back what he stole. But I stood on that cliff and I watched him go down a rock face in the dark for strangers, and I don't believe a man like that climbs toward a vault for money." Her eyes held his. "What was he looking for, Jude? Do you know? Did he ever tell you anything — anything at all — before the end?"

And Jude opened his mouth to say *no*, to say *he never told me anything*, and stopped.

Because the compass was in his pack. And the compass had markings that matched a dying prospector's map. And his father had spent his last years surveying the Kess cliffs alone, half-mad, in the wet, with his notebooks — the same cliffs, Mara had said, the same river, the same country where a map to the Sunken Vault of Kesh-Tamar now sat folded in Jude's jacket, bought with a promise to a glacier village his father might have stood in.

"He was surveying," Jude said slowly. "You said it yourself. Everyone thought he was mad. But he wasn't mad. He was *looking*." The thought assembled itself as he spoke, piece by piece, like the hull coming back together under their hands. "Elias Vane drew that map from memory of a route no one's sailed in a century. And my father — my father was in these same gorges twelve years ago with a compass that matches it. Mara, I don't think my father came upriver to hide from what he'd done. I think he came upriver to fix it. Whatever he sold, whatever he forged — I think he was trying to find something worth more than the lie. Something he could give back."

Mara was very still. "Or you're telling yourself a story."

"Maybe." Jude looked into the fire. "But it's the first story about him that doesn't make me want to burn my own name off."

The rain drummed. The river breathed through the church. And Mara reached into her coat and brought out something Jude had never seen her wear or handle — a small brass token on a cord, worn smooth, stamped with a river pilot's guild mark and a number. She turned it over once in her fingers.

"Tomas gave me this after. Said a debt like that doesn't die with the man; it just waits for someone to carry it." She looked at the token, then at Jude, and something in her face settled, like a boat finding its trim. "I signed onto this voyage to find out which story was true. I'm still not sure. But I'm done waiting on the bank to decide."

She stood and walked to the beached boat, checking the patch by lantern light, pressing her thumb along the seam — and stopped.

"Jude."

He came over. Her thumb was resting on the new pitch, just aft of the patch, where the lantern's angle caught the hull's old scarred planking — and beneath the river grime, half-erased, was a brand. A surveyor's mark, burned into the wood years ago.

He knew it instantly. He'd know it anywhere. He'd grown up watching his father press it into tent pegs and instrument cases and the spines of field notebooks.

The boat they'd bought cheap at a stranger's dock, two days ago, in a hurry, without questions — had been his father's.

Mara looked at him, and the firelight behind her made her face unreadable, and out in the dark of the flooded nave the river kept moving, patient, carrying everything toward the ice.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you buy this boat?"
