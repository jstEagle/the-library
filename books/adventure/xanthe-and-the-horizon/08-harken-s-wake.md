## 8. Harken's Wake

Mara heard the engine before she saw it — a low, greedy throb rolling up the river like a fist knocking on a door.

She was at the tiller before her mind finished the thought. That was the thing about rivers: they taught you to let your body answer first and your questions later. The *Kestrel* was wallowing under full canvas of patched sail and half her oars shipped, riding low with water still seeping through seams that three days at the Drowned Church had only mostly closed. Behind them, around the long green shoulder of an island called Serpent's Tooth by the pilots who bothered naming things, came a boat that did not wallow.

"Jude." She said it once, flat, the way you say a word you don't want to repeat.

He came up from below with his hair plastered to his forehead and the bilge bucket still in his hand, and he read her face the way she'd taught him to read water — looking for the deep spots. He turned and looked back downriver.

The gunboat was maybe two kilometers off and closing. It rode high and mean, a converted patrol hull with a canopy strung with drying laundry and a bow-mounted engine that had no business on this stretch of river. Painted along its flank, in letters some drunk hand had made too large: *Prosperity*. Harken's idea of a joke, or his idea of a prayer. With Harken it was hard to tell where one ended and the other began.

"He's got the current," Jude said. "We've got the current."

"We've got a boat held together with church lead and prayer," Mara said. "He's got forty horsepower and men who get paid whether we drown or not."

The rain had been coming down in fits all morning, warm as breath, and the river had risen a hand's width since dawn. She could feel it in the way the water moved — not flowing so much as hurrying, shouldering past the banks, impatient. Upstream of them, if her charts in her head were right, the valley narrowed into the Karst Narrows, where the river dropped through old limestone in three steps and the maps the government printed simply stopped drawing detail, as if the cartographers had agreed among themselves to look away.

Nobody ran the Narrows in rising water. Nobody ran the Narrows in a patched hull.

"Harken won't shoot," Jude said. "He wants the map."

"Harken wants the map," Mara agreed. "Harken's hired men want their bonus, and bonuses come easier when the cargo stops moving." She spun the tiller and brought the *Kestrel* around, hard, toward the north bank. "Get everything loose lashed down. Everything. If it floats away, we don't need it."

"What are we doing?"

"Running the Narrows."

He stared at her. Rain ran off his jaw. For a moment she saw the boy in him, the son of a man who had once stood on her brother's deck with exactly that expression — the arithmetic of fear happening behind the eyes — and then she saw something else settle over him, something harder and more like his father than either of them would have liked.

"The Narrows," he repeated. "In this."

"In this," she said. "Because behind us is Harken, and ahead of us is only water, and I have never once met water that held a grudge."

---

The entrance to the Narrows announced itself long before they reached it: a change in the sound of the river, from the broad hush of moving water to a deeper, grinding roar, like millstones turning somewhere under the world. Mist stood over the gap between the cliffs. The banks drew in — jungle leaning close, vines trailing in the current, stone walls going up and up until the sky was a gray ribbon overhead.

Mara took the *Kestrel* into the eddy just above the drop and killed the sail. The sudden quiet was enormous. She could hear her own heart, and beside it, Jude breathing.

"Listen," she said. "There are three drops. The first is a chute — stay center-left, ride it straight, do not fight it. Below that there's a rock garden, water braiding around limestone teeth. You'll want to steer. Don't. Pick your line early, commit, and paddle like your soul depends on it, because it does. Then the second drop, wider, but there's a hole in the middle big enough to swallow us whole — river left, hug the cliff, even if the cliff looks like it wants to kill us. The third—" She stopped. Listened to the roar. "The third is the reason the maps stop."

"And if we miss a line?"

"Then we find out what's after the maps together."

He looked at her for a long moment. "You knew my father," he said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't the moment for it, and he said it anyway, because fear makes people honest. "You knew him, and you never said."

"I did," Mara said. "And I'll tell you everything, Jude Quintana, when we're below these rapids. Not because I'm hiding it. Because I want you alive when you hear it." She put out her hand. "Now take the forward oar and do exactly what I say."

He took it. His grip was white-knuckled and steady both, which was, she thought, about the best a man could offer.

She turned the bow downstream.

---

The first drop took them like a hand closing.

One moment they were sliding toward the mist; the next the river tilted and the world became noise and cold green violence. The chute was narrower than she remembered — or the water was higher, shoving them faster, giving less room for error — and the walls blurred past close enough that spray whipped off the stone and stung her face. The *Kestrel* groaned. Somewhere below decks something broke loose and banged against the hull, once, twice, and went silent.

"Center-left!" she shouted, though she was already holding them there, tiller jammed against its stop, and Jude was pulling the forward oar in long desperate strokes that did nothing and meant everything.

They shot out of the chute into the rock garden, and the river fell apart around them.

It was worse than she remembered. The rising water hadn't smoothed the teeth — it had drowned the gaps between them, so the channels that showed clean on her memory now ended in submerged rock, and the channels that looked open funneled into standing waves. A boulder loomed dead ahead, black and glistening, and she threw her weight on the tiller and the *Kestrel* slewed sideways, shipping a wall of water across the gunwale, and for one endless second they were surfing sideways down a green hill of river with the cliff rushing up—

"Pull! Pull LEFT!"

Jude pulled. Whether from obedience or terror she never asked. The bow swung, caught the current, and they threaded the gap between two teeth with a handspan on either side, the hull shrieking along stone beneath the waterline, and then they were through, spinning into the relative calm above the second drop.

Mara realized she was shouting words in three languages, none of them useful. She shut her mouth. Bailed nothing — there was no time. Ahead, the second drop roared, wide and white, and the hole sat in its middle like a mouth.

"River left! The cliff! Whatever happens, the cliff!"

The *Kestrel* went in nose-down. The world went white. Water hit her like a collapsed building — not falling water but rising water, the whole river standing up at once — and then they were through the seam, hugging the cliff face where the current ran smooth and fast and dark, so close that Jude's oar scraped rock and the overhang dripped cold water down her neck, and the hole they'd missed boiled and breathed behind them like a living thing denied a meal.

Two down.

She allowed herself one breath. One. Because ahead the river bent left around a shoulder of stone, and beyond the bend she could hear the third drop, and it sounded wrong. Louder than it should be. Angrier.

"Jude." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Below decks. Now. Anything that can bail, bring it. If we ship water on the last one, we sink inside a minute."

He hesitated — she saw him weigh leaving her alone at the tiller against obeying — and then he went, scrambling, and she was alone with the river for thirty seconds, thirty seconds of spray and roar and the memory she'd been carrying for eleven years, of another man at another tiller saying *I'll come back for your brother's grave, Mara, I swear it on the compass*, and she had believed him, God help her, she had believed him because he was the kind of man who swore things on compasses.

The *Kestrel* rounded the bend.

The third drop was not a drop anymore. The rising water had turned it into a single collapsing wave train, a quarter kilometer of standing hills breaking upstream against the current, and at the bottom, where the valley finally opened, the river spread into a churning flood plain dotted with the tops of drowned trees.

No line. There was no line. There was only down.

"Jude!" she screamed. "Hold on to something that's bolted down and pray to whatever your father prayed to!"

And then the river took them, and she stopped being a pilot and became cargo.

---

Later — hours later, or minutes, time went strange in whitewater — she would piece it together from evidence: the tiller cracked in her grip, the bruise blooming across her ribs where the boom had swung, the fact that they were alive at all. What she remembered was fragments. The *Kestrel* airborne, briefly, absurdly, like a fish leaping. Water from every direction at once, so complete that up ceased to exist. Jude bursting back up from below decks at the worst possible moment, taking the flooded cockpit's full weight across his chest, going down under it, coming up coughing and grabbing the lifeline and *holding*, holding with a stubbornness that looked, from the corner of her eye, exactly like inheritance.

They surfed one wave sideways. They buried the bow in the trough of the next and the boat did not come up — a full second, two, three, the world nothing but brown thunder — and then the buoyancy of the patched hull, the church lead, the stubborn corking of a drowned chapel's roof beams, lifted them, and they were through, spat out sideways into the flooded forest below the last drop, drifting among the crowns of drowned trees with the rope in her hands and her lungs on fire.

The *Kestrel* floated. Low — dangerously low, riding with her gunwales almost awash — but floating.

Mara became aware of sound. Of her own voice, hoarse, repeating something. She stopped. It had been his name.

Jude was slumped against the mast step, soaked through, blood running from a cut above his eyebrow into his eye. He was laughing. It was not entirely a sane sound.

"You," he managed, "said the maps stop."

"They do," Mara said. "We're past them now. Congratulations."

He laughed again, and then it turned into coughing, and she let him cough, because she needed a moment herself. Her hands would not stop shaking. She hid them in the ropes and watched the flood plain slide past — drowned trees, drifting debris, a dead cow turning slowly in an eddy — and she thought about how close that had been. How close they had all come to becoming a story other pilots told each other in port taverns, the woman Oduya and the archaeologist's son, lost in the Narrows in rising water, chasing a dead man's map.

A dead man's map. Harken's map, now, if Harken had his way.

The thought landed in her stomach like ballast.

"Jude." She kept her voice level. "Look astern. Slowly."

He wiped the blood from his eye and turned.

Far downstream — impossibly far, at the edge of the haze, but unmistakable — the *Prosperity* sat motionless against the bank. Her engine was silent. Even at this distance Mara could see why: the Narrows' flood had backed up behind the constriction, and below the last drop the river was choked with debris, whole trees riding the current like battering rams. No pilot alive would take a steel-hulled gunboat into that. Not for a bonus. Not for Harken.

"They stopped," Jude said.

"They stopped." She let the tiller drift and looked at him — really looked, for the first time since the entrance to the Narrows. Soaked, bleeding, half-drowned, and still watching the gunboat with the expression of a man doing sums. "They'll run the long portage around the Karst plateau. Four days, maybe five, hauling that boat overland with hired bearers. Maybe six, if the monsoon floods the trail."

"So we have four days."

"Four days," she agreed, "to reach Tsering, provision, and start up the ice before he can buy his way through the village gates. Which means no more easy water. Which means—" She stopped.

He was looking at her differently. Not with gratitude, exactly, and not with suspicion either. With the particular attention of a man who has just seen someone hold a line through impossible water on his behalf, and who knows that such things are not done for strangers.

"Why?" he said quietly. "Back at Kiln Landing, you wouldn't take my fare. Then you saw the compass and everything changed. You ran me through the Narrows today — you nearly died for a man you've known five days. That's not piloting, Mara. That's something else." The rain had stopped; the light through the torn clouds was pale gold, lying on the flooded forest, on his ruined face. "My father. Tell me."

Mara looked downstream, where the *Prosperity* waited like a debt. She thought of her brother's grave under the fig tree at Kiln Landing, and of a man who had sworn on a compass and never come back, and of the field notes she had glimpsed once, years ago, in a tent lit by a storm lantern — pages of a handwriting she'd know anywhere, describing a vault under a glacier, and a promise written in the margin that she had never been able to forget:

*If anything happens to me, the valley keeps it. Not the collectors. The valley.*

"I will tell you," she said, "everything, tonight, over a fire. But first you need to understand one thing, Jude Quintana, because it changes what that map is worth and who we can trust with it." She leaned closer, and the river carried them gently through the drowned trees, east, toward the mountains that were beginning to show their white shoulders through the haze.

"Your father didn't fail to find the Sunken Vault of Kesh-Tamar. He found it. And what he wrote in his notes about why he sealed it again — that's the reason Harken will follow us to the end of the ice, and the reason we can't let him arrive first."
