## 9. The Village Under the Glacier

The river gave out where the ice began.

For three days after the drowned church, the water had narrowed and quickened, braiding itself around gravel bars the color of old bone, until at last it thinned to a stream too shallow for even Mara's shallow-drafted boat. She ran it aground on a fan of gray silt with the gentleness of a woman setting down something she intended to pick up again.

"End of the line," she said, though her eyes stayed on the valley ahead.

Jude climbed over the gunwale and stood in water that came to his ankles and was so cold it felt less like temperature than like opinion. Ahead, the river valley climbed in a long green stair toward a wall of white that filled half the sky — a glacier coming down between two dark peaks like a tongue from some enormous sleeping mouth. Below its snout, stitched into the moraine where the rock had been ground to soil, terraces of barley glowed gold-green in the thin light.

And above the terraces, stone houses, flat-roofed, smoke rising straight up in the windless morning.

Tsering.

He'd imagined it a hundred times since Elias Vane had pressed the map into his hands in that port tavern — imagined it as Elias must have first seen it: a refuge, an accident of shelter, a place you washed into when the world spat you out. He hadn't imagined smoke rising straight. He hadn't imagined barley. He hadn't imagined that anyone would have bothered to make this beautiful.

"You're quiet," Mara said, hauling the pack with the map up onto her shoulder. She'd carried it since the rapids. Jude suspected she didn't trust him not to lose it, and he couldn't argue; he'd nearly lost everything else.

"Just trying to figure out what I'm going to say."

"The truth's short. Old man gave me a map. Told me to bring it here."

"That's not the truth."

Mara looked at him then, really looked, the way she did before running bad water — reading the surface for what moved underneath. "No," she said. "It isn't." She shouldered past him onto the bank. "Come on, then. They'll see us before we see them. They always do."

* * *

They always did.

Jude heard them before he saw them — dogs first, three of them pouring down the switchback trail like spilled ink, barking with the full-throated joy of animals announcing visitors rather than threatening them. Then children, a dozen or more, spilling out of the village gate behind the dogs, and behind the children, slower, the elders.

The children surrounded them at the second switchback, fearless, grabbing at Mara's sleeves, at the straps of Jude's pack, chattering in a language he didn't speak until one boy — maybe ten, gap-toothed, bold as brass — pointed at Jude's chest and said something that stopped the others mid-laugh.

An old man translated from behind them. His voice was dry as seed husks. "He says you walk like Elias."

Jude turned. The speaker was small and brown and folded into himself like a piece of weathered driftwood, but his eyes were quick and black and missed nothing. He wore a robe of undyed wool and a hat that might once have been red. Behind him stood six more elders, three women among them, all watching Jude with an attention that made his skin prickle.

"I don't know who told you I—" Jude began.

"Elias Vane." The old man said the name carefully, the way you handle a tool with a cracked handle. "You carry his map. You have his walk. And you have—" he leaned forward, squinting, "—the look of a man being chased by something he cannot name. Yes. That is how he arrived also."

A ripple went through the children — laughter, quickly hushed by one of the women elders.

"My name is Jude Quintana."

Something changed in the circle. Not hostility. Something worse: recognition. An old woman made a small sound, hand going to her mouth. Another murmured to her neighbor. The gap-toothed boy looked from Jude to the elders and back again, sensing weather he didn't understand.

"Quintana," the old man repeated. He looked at Mara. "And you are Oduya's daughter. Pili's girl."

Mara went very still. "You knew my father?"

"We knew your father's boats." The old man smiled without much humor. "He ran the lower river. We traded with him every season until the war took the traffic away. Come. You are expected. You have been expected for eleven years."

* * *

The house they were brought to sat at the top of the village, closest to the ice, with a view down over every roof and terrace. Inside it smelled of juniper smoke and butter tea and something older — cold stone and centuries. The elders arranged themselves around the central hearth with the unhurried precision of people who had done exactly this many times, and tea was poured, and Jude sat with the map case against his chest like a shield, waiting for the interrogation.

It didn't come.

Instead the eldest of the women — her face a landscape of creases, her hands knotted like tree roots — asked him about the river. About the monsoon. Whether the bridge at Kiln Landing still stood, whether the ferryman there was still the son of the ferryman she remembered. Small questions. Human questions. As if the map were a formality, and what they actually wanted to know was whether the world below still held anything familiar.

Then, when the tea was gone and the light through the smoke-hole had shifted one hand's width, the old man — whose name, they learned, was Dorje — nodded to the youngest elder, and she left, and returned carrying a wooden box.

Dorje opened it on the hearthstones between them.

Inside, wrapped in silk gone gray with age, lay a fragment of carved slate no bigger than a prayer book. Jude leaned forward despite himself. The carving showed a mountain — unmistakably *their* mountain, the twin-peaked wall above the village — and beneath it, descending in careful incised lines, a stair. A white stair. And at the bottom of the stair, rendered in strokes so fine they could only have been cut with iron, a door.

Not just any door. A door with a lintel of seven rays, fanning downward like sunlight frozen mid-fall.

Jude's hands were shaking when he drew out Elias's map. He unrolled it on the stones beside the slate, and the room went silent.

The same stair. The same door. The same seven rays — drawn in Elias's blunt prospector's hand, copied, Jude now understood, from carvings like this one, or from the thing itself. Around the door Elias had written coordinates in his cramped notation, and beneath them, in letters pressed deep enough to tear the paper:

*FOR TSERING. NOT FOR SALE. NOT FOR SALE. NOT FOR—*

The last word trailed off into a scratch where the pen had failed.

"He wrote that part here," Dorje said quietly. "In this house. Eleven years ago. He came down off the ice half-dead, frostbitten, raving about a door under the glacier. We fed him through a winter. In spring he swore the promise." The old man's black eyes lifted to Jude's face. "He said someone would come. He said it might take years. He said whoever carried the map would owe us a debt already spent, and that we should feed them anyway."

"And you believed him?"

"We believed the map." Dorje touched the slate fragment with one finger, gently, the way you touch a scar. "Our grandmothers' grandmothers carved its sisters. There were nine tablets once. This is the fourth. The others are buried deeper than Harken's greed will ever dig — no offense meant to your debts, river-man. The vault is not a story to us. It is a wound we agreed to forget. Elias reminded us why forgetting was wise. And why remembering might someday be necessary."

Jude looked at the map, at the furious repetition — *not for sale, not for sale* — and felt the weight of it settle onto his shoulders like wet rope. "I swore him a promise. Whatever's found goes to this village. Not to collectors. Not to me."

"We know," Dorje said. "He told us you would say that. He told us you'd say it badly, and mean it completely."

The laughter that went around the circle was warm, and Jude found, to his horror, that his eyes were burning. He stared hard at the hearth until it passed.

* * *

They fed him that night in the common house — barley, dried meat, turnips in broth, more butter tea than any human being should drink — and it was there that the belonging began, the unsettling kind, the kind offered before it was earned.

Because they didn't treat him like a stranger. They treated him like a letter that had finally arrived.

The old woman with the root-knotted hands — her name was Lhamo — took his face in her hands and studied it and pronounced that he had Elias's impatience in the brow and someone kinder in the mouth. Children followed him from house to house. When he helped haul water up from the melt channel, nobody thanked him, because nobody thanked family for hauling water; they simply handed him the next bucket. When he misplayed a word in their language, they corrected him the way you correct a cousin — laughing, repeating, moving on.

By the third evening he caught himself answering to a nickname the children had given him — something that meant, roughly, *Elias-who-came-back* — and had to step outside into the cold to breathe.

Mara found him at the low wall above the terraces, where the glacier hummed its constant note in the dark, a sound like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

"They're calling me Elias," he said, without turning. "Like I'm a ghost come home to collect."

"You could do worse ghosts."

"I could do better ones." He gripped the wall. The stone was icy under his palms. "Mara, I've spent my whole life running from my father's name. Dug other men's trenches under other men's names just so I wouldn't have to wear my own. And now I walk into a village I've never seen, and they hand me buckets and call me kin, and there's a dead prospector's promise sitting on my chest like a debt I never signed for." He shook his head. "I haven't earned any of this. I patched a boat. I survived a storm. That's the entire ledger."

Mara was quiet a while. Below them the barley moved in the wind like something breathing.

"You know what my father used to say about rivers?" she said finally. "'The current doesn't ask if you deserve to float. It asks if you can swim.'" She bumped his shoulder with hers, briefly, roughly — the most affection she allowed herself. "They're not saying you've earned it. They're saying the water's holding you. Swim or don't."

Before he could answer, a horn sounded from the watchtower at the village gate — two notes, low and urgent, repeated. Down in the terraces, lanterns began to move. Dorje appeared at the corner of the common house, and though he spoke calmly, his face in the lantern light was grim.

"A trader came up from the south fork today," he said. "Buying porters. Paying triple. Asking after foreigners on the river." He looked at Jude, and then, deliberately, at the glacier looming pale above them all. "Harken is upstream. And the ice route opens soon."

Jude's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. Nine days, the elders had said the calving season would give them. Nine days, and somewhere below in the dark, a man with a gunboat and a grudge was buying his way toward them, porter by porter.

Above the village, the glacier rang its long unfinished note, patient as a debt.

The clock had started.
