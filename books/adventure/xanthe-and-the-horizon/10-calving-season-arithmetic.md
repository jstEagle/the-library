## 10. Calving Season Arithmetic

Mara Oduya had learned to count in ice years before she learned to count in money. Her father taught her on the Kiln Landing docks: *a river is a ledger, girl — every season an entry, every flood a debt collected.* The glacier above Tsering kept the same books. You just had to know how to read them.

She read them now from the flat stone behind Dorje's house, where the whole village seemed to have gathered without anyone calling a meeting. Old men with faces like wind-scoured granite. Women spinning wool while their eyes did the real work. Children too young to be useful but old enough to listen, perched on the low wall like sparrows.

In the center, Tsering's headman — a broad-shouldered woman named Lhamo who spoke the way avalanches moved, all at once and downhill — had unrolled Elias Vane's map across the stone and weighted its corners with four river pebbles.

"Nine days," Lhamo said, tapping the upper margin of the map where the glacier's snout was drawn in faded blue ink. "Maybe eight if the heat holds. Maybe eleven if the clouds come back. After that—" she drew her thumb across the map's throat, a small, casual violence— "the gate closes until next year."

Mara watched Jude's face as he listened. He stood at the edge of the gathering, arms crossed, wearing that expression she'd catalogued somewhere between Kiln Landing and the drowned church: a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. Not about gold. About time. She was beginning to understand that Jude Quintana counted everything twice — once for what it cost him, once for what it cost everyone else. It was a habit of men who'd been made to feel like a debt themselves.

"The route through the ice gate," Lhamo continued, "is open when the serac wall calves and drops the bridge of old ice into the melt channel. Elias crossed it twice. He told us it holds for three days, no more, before the new face collapses inward." She looked up, found Mara in the crowd as though she'd known exactly where she was standing all along. "Your pilot has crossed ice before?"

"Sea ice," Mara said. "River ice. Glacier ice is a different animal."

"It is," Lhamo agreed. "It bites."

A ripple of dry laughter went through the elders. Mara didn't join it. She was looking at the map — really looking at it for the first time since the tavern in Kiln Landing, when it had been a dying man's fever-dream and not a document with her life written into its margins. Elias had drawn the ascent in careful, obsessive detail: the moraine ramp, the white stair of switchbacks, the crevasse fields marked with little crosses like graves, and then the gate itself, the serac wall, sketched with a precision that could only come from having stood beneath it and felt it breathe.

And beside the gate, in Elias's cramped hand, a note she hadn't been able to read by lamplight:

*The mountain gives you a window. Do not mistake a window for a door.*

* * *

They planned in Lhamo's kitchen that evening, because planning in the open invited every opinion in the village and half of them were wrong. Mara sat at the plank table with the map, a pencil stub, and a growing sense that the numbers were conspiring against her.

"Porters," she said. "How many can we raise?"

Lhamo poured butter tea without asking. "Twelve who know the ice. Six more who think they do. I will give you the twelve."

"Ropes? Anchors? A ladder for the crevasse fields?"

"Iron we have little of. Wood we have. Yak hide rope, good for cold, bad for wet." Lhamo set down the pot. "You are asking me to outfit an expedition my village has fed all summer. Ask me also what we eat in spring."

The question landed where it was meant to. Mara had run boats through debt-ledgers her whole life; she recognized one being opened in front of her. "Elias promised you the vault," she said carefully. "Not us. But if the vault is there, and if even part of what he described is true—"

"If," said Lhamo.

Jude spoke from the corner, where he'd been cleaning the compass with a cloth gone gray from use. "If it's true, the village won't need to eat its seed grain to buy rope." He didn't look up. "I'll sign for it. Whatever the outfitters upstream charge, whatever the difference is. My mark against it."

Mara turned. "With what?"

"With the only thing I own, which is my labor, which you've both seen is worth something." Now he looked up, and there was no self-pity in it, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not promising treasure. I'm promising work."

Lhamo studied him the way she'd study a river before crossing it. Then she nodded once, and that was that; in this country, apparently, a nod was a contract. "Twelve porters. Rope and wood. And you—" she pointed the ladle at Jude— "will carry as much as any porter and complain less."

"He complains plenty," Mara said.

"I am learning," Jude said, "to do it silently."

* * *

The arithmetic got worse after dark, when Dorje — the guide Lhamo had assigned them, a quiet man with frost-scarred cheeks and hands that never stopped moving — spread out the supply list and began striking things off it with a charcoal stick.

"Flour, enough for nine days up and five down: short by forty pounds. Tsampa: short. Oil: short. Medicine—" he paused— "there is no medicine. There is willow bark and there is prayer."

"Fuel?" Mara asked.

"For melting snow at altitude? We burn dung. It burns cold and slow. Your water will take an hour to boil and taste like the yak that donated it."

"And the weather window," Jude said slowly, "is nine days out. So we climb in the window, spend three days maximum inside the gate, and come down before it closes."

Dorje shook his head. "You are counting like a lowlander. The window does not begin when the serac falls. It begins when the meltwater rises behind it. Some years the gate opens and floods within a day. Elias said the vault sits below the old meltline. If the summer runs hot—"

"The vault drowns," Mara finished.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, the wind came down off the icefield and pressed against the shutters like something impatient.

"So we don't wait for day nine," Mara said. "We stage now. Move supplies up to a basecamp below the moraine while the lower routes are still safe. When the gate opens, we're not starting from the village — we're starting from six thousand feet already climbed."

Dorje's scarred face did something that was almost a smile. "The pilot thinks like ice."

"The pilot grew up reading rivers," Mara said. "Same book, different font."

* * *

It was two days later, hauling the first loads up the moraine ramp, that the boy came running down from the watch post above the village, shouting words Mara didn't have yet but understood anyway from the way the whole caravan stopped and the color left Dorje's face.

She climbed to the watch post herself, because waiting for translations was a luxury the calendar wouldn't allow. From the cairn at the top, the valley ran north in a long green seam, and far up that seam — a hard day's travel, maybe less — she saw it through the scout's battered spyglass.

Smoke. Not cookfire smoke. Engine smoke, thin and black, hanging over the river landing at the head of navigation where no boat had reason to be.

Harken.

Of course. While they'd been counting rope and flour, Harken had been counting something else: porters. Men with mountain legs and mountain judgment, the one resource no amount of money could fake and every amount of money could rent. If Harken bought the local guides out from under them — or worse, bought one who knew the route — the race would be over before the gate opened.

She lowered the glass and found Jude beside her, breathing hard from the climb, squinting north against the light.

"How bad?" he asked.

"He's hiring." She handed him the glass. "Which means either he knows where we're going and wants to beat us to it, or he doesn't and wants to make sure nobody else gets there first. With Harken, those are the same plan."

Jude watched the smoke for a long time. When he handed the glass back, his jaw was set in a way she recognized from the rapids, from the night they bailed the boat together, from every moment this stubborn, damaged man decided to keep going instead of turning around.

"Then we move faster," he said.

"Everyone says that." Mara slung the glass over her shoulder and started down toward the village, already re-running the numbers, tearing apart the schedule she'd built and rebuilding it meaner and leaner. "Nobody accounts for what faster costs. Faster means heavier loads. Heavier loads means tired porters. Tired porters means mistakes on the ice, and the ice charges full price for mistakes."

"So what do we cut?"

Mara reached the bottom of the cairn and looked back north one last time. The engine smoke had thickened. Somewhere under it, Harken's silver was changing hands.

"We don't cut weight," she said. "We cut days. We leave for basecamp tomorrow at first light — all of us, everything, nine days early. We'll sit on the moraine and watch the serac wall and wait. And when that gate opens—"

She let it hang. Below them, the village was already moving, answering the alarm with the calm efficiency of people who had always lived under a clock made of ice. Dorje was counting ropes. Lhamo was counting heads. Somewhere a child was chasing a goat out of the supply tent, and the goat was winning.

"—we're already there," Mara finished, "and Harken is still buying his army."

Jude almost smiled. "Calving season arithmetic."

"Don't get fond of it," she said. "Tomorrow we find out if the numbers hold."

That night she checked the compass where Jude had left it on the table between them, its needle steady, its brass case worn smooth by two generations of Quintana hands. North, always north. She thought of the black smoke on the northern horizon and the blue line of the glacier beyond it, and she understood, with a pilot's certainty, that the needle was pointing at both.

First light came gray and cold. They loaded the yaks in silence, twelve porters and six hundred pounds of hope, and started up the white stair while the smoke behind them grew.
