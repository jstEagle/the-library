## 11. Lessons in Ice

The rope was supposed to save him. Instead it nearly strangled him.

"Again," said Dawa Tsering, the village's senior guide, a woman of perhaps sixty whose face had been cured like leather by forty years of glacier wind. She stood at the lip of the practice crevasse with her arms folded, watching Jude dangle above the blue dark like a hooked fish.

"I'm—" The words came out in a wheeze. The chest harness Dawa had tied that morning was cinched wrong; he'd retied it himself to prove he didn't need help, and now every breath cost him. "I'm fine."

"You are upside down and purple," Dawa said. "These are not the same as fine."

Above him, on the snow bridge they'd built for the drill, half the village's climbing-age children sat in a row like crows on a fence, watching. They'd been watching all morning. Word traveled fast in Tsering — the stranger who'd come with Mara Oduya couldn't tie a prusik, couldn't self-arrest without gouging his own thigh, and had fallen into the practice crevasse twice before lunch. The second time he'd screamed. Not loudly, but enough.

Jude hauled himself up the rope hand over hand, boots scraping ice, and dragged himself over the lip onto his back, staring at the white sky. His lungs burned. Somewhere below the surface of his humiliation, an older feeling stirred — the familiar arithmetic of failure, the ledger he'd been keeping since he was nineteen years old and watched his father walk out of a hearing room with his career in pieces.

He'd been good at this once. Not great — but competent. He'd spent six years digging other men's trenches in three countries, hauling coil after coil of rope, and he knew how to move over stone. But stone forgave. Ice did not. Ice kept score.

Dawa crouched beside him. Up close her eyes were the pale gray of old river glass, and they missed nothing.

"You learned on rock," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Dirt, mostly. Rock when I could get it."

"Mm." She took the rope from his hands and coiled it with a speed that made the motion look like nothing at all. "Rock teaches you to fight. Ice teaches you to wait. On rock, when you slip, you grab. On ice, when you slip, you already grabbed — three moves ago, or you are dead." She set the coil across his chest like a weight. "Your body knows the wrong lessons. We will teach it slower ones."

* * *

They broke for salt tea at midday in the longhouse, and Jude sat at the edge of the room where the smoke thinned, working the rope through his blistered palms and trying to be invisible. He was not good at that either. In a village of two hundred souls, nobody was invisible.

It was the girl who found him — Pema, maybe ten years old, one of the crows from the crevasse rim, with a gap in her smile and a goat on a braided lead. The goat was gray and disagreeable and looked at Jude with the flat contempt of its kind.

"You fell in the hole," Pema said.

"Twice."

"The second time you yelled."

"I'm aware."

"My uncle yelled the first time he went in a real one," she said, settling cross-legged beside him with the ease of someone claiming territory. "Except he didn't yell coming out. He yelled going down, and then we didn't hear anything for four days, and then Grandmother Dawa went and got him and he never yelled again about anything. Even when the yak stepped on him." She offered this as encouragement. "You'll stop yelling too. Probably."

"Thank you," Jude said gravely. "That helps."

She beamed. The goat ate a corner of someone's prayer flag.

Mara came in from the boat then, shaking rain off her oilskin — it had started again around noon, the thin persistent drizzle that preceded real weather — and dropped onto the bench across from him. She looked tired in a way she tried to hide, and he'd learned enough of her by now to know why: she'd spent the morning with the elders, going over Elias's map line by line, translating the old prospector's crooked annotations into terms the village guides could use. She carried the map itself in an oilcloth roll against her spine, always, like a second backbone.

"How's school?" she asked, nodding at the rope in his lap.

"Enlightening. I've learned I've been doing everything wrong for six years and surviving on luck."

"That tracks," Mara said, and drank her tea.

He almost smiled. Almost. Then Dawa rose at the head of the longhouse and said something in the village tongue, short and clipped, and the room's easy noise settled into attention. Mara translated low, for his benefit: "Weather window's holding. Nine days until calving season opens the route properly — maybe less if the melt comes early. Dawa says anyone going up trains tomorrow at first light, no exceptions, no passengers." A pause. "You're listed."

"As what?"

"As someone going up." Mara's eyes held his, and beneath the dry humor there was the thing she rarely let show: the fear she'd confessed once, badly, at the drowned church — that she'd followed him because she couldn't stand the thought of him dying alone on the ice, and hated herself a little for the softness. "So don't fall in any more holes, Quintana. I've only got the one heart attack left in me and I'm saving it."

* * *

The next three days were the hardest labor he'd known since Harken's trench crews, and harder, because here failure had witnesses who remembered.

Dawa ran the drills like a surgeon runs a ward. Morning: knots, until his fingers bled and re-bled and finally calloused into something useful. Midday: self-arrest on the training slope above the village, where generations of boots had polished a groove in the ice, and where Jude learned that his instincts were exactly backwards — that throwing his weight toward the slope, the way he'd do on scree, was the way men died on ice, and that the pick of the axe had to bite across his body, not with it. Evening: crevasse work, over and over, lowering and raising, the pulley systems that turned one climber's strength into enough force to lift another human being out of the blue dark.

He failed publicly on the second evening, and it was worse than falling.

They were running a haul system — Pema's uncle Norbu, the man who'd once spent four days in a crevasse, playing the victim at the bottom of the practice crack while Jude rigged the anchor and ran the raise. Jude got the anchor in wrong. Not dangerously wrong — Dawa checked everything twice, always — but wrong enough that when Norbu's weight came onto the line, the anchor shifted, the whole system racked sideways, and Norbu dropped six feet before the backup caught him, swinging gently in the dark like a man on a porch swing.

The silence at the rim was total. Twenty villagers watched Norbu swing. Norbu, to his credit, called up cheerfully in his own language, and someone translated, laughing: "He says the view is better down there anyway."

But Dawa didn't laugh. She climbed down, freed Norbu, and came up the rope with a face like weather, and she stood in front of Jude while the others quietly dispersed, and she said, in English, so there would be no mistake:

"On the glacier, that error kills two people. The one in the hole, and the one who goes down after them." She let that sit. "Why did the anchor fail?"

Jude made himself go back through it, step by step, out loud, while she listened without mercy. The placement was sound. The angle was sound. The flaw was in the equalization — he'd tied off the legs of the anchor at different tensions because it was faster, because on rock it never mattered, because he'd been doing it that way for six years and nobody had died yet.

"There," Dawa said. "*Yet.* You have been spending luck like a rich man spends coin, and you came here poor." She took the rope and began re-rigging the anchor herself, slow, narrating each motion. "Now. Again. Until your hands know it even when your mind is afraid. Because on the real ice, your mind will be very afraid."

He ran it eleven more times that night, by lamplight, until the knot tied itself and his shoulders shook and Dawa finally put her hand on the rope and said, "Enough. Sleep. The ice is patient; learn to be."

Walking back through the village in the blue dusk, past houses glowing amber, past the chorten where an old woman spun her wheel and nodded to him — nodded, just slightly, the way you nod to a neighbor — Jude felt something shift in his chest that he didn't have a name for. It wasn't competence exactly. Competence was still days away, maybe weeks. It was more like being measured honestly, and found unfinished, and not thrown out for it.

Harken would have thrown him out. Harken's world had no use for a man mid-lesson.

* * *

On the fourth day the melt came early.

It announced itself as sound — a deep, irregular knocking under the valley floor, like a fist on a door, and then a rush of water finding new channels through the ice below the training slopes. Dawa called the morning short and sent everyone down off the glacier's toe, and the village adjusted around the danger the way water adjusts around a stone, without drama, without wasted motion. Channels would be marked. Routes redrawn. Children warned with names attached, which was how warnings worked here: not *stay away from the meltwater*, but *Tenzin, Dolma, little Karma — the east channel is hungry, stay west.*

Jude was carrying rope down from the slope when he heard the screaming.

Not human. Goat — high, ragged, panicked, coming from the line of moraine below the east channel, where the melt had cut a slot deeper than a man was tall through the old dirty ice. And then a child's voice layered over it, sobbing, and he was moving before his mind finished the sentence.

Pema stood at the edge of the channel, both fists in her mouth, and below her, wedged on a shelf of rotten ice six feet down, was the gray goat — the same disagreeable animal, its hind leg bloodied, scrabbling at walls too steep to climb. Worse: the shelf it stood on was the last dry patch in a slot that was filling. The meltwater came from somewhere upstream, and it was coming faster, the knocking under the ice turning into a continuous roar.

"Pema." He caught her shoulders. "How long has it been down?"

"It slipped, it slipped, I couldn't—"

"How long?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe—"

Ten minutes of melt behind a wall of ice upstream. He didn't know how much time that bought. Nobody would know until the water arrived or didn't. He looked at the channel: narrow, vertical-walled, the goat on a shelf at six feet, water pooling at eight inches and rising visibly. He looked at Pema, whose face was doing what his face must have done at nineteen, watching his father's name get read into the record.

Every instinct he'd been trained out of over four days said *go for help*. Dawa was twenty minutes away with the main party. The water might arrive in five. It might arrive in thirty. The goat had perhaps one choice of outcome either way.

"Go find Dawa," he said. "Run. Tell her: east channel, animal down, water rising, I'm going in. Go *now.*"

She ran. He dropped the coils of rope off his shoulder and paid one out fast, hands moving through the anchor sequence Dawa had beaten into him the night before — placement, angle, equalization, tensioned legs, tested with his full weight, tested again. Eleven repetitions by lamplight. His hands knew it even though his mind was afraid. His mind was extremely afraid.

Then he went over the edge.

The cold hit like a blow — meltwater cold, the cold of things that had been ice an hour ago — and filled his boots instantly. The channel walls pressed close, slick with runoff, and the goat screamed directly into his face, a smell of wet animal and terror and blood. He got a hand on the shelf edge, got his knee up, and the shelf groaned under the doubled weight.

"Hey," he said to the goat, absurdly, the way you'd talk to a spooked horse. "Hey. Easy. Easy."

It was not easy. It was a terrified animal in pain, and when he reached for its injured hind leg it lashed out and caught him across the forearm, opening a line of red. He swore, breathed, and changed tactics — wrapped his arm around its neck instead, pinned it against his chest the way he'd seen villagers hold them, and got his boots braced on the shelf's downstream lip.

The water was at his knees.

Upstream, the knocking had stopped, which was somehow worse than the knocking. Silence meant the water had found its path. He could feel it now through the soles of his boots, a pressure building, a shudder in the ice.

He hauled. One arm around the goat, one arm on the rope, boots driving against slick ice, and for a horrible moment nothing moved except the water, which jumped from his knees to his thighs with a sound like a door slamming open somewhere above. Then the shelf cracked under him, tilted, and slid — and he rode it, deliberately, angling the fall toward the rope, the goat bawling against his throat, and threw his weight upward as the shelf went out from under them entirely.

His hands burned. The rope held. The anchor held — equalized, tensioned, tested twice. He came over the lip with the goat in a death-grip and rolled clear as the channel below filled end to end with brown roaring melt, a river born in the time it took to say a name.

He lay on the moraine gasping, bleeding, soaked to the ribs, with sixty pounds of furious goat pinned under one arm. The goat bit him on the shoulder. He barely felt it.

* * *

They came at a run — Dawa first, then Norbu, then half the village behind them, Pema leading and crying and laughing at once. Dawa took in the scene: the flooded channel, the anchor, the blood, the boy and the goat. Her face gave away nothing. She waded past him, checked the channel edges, examined the anchor with two fingers, and tugged it hard, twice.

Then she looked at him.

"The anchor was correct," she said.

"Yes."

"You went in without waiting."

"Yes."

"That was wrong." A pause, wind moving the prayer flags overhead. "And the rigging was right, and the animal lives, and the child saw you do it." She crouched, took his bleeding forearm in her cured-leather hands, and began binding it with strips from her own sleeve, efficient and rough. "In Tsering we say the mountain does not care whether you are brave or careful. But the village remembers which one you were when it mattered." She tied off the bandage and met his eyes. "Today you were careful inside the brave. That is the whole lesson. There is no other one."

Pema arrived at a skid, snatched her goat, and buried her face in its filthy neck. Then she looked up at Jude with wet eyes and the gap-toothed grin and said, "You didn't yell."

"No," Jude managed. "I didn't yell."

By evening the story had gone around the village twice and improved with each telling — by supper the channel was twelve feet deep and the water a wall of foam, and Jude had personally wrestled a demon-goat, depending on the teller. He sat in the longhouse with his shoulder throbbing and his forearm bound and let it happen, embarrassed and warm, while Mara watched him from across the fire with an expression he couldn't read.

Later, walking her back along the lantern-lit lane, she said quietly: "Norbu told me what Dawa said to you. Careful inside the brave." She glanced over. "Your father would have liked that. He used to say almost the same thing — that courage was just fear that had done its homework."

Jude stopped walking.

"He talked about that? About—"

"About a lot of things." Mara faced him, and the lantern light moved on her face, and he saw that she'd been deciding all day whether to tell him whatever came next. "There's a reason I stayed in the boat that day at Kiln Landing, Jude. A reason I keep not saying." She pulled her coat tighter against the cold coming off the icefields. "Elias's map isn't the only paper pointing at that vault. Your father wrote me a letter, years ago. I never answered it. And there's a part of it I've read a hundred times since, and never understood — until I saw what you did today on that ice."

The knocking under the glacier sounded again, far off, patient.

"What part?" Jude said.

Mara looked up the valley, toward the white stair and the gate of ice at its top, and said, "The part where he asks forgiveness for what he sealed inside it."
