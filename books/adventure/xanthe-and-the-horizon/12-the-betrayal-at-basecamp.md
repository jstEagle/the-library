## 12. The Betrayal at Basecamp

**MARA**

The storm came down off the icefield an hour after dark, and Mara knew before the first gust hit that something else was coming with it.

She had spent twenty years reading rivers, and rivers had taught her that trouble never traveled alone. It came the way silt came after a flood — riding on the back of something bigger. The wind arrived first, flattening the cook-fire smoke sideways across the camp, and then the snow, thick as ash, and through it all she caught a sound that didn't belong: boots on scree, more than one pair, moving fast toward the porter lines instead of away from them.

She stood up from the fire where she'd been re-waxing the tent seams and looked down the slope to where the porters had pitched their lean-tos below the supply cache. Lambswool light from their cookfire. Shadows crossing it. Too many shadows.

"Old Manu," she said quietly, to the guide crouched by the flames, "count your people."

Manu counted. His lips moved; his face went still in the way faces go still when the arithmetic comes out wrong. "Dorje is not here," he said. "Nor Tenzin's boy."

"Dorje." Mara said the name like she was tasting it. The young porter with the quick smile and the quicker debts — the one who'd asked her twice already how much a boat pilot earned in a season, who'd lingered near the map case when they planned the route, whose eyes had gone to Jude's pack the way hungry eyes go to bread. "When did anyone see him last?"

"Before the light went. He said he was walking down to check the yak line."

Nobody checked a yak line in weather like this. Nobody checked a yak line at all unless the yaks were already screaming, and the yaks were quiet in their corral below the moraine.

Mara looked up-slope, to where Jude's tent glowed faintly against the dark, and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her shoulders — the weight of knowing a thing before she could prove it. She'd carried that weight once before, on another expedition, with another Quintana, and it had ended with her brother alive and a man gone before dawn owing her answers.

She would not let it end the same way twice.

"Get everyone into the big tent," she told Manu. "Wake the others. If Dorje sold us, we have very little time."

"Sold us?" Old Manu's voice cracked with something between disbelief and grief. He was Dorje's uncle.

"I hope I'm wrong," Mara said, which was the only kind lie she allowed herself anymore. "Move."

The wind hit her full in the chest as she turned uphill, driving needles of snow into her face, and somewhere below them all — far below, past the glacier's snout, past the frozen falls where the route began — she imagined she could hear the diesel knock of Harken's gunboat engine converted to some mountain transport, grinding its way up the valley in the dark.

Or maybe that was just the storm. With storms, you never knew until it was too late. That was the whole nature of them.

* * *

**JUDE**

Jude was copying the day's bearings into his notebook by headlamp when the first rope of the supply cache snapped.

He heard it as a crack — sharp, wet, like a branch breaking under snow-load — and then a second sound he recognized from a lifetime around dig sites: the particular slide of heavy things being moved by hands that shouldn't be touching them. He ducked out of the tent into the white roar and saw figures at the cache platform, three of them, hunched against the wind, hauling crates down the slope on a pre-rigged sledge.

Not porters. They moved wrong for porters. Porters moved like water, economical, finding the path of least resistance even in the dark. These men moved like men following instructions written by someone who had never been on this mountain.

"Harken," Jude breathed, and the word tore away in the wind.

He ran. It wasn't brave and it wasn't smart; it was reflex, the same reflex that had him bailing a leaking boat with his bare hands while lightning walked the river. He got two of the crates back by main force, shoving one man into the snow and wrenching the other's grip loose, and then a third figure stepped out of the white and put the barrel of a rifle against his sternum, gentle as a finger tap.

"Quintana." The man behind the rifle was broad, frost-bearded, professionally calm. One of Harken's lowland men, transplanted badly to altitude, breathing too fast. "Boss says you can keep your life and lose the map. Or keep neither. Your pick."

"The map's not here."

"Then we'll dig for it." The man smiled without warmth. "We're good at digging. Heard that runs in the family."

Jude's blood did the thing it always did at that — the hot flare, the old shame — but there was no time for it, because that was exactly when the mountain decided to join the argument.

The sound came up through the soles of his boots before his ears caught it: a deep, tectonic groan, the glacier shifting its weight in the cold snap ahead of the storm front. Then the crack, enormous, rolling, and the whole slope beneath the camp gave a lurch like a ship taking a wave broadside.

The cache platform slid. The men with the rifle staggered. And above them, with a sound like the sky tearing along a seam, the cornice that had hung over the col since they'd arrived — the one the guides had marked with prayer flags and given a wide, respectful berth — let go.

"Avalanche!" someone screamed, in two languages.

Jude didn't decide to run. His body made the decision the way it makes decisions in dreams, and he was thirty meters downhill and diving behind the moraine ridge when the white wall came through the campsite and erased it.

* * *

**MARA**

The world ended and restarted, over and over, in pulses of white noise.

When it stopped, Mara dug herself out of a drift with both hands, spat snow, and took inventory the way her father had taught her on the Niger long ago: body first, kit second, people third. Body — bruised ribs, left hand bleeding where a carabiner had opened it. Kit — knife, yes; the compass no, that was Jude's anyway; the map case — her stomach dropped — the map case had been in the big tent.

People.

She screamed names into the settling powder fog until her throat tore. Answers came back, thin and scattered: Manu, alive, coughing. Two porters, alive, one limping. Tenzin, alive, holding his son, who was crying, which meant breathing, which meant alive. She counted again and again, and each count came up short by the same terrible margin, and each time she refused to write the number down because writing it would make it true.

The big tent was gone. Buried under four meters of consolidated snow, along with most of the food, the ropes, the stove fuel, the medical kit, and Elias Vane's map.

And Jude — she found him at the edge of the debris field, on his knees in the blue half-light, digging with his bare hands at a shape in the snow, and for one lurching second she thought it was a person and her heart stopped. Then she saw it was a crate. One of Harken's men lay nearby, unconscious or worse, half-buried, his rifle snapped like a twig.

"You," Mara said.

Jude looked up. Snow crusted his eyebrows, his split lip. There was something wild in his face that she couldn't read yet — guilt or fury or both braided together.

"They came for the map," he said. "Three of them, maybe more. They were loading the cache when—"

"When what, Jude?" Her voice came out flat and cold, colder than the air, and she heard herself speaking from a place she hadn't visited since the Drowned Church. "When your debt followed you up a sacred mountain? When the man you owe money to bought our route out from under us?"

"I didn't sell anything."

"No." She stepped closer, and the wind pushed at her back like it wanted her to say this, like the storm itself had opinions. "Dorje sold. Dorje, who ate at our fire. But Harken is *your* creditor, Jude. This is *your* wake we're lying in. You brought it to Tsering's door. You brought it to my brother's grave-mountain, if you want to know the truth of it, because I have been running from what your father left unfinished my whole adult life, and I got on that boat anyway, and now look at it. Look at it!"

She flung her arm at the devastation — the torn slope, the buried tents, the prayer flags from the cornice draped over the rubble like offerings at a mass grave. Somewhere under that whiteness lay everything they needed to survive the descent, and possibly everything they needed to survive the night.

Jude knelt in the snow and took it. She watched him take it, all of it, the way you watch a man accept a blow he knows he earned. He didn't argue. He didn't defend himself. And somehow that was worse than if he had, because she had built this alliance plank by plank on the Drowned Church hull-patches and the shared bail-buckets and the long nights of river talk, and she could feel the planks pulling loose under her feet, and she was too tired and too frightened to stop hammering.

"Say something," she demanded.

"What do you want me to say?" His voice was hoarse. "That I'm sorry? I am. That I'll fix it? I don't know if I can. That Harken would've come regardless?" He stood, slowly, favoring his side. "Actually — that last one might be true. He was buying porters upstream before we ever loaded the boats. You know that. You told me that yourself, in the village, planning this ascent."

"And I was wrong to come," Mara said. "That's what I'm telling you now. I was wrong."

The words landed between them and neither picked them up.

Old Manu appeared out of the fog, snow-blind and shaking, and said the thing that ended the argument whether they wanted it ended or not: "There is a boy missing. Whatever quarrel is between you, bury it till morning. We dig, or we freeze."

So they dug.

* * *

**JUDE**

They dug through the worst hours of the night, a chain of bodies passing snow in buckets and pans and cupped hands, and Jude worked at the point of the chain because it was the only penance available to him.

They found the cook-stove at midnight. They found the food tins at two. At three, Tenzin's shout went up from the lower drift, and Jude reached him first, and together they uncovered the small shape curled in the pocket of air that avalanches sometimes leave, the way the mountain sometimes, inexplicably, forgives.

It was not Dorje.

It was the boy Pemba, twelve years old, who had come up the mountain against his mother's wishes because he wanted to see the ice gate open just once. They dug him out blue-lipped and still, and Old Manu worked on him with bare hands and breath and prayers, and the whole camp stood in the falling snow and waited, and waited, and waited.

Pemba coughed.

The sound went through the camp like sunlight through a crack. Men sat down in the snow where they stood. Tenzin held his son and made noises Jude had never heard a grown man make. And Jude stood apart, shaking, his hands ruined and useless, understanding that he had been given something tonight he had done nothing to earn — again — and that the ledger of his life was written entirely in other people's grace.

Mara came and stood beside him. Neither spoke for a long time.

"He'll live," she said finally. Her voice had lost its blade. Not forgiven — he could hear that clearly — but set down, the way you set down a tool too heavy to carry and still work. "But we've lost the tents, half the food, the ropes, the medicine, and the map. Harken has the route and probably the cache crates his men dragged clear before the slide. And we have—" she gestured at the survivors, the wrecked slope, the dying fire they were rebuilding from a tin of stove fuel fished out of the snow, "—this."

"We have the compass," Jude said.

She looked at him.

He pulled it from his inner pocket, where it had ridden against his skin since Kiln Landing, warm from his body heat. His father's compass. The brass worn bright at the edges from a dead man's thumb. In its lid, scratched into the metal in a hand Jude had spent years trying to forget, the markings that matched Elias Vane's map — the bearings, the little glyph like a fish, the numbers that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't studied that map for weeks.

"The map's gone," Jude said. "But I memorized it. Every bearing. Every camp. I've been redrawing it every night in my notebook since the village." He met her eyes. "Harken has paper. I have it here." He tapped his temple, then closed his fist around the compass. "And I have this. He doesn't get either."

Mara studied him for a long moment, the firelight moving on her face, and whatever she was deciding, she decided it in silence, the way river pilots decide things — fast, final, without showing the working.

"Dorje," she said. "We still haven't found Dorje."

Jude's stomach tightened. "You think he went down to meet them."

"I think a man who sells a route doesn't stay to see what he's sold it to." She looked down-valley, into the dark where the storm was still unspooling off the icefield, and her jaw set. "And I think if Harken has Dorje, then Dorje has told him one more thing. The thing we told no one outside this camp."

Jude felt it land before she said it.

"The ice gate," he said quietly. "The calving window. Nine days."

"Eight now," Mara said. "And Harken doesn't need to climb well, Jude. He only needs to climb once, behind us, with our route and our timing and enough hired guns to take the vault off us at the threshold." She turned back to the fire, to the survivors huddled around it, to the boy Pemba sleeping under three coats with his father's arm around him. "So tomorrow we do the arithmetic again. What we have, what we've lost, what we can still carry. And you and I are going to decide — together, this time, no secrets — whether we go on."

"And if we go on?"

Mara fed a splinter of crate-wood to the small flame and watched it catch.

"Then we climb faster than grief," she said. "Because grief is what's chasing us now. Yours and mine. And it's heavier than any gun Harken carries."

Above them, through a tear in the clouds, the icefield showed itself for one moment — vast, silver, indifferent — and somewhere high on the White Stair, the gate they were racing toward waited in the dark, keeping its own counsel about who would reach it first.
