## 14. The Gate Opens

Mara heard the mountain before she saw it move.

She was standing at the edge of the village square, a cup of butter tea going cold in her hands, when the sound came rolling down the valley — not thunder, though the sky had been grumbling all morning, but something deeper and more deliberate. A groan from inside the glacier itself, as if some enormous animal pinned beneath the ice had finally decided to turn over.

The tea trembled in her cup. Ripples crossed its surface like rings on a struck drum.

Then the whole eastern face of the icefield let go.

She would remember it afterward in fragments: the white cliff shrugging off a slab of ice the size of a temple roof; the slab tipping, hanging for one impossible heartbeat; then the roar as it fell — a sound she felt in her sternum, in her teeth, in the soles of her boots — and the eruption of snow that swallowed the lower moraine whole. The cloud rolled out across the valley floor and past the village walls, dusting the prayer flags with fresh powder, and behind it, where the face of the glacier had been, there was now a gap.

A gate.

Mara set down the cup because her hands were no longer steady enough to hold it.

"Nine days," old Dorje said beside her, his voice carrying the particular calm of a man who had watched this mountain calve for sixty years. "The elders said nine days before the route opened." He squinted up at the new gap in the ice wall, where blue light leaked through like water through a crack in a hull. "The mountain has decided to be generous. Or impatient."

"It's been three days since Jude left."

"I know how long it's been, river pilot."

Three days since the storm scattered their camp. Three days since the porter Tenzin's betrayal came to light, since Harken's men had appeared on the wrong side of the ridge line, since everything had come apart in wind and accusation. Three days since Mara had stood in the wreckage of basecamp and told Jude Quintana, in words she could still feel lodged under her ribs like a fishhook, that he had brought death to Tsering's doorstep — that his name, his father's name, his map, his debt, all of it was a contagion and the village was bleeding from it.

And three days since Jude had looked at her with something worse than anger. Acceptance. As if she had only confirmed what he'd believed about himself all along.

He'd taken a rope, two days' rations, and gone up alone toward the ice gate. Toward the vault. Toward whatever his father had left him besides a surname people spat at.

*Let him go,* she'd told herself that first night, sitting by the fire while the villagers repaired what the storm had torn loose. *He made his choice. You made yours.*

But she hadn't slept. She'd lain awake listening to the wind come down off the icefield and thinking about all the rivers she'd ever run, and how every one of them had taught her the same lesson: you can hate the current all you want, but if someone's drowning in it, you go in after them or you live with the sound of the water for the rest of your life.

Now the mountain had opened its gate, and somewhere above, alone, Jude Quintana was walking into it.

* * *

She went to find Pemba first.

The young villager was in the stable mending a harness, his fingers quick and sure despite the burn scar that pulled the skin of his left hand tight. He'd been one of the two porters who refused Tenzin's coin — who'd stood in the storm with the tents collapsing around them and said, simply, *we go where Mara goes*. She had never asked him why. She suspected it had something to do with his sister, whom Mara had once pulled from a melt channel on the lower icefall, back when she'd first come to Tsering with Elias Vane's map folded in her coat and questions about a man named Quintana folded deeper still.

"The gate is open," she said.

Pemba didn't look up from the harness. "I heard it. The whole valley heard it."

"Jude went up alone. Three days ago. If he reaches the vault, he'll be coming back through that corridor with Harken behind him, and he'll be doing it without rope partners, without a camp, without anyone watching the ice for him." She paused. "You don't have to come. What I said to him at basecamp — some of it was true. Trouble follows that man. But—"

"But you're going anyway." Pemba set down the harness and finally met her eyes. There was no judgment in his face, which somehow made it harder. "My grandmother says the mountain opens the gate for whoever it wants to test. Not whoever it wants to reward." He stood, flexing his scarred hand. "I'll get the ropes. Sonam will want to come — he's been sharpening his axe since the storm, waiting for you to ask."

"You were waiting for me to ask?"

"We were waiting for you to stop pretending you weren't going." He smiled, brief and crooked. "River pilots are stubborn, but they're not subtle."

* * *

They left within the hour: Mara, Pemba, and Sonam, a quiet older man whose weathered face gave away nothing except competence. The elders did not stop them. Old Dorje pressed a bundle into Mara's hands at the village gate — dried meat, tsampa, a small brass lamp — and said only, "The promise Elias Vane made, your friend is trying to keep it. That makes him ours too. Bring him home, or bring us word honestly. Either is better than silence."

The first day's climb took them through territory Mara knew from years ago, when she'd come partway up this same route with Elias, before the old prospector's lungs failed him and he'd turned back, coughing blood into the snow, swearing the vault would outlive them both. The moraine had shifted since then, the way glaciers always shift, so that landmarks she remembered — a split boulder, an ice cave shaped like a whale's mouth — had migrated or vanished entirely. They navigated instead by the mountain's logic: reading the pressure ridges, avoiding the sagging hollows where crevasses hid under snow bridges, keeping to the shaded side where the ice stayed solid.

By the second evening they could see the gate.

It was even larger than it had looked from the village — a corridor of blue ice perhaps forty meters high, walled by fresh fracture faces that glittered like cut gemstone in the low sun. Meltwater ran along its floor in braided silver threads, already carving channels. Above the gate, the seracs leaned against each other like drunkards holding each other upright, and every few hours one of them shed a block the size of a house, the impacts booming through the valley like artillery.

"He's in there," Sonam said quietly. It wasn't a question. Fresh boot prints stitched up the corridor floor, half-melted at the edges but unmistakable, a single line of them heading deep.

"One man, alone, three days ahead of us," Pemba murmured. "Through terrain like this."

"He's had practice being alone," Mara said, and hated how much of herself she heard in the sentence.

They camped that night just inside the gate's shadow, in a hollow beneath an overhang of ancient ice. The cold came down off the seracs in waves, and the glacier talked to itself all night — cracks and groans and sudden sharp reports like distant rifle fire. Mara lay awake listening to it, and found herself doing what she always did on hard nights: talking to the dead in her head.

*I told him he brought trouble,* she thought. *I said it to hurt him, because I was frightened, and because it was easier to blame him than to admit I'd known what Harken was the moment I saw that gunboat on the river and signed on anyway.* Her father's ghost had nothing to say to that. Neither did Elias Vane, wherever the old man was. Only the glacier answered, groaning and shifting, patient as water.

In the morning they went in.

* * *

The corridor was beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful.

Light entered through fissures far overhead and came down transformed — green and blue and a strange luminous violet, so that the walls glowed from within and the meltwater channels burned like veins of light. Their crampons rang on the ice. Their breath hung in clouds. Sound behaved strangely, their voices arriving back at them seconds late from directions that made no sense, so that Mara kept hearing footsteps that weren't there, and once — distinctly — her own voice saying words she hadn't said.

They found the first sign of him near midday: a piton hammered into a fracture seam, with a worn sling tied to it, marking a crossing over a crevasse that had opened across the full width of the corridor. Someone had bridged it with an ice axe laid flat and anchored at both ends — crude, desperate work, done fast and alone.

"He crossed on that?" Pemba stared at the axe-bridge spanning a gap where the darkness below swallowed the light of their lamps. "Alone? Without a belay?"

"He crossed on that," Mara confirmed, testing the anchor with her boot. It held. Barely. "And we're going to cross on it properly, with ropes, like people who intend to survive the week."

Sonam went first, running the line across, and they crossed one at a time while the glacier ticked and settled around them. When Mara reached the far side she looked back at the axe-bridge and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite grief.

*You stubborn fool,* she thought, aiming it upward through the glowing blue dark, at a man three days ahead of her. *You absolute stubborn fool.*

They climbed until the light began to fail, then camped in a niche carved by meltwater centuries ago. Over the small flame of the brass lamp they ate tsampa paste and dried meat, and Pemba asked the question he'd clearly been carrying since the village.

"Mara-la. Why does it matter to you? Truly." He nodded at her pack, where the compass sat wrapped in oilcloth — Jude's father's compass, the one Mara had recognized at Kiln Landing all those months ago, the one that had made her sign onto this doomed voyage without telling him why. "You knew his father. You knew the map. You've known since the river. And at basecamp you turned on him like—" He stopped himself, embarrassed.

"Like someone throwing ballast overboard in a storm," Mara finished. She turned the lamp wick down and watched the shadows climb the ice walls. "His father saved my brother's life. Pulled him out of the Kapu River after the ferry went down — dove into floodwater, which no sane man does, and got him to shore, and then disappeared before my brother could even learn his name properly. Just a stranger who happened to be there. Except he wasn't a stranger. Later I learned he'd been asking about the upper valleys, about Kesh-Tamar, about the vault. He used my brother's rescue to get close to our family. To get information." She exhaled slowly. "Or that's what I told myself. For years. Because it was easier than the other possibility."

"Which is?"

"That he was exactly what he seemed. A man who did one good thing and then ran from everyone who might have needed a second one." She looked at her hands. "Jude is so afraid of becoming his father that he can't see he already carries the best part of him. And I'm so angry at the father that I keep punishing the son for it. At basecamp I—" Her jaw tightened. "At basecamp I needed someone to blame, and he was standing there offering himself up. That's not justice. That's just cruelty wearing justice's clothes."

Sonam spoke for the first time in hours, his voice a low rumble. "On the ice, guilt is heavy cargo. Leave it here tonight. Pick it up tomorrow if you still want it."

Mara almost smiled. "Did Elias teach you that?"

"Elias stole it from me," Sonam said, and for a moment the old man's eyes held something warm and far away. "Thirty years ago, on this same glacier. Then he carried it home to a dying prospector in the lowlands, who carried it to your friend. Words are like that. They travel further than the people who say them."

Above them, deep in the mountain, something boomed — a serac falling somewhere beyond the ridge, the sound arriving through the ice itself, through the stone, through the soles of their bodies. None of them slept well after that.

* * *

They found him on the fourth morning.

Not at the vault — not yet — but at the edge of a vast chamber where the corridor widened into a cathedral of ice, its ceiling lost in darkness far overhead, its floor broken by a field of melt pools linked by thinning bridges. And at the center of it, sprawled at the lip of a pool where a snow bridge had given way beneath him, hanging by one arm over water the color of deep twilight — Jude Quintana.

He was alive. Even from fifty meters Mara could see the rise and fall of his shoulders, hear the ragged effort of his breathing as he tried to swing a leg up over the broken edge. His right arm was hooked over a shelf of solid ice; below him the pool stretched away into blackness, and the shelf he gripped was shedding fine white crumbs with every movement, eroding under his weight like a sandbar in current.

"Pemba!" Mara was already moving, uncoiling rope. "Anchor point, that serac base — move!"

They worked the way she'd drilled them on the river: fast, wordless, certain. Sonam drove the pickets. Pemba flaked the rope into clean coils. Mara tied the harness knot with fingers that should have been shaking and weren't, because this — this was the one place in the world where her hands never betrayed her.

"QUINTANA!" Her voice cracked across the chamber and came back doubled, tripled, from the ice walls. "DON'T MOVE! WE'RE COMING TO YOU!"

His head came up. Even at distance she saw the moment he understood — saw him freeze, then try to speak, then simply hang there, breathing, while she ran the last stretch of ice with the rope paying out behind her.

The edge crumbled under his grip just as she arrived. He dropped — six inches, a foot, the splash of his boots hitting the submerged ledge below the waterline — and Mara threw herself flat at the pool's rim and slammed the rescue coil down to him. "GRAB IT! BOTH HANDS — GRAB IT!"

His hands closed on the rope. She braced, locked the line around her hip, and hauled, and Pemba and Sonam hauled behind her, three bodies against one man's weight and the water sucking at his legs, and for a long terrible moment nothing moved — and then he came free of the pool like a cork from a bottle, sliding across the ice on his belly, streaming meltwater, coughing, alive.

For a moment nobody spoke. The glacier creaked overhead. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of centuries.

"You," Jude managed at last, pushing wet hair out of his eyes, staring at her with an expression she couldn't read — disbelief, exhaustion, and underneath both, something raw and unguarded, "came after me."

"You're welcome," Mara said.

"You told me I brought trouble to your village. You told me—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You meant it."

"I meant some of it." She knelt beside him, checked his hands — scraped, half-frozen, still gripping the rope as if letting go might kill him — and forced herself to meet his eyes. "I said cruel things because I was scared, and I dressed it up as wisdom. That's my confession, Jude Quintana, and you can carry it or throw it in that pool, but either way I'm not leaving this mountain without you." She pulled the oilcloth bundle from her pack and pressed his father's compass into his palms, closing his frozen fingers around it. "Your father saved my brother's life and I spent ten years being too proud to say thank you to his ghost. I'm done. Do you understand me? I am *done* being proud on this ice."

He looked down at the compass in his hands. Water dripped from his sleeves. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough as gravel.

"There's something at the vault you need to see," he said. "Something of his. And Mara—" He looked up, and behind the exhaustion his eyes had caught the strange blue light of the chamber and held it. "Harken's men reached the lower moraine yesterday. I saw their fires from the ridge. We have maybe a day before they're in this corridor."

Pemba, coiling rope behind them, muttered a curse in three languages.

Mara stood, shouldered her pack, and looked up the glowing throat of the ice toward whatever waited at its end — treasure, truth, and a gunboat's worth of trouble closing in behind them.

"Then we'd better be faster than the mountain," she said. "Move."
