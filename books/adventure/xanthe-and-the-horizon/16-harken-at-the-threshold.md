## 16. Harken at the Threshold

**JUDE**

The vault breathed.

That was the only word Jude could find for it. The chamber exhaled a slow, cold wind through the ice tunnel behind them, and somewhere far above, the glacier groaned like an old ship changing its mind. He stood at the threshold of the Sunken Vault of Kesh-Tamar with his father's field kit open on a stone shelf, and he did not touch the gold.

He couldn't stop looking at it, though.

It filled the chamber the way water fills a bowl. Stacked ingots darkened with age. Ceremonial masks with eyes of lapis. A bronze astrolabe the size of a cartwheel, its rings still turning on greased bearings after four hundred years in the dark. And beneath all of it, half-buried, the thing that had made Mara go silent when her lamp found it: a carved door, floor to ceiling, covered in the same script that ran along Elias Vane's map — the script that matched the carvings in Tsering's shrine hall, the script that matched, stroke for stroke, the marginalia in his father's field notes.

*He found it first,* Jude thought again. *He stood here. He saw this. And he walked away.*

His father's kit lay open between them and the treasure: brass instruments wrapped in oilcloth, a journal swollen with damp and dried flat again, a tin of tea gone to dust, and — Jude's throat tightened every time he looked at it — a photograph of himself at nine years old, gap-toothed, squinting into sun on some dig his father had dragged him to before everything went wrong. His father had carried that picture up here, into the ice, and then sealed the tunnel behind him rather than let anyone else follow.

"Jude." Mara's voice came low from the corridor mouth. "Come look at this."

She was crouched where the ice tunnel met the vault's outer antechamber, her lamp angled down at the floor. Water sheeted across the stone in a film thin as breath, moving — not pooling, *moving*, a slow pulse toward the inner chamber, as though the glacier were drinking.

"That wasn't there when we came down," she said.

"It's meltwater." Even as he said it, Jude heard how thin it sounded. "Calving season. The whole mass is shifting."

"The whole mass is shifting *toward us*." Mara straightened. She'd taken a bad fall on the White Stair and favored her left side when she thought he wasn't watching; now she planted herself deliberately square, refusing the limp. "How long do we have?"

"I don't know."

"You're the archaeologist."

"I'm the son of one," Jude said. "There's a difference, and you've spent two weeks reminding me of it."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, not quite forgiveness for what he'd said at basecamp, or for what she'd said back. They had come down the ice gate together because the alternative was watching each other die alone, and neither of them had decided yet whether that made them partners or just witnesses.

From deeper in the village-side passage, Old Dorje called out. The guide who had followed Mara down — Tenzin, nineteen, steady as a plumb line — was checking the anchor lines they'd fixed for the climb back out. Dorje's voice echoed strangely off the ice, flattened and multiplied, so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

"He says the light is wrong," Mara translated. "The blue in the ice has changed since morning. In his grandfather's time, men read the color like we read weather. Blue going white means the ice is letting go of itself."

Jude looked up. The tunnel walls glowed with that deep glacial blue he'd first seen from inside, the color of deep water, of held breath. But along the seams, threads of white had begun to spider outward, like frost forming in fast motion, like cracks deciding where to go.

"We take what we can carry," he said. "Notes. Nothing else."

Mara turned and looked back at the treasure — really looked, and Jude watched her do the arithmetic he'd been doing since they arrived. Enough gold to buy a fleet of boats. Enough to clear Harken's debt twice over and never pilot another leaking hull through monsoon water. Enough to buy her brother's memory a proper headstone instead of the unmarked one she'd described at the drowned church, in a voice she'd kept level by force.

"We could carry out enough," she said quietly. "Not all of it. Just enough."

"If we take any of it, we'll take all of it," Jude said. "And if we take all of it, we're Harken. We're every collector who ever threw me off a site because my name was worth more than my work." He touched the carved door once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Elias gave this to Tsering. My father sealed it. I'm not the man who breaks both those promises in one afternoon."

Mara held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and began rolling the field notes into oilcloth with the efficiency of a woman who had made her peace with a decision and didn't want to examine it further.

That was when the sound started.

At first Jude took it for the glacier's usual groaning — the endless conversation of a living river of ice. But this was different. This was rhythmic. A deep, regular concussion, transmitted through the floor and up through his boots into his teeth.

*Boom. Boom. Boom.*

Tenzin appeared at the corridor mouth, and his face told Jude everything before the boy spoke a word.

"Men," Mara translated, though Tenzin hadn't spoken yet. "On the ice above. Many men."

* * *

**MARA**

She had run rapids at flood stage. She had piloted through a night storm with the bilge water over her ankles and a man she barely trusted bailing beside her. She had watched her brother walk into a river that never gave him back.

None of it had prepared her for the sound of boots on glacier ice.

Harken's party came down the ice gate in single file, roped together, moving with the ugly competence of men who had done this for money and would do it for more. She counted eight through the blue murk as they descended the last pitch — Harken in front, big and deliberate, his parka open despite the cold because Harken had never once in his life dressed for the weather instead of the impression. Behind him, hired men with climbing gear and, unmistakably, rifles slung across their backs.

"They brought guns onto a glacier," she murmured to Jude. "In calving season."

"Harken doesn't believe the world can kill him."

"Harken believes the world can be *billed* for trying."

They had retreated into the vault's antechamber, and it was the right choice tactically and the worst choice possible in every other way, because now they were trapped between the armed men in the corridor and the rising water at their feet. The meltwater film had become a stream, ankle-deep and quickening, pouring through channels in the ancient stone toward the inner chamber — toward the carved door — as though the vault were being refilled after four centuries of emptiness.

Dorje put himself between the corridor and the others without being asked, an old man with a walking staff standing in front of a rifle line. Tenzin moved to stand with him. Mara felt something twist in her chest — these were Elias's people, Elias's promise, and she had helped lead their ruin down to them.

Harken stopped ten paces away. Up close, the cold had gotten into him at last; his lips were cracked, his eyes watering in the wind that funneled through the tunnel. But his voice, when it came, was warm as a debt collector's handshake.

"Quintana." He let the name sit in the air, savoring it. "You know, I've chased a lot of men over a lot of years, but I've never chased one this far uphill. You've cost me a season, a gunboat, and three good porters who quit at basecamp. Do you know what that adds up to?"

"It adds up to nothing," Jude said. "Because none of it was ever yours."

"There it is." Harken smiled. "The famous Quintana pride. Your father had it too, near the end. Did you know that? When I bought his debts — and I bought *all* of them, every marker, every note — he told me the same thing. That it was never mine. Then he went up a mountain and died owing me, and I learned that pride is just interest compounding." He gestured past them, into the glow of the chamber, and his eyes went bright in a way Mara recognized from card rooms and auction floors. "And now look. Look at it. Four hundred years sitting under the ice, waiting for someone with the sense to pick it up."

"It belongs to Tsering," Mara said.

Harken glanced at her as if noticing a piece of furniture. "The village gets a cut. I'm not a monster. Ten percent."

"Ten percent of stolen—"

"Twenty," Harken said magnanimously, "for the pilot who flies straight. Quintana, I have eight men, three days of supplies, and a legal claim on your father's estate that predates anything Elias Vane scribbled on a napkin. The gold comes out of this mountain with me. The only question left is whether you're standing in the way when it does."

And that was when the glacier answered him.

It began as a crack — not the spiderwebbing in the walls but a single enormous report somewhere above them, sharp as a rifle shot, followed by a silence so complete Mara could hear the water running at their feet. Then the silence broke, and the groan that came through the ice was unlike anything she had felt in twenty years on rivers. It was the sound of something vast changing its mind about holding still.

White threads raced along the tunnel walls. Somewhere overhead, tons upon tons of ice shifted, settled, and began — slowly, terribly — to move.

"Out," Dorje said, in accented but perfectly clear trade-tongue. "*Now.*"

Nobody moved, because there was nowhere to go. The corridor behind Harken's men was the way they'd all come in, and it was flexing — visibly flexing, walls breathing in and out like a throat. Ahead, the inner chamber was flooding, the water around the carved door now knee-deep and rising, and the door itself—

Mara stared. The water wasn't rising past the door. It was rising *to* the door, and along its ancient seams, channels were opening, drinking the meltwater down, exactly as the carvings in Tsering's shrine had shown: the vault swallowing its own moat.

"The seal," Jude breathed beside her. "My father understood it. The vault floods its own approach. It's not being destroyed — it's *defending itself*. Anyone inside when the water finishes filling the corridor—"

"—drowns in a hole in a glacier," Harken finished. For the first time, warmth left his voice entirely. "Then we leave now, with what we can carry, and come back with pumps and blasting powder when the season turns."

"You'll never get machines up the ice gate," Mara said.

"Then I'll take the easy cargo first." Harken turned to his men and jerked his chin at the chamber. "Load what fits in packs. Move."

Two of the hired men stepped forward — and stopped, because Dorje had not moved, and Tenzin had not moved, and because the ice chose that exact moment to emit a sound like a cathedral tearing.

A section of the tunnel wall behind Harken's column bulged, cracked, and shed a block the size of a cart horse. It missed the last man in the file by an arm's length and shattered on the stone, and the report of it rolled through the corridor like thunder learning its trade. The hired men looked at their ropes, at the flexing walls, at each other. Mara watched the arithmetic happen behind their eyes — the same arithmetic she'd done at the treasure, the same every man does when the mountain starts talking.

One of them shouldered his rifle and started back up the corridor. A second followed. Harken grabbed the third by the strap of his pack and hauled him close.

"You're paid through the month," Harken snarled.

"And dead through the week," the man said, and pulled free, and went.

Harken stood alone in the shaking blue light with three men who hadn't decided yet, and Mara saw something in his face she had seen once before, years ago, on a man watching his boat take water faster than he could bail: the moment greed stops being ambition and becomes panic wearing ambition's clothes.

"Quintana," Harken said, and now his voice had dropped, gone intimate, dangerous. "Last chance. Help me load, and we all walk out rich. Refuse—" he gestured upward, at the groaning dark, "—and the mountain settles the account for me."

"The mountain already has," Jude said.

And then the water reached their knees, and the carved door began, with a grinding whisper four centuries stilled, to close.

* * *

**JUDE**

The door was closing the way glaciers move — too slow to see, too certain to doubt. The great bronze-banded panels were drawing together along channels that drank the meltwater, and Jude understood, with the clarity that comes only at the exact center of disaster, that his father had stood in this same spot and watched the same thing, and had chosen to let it close, and had climbed back out alone, and had carried the secret until it killed him.

The mechanism wanted water. The vault was sealing its own tomb, and everything inside it — the gold, the masks, the astrolabe, the proof of his father's redemption — was about to go under for another age.

Unless someone kept the channel open.

"Jude." Mara's hand closed on his arm. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"The door drinks the water. If the water keeps flowing, it stays open long enough for everyone to get out. If it closes—" he looked at Harken, at the three remaining hired men, at Dorje and Tenzin, "—it closes on whoever's inside."

"Then everyone leaves," Mara said. "Everyone. Now."

"Someone holds the channel."

"No." Her grip tightened. "*No.* I have buried one man I— I am not doing this again. Not you. Not for gold, not for a door, not for a promise."

"Not for gold," Jude agreed. He was already moving toward the inner chamber, boots splashing, lamp held high. "For the ledger. Harken says my father died owing him. I'm going to make sure the books balance before this mountain closes."

Behind him, Harken shouted something — an order, a threat, Jude couldn't say which and didn't turn to find out. The water was thigh-deep now and bitterly cold, cold enough to stop thought, and he waded toward the carved door with the last of his father's rope coiled over his shoulder, and the glacier screamed above them like a living thing, and the door ground shut by inches while the whole white world came down toward the sound of greedy men arguing with it.

He reached the threshold, braced against the flow, and jammed the coil of rope into the drinking channel at the door's base.

The door shuddered. Held. Began, slowly, to grind backward open again — buying minutes, no more.

Above, the ice gave way.
