## 17. What Cannot Be Carried

The water was rising faster than Jude had thought possible.

It came in sheets now, sheeting down the ice corridor in silver ropes, pooling at their boots, and the vault's great bronze doors — half-sunk, half-buried, older than any kingdom he could name — stood open before him like a mouth. Beyond them, torchlight caught on gold. Not scattered gold, not coins and trinkets, but *walls* of it: votive plates hammered with suns and rivers, ceremonial blades fanned like the feathers of some enormous dead bird, ingots stacked in courses as neat as masonry. A treasury sealed against the world for a thousand years, breathing cold air into his face.

Behind him, thirty meters back up the corridor, Harken's men were shouting. The ice groaned around them all, a long animal sound, the glacier shifting its weight as meltwater found new paths through its body.

"Quintana!" Harken's voice carried flat off the ice. "Step away from the door!"

Jude didn't step away. He stood in the threshold with his father's compass open in one hand and Elias Vane's map folded in his breast pocket, and he did the arithmetic that had been doing itself in his head since the moment the vault opened.

The debt to Harken was four years old and compounding. It had begun as a shovel loan and become a chain; it had followed him across three provinces and two rivers and finally up a glacier, wearing a gunboat's wake. Enough gold sat ten paces inside these doors to erase it twice over. Enough gold to buy back his father's name from every tongue that had used it as an insult. Enough gold to never dig another man's trench again.

He could carry maybe forty pounds out of here at a run. He'd carried heavier.

Mara stood beside him, breathing hard, her left arm cradled against her ribs where a fall on the descent had done its damage. Behind her, young Deni of Tsering held the torch, and the boy's face in the firelight was not greedy or awed but *afraid* — afraid the way you're afraid when you understand that something is about to be lost forever and you are standing inside the losing.

"The seal," Deni said. "Elder Somo said there is a seal. The carvings —" He thrust the torch toward the doorframe, and Jude saw what he had been too dazzled to see: beneath the suns and rivers, running down both jambs, a channel. Grooved stone, or grooved something, descending into the floor and vanishing. And set into the sill, a wheel of dark metal no bigger than a cartwheel hub, its spokes worn smooth by hands a millennium gone.

His father's field notes were in his other pocket. He had read them by headlamp three hours ago, hunched in the ice tunnel while Mara slept: *Found it. Found all of it. Sealed the approach myself — the melt year is coming and this valley cannot hold both the treasure and the village. Let the ice keep what I could not carry. Forgive me the years. J.Q.*

Sealed the approach himself. His father had stood exactly here, with exactly this choice, and had chosen the valley.

"Jude." Mara's voice, low. She wasn't looking at the gold. She was looking at him. "Whatever you're deciding, decide fast."

* * *

Harken came down the corridor at a walk, which was worse than a run. A man who runs has lost his nerve; a man who walks owns the ground. His hired men fanned behind him, boots squealing on wet ice, and their lamps threw long shadows that made six men look like twelve.

"I watched you climb out of holes your whole life," Harken said, stopping ten paces off, water to his ankles now. "You're good at leaving things behind. That's your one talent. So leave. Take your pilot and the boy and go, and I'll even call the debt settled, because what's under that door settles everything."

"You can't carry it," Jude said. "Not with six men. Not with sixty. The route closes in days. Every ingot you haul up the White Stair is a day you don't have."

"Then I'll take what I can." Harken smiled, and it was almost friendly, which was how he always looked when he was about to take something. "Starting with the map."

The first lamp went out then — not blown, *drowned*, guttering as meltwater surged past the men's knees — and in the dark Jude moved.

Not toward the gold. Toward the wheel.

It was stiff beyond reason, as if the hands that built it had intended exactly that: a mechanism that would only turn for someone willing to break themselves against it. He got both arms around the spokes and pulled, and nothing, and pulled again, and felt something deep in the mountain shift — a sound like a knuckle cracking in the earth's own hand.

"What is he doing?" one of Harken's men shouted. "What's he *doing*?"

"Stop him!"

But the corridor was water now, thigh-deep and shoving, and running through thigh-deep water takes time, and Jude had spent four years learning exactly how much work a body could do in the space between a shout and a arrival. He put his boot against the sill and hauled with his legs, with his back, with every borrowed shovel of strength he'd ever earned in other people's dirt, and the wheel turned.

A quarter revolution. Half.

And the vault answered.

* * *

The sound was not loud. That was the terrible part. He had expected grinding, thunder, some cinematic collapse. Instead there was a soft hydraulic sigh from somewhere far below, a pressure change that popped his ears, and then the water in the corridor reversed — pulled backward, toward the doors, as if the mountain had inhaled.

Then the channels in the jambs began to pour.

They poured upward, that was the impossible thing, water climbing the grooves in the doorframe as though the stone remembered being a fountain, spilling over the lintel, sheeting down the outside of the bronze doors in curtains. And everywhere it spilled, the ice *grew*. He watched it happen: white blooming across wet rock the way frost blooms on glass, fast and total, the meltwater flash-freezing as the vault exhaled its ancient cold through channels designed for exactly this. A seal made of winter. His father hadn't flooded the approach. He had *frozen* it.

"No," Harken said. Just that. Quietly. A man watching arithmetic fail.

"Deni — Mara — *run*." Jude shoved the boy ahead of him and grabbed Mara's good arm, and they ran, because the freezing was spreading outward from the doors along the water, and the water was everywhere, and the corridor that had taken them an hour to descend was becoming a white throat closing behind them.

Harken's men ran too. Everyone ran. But greed makes poor decisions at speed, and two of the six had gone for the gold instead of the exit — Jude heard them, heard the scrape of an ingot coming free, heard Harken screaming at them to leave it, *leave it* — and then the ice took the corridor floor in a single crackling rush and the shouting changed key. Became shorter. Became the kind of sounds men make when the ground stops agreeing to hold them.

Jude didn't look back. He wanted to believe he wouldn't have looked back regardless, but honesty was the inheritance of this whole journey, so he admitted it: he looked back once, at the doorway of light and gold, at Harken silhouted against it with his arms full of nothing, at the white climbing the walls like a tide in reverse.

Then the ice took the corridor between them, and the vault of Kesh-Tamar closed its mouth, and the last thing Jude saw of it was his own reflection in the freeze — a thin man with his father's jaw, empty-handed, running.

* * *

They came out of the ice gate into evening light so clean it hurt.

The calving field spread below them, blue and vast, and somewhere out on it the glacier was still doing its slow seasonal work, shedding centuries into the sea with sounds like distant artillery. Mara collapsed onto a rock and laughed — actually laughed, breathless and slightly hysterical, holding her bad arm. Deni sat down where he stood, torch still clutched in his fist, burned down to a stub he didn't seem to know he was holding.

Jude stood apart, shaking. Not from cold. From the specific tremor of a man who has just discovered what he is worth when everything sellable is gone.

Four years of debt, drowned in ice. His father's name, unbuyable now, unredeemable in coin — redeemable only in the way it had finally been redeemed, by a dead man's choice that his living son had just repeated. He had come up the mountain intending, if he was honest, to take *something*. Everyone takes something. And the vault had offered him the only payment that mattered: the knowledge of what his father was, and the chance to be the same thing.

"Your pockets," Mara said, watching him. "You could've filled them. Nobody would've known."

"I'd have known."

She nodded slowly, and something passed between them that neither of them named — the same thing that had been passing between them since Kiln Landing, getting heavier each river each mile each storm.

Deni stood, finally, and looked back up at the ice gate, already whitening, already forgetting them. "Elder Somo will ask what we saw."

Jude took out Elias Vane's map, creased and soft as cloth now, and held it a moment. Then he put it away. "Tell them the truth. Tell them Elias kept his promise." He looked east, down-valley, where the lights of Tsering would be coming on in the blue dusk, small and stubborn against the ice. "And tell them we should get down before dark. Because Harken's still up here somewhere —"

The crack came from the calving field then, enormous, closer than any sound had a right to be, and out on the blue ice a serac the size of a church leaned, groaned, and began to fall — directly across the route down.
