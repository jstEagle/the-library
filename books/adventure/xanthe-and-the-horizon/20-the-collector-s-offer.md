## 20. The Collector's Offer

The letter arrived with the first hard frost, carried up from the valley by a trader who also brought salt, lamp oil, and a crate of oranges that had gone soft in transit. Jude found it waiting on the table in the longhouse where he'd taken his meals all winter — a thick envelope of good paper, wax-sealed, with a London postmark and his name written out in full: *Mr. Jude Quintana.* Not *son of Aldous Quintana.* Not *the archaeologist's boy.* Just his own name, spelled correctly, which was rarer than he liked to admit.

He turned it over twice before opening it. Good paper meant money. Money meant someone wanted something.

Inside, on cream stock that smelled faintly of pipe smoke even after three months at sea and two more overland, was an offer.

*Dear Mr. Quintana,*

*The Whitcombe Institute for Antiquities has followed with great interest the reports of glacial movement in your region — reports, I might add, that have been frustratingly vague. We are aware of certain rumors concerning a site of pre-dynastic origin in the high icefields. We are further aware — through channels I will not insult either of us by detailing — that you were among the last parties to ascend before the calving event of last spring.*

*The Institute is prepared to retain your services as a consultant. Your knowledge of the terrain, the route, and the probable location of the site would be invaluable to our survey team. The fee would be four thousand pounds, payable upon signature, with a further six thousand upon successful relocation of the site's contents to our facilities.*

*Four thousand pounds, Mr. Quintana, would clear any debts you may carry. Ten would make you a wealthy man. I am told you are not currently a wealthy man.*

*I will be at Kiln Landing through the end of the month. I would consider it a professional courtesy if we might speak face to face.*

*Yours in anticipation,*
*Aurelius Fenn, Agent*
*Whitcombe Institute for Antiquities*

Jude read it three times. Then he folded it along its original creases, set it on the table, and went outside into the cold to look at the mountains, because there were mornings when he still needed to remind himself they were real.

The peaks above Tsering wore their winter white now, clean and absolute, the glacier's snout hidden behind the shoulder of the ridge where the ice gate had opened and closed like a held breath. Somewhere under all that blue weight lay the Sunken Vault of Kesh-Tamar, sealed again — sealed by his own hand, by water and stone and the ancient mechanism his father had understood well enough to leave sleeping. Somewhere under it, too, if the ice hadn't crushed them, lay the last of Harken's hired men, though the river had carried most of that story away downstream and no one in the village spoke of it unless the children asked, and then only gently.

Ten thousand pounds. He did the arithmetic the way he'd once done it in trench camps, scratching numbers in dirt with a trowel handle. His debt to Harken was dead — drowned with Harken, or at least unowned by anyone who could prove it existed. But there were other ledgers. The boat, the *Kestrel*, sat patched and sound at Kiln Landing, and Mara's share of her season's pilot wages was still unpaid because she kept refusing to discuss it. The village had fed him since autumn without once mentioning cost. And there was Elias Vane's grave to mark properly come spring, with carved stone instead of a cairn of river rocks.

Four thousand would do all of it. Ten would let him stop counting.

That was the trap of it, and he knew it was a trap, and he sat with it anyway for most of a day, because refusing temptation honestly meant looking at it straight-on first, not pretending it wasn't glittering.

* * *

Mara found him at the drying racks at dusk, where he was turning strips of yak meat that weren't his responsibility but had become his anyway sometime around midwinter. She leaned against the post, arms crossed against the cold, and looked at the letter poking out of his coat pocket the way she looked at rapids — reading the water before committing.

"Fenn," she said. "Whitcombe."

"You know him?"

"I know the Institute." Her breath fogged. "They bought half my father's river charts after he died. Paid fair, actually. Then they used them to run salvage crews up every tributary between here and the delta, stripping graves. Fair payment doesn't make them clean, Jude. It just makes them tidy."

"He'll wait at Kiln Landing until month's end."

"Then you've got three weeks to decide what kind of man answers that letter." She took one of the meat strips, tested it between her fingers, nodded approval at his work without saying so aloud. "You already know, though. Don't you?"

He did. He'd known since the second read-through, maybe since the first. But knowing and saying were different muscles, and one of them had atrophied.

"My father sold things," Jude said. "Sites. Secrets. Whatever a collector would pay for. That's the shape of the disgrace — not just that he faked a provenance or two, but that everything real he ever touched ended up in a glass case in another country, and everyone who lived near it got nothing but the hole he left." He turned a strip of meat. The smoke rose straight up in the windless cold. "Fenn's offering me my father's life with better terms."

"And you're going to take the meeting anyway."

"Isn't that what you'd do? Look the water over before you commit?"

Mara smiled — the thin, sharp version she saved for moments when he surprised her. "I'd want to see his face when you tell him no. Yes. Go down. I'll take you as far as the landing. But I'm charging you the fare this time, and you're finally going to pay it."

* * *

They ran downriver over two days, the *Kestrel* riding low and steady through channels that had shifted with the autumn floods, Mara calling depths from memory and instinct while Jude worked the sweep oar. Winter had thinned the river; where the monsoon had been a brown animal fighting its own banks, the dry-season current moved green and deliberate, and the boat seemed almost to remember being a wreck and to be grateful otherwise.

Kiln Landing in the cold months was a smaller town than the one Jude had fled from in spring — fewer boats, fewer voices, the tavern shutters half-closed against the damp. Aurelius Fenn had taken rooms above the customs house, and he received Jude in a parlor with a coal stove, a decanter of brandy, and a wall map of the river system so detailed it could only have been compiled from stolen sources. Fenn himself was sixty-odd, silver-bearded, dressed with the expensive plainness of a man who wanted to be underestimated. His handshake was warm and dry and perfectly calibrated.

"Quintana. Sit, please. Brandy?"

"No, thank you."

"Of course not. Discipline. I respect it." Fenn poured one for himself and settled into the chair opposite, folding his hands. Up close his eyes were pale gray and entirely without warmth, which Jude found oddly restful — at least the man didn't pretend. "I'll be direct, since your time up-mountain is presumably valuable. The Institute has spent eleven years and a considerable fortune chasing the Kesh-Tamar site. Every expedition has failed. Every informant has lied or died. And then, last spring, a glacier calves on schedule, a village in the high valley suddenly acquires new roofing iron and imported flour, and a river pilot with a patched boat makes two trips to Kiln Landing carrying crates she won't declare." He sipped. "I don't need the vault, Mr. Quintana. I need to know where it isn't. Elimination is cheaper than excavation. Tell me the drainage basin, the aspect, the approximate elevation, and the fee is yours. You never have to set foot on the ice again. You never have to say the word *vault*. Four thousand pounds for a conversation."

Jude had rehearsed refusals on the river — blunt ones, angry ones, the speech about graves and glass cases he'd been composing since he was nineteen years old and watched men in white gloves lift a burial mask out of his father's trench. None of them came. What came instead was something quieter.

"May I ask you something first?"

"Certainly."

"The Institute bought my father's collection. After the scandal. At auction."

"We did. A significant acquisition. The Quintana material remains one of our flagship holdings."

"Do you display it?"

Fenn's eyebrows rose slightly, the first genuine flicker of surprise. "The core pieces, yes. The goldwork, the inscribed vessels. Why?"

"Because I've never seen them." Jude heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him more than anything else in the room. "Twenty years of hearing about what my father found, and I've never stood in front of a single piece. Neither has anyone who ever lived within a hundred miles of where it was dug up. It crossed an ocean in crates and became *yours*, and the people it belonged to got a story about a fraud." He leaned forward. "Here's my answer, Mr. Fenn. No. Not for four thousand, not for forty. I won't sell you a direction, a bearing, a guess, or a silence. But I didn't come down the mountain just to refuse you, because refusing men like you is cheap and I've done enough cheap things."

He reached into his coat and set a wrapped bundle on the table between them — oilcloth, tied with cord, soft-edged from a winter of handling.

"What's this?"

"My father's field notes. The legitimate ones. The ones from before the forgery, before the auctions — the surveys, the stratigraphy drawings, the translation work he did on the Kesh-Tamar script. Twenty years of actual scholarship, and it's good scholarship, whatever else he was. There's nothing in there that locates the vault. He found it and he sealed it and he burned the coordinates, and what he left me was the apology, not the address. But the linguistics alone would keep your Institute's scholars busy for a decade, and it belongs in a public archive, not a private case." Jude untied the cord partway, enough that Fenn could see the edge of a notebook, the careful slant of Aldous Quintana's handwriting. "I'm giving it to you. Free. On one condition."

Fenn had gone very still. Jude recognized the stillness — he'd seen it in collectors' faces his whole childhood, the moment the object on the table became the only thing in the room. "Name it."

"That it's catalogued openly. Published. Digitized if your Institute can manage it, so that anyone, anywhere, can read it — including students from the valley school we're building in Tsering, if the mountain roads ever allow it. My father's name goes on the cover exactly as history wrote it, disgrace and all. No rehabilitation. No editing. Let the work stand next to the shame and let people decide what a man was worth." He pushed the bundle across. "That's my price. Not ten thousand pounds. A library card."

For a long moment Fenn said nothing. The stove ticked. Outside, a gull argued with the tide.

"You understand," Fenn said finally, slowly, "that I came here prepared to buy a secret, and I'm leaving with homework."

"Yes."

"And that my directors will call this the worst transaction in Institute history."

"They'll call it that right up until the citations start coming in." Jude stood. "One more thing. The village that keeps the vault — if your survey teams come up the river asking questions, I'll hear about it. And everything I gave you today gets recalled, publicly, with a letter explaining why. I keep my promises now. It's a recent habit, but a firm one."

Fenn rose too, and extended his hand, and this time the calibration was gone from it — what remained was genuine, and slightly rueful. "My predecessor warned me the high valleys bred stubborn men. He undersold it." He gathered the bundle with both hands, the way one carries something heavier than paper. "Your father, Mr. Quintana. Was he a fraud?"

Jude thought of the field kit in the ice. Of the seal, tripped from inside, buying the valley another century. Of a letter written at altitude by a dying man who chose coordinates he knew his son would have to earn.

"He was both," Jude said. "Mostly the second thing, at the end. That's the version I'm keeping."

* * *

Mara was waiting at the dock with the stove lit in the *Kestrel*'s cabin and tea already poured, because she had guessed — correctly — that the meeting would either be very short or very strange, and either way he'd want something warm afterward. He told her all of it while the town lights came on across the water: the offer, the arithmetic, the notes, the condition. She listened the way she listened to river reports, interrupting only once, when he mentioned the digitization.

"A library card," she said. "You priced your father's life's work at a library card."

"It felt right."

"It was right." She handed him his tea. "Harken would have paid you ten thousand and burned the valley. Fenn would have paid you ten thousand and put it behind glass. You paid yourself nothing and put it in the world." She shook her head, but she was smiling at the windshield, at the dark river, at something ahead of them that he couldn't see yet. "Elias would have laughed himself sick. The old man swore his promise to a stranger in a tavern because he said you had honest hands. Turns out he could read a whole man off a handshake."

Jude drank his tea and let the compliment sit without deflecting it, which was also a recent habit, and getting firmer.

They cast off at dawn to catch the upstream wind. The river ran green and slow toward the mountains, and by the second bend the peaks of home were visible again, white on white, the glacier holding its secret the way the village held the map — patiently, in trust, for whoever came next needing to learn how to read ice.

It was Mara who spotted it first: a runner on the landing stage at Tsering's footpath, waving both arms, and beside her old Dorje the elder, and behind them, unmistakable even at distance, a knot of children running down toward the water shouting something the wind tore apart and delivered in fragments.

Jue brought the boat in fast. The children reached the dock before the lines were thrown, breathless, cheeks bright with cold, and the eldest — Tashi, who had lost a goat to a melt channel and gained a reluctant teacher — thrust a folded paper into Jude's frozen hands.

It was a letter. From the valley school board, such as it was — three elders and a headman, signed with thumbprints and one laborious inked name. Written in the careful hand of the lama who taught letters.

*The winter term begins when the ice breaks. We have nine students. We have a room. We have no teacher who knows rivers.*

*Come home. The blackboard is waiting.*

Mara read it over his shoulder. Then she looked at him, and the smile she wore then was not the thin sharp one, nor the windshield one, but something he had no name for yet — though he suspected, standing on the dock with frost melting in his beard and a blackboard waiting up the mountain, that he was going to spend the rest of his life learning what to call it.

"Well," she said. "Better answer fast. Class starts at thaw."
