## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The lower Meridian in late summer was a river that had already made up its mind. It ran wide and brown-gold and unhurried, braiding itself around sandbars the color of old coins, and it asked nothing of a pilot but patience and respect. Xanthe Sandoval had learned both at her father's knee, before her knees were even strong enough to hold a wheel, and ten years of running freight from Gray Landing down to Port Meridian had taught her the rest: that the river forgave nothing, forgot less, and rewarded those who listened more than those who looked.

She stood at the wheel now with the morning sun coming up behind her like a slow fire, throwing her shadow out over the bow of the *Provision II* so that she seemed to steer a boat made partly of herself. The deck plates ticked as they warmed. The paddlewheel turned its steady, patient circle astern, slapping water in a rhythm she could feel through the soles of her boots — *whump, whump, whump* — the heartbeat of everything she owned in the world.

Which was not much. Which was less than it had been last spring.

The debt notices were pinned to the inside of the pilothouse door, three of them now, each one folded into a crisp official rectangle by clerks who had never smelled river mud in their lives. She'd stopped reading them after the second. The numbers only got larger when you looked at them; that was the whole trick of paper. Out here on open water there was no paper at all, just the wheel warm under her palms and the channel unspooling ahead, and for six hours at a stretch she could almost believe the boat belonged to her instead of to the Merchants' Trust of Port Meridian.

"Sandbar off the starboard bow," Bram said from the wing. "The long one. It's grown."

Bram Hollis was sixty-one years old and built like something the river had rejected twice — all knuckle and gristle, a face weathered into the gray-brown of driftwood, eyebrows gone white as gull feathers. He'd been pilot's mate on her father's original *Provision*, back when Xanthe was small enough to sleep in a hammock strung between the chart lockers, and he had signed onto her boat without being asked, the way a man signs onto a thing he has decided is his religion. He did not say good morning. He said what the river said.

"It's always growing," Xanthe said. "Everything grows except my margins."

"Hm." Bram lifted his brass glass, studied the bar's pale shoulder where the current curled off it in a long lazy seam of ruffled water. "Two feet of new sand since the spring flood, I'd say. River's been generous this year."

"Generous with sand."

"Sand's money too, if you're a river." He lowered the glass. "Take us wide on the left seam. There'll be a cut through the middle of it by October, but today it wants respect."

Xanthe eased the wheel over, two spokes, three, feeling the current take the hull and lean against it like a large animal leaning into a halter. The *Provision II* answered her the way she liked best — without complaint, without drama, the way an old dog answers a hand it trusts. Below her feet she felt the engine change its note as Odile adjusted the throttle without being told, reading the same water through the deck plates that Xanthe read through the wheel. That was the thing about Odile Marchetti that people who didn't know boats never understood: an engineer who listened was worth ten who calculated.

From the galley below came a crash, followed by Ferro's voice raised in what might have been anger or might have been prayer — with Ferro it was often both.

"He's dropped the coffee tin again," Bram said.

"Third time this run."

"He says the tin wants dropping. Says it's bad luck to catch it."

"Did he catch it?"

"No."

"Then we're safe," Xanthe said, and Bram made the sound that passed for his laughter, a dry exhalation through the nose, like wind through a reed bed.

Ferro appeared on the stairwell a moment later, bearing a tray of fried dough and black coffee, a broad dark man with forearms like mooring posts and a medal of some river saint on a cord around his neck that clinked against the tray. He set the food on the chart table with ceremony, then paused at the pilothouse door, frowning at the papers pinned there.

"You should take those down, Captain," he said. "Bad paper in the pilothouse. Paper with teeth in it. It gets into the wood."

"They're just notices, Ferro."

"Notices are how it starts." He touched the saint at his throat, touched the doorframe, touched the doorframe again for good measure — Ferro believed in doing things in threes, or possibly in sevens, depending on the day and the depth of the trouble. "My uncle borrowed money once. In Vellano, on the coast. First it was notices. Then it was men standing outside his door. Then it was no door." He shrugged enormously. "I'm just saying the river doesn't like debt. Water always finds the lowest place, and debt is very low."

"Thank you for the philosophy," Xanthe said. "Is there breakfast philosophy too?"

"There is fried dough," Ferro said with dignity, and went below.

Bram waited until the stairwell door banged shut. "He's not wrong, though."

"I know he's not wrong."

"The Trust won't wait past harvest. Old Man Corvane's daughter runs the office now, and she's hungrier than he ever was."

"I said I know, Bram." Xanthe took a piece of fried dough and ate it looking at the water, because it was easier than eating it looking at the door. The bar slid past on their right, pale and patient, wearing its new sand like a fresh coat of paint. Somewhere downstream, past the long green miles of the lower reach, a clerk in Port Meridian was sharpening a pen.

* * *

They tied up at noon at Halfway Light, a fueling stop that consisted of a coal dock, a shed, a flagpole, and a dog of indeterminate ancestry who greeted every arriving boat as if it owed him money. While the deckhand — it was just Xanthe and the crew and a part-timer named Sedge who came and went with the seasons — while Sedge swung the coal baskets aboard, Xanthe walked the length of her boat out of habit, checking seams, checking lines, running her thumb along the rail cap where the varnish had worn through to bare wood in the shape of exactly where her hand always fell.

The *Provision II* was forty years old and looked every year of it, which Xanthe considered honest. Her father had built her on the bones of the first *Provision*'s sister ship, and she carried the name the way families carry names — heavily, proudly, and with a certain amount of dread. Her bell still bore the original casting date. Her engine was Odile's great ongoing argument with entropy, held together with custom-machined fittings and what Odile called "aggressive maintenance" and what Bram called "witchcraft with a wrench."

Odile was in the engine room when Xanthe climbed down, lying half inside the port cylinder housing with her boots sticking out into the alleyway, a rag in one hand and a torque wrench in the other. A smudge of grease crossed one cheekbone like war paint. Her dark hair was tied back with what had started life as a gauge wire. She did not look up.

"If you're here to tell me we're taking on coal," she said, voice echoing slightly inside the housing, "I can hear it through the frame. It sounds like someone burying me alive, slowly, in very small portions."

"Ferro sent coffee down."

"Then you may stay."

Xanthe sat on the tool locker and drank the coffee, which was terrible in the specific way Ferro's coffee was always terrible — burnt, bitter, faintly of the tin — and watched Odile work. There was a language to it. The set of her shoulders when a fitting came loose easy, the different set when it fought. The little exhale through the nose that meant *there you are*. Ten years ago Odile had come aboard as a hired hand for a single run and simply never gotten off, and Xanthe had never asked her why, and Odile had never said, and somewhere in the unasked and the unsaid a partnership had grown roots so deep that neither of them bothered anymore to distinguish it from the boat itself.

"Bearing's singing again," Odile said, sliding out at last and sitting up, wiping her hands on a rag that was mostly grease by weight. "Starboard side. High note, only under load. I've shimmed it twice."

"How long?"

"If I'm gentle with her? To Port Meridian and back. If we push hard through the shallows, less." She looked up, and her eyes were the flat gray-green of river water in shade, and they went to the pilothouse above them as if she could see through the deck. "How long do we have on the mortgage?"

"Harvest. Maybe."

"That's what I thought." Odile took the coffee Xanthe offered and drank it without flinching, which Xanthe had always privately considered proof of love. "There was a letter waiting at the dock office. I didn't read it. But it was heavy paper, and heavy paper means either money or lawyers, and we don't have any friends with money."

"We have friends with money?"

"We have acquaintances with money. Friends we have." Odile bumped her shoulder against Xanthe's, brief and deliberate, the way she said things she didn't say. Then she stood, rolled her sleeves down, and became the engineer again. "Tell your bearing I said behave."

* * *

They ran the afternoon reach with the sun full on them, the banks sliding by in walls of willow and cottonwood, kingfishers stitching blue along the cutbanks. Xanthe gave the wheel over to Bram for two hours — he refused to call it a watch, insisted it was "sitting" — and went below to mend a torn window screen and lose an argument with the ledger.

The ledger lost. It usually did. Freight rates on the lower river had been bleeding for three years as the rail line crept further south, and the *Provision II* was running cargo cheaper every season to keep the cargo at all. She could do the arithmetic in her head now without the book: two more runs at current rates put them even with the interest and nowhere near the principal. One bad season — one snagged hull, one dead engine, one month of low water — and the Trust would own the boat outright, and Xanthe Sandoval would own a seabag and a reputation.

She closed the ledger and went up on the roof of the texas deck, where the evening was turning the river to hammered copper, and found Odile already there, sitting against the smokestack with her knees up, watching the water go by.

"This seat's taken," Odile said.

"By whom?"

"By whoever sits here."

Xanthe sat down. The stack was warm at her back, ticking as it cooled and heated, and below them the paddlewheel kept its time. For a while neither of them spoke, because they were two people who had learned that silence shared was worth more than most conversations, and the river talked enough for everyone.

"Letter at the dock office," Xanthe said finally.

"I figured."

"Heavy paper."

"I figured that too."

They watched a heron stand on a bar with the patience of a saint, motionless, until the boat had passed and the heron was a gray smudge behind them.

"When I was nine," Xanthe said, "my father told me the river pays its debts. Everything comes around. Flood takes, drought gives back. He believed it like scripture." She turned the ring on her right hand — her mother's ring, worn thin as wire. "I've been trying to collect on that belief for ten years and the river hasn't returned my calls."

"Maybe the river's credit works slower than the Trust's."

"Maybe the river's a liar with good publicity."

Odile laughed — short, low, real — and leaned her head briefly against Xanthe's shoulder, a touch that lasted three seconds and settled something in Xanthe's chest that had been rattling loose since the third notice arrived. Then she straightened, because the roof of the texas deck was still work-adjacent and both of them were still captains of their own particular machinery.

"Whatever the letter says," Odile said, "we decide it together. That's still the arrangement."

"That's still the arrangement."

"And if it's bad news—"

"Then it's bad news on the water," Xanthe said, "which is better than good news ashore."

Odile nodded slowly, accepting this, or at least filing it. Downriver the first stars were coming out over the far bank, and the channel markers had begun to wink their small red and green eyes, and the world narrowed kindly to the width of the navigable channel and the length of the next bend.

* * *

They ran through the dusk and into full dark, Xanthe at the wheel with the searchlight sweeping the banks and the channel lights sliding toward them like slow fish. This was her favorite hour, though she'd never have admitted it in daylight: the river reduced to essentials, black water, lit marks, the wheel, the bell rope, the hum of Odile's engine coming up through the floorboards like a second pulse. Bram had gone below to sit — his word — and Ferro had left a thermos of coffee on the chart table as a kind of armed truce.

At midnight Odile came up, as she did on the long runs, carrying two mugs and the excuse of checking the steering gear. She checked the steering gear. It was fine. It was always fine. Then she poured the coffee and stood beside Xanthe at the wheel, close enough that their elbows touched, and they watched the night river together.

"Starboard marker's off station," Odile said. "Number fourteen. It's drifted maybe thirty yards into the channel."

Xanthe had seen it too. "I'll report it at Port Meridian."

"Nobody'll move it for a week."

"Then we'll remember it for a week."

"That's the job, isn't it," Odile said quietly. "Remembering what nobody else will bother to."

The boat slid past the errant light, close aboard on the correct side, and Xanthe felt the old pleasure of it, the exactness, the craft of putting forty years of iron precisely where it needed to be in the dark. Above them the sky was thick with stars, more stars than the towns ever let you see, and the river ran on beneath them carrying all of it upside down.

"My father used to say the upper river keeps a mood," Xanthe said. She didn't know why she said it; the words came up the way the bearing's high note came up through the frame, from somewhere deep and structural. "Down here it's all sandbars and patience. Up there, above the rapids, he said the water gets a mood on it. Like it remembers things."

Odile was quiet a moment. She knew — everyone who mattered knew — that above the Provision Rapids lay the gorge, and that the gorge was a word Xanthe did not say, and that the last boat to run it had been captained by a man whose empty grave stood on the bluff at Gray Landing with Xanthe's oath buried in it like a keel.

"Do you remember things?" Odile asked. "Or does the river do it for you?"

"Both. That's the problem."

The wheel spoke softly under her hands, correcting for a seam of current she felt before she saw it. Ahead, the channel bent east toward Port Meridian and its heavy-paper letters and its hungry clerks, and the river ran on, brown and broad and keeping whatever moods it kept.

It was nearly two in the morning when the dock at Port Meridian came into view, lamplit and smelling of tar and fish, and Xanthe brought the *Provision II* in against the ebb with the wheel whispering and the engine answering, a landing so smooth that the fenders barely sighed. Sedge threw lines. Bram appeared on the deck in his shirtsleeves, squinting at the dock office, where a lamp still burned at this hour, which was unusual.

"Someone's waiting," Bram said.

"At midnight?"

"Someone with money waits whenever they want." He handed her the dock office boy's note, folded in heavy paper, and Xanthe felt the weight of it before she read a word — the particular dense weight of paper written by people who expected to be obeyed.

By lamplight, in a handwriting too elegant for the river, it said:

*Captain Sandoval — Cassius Vane requests the favor of a meeting regarding a charter. Triple your published rate. He asks that you come alone.*

Below the signature, in smaller script, as if added as an afterthought or a hook:

*He knows the way to the source.*
