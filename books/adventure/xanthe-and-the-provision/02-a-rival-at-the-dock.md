## 2. A Rival at the Dock

The mortgage notice had been pinned to the pilothouse door for eleven days, and the wind had worried its corners into little curled horns, like something trying to become a moth. Xanthe Sandoval read it again anyway, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. *Final notice. Vessel and moorage subject to seizure.* The paper was cheap, the ink already fading in the river light, but the words had weight enough to ballast a ship.

She folded it back along its creases and slid it under the brass clip on the wheel where she kept her tide tables, her father's old river almanac, and the single photograph of the *Provision* that she allowed herself to look at no more than once a week.

Below her, Gray Landing was waking up. Fish carts rattled over the cobbles. Gulls argued over the offal pile behind Ferro's galley scuttle. The *Merrilee Ann* — her boat, her father's name for her mother, God rest them both — sat low in the water with a hold full of cured fish and copper ingots, ready to run the lower Meridian down to Corvane's wharf and back. A routine freight run. Eleven days of honest water.

Eleven days of not thinking about the mortgage.

"Captain." Bram came up the ladder with his cap in his fist, which he only did when there was news or trouble, and sometimes both. "Boat coming up the channel. Steam launch, white hull, flying colors."

"Colors? Nobody flies colors on this stretch but the commission boats."

"That's what I'm saying."

Xanthe took the glass from its bracket and found the launch threading the moored skiffs with an arrogance that told her everything before the flag did. The helmsman didn't slow for wakes. He made them. And standing at the rail in a coat the color of good whiskey, one gloved hand raised as if greeting a crowd that hadn't gathered yet —

"Cassius Vane," she said.

"You want me to turn him away?"

She lowered the glass slowly. Eleven days of mortgage notices. Triple rates. She could smell the rot in the offer before it was even offered, the way you could smell a dead fish under a dock — but she had learned, these last two years, that rotten things could still be eaten when you were hungry enough.

"No," she said. "Let him come alongside. But Bram — nobody signs anything until I've read it twice."

* * *

Odile Marchetti heard the launch before she saw it — the wrong engine note, too high-strung, a pleasure-craft motor being asked to work for a living — and she wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than rag and climbed out of the engine room into the gray morning light.

She stood at the rail with her forearms resting on the paint-scuffed wood and watched Cassius Vane's launch come in. She had never met the man. She knew him the way everyone on the lower river knew him: by reputation, by the shipping manifests that passed through her hands, by the way other captains said his name — half admiration, half spit. Vane Lines ran the middle Meridian like a man running a private toll road. Polished boots. Chartered surveyors. Contracts with clauses like fishhooks.

And now here he was, stepping aboard the *Merrilee Ann* as if he owned the gangplank, and behind him four deckhands were lowering crates onto her clean deck. Big crates. Crated machinery, by the stenciling — though the stencils had been painted over, badly, and Odile's eye caught the ghost-letters underneath the way her fingers could find a hairline crack in a casting.

*Meridian Geological Survey,* the fresh paint said.

Underneath, in older letters, half-sanded: *DRILLING APPARATUS — HANDLE AS —*

She looked up. Vane was shaking Xanthe's hand on the afterdeck, all teeth and charm, and none of the deckhands were looking at the crates because they'd been paid, presumably, not to look at the crates.

Odile looked at the crates.

* * *

"Captain Sandoval." Vane's voice carried the way money carries — smoothly, and into every corner. He removed his hat, revealing silver at the temples that had not been there ten years ago, when they'd last stood face to face at an auction where he'd tried to buy her father's charts out from under her and lost. "You look well. The river agrees with you."

"The river doesn't agree with anybody, Mr. Vane. It tolerates us. What do you want?"

"Direct. Good. I've always admired that in you — it's your father in you, I think, though I'd never have told him so to his face." He smiled at some private memory, and Xanthe felt the old cold move through her chest, because Cassius Vane had no right to memories of her father that she didn't share. "I want to charter you. Upstream."

The word landed on the deck between them like a dropped wrench.

"Upstream," Xanthe repeated.

"A survey charter. My people need to reach the upper reaches before the season turns. Water depths, current speeds, bank surveys — dull work, honestly, but the commission wants it done properly and my firm holds the contract." He produced a folded document from inside his coat with the flourish of a magician producing a dove. "Triple your standard rate. Paid half in advance, half on completion. Provisions included. You'd be a fool to refuse."

"I've been called worse," Xanthe said, and did not take the paper. "How far upstream?"

Vane's smile didn't move but something behind it did, some small adjustment of the machinery. "To the head of navigation. Wherever your boat can go."

"My boat can go a long way."

"I know. That's rather the point." He held the contract out again, patient as a heron. "Captain. I'll be frank, since you value it. I know about the mortgage. Everyone on this river knows about the mortgage; the bank talks the way banks talk. This charter pays off your debt twice over and leaves you enough to recaulk the *Ann* stem to stern. All I ask is that you point her upstream and keep her off the rocks while my people do their tedious measuring."

Xanthe looked at the contract without touching it. Triple rates. Half in advance. The numbers arranged themselves in her head with terrible ease — the mortgage gone, the hull renewed, Ferro's wages current, Bram's bad knee seen by a real doctor in Corvane instead of the dockside bonesetter. And underneath the arithmetic, quiet as silt, the other thing: *upstream.* The word itself was a door she had nailed shut ten years ago, and here was Vane with a crowbar, smiling.

"Where exactly are your people surveying?" she asked.

"The upper Meridian."

"The upper Meridian is forty miles of water. Be specific."

"Captain." Vane sighed, and for one moment let the charm drop, and what was underneath was colder and older and much more honest. "Your father went up the gorge chasing the source of this river. Everyone knows it. Everyone knows he didn't come back. Do you imagine I don't understand what asking this of you costs?" He tapped the contract against his palm. "But the river doesn't care about our fathers, Captain. It doesn't care about oaths or griefs or mortgages. It just runs. The question is whether you run with it or sink beside it."

Somewhere below them a gull screamed, and the sound went through Xanthe like wire.

"I'll read the contract," she said.

"Of course." Vane set it on the binnacle, weighted it with his hat, and stepped back with the satisfaction of a man who has already won. "Take your time. I'll be aboard the launch until evening. Oh — and Captain?" At the gangplank he turned, and the smile returned, brief and bright as sun on ice. "My compliments to your engineer. Those crates are delicate instruments. I'd trust no one else's hands with them."

He went down the plank. Xanthe stood at the wheel with the contract under her hand and the river sliding past below, going somewhere, going somewhere always, and thought: *he knew about the engineer. He's been watching us longer than today.*

* * *

The crates were lashed amidships by noon, and Odile spent the afternoon finding reasons to walk past them.

She wasn't spying. That was what she told herself, wiping down the engine room steps that didn't need wiping, coiling rope that was already coiled. She was an engineer. Machinery was her business, and unlabeled machinery sitting on her deck was as offensive to her as a misfiring valve. Any professional would do the same.

The fourth pass, she stopped.

The crate nearest the rail had taken a knock during loading — she'd seen it happen, a deckhand's carelessness, the whole stack shuddering — and the fresh paint over the stencil had cracked along a seam. Through the crack, in old factory lettering: *BORE HEAD — 14 INCH.*

Fourteen-inch bore heads were not survey equipment. Survey crews carried sounding lines, sextants, theodolites, notebooks. Fourteen-inch bore heads went on drilling rigs, the kind that chewed thirty feet a day into bedrock, the kind mining concerns shipped up rivers when they intended to take a mountain apart and carry it away in pieces.

She crouched, casual as a woman tying her bootlace, and put her palm flat against the crate's side. Through the planking she felt the packing straw, and beneath the straw, the unmistakable dense, balanced weight of machined steel — and something else, a faint rattle, glass on glass. Sample vials. Dozens of them.

Surveyors didn't bring empty vials, she thought. Surveyors brought vials home full.

"Odile."

Xanthe stood at the head of the engine room stairs, arms crossed, watching her with the flat patience she used on rivers and on people. Behind her, the pilothouse door stood open, the mortgage notice still pinned there like a wound that wouldn't close.

"You've been walking past those crates all afternoon," Xanthe said.

"They're drilling equipment." Odile straightened up. No point dressing it. "Bore heads, fourteen-inch. Sample vials by the gross. Whatever Vane's calling it, it isn't a survey. Surveys measure. This—" she nodded at the crates "—this takes."

Xanthe came down the steps slowly and stood beside her, and for a moment they both just looked at the crates, and at the river beyond them, moving south with its cargo of light.

"He says triple rates," Xanthe said. "Half in advance."

"I saw the advance draft. It's real. The bank will clear it." Odile wiped her hands on the rag, though they were clean. "That's what bothers me most. It's all real. The money, the contract, the polite menace of him. Men like Vane don't lie, Xanthe. They arrange the truth until it does the lying for them."

"Where do you think he's really going?"

Odile looked upstream, north, toward the haze where the valley narrowed and the water began to speak in a different voice. Toward the stretch of river whose name the crew didn't say above a whisper, and which their captain didn't say at all.

"There's only one thing up there worth a fourteen-inch bore head," she said quietly. "And you swore an oath about it."

Xanthe was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was level, and Odile knew that levelness, knew it was the sound of a woman holding a heavy door shut from the inside.

"I signed nothing yet."

"No. But you're going to." Odile said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "I watched your face while he talked. We're eleven days from the bank calling the loan, the boiler plate's thinner than regulation, and Ferro's been stretching the salt pork since March. You're going to sign, because the alternative is losing the boat, and the boat is—" she stopped, adjusted, "—the boat is everything. I know. I'm not saying don't. I'm saying we should know what we're carrying before we carry it."

Xanthe turned and looked at her then, fully, and Odile felt the look the way she felt weather changes — pressure first, then meaning. Ten years they'd worked this boat together, and three of them as whatever it was they were now, the thing that lived in shared night watches and hands touching over an open engine and a hundred small unspoken agreements. Odile could read a gauge faster than she could read a sentence, but she could read Xanthe Sandoval fluently.

"You think I should refuse him," Xanthe said.

"I think you already have refused him, in your heart, and your heart doesn't get a vote until Monday when the bank opens." Odile tossed the rag onto the coil of rope. "So yes. Sign. Take his money. But we open those crates tonight, before we cast off, and we know exactly what we've agreed to haul. Because if it's what I think it is, captain, then this isn't a charter anymore. It's a decision. And I'd like us to make it awake."

For the first time all day, something moved behind Xanthe's eyes that wasn't arithmetic or dread. It might have been gratitude. On the *Merrilee Ann*, gratitude was expressed as orders.

"Tonight, then," Xanthe said. "After the crew's asleep. Bring the pry bar."

* * *

They opened the third crate at midnight, by lamplight, in the shadow of the deckhouse with the harbor watch snoring fifty feet away.

The nails came out reluctantly, each one squealing like something alive. Inside, packed in oilcloth and straw, lay the bore head itself — a great ringed crown of hardened steel, its teeth glazed with fresh grease, beautiful and obscene, built for nothing in the world but eating stone. Around it, nested like eggs: the sample vials, rows of them, empty and waiting. And beneath the vials, wrapped in oilcloth of a different weave — government issue, Odile knew the stamp on sight — a sealed folio thick as a Bible, tied with wire, stamped with an insignia neither of them recognized and both of them understood.

Xanthe lifted the folio, weighed it in her hand, and set it back exactly as it had lain. She didn't try to break the seal. Some doors you didn't open until you had to.

"Bore heads," she said softly. "Sample vials. Commission seals." She looked up, lamplight catching the hard line of her jaw. "He's not surveying the source, Odile. He's claiming it. Or proving someone can."

"And he needs us to get him there," Odile said. "Because no pilot alive will run that water for him, and the one who might—" she stopped.

"The one who might is standing right here holding a pry bar," Xanthe finished.

The lamp flame leaned in a breath of river wind. Down the dock, Vane's white launch rode at its moorings, riding lights burning all night, patient as a creditor. Somewhere north, forty miles and a lifetime away, the river narrowed and rose and kept, as the villagers said, what it took.

Xanthe closed the crate and drove the nails back in, one by one, each blow of the hammer landing harder than the last, until the crate was sealed and the silence around it was sealed too.

"We cast off at dawn," she said. "Downriver. The freight run, like always. I haven't signed anything."

"But?"

Xanthe blew out the lamp. In the dark, her voice was very quiet.

"But he left the contract on my binnacle. And I haven't thrown it in the river yet."
