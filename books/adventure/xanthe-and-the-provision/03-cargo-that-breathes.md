## 3. Cargo That Breathes

Odile Ferreiro had a rule about machines: they never lied, but men who owned them often did.

She was thinking about that rule at half past four in the morning, standing on the *Meridian Rose*'s freight deck with a pry bar in her hands and the river breathing fog around the pilings below her. The dock lamps of Provision Town burned orange through the mist, and somewhere upstream a night bird called once and thought better of it. The crates Vane's men had delivered overnight sat stacked in three rows of six, tarped against the dew, each one stenciled with the same tidy legend: **VANE SURVEY EXPEDITION — INSTRUMENTATION — HANDLE WITH CARE.**

Eighteen crates. She'd counted them twice, because the manifest said fourteen.

That discrepancy alone wouldn't have kept her awake — manifests lied all the time, usually out of laziness rather than malice — but it was the weight that bothered her. She'd walked the rows before dawn while the deckhands slept, pressing her palm flat against each crate the way a doctor palpates a belly, reading what she could through pine and nails. Survey instruments were light. Theodolites, ranging poles, glass and brass and folded canvas: a crate full of survey gear had a particular hollowness to it, a rattle of loose fittings when you rocked it. Xanthe had run survey charters before, back when the mortgage was young and desperate, and Odile knew the sound of honest instrumentation the way she knew the knock of a healthy engine.

These crates did not rattle. These crates sat like stones. When she leaned her shoulder against the third one in the second row, it didn't so much as shrug. Something inside it was packed in oilcloth and packed tight, and whatever it was, it had been built to survive being dropped off a cliff.

She set the pry bar under the lid of the nearest crate and paused.

The fog moved. Down at the gangway, one of Vane's hired men — Pell, the surveyor, a narrow man with narrow hands and a smile like a ledger line — stood watching her from the shadow of the warehouse eave. He'd been there a while. She hadn't heard him arrive, which meant he'd arrived quietly, which meant he'd wanted to.

"Early," he said pleasantly.

"I keep early hours." She let the pry bar rest where it was, angled just enough to look idle. "So does your cargo."

"It's instruments."

"Mm." She straightened, wiped her palms on her trousers, and gave him the flat, unimpressed look she usually saved for boiler inspectors with dirty hands. "Then you won't mind if I check them for shifting. Captain's orders. Anything that breaks loose in a lock chamber becomes shrapnel, and I'm the one who has to explain the holes to her."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not guilt exactly; calculation, quick and smooth as water finding a crack. "Of course," he said. "Though I'd hate for you to void the seals. Some of the instruments are calibrated to the tenth of an arc-minute. Touch them wrong and the whole charter's wasted."

*There it is,* Odile thought. *Don't open them.* He hadn't said *don't open them*, not in those words, but the shape of it was there, dressed up in arc-minutes and courtesy. She smiled at him the way you smile at a man standing on your air hose.

"Then I'll be careful," she said, and turned back to the crates as though the conversation were over, and listened to his footsteps retreat down the gangway into the fog, unhurried, deliberate, stopping once — she was almost sure — before the dock swallowed him.

* * *

By full light the whole crew was on the deck and the fog had thinned to a gray gauze that made everything look unfinished, like a sketch of the world waiting for ink. Bram worked the derrick with his usual economy, hands moving over the winch levers without looking at them, and Ferro hauled chocks and cribbing while muttering something under his breath that was either a prayer or a complaint; with Ferro the two had long since merged into a single devotional register. Xanthe stood at the rail near the pilothouse door, arms crossed, watching the loading with the expression she wore when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Odile waited until the fourth crate was swung aboard and settled before she climbed to the rail beside her.

"The manifest says fourteen," she said quietly.

Xanthe didn't look at her. "I counted eighteen."

"You counted too."

"I count everything now. It's what debt does to a person." A pause. The derrick groaned; Bram feathered the brake; the fifth crate swung out over the water, pendulum-steady, and came to rest on the deck with a thud Odile felt through her boot soles. "You've got that look," Xanthe said. "The one you get before you tell me something I'm not going to enjoy."

"I haven't opened them yet."

"But you want to."

"I want to know why a man paying triple rates for a *survey* is shipping crates heavy enough to be anchor stock, and why his surveyor stands in the fog at four in the morning making sure nobody looks inside them." Odile kept her voice low, pitched under the creak of the derrick. "Surveyors don't guard their own theodolites, Xanthe. They complain about them. They leave them lying around and then blame everyone else when the lenses fog. This man treats those crates like they're full of teeth."

Xanthe was quiet for a moment. Below them, Ferro paused in his muttering to spit ceremonially over the side, appeasing some river spirit or other, and resumed.

"We're taking the money," Xanthe said at last. It wasn't defensive. That was the worst part — it wasn't defensive at all. It was tired, and clear-eyed, and it sounded like a woman reading a number off a notice pinned to a door. "I know what you're going to say."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're saying it with your face. You have a very loud face." But there was no edge in it, and when she finally turned, her eyes were soft in the way they only got in the small hours, on watch, when the river ran quiet and the two of them shared a thermos of coffee gone bitter and neither felt the need to fill the silence. "Triple rates clears the mortgage by midsummer. Triple rates re-caulks the hull, replaces the starboard shaft bearing I've been nursing along on prayer and baling wire, and puts actual wages in Ferro's pocket for the first time in a year. Whatever's in those crates, it isn't guns. I looked at the stenciling. And Vane's a snake, but he's a snake with a notarized charter."

"Notarized by whom?"

"A clerk in Provision Town who'd notarize a marriage between a mule and a barge if the fee cleared." Xanthe exhaled through her nose. "Here's what we do. We take the contract. We deliver him where he's paid to go. And if anything in those crates turns out to be trouble — real trouble, the kind that comes back upriver looking for us — then we ask our questions loudly and in public. Until then, we're a freight boat, and freight boats don't interrogate their cargo. They carry it."

"That's not how you used to talk."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "That's how I talked before the bank started writing to me monthly." She reached over and touched Odile's wrist, brief and warm, there and gone the way most of their tendernesses were on deck, in daylight, among crew. "Ask your questions later. That's the deal I'm striking with my own conscience this morning. You can strike the same one or not. But if you're going to open those crates anyway—"

"I am."

"—then do it before Vane's people are aboard, and put everything back exactly, and tell me what you find before you tell anyone else. Even Bram." Her jaw tightened fractionally. "*Especially* Bram. Bram's loyalty is a load-bearing wall, but he wears every thought he has on his face, and Vane reads faces the way I read water."

* * *

Odile opened the first crate at noon, in the engineering bay, with the door dogged shut and the ventilation fan running to cover any noise.

It took her forty minutes. The lids weren't nailed like ordinary crating — they were screwed, and the screws were bedded in wax, the kind of packing job you did when you expected the box to be inspected by someone you wanted to fool, or when you never expected to open it again at all. She worked the screws out with a driver wrapped in cloth to keep the metal from singing, lifted the lid, and peeled back oilcloth, and then she stopped moving entirely for a good thirty seconds, because what lay inside was not a theodolite.

It was a drill head. Tungsten-toothed, hydraulically driven, the kind of bit that chewed through frozen gravel and bedrock both. She'd seen its cousins mounted on mining barges on the lower Meridian, tearing at the placer claims down past Sallow Bend. Underneath it, nested in excelsior, lay drill rod sections, a pressure gauge assembly stamped with a foundry mark she recognized from the government works at Corvane, and — wrapped separately, at the bottom, in a waterproof wallet sealed with lead tape — a packet of papers. The top sheet showed through the waxed window of the wallet: a crest, a serial number, and the words **MINISTRY OF TERRITORIAL SURVEY — CLASS B — DO NOT DUPLICATE**.

Class B meant mineral rights. Class B meant the government's hand on the paper, which meant Vane wasn't freelancing. Which meant that whatever he was hunting upriver, he intended to find it, measure it, stake it, and own it — with the law riding shotgun.

She checked the other crates. Sixteen of the eighteen held variations on the same theme: drilling plant, core barrels, sample jars with ground-glass stoppers, a compact assay kit, chemical reagents packed in straw. One held tents and cold-weather gear, genuine survey equipment, a fig leaf of respectability laid over the rest. The last one — the eighteenth, the one that had been added beyond the manifest — held nothing but paper. Reams of it. Blank claim forms, pre-signed letters of authorization, and a leather folio whose brass clasp had been pried at and re-soldered, badly, by someone in a hurry.

Odile sat back on her heels in the lamplight of the bay, surrounded by the smell of machine oil and pine excelsior, and felt the first small cold stone of complicity settle in her chest.

Because here was the arithmetic, and she was good at arithmetic: she could go to Xanthe right now, and Xanthe would do the right thing, and the right thing was to march down the gangway and tear that notarized charter in half and watch the mortgage notices keep coming and maybe lose the boat by autumn. Or she could screw the lids back down, bed them in wax, and say nothing, and take triple rates, and tell herself — as Xanthe was already telling herself — that carrying a thing was not the same as doing a thing. That the river was long and conscience was a luxury item, priced like everything else these days.

She thought about the starboard shaft bearing. She thought about Ferro counting coins at the mess table, lips moving. She thought about Xanthe's hand on her wrist that morning, brief and warm.

She screwed the lids back down.

* * *

She told Xanthe at dusk, in the pilothouse, with the wheel lashed and the lamps of Provision Town strung out along the bank like a garland someone had dropped. Xanthe listened without interrupting, which she almost never did, and when Odile finished — the drill heads, the ministry seal, the blank claim forms, the folio with the soldered clasp — she stood at the wheel for a long time, looking at nothing, her reflection ghosted faint against the glass.

"Drilling equipment," she said finally. "For a survey."

"People survey with drills all the time. If what they're surveying is worth pulling out of the ground."

"Upstream of the Provision Rapids there's nothing worth pulling out of the ground. That's half of why nobody goes. My father—" She stopped. The name of the stretch of river hung between them, unspoken, the way it always did, a room in the house of their conversation that neither of them entered. "There's rock and ice and water that kills you for looking at it sideways. Whatever Vane thinks is up there, he's wrong, or he knows something nobody else does, and either way—" She turned from the wheel. In the lamplight her face was doing the thing Odile had learned to read years ago: the jaw set, the eyes moving fast behind stillness, a woman arguing with herself and losing. "Either way we're carrying the tools of it. Eighteen crates of it. To the mouth of the gorge."

"To the mouth," Odile said. "Not up it. His charter says survey stations on the middle reaches. If he tries to go above the rapids, that's outside contract, and you can put him off at Gray Landing and let him walk."

"And he'll pay us the balance owed to that point and no more, and the balance owed to that point doesn't cover the shaft bearing, let alone the mortgage." Xanthe laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "Listen to me. Negotiating terms for how much of a bad thing we'll carry."

"We can still tear the charter."

"I know."

"Say the word and I'll start unscrewing."

"I know." Xanthe pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered it, she looked older, and very tired, and very much like the woman who had once told Odile, on a night watch years ago, that the difference between a captain and a coward was mostly a matter of what the river asked of you that day. "We take the money and ask questions later. That's the deal. I struck it this morning and I'm going to be sick about it tonight, and tomorrow we load the rest and cast off, and if Vane so much as glances above the rapids, the contract dies on the spot and he can swim home with his drill bits. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Odile said, and hated how easily the word came, how smoothly it slid past the cold little stone in her chest, and understood, with the clarity of someone who has spent her life listening to engines begin to fail — the small wrong note before the big one — that this was how it started. Not with a bang. With a waxed screw, put back exactly where you found it.

They stood together at the glass a while longer, shoulder to shoulder, watching the town lights waver on the black water. Then footsteps on the stair, and Bram's voice announcing that Vane's party would board at first light, all seven of them, and that the tall one — Pell — had come by the office asking which cabin locks were changed since last season.

Below them, in the dark hold, eighteen crates sat in their rows, silent and patient as eggs.

Odile went down to the engine room to check the bearing she couldn't afford to replace, and found herself, without deciding to, checking the rudder linkage too.
