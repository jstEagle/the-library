## 4. The Oath at Gray Landing

Ten years is long enough to forget the sound of a man's voice and not nearly long enough to forget the shape of his hands.

Xanthe Sandoval was seventeen the spring they buried her father's grave empty, and she remembered everything about that day except the words the priest said. She remembered the mud sucking at her boots on the bluff above Gray Landing. She remembered the way the river ran high and brown with snowmelt, carrying sticks and whole trees southward like a procession of the drowned. She remembered her mother's coat hanging off her shoulders because her mother had stopped eating in the third week of waiting and the coat had been cut for a fuller woman. And she remembered — this above all, this she would carry into every pilothouse she ever stood in — the exact angle of the current where the Meridian split below the landing, the main channel bending south toward the low country and the smaller channel cutting north, hard and white, into the gorge.

The *Provision* had gone up that northern channel with eleven men aboard and come back as nothing at all: no boat, no bodies, no wreckage worth the name. A hatch cover washed ashore at Miller's Bend six weeks later, and a tin cup, dented, that Xanthe kept in her sea chest for ten years and never once drank from.

They put an empty coffin in the ground because the ground demanded something. Her mother wept the small, dry tears of a woman who had already spent all her grief on waiting. The village elders came down from the cliff houses in their gray wool and stood at the edge of the crowd like herons at the waterline, saying nothing, which Xanthe would later understand was their way of speaking.

Her father's name had been Aurel Sandoval, and he had loved the river the way some men love God — with a devotion that asked questions it should not have asked. He had run freight on the Meridian for twenty years, and he knew every sandbar from the delta to the Staircase, and somewhere in those twenty years he had begun to believe what the old charts only whispered: that the river had a source, a single pool of meltwater under the glacier, and that whoever found it would find the river's heart. The elders said the source was not a thing to be found but a thing to be left alone. The river keeps what it takes, they said, and gives back only what it chooses. Aurel Sandoval had smiled at them the way he smiled at weather warnings — politely, and while already calculating how much canvas to carry.

He went up in April, chasing the last cold window before calving season sealed the upper water. He took the *Provision*, her best boiler, and eleven men who trusted him more than they trusted the river, which Xanthe understood now was the mistake. You could love the river. You could not trust it. It did not deal in trust. It dealt in tonnage and current and the arithmetic of what a hull could bear, and it collected its debts without malice and without mercy, the way winter collects them.

At the graveside, after the priest gave up and the crowd began to drift back toward the landing, Xanthe stayed. The mud was cold through her boots. The coffin sat in its hole like a lie everyone had agreed to tell together.

She knelt and put her palm flat on the raw earth, and she spoke aloud, because her father had always said a promise made silently was a promise made to yourself, and promises to yourself were the first ones broken.

"I'll keep your boat," she said. "Whatever's left of her name, I'll keep it running."

And then, because the gorge was there — she could see the white seam of it between the bluffs, three miles north, thin as a scar — she said the other thing. The thing she had not planned to say.

"And I will never run the water you died in. Not for money. Not for glory. Not even to find out what happened to you. I swear it on this grave, Papa. On the empty part especially."

The wind came down off the mountain and moved through the pines, and if it answered her, it answered in a language she couldn't read. She stood, wiped her hands on her coat — her mother's coat, which she had claimed without asking — and walked down to the landing where the villagers were already pushing a borrowed skiff into the shallows for her, because even at seventeen she had a reputation for being able to handle water, and even at seventeen the reputation was heavier than any boat.

The elders watched her go. One of them, a woman named Maren with a face like driftwood, called out something in the old tongue that Xanthe didn't understand then. Ten years later, working the lower river, hearing the cliff villages' dialect from dockmen and traders, she finally translated it, and wished she hadn't:

*The daughter swears by the door she has not yet reached.*

* * *

Present-day, the turnoff came at mile one hundred forty, and Xanthe felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a storm front in the ache of an old break.

It was midmorning, the light still coming down slantwise off the eastern ridges, gilding the water so the whole river looked poured rather than flowing. They were four days out of Vane's charter now, four days of hauling his silent crates upstream against the current, and the *Provision II* — her father's old name rebuilt around a new hull, or maybe the same hull, depending on how superstitious you were, and Ferro was very superstitious indeed — was riding heavy but true. Odile had the engine tuned to a purr so fine you could balance a coin on the rail amidships. Bram had the lead line out every quarter mile, calling depths in his gravel voice like a man reciting scripture he half believed.

"Twelve feet," Bram called. "Bar shifting left. Give her the middle."

"Middle," Xanthe echoed, and touched the wheel, and the boat obeyed, and for a moment — a whole golden moment — the river was just the river, and the work was just the work, and the debt notices pinned inside the pilothouse door were just paper.

Then the channel bent north around Hanging Rock, and she saw it.

The mouth of the gorge opened in the western bank like a held breath let go. From this distance it was only a seam of brighter water between dark cliffs, a white thread stitched into the green, but Xanthe's eyes had been trained on rivers since before she could read, and she could see the truth of it underneath: the drop, the constriction, the water going from wide and slow to narrow and furious in less than a cable's length. The upper gorge. The stretch above the Provision Rapids that no licensed pilot ran and no insurance underwriter would name. The water her father had steered into on an April morning ten years ago with his daughter waving from this same rail, or near enough to this same rail, too young to know she was watching a door close.

Her hands tightened on the wheel without her permission.

That was the thing about oaths made young: they didn't live in the mind, where you could argue with them. They lived in the body. In the grip of the fingers, in the set of the shoulders, in the sudden cold weight behind the sternum that felt exactly like a hand laid flat over her heart — her own hand, seventeen years old, pressed to raw earth over an empty box.

"You're fighting her," Odile said quietly.

Xanthe blinked. She hadn't heard the engine-room door open. Odile stood at the pilothouse steps with a rag in one hand and grease to the elbow, hair tied back, watching Xanthe with the particular attention she paid to machinery under strain — not alarmed yet, but cataloguing.

"I'm steering," Xanthe said.

"You're steering *against* something." Odile climbed the last step and came to stand at the rail beside her, following the bow wave out ahead of the boat, and then, inevitably, her eyes went where Xanthe's had gone. To the seam of white in the western bank. To the door.

Neither of them said anything for a while. The engine purred. Somewhere below, Ferro was banging a pot, arguing with it about beans.

"We pass it tomorrow," Odile said at last. "The survey grid Vane filed puts his first station above the rapids. Whatever's in those crates, it comes out of them up there." She paused. "You've known since the dock. Since before the dock. You've known where he wants us to go."

"I know where he *paid* us to go," Xanthe said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" Odile wasn't accusing. That was the worst of it. She asked questions the way her gauges asked questions — flatly, accurately, giving you nowhere to hide. "Because I've watched you take this bend twice today, and both times your hands did that."

Xanthe looked down. Her knuckles were white on the spokes. She made herself loosen them, finger by finger, the way you'd pry open a fist around a knife.

"When I was seventeen," she said, and then stopped, because she had never told anyone the whole of it, not Bram, not anyone, and the story had grown a shell around itself like a barnacle. But Odile was standing at her shoulder smelling of engine oil and coal smoke, and the river was right there, showing her the door, and the shell cracked easier than she expected.

"When I was seventeen, my father took the original *Provision* up that channel. Eleven men. Chasing the source." She nodded at the white seam. "Nothing came back. Not them, not the boat. Six weeks later a hatch cover washed up at Miller's Bend and the village decided that was the river settling accounts. We buried a coffin full of rocks at Gray Landing. I swore at the graveside I'd never run that water. Not for anything." She laughed, short and without humor. "I was so sure when I said it. You're so sure at seventeen. It's the last time you get to be sure about anything for free."

Odile said nothing. Below them the river carried on, indifferent, glittering.

"The mortgage," Xanthe said. "Vane's contract. Triple rates, half up front, and the bank's notice nailed to my own door where my crew can read it." She turned the wheel a degree to hold the channel as the current shouldered them along Hanging Rock. "I told myself it was just freight. Just a survey. Charts and instruments and men in good boots taking measurements. I can run a hundred miles of river and stop a hundred yards short of a line I drew at a grave. That's what I told myself."

"And now?"

"Now I can smell the gorge from here." She meant it almost literally — the upper water had a smell, the old-timers said, cold stone and old ice, a smell like the inside of a locked room — and the wind off the western bank was bringing it to her across four miles of bright morning water. "And I signed papers that don't say where the freight stops."

Odile set her rag on the rail. "Bram flagged me this morning. Vane's crates — the big ones, the ones he wouldn't let the dockhands touch — they're not instrument cases. The bracing inside is wrong for glass. It's braced for weight. For steel." She looked at Xanthe steadily. "I'm not saying it's a drill rig. I'm saying I know what bracing for a drill rig looks like, and I know what bracing for a theodolite looks like, and those crates are the first kind."

The word landed in Xanthe's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for it to hit bottom and it kept falling.

A drill rig. At the source. The source her father had died — or worse than died, *disappeared* — trying to reach. The source the elders said must be left alone. The source she had sworn, palm on raw earth, never to seek.

And the contract that would save her boat pointed straight at it like a compass needle.

"Captain?" Bram's voice floated up from the foredeck, easy, routine. "Water shoaling ahead, ten feet and dropping. Bar's building off the point. Want me to swing the lead again?"

"Swing it," Xanthe called back, and her voice came out level, captain-level, and she hated how easily the levelness came, how many years she'd practiced it.

Ahead, the channel forked around the building bar — deep water south, hugging the safe bank, the long way around; or the cut north, tight against Hanging Rock, saving a mile and a half of fuel and time, time Vane was paying triple rates to save.

Time. Everything came back to time. The bank's time, Vane's time, calving season's time. Everyone wanted her to move faster, and the oath wanted her to stand still, and the wheel was warm under her hands and alive, the way it always was, the way her father's hands had made it feel when she was small and he'd let her stand on a crate and hold it with him, both of them steering together, him saying, *feel it, little fish, the river talks to you through the wood if you listen.*

She chose the north cut. It was the better water, that was the thing — it genuinely was, the bar had built wrong and the south channel was fouled — and she told herself that was why, and she mostly believed it, and the part of her that didn't believe it stayed quiet, the way a passenger stays quiet in a boat they can't swim from.

The *Provision II* slid past Hanging Rock, and the mouth of the gorge swung abeam to starboard, close enough now that she could hear it: a low, continuous sound beneath the engine's purr, like breathing, like something enormous asleep behind a wall of stone.

Like a hand on the wheel, guiding hers, or holding it still — she couldn't tell which, and that was the terror of it.

Odile's hand covered hers on the spokes. Warm, greasy, steady. Neither of them spoke. The gorge fell astern, and the white seam narrowed, and narrowed, and was gone.

But the smell of cold stone stayed in the pilothouse the rest of the day, and that night, checking the cargo manifest by lamplight, Xanthe found the page where Vane's clerk had listed their destination in his neat clerk's hand — and the ink of the word *above* had been blotted and rewritten, and beneath it, faint as a watermark, she could make out the older word it had replaced.
