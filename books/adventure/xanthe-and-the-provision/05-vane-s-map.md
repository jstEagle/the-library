## 5. Vane's Map

The river gave them three days of kindness after the Staircase's first teeth, and Xanthe distrusted every hour of it.

Kindness on the Meridian was like a sandbar in fog — you didn't celebrate it, you sounded for it. She stood the morning watch alone in the pilothouse while the *Provision II* slid through a country of yellowing cottonwoods and gravel shoals that shifted overnight like sleeping animals turning over. The water ran low and glass-green, carrying the smell of frost coming down from the high country, and somewhere behind her in the deckhouse Cassius Vane slept in the stateroom he'd paid triple to occupy, and she had not yet decided what to do about him.

She had been deciding for two days.

Bram came up the ladder with two cups of coffee, handed her one without asking, and leaned his forearms on the wheel rail beside her. He was sixty if he was a day, built like a bollard, with a face the river had spent decades sanding down to its essentials: eyes, frown, hands.

"Vane's awake," Bram said. "Been up since before light."

"Since before light," Xanthe repeated. Passengers slept past light. Passengers who'd paid for a berth slept until Ferro's breakfast smell dragged them out of their bunks like fish on a line. "Where is he?"

"Aft. By the coal locker." Bram sipped his coffee. His voice stayed level in the way it did when he was handing her something heavier than it sounded. "He's got a fire going in the little brass burner. The one he uses for his tea."

"So he's having tea at dawn by the coal locker."

"He's burning paper, Captain."

* * *

She went aft quietly, coffee abandoned on the chart table, and stopped in the shadow of the texas cabin where the deck narrowed between the coal hatch and the rail. The morning mist still hung over the water in rags. Vane stood with his back half-turned to her, polished boots planted neat on her scarred decking as though they were a hotel floor, and he was feeding pages into the little brass burner one at a time — not crumpling them, not tearing them, but laying each sheet flat over the flame so it caught evenly and burned whole, corner to corner, the way a man burns documents when he wants them gone entirely rather than merely destroyed.

Xanthe had seen men burn letters from wives. She had seen a claim-jumper burn a deed rather than surrender it. She knew what careful burning meant.

"Mr. Vane."

He didn't startle. That was its own kind of answer. He turned with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been caught doing anything, and the firelight from the burner moved on his face, and in his hand was another page — and even across ten feet of gray morning air, even upside down, even with twenty years of river grime and one long decade between, Xanthe knew that handwriting.

She knew it the way you know your own name shouted across water.

It was a chart page. Heavy stock, hand-ruled, the ink faded to the brown of old blood, and the handwriting marching across it in that slanted, impatient cursive that had filled the margins of every book in her childhood — soundings noted in fathoms, current speeds in feet per second, and along the top edge, underlined twice, four words she had spent ten years trying to forget:

*ABOVE THIS, NO CHART.*

Her father's hand. Her father's words. On a page that should have gone down with the *Provision*, or lain drowned in some government archive, or rotted anywhere in the wide world except here, in Cassius Vane's fingers, being fed to a tea flame on her deck.

"Give it to me." Her voice came out lower than she intended, almost conversational, which was how her worst anger always arrived — quiet as deep water.

Vane looked at the page, then at her, and did something worse than refusing. He smiled, faintly, and laid the page flat on the burner anyway, and they both watched Elias Sandoval's handwriting curl and blacken and lift away as ash over the rail.

"You could have asked nicely," Vane said.

"I am asking." She crossed the deck in four strides and took the burner off the coaming and dumped it over the side, hissing into the river, and then she stood there with her fists at her sides because what she wanted to do with them would end the charter and probably her command, and the mortgage sat on her shoulders like ballast. "How many more."

"How many more what?"

"Don't." The word cracked now, the quiet splitting like ice. "Don't stand on my deck and play the gentleman with me. How many pages of my father's charts do you have, where did you get them, and what exactly are you burning out of them?"

Vane considered her. He was good at considering; she'd grant him that. He had the patience of a man fishing with a line too fine to see, and she realized, with a coldness that started in her stomach and spread outward, that he had wanted her to catch him. That this whole small conflagration at dawn might be bait.

"Below," he said. "My stateroom. If you'd care to continue this conversation like professionals."

* * *

His stateroom smelled of cedar and gun oil, which told her more than anything else he'd said since the dock. He'd brought a weapon, whatever the manifest claimed. He closed the door and unlocked a leather case — an actual lock, on her boat, in her hull — and lifted out a chart roll wrapped in oilcloth, and unrolled it on the bunk with the reverence other men reserved for scripture.

It was the upper Meridian. The gorge. The stretch above Provision Rapids that appeared on every commercial map as blank white space with a printed warning and nothing else, the stretch her father had gone up into and not come down from. But this wasn't a commercial map. This was hand-finished, annotated, alive — soundings threaded up the gorge like a pulse, campsites marked, portage lines drawn around falls that had no names on any official survey. And everywhere, crowding the margins, that same slanted cursive.

Some of it was burned. Whole sections had been cut or scorched away, ragged-edged gaps like missing teeth, and Xanthe understood with a sick lurch that the pages in the burner had been pieces of this — Vane editing, Vane curating, Vane deciding what she would and wouldn't see.

"You've had this ten years," she said. "Or close to it."

"Nine." Vane sat on the bunk's edge, unhurried, master of his own parlor. "Your father sold passage to a man named Aldous Wren, the summer before he went up. Wren kept copies of everything Sandoval let him see. Wren died. His widow sold his papers to settle debts, and I bought the lot, because I buy rivers, Captain, wherever I find them."

"You don't buy rivers. Nobody buys rivers."

"Nobody has yet." He smoothed the chart with one pale hand. "That's the entire point of me."

Xanthe looked down at her father's handwriting and felt the deck tilt under her, though the river outside was lying flat as poured tin. Ten years she had built her life on a single sentence — *he went chasing the source and the river took him* — grief shaped like a moral, clean and simple as a headstone. And here was the gorge mapped in his living hand, confident, precise, annotated with jokes in the margins — she could almost hear him saying them — and the simple shape of her mourning began to crack along lines she didn't have words for yet.

"Why burn any of it?" she asked. "If you're showing me this, why show me less?"

"Because parts of it are mine now, and parts of it are yours, and I'm a fair man distinguishing between them." He tapped a burned gap near the head of the gorge. "This section, for instance. Your father's final soundings. The approach to the source pool itself. I burned those pages this morning because I will not have them copied, photographed, or memorized by anyone whose loyalty I haven't purchased. Including you." He met her eyes. "Especially you. You're Sandoval's daughter. You have more reason than anyone alive to walk up that gorge and take it for yourself, and I'd be a fool to arm you."

"The source isn't takeable. It's a glacier melt. It's cold water on a rock."

"It's a watershed head feeding six hundred miles of navigable river," Vane said softly, "and beneath its outwash, according to assays your father himself carried upriver in sealed tubes, are rare-earth deposits that would make the Louisiana Purchase look like a land swap between farmers. Your father found it, Captain. Found it, measured it, sampled it — and then, for reasons his letters make painfully clear, he threw it all away. Sank his duplicates. Scattered his crew's copies. Went up that gorge a discoverer and came back down—" Vane paused, delicately, "—well. He didn't come back down."

The stateroom was very quiet. Somewhere below, Odile's engine turned over steady as a heart, and Xanthe gripped that sound and held onto it.

"You have his letters," she said. It wasn't a question. It barely qualified as speech.

"I have his letters." Vane reached into the case again and drew out a packet tied with waxed cord — oilskin-wrapped, river-stained, and even before he set it on the chart she could see the water mark blooming across the topmost envelope, and the handwriting on it, and her own throat closed so hard she tasted iron. "Eleven of them. Written upriver, the last season, posted through the village at Gray Landing when a trader came down. Addressed to Wren. They describe the deposits, the route, the depth of the source pool, the calving seasons. Everything a syndicate needs to file claim, build a road, and own the roof of the Meridian for a hundred years." He laid his hand flat on the packet, lightly, the way a man rests his hand on a dog he trusts. "I'm not going to read them to you, Captain. I'm telling you they exist, and that they prove your father found something worth owning — and that he chose, at the end, that no one would own it. Not even his own daughter. There's a paragraph in the last letter about you. Would you like to know whether it's kind?"

For one white-hot moment Xanthe forgot the mortgage entirely. She forgot the crew, the contract, the triple rates, the oath at the empty grave. She saw only her hands around Vane's throat and the packet torn free and the door open to the river where she could feed him to it piece by piece, starting with the smile.

And underneath that fury, braided through it, tight as wire through rope — temptation. Because the letters existed. Eleven of them, in her father's voice, from the last months of his life, and she had spent ten years talking to a grave and getting silence, and here was a man holding ten years of answers in a waxed cord, dangling them, and he knew it. He had known it since the dock. Every polished inch of Cassius Vane had been aimed at this: not the source first, not the money first — her. Her hands on the wheel. Her oath. Because no pilot alive could run the upper gorge unmapped, and the only person who might was the daughter of the man who'd mapped it, and the only way to steer her was to put her father's ghost in her ear and whisper *come and get him.*

"You're not hiring a survey charter," Xanthe said. Each word placed like a footing stone in fast water. "You're hiring me to run water I swore off over my father's grave, using his own charts, to a source he died keeping secret — and you're paying triple because you know exactly what you're asking and exactly what it costs me."

"I'm offering you the truth," Vane said. "Which is more than the river ever offered either of you." He began rolling the chart, unhurried, rewrapping his property. "We're two days from the turnoff above the Rapids, Captain. You don't have to decide tonight. But decide before the turnoff — because after it, there's no honest way back downstream for a boat our draft, and we'll all be committed to the gorge together. You, me, your crew, and your father's handwriting." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was the worst thing yet, because there was nothing cruel in it. Only certainty. "Sleep well."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She walked the deck until the moon went down behind the western bluffs, and then she climbed to the pilothouse and stood at the wheel she wasn't steering, because Odile had the watch, and Odile always knew when to say nothing.

Odile said nothing for a long time. She stood at the engine-order telegraph with her sleeves rolled, grease to the elbow, hair tied back with a length of tarred twine, watching Xanthe the way she watched gauges — patiently, reading pressures.

"He showed you the letters," Odile said finally.

"He showed me the *existence* of the letters." Xanthe's knuckles ached; she made herself loosen her grip on the wheel rail. "He keeps them locked like a pawnbroker. He burns pieces of the charts to show me he can. He wants me hungry, Odile. He's been fattening me on triple rates since the dock so I'd be hungry enough to bite."

"And are you?"

The question hung in the pilothouse with the lamp smoke. Downriver, far behind them, lay the mortgage, the debt notices pinned to the pilothouse door like knives, the bank man's soft voice. Up ahead, two days' steam, lay the turnoff — the green fork in the channel where the Meridian split toward the rapids or up into the gorge, the water she had pointed her bow away from every season for ten years, the water where her father's voice lived.

"I'm furious," Xanthe said. "That's not the same thing."

"It's upstream of it," Odile said. "Fury's where hunger starts, on you." She wiped her hands slowly on a rag, not looking at Xanthe, giving her the mercy of profile instead of face. "Whatever you decide at the turnoff — decide it out loud. To the crew. Not to him. He's already decided what you are. Don't let the crew find out from his version."

Xanthe looked at her — at the grease and the tarred twine and the steady dark eyes that had been reading her for years the way she read water — and felt the temptation and the fury twist together once more, tighter, and understood that whichever one won, it would cost her something she couldn't price.

"Bram," she said. "Wake Bram. And Ferro. Get them to the mess."

Odile paused, rag in hand. "Now? It's past midnight."

"Now." Xanthe turned to the forward windows, where the black river slid past and somewhere up in that darkness, two days north, the gorge waited with her father's handwriting folded inside it like a knife in a wound. "Because tomorrow I'm putting it to a vote — and God help us all, I don't know yet which way I'm praying it goes."
