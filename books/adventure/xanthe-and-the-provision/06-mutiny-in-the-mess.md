## 6. Mutiny in the Mess

The lamp above the mess table swung on its hook, and every face beneath it moved through shadow like figures in a lantern show.

Xanthe had called them down after supper — Bram, Ferro, Odile, and the two hired men Vane had foisted aboard with his crates, Pell and the silent one called Doss. She had waited until the plates were cleared because she knew how hunger bent a man's judgment toward comfort, and she wanted no comfort in this room. She wanted them raw.

She stood at the head of the table with her hands flat on the wood. The *Marianne's* hull ticked around them, the river murmuring against the planks, patient as a creditor.

"You've all heard the talk by now," she said. "So we'll have it plain. This charter isn't a survey. It's a run to the source of the Meridian, up the gorge above Provision Rapids. The stretch I've refused for ten years. The stretch my father didn't come back from."

Ferro crossed himself with a hand still greasy from the frying pan. Pell's face did nothing at all. Doss looked at the floor. Bram leaned back, arms folded, jaw working like he was chewing on something too big to swallow.

"Vane says the contract stands regardless," Xanthe went on. "Triple rates, paid at the source or not at all. He says we're committed." Her thumb found the grain of the table, followed it to the edge. "He doesn't decide what this boat does. We do. So we vote. Finish it upstream, or turn back now and eat the breach-of-contract suit together. That's the choice. Every voice counts the same."

The lamp swung. Shadows tilted.

"Before anyone speaks," Xanthe said, "I'll say what I'm voting. Not because I want to lead you there — because you deserve to know where your captain stands before you stand anywhere." She drew a breath that tasted of lamp oil and river damp. "I voted my oath ten years ago over an empty grave. Everything in me wants to turn this boat around. But I won't pretend the mortgage isn't real, or that turning back doesn't sink us slower but just as sure. So I'm putting it to you honest: I don't know which way I break. And I hate that I don't know."

The silence after that was the worst kind — the kind that has weight.

Odile sat at the far end of the table, forearms bare, a smear of engine grease at her wrist she hadn't bothered to wash off. She did not look at Xanthe. She looked at the tabletop as though reading something written there in old water stains, and her silence had a different temperature than everyone else's.

"Ferro," Xanthe said gently. "You first."

* * *

Ferro had been dreading this moment since dawn, when he'd watched Vane's men carry the last crate aboard and felt the boat change under his feet — not in trim, not in draft, but in *temper*, the way a house changes when a coffin comes into it.

He was not an educated man. He knew it, and he had made his peace with it the way you make peace with a limp. But the river had taught him things no school kept in its books, and one of them was that water remembers. Water remembered every drowned thing set down in it, every oath sworn on its banks, every name shouted across it and never answered. His grandmother in Porto Alegre had told him the sea forgot nothing; the Meridian, being younger and angrier, remembered even less kindly.

And they were talking about going up to its *head*. To the place where it was born. No man went to the birthplace of a river and came back the same, if he came back at all.

"I vote we turn," Ferro said. His voice came out lower than he intended, so he made himself sit up straighter, which helped. "Captain. I cook for this crew six years. I never voted on anything, I make the food, everybody happy, that is my whole philosophy." He spread his burned-again hands — the knuckles still shiny from yesterday's scald. "But I am telling you what I know. Last night I dreamed of white water standing up like a wall, and in front of the wall there was a boat with no name on it. No name, Captain. You know what that means on the water. That means a boat whose name the river already took."

"That's a dream, Ferro," Pell said mildly.

Ferro turned on him with more heat than he'd meant to spend. "You are three weeks on this boat. You do not get to tell me what my dreams weigh." He looked back down the table, at Bram, at Odile, at his captain. "I know you think I am a foolish man with the saints and the signs. Maybe yes. But everything I put salt on, I put it there because the river taught me, and the river is telling me — clear as a bell buoy — *do not go up.*" He swallowed. "I vote downstream. I vote home. If we go up, I go anyway, because I will not leave this crew on the water. But I want it said that I said no."

He sat back, spent, ashamed of how much it had cost him to say so little.

* * *

Bram had been waiting for this meeting the way you wait for a tooth to be pulled — wanting it done, dreading the sound.

He'd piloted the lower Meridian twenty-two years, first under Sandoval the elder, then under the daughter who'd grown into her father's shoulders and then some. He'd seen crews vote before, once, on the *Kestrel*, back in the bad years, and he'd seen what a split vote did to a boat. A boat could survive rocks. It could not survive a crew pulling two directions on the same rope.

So he'd done his arithmetic, and he hated it, and he was going to say it out loud anyway.

"We're eleven thousand mortgaged," he said. "That's the hull, the engine, and the berth we sleep in, signed against the bank at Gray Landing. You've all seen the notices. Captain pins them to the pilothouse door like weather warnings, but they're worse than weather. Weather passes." He held up one thick finger. "Turn back now, we breach the charter. Vane sues. Even if he loses, the lawyers eat a year while the bank eats the boat. We lose the *Marianne*, we lose our livelihood, and half this crew loses their houses, because I know for a fact Ferro sends money to his sister and Odile's got parts owed in Harrow Bend." A second finger. "Go upstream, we take our chances with the gorge. Hard water, yes. But hard water is just work. I've run bad water my whole life and I'm still here, and so is she—" he nodded at Xanthe, "—and she pilots better than her father did, God rest him, and nobody on this river would say that lightly."

"The gorge isn't bad water, Bram," Xanthe said quietly. "It's the water he died in."

"It's water," Bram said, and heard the stubbornness in his own voice and pushed through it, because the alternative was watching these people lose everything to paper instead of stone. "I've buried friends to sandbars and snags and one damn fool bet with a storm. The river takes people everywhere along its length. All the gorge offers is a longer drop and colder news." He looked around the table. "Here's what I actually think, since we're being honest tonight. I think the money saves us. All of us. Triple rates clears the mortgage, refits the hull, and puts a year of wages in every pocket on this boat. I'm fifty-eight years old. I don't have another decade of freight runs left in these knees. This charter is the last good card I'll ever hold, and I say we play it."

He paused, and his voice roughened despite himself.

"And I'll say the other thing too, since we're being honest. Ten years ago I wanted to go looking for Elias Sandoval. I wanted it bad. And I let it go because the living needed feeding. Well — maybe the dead need something fed too. Maybe this is how." He shrugged, huge and uncomfortable. "Vote upstream. That's mine."

* * *

Pell spoke fourth, smooth as poured oil.

"I'm contracted to Mr. Vane, not to this crew, so my voice is courtesy only — but for what it's worth, I vote upstream. The survey must be completed. The science matters." He smiled his small, correct smile. "Also, gentlemen, consider: whatever you decide, the decision is easier if you remember that Mr. Vane holds the payment. Turning back is not simply going home. It is going home poorer *and* sued."

"Your courtesy is noted," Xanthe said, in a voice that noted it the way a ledger notes a debt.

Doss grunted, "Upstream," without lifting his eyes from the table, and that made four.

Which meant it came down to Odile.

* * *

Odile had known this moment was coming since the night she'd pried open the third crate and found the drill casings nested in straw like eggs. She'd had weeks — no, days; it only felt like weeks — to compose what she would say, and every version of it had rotted in her mouth.

Because here was the thing she could not say at this table, in front of Pell and Doss and the swinging lamp: that she wasn't angry about the gorge. She was angry about the *hesitation*. She had stood in the dark of the engine room with Xanthe three nights ago, grease to the elbows, and told her what was in those crates, and Xanthe had said, *we take the money and ask questions later,* and Odile had agreed, had *chosen* it, had let herself be talked one inch down a road she distrusted — because it was Xanthe doing the talking. Love had made her complicit and she had let it, gladly, stupidly, and now the bill for that inch had come due at the far end of the table in the form of a captain saying *I don't know which way I break.*

Xanthe Sandoval always knew. That was the whole architecture of the woman. That was why Odile had signed onto this boat and this life and this slow, unspoken thing between them — because the river was chaos and Xanthe was not.

"I abstain," Odile said.

The word landed like a dropped wrench.

"Odile." Xanthe's voice, careful.

"No." Odile finally raised her eyes, and she saw Xanthe flinch, and hated how much it satisfied her, and hated the satisfaction more. "You asked for honest voices. Here's mine. I don't care about the gorge. I can keep an engine alive through anything if someone points it at the water. What I can't keep alive is a crew that doesn't know what it wants. You stood up there and told us you don't know which way you break. Ten years you've carried that oath like ballast, and the first time it's tested, you hand it to a vote?" She shook her head slowly. "Bram's right about the money. Ferro's right about the river. They're both honest. You're the only one at this table hedging, and I won't cancel anybody's vote out, and I won't add mine to a maybe."

She stood, chair scraping. "I'll be in the engine room. Whatever you decide, I'll keep the boat running. That part I'm sure of." At the door she stopped, one hand on the frame, and said — quieter, and only for one person, though everyone heard it: "I just wish you were sure of something too."

Then she was gone, and the lamp swung hard, and the shadows reeled.

* * *

Xanthe stood alone at the head of the table with four votes cast and the fifth walking away down the companionway, and understood that whatever happened next, something had cracked tonight that would take more than triple rates to mend.

She thought about her father. Not the legend — the man, hunched over charts by lamplight much like this one, chewing the end of his pencil, forever weighing, forever measuring, a man who believed every decision could be made correctly if you gathered enough information. He had gathered information all the way up the gorge. It hadn't saved him. Maybe certainty wasn't a thing you found. Maybe it was a thing you *made*, the way you made a channel through a shifting bar — by committing, hard, and steering like hell afterward.

"Call it," Bram said softly.

Xanthe looked at Ferro, who gave her a small, sad nod — *say it, Captain, I'll live under it.* She looked at Pell's smooth waiting face, at Doss's bowed head, at the empty doorway Odile had gone through.

"The vote stands three to one for upstream, one abstention," she said. "We go up." Her voice didn't shake, and she was grateful to it for that small loyalty. "Ferro — I'll not order you past the Rapids turnoff. If you want off at Gray Landing village, I'll see you paid through the season and walked to the dock myself."

Ferro shook his head, miserable and steadfast. "I said I stay with the crew. A cook who leaves the pot mid-meal, even the saints stop listening to him."

"Then we provision heavy and we go at first light." Xanthe put both palms flat on the table again, steadying herself against the wood the way she'd steady against the wheel. "One more thing, and then this meeting's done. When we pass the turnoff tomorrow, I'll be breaking an oath I swore on my father's grave. I want you all to witness it. Not to share the blame — to share the *truth* of it. Whatever waits up there, we walk into it with our eyes open and our names on each other."

Nobody spoke. The lamp had gone still.

Xanthe dismissed them with a jerk of her chin, and they filed out — Bram last, pausing beside her to squeeze her shoulder once, hard, saying nothing, which from Bram was a speech.

She stood alone in the mess a long time, listening to the river press against the hull, and then she went looking for Odile.

She found her in the engine room, of course, sleeves rolled, doing maintenance that didn't need doing, and Xanthe stood at the top of the ladder for a moment watching the lamplight move on her partner's bent back.

"Odile."

"Don't." Odile didn't turn. Wrenches clinked. "Not yet. Let me be angry tonight. Tomorrow I'll be your engineer again, and the day after, God help us both, I'll be your witness at the turnoff. But tonight—"

"Tonight I'm sorry," Xanthe said. "And tomorrow I'll be certain. You have my word on it."

The wrenches went quiet. Odile's shoulders lowered a fraction, not forgiveness, not yet — but ceasefire.

Above them, through the deck, the river ran on in the dark, northward, toward the gorge, carrying their answer whether they liked it or not.
