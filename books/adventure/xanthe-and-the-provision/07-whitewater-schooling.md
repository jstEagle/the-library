## 7. Whitewater Schooling

Bram had run the Meridian for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one thing worth teaching: the river did not care. It did not care about your mortgage or your mother or the name painted on your stern. It cared about tonnage and current and the angle at which a hull met water, and everything else — every prayer, every promise, every oath sworn over an empty grave — was so much ballast to be jettisoned when the water rose.

He was thinking this as he stood at the rail below the pilothouse, watching the Staircase come on like a rumor made solid.

They had heard it before they saw it. That was the way of big water — it announced itself through the hull first, a tremor in the iron bones of the boat, then through the air, a low grinding hush like millstones turning somewhere out of sight. The trees along the banks leaned back from it. The morning fog that had ridden with them since Gray Landing began to shred and lift, as if even the mist wanted no part of what waited downstream.

"Sound's wrong," Ferro said beside him. The cook had come up from his galley with flour still dusting his forearms, and he stood with his head cocked like a bird listening for worms. "Too deep. Water that talks low is water that means it."

"It always means it," Bram said. "That's never been the question."

The question, he thought, was whether the woman up there in the pilothouse meant it too — whether she understood what she'd steered them into when she'd voted herself upstream along with the rest of them. He glanced aft, where Odile stood at the engine hatch with her sleeves rolled, oil to her elbows, watching the water ahead with an expression Bram had seen on engineers facing boilers they didn't trust. And beyond her, lashed amidships under canvas, Vane's crates rode silent as coffins.

The *Provision II* — no, damn it, the boat was still the *Meridian Star*, whatever name Xanthe would give her someday if the bank ever let go of her throat — the *Meridian Star* rounded the last bend, and the Staircase opened below them.

Bram had seen it twice in his life, both times from the bank, both times with the particular gratitude of a man watching someone else's funeral. It wasn't a rapid so much as an argument between the river and the mountains, conducted in granite. The channel dropped in a series of ledges — five of them, maybe six depending on the season and the river's mood — each ledge spilling into a churning pool that fed the next. Between the pools the water ran narrow and fast as spilled mercury, shouldering through rock gates that would take a coat of paint off either side of a boat that came through crooked. White foam stood on the crests of the standing waves like snow that refused to melt.

Nobody ran the Staircase. That was not bravado; it was arithmetic. Freight went overland around it on the mule road, at cost, because boats that tried it became kindling and news items.

Xanthe Sandoval brought the boat down toward it at a steady eight knots, and Bram climbed the ladder to the pilothouse because if they were going to die he intended to die within arm's reach of the wheel.

* * *

She looked calm. That was the thing that unsettled him most, in the end — not fearlessness, which he could have dismissed as ignorance, but a specific and terrible attention, the look of a woman reading a letter written in water. Her hands rested on the wheel spokes light as a pianist's. Her eyes moved constantly, left to right, reading the ledges, the boils, the seams where two currents met and fought.

"You've seen this from the deck," Bram said. "Not from up here."

"I've seen it in my father's notes." She didn't look at him. "Forty-one pages on the Staircase. He drew every eddy."

"He never ran it either."

"No," she said. "But he measured it. There's a difference between a door that's locked and a door nobody's bothered to open." She tilted her chin at the water ahead. "There. See the boil off the far point? That's the third pool backing up against the ledge. Means the drop's deeper than it looks. We'll want the near channel, hard left after the second gate, and we'll take the third drop at an angle or it'll stuff our bow under."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then Ferro's cooking won't matter anymore." A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile. "Get Odile ready for full reverse on my signal. I'll need her engine to be honest with me. No lag, no sulking."

"She doesn't sulk."

"Her engine does." Xanthe's voice dropped, and for a moment Bram heard the girl she'd been, the one who'd grown up on this deck learning the sound of every plank. "Tell her I'm sorry in advance. Whatever I ask for down there is going to be more than the book says the engine can give."

Bram went down the ladder and told her, and Odile wiped her hands and looked upriver once, briefly, the way people look at a house they may not see again, and then bent back over her machinery with a tenderness Bram found almost indecent. By the time the bow entered the seam of faster water, she was standing at the throttle linkage with her hand on it like a rider's hand on a horse's neck.

The river took them.

* * *

Later, Bram would try to describe it to himself and fail, because memory smoothed things and the Staircase deserved to stay rough. What stayed with him were fragments: the first gate coming at them faster than anything that size had a right to move, walls of wet granite sliding past close enough to read the lichen; the wheel answering under Xanthe's hands in small, precise corrections, never a wasted degree; the boat's whole frame shuddering as the first drop took them and the world went white and roaring and sideways.

She called her marks aloud, half to herself, half to the boat. "Gate. Ease. *Ease.* Now left — left and hold—" The hull struck something that wasn't rock, just a wall of rebound water, and the deck canted twenty degrees and came back. In the galley below, Bram knew, Ferro's pots were becoming projectiles. Above, Xanthe's face never changed. She stood slightly forward on the balls of her feet, knees soft, absorbing the boat's violence through her legs the way a rider absorbs a horse's, and her eyes never stopped moving, never stopped reading.

The second pool nearly had them. An eddy line lay across the outflow like a seam in cloth, and where the slow water met the fast, the surface stood up in a ridge of confused green. Xanthe drove them across it at the exact angle her father had drawn forty years ago on a page now ash in Cassius Vane's stove, and the boat wallowed, shipped a ton of water over the starboard gunwale, and shook itself like a dog.

"Odile!" Xanthe's voice carried down the speaking tube. "Half astern on my mark — I want the prop biting before the bow lands!"

"Astern on your mark," Odile's voice came back, flat and steady as a spirit level.

The third drop was the one she'd warned about. From above it looked like nothing — a lip of white, a shimmer of falling light. From the moment the bow went over, it looked like the inside of a bell being struck. They fell farther than felt possible. The bow buried. Green water came over the forward hatch in a sheet, and Bram, braced against the pilothouse doorframe, heard the timbers groan the long groan of a thing deciding whether to hold together.

And then the propeller caught, astern, exactly when she'd asked for it, and the *Meridian Star* stood on her tail like a rearing animal and shrugged her bow free of the water, and they were through, slamming into the fourth pool, still afloat, still pointed downstream.

Bram realized he'd been holding his breath since the second gate. He also realized his hands hurt, and looked down to find he'd gripped the doorframe hard enough to leave marks in the wood.

Above him, Xanthe adjusted her collar with two fingers, the only concession to the fact that she was soaked to the skin, and read the next ledge.

* * *

The fifth drop nearly killed Ferro.

It happened fast, the way real disasters do — no drama leading up to it, just physics. The boat yawed coming off the fourth pool, a half-second late on Xanthe's correction because the current had other ideas, and the port side kissed a submerged shelf with a grinding crunch that Bram felt in his teeth. Not fatal. Not even structural, probably. But the impact threw the whole deck sideways, and Ferro, who had abandoned his galley to stand at the rail and curse the river in three languages, lost his grip and went down with his hands out to catch himself — onto the steam line that had ruptured along the deckhouse when the hull flexed.

His scream cut through the roar like a knife through fog.

Bram got to him first. The cook was on his knees, cradling both hands against his chest, and even in the spray and chaos Bram could see the skin already lifting, red and shining, the blisters rising fat and quick as the scald went on working. The man's lips were moving — prayer or profanity, impossible to tell.

"Ferro! Look at me!" Bram hauled him by the collar back from the line, dragged him toward the sheltered side of the deckhouse. "Ferro, eyes on me—"

"The river keeps what it takes," Ferro babbled. "I told them, I told them, the water talks low—"

"Shut up and breathe."

Odile appeared out of the engine room like smoke, a roll of clean cotton waste already in one hand, a jar of salve in the other — she kept burn dressings stocked the way other engineers kept spare gaskets, and Bram had never been more grateful for her grim thoroughness. She knelt, took Ferro's wrists without asking permission, and began working salve into the ruined skin with a gentleness that sat strangely on her calloused fingers.

"Hold still," she said. "It hurts worse if you fight it."

"It hurts worse, period," Ferro gasped, and then, absurdly, started laughing, high and ragged. "The saints are testing me. This is a test. I failed the last one, I know I did, I ate the eggs on Friday—"

"Eat all the eggs you want," Odile said. "You're going to keep those hands. Hold still."

Up in the pilothouse, Xanthe brought them through the final drop without ever looking down at the burning man on her deck. Bram understood why, later, when he thought about it in the quiet: because looking down was how pilots died. She gave the wrecked, scarred, screaming boat to the last ledge the way she'd given it to the first, and when the water finally widened and slowed and the Staircase fell away behind them in a long receding thunder, she rang the engine bell to all-stop and stood at the wheel with her shoulders finally dropping a quarter inch, and that was all.

No whooping. No shaking hands. Just a woman who had done a job that couldn't be done, and filed it away like freight signed for.

* * *

They anchored that night in a backwater below the rapids, in a pool so still it held the early stars upside down. The damage list, tallied by lamplight on the cargo deck, read like a confession: hull planking sprung at two seams, the starboard fender staves gone entirely, the deckhouse steam line ruptured, a lifetime of paint scraped down to bare iron along the port side, and Ferro's hands wrapped in cotton until they looked like two loaves of bread.

Vane emerged from the cabin he'd claimed for himself, immaculate despite everything, not one hair out of place, and surveyed the wreckage of the day with the expression of a man checking his watch.

"Remarkable," he said. "Truly. I confess I had my doubts."

"You confessed nothing," Bram said. "You hired us and hid behind your crates. Doubts don't bleed. Ferro bleeds."

"Your cook will recover. The boat will recover. And we are through the worst of it, thanks to your captain's—" Vane paused, choosing the word with care, "—instincts."

"Her father's charts," Bram said, and watched the word land. It landed. He saw it land, a flicker behind Vane's pleasant gray eyes, there and gone like a fish turning under the surface. "Whatever you burned, she remembers better than you think."

"Captain Sandoval," Vane said smoothly, "is full of surprises," and he withdrew below decks with the unhurried grace of a man who believed he owned the evening.

Bram stood a while longer at the rail. Below him, the backwater breathed. Somewhere in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

* * *

He found Xanthe on the bow, alone, her boots hooked over the anchor windlass, looking back upriver the way they'd come. The Staircase was invisible now, but its sound carried — a faint, continuous mutter on the edge of hearing, the river clearing its throat.

"That shelf we hit," Bram said, settling beside her. "Port side, fourth pool. Your father's notes wouldn't have shown it. Channels move."

"I know." She turned her bandaged-free hand over, studying the rope burns and blisters across her own palms — smaller wounds than Ferro's, earned on the wheel, but hers. "Thirty years of drift changes everything except the shape of the fall. He taught me that. Read the shape, not the details. Details rot. Shape stays."

"Your father," Bram said carefully, "never ran this water."

"No."

"But he wrote it down like a man planning to."

Xanthe was quiet long enough that the owl asked its question again, closer now. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, and Bram had the sense she was saying something she hadn't said aloud in ten years.

"When I was twelve, I asked him why he measured the Staircase if he'd never run it. He said — and I've never forgotten the words — 'Because somebody has to leave the map honest, Xanthe. Even if nobody follows it. Especially then.'" She flexed her burned hand, wincing. "I used to think that was about the river. I'm starting to think it was about Vane. About men like Vane. Somebody has to leave the record honest."

"Tonight, on that water," Bram said, "you did something I've watched pilots try and fail at for thirty years. Some of them died trying. And you did it like you were docking at a fuel wharf." He shook his head slowly. "I've followed you because you pay me and because you're good. Those are honest reasons, but they're shallow ones. Tonight I learned the deep one."

"Which is?"

"That when you're at that wheel, the impossible becomes a scheduling problem." He stood, joints cracking, old man's knees announcing themselves. "The crew feels it too. Ferro was cursing the river this morning. Tonight he told Odile the saints sent you. That's a long road for a superstitious man to travel in one day."

Xanthe almost smiled. "The saints had help. So did I. Tell Odile her engine answered every dishonest thing I asked of it."

"I'll tell her." Bram moved to go, then stopped. "One more thing, Captain. Vane flinched tonight. When I mentioned the charts. Men like him don't flinch at danger — they flinch at exposure. Whatever he's carrying up this river, it isn't just drilling equipment and government paper. There's something aboard, or something he knows, that he's afraid of us finding out."

Xanthe looked back upriver, toward the gorge they would enter in two days' time, the water she had sworn off at her father's grave, and Bram watched her jaw set in the lamplight spilling from the galley ports.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we start finding out."

Behind them, unnoticed, the canvas over Vane's crates had shifted in the night breeze, and beneath it, where the seal had been broken and hastily reglued, the stenciled letters on the topmost crate caught the light: not SURVEY EQUIPMENT at all, but a government ordnance mark, and a destination stamp reading SOURCE CLAIM OFFICE — PRIORITY ONE.
