## 8. The Village That Forgets Nothing

The village appeared the way all true things appear on the Meridian — suddenly, and as if it had always been there.

One moment Xanthe was reading the river's gray grammar: riffles spelling caution, an eddy line curling like a signature around a drowned pine. The next, the cliffs shouldered up out of the morning mist on the eastern bank, four hundred feet of black basalt stacked in courses like a mason's abandoned work, and there, stitched to the rock face on stairways and gantries and rope-lifted platforms, hung the village of Serat's Landing.

It had no official name on any chart Xanthe owned. Her father's charts called it *the Ledges*. The rivermen called it the Eyrie, or sometimes just Up There, spoken with a tilt of the head. Forty households, maybe fifty, lashed to the cliff with iron pins and generations of stubbornness, their smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Dugouts were winched up out of the water each night like buckets at a well. Nothing about the place touched the river except by choice.

"They're signaling," Bram said from beside her at the wheel.

Xanthe saw it. A figure on the lowest platform, small with distance, swinging a woven mat against a post — three beats, pause, three beats. Not a warning rhythm. An invitation, if you knew the code, and her father had taught it to her when she was nine years old, standing exactly where she stood now, his hand over hers on the spokes.

*Three and three means come ashore, little fish. Three and two means run. You'll know which is which when it matters.*

"Answer them," she said.

Bram gave her a look. "With what? We don't carry mats."

"Ferro's got the brass pan he burns the fish in. Hit it slow. Three, rest, three."

Ferro objected from the galley hatch that his pan was for cooking, not conversation, but he did it, and the clang rolled across the water flat and bright, and after a long moment the figure on the platform raised both arms overhead. Below it, a wooden stair unfolded down the cliff face like something jointed, section by section, until its foot rested on a shelf of gravel no wider than the boat.

"They want us in," Odile said, coming up the pilothouse ladder with grease still on her knuckles and a wariness on her face that hadn't left since Vane came aboard. "Nobody wants us anywhere on this stretch. Why would they want us?"

"Because they've seen our cargo before," Xanthe said, before she could stop herself.

She felt Vane's attention shift at the rail below, the way you feel weather change. He had spent the morning at the stern with his charts, and now he folded them away and climbed to the pilothouse with the unhurried grace of a man who believed every room was his.

"The Ledges," he said pleasantly. "I hoped we'd make contact here. Their elders are said to remember the old routes better than any survey."

*Their elders remember more than routes,* Xanthe thought, and said nothing, and brought the boat in.

* * *

The mooring was a matter of fenders and nerve; the current along the gravel shelf ran fast and shallow, and the *Merrilee*'s hull kissed stone twice before Bram got lines around the iron rings some forgotten generation had driven into the rock. Above them the stair rose in switchbacks, and villagers had begun to gather along it, watching with the particular silence of people who had decided not to speak first.

They were not unfriendly faces. They were *waiting* faces. Old coats, good boots, children peering between adult legs. A woman with a rifle slung backward across her shoulder the way farmers carry tools. And at the top of the stair, where it widened onto the first real platform, stood three elders on carved stools, wrapped against the cold, arranged with the deliberateness of a court.

Vane went up first. Of course he did. He took the stairs like a man ascending to receive an award, and Xanthe watched the elders watch him, and she saw the moment it happened.

The youngest of the three — a man perhaps sixty, with a beard braided into two gray ropes — leaned forward as Vane unrolled his chart on the platform boards to demonstrate his legitimacy. The wind tried to take the corner of it. Vane pinned it with one polished boot, and the chart lay open under the gray sky, and the bearded elder went very still.

He said something in the ledge-people's tongue, low and quick. The eldest woman — small, bent, with eyes the pale opaque blue of glacial ice — rose from her stool without hurry and came to look.

Then she looked past the chart entirely. She looked at Vane. And she spat, delicately, off the edge of the platform, into the river.

The whole stair went quiet.

"I don't believe we've been introduced," Vane said, with a smile that cost him effort.

"You have been introduced," the old woman said, in accented but fluent river-trade speech. "Ten years ago. By another man with your map." She put one finger, light as a moth, on the painted corner of the chart — and Xanthe's breath stopped, because there it was, inked in faded pigment in the lower margin where she'd never looked closely: a ship's figurehead. A woman carved of pale wood, hair streaming back, arms spread wide against the current.

The *Provision*'s figurehead. Her father had named the carving Sorrowing Dawn, because her mother had loved the phrase, and he'd painted her lips red every spring until the year he didn't come back.

"That is my father's boat," Xanthe heard herself say.

The old woman turned. Up close her face was a landscape of its own, crevassed and wind-scoured, and her eyes held Xanthe's with an attention so complete it felt like being read aloud.

"So," she said. "Sandoval's girl. You have his jaw. You have his way of standing at a wheel." She looked Xanthe up and down. "You also have his stubbornness, God rot it, or you would not be here."

"My name is Xanthe Sandoval. This is my boat. We're under contract to—"

"To go where?" the old woman said. "Show me on your captain's fine chart where you are going. Point to it."

Xanthe didn't answer. She didn't have to. The silence stretched, and the elder nodded slowly, as if Xanthe had spoken volumes.

"You will not pass," the old woman said. "Not through our water, not past our lines, not with that map. The river keeps what it takes. It has kept enough of us. It will not be fed from this cliff again."

* * *

They were given hospitality anyway, because refusing food to travelers was a sin older than the refusal of passage, and the ledge-people honored the older law. They ate that evening in a long hall built half into the cliff itself, a chamber of smoothed stone lit by seal-oil lamps, warm where the rock breathed out the day's stored heat. Fish and dark bread and a broth of river greens that tasted of iron. Ferro sat pressed against Odile, whispering that the lamps were lucky, that the carvings on the walls were wards, that he liked this place and distrusted it completely. Bram ate steadily and listened with his whole body, the way he did, cataloguing exits.

Vane was not invited to the hall. He dined aboard with Pell and the two hired men, and Xanthe understood this too was law, not accident. The elders had separated him the way you separate a contaminated crate.

Afterward, while the crew slept in guest alcoves cut warm into the stone, the eldest woman sent for Xanthe alone.

Her name, Xanthe learned, was Maren. She lived at the very top of the village, in a cell of a room whose single window framed nothing but sky and the white shoulder of the glacier far upstream, visible tonight because the clouds had torn open. She poured tea made from something resinous and bitter and sat across from Xanthe with her hands folded, and for a long time she simply looked at her.

"You were twelve when he passed us last," Maren said. "You stayed aboard. I remember the red paint on the figurehead's mouth. Fresh that year. He touched it up at my fire." She sipped. "He argued with us for two days, your father. He wanted the high route. We refused him. He went anyway, around the point, below the scree, where our lines don't reach."

"He told us he was going to survey the upper tributaries," Xanthe said. "For the commission. For maps."

"Yes," Maren said. "That is what he told you."

The words landed like a hook behind the ribs. Xanthe set down her cup. "Then tell me what he really told you."

Maren rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to a niche in the stone wall. From it she took a bundle wrapped in oiled cloth, and unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a relic or a wound. Inside lay a strip of birch bark, written on in charcoal, the letters cramped and hurried.

"He gave me this the night before he ran the gorge. He said, *If I don't come back, and men with money come asking what I found, give this to whoever of mine comes looking.*" She held it out. "I have waited ten years for someone with his jaw."

Xanthe took the bark. Her father's handwriting was unmistakable — she'd grown up copying his log entries as penmanship practice, the big looping capitals, the way his *r*s leaned like men walking into wind. She read it once in the lamplight. Then again. Then a third time, because the first two times the words refused to mean anything.

*They offered me more than the commission pays in a lifetime. I said no. They will send someone who says yes. If you're reading this, the source stays unmarked. Burn nothing, sell nothing, sign nothing. What's up there isn't treasure. It's the throat the whole river drinks from. Some things you protect by keeping them worthless on paper.*

"What does it mean, *they'll send someone who says yes*?" Xanthe asked, though the cold already climbing her spine had answered.

"It means," Maren said, sitting back down with terrible patience, "that your father did not die seeking the source, girl. He died defending it. There is a difference wider than the gorge."

"But the wreck — the commission report said lost with all hands, presumed swamped in the Staircase—"

"The Staircase did not kill him." Maren's voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "We found what the river gave back. Two of his crew, weeks later, at the calving flats. Not drowned. Shot. Your father we never recovered, but the man who survived longest — the engineer, a quiet fellow, half-frozen when we found him — lived nine days in my mother's care. Nine days talking. We wrote down everything." She tapped her own temple. "It is all still here. Every word. This village forgets nothing. That is why we are still alive up here, and why the men with money hate the sight of our smoke."

Xanthe's hands had gone numb around the birch bark. Somewhere below, faint through the stone, she could hear the river working the gravel bar, patient as a debt collector.

"Who," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Who sent them?"

"A company that doesn't use its own name. Men who pay in gold and ask for signatures. Your father's letter names no one, because he didn't know the name either — only the face of the man who made the offer." Maren looked at her for a long moment. "But the man who came after, the one who bought the search parties, who paid our own young men to lie to us about what they'd seen upstream — he had a partner. A young man then. Polished boots. Beautiful manners. He stood in this same hall and smiled at my mother the way a knife smiles at a whetstone."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

"No," Xanthe said.

"He was younger. Handsomer, if you like that kind of handsome. But he had the same walk. The same way of standing in a room like he'd already bought it." Maren folded her hands again. "Cassius Vane has been coming to this river since before you could spell *mortgage*, child. Whatever he told you he wants, he has wanted it for twenty years. And whatever your father died to keep off paper—" she nodded at the birch bark in Xanthe's frozen grip, "—is still off paper. Which means Vane has failed for twenty years. Ask yourself what a man like that does to a failure that old."

Xanthe thought of the burned chart pages Bram had caught in the lantern light. Pages bearing her father's handwriting. Vane wasn't preserving the record. He was editing it.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Why now? You refused us passage. Why hand me this?"

"Because refusing passage keeps the river safe from your cargo," Maren said. "It does nothing for what's already aboard. That man has been carrying his purpose inside his coat since Gray Landing, and no cliff of mine can stop it." She leaned forward, and the lamplight filled the creases of her face like water filling channels. "Your father swore nothing, girl. That was his mistake. He thought courage meant going anyway. You swore an oath at his grave — oh yes, we heard even that, the river carries gossip faster than it carries water. So hear me: the oath was never *don't run the gorge*. The oath your heart actually made, the night you knelt on that grave, was *finish what he started.*"

Xanthe stood so fast the tea slopped. "That's not—"

"Isn't it?" Maren settled back, insufferably calm. "Go down to your boat. Look at your crew. Look at the man with the polished boots. Then decide whether you came all this way to turn around."

* * *

Xanthe walked the stair down in the dark, the birch bark pressed inside her jacket against her heartbeat, and stopped halfway on a landing where the cliff fell away sheer beneath her feet and the whole river lay exposed below — a ribbon of moving black, starred with the reflection of the opened sky, running down and down toward country she knew, fed from a throat she'd sworn off forever.

Far above, the glacier's shoulder glowed faintly, the way snow does when moonlight gets behind it.

She thought about her father touching up the red paint on Sorrowing Dawn's mouth. She thought about him arguing with these elders for two days and going anyway — not for glory, not for maps, but because he'd been offered a fortune and had said no, and had known saying no wouldn't be enough. He'd gone up to stand between the source and the men who wanted it, armed with nothing but a boat and a crew who trusted him.

And she had spent ten years believing he'd died chasing something. Believing ambition killed him. Building her oath on a grave that wasn't the shape she'd thought.

Grief didn't leave her on that landing. It changed state, the way water changes to ice — same substance, new structure, suddenly able to cut.

Below her, lamplight moved aboard the *Merrilee*. Vane's silhouette crossed the pilothouse glass, unhurried, proprietary, checking on *his* vessel. Beside the rail, smaller, Odile stood her midnight watch over the engine, arms crossed against the cold, faithful as gravity.

Xanthe descended the rest of the stairs, crossed the gravel shelf, and swung herself aboard.

Odile turned, and her tired face did the thing it always did — softened a degree, guarded a degree, both at once. "You've been up there three hours. You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," Xanthe said. "I've seen a portrait." She glanced up at the pilothouse, where Vane's shadow still moved. "Wake Bram. Quietly. And get the engine ready to turn over cold, Odile — before dawn, before anyone asks questions. We're leaving this anchorage tonight."

Odile searched her face. "Upstream or down?"

Xanthe put her hand flat against the birch bark over her heart, feeling the words her father had written for a stranger to deliver, and heard Maren's voice laid over them like a keel laid over ribs.

"Upstream," she said. "All the way."
