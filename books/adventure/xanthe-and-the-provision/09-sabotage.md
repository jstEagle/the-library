## 9. Sabotage

Odile had never trusted a quiet engine.

A good engine spoke to you — the tick of cooling iron, the hum of bearings running true, the small complaints that meant a machine was alive and honest. Silence was what engines did when something inside them had given up, or when someone outside them had made sure they would. So when she came up from the engine room at half past eleven that night and heard nothing but the river talking to itself against the hull, she stopped on the ladder with one hand still on the rail and listened the way other people prayed.

The *Merrilee Ann* lay tied off at a gravel shelf below the second gorge, banked for the night because Xanthe refused to run unfamiliar water in the dark. The fires were banked too. Above her, through the deck boards, she could hear Bram's boots cross the pilothouse twice and go still — the man slept standing up more often than lying down these days. Somewhere aft, Ferro was muttering to his saints in the low singsong he thought nobody heard. And forward, in the cabin Vane's money paid for, Cassius Vane and his surveyor Pell had their lamp lit, its glow leaking yellow under the door like something spilled.

Odile stood in the engine room doorway and let the silence tell her what it knew.

She had checked the rudder linkage at dusk. She always did — three turns of the gland nut, a thumb along the tiller arm looking for hairline cracks, a drop of oil worked into the quadrant where the salt and grit of the Meridian liked to grind themselves into every joint. It was ritual, the way Ferro's saints were ritual, and rituals kept you alive on this river better than luck ever had. At dusk the linkage had been true. She would have staked her hands on it. Her hands were her whole fortune; she staked them carefully.

So why, now, in the dark, did the steering flat feel wrong before she'd even touched it?

She took the lamp down from its hook and held it low. Lamplight is a liar at the best of times — it pools and flatters and hides — so she crouched until her knees cracked and brought the flame within a hand's width of the linkage rods, the clevis pins, the tiller arms that translated Xanthe's will at the wheel into the swing of the rudder blade below. Everything looked right. That was the first thing that frightened her. Sabotage should look like something: a sheared pin, a backed-off nut, a rod bent by force. This looked like a linkage that had simply been loved back into place by careful hands.

Except for the grease.

There was fresh grease on the forward clevis — fresh, bright, unweathered grease, smeared in a thumbprint arc that no one aboard had any business making at midnight. Odile didn't use grease on the clevises. Grease attracted grit, and grit on the Meridian was a religion unto itself; she used oil and tallow and suspicion. Someone else had greased that fitting tonight. Someone who wanted it to look maintained. Someone who understood that the best lie you can tell an engineer is a well-tended machine.

She touched the smear with her fingertip. Still wet enough to shine.

Her mouth went dry in a way the river wind couldn't account for. Because there were only two reasons a man greases a clevis at midnight, and both of them meant that somewhere between here and the next gorge, the rudder was going to matter more than it had ever mattered in the boat's life — and one of those reasons meant it wasn't going to work when it did.

She got the inspection light out of its box, the little brass-cased thing with the hook, and hung it inside the steering flat so both hands were free. Then she began at the tiller arm and went over the linkage the way a priest goes over a soul: joint by joint, pin by pin, thread by thread, saying each part's name under her breath like a litany. Tiller arm, sound. Quadrant bolts, sound. Drag link forward, sound. Drag link aft —

She stopped.

The aft drag link connected the quadrant to the rudder post collar through a clevis pinned with a taper pin and secured with a split cotter. Standard. Boring. The kind of fitting you could forget existed for a year and it would serve you faithfully anyway. Odile hooked her finger through the clevis and felt it, rather than looked at it, because her fingers had been reading machines since she was nine years old and her eyes were easier to fool than her hands.

The cotter pin was gone.

Not sheared — gone. Removed. And in its place, seated snugly in the hole where the cotter should have been, was a soft iron nail, cut to length, its head nipped off, driven in flush so that anyone glancing at the fitting would see *a pin, fine, a pin's there.* But a nail isn't a cotter. A nail doesn't flex. A nail holds — right up until the load comes on hard and sideways, the way a load comes on when a boat is bucking through gorge water and the pilot hauls the wheel over hard to keep her off a rock wall. Under that load the soft iron would shear clean as a snapped twig, the clevis would drop off the rudder post collar, and the *Merrilee Ann* would be a hundred tons of blind momentum with no steering at all, exactly where no boat in the history of the upper Meridian had ever survived being without steering.

Odile knelt there a long moment with her finger hooked through the clevis, feeling her own heartbeat in her throat.

Then she did the hardest thing she had done in ten years of hard things. She didn't shout. She didn't wake the boat. She sat down on the grating with the inspection light hooked above her, took out her tool roll, and began, quietly, to put the linkage right.

Because whoever had done this would be listening for shouting. And because if she woke the boat screaming *sabotage*, there would be accusations flying before dawn and no proof worth anything, and Vane — smooth, patient, expensive Vane — would stand there with his lawyer's face and ask, gently, *who benefits from such a claim?* No. Proof first. Proof was a language even liars had to answer in.

She drew the nail with pliers, working slow so the taper pin wouldn't shift. The old cotter hole was scored where the nail had been forced through — good, that was evidence, that was a story written in metal. She wrapped the nail in her rag and put it in her breast pocket like a relic. From the spares locker she took a proper cotter, spring brass, and drove it home, spread the legs, dressed the ends. Greased nothing. Oiled everything. Then she sat back on her heels and let herself think about who.

The crew was seven souls and a stowaway cat Ferro denied feeding. Herself — she hadn't done it, and she knew the shape of her own nights. Xanthe had turned in at ten, exhausted from the day's water, and Odile had felt her breathing change against her shoulder before she'd left the cabin; whatever else the river did to them, it didn't make Xanthe get up after that and creep about with grease. Bram slept in the pilothouse like a gargoyle and snored like a landslide; the whole boat would have known if he'd moved. Ferro hadn't left the galley since sundown except to spit over the leeward rail, and Ferro wouldn't sabotage a boat he believed was carrying his own salvation downstream. That left the men whose money bought the charter.

Vane didn't do his own dirty work. That wasn't contempt, just observation — Vane's hands were for maps and contracts and other people's futures. Which left Pell.

Pell, the surveyor. Quiet Pell, competent Pell, who could shoot a transit and splice a line and laugh at the right moments and who never once, in two weeks aboard, had asked Odile a single question about the engine — which on a riverboat was like never once asking about the weather. Pell, who volunteered for night watches nobody wanted. Pell, whose kit Odile had helped carry aboard at Gray Landing and remembered thinking was heavier than survey instruments had any right to be.

She banked the lamp, climbed the ladder, and stood a moment in the night air letting the river cool her face. The gorge ahead was a blacker black than the sky, a seam cut into the mountains where the water ran loud and mean. Tomorrow they'd run it. Tomorrow, if a certain soft iron nail had stayed where some careful hand had set it, the *Merrilee Ann* would have entered that seam with a rudder scheduled to die at the worst possible moment, and the river would have taken them apart against the walls, and the official record would have said *lost attempting the passage*, and no one would ever have looked further.

Unless the engineer died too. Unless everyone died. Odile felt the cold of that thought go all the way through her. This hadn't been sabotage meant to strand them or scare them. Stranding leaves witnesses. This was murder with a mechanical alibi, and the alibi was the gorge itself.

She looked at the lit rectangle under Vane's cabin door.

The lamp was still burning. Two shadows crossed it, briefly — one broad and still, one narrow and pacing. Then the narrow shadow stopped, and though she couldn't hear the words through the hull, she saw the lamplight flicker as the door was opened and closed, quick, like a man slipping out, and then the deck board near the rail creaked once, softly, the particular creak of a boot placed with care.

Odile stepped back into the shadow of the deckhouse and became, in the old phrase of her apprenticeship, part of the machinery.

Pell came along the rail with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, moving toward the stern. Toward the steering flat. He walked like a man revisiting his own handwriting, and he carried something in his free hand — a rag, or a cloth-wrapped bundle, small. He reached the steering flat hatch, lifted it without hurry, and stood looking down into the space where her lamp still burned.

Odile watched him see the lamp. Watched him understand what a burning lamp in an empty steering flat meant at midnight. Watched him do the arithmetic — *she found it, she fixed it, she knows* — and watched him close the hatch again, gently, and turn around, and find her standing behind him with her arms folded and the river loud at their backs.

"Well," Pell said. His voice was pleasant. His voice was always pleasant. "Couldn't sleep either?"

"Nice night for maintenance," Odile said.

"Is that what we're calling it." He smiled. In the coin of shuttered lantern light his face was all reasonable planes, a face built for denying things in front of commissions. "I heard a noise earlier. Thought I'd check the fittings."

"At midnight."

"I'm thorough."

"You're something." She let the silence stretch until it hurt. "Cotter pin on the aft drag link. You want to tell me about it?"

Something moved behind Pell's eyes — not guilt; she'd seen guilt on sailors' faces and this wasn't it. Calculation. The rapid, cold arithmetic of a man deciding how much truth a situation can bear. "I don't know what you're implying," he said, "but I'd choose my words carefully, Miss Marchetti. Accusations aboard a chartered vessel can void a contract. Void contracts don't pay crews."

"There it is," Odile said softly. "The price of my silence, itemized."

"I'm saying," Pell said, and now the pleasantness thinned enough to show the wire underneath, "that boats are lost on this river every season. Rudder failures especially. Terrible stretch of water ahead of us. If I were you, I'd worry less about cotter pins and more about whether your captain can actually run that gorge tomorrow — because if she can't, none of us sees home, and Vane will want to know why his engineer spent the night playing detective instead of sleeping."

He stepped past her. Close, deliberate, close enough that she caught the smell of him — coal smoke and pencil shavings and, faintly, machine oil that wasn't hers. At the head of the deckhouse stairs he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "whatever you think you found down there — you should check your work. Engineers make mistakes. So do engineers who find things."

Then he was gone, down the steps, across the deck, and the light under Vane's door swallowed him.

Odile stood alone in the dark with the river roaring its one long sentence and her heart going like a badly balanced flywheel. She put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the cut nail, its sheared head, its evidence, and told herself the fear was just fatigue. But she knew sabotage when she held it, and now she knew something worse: she knew that Pell knew she'd found it, and knew that Pell didn't care — which meant the nail had never been the whole plan. A man doesn't leave his sabotage discoverable unless the discovery itself serves him. Unless suspicion, once planted, was the cargo.

She climbed down to the cabin she shared with Xanthe, moving quietly, and stood a moment in the doorway watching her partner sleep — the tangled dark hair, the rope-burned hands curled open even in dreams, the small frown of a woman steering something even unconscious. Ten years Odile had kept this boat's heart beating. She had never once, in ten years, been afraid of anything that lived aboard her.

She lay down beside Xanthe without waking her, in the dark, holding the nail against her sternum like a coal she couldn't put down, and listened to the gorge rehearsing tomorrow's water, and understood at last that the most dangerous thing on the *Merrilee Ann* was not the river at all.

Above her, through the deck, she heard the pilothouse door open. Bram's voice, low and puzzled, calling out into the night: "Who's walking my deck?"

And from somewhere aft, in a pleasant voice pitched perfectly to carry, Pell answered: "Just checking the lines, Mr. Hale. Couldn't sleep."

* * *

Morning came up gray and thin, the gorge ahead wearing its mist like a bandage over a wound. Odile was at the engine telegraph before anyone else was awake, and when Xanthe came down the ladder with her hair still damp and her eyes already reading the water, Odile was waiting for her with the rag unfolded flat on the workbench, the cut nail centered on it like an exhibit.

Xanthe looked at it for a long time. Then at Odile. Then, through the engine room's open skylight, at the pale square of morning where the gorge waited.

"Where," she said quietly, "was this."

"Aft drag link. Driven in place of the cotter. It would have sheared in the worst water between here and the bend." Odile kept her voice level the way she kept her engines level, with constant invisible work. "I found it at midnight. I fixed it at ten past. And then I stood in the dark and watched Pell come back to admire his work."

Xanthe picked up the nail. Turned it. Odile watched her captain's face do what it always did — go still and river-deep, feeling moving far beneath a calm surface — and when Xanthe spoke again her voice was almost gentle, which was how Odile knew how much fury was underneath.

"He knows you found it."

"He doesn't care that I found it. That's the part that keeps me up, Xanthe. A saboteur hides. He walked right past me and threatened my contract and went to bed." She nodded upward, toward the deck, toward the men stirring above. "You don't leave your fingerprints on a knife unless the knife isn't the weapon. Suspicion is the weapon. He *wants* us at each other's throats before the gorge — wants us watching each other instead of the water."

Xanthe set the nail down on the bench with a small precise click, like a chess piece placed.

"Then we don't give him the fight," she said. "We say nothing. Not to Bram yet, not to Ferro. We run the gorge today on a linkage I'll check myself every hour, and we watch him — you and me, quiet, together. Let him think his nail was the whole plan."

"And if he has another plan?"

Xanthe looked up through the skylight at the gorge, and Odile saw her father in her face for just a moment — the same look, the same stillness, the same terrible love of a river that had already taken everything once.

"Then," Xanthe said, ringing the telegraph to Stand By, "he'll learn what he should have learned at Gray Landing. Nobody sabotages this boat twice."

Above them, boots crossed the deck. The mist began to lift off the gorge in long white ribbons, and through it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Pell laughing at something Vane had said — easy, warm, and utterly without joy.
