## 10. Odile's Choice

The engine room at night was the only honest place on the river.

Odile had believed that for eleven years, and she believed it still, though the belief was getting harder to hold. Down here there were no charts with burned corners, no crates that breathed, no captain's face going gray around the mouth when Vane said *the source* like a man saying a woman's name he had no right to. There was only the great slow heartbeat of the *Meridian Queen* — the thump and shudder of the paddleshaft, the tick of cooling metal, the smell of hot oil and coal smoke and the green breath of the river seeping up through the seams. Machines did not lie. A bearing worn thin sang its weariness plainly. A gasket failing wept where it failed. If you put your ear against the hull you could hear the water itself, patient as God, asking nothing and telling everything.

She lay on her back in the bilge alley with a lamp hooked above her head, running her fingers along the rudder linkage for the third time since dark, because the third time was the one that counted. Twice was habit. Three times was certainty. The pins were seated. The clevis bolts were torqued. The tiller arms showed no fresh bright metal where metal should have been scarred. Whatever had been done — or not done — two nights ago, it was not being done again tonight.

Or it was being done better tonight.

That thought arrived uninvited, the way the worst thoughts do, and she let it sit in her chest while she wiped her hands on a rag gone gray with years of grease. Above her, through three decks, she could hear the muffled argument of the crew at supper — Ferro's voice rising and falling like weather, Bram's low rumble under it, Xanthe's rare laugh cutting through once, brief as a struck match. And beneath all of it, another sound: Vane's voice, smooth as poured wax, saying something she couldn't make out and didn't need to. His voice carried the way oil carries on water. You never heard the words, only the spreading.

Odile climbed out of the bilge, doused the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the boat breathe. Then she went up.

* * *

Vane found her at the rail an hour past midnight, which told her he'd been waiting for her, which told her he'd been watching, which told her everything she needed to know about how the next quarter hour would go.

The river ran high and black under a sky scoured down to bare stars. Mist stood off the banks in columns, ghost-pale, and somewhere upstream a loon called three times and got no answer. Odile had come up to cool her hands and think about nothing, and she had almost managed it when his boots clicked on the deck behind her — polished boots, even now, even here, ten days into water that had eaten the varnish off everyone else's souls.

"Mrs. Ferreiro." He said her name carefully, the way a man handles a tool he suspects is sharper than advertised. "May I?"

"You may stand there," she said. "I can't stop you."

He laughed softly, as if she'd said something charming, and came to the rail beside her. Up close he smelled of bay rum and clean linen, an offense this far upriver. "You don't sleep much."

"I sleep fine."

"On your back in the bilge alley with a lamp. I checked the log of the engine room watch." He turned his head; she felt his eyes on her profile. "Three inspections of the rudder gear in one night. That's either devotion or fear."

"It's arithmetic," Odile said. "A linkage fails in the Gorge of Sworn-Off Water, we lose the boat, the cargo, and every soul aboard. I check it three times so I only have to be wrong zero times."

"And have you found anything?"

She looked at him then. He held her gaze without flinching, which she granted him; most men looked away first. His face in the starlight was handsome the way a ledger is handsome — balanced, ruled, every figure in its place, and empty of anything but figures.

"Not yet," she said.

"*Yet,*" he repeated, tasting it. "Good. I hoped you were a thorough woman. It's why I'm here."

"I thought you were here for the source."

"I'm here for what pays for the source." He drew something from inside his coat — not papers this time, she noticed, not the sealed government envelopes Pell guarded like relics, but a single folded sheet of heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that cost more per page than Ferro made in a week. He did not offer it to her. He simply held it, letting her see the weight of it, the way a man lets a dog see the meat. "Your contract with Captain Sandoval. I've read it. It's generous by river standards, which is to say it's poverty with good manners. Double rates for hazard pay, and half of it contingent on delivery." He paused. "I'm prepared to double it again. For you alone."

The loon called again, farther off. Somewhere below, the paddleshaft turned in its bearings, singing its one true note.

"Say the rest," Odile said quietly.

"The rest?" Vane smiled. "There isn't any rest. I want you to keep a log. Depths, headings, bank features, channel marks through the gorge and above it. Soundings where soundings are possible. Nothing you're not already doing in your head every waking hour. Just write it down, in your own hand, in duplicate. One copy stays with you until we're paid out at the end of the run. The other comes to me." He finally extended the sheet. "It's a surveyor's fee, Mrs. Ferreiro. For surveying work. No one has to know. Not the captain, not the crew. Especially not the captain."

Odile took the paper. She read it slowly, because reading slowly gave her time, and time was the only weapon she had that he couldn't match. The figures were real. She knew figures; she had kept the *Queen*'s books through two lean winters and one flood year, and she knew exactly what that number meant. It meant the mortgage paid twice over. It meant new boiler tubes. It meant Xanthe sleeping whole nights instead of sitting up doing sums by lamplight with that look on her face, the one like a woman counting the water rising in a hold.

It meant betrayal, itemized and signed.

"What are they after?" Odile asked. "Your backers. Truly. Not a claim on navigation — you could buy navigation rights in any commission house in the capital. Not timber, not fur. What's worth this?"

Vane looked upstream, toward the gorge, toward the white line of the Provision Rapids just visible beyond the last bend as a smudge of perpetual cloud. When he spoke, his voice dropped into something almost reverent, which was the most frightening register he owned.

"Do you know what the old maps call that valley? *Terra Incognita.* Blank space. Every government survey for sixty years has stopped at the rapids and drawn a wavy line and written 'unexplored.' Do you understand what blank space is worth now?" He turned back to her, and his eyes had the flat shine of a man looking at something far away and very bright. "The glacial gravels up there carry heavy sands. Rare earths — cerium, thorium, metals that make steel into miracles and lamps into suns. The assays from your father's era were suppressed, but my people have seen fragments. Enough to know. There is enough metal in that valley to buy a nation's ransom, Mrs. Ferreiro, and right now it belongs to no one, because no one has ever come back to say otherwise. Whoever logs that route first doesn't just own a river. He owns the door to it. Every drill rig, every tramway, every company town for fifty years — all of it funnels through the man who holds the chart." He smiled again, warmer now, a salesman's warmth. "And the woman who writes the log holds the pen."

The river slid past beneath them, black and indifferent, carrying their reflections apart.

"You've got it wrong," Odile said.

"Have I?"

"I don't hold pens. I hold wrenches." She folded the paper along its original crease, precisely, and handed it back. "No."

Vane did not move to take it for a moment. Then he did, unhurried, and tucked it away inside his coat as if it were a letter of no consequence. "May I ask why?"

"You may."

He waited. She made him wait longer. Out on the water, mist moved through the starlight like slow cattle.

"Because I've seen what happens to rivers when men like your backers find them," she said at last. "I grew up on the Corvo, before the dredges. My father fished it; his father before him. There were sturgeon in the Corvo long enough to live longer than the men who caught them. There were willows you could walk under. Then the surveys came, and the assays, and the dredges, and inside nine years the river ran gray and the fish were gone and my father died of the dust in his lungs at forty-four, coughing into a rag in a company town built on the gravel that used to be his living." Her voice stayed level; she was proud of that. "So when I look at your blank space, Mr. Vane, I don't see ransom. I see the Corvo. I see what comes after the pen touches the paper. And I will not hold that pen."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. The paddleshaft thumped. The mist drifted. And then he inclined his head, gracious as a diplomat, and Odile understood that she had made a mistake — not in refusing, never in refusing, but in thinking refusal would end the conversation rather than begin the next phase of it.

"Well," he said. "I admire a woman who knows her own mind. It's rare. It's valuable." He straightened his cuffs, a small precise gesture, a man closing a ledger. "I'll not press. But I'll say one thing more, and then leave you to your stars." He glanced at her sidelong, and the warmth was gone from his face entirely now, leaving the ledger-balance underneath, and beneath that something colder that the balance had been drawn over. "Refusals are expensive, Mrs. Ferreiro. Not today. Today it costs you nothing. But a refusal is a fact, and facts accumulate, and my backers are not sentimental men. They asked me to identify who aboard this vessel could be bought, and who could not. I now have my answer regarding you." He smiled faintly. "You've made yourself legible. Legible things get managed."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's an observation," Vane said. "Threats are for men who lack patience." He touched two fingers to his hat brim. "Good night, Mrs. Ferreiro. Check your linkage. I hear the water gets rough ahead."

His boots clicked away across the deck, polished and unhurried, and disappeared down the companionway toward the lighted cabin where Pell sat up late with his sealed papers.

* * *

Odile stayed at the rail a long time after he was gone.

She tried to count the cost of what she'd done, the way she counted everything — boiler pressure, coal remaining, days of food, feet of draft. Refusal, entered in the ledger. Asset: one conscience, intact. Liability: one enemy with capital, patience, and two hired men who did not appear in any manifest. She turned the figures over and over and they would not balance, because the whole column was wrong, because Vane had understood something in the last ten minutes that she was only now catching up to: that she had never been negotiating at all. He had offered her a price and she had answered with a self, and selves don't clear the market.

Below her, through the deck, the engine breathed. She thought about the rudder linkage, and the three inspections, and the night two nights ago when something had been loosened and only luck — or Odile's own stubbornness — had caught it. She had suspected Pell then. She suspected him still; he denied too smoothly, watched too little, and slept like a man with no dreams, which on this boat was its own kind of confession. But suspicion was a lamp with a short wick. It lit only what was directly in front of it.

Vane had just shown her the shape of the dark around it.

If she could be marked, so could Xanthe. So could Bram, whose vote had carried them upstream; so could Ferro, who talked to the river like an old adversary he respected. And Xanthe — Xanthe above all, who had broken a ten-year oath to bring them here, who steered by her dead father's letters read aloud by lamplight, who had given Vane exactly what he wanted without knowing it: the route, run and proven, by the one pilot alive who could run it. Everything Vane needed from Xanthe Sandoval, Xanthe had already given. Which meant that everything Vane needed *next* from Xanthe was nothing at all.

And a man needs nothing from a person he no longer requires.

Odile pushed off the rail and went below, taking the stairs two at a time, past the galley where Ferro's bread rose under cloth in the warm dark, past the mess where the supper argument had long since guttered out, down and down to the engine room where the honest machines waited. She lit the lamp. She got out the logbook — the real one, the official one, the one the commission would audit if the *Queen* ever came home — and she uncapped her pen.

Then, on a fresh page, in her small square engineer's hand, she began a second record. Not depths and headings. Dates, times, movements. Vane's meetings. Pell's absences. The hours the surveyor's crate was open and the hours it was locked. Who stood watches near the cargo deck and who did not. She wrote until the ink ran low, and then she blotted it, tore the pages free, folded them small, and hid them where no searcher would think to look — wrapped in oilcloth, wired inside the cold-water jacket of the starboard condenser, where the only way to find them was to already know engines the way she knew engines.

Some evidence you burn, she thought, remembering Bram's story of Vane and the chart. Some evidence you sink. And some you seal inside the heart of the machine and let it ride there, beating, until it's needed.

She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the boat, and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, the thing she had refused to feel at the rail: fear. Clean and cold and clarifying, like river water in early spring. Then she banked it with the rest of the fires and went to find Xanthe.

She found her in the pilothouse, of course, standing the dead watch alone, one hand resting on the wheel as if the wheel were a shoulder she was steadying. The mist had thickened. Ahead, faint through the glass, the cloud-smudge over the Provision Rapids had begun to glow with the first gray suggestion of dawn.

Xanthe turned when the door opened, and whatever she saw in Odile's face made her say, quietly, "What happened?"

"He offered me double pay," Odile said. "To log the route for his backers. Behind your back."

The wheel creaked under Xanthe's hand, a small sound, very loud in the stillness.

"And?"

"And I told him no." Odile crossed the pilothouse and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked out at the growing light on the water. "And I think, love, that we just found out what he does with people who tell him no."

Ahead of them, upriver, the gorge waited with its mouth open, and somewhere in the dark between the boat and the ice, Pell's watch ended and did not resume.
