## 11. Pell Overboard

Bram had spent thirty years learning to read weather the way other men read faces, and the sky that evening wore an expression he did not like at all.

It had been building since sundown — a bruised green-gray band low along the western rim of the gorge, where the clouds came down off the ice fields like something poured rather than blown. The air had gone heavy and sweet with glacier melt, that cold mineral smell that meant water was moving somewhere it shouldn't be. The river itself had changed its voice. All day it had run with its usual gravel-and-glass mutter; now there was a deeper note underneath, a long sustained groan from the narrows ahead, as if the Meridian were clearing its throat before saying something unpleasant.

"Barometer's falling like a drunk down a stairwell," Ferro said, appearing at his elbow on the afterdeck with two mugs of chicory he hadn't been asked to bring. The cook handed one over without ceremony. "Ferro's knees say storm. Ferro's knees have never lied."

"Your knees said we'd all drown in the Staircase," Bram said. "We didn't."

"The Staircase was a warning shot." Ferro sipped, grimaced, added sugar from a tin in his apron pocket. "This is the argument."

Bram grunted and drank. From the pilothouse above them came the soft creak of Xanthe shifting her weight at the wheel, the way she did when she was thinking about water instead of watching it. Below, through the deck plates, he could feel the engine's steady heartbeat — Odile's heartbeat, really, since she'd rebuilt half the machinery herself — and beyond it, fainter, the sound he'd been cataloguing all day without quite admitting it: Vane's surveyor Pell, pacing.

The man paced like a metronome. Foredeck to stern rail, stern rail to foredeck, forty steps each way, never varying, never stopping except to stand at the rail and look upriver toward the source none of them were supposed to know they were steaming toward. Bram had watched a lot of men pace on this boat over the years — debtors, deserters, one bishop — and he'd learned that pacing wasn't nervousness. Pacing was arithmetic. A man counting his options.

Pell had stopped counting around noon. That was the first thing wrong.

* * *

The squall hit an hour past full dark, and it hit sideways.

There was no polite build-up, no first spatter of rain to warn the canvas. One moment the night was black and close, the river a sound without a shape; the next the wind arrived off the western wall like a thrown door, flat and hard, carrying hail with it — small, vicious pellets that rang off the pilothouse glass and bounced white across the deck. The *Provision II* — no, Bram corrected himself, the old habit dying hard, the *Meridian Queen*, though she'd carry her dead sister's bell soon enough if Odile had anything to say about it — the boat yawed hard to starboard, and Xanthe's voice came down the speaking tube sharp as a cleaver: **All hands! Lines off the cargo, double-lash the crates, move like you mean it!**

They moved like they meant it. Thirty years on the river teaches a crew to work in the dark by feel, and for ten minutes the only language aboard was hands: Bram hauling the forward lines while Ferro wrestled the tarp over the galley hatch, both of them leaning into wind that felt personal, hail needling the back of his neck like a hundred small accusations. Lightning opened the gorge once, twice — a white instant in which the cliffs stood revealed on either side, wet stone towering, the river below them suddenly visible and enormous and going somewhere fast — and then darkness again, total, the kind you could lean against.

It was during the third flash that Bram registered what was missing.

Not a sound. An absence of sound. The metronome had stopped.

"Pell!" he shouted into the wind, more reflex than worry. "Pell, get below!"

Nothing answered him but the hail and the river.

He made his way aft along the starboard rail, one hand on the wire, boots slipping on hailstones that were already melting to slick beads underfoot. The stern was a chaos of shadow and streaming canvas. He found the man's oilskin where it had caught on the bollard — or been caught on it, some careful part of his mind noted, because the coat was snagged neat as a laundry line, not torn free — and beyond it, snagged on the same iron ring, a scrap of yellow canvas flapping like a trapped bird.

A life vest. Torn clean through the chest strap.

Bram stood with the scrap in his fist and let the rain hammer him, and thought about torn things. Canvas doesn't tear clean on a bollard. Canvas tears clean on a knife, or on a rock, or on teeth. And men don't go over rails in squalls wearing their vests half-fastened unless someone else dressed them for the occasion.

Lightning came again and showed him the whole stern empty, the river running black and boiling below, and no Pell anywhere in the world.

* * *

Vane took the news the way a banker takes news of a fire in a rival's building.

"Overboard?" He stood in the saloon in a dry dressing gown that probably cost more than Ferro's annual wages, hair combed, face composed into a mask of concern so smooth it might have been lacquered. "In this weather? Good God. Was he wearing his vest?"

"He was wearing part of it," Bram said. He laid the torn scrap on the table between them. It looked obscene there, yellow against the polished mahogany, among Vane's charts and his little silver cup and his government papers in their oilcloth wallet. "Torn strap. Found snagged astern."

For just a moment — less than a breath — something moved behind Vane's eyes. Not grief. Recalculation. Bram had seen bookkeepers wear that look when a column refused to sum.

"That's terrible," Vane said. "Terrible. Though I confess—" and here he spread his hands, the very portrait of reluctant honesty, "—I must ask the question any reasonable man would ask. Your crew and Mr. Pell had not, shall we say, warmed to one another. There were words in the mess. Words about sabotage, if I recall, which your engineer leveled at my people with remarkable confidence. Men have done worse than throw a disliked stranger to a river in a squall."

The saloon went very quiet. Xanthe stood by the door with riverwater still dripping off her braid, and Bram watched her watch Vane, and understood that the captain had already walked three steps ahead of everyone in the room.

"You're accusing my crew," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"I'm observing circumstances," Vane said gently. "There's a difference. A legal difference, in fact, which may matter when we reach territory with a magistrate in it. A man is missing. His vest was cut. The last words exchanged between him and your cook involved a knife being waved in the galley over the matter of salt pork — I heard it myself. I don't *want* to believe any of you capable of it. But the river keeps what it takes, Captain, and it also takes what it's given."

"Ferro waved a spoon," Bram said.

"A large spoon."

"It was a serving spoon and he was pointing at the beans."

"Gentlemen." Vane raised both palms, wounded reasonableness incarnate. "I'm not your enemy here. Find the truth however you like — search my things, question my remaining men, I authorize it freely. I only ask that we not pretend the possibilities are complicated. Either the river took him, or someone aboard helped it. And either way—" he picked up his silver cup, turned it slowly, "—we are four days above the last settlement, committed to a channel that only you can read, Captain, with a contract that pays out on arrival and not one copper before. I'd think very carefully about how much appetite anyone has for suspicion. Suspicion is expensive this far upstream."

He drank, set the cup down with a small precise click, and wished them all goodnight.

* * *

Bram didn't sleep. Sleep was for men whose ships weren't lying to them.

He sat in the fo'c'sle with a lamp turned low and went through the evening in his head the way he'd once gone through leadsheets, marking every fix. Pell had stopped pacing at noon. Pell had eaten nothing at supper — pushed his beans around, claimed a sour stomach. Pell had asked Ferro, casually, too casually, what time the watch changed. Pell had stood at the rail looking west at the storm-light for the better part of an hour, and Bram had assumed he was dreading the weather.

Men who dread the weather go below. Men who are waiting for the weather stay at the rail.

And then there was the vest. Bram turned the yellow scrap over in his big hands. He'd cut canvas his whole life — sails, tarps, awnings — and he knew the difference between a blade drawn quick and a blade drawn careful. This cut was careful. Two strokes, actually, if you looked close: the main slash, and a smaller nick beside it where the knife had started, bitten shallow, and reset. Someone had taken their time. Someone had wanted it to *read* as violence.

Which meant the vest had been cut before Pell ever went into the water. Which meant Pell had gone into the water some way other than a knife.

Or — and here Bram's stomach turned slow and cold — which meant Pell had cut his own vest, and gone over the rail on purpose, and was even now swimming for a prearranged bank in the dark with everything he knew tucked in an oilskin pouch, leaving behind a stage-set of foul play designed to hang the crew the moment they reached a magistrate's reach.

Either way, the man was an instrument, not a person. Bram had suspected it since Gray Landing. Now he knew it the way you know a channel has shifted — not by the chart but by the feel of the keel grinding.

There was one more thing. He'd been putting off thinking about it because it was ugly, and the hour was ugly enough already. In the lightning-flash when he'd found the coat on the bollard, he'd also seen — just for that white half-second — that the stern rail's gate latch hung open. The gate was kept dogged shut; Xanthe was religious about it; Odile oiled the latch weekly and checked the pin. Open gates don't happen in squalls. Open gates happen beforehand.

Someone had prepared the deck for a disappearance. Whether Pell had prepared it for himself or someone had prepared it for him, Bram couldn't yet say. But coal dust, he thought, remembering the open bunker hatch they'd used at the last coaling stop, remembers hands.

* * *

He went looking at two in the morning, when the boat ran quiet between Xanthe's watch and Odile's, when the only sounds were the engine's lullaby and the river's long complaint against the hull.

The coal bunkers flanked the boiler room, two iron-lined cells reached through hatches in the stokehold floor. Nobody searched coal bunkers. Coal bunkers were where you found dead stowaways and forgotten tools and, once on the old *Provision*, a crate of contraband brandy that Ferro still denied knowledge of. They were also, Bram reflected, wedging himself through the hatch with the lamp held high, exactly where a careful man would hide things — because a careful man knows that nobody searches coal, and because coal dust settles over everything left undisturbed and writes its own honest record.

The port bunker was half-drawn down, black anthracite glittering in the lamplight like a field of crushed stars. Bram played the light across it patiently, reading the surface the way he read sandbars. Here, the smooth slump of settled coal. Here, his own bootprints from coaling day, half-buried. And there — back in the corner, beneath the bunker's sloping roof-plate where the lamplight barely reached — a patch of coal pressed flat and swept, swept with something broad, and in the middle of the swept patch, a rectangle of absence where the dust lay thinner.

He dug with bare hands. The coal was cold and bit his knuckles. Three feet down his fingers struck oilcloth.

The package was wrapped tight and tied with waxed cord, and it came up trailing black dust like something exhumed. Inside the oilcloth: a journal, cheap bound, its cover swollen with damp; a pencil stub; and a folded paper that crackled when he touched it — a printed schedule of some kind, columns of figures, a route. Bram set the paper aside and opened the journal with the care of a man defusing something.

The early pages were dull as ditchwater: barometric readings, soundings, notes on current speed, the ordinary grammar of a surveyor's trade. Then, beginning maybe a third of the way in, the entries changed. The handwriting stayed the same but the language shifted into something else — strings of letters and figures in groups of five, page after page, dense as woven cloth. Code. Not casual cipher either; this had the disciplined repetition of military or ministry work, the kind of thing that got funded by budgets instead of salaries.

Bram couldn't read it. But he could read the shape of it, and the shape told him plenty. Surveyors didn't encrypt soundings. Governments encrypted intentions.

One entry near the end, though, wasn't coded. It was written hurriedly, the pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages, as if the man had needed to say it plainly even to himself:

*V. wants the route logged in duplicate, one copy to travel separately from the expedition. If the captain proves obstinate, contingency applies. I did not sign on to drown anyone. Reread clause 4. Clause 4 does not care what I signed on for.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, smaller:

*The letters are real. God help us all, the letters are real.*

Bram sat back on his heels in the dark of the bunker, coal dust settling on his shoulders like ash, and felt fear curdle in his chest and come out the far side as something harder. Fear was for men with options. He counted theirs and found the list short: they were four days above the last settlement, committed to a channel only Xanthe could read, with a dead-or-fled spy, a saboteur uncaught, a hostage contract paying out only at the source, and now a document proving that contingency plans for drowning people had been drafted before they ever left the dock.

Past the point of turning around. They'd passed it days ago, probably at the moment Vane's boots first touched the deck, and every mile since had just been the long process of finding out.

He climbed out of the bunker, closed the hatch softly behind him, and stood a moment in the stokehold's red gloom, wiping his hands on his trousers and getting them blacker instead. Through the bulkhead he could hear Odile murmuring to her engine, the way she did, coaxing it through the night watch. Above, the wheel creaked under Xanthe's hands.

He carried the journal up to the pilothouse, and when Xanthe saw what was in his arms and what was written on his face, she took the wheel out of gear and gave him the whole of her attention, and Bram said:

"Captain. We need to talk about clause four."

Outside, the storm had passed on eastward, and the river ran black and quiet under the stars, keeping what it kept.
