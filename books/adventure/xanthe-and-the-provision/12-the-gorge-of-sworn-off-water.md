## 12. The Gorge of Sworn-Off Water

The turnoff came at dawn, the way terrible things do — quietly, without ceremony, wearing ordinary light.

Xanthe saw it before anyone spoke of it: the place where the Meridian split around a granite shoulder hung with deadfall, the left channel sliding wide and brown and easy toward the lower country they knew, the right channel narrowing into shadow between walls that rose like the pages of a closed book. Ten years ago she had stood on a dock at Gray Landing and watched her father's boat disappear into that shadow. She had been nineteen. She had sworn an oath over an empty grave with river mud on her knees, and for ten years the oath had held like iron.

Now the wheel was in her hands, and the iron was bending.

"Captain." Bram's voice from behind her, low, careful. He had been careful with his voice since the vote, since Odile's raised hand had tipped them upstream by one. "Left channel's open."

"I know what's open," Xanthe said.

She felt Odile come up beside her without looking — felt it the way you feel a change in the wind, a warmth at your shoulder that has nothing to do with the stove. Odile said nothing. That was its own kind of speech. *You voted for this,* the silence said. *Now steer.*

Xanthe put the wheel over to starboard.

The boat answered with a groan she felt through the deck plates, through her boots, through her teeth. The *Provision II* swung her blunt bow toward the gorge, and the shadow took them the way water takes a stone.

* * *

For the first hour there was only noise.

Not rapids yet — something worse, the sound of rapids still to come, a long rolling thunder that lived under the hull and up through the soles of Xanthe's feet like a heartbeat belonging to some enormous sleeping animal they were walking across. The walls closed in. Pine clung to the rock in black tufts. Mist moved through the gorge in slow ropes, and the air went cold enough that Ferro appeared on deck wrapped in two blankets, muttering to his saints, and did not stop muttering even when Bram barked at him to get below.

Xanthe stood at the wheel and let the river talk to her hands.

That was the thing no one understood about piloting who hadn't done it — that the wheel wasn't wood and brass, it was skin. Every eddy wrote itself up the spokes. Every crosscurrent tugged. A good pilot read the water through her palms the way a blind woman reads a face, and Xanthe had spent twenty years learning this river's grammar in the lower reaches where the sentences were short and forgiving.

Up here the grammar changed. Up here the river spoke in her father's accent.

"There." Odile stood at the engine-room hatch, oil-black to the elbows, her gray eyes fixed ahead. "Sound's getting organized. Whatever's coming, it's coming soon."

"How soon?" Bram asked.

"Soon enough that I'd like to know what we're steering into."

Xanthe reached beneath the wheel housing, to the shelf where she'd lashed the oilcloth packet three days ago, and her hand stopped above it. Her father's letters. Recovered from the wreck above — no. Not yet recovered. Still waiting. But his words, copied out in her own hand years ago from the one letter he'd sent before vanishing, the letter she had carried against her heart since she was nineteen and could recite the way other people recited prayers. *If you ever come up past the Rapids, mind the third bend — the water piles high on the outside wall, and there is no rock there to save you, only the reading of it. Trust the boil, not the calm. The calm is the liar.*

She had hated those words for ten years. She had hated that he'd written instructions instead of coming home.

"Bring the lamp," she said.

Bram looked at her. "Captain?"

"Lamp. Matches. And my father's letter — the copy, in the chart drawer under the barometer." She heard her own voice come out flat and steady and wondered whose it belonged to. "I'm going to need him talking in my ear."

* * *

They rigged the lamp on the compass stand, shielded from the wind behind a sheet of tin, and when Odile struck the match the little flame leaned and steadied and threw their shadows huge against the pilothouse glass. Outside, the gorge had gone dark as a throat. The thunder was louder now, close now, and spray came up the rock walls in cold breaths.

Xanthe unfolded the letter. Her father's handwriting — no, her own imitation of it, but she had copied every stroke faithfully, even the places where the ink had blurred, because grief does not permit paraphrase.

"Read it," she said.

Odile looked at her. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow, and Xanthe saw something move through it — fear, and under the fear, the stubborn love that had gotten them both into this. "You're sure?"

"He steered this water once. Maybe twice. He wrote down what he learned." Xanthe set her hands on the wheel. "I'm not too proud to borrow his eyes."

Odile began to read. Her voice, usually so precise with machinery and figures, went soft and rough, and the words came off the page like a man speaking from the far side of death:

*"Mind the third bend. The water piles high on the outside wall..."*

The first rapid hit them before she finished the sentence.

It didn't arrive so much as rise — the whole river standing up ahead of them in the dark, a white stairway of collapsing foam, and Xanthe hauled the wheel hard over as the bow lifted and the world tilted. The lamp swung. Ferro screamed something devotional from the deck. The hull struck the first wave with a blow that rang the length of the boat like a church bell, and then they were inside it, inside the roar, buried to the gunwales in spray and thunder.

"The boil!" Xanthe shouted. "Trust the boil!"

Ahead, where the water should have been smoothest, the surface bulged and rolled — the liar's calm, the deep quiet channel that would have folded them under the falls like a hand closing. She steered for the ugly water instead, for the chaos, because her father had told her the chaos was honest, and the *Provision II* climbed the standing wave and slid down its back with a shudder Xanthe felt in her spine.

Then the second drop. Then the third.

She lost count after that. The night became a sequence of blows and recoveries, of the wheel kicking in her grip like a living thing, of Odile's voice cutting through the roar — *"the outside wall, mind the outside wall"* — and Xanthe leaning her whole body into the turn as the gorge bent and the piled water came at them like a moving cliff. They missed the wall by less than a boat's length. She saw the rock slide past close enough to count the wet striations in it, close enough that the backwash sucked at their stern and spun them half around, and for one full second the *Provision II* lay broadside to the current with the river pouring over her rail.

Later, Xanthe would remember that second more clearly than anything else in her life: the weight of the water pressing the boat sideways, the lamp flame lying flat, Odile's hand finding hers on the wheel — not taking it, just closing over her knuckles, warm and certain — and somewhere underneath everything the memory of her father's voice saying, in a tone she hadn't heard since she was a child, *you can hold her, girl. Boats want to live.*

She put the wheel over. The river argued. The river always argues. But a boat is built to answer her helm if the helm answers honestly, and Xanthe answered honestly, inch by screaming inch, until the bow came round and bit downstream and the current took them the way a fist takes a thrown ball.

They were through. The first gorge, anyway. The thunder fell away behind them into a deeper, slower drumming, and the mist closed in, and no one aboard made a sound for a long time except Ferro, who was praying, and Bram, who was breathing like a man who'd been underwater.

* * *

They tied up at midnight in a slot of slack water between two stone ribs, lines doubled, anchor down, the boat trembling faintly against her restraints like a horse after a hard ride. Xanthe sent everyone below but kept the lamp burning in the pilothouse. She couldn't have said why. Perhaps because putting it out felt like letting him go again.

Odile came up anyway. She always did. She brought two mugs of Ferro's terrible coffee and set one on the chart table without asking, which was how Odile loved things — practically, without ceremony, in objects and repairs and presence rather than declarations.

"You counted," Xanthe said. "Back there. You were reading the letter and watching the water at the same time."

"I can do two things." Odile leaned against the doorframe, steam rising off her mug. "You should see me walk and chew."

Xanthe laughed. It came out of her cracked and unexpected, half sob, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until it passed. "Ten years I thought he died because he was reckless. Because he wanted the source more than he wanted us. And tonight I'm running his water, following his notes, and it's—" She stopped. Started again. "It's like he's at the wheel with me. It's like he never left."

"He did leave." Odile set her mug down and crossed the small space and put her hands on either side of Xanthe's face, oil-stained thumbs against her cheekbones, gentle as a man handling a bearing he couldn't afford to score. "He left, and you're still here, and you got us through a stretch of river that's killed better boats than ours. That's not him, Xanthe. That's you."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the broken seal above the door. Xanthe turned her head and pressed her mouth into Odile's palm, brief and fierce, and neither of them said anything else, because there are moments language only makes smaller.

* * *

Dawn found them at the mouth of the second gorge, and this one was worse.

She could tell from the sound — not the rolling thunder of last night but a sharp, rhythmic booming, like artillery, like the river beating on a door. The walls here pinched to less than sixty feet. The water ran green-black and fast as spilled mercury, and through the middle of it, where the channel should have been, stood a line of white fangs — rocks, or the tops of rocks, breaking the current into fans of spray.

Xanthe studied them through the glass for a long time. Then she opened the letter again, her father's words in her own ink, and read the passage she'd skipped last night because she hadn't needed it yet:

*"At the narrows there is no line through the teeth themselves. Go left of the first, right of the second, and then commit to the seam — the dark thread between the boils. It looks like nothing. It is the only water deep enough to float a loaded hull. Do not hesitate in the seam. Hesitation is the only thing the narrows punishes."*

"Do not hesitate," she repeated aloud.

"That's reassuring," Bram said dryly, coiling a line with hands that hadn't quite stopped shaking since midnight. "Real comforting, Captain."

"Ferro." She turned. The cook stood at the rail wrapped in his blankets, gray-faced, his burned hands bandaged in strips of shirt. "Get below and lash everything. Double-lash. If we ship water, I want nothing loose enough to kill anyone."

"And if we don't come through?" Ferro asked. It wasn't insolence. It was a genuine question, the question of a man who had made his peace with the answer.

"Then lash it anyway," Xanthe said, "for whoever finds us."

Ferro crossed himself and went below, and Xanthe took the wheel.

The narrows took them the way a mill takes grain. The current seized the bow and dragged, and the first fang came at them out of the green water faster than anything had a right to move, and she went left of it so close that the suction of its wake pulled the stern out and she had to fight the wheel through a full quarter-turn while the second rock swung into view. Right of the second. The hull shrieked along something beneath the surface — not a strike, a kiss, a scrape that would cost them paint and maybe planking — and then the seam was there, exactly as he'd written it: a thread of dark water no wider than the boat herself, threading between two boiling cauldrons of white.

It looked like nothing. It looked like a trick, a lie, a grave with a painted floor.

*Do not hesitate.*

Xanthe committed. She drove the *Provision II* into the seam at full throttle, and the river closed over them on both sides — walls of standing water higher than the rail, curling, hungry — and for eleven seconds, by Bram's later count, they rode a ribbon of black glass through the heart of the rapid with destruction on either side close enough to touch. Spray rained down. The lamp went out. Somewhere below, Odile's engine roared like a caged animal and never once faltered, and Xanthe understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy, that this was what her father had meant. Not the letters. Not the route. This — a crew that holds, a wheel that answers, a seam that holds true if you trust it all the way through.

The seam spat them out into a long pool below the narrows, and the pool was so still after the roaring that the silence rang.

* * *

They drifted that pool in the early light, everyone on deck, no one speaking. The gorge walls had fallen back. Ahead, the river widened into a valley of gray scree and hanging glaciers, and the air had the clean knife-edge smell of ice.

Xanthe stood at the wheel with her father's letter folded in her breast pocket, over her heart, where it had lived when she was nineteen. She had carried it for ten years as a relic of a dead man's obsession. She carried it now as something else — a chart drawn by someone who had loved this river enough to learn its honesty, and who had trusted his daughter, someday, to read it.

The oath was broken. She had known it since the moment the shadow of the gorge swallowed the bow, and she had braced for the guilt to crush her, and it hadn't come. What came instead was stranger: release. Grief going out of her like water out of a holed hull — not all at once, not clean, but going. Ten years of anger at a man who had perhaps only ever been doing what she had done last night: holding his crew, trusting the boil, answering honestly.

"Captain." Bram's voice, odd. He was pointing forward, to the right-hand bank, where the morning sun had finally cleared the ridge.

Around the next bend, half-sunk in a cold shallow, lay a boat.

Black hull. Familiar lines. A figurehead at the broken bow — a carved woman with her arms spread wide, weathered silver-gray, unmistakable even at a hundred yards, even after ten years of ice water.

The *Provision*. Her father's boat. Sitting in the shallows like a patient ghost, waiting for someone to finally come and ask it what happened.

No one on deck said a word. Below them, faint and steady, the river went on talking in the dark, keeping what it keeps.
