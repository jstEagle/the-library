## 13. What the Ice Keeps

The river above the gorge was a different animal entirely.

Xanthe had known it would be — her father's letters had said as much, and the letters had not lied to her yet — but knowing a thing and standing in the middle of it were two countries apart. The Meridian up here ran shallow and glass-clear over beds of pale stone, so transparent that the bottom seemed to hover just beneath the hull, close enough to touch. The water was cold enough to ache. It came off the glacier in long green pulses, and where the sun struck it the whole river glowed like something lit from underneath, like a lamp behind jade.

Nobody spoke. They hadn't spoken much since the gorge.

Bram stood at the rail with his arms folded, watching the banks slide past — scree slopes, then stands of silver-barked pine bent inland by wind no one downriver ever felt. Ferro had come out of his galley for the first time since the Staircase, hands still bandaged from the burns, and stood at the bow with his cap off, murmuring something under his breath that might have been prayer or might have been inventory. Odile was below, coaxing the engine back into some kind of temper after what they'd put it through, and the sound of her hammer came up through the deck plates in slow, patient strokes, like a heartbeat deciding whether to continue.

And Vane stood at the stern with his chart case under one arm, saying nothing at all, which frightened Xanthe more than anything he'd said yet.

They rounded a bend where the valley narrowed and the cliffs came down to the water in shelves of gray stone, and there the current slackened, pooling out into a wide still reach perhaps four hundred yards across. Too wide. Too still. Rivers didn't spread themselves thin unless something upstream was holding them back, and Xanthe knew without being told what held them: the glacier, somewhere beyond the next bend, sitting on the throat of the valley like a fist.

It was Odile who saw it first. She came up the ladder two rungs at a time, wiping her hands on a rag, and stopped dead at the rail.

"Captain," she said quietly. "Look at the shallows."

Xanthe looked.

The eastern shore shelved gently into the pool, and in the clear green water, maybe six feet down, lay a shape that did not belong to rivers. A keel. A long dark spine of timber, furred with pale silt, running thirty feet along the bottom with the patient posture of something that had lain down intending never to rise again. Forward of it, half-buried, the curve of a stem post. And rising from the stem, tilted, crusted white with mineral bloom but unmistakable even through six feet of cold water —

A figurehead. A woman carved of oak, one arm extended forward into the current, her face worn smooth by ten years of ice-melt, still pointing upriver toward the source she'd never reached.

The *Provision*.

Xanthe heard the sound she made as if it came from someone else — a small broken noise, half breath, half word — and then her hands were on the wheel and she was bringing the boat around in a slow arc toward the shallows, because her body had decided before her mind could vote. The anchor went down in eight feet of water with a rattle that echoed off the cliffs. The echo took a long time to die.

"That's her," Bram said. Not a question. His voice was rough. "That's your father's boat."

"Yes," Xanthe said.

She was already moving for the skiff.

* * *

The water was so cold it stopped being pain almost immediately and became something larger, a white pressure that closed around her chest and made her ribs feel like someone else's. Xanthe waded the last few yards in water to her thighs, boots filling, breath coming in short involuntary gasps, until the wreck rose beneath her and she could see it whole.

Ten years in meltwater should have eaten a boat to lacework. But this water was different — too cold for the worms and rot that worked the lower Meridian, too poor in oxygen, sealed in its own chill like a cellar. The *Provision*'s hull lay intact on the pale stones, planking sound, seams tight, the paint gone but the wood itself preserved to a gray-brown color like old bone. Her father's boat had not decayed. It had been *kept*. The thought arrived in Xanthe's mind with a weight that had nothing to do with grief and everything to do with dread, though she couldn't have said why.

The cabin roof had partially collapsed inward. Through the gap where the companionway hatch had been, she could see down into the dim interior, where the water moved in faint slow currents, stirring nothing.

"Xanthe." Odile had followed her into the shallows, stripped to her shirt, teeth already chattering, holding the boat hook like a lance. "I'm coming down with you. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to try."

"I was going to try," Xanthe admitted, and took her hand.

They dove together, twice, three times, hauling things up through the hatch into the light: a lantern crusted shut, a sea chest whose lock had rusted open, coils of line turned to stiff rope-sculpture. On the fourth dive Xanthe came up gasping with something clutched against her chest, and it took her a moment to understand what she was holding, because her hands had recognized it before her eyes did.

A ship's bell. Bronze, green-black with age, the word **PROVISION** cast deep into the metal in her father's own chosen lettering. She remembered the day it was hung. She had been nine years old, and he had lifted her up so she could strike it once, and the sound had gone down the whole dock at Gray Landing, and every man who heard it had turned to look.

She held it above the water and it dripped, and rang once, very softly, against her wrist.

Behind her, in the shallows, Ferro made a sound like a man being strangled slowly and sat down in six inches of water, cap pressed to his chest.

* * *

It was Bram, working along the starboard side where the hull gaped where it had struck stone, who found the crew.

He didn't shout. He went very quiet, which was worse, and when Xanthe swam around to him he was standing waist-deep beside the wreck, looking down, and his face had gone the color of the stones.

"Captain," he said. "You should — you need to see this. Or maybe you don't. God help me, I don't know which."

There were five of them.

They lay in and around the wreck in attitudes that the cold water had preserved with terrible clarity — not scattered, not flung about as drowning scatters the drowned, but *arranged*, or so it seemed to Xanthe's eye in the first endless moment, laid along the bottom like tools set down at the end of a shift. Two were inside the cabin. One was wedged beneath the fallen roof beam. One lay on the stones beside the hull with his arm still extended toward the shallows, fingers reaching for a shore he'd died eighteen feet from.

The fifth was nearest the bow, and he was the reason Bram had gone quiet.

Because the fifth man had an iron spike driven through him.

It had gone in below the collarbone and pinned him to the deck planking, and the planking had rotted away around the wound over the years so that now he lay on the stones with the spike standing up out of his chest into the clear green water, rusted to a soft feathered orange, unmistakable, undeniable, a thing that had been done to him by human hands while he was alive or nearly alive. Around the entry point the bone showed pale fractures radiating outward like frost on a window.

Xanthe surfaced. She found she was holding the bell against her body with both arms, hard, the way you hold a child in bad weather.

"Spear butt," Odile said quietly beside her. She'd seen it too; of course she had. "Or a boat hook, driven. That's not an accident. That's not the river." Her voice was steady the way a wire is steady under load. "Xanthe. Look at their hands."

Xanthe looked. It took her a moment, through the distortion of the water, through the mineral bloom, and then she saw it, and the cold she felt then had nothing to do with the glacier.

Three of the five skeletons wore leather map cases on their belts — survey men carried them slung so, her father had taught her, keeping the charts dry against the body. And every one of those cases had been cut open. Slashed lengthwise with a blade, deliberately, methodically, and the charts themselves were gone. Not scattered, not dissolved. Gone. Taken.

"They wouldn't mark it," Xanthe heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, flattened by the cliffs, by the water, by ten years. "That's what the old woman said. He died protecting the source. I thought — I thought it meant he died in the rapids trying to turn back. I thought it meant the river took him." She looked down through the green glass of the water at the man with the spike in his chest, at his reaching hand. "It wasn't the river."

"No," Odile said. "It wasn't."

Above them, on the deck of the steamboat, Cassius Vane stood at the rail looking down at the shallows, and his face wore an expression Xanthe had never seen on it before, and it took her a long moment to name it, because she had no practice with it.

It was fear.

* * *

They brought the last of it up as the light went long and gold across the pool: the captain's strongbox, pried open on the stones, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth that the cold had kept perfect, a bundle of papers.

Most were logs. Xanthe read them by lamplight in the mess that evening while the crew crowded close and Vane stood apart by the stove, uninvited, unremoved, watching her with the careful stillness of a man counting exits. The log told a story she already understood in her bones: the *Provision* running the gorge against her father's better judgment because his backers' letters had demanded it, the near-losses, the repairs, and then the final entries, written in a hand grown hurried and angry.

*Vane's principals insist we proceed to the source and fix its position by instrument. I have refused. Told them plainly: some water is not for sale. They did not like the answer. Pell's man aboard says there are those among the crew who will mark it for a share. I have ordered the charts of the upper reaches burned.*

The date was eleven days before the *Provision* was given up for lost.

At the bottom of the bundle, folded separately, sealed with wax that had cracked but held, was a letter addressed in her father's hand: *To my daughter, if the river gives this back to anyone, let it be her.*

Xanthe did not open it at the table. She carried it out onto the deck alone, into the blue cold of the evening, and broke the seal with her thumbnail, and read it twice by the light of the deck lantern while the glacier groaned somewhere beyond the bend like a thing turning over in its sleep.

*My girl. If you are reading this, then you have come farther than I ever wanted you to, and I am sorry for it, and proud of you, both at once, which I suspect is the permanent condition of loving someone.*

*We found it. The source is everything they hoped — more. There is metal in the mountain here, rare and worth fighting wars over, and the men who sent me do not intend to fight wars. They intend to own. I have seen the surveyor's orders. Whoever fixes the source and files the claim holds the whole watershed, and everyone downstream of it, which is everyone I have ever loved.*

*I have sunk my instruments. I have burned my charts. My crew — some of them — would not agree, and I write this tonight not knowing how it ends between us. If it ends badly, know that I did not die chasing glory. I died refusing to sell you. All of you. The river keeps what it takes, and I give it gladly, if giving it means no man can hold its throat.*

*Do not avenge me. Protect them instead. That is the only inheritance I have to leave you, and it is enough. It has always been enough.*

*Your father,*

*Aurelio Sandoval*

Xanthe stood a long time with the letter in her hands. The pool lay still around the anchored boats. Somewhere in the shallows below, five men waited in the cold green light with their opened map cases and their reaching hands, and the woman on the prow of the wreck went on pointing upriver, faithful and blind, toward the thing that had killed them all.

When she came back into the mess, the crew fell silent. Vane straightened by the stove.

"Well?" he said. "The letter. Does it confirm the deposits? Does it give the bearing? Captain, whatever sentiment you're entertaining, there are men of substance waiting on—"

"It names them," Xanthe said.

Her voice came out level, and that surprised her, because everything behind it was fire.

"Your backers. By name. Meridian Consolidated. Hollis and Crane. And the signature of the man who gave the order to *fix the source by any means necessary*" — she watched him flinch at his own words coming back to him — "*is the same hand that signed your charter. Isn't it, Mr. Vane. You've been carrying his seal this whole voyage. You've been carrying his orders.*"

The silence stretched. Ferro had gone gray. Bram's hand had drifted, casually, terribly, to rest on the iron pry-bar leaning against the bulkhead.

"My father's crew refused to mark the source," Xanthe said, and now the levelness cracked, and the fire came through, and she let it. "So your principal's men drove a spike through the ones who wouldn't bend, cut the charts out of their belts, and left them for the river. Ten years ago. And you've spent ten years polishing your boots and buying debts and hiring pilots, trying to finish what they started." She stepped forward, and Vane, to his lasting credit or his animal cunning, did not step back. "You didn't bring us up here for a survey, Vane. You brought us up here because I'm the only living soul who could run that gorge, and because you needed my father's daughter to open his grave for you."

"Captain," Vane began, smooth as oil on water, "whatever you think you've found—"

"I've found witnesses," Xanthe said. "Five of them. And a letter. And a bell. And tomorrow we go up to that glacier, and we take back what's left of my family's name — and then, Mr. Vane, you and I are going to settle accounts."

Behind Vane's shoulder, unnoticed by everyone but Odile, the surveyor Pell slipped quietly out of the mess, and the deck outside creaked once, twice, and went still.
