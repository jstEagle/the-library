## 14. The Letters

The wreck of the *Provision* sat in the shallows like a thing the river had decided to keep rather than kill.

They had moored for the night in a cove of gray stone above the gorge, close enough that Xanthe could see her father's boat from the pilothouse window — hull half-submerged, deck timbers silvered and sound, preserved by water so cold it refused to let anything rot. The figurehead still stood at the bow, a carved woman with her arms open, her face worn smooth by a decade of ice-melt. Xanthe had looked at that face since she was small enough to sit on the rail. She had never noticed until now how much it looked like her mother.

Below decks, in the saloon where the lamp burned low against the blue dusk coming off the glacier, the crew gathered without being asked. Bram brought his mended knee and said nothing. Ferro crossed himself with a hand still bandaged from the Staircase and set a pot of coffee on the table as if it were an offering. Odile came last, carrying the oilskin packet they had pulled from the captain's cabin of the dead boat — dry inside, impossibly, after ten years, because her father had wrapped it the way he wrapped everything: like a man who expected the river to test him.

Xanthe untied the cord with fingers that would not quite obey.

Inside were letters. Dozens of them, written on the backs of chart margins, on butcher paper, on the flyleaves of his tide almanac — all addressed to her. All dated in the last months of his final voyage.

She read the first one aloud because she could not have kept silent if she'd tried.

---

*"My Xanthe —*

*Day forty-one above Gray Landing. The gorge is worse than the stories, which means the stories were told by liars or survivors, and I have met both.*

*We found the source pool today. I will not describe it, because a description is the first step toward a map, and a map is the first step toward a claim, and I did not come up here to make the Meridian into somebody's property. Pell's grandfather tried to buy this water thirty years ago and the mountain laughed at him. His grandson is aboard my boat now, drawing in his little book, and I pretend not to see.*

*The water is bluer than any blue I have a word for. It does not move. It is the most frightening thing I have ever seen, and I have seen you learn to swim in flood season.*

*— Your father"*

---

Ferro made a small sound, half prayer, half cough. Nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the glacier ticked and settled, the way a house does at night.

Xanthe turned the page. Her hands were steady now. That surprised her; she had expected them to shake, and instead they felt like hands on a wheel in fair weather, the strange calm that comes after the worst has already happened.

---

*"My Xanthe —*

*Pell showed me his papers tonight. Not the surveyor boy — the other one, the one Vane sent to watch us. They are offering more money than I have earned in my whole life for the coordinates, the depth soundings, the seasonal calving tables. Enough to buy you a new boat. Enough to buy ten boats, and a dock, and your name on a building.*

*I told him no.*

*I want you to understand that it was not hard. That is what I want you to carry, when you read this, if you ever do. People will tell you that every man has his price, and they will say it in a way that makes refusal sound like pride or foolishness or bad arithmetic. It is none of those things. Some things are not for sale because selling them is not possible. You cannot sell the rain. You cannot sell the north wind. You cannot sell the place where the river begins, because the river does not belong to the place — the place belongs to the river, and the river belongs to nobody, and that is the whole of the law up here.*

*He grew angry. He said I was condemning my crew to poverty. I looked at my crew, and they were playing cards, and none of them looked poor.*

*— Your father"*

---

Odile moved. Xanthe saw it only at the edge of her vision — Odile rising, crossing to the stern windows, standing with her back to the room and her arms folded, looking out at the black water and the pale ghost of the wreck. Xanthe knew that stance. It was the stance Odile took when feeling got too large for sitting down.

She kept reading.

---

*"My Xanthe —*

*They have stopped pretending. Harrow — that is the watcher's name, though I doubt it is his — has turned two of my men. I know which two. I let them believe I don't. Tonight I am going to sink my charts.*

*Not hide them. Sinking is better. Hiding is just ownership postponed; some other fool finds the box someday and thinks it a treasure. But the river keeps what it takes, and the river can be trusted with a secret better than any man alive.*

*If you are reading this, then someone found the letters and not the charts, and I am glad. I ordered it so. The letters are worth finding. The route is not.*

*Xanthe, listen to me. I know what you will want to do. You will want to come up here and find out what happened, and you will be right to want it, and you must not do it alone, and you must not do it angry. Anger is a current. It feels like power because it carries you fast, but it decides the direction, not you.*

*Whatever they offer you — and they will offer, they always offer — remember that the answer costs nothing to give and everything to withhold.*

*Say no slowly. Say it kindly if you can. But say it.*

*— Your father"*

---

Bram cleared his throat. "Captain." His voice came out gravel over gravel. "That last part. He wrote that before—" He stopped, worked his jaw. "Before whatever happened."

"He knew it might," Xanthe said. "He sank the charts and kept writing anyway."

"That's either faith or stubbornness."

"It's both. It was always both. Where do you think I got it?"

Something almost like a smile moved under Bram's beard and died there. Ferro poured coffee nobody had asked for and set the cups down gently, like laying stones on graves.

Xanthe read on. There were three letters left. The next was short, and her voice caught on its first line and she had to stop, drink cold coffee, and start again.

---

*"My Xanthe —*

*Harrow came at me with a knife tonight over the sounding book. I am writing this by lamplight while the crew sleeps, and my arm is bound, and I find I am not afraid, only tired, and sorry — sorry that men can look at a thing as clean as that water and see nothing but what can be dug out from under it.*

*Tomorrow we go down through the gorge. Harrow thinks I am beaten. Let him. A man who thinks you're beaten steers badly.*

*If the Provision doesn't reach Gray Landing, don't look for wreckage above the falls. Look below them. And when you find what's below them, remember: I chose the river over the money, and I would choose it again, and choosing it did not feel like losing.*

*It felt like coming home wet.*

*— Your father"*

---

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the door and straightened. Outside, the ice groaned — long, deep, patient, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

One letter remained. It was written on a scrap of chart margin, the ink faded to brown, the handwriting cramped, hurried, the letters of a man writing against time. Xanthe held it a moment before reading, the way you hold a rope before trusting your weight to it.

---

*"My Xanthe —*

*Last night. I feel it in the way the crew won't meet my eye, all but young Odile's father, who sleeps across the cabin from me with his boots on.*

*So this is the truth, and then I am done writing: Harrow's backers have a name, and it is not Vane. Vane is boots and polish and other men's money. The name behind him is Corvane & Associates, of the capital, and they hold claims on three rivers already and call themselves stewards. Steward is what a thief calls himself when the theft is legal.*

*Sink the charts, Elias. Sink them tonight. If they take this boat, let them take a boat full of nothing.*

*And girl — if you ever stand at that water — don't claim it. Don't guard it either. Guarding is just owning with a rifle. Just love it and leave it be. That's the whole inheritance. It fits in one sentence. I spent my life learning it so you wouldn't have to spend yours.*

*— Your father"*

---

Silence took the saloon the way cold takes a cabin — gradually, then completely.

Xanthe set the last letter down on the table and looked at it for a long time. Ten years she had carried a question shaped like a wound: *what happened to him?* And beneath it, older and quieter, another question she had never dared speak: *what would he have wanted?* She had assumed the answers were buried in the gorge, in the wreck, in the ice. She had run rivers for a decade half-believing that somewhere upstream waited a truth that would finally let her put the wheel down.

But the truth had been waiting in an oilskin packet, and it fit in one sentence.

*Don't claim it. Don't guard it. Love it and leave it be.*

"I spent ten years thinking my inheritance was a grave," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, scraped hollow by the reading. "A debt. Something owed. I thought if I ever learned what happened up here, I'd finally know what I was supposed to do about it." She shook her head slowly. "He wrote it all down. He handed it to me across ten years of cold water, and it isn't a route and it isn't a reckoning. It's a refusal. That's what he left me. That's the whole estate."

Ferro spoke first, softly, in the way he spoke to the river when he thought no one was listening. "Then the old man's boat wasn't lost, Captain. It was delivered."

Bram grunted. "Delivered ten years late."

"The river keeps what it takes," Ferro said. "And gives back what it chooses."

* * *

Odile stood at the stern windows a long time before anyone joined her, and when footsteps came she knew them without turning. Xanthe settled beside her, shoulder to shoulder, both of them looking out at the cove, at the pale shape of the *Provision* lying in the shallows like a sleeping animal.

"You heard it," Xanthe said. "All of it."

"I heard it." Odile's breath fogged the glass. She wiped it with the heel of her hand, an engineer's gesture, impatient with anything that obscured a view. "Your father slept across the cabin from mine. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Neither did I, until tonight." Odile was quiet a moment. "Two girls downstream, each thinking their father went up that river alone. And the whole time they were up here together, keeping each other's watch." She laughed, but there was no humor in it — something rawer, closer to grief finally finding its level. "The river keeps what it takes. Maybe it also keeps what we give it."

"My father says anger is a current."

"Your father says a lot of true things for a dead man." Odile turned from the window. In the lamplight her face was tired and open and certain, all at once. "Xanthe. Vane is still out there with two hired guns and my engine, and tomorrow he'll be drilling at the roof of the world. Whatever your father's sentence means — love it and leave it be — it doesn't mean letting Vane own it. Leaving it be isn't the same as leaving it to him."

"I know."

"Do you?" Odile took her hands. Hers were warm, oil-stained, steady. "Because I've watched you carry that oath like ballast for ten years, and tonight you put it down, and I'm glad, I'm so glad — but don't put down the wheel with it. What he refused, we protect. Not with a rifle. With witnesses. With testimony. With every soul on this crew swearing to what we found in those shallows. Vane fears failure, yes. But what he fears more is being *seen*. Men like him operate in blank spaces on maps. So we fill in the blank space — with names, dates, skeletons, letters. We make the source famous for having no price."

Xanthe looked at their hands together, at the grease in the creases of Odile's knuckles, and felt something settle in her chest that had been out of trim for a decade — like a boat taking the current square, finally, after years of crabbing sideways against it.

"Witnesses," she said.

"And testimony."

"And the evidence sinks."

"The evidence sinks," Odile agreed. "Let the river keep what it takes. We keep the telling of it. That can't be seized, taxed, or drilled."

Xanthe smiled — the first real one in days, tired and crooked. "You've been saving that speech."

"Since the mess hall vote. I abstained because I hadn't finished writing it."

Outside, the glacier spoke again — longer this time, a groan that climbed in pitch until it became almost a whistle, and both women went still, listening the way sailors listen to rigging in a rising wind.

"That's early," Odile said quietly. "Calving shouldn't start for days yet. The meltwater's running high and warm off the face. Whatever clock Vane is working against, it just started ticking faster."

Xanthe turned back to the table, to the letters lying in the lamplight like a hand of cards finally shown. She gathered them, wrapped them in the oilskin, tied the cord — her father's knot, the one he'd taught her when she was six, the one that only tightened under strain.

"Bram," she called. "Wake the others. We walk at first light."

"Aye, Captain." A pause. "Walk where?"

Xanthe looked out the dark window, north, toward the pale glow where the ice field threw light back at the stars.

"To the source," she said. "Not to claim it. To witness it. And if Vane's drill has already bitten stone—" her hand closed around the oilskin packet, her father's knot tightening under her grip exactly as it was made to do, "—then he's going to learn what my father learned. Some things can't be bought. And some things can't be stopped, either. The river taught him the first. Tomorrow it teaches Vane the second."

Above the cove, far up the valley, the ice groaned again — and this time, faint but unmistakable, came the crack and boom of the first serac letting go, falling into water that had been still for ten thousand years.
