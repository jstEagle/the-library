## 15. Vane's Coup

Odile heard the engine die before she understood why.

She was below, elbow-deep in the boiler's warm throat, coaxing the firebox to hold its heat through the last bend before the high shallows. The *Provision II* had been running sweet all morning — too sweet, she'd thought, the way a horse runs sweet just before it founders. And then the sound of the river swelled up around her like water filling a room, and she knew: someone had thrown the fuel cut.

She came up the ladder with grease to her wrists and found the pilothouse door open and Xanthe standing very still at the wheel, and Cassius Vane standing behind her with a revolver held low against his thigh, the way a man holds a tool he has used before.

"Captain," Vane said pleasantly, "I believe we've arrived."

* * *

It had happened fast. That was the thing Odile kept returning to afterward, turning it over in her mind like a stone that wouldn't warm. It had happened in the space of one watch change. Bram had gone below to spell Ferro at the coal. Pell's two remaining men — the quiet ones, the ones who'd barely spoken since Pell went overboard — had come up from the foredeck where they'd been ostensibly coiling line, and one of them had taken the wheelhouse steps and the other had covered them, and Vane had simply walked aboard out of the morning mist as though he owned the deck planks under his polished boots.

Which, Odile thought bitterly, in every way that mattered, he did.

The boat drifted now in the slack water above the last gorge pool, current nudging her lazily toward the gravel bar on the eastern shore — a long pale tongue of stone and silt that the spring melt hadn't yet reclaimed. Vane had chosen it deliberately. Odile could see that even through her fear. A bar like that would be an island by afternoon, when the glacier fed the river harder. An island with no wood worth burning, no shelter but the willow scrub, and forty miles of scree between it and anything resembling help.

"Engineer." Vane's voice found her at the base of the ladder, and she made herself turn and face him. He looked rested. That was the obscenity of it. Ten days of rapids and sabotage and a man overboard, and Cassius Vane looked like he'd slept in linen. "You'll stay aboard."

"The hell she will," Bram said. He stood near the gangway with his fists half-closed, and one of Vane's men shifted his weight, and the whole deck seemed to lean toward violence the way a hull leans into a turn.

"Bram." Xanthe's voice came down flat and level from the wheelhouse. "Nobody dies today."

"That," said Vane, "has always been my hope as well. I'm glad we're agreed." He gestured with the revolver, an almost courteous little wave, toward the gangway. "Mr. Ferro, Mr. Bram. Off you go. Take water. Take the tent fly if you like; I'm not a monster. You'll be collected within the week, once I've run the upper water and come back down for you. The river commission will hear a story about engine trouble and a prudent captain putting her crew ashore for safety. Nobody need be embarrassed."

"And if we don't go?" Ferro asked. His voice was thin. He had his lucky iron nail in one fist, Odile saw, worrying it the way he worried everything.

Vane smiled at him with genuine warmth, which was worse than any threat. "Then my men will help you off, and they are not gentle helpers. Please don't make me choose between your dignity and your bones."

* * *

They put ashore because there was nothing else. Odile watched it happen from the rail — Bram carrying the water kegs with his jaw set like a cleat, Ferro clutching his bag of charms and muttering to the mountain, both of them looking back every third step. Xanthe came down from the wheelhouse last, and for a moment she and Odile stood close enough to touch, and neither of them touched, because one of Vane's men was watching with the flat patience of a man being paid by the hour.

"Listen to me," Xanthe said, low, urgent, her words coming fast and quiet. "The linkage bolt in the reversing gear — port side — I felt it working loose yesterday. If it goes, the engine won't answer astern. She'll only run forward."

Odile stared at her. Then she understood, and something moved through her chest that wasn't quite hope and wasn't quite terror but was some alloy of the two. "Forward into what?"

"Whatever I choose." Xanthe's eyes were steady. Her hand brushed Odile's knuckles — one second, no more, a touch so brief it might have been an accident, except that nothing between them had ever been accidental. "Don't fix it. Whatever he tells you. Don't fix it."

"Miss Sandoval." One of the hired men stepped forward, and the moment broke like ice underfoot.

Xanthe turned to Vane. "You understand she can't run this engine alone. Not upstream, not down. You're taking a hostage, not a crew."

"I'm taking insurance," Vane corrected mildly. "And a mechanic. She is, I'm told, the finest engineer on the Meridian. I expect she'll manage. People rise to their circumstances." He glanced at Odile then, and his gaze was appraising and cold and entirely without malice, which was somehow the most frightening thing about him. "Miss Marchetti. I want you to know that I regret the necessity. Truly. Of everyone on this boat, you're the only one who ever looked at my crates and asked the right questions. I'd have hired you properly, given time."

"You did offer," Odile said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I remember what I said."

"Yes. Pity." He tipped his hat to her, actually tipped it, and turned away. "Cast off when you're ready, gentlemen. We have a source to find."

* * *

The first hour was the worst.

Not because of what Vane did — he did almost nothing, merely sat in the pilothouse with the chart spread open and let his men handle the lines — but because of what Odile could see happening to the boat without her hands on her. Every vessel has a voice, and the *Provision II* spoke to her constantly: the pitch of the stack, the shudder in the guards, the particular complaint of the shaft bearing when the current took her stern. Now Odile stood at the rail like a passenger, listening to her own ship mispronounce herself, and every wrong note was a small wound.

Vane's man at the throttle — the taller one, whose name might have been Hask — worked the levers like a man driving a mule. Too much steam, too sudden. She heard the firebox gasp. She heard the safety valve lift with a shriek and fall silent again.

"You're wasting pressure," she said finally, because she couldn't stand it. "Ease her. She's not a freight locomotive."

Hask glanced back at Vane, who nodded once, amused. "By all means, Miss Marchetti. Advise him."

So she did. She stood at the throttle-man's shoulder and coached him through the reach above the gorge pool, hating herself and hating him and loving the engine all at once, because the engine was innocent, the engine deserved better than this, and keeping her alive was the only power Odile had left. That was the lesson of the hour, and she learned it thoroughly: captivity was not chains. Captivity was competence pointed at another man's purpose.

Below them, shrinking with each bend, the gravel bar receded. She thought she saw figures moving along its spine — three of them, maybe four, already climbing toward the treeline. Already walking. Good, she thought. Walk. Climb. Find us.

Then the gorge walls closed in, and the world became rock and water and the smell of glacial dust, and there was nothing to do but work.

* * *

Vane came to the engine room at midday.

He descended the ladder carefully, boots finding the treads with a dandy's precision, and stood in the heat and the oil-smell with his coat over his arm and looked around at her domain with the expression of a buyer at auction. Odile kept her hands on the reversing gear and did not stop working. Sweat ran down her spine. The firebox painted one side of her face orange.

"She's a good boat," Vane said. "Her father built well. I ran against the old *Provision* twice, you know. Lost both times. Xanthe probably never told you that."

"She doesn't talk about you much."

"No. I imagine I'm a sore subject." He came closer, and Odile made herself keep her eyes on the gauge glass, watching the water level tremble. "I want to show you something."

He unfolded a paper — not the chart, something smaller, a letter or a copy of one — and held it where she could see without taking her hands from the gear. It was dense with handwriting, faded brown ink, and at the bottom a signature she recognized from the nameboard in the pilothouse: *Elias Sandoval.*

"Your partner's father wrote this eleven days before he vanished," Vane said. "It names the deposits. Rare earths, Miss Marchetti — cerium, neodymium, enough of both to make a nation rewrite its maps. Enough to buy every riverboat on the Meridian and the yards that built them. My backers have spent nine years and a fortune getting to this point. Do you know what stands between them and it now?"

"You," Odile said.

"Me." He sounded pleased. "And a broken linkage bolt, if I'm not mistaken. Yes, Captain told me. Or rather, she told you, thinking I wouldn't notice the telling." He folded the letter away. "Here is what happens next. You will repair that bolt. You will keep this engine answering until we reach the source pool, and after — because I'll need her to run home loaded, and loaded heavy. In exchange, when this is done, you walk away rich. Not comfortable. Rich. Your share of the survey stake, signed and witnessed, more money than ten years of freight runs would earn you. You could buy the *Provision II* outright. You could buy her a sister."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll have Hask hold the throttle and we'll all take our chances with the gorge, and I suspect the gorge will win, and your partner will drown in a boat you could have saved." He said it without heat, the way a man reads a shipping schedule. "I don't want that. I genuinely don't. But I've come too far and spent too much to be stopped by a bolt."

Odile wiped her hands slowly on her rag. She thought of Xanthe's mouth at her ear: *don't fix it.* She thought of the reversing gear, the worn bolt, the geometry of failure. She thought of the letters they'd read by lamplight in the gorge, Elias Sandoval's voice rising off the page — *the river is not a larder, it is a living thing, and what it keeps it keeps for reasons* — and she thought of the skeletons in the cold shallows, men who had died refusing exactly this.

"You know what I find interesting," she said. "You keep saying *my backers.* Not *we.* Not *our claim.* You've been calling them that since Gray Landing. A man who owns a thing says *mine.*"

Something flickered across Vane's face — quick, quickly mastered, but she caught it, and it was the most human thing she'd seen him do. "Careful, engineer."

"I'm always careful. It's my trade." She turned back to the gauge glass. "I'll keep the engine running. That's my job, and I do my job. But I don't fix bolts on command, and I don't sign things, and if you put that letter in front of me again I'll tell you what I think of a dead man's handwriting in a thief's pocket."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, the safety valve sighed. Then Vane laughed — short, genuine, almost admiring — and climbed back up the ladder.

At the top he paused. "You realize," he said, "that refusing marks you. It marked Pell, in the end."

"Pell fell overboard," Odile said.

"Yes," said Vane. "Didn't he."

* * *

Night came down into the gorge like a lid.

They anchored in a deep eddy below a wall of black granite, and Vane posted his men in watches and allowed lamps, and Odile worked the fire down to a banked glow and tried to think. The engine room was hers still, in the way that mattered — Vane needed it running, needed *her* running it, and that need was a door left ajar. She catalogued what she had: tools, a coil of copper wire, the spare whistle valve, a tin of lamp oil. She catalogued what she knew: the channel notes Xanthe had drummed into her over ten years of shared watches, the sound the river made at each bend, the fact that above the gorge, somewhere, three people were walking through freezing scree toward them with wreckage-iron and fury.

And she catalogued the bolt.

She knelt at the reversing gear with her lamp and looked at it honestly, engineer to engineer. Xanthe wanted it loose. But loose meant no astern power, and no astern power in the upper water meant that whatever Xanthe planned, the plan ended either in victory or in the bottom of the river, with no middle ground and no second try. Odile pressed her thumb against the bolt head. It shifted, a hair's breadth, a whisper of metal.

*I could tighten it,* she thought. *One wrench, five minutes, and we'd have a real boat instead of a gamble.*

But a real boat was also what Vane wanted. A boat that answered him. A boat he could load with samples and papers and run home to his backers, and the source would be a claim on a map forever, and Elias Sandoval's refusal would have died in the cold for nothing.

She thought about love, then — really thought about it, the way she rarely let herself in daylight. About how it wasn't the touches or the shared watches or even the lamplit letters. It was this: trusting another person's judgment with your life inside their plan. Xanthe had steered the Sworn-Off Water on faith and her father's words. Odile could leave one bolt loose on the same faith.

She left it loose. She oiled around it, cleaned it, made it look tended, and let it whisper.

Above her, boots crossed the deck. Vane's voice drifted down through the floor grating, low, talking to Hask — and she caught fragments, and the fragments were not about the source. Something about *the wreck.* Something about *the papers still aboard it, if the cold kept them.* Something about *the daughter being useful after all, once the claim is filed — a Sandoval signature lends legitimacy, and grief makes people agreeable.*

Odile sat very still in the orange dark.

He didn't want the source. He wanted the proof. The wreck, the letters, the evidence — the thing Xanthe had gone upriver grieving for was the same thing Vane intended to carry home in a locked case, and her father's death would become a signature on someone else's fortune.

She rose quietly, took up her lamp, and began, methodically, to prepare the engine room for whatever tomorrow brought — and as she worked, she heard, far off through the granite, faint and impossible and unmistakable, a sound that did not belong to any engine.

A bell. Ringing upstream, in the dead of night, from the direction of the wreck.

Someone had reached the *Provision.*
