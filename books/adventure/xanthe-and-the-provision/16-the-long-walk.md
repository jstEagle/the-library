## 16. The Long Walk

Ferro had always believed the river was a living thing with a temper and a memory, and he had spent thirty years feeding it small offerings to keep that temper sweet — a splash of coffee at every confluence, a pinch of salt over the rail on Fridays, a word in the old tongue his grandmother used when the fog came down thick enough to drown a man's own name. The crew laughed at him for it. Bram called it kindling-superstition. Odile called it ballast for the soul. Xanthe never said anything at all, but she never once told him to stop.

Now the river was gone from under them, taken away like a floor pulled out of a house, and Ferro discovered that everything he knew about keeping alive had been river-knowledge, boat-knowledge, knowledge of water and wood and fire in a stove. On the gravel bar where Vane had marooned them, with the Provision II shrinking upstream under her own steam and Odile standing rigid at the rail as a hostage, none of his offerings meant anything. There was no water here to give them to. Only stone, and wind, and the long gray light of a country that did not care whether men lived in it or not.

He knelt anyway. He took a pinch of salt from the little tin he kept in his coat pocket, the one he'd carried since Gray Landing, and he cast it onto the gravel, and he said the old words, and if they fell on deaf ears then at least they fell somewhere.

"Ferro." Bram's voice behind him, rough as a rasp on a fouled propeller. "You praying or you eating? Because if you're eating, I want some."

"Praying," Ferro said, without turning around. "And you should be too. Look at your knees."

Bram looked at his knees. They were the size of winter melons inside his torn trousers, both of them, swollen from the walk already begun and the walk still owed. He had tried to laugh about it an hour ago when they first stood up from the gravel and started along the bar, and the laugh had come out wrong, and nobody had picked it up, and now it lay between all of them like something dead.

"We can't carry you," Ferro said. It was cruel and he knew it was cruel and he said it anyway, because someone had to say the true things on this mountain or they would all die polite.

"I know what we can't do," Bram said. "I've been doing arithmetic since the boat went. It keeps coming out bad."

* * *

They were four: Xanthe, Bram, Ferro, and the boy Rennick, the deckhand who had signed on at the last port and who had not yet learned that silence could be a kindness. Rennick talked because talking kept the fear from pooling. Ferro understood this. Fear pooled in a man like bilgewater, and if you didn't pump it out it found the low places and sat there rotting. So he let the boy talk, and answered him in monosyllables, and saved his own breath for walking.

The plan — if it deserved the word — was Xanthe's. The river bent east above the gorge; they had watched it go from the bar, a silver thread stitching through a valley of scree and dead pine. Where the river went, the glacier's meltwater went, and where the meltwater went, Vane would have to take Odile and the drill rig eventually, because no rig flew and no engine ran without fuel and there was only one way down off this roof of the world. Follow the sound of the water, Xanthe had said. Stay high enough to see, low enough to hear. And don't stop moving after dark, because stopping is how mountains kill you.

Ferro had heard better plans. He had also heard worse ones end with everyone drowned.

They climbed away from the bar as the light went copper, up a slope of loose stone that shifted and sighed under their boots like something breathing in its sleep. Ferro hated scree the way other men hated snakes. It gave nothing back. Every step was a small betrayal, the ground agreeing to hold you and then reconsidering. He climbed by rhythm, the way he kneaded bread, one-two-three-rest, one-two-three-rest, and he put his grandmother's words under his breath like a drumbeat to keep the rhythm honest.

By full dark they had gained the shoulder of the ridge, and below them, faint as a whisper through a wall, they heard it: the river. Still there. Still going where it had always gone.

"There," Rennick said, pointing with his whole arm, as though anyone could miss a sound.

"Hear it, yes," Ferro said. "See it, no. We camp."

Xanthe crouched at the edge of the ridge, and the starlight caught the line of her jaw, set hard as a cleat. "We should push—"

"No," Ferro said.

It came out of him before he thought, flat and certain, the voice he used when a pot was boiling over and everyone else was too slow. Xanthe turned and looked at him, and he felt the weight of being looked at by a captain, which was like being looked at by weather.

"The mountain makes its own night-water," he said. "Snow melts in the day sun, comes down these gullies after dark. You cross a dry wash at midnight, you're gambling with a flood you can't hear coming until it's on you. My grandmother's people herded goats above the snowline. I know this much if I know nothing." He held up the salt tin. "Also, nobody eats hot food tonight, so nobody's hands shake tomorrow. Cold bread, cold fat, sleep in a row with the smallest in the middle. That's how you live at altitude. Not by pushing. By not dying stupidly."

Silence. Then Bram, from the ground where he'd collapsed like a dropped mast, began to laugh — a real laugh this time, wheezing and painful and true. "Listen to him. Thirty years of burning the rice and now he's Shackleton."

"I never burned the rice," Ferro said with dignity. "That was once, and it was your fault for distracting me."

* * *

They slept in a row as instructed, wedged into a hollow behind a fin of rock, Rennick in the middle because he was youngest and thinnest and would lose heat fastest. Ferro lay awake longest, listening. This was the other thing his grandmother had taught him, the thing he had never told the crew because it sounded like madness: the mountain talks before it does anything. Wind changes its mind in a certain order. Stone ticks as frost gets into it. Snow on the high faces makes a sound like silk tearing just before it lets go.

He listened, and the mountain talked, and what it said was: *cold tonight, colder tomorrow, and something wet three days out.*

He slept.

* * *

Morning came up white and merciless. Bram's knees had stiffened overnight into two hinges of rusted iron, and the first hour of walking was agony performed in slow motion, Bram leaning on Ferro's shoulder, swearing steadily and creatively, Ferro counting steps for him the way you count for a man hauling a rope — not because the counting helps but because a mind occupied with numbers has no room left for despair.

"One hundred. Rest. One hundred. Rest."

"You're enjoying this," Bram gasped.

"I am enjoying nothing," Ferro said. "But I will enjoy telling this story for the rest of my life, so walk faster and make it a good story."

They followed the sound of the river all that day, dropping toward it and climbing away again as the terrain demanded, and by afternoon they saw it for the first time since the bar: a green ribbon far below, violent and white in its gorge, going exactly where Xanthe had said it would go. Seeing it did something to all of them. Rennick whooped. Xanthe stood very still with her fists closed, and Ferro, watching her, understood that she was not looking at the river but downriver, toward wherever Vane and Odile were, and that love at altitude looks exactly like hatred except quieter.

In the late afternoon the sky changed.

Ferro felt it before he saw it — a pressure shift, a taste in the air like iron filings — and he stopped mid-stride and made the crew stop, and they stood on a bench of tundra while he read the west.

Clouds building over the high peaks, but wrong, stacked too tall, boiling upward instead of drifting. The wind swinging from the north in gusts that smelled of snow even though the sun was still out. His grandmother had a word for this sky. She had said it meant *the mountain is going to argue*, and that you wanted to be nowhere exposed, nowhere near water, nowhere under a face of hanging ice when the argument started.

"Storm," he said. "Big one. Tonight or tomorrow morning. We need walls and we need them now."

"Walls," Bram repeated. "Out here."

"Stone walls. There—" Ferro pointed to a jumble of boulders a quarter mile on, tumbled against the hillside below an overhang, the leavings of some ancient collapse. "There. Old rockfall. Hollows between the stones. If we get inside before it hits, we live. If we're caught in the open, the wind alone will take the heat out of us faster than any fire can put it back."

They reached the boulders as the sun went down behind the boiling clouds, and Ferro directed them — directed *them*, the captain included, and no one objected, which was its own quiet miracle — to stuff the gaps between stones with tundra sod and their spare clothing and packs. They built a fire in the deepest hollow, small and shielded, fed with driftwood-bleached pine roots Ferro dug from the frozen ground with a broken oar-blade he had carried off the bar like a relic.

The storm arrived at moonrise.

It did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a siege. The wind came down off the peaks in a single sustained scream, carrying ice grains that flayed any skin left uncovered, and the temperature fell so fast that the fire seemed to shrink in self-defense. They huddled in the hollow, four bodies pressed together, and Ferro fed the little fire and told stories.

Not adventure stories. Bread stories. Stories of dough rising in warm kitchens, of fish stew on the Provision's deck in the rain, of the year the river froze early and they'd played cards for a week in the mess while Ferro cooked everything twice to keep the stove fed. Small warm stories. He talked until his voice was a rasp, and he noticed that Xanthe had stopped shivering, and that Rennick was asleep against Bram's shoulder, and that Bram was looking at him across the fire with an expression Ferro had never seen on that scarred old face before — something open, something almost young.

"You've been holding us together since the bar," Bram said. "I owe you an apology for thirty years of 'kindling-superstition.'"

"You owe me a month of washing dishes," Ferro said. "Apology accepted anyway."

Outside, the storm argued with the mountain all night, and the mountain, being older, won.

* * *

The storm broke at dawn into a world remade in white. Everything soft-edged, silent, blinding. And Ferro, crawling out of the hollow to look, saw what the storm had written on the land, and his heart went cold in a way the wind couldn't account for.

Avalanche slides. Three of them, on the slopes across the valley, long pale scars where the storm-shaken snow had let go. One of them had crossed the very bench of tundra they had walked the day before. Had the storm come six hours earlier, they would have been under ten feet of it, and no one would ever have known where.

He said nothing about it. Some knowledge is a burden best carried alone. But he added a new rule to the walking: no travel under any slope steep enough to hold snow, no matter how much shorter it made the way. And when Xanthe, studying the route ahead, proposed cutting directly beneath a great white shoulder to save half a day, Ferro said "No" again, in that same pot-boiling voice, and explained about the sun warming the snowpack, and how avalanches come loose in the warm hours of the day like a man rolling over in bed, and Xanthe looked at him for a long moment and then simply changed the route on the map without a word of argument.

After that, something shifted aboard the expedition-that-was-not-a-boat. It was subtle, the way current shifts are subtle — you feel it in the helm before you see it in the water. When Ferro spoke now, people listened the first time. When he stopped, they stopped. Rennick began walking beside him, asking questions about weather signs, and Ferro found himself teaching — cloud shapes, wind smells, the meaning of a ring around the moon, the way birds fly low before a blow. The boy drank it in like a man dying of thirst. And Ferro, who had spent thirty years as the comic figure in the galley, the salt-caster, the burner of offerings, felt something unfold in his chest that he had no name for and did not try to find one.

His grandmother, he thought, would have laughed herself sick. All those rituals she'd taught him to keep the river kind, and in the end the useful inheritance was this: pay attention. Watch the sky. Respect what is bigger than you. The rest was decoration.

* * *

On the third day they came down within sight of the river itself, close enough to smell it — that cold mineral breath of glacial water — and close enough to see, far ahead, where the valley narrowed and the water flattened into a pale lake backed by a wall of blue-white ice that rose out of the earth like the rampart of a fallen god.

The glacier. The source. The place all of this had been about from the beginning.

And on the shore of the lake, tiny with distance, a smudge of canvas and metal: a camp. A drill rig's derrick, thin as a needle against the ice. Figures moving. Smoke rising straight in the windless cold.

And beside the camp, moored at a crude landing, a boat. Their boat. The Provision II, her lines unmistakable even at this distance, even to eyes as old as Ferro's.

"She got her here," Bram said quietly. "Odile got the engine to run for him."

"She got herself here," Xanthe said, and her voice was terrible and level, "because the alternative was worse. Now we get her back."

Ferro looked at the sky. The storm had passed but the pattern hadn't broken — the high clouds were still stacking wrong over the peaks behind the glacier, and the air had that iron-filing taste again, stronger than before. Below the great ice wall, the meltwater lake lay flat and pale, and along its shoreline ran ridges of old stranded ice, marks of higher water, marks of what the lake did when—

When the calving started.

He remembered the elders at the cliff village, the old woman with her voice like dry leaves: *the river keeps what it takes.* And he remembered something else she'd said, something he'd half-heard, that the ice above the source groaned for days before it gave, that the mountain sang a warning to anyone patient enough to listen.

He listened now. Wind, distance, the river's murmur. And underneath it all, faint, intermittent, from the direction of the ice wall — a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"Ferro?" Xanthe said. "What is it?"

He took out his salt tin. His hands were steady.

"That ice is going to come down," he said. "Soon. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe sooner. And everything on that shore is going to be underwater." He pocketed the tin and started down the slope. "So we'd best hurry. I want to be standing next to Odile when it does — and I want Cassius Vane to have a front-row seat."
