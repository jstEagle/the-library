## 17. Calving Season Opens

The cold came up through the gravel like a living thing.

Odile felt it first in her boots, then her knees, then the small of her back where she'd been kneeling on the stony shore for what felt like an hour, coaxing the drill rig's fuel lines to life with fingers that no longer wanted to be fingers. The lake lay before her — a mile of meltwater the color of skimmed milk, floes drifting on it like broken plates — and beyond the lake rose the glacier itself, a wall of blue-white ice two hundred feet high and older than any nation that would want to own it.

It was beautiful the way a loaded gun is beautiful.

"Is it ready?" Vane stood behind her, close enough that she could hear his breathing, which was too quick for a man who wanted to seem in control. His two hired men had names she refused to learn — the tall one and the one with the scarred lip — and they had spread themselves along the shore with rifles held with the casual wrongness of men who had carried them more often than they had fired them.

"The engine's ready," Odile said. "The ice isn't."

Vane laughed. It was not a kind sound; it had never been a kind sound, but out here, under the glacier, it came back off the ice in pieces, as if the mountain were repeating it mockingly. "The ice is exactly what it's supposed to be. Frozen. That's rather the point."

"You don't understand what you're listening to." She sat back on her heels and made herself look at him. Her hands hurt. She let them hurt. "That groaning you hear every few minutes? That's not wind. That's the glacier moving. Calving season opened early this year — Ferro could have told you that, if you hadn't marooned him on a gravel bar. The whole face is unstable. Every ton of ice above us is waiting for an excuse."

"Then we should give it no excuses," Vane said pleasantly, "and you should start the drill."

She turned back to the rig. It was a good machine — government money bought good machines — a steam-and-diesel hybrid with a core barrel meant to bite forty feet into bedrock and pull up stone that would tell men in distant offices everything they needed to know about what slept beneath the glacier: the rare-earth seams, the wealth worth a nation's ransom, all of it locked under ten thousand years of ice that had never signed anything away to anyone.

Her father had been a machinist. He used to say you could tell a machine's soul by its idle. This one idled like a liar — smooth on the surface, something grinding underneath.

She thought about sabotage. She had thought about nothing else since they'd come ashore, since Vane had put the pistol against Bram's chest on the gravel bar and said *you'll come along, Miss Marchetti, or I'll make an example*, since the long walk over scree with her wrists bound in front of her like some medieval penitent. She could crack a fuel line. She could foul the injector. She could do it so cleanly that Vane would spend a day blaming the altitude, the cold, the quality of his fuel drums.

And then he would blame her, and there was no Bram here to stand between her and his temper, and Xanthe —

*Don't think about Xanthe.* Thinking about Xanthe was a wound she kept touching. Somewhere downriver, on a gravel bar with a crippled boat and no engineer, Xanthe Sandoval was doing what Xanthe always did, which was refuse to accept the shape of the world as given. She would come. Odile knew it the way she knew the load limits of steel: not hope, arithmetic. Xanthe would come because Xanthe had spent ten years learning this river and three days learning that her father had died protecting it, and there was no force on the Meridian — not debt, not oath, not death itself — that would keep her from finishing what he started.

So Odile's job was simpler and harder: stay alive, stay useful, and make sure that when the moment came, the drill did not work.

She primed the lines. She checked the water feed. She did everything correctly, because a saboteur who gets caught is worse than useless, and because Vane was watching her hands the way a card sharp watches a dealer.

"Slowly," he said. "We have time."

"We don't," she said, and nodded at the lake. "Look at the waterline on those floes. Look at the color of the melt coming off the face. That's not spring melt, Captain Vane. That's the glacier sweating. When a glacier sweats like that, it's getting ready to shed weight. And when it sheds—" she pointed, without standing, at a serac near the summit of the ice wall, a leaning tower of ice the size of a church, "—that comes down first. It's been leaning since yesterday. Ice doesn't lean forever."

Vane looked at the serac. For one unguarded second, something moved behind his eyes — not fear, she realized, but calculation, the same look he gave a river he intended to run. Then it closed again, and he was only a man in a good coat at the roof of the world.

"Start the drill, Miss Marchetti."

* * *

The rig caught on the third try.

The noise was obscene. Out here, where the loudest natural sound was the glacier's slow internal thunder and the occasional gunshot crack of expanding ice, the diesel hammering to life was a violation. Steam hissed from the boiler joints and feathered white against the blue air. The core barrel began to turn, and Odile fed it down toward the rock shelf at the lake's edge where Vane had decided — with a surveyor's instinct for the cheapest possible crime — the drilling site should be.

The tall hired man anchored the guy-wires. The scarred-lip one stood with his rifle and tried to smoke a cigarette in the cold and failed, cursing softly. Vane stood at the water's edge with his chart case open on his knees, checking coordinates against a brass theodolite that had probably belonged to better expeditions than this one.

Odile ran the machine and listened past it.

You learned that in the engine room of the *Provision II*: listen past the machine. Any fool can hear an engine. An engineer hears what the engine is sitting inside of — the hull flex, the water slap, the bearing that will fail on Thursday. So while her hands kept the drill steady and her face kept the blankness Vane expected from hired labor, her ears were full of the glacier.

It was talking constantly now. Not the deep, patient groans of the day before — those had been the sound of a sleeping giant turning over. These were sharper. Cracks like rifle shots, high up on the face, followed by long silences that were somehow worse. Meltwater ran everywhere, threading silver down the blue ice, pooling at the base, feeding the lake, and the lake itself had risen — she could see it, the old strandline of gray stones eight inches above the current waterline, still damp. Eight inches of new water in a day. Eight inches of a mountain deciding to become a river.

Every piece of ice that fell into that lake displaced its volume. Every displacement sent a wave. And the wave, she thought, watching the drill bit chew into bedrock, the wave travels back to the ice foot and undermines it, and undermines it, and—

The first serac fell while she was thinking it.

There was no mistaking it for anything else. A sound like the sky tearing, then a section of the upper ice wall — smaller than the leaning tower, but big enough, a house-sized block of blue — detached and dropped, and the drop lasted long enough for Odile's stomach to rise with it, and then it hit the lake and the lake *answered*. A wave ran out from the base of the glacier in a widening ring, lifted the drift floes, slapped the shore six feet below their feet, and sucked back with a grinding hiss of gravel.

Steam and spray hung in the air. The echo went around the valley twice.

Neither of Vane's men said anything. The scarred-lip one had stopped trying to smoke.

"That," Odile said, into the ringing quiet, "was a small one."

Vane had not moved. Snow dust had settled on the shoulders of his coat, and he brushed it off with two fingers, deliberately, like a man refusing to flinch in front of subordinates. "Distance to core depth?"

"Forty minutes at this rate. If the rock cooperates." She paused. "If the ice does."

"It will cooperate," Vane said, "because it has no choice, and neither do you. Continue."

* * *

They drilled through the afternoon, and the glacier drilled back.

It became, in Odile's exhausted reckoning, a conversation conducted in collapsing tons. Every twenty minutes, another piece of the face let go — sometimes small, a boulder of ice that shattered on the shore rocks like dropped glass; sometimes large enough that the wave reached their boots and the tall man swore and scrambled back up the beach with his guy-wires dragging. Between falls, the cracks rang out overhead like a ship's timbers working in a storm, and once — a sound Odile would carry for the rest of her life — a long, low, musical note, almost sweet, as a fissure somewhere in the two-hundred-foot wall opened from top to bottom and the whole glacier seemed to sigh.

The meltwater channels widened as she watched. What had been threads in the morning were ropes by mid-afternoon, pouring off the face in white braids, and the lake climbed another handspan up the stones. Floes that had drifted lazily now moved with purpose, riding a slow circulation that hadn't existed at dawn. The whole basin was waking up, and everything in it — water, ice, rock, and the four small stubborn humans on the shore — was being pulled into a rhythm older and larger than any of them.

She thought about engines again. An engine at redline tells you it's going to fail; the question is only whether you shut it down or ride it until it throws a rod. This valley was at redline. Anyone honest would shut it down.

Vane was not anyone honest.

"Core's coming up," the tall man called, and Odile eased the winch, and the barrel rose dripping, and inside it — packed in the cutting teeth like meat in a grinder — was stone. Gray-green stone veined with something darker, something that even she, who knew nothing of minerals, could see was not ordinary rock. It glittered faintly in the flat polar light, as if the mountain had been hiding coins in its fist for ten thousand years.

Vane knelt over it. He actually knelt — this man who had never knelt to anything in his life — and pried a fragment loose and held it up to the light, and for a moment his face did something terrible and simple: it shone. Pure, unguarded greed, the kind children have, preserved intact in a man of fifty-odd years like a fly in amber.

"There it is," he whispered. "Do you know what this is worth?"

"I know what it cost," Odile said.

He didn't even look at her. "Another core. Deeper. We need depth samples for the assay — the backers will want—"

The glacier interrupted him.

This time it was the leaning tower. Odile saw it go — saw the whole serac, the church-sized spire she'd warned him about, tilt with a strange unhurried grace, as though it had all the time in the world, as though gravity were a formality it had finally gotten around to attending. The crack that preceded it ran the length of the ice wall, left to right, horizon to horizon, a single continuous report like the world's largest door slamming.

"*Down!*" she screamed, and dove behind the rig's boiler, and heard the tall man do the same, and heard — this was the part she would remember — Vane *not* move, because he was holding his sample fragment up to the light and the light was all he could see.

The serac hit the lake.

The wave it threw was not a wave; it was a decision. Forty feet of water stood up and walked across the lake, and when it reached the shallows it did not slow down. It took the drilling site's lower platform and folded it like paper. It took the fuel drums — Odile saw one cartwheeling end over end, gushing diesel in a black ribbon — and hurled them up the beach. It took the theodolite stand, the spare line, the tent half, and it went for *them*, and Odile pressed her face against the boiler plate and the water hit her like a train of cold hands, tore at her, tried to peel her off the world, and receded.

When she got her head up, coughing, soaked to the skin, the lake was littered with new ice. The drill rig stood ankle-deep in lakewater, its firebox hissing where the wave had found the seams. Half their fuel was gone, floating in rainbow skins on the milk-colored water.

And Cassius Vane was on his knees in the wash zone, soaked, hatless, one hand still clenched around his fragment of glittering ore, staring at the hole in the world where the serac had been.

"The next one," Odile said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "will be bigger. That's how calving works, Captain. It's not random. It's a conversation, and the glacier is telling you it wants you gone. You can still take your sample and walk out of here alive. Some men would call that a victory."

Vane rose slowly. Water poured off him. He looked at the ore in his fist, and then at the glacier, and Odile watched — actually watched — the calculation complete itself behind his eyes, and understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the water that he was going to choose the ore. That he had always been going to choose the ore. That a man who burns other men's charts and murders other men's crews does not stop at the water's edge.

"Restart the drill," he said. "We drill tonight. We drill until we have what we came for."

"The firebox is flooded. The fuel's in the lake. Even if I get it running, you're drilling on a shoreline that's being demolished piece by piece, and the next wave won't leave the rig standing, let alone us."

"Then you had better be fast," Vane said, "hadn't you?"

He walked away up the beach to confer with the tall man, and Odile knelt in the wreckage of the drilling site with her heart slamming and the glacier groaning overhead, and did the only thing she knew how to do with a broken machine: she opened it up and looked for what could be saved.

The firebox could be dried. The lines could be cleared. With the remaining fuel she could give him perhaps two hours of drilling — enough, if the rock cooperated, for one more core, maybe two. Enough for Vane to hold proof in his hands.

Or enough, if her hands slipped in just the right way, for nothing at all.

She looked at the flooded firebox, and at the fuel drum that remained, and at the glacier shedding its centuries into the rising lake, and she began to work — slowly, methodically, with the patience of a woman who had spent her life among machines that punished haste — and as she worked, she listened past everything, past the rig and the wind and the armed men, to the far-off southern shore of the lake.

Where, very faintly, threaded under the thunder of the ice, she heard something that was not ice at all.

A boat whistle. Three short blasts — the old signal, her signal, the one she and Xanthe had invented on night watches years ago, *I'm here, I'm coming, hold on.*

The glacier roared. The lake climbed. And Odile Marchetti, kneeling in the wreck of Cassius Vane's ambition, began — very quietly, so that no one would see — to smile.
