## 18. The Source

The last mile of the climb, the river stopped sounding like a river.

Xanthe noticed it first as an absence — a hush where there should have been roar. For eleven days she had lived inside the water's voice, the Meridian shouting through gorges and muttering over gravel bars, a voice so constant it had become her own pulse. Now, as she came up out of the scree with her palms bleeding and her boots full of glacier grit, that voice thinned to a whisper, then to nothing at all.

She crested the shoulder of stone and forgot how to breathe.

The source pool lay below her in a bowl of granite like a coin dropped by some careless god. It was blue — not river-blue, not sky-blue, but a blue that seemed lit from underneath, from somewhere deeper than depth, as though the ice above had spent ten thousand years distilling its color into this one still place. The surface did not ripple. It did not move. It lay beneath the towering face of the glacier like a held breath, and in it she could see the reflection of the seracs, those leaning towers of ancient ice, so perfectly rendered that for a moment she could not tell which was real and which was memory.

*Father stood here.*

The thought arrived without permission and went through her like a spear. Ten years of imagining, of nightmares, of the empty grave at Gray Landing with its river-stone marker — and here was the ground his boots had pressed. Here was the water he had seen last. She put her hand against the warm rock and felt, absurdly, that she might find the warmth of him still in it.

Then she heard the drill.

It came as a vibration before a sound, a tremor in the stone under her palm, and then the noise itself — a grinding, industrial shriek that had no business in a place like this, a sound made by men who believed the world owed them something. Down at the near shore of the pool, on a shelf of gravel that had probably not known a human footprint since the last age, Vane's rig squatted like a wounded insect. Canvas shelters. Coiled cable. A derrick of drilled steel angled down toward the waterline, and at its base, the bit already biting into living rock, throwing up a fan of gray slurry that bled into the blue like ink into a wound.

And beside the rig, small in the vastness, Odile.

Xanthe's heart did something complicated and painful. Her engineer sat on a supply crate with her wrists bound in front of her with electrical wire, her hair loose and wild around a face smudged with grease and exhaustion — but her eyes were open, and they were moving, cataloguing, *working*, even now. Two men Xanthe didn't know stood guard with rifles slung casual as fishing poles. Vane himself stood at the edge of the drilling site with his back to the pool, watching the bit go down, and even at this distance Xanthe could read the hunger in the set of his shoulders. He looked like a man at a graveside who had finally found the coffin he'd been digging for.

She sank back behind the ridge before the wind could carry her scent or silhouette to them. Her legs were shaking — not from fear, she realized, but from the sheer altitude of everything, the thin air, the impossible blue, the fact that she was standing on the roof of the river her father had died beneath.

*He died protecting this,* the old woman at the cliff village had said. *Not seeking it.*

Xanthe looked at the drill, and at the slurry staining the pool, and understood for the first time, completely, what that sentence meant. It wasn't a metaphor. It wasn't grief talking. A man had stood on this shore with charts in his hands — charts that would have sold this water, this color, this silence, to men who measured everything in tons and claims — and he had chosen to sink them. Chosen the river over the ledger. And they had killed him for it, and left him in the cold shallows downstream with his crew, and called it the river's work.

Her hand went to the wreckage-iron at her belt — a length of the *Provision*'s hull plating, torn free from the wreck they'd found in the gorge, hammered at one end into something between a club and a promise. She'd carried it every step of the climb. She carried it now the way you carry a name.

Below, Vane turned toward the pool, and shouted something to his men, and laughed.

Xanthe began to climb down.

* * *

She chose her line the way she'd choose a rapids run: fast water on the left, eddy on the right, the rock fall in the middle reading like a chart if you let your eyes do the work they'd been trained for. Twenty years on the Meridian taught her more than steering. It taught her that every dangerous thing has a shape, and the shape can be learned, and the learning is the only inheritance worth anything.

She came down through the boulder field without a sound, keeping the ridge between herself and the camp until the last possible moment, and when she finally stepped out onto the gravel shelf, she stepped into the open deliberately — no cover, no shadow, hands visible, the iron hanging loose at her side.

"Vane."

Her voice crossed the stillness of the pool and came back off the glacier, doubled, so that it sounded like two women speaking, or one woman speaking for everyone who had ever been silenced here.

Vane spun. For half a second — she would treasure this half-second for the rest of her life — Cassius Vane's face was naked. Not the polished dockside smile, not the boardroom gravity, none of the lacquer. Just a man caught, alone, a hundred miles past help, by the one person he had built his whole expedition around never having to face. Then the lacquer came back down like a visor, and he smiled, and it was almost convincing.

"Sandoval." He glanced past her, up the scree, counting heads she hadn't brought. "You walked. In God's name, you actually walked. I told them you would." He said it to his guards rather than to her, and there was something in it — vindication, almost admiration — that made her want to break his teeth. "Where's the rest of your gallant crew?"

"Behind me." She let the lie sit there, fat and patient. "Bram. Ferro. All of them. They're slower because Ferro insisted on bringing the ship's bell."

That landed. She watched it land — watched the flicker in his eyes, because Vane knew exactly whose bell that was, and what it meant that it was here. His smile stayed fixed but his weight shifted, just slightly, off his back foot.

"You should have stayed with your boat," he said. "This is survey work. Delicate instruments. Men of science."

"Men with rifles, you mean." She started walking along the shoreline, unhurried, angling toward Odile. One of the guards tracked her with his muzzle; the other looked at Vane, waiting for an order that didn't come. "Let her go, Cassius."

"She's my insurance."

"She's my partner." The word came out of her with more force than she'd planned, and she let it stand there in the thin cold air, unhidden, undefended. Ten years she'd kept that word folded away like a letter never sent. Not today. Not on this shore. "And she's the only person alive who can keep that rig running long enough for you to get what you want. Which means she's worth nothing to you dead and everything to you fed and freed. So here's my offer."

"Your offer." Vane laughed, short and sharp. "You're one woman with a piece of scrap metal, Sandoval, on a gravel bar at the top of the world, and I have—"

"Witnesses."

The word stopped him the way a snag stops a boat.

"That's what you're afraid of," Xanthe said, and took another step, and another, close enough now to see the sweat freezing at his temples despite the cold, close enough to see the drill slurry drying gray on his polished boots — his polished boots, up here, a hundred miles past the last road, because Cassius Vane would dress for ownership even at the end of the earth. "Not failure. You've failed before; failure is just money. You're afraid of being *seen*. Of what happens when the commission sits in its warm room in Meridian City and hears, from sworn mouths, that Cassius Vane hired a riverboat under false papers, sabotaged her rudder, lost a man overboard in a squall nobody believes was an accident, and murdered a crew of nine honest sailors ten years ago because they wouldn't sell him a river."

"I don't know what you're talking about." But his voice had gone flat, and flat was worse than angry.

"The letters," Xanthe said. "My father's letters. You claimed you had them — proof he'd found something worth owning. You burned the charts because you couldn't read them, couldn't crack his shorthand, and you needed Pell's codes to make sense of what was left. But we found the rest, Cassius. We found the *Provision*. We found her crew. We found the letter he wrote with his hands shaking, naming your backer, describing the man who offered him gold for the source and put a knife in him when he said no." She was close enough now to see Odile's eyes on her, steady and fierce and alive, and it steadied her own voice like ballast. "You've spent ten years and someone else's fortune climbing toward this pool, and the whole time, the thing you were really chasing was the evidence. Because you knew it existed. Because you helped make it."

The nearest guard shifted. "Sir?"

"Stand fast," Vane said, without looking at him. He was looking at Xanthe the way a drowning man looks at a rope he doesn't trust. "You have nothing. Letters in a dead man's shorthand. Skeletons in a lake. Hearsay from cliff-dwellers who worship weather."

"Testimony," Xanthe corrected. "Sworn testimony, from a licensed master, her pilot's mate, her cook, and her chief engineer — who is sitting right there, Cassius, listening to every word, and who you bound with wire instead of silencing, because you needed her hands. You've been making witnesses since Gray Landing. You just didn't know that's what you were doing."

And that was when the scree sang.

* * *

It was a small sound, at first — a click of stone against stone high on the ridge, the kind of sound a mountain makes all day without meaning anything. But Vane's guards heard it too, and their heads came up, and Xanthe saw Bram's silhouette crest the ridgeline like a bad omen, big and slow and deliberate, Ferro beside him with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a look on his face that Xanthe had never seen there before: not fear. Certainty. Behind them, limping, grim, came the rest — what was left of them, four figures against the sky, each carrying a piece of the *Provision*: hull iron, a stanchion, the broken spoke of her wheel. Wreckage-iron and testimony. Exactly as promised.

They came down the slope in a loose skirmish line, unhurried, and Xanthe understood why: there was nowhere for anyone to run, and everyone on this shore knew it. The pool on one side, blue and blind and patient. The glacier on the other, groaning now — she heard it clearly for the first time, a deep tectonic complaint, like a giant turning over in sleep. Calving season, opening early. The old woman's warning rang in her skull: *the river keeps what it takes.*

"Hold!" Vane's voice cracked upward. "Hold, or I'll—" He faltered, because there was nothing to hold or shoot that changed the arithmetic. Six of them. Two rifles. And a mountain coming apart overhead.

Bram reached the flat and planted himself like a piling, breathing hard, his ruined knees braced wide. He didn't raise the iron bar in his fists. He just stood, which from Bram was louder than any threat. Ferro drifted right, muttering — and Xanthe caught the words, old words, river-words, the blessing cook-superstition that Ferro had mumbled over every departure for a decade, *keep what's yours, take what's given, let the rest go by* — and she saw that he was saying it not to himself but to the glacier, politely, the way you'd address a landlord.

Odile got to her feet. The wire bit into her wrists and she ignored it, and her eyes found Xanthe's across twenty feet of gravel, and neither of them smiled, because there was nothing funny anywhere in the world, and yet something passed between them anyway that was better than smiling. *You came.* *Of course I came.* *You walked.* *You waited.* Everything important, said in silence.

"Cut her loose," Xanthe said quietly. "Ferro."

Ferro produced a knife — of course Ferro had a knife, Ferro had seventeen knives — and crossed to Odile, and the guards watched him do it because Vane had not told them to stop, and Vane did not tell them to stop because Vane was doing arithmetic of his own and losing.

"This changes nothing," Vane said. His voice had found its footing again, or thought it had. "The claim filings are already lodged. The deposits are documented. Even if you crawl back down this mountain with your fairy tales, the consortium's lawyers will bury you in paper until spring."

"Then it's fortunate," Xanthe said, "that we're not going to crawl back down and argue with lawyers."

Vane frowned. "What does that mean?"

"It means," said Odile, flexing her freed hands, her voice raw from cold and disuse and absolutely level, "that you should look at the lake."

Everyone looked at the lake.

The blue surface, still as glass all morning, had begun to shiver. Not from wind — there was no wind. From below. From the base of the glacier, where the meltwater ran dark and deep, something was shifting, settling, deciding. Along the ice face, a crack opened with a sound like a rifle shot, then another, stitching down the serac wall in bright white lightning-lines. A fine mist of ancient air breathed out of the fracture and hung over the pool.

"Calving season," Ferro said, and his voice was gentle, almost apologetic, the way he announced a death at sea. "Early. I told them on the climb. The mountain reads warm. Ice keeps its own schedule, senhor. It does not file paperwork."

Another crack, longer, deeper. A chunk of ice the size of a rowboat shrugged loose from the face far above and fell three hundred feet into the pool, and the splash threw spray forty feet in the air, and the wave it sent across the still blue surface lapped at the gravel shelf and soaked everyone's boots, and the drill rig swayed on its cribbing like a drunk.

Vane's men broke first. The younger one simply dropped his rifle and ran for the scree, and the older one looked at Vane, and looked at the ice, and followed. Vane shouted after them — names, threats, offers — and his voice bounced off the glacier and came back thinner every time, and then he stopped shouting, because the glacier was making a sound now that made all human sounds ridiculous: a long, rising groan, the sound of ten thousand years letting go.

"Rig comes down," Odile said. She was already moving, not away from the rig but toward it, because she was Odile, and her hands needed to be doing something even at the end of the world. "I can strip the ignition and the bit assembly. Two minutes. After that it's a monument."

"You'll touch nothing," Vane snarled, and grabbed for her arm —

— and Xanthe's wreckage-iron came to rest against his chest, gently, the way you'd stop a stranger boarding your boat without invitation.

"No," she said.

For a moment they stood like that, eye to eye, the iron between them, the mountain screaming overhead, and Xanthe searched his face for the rival from the dock, the grudge, the polished boots, and found instead something smaller and sadder: a man discovering, at the roof of the river, that he had climbed all this way to a place that could not be owned, holding papers that were already blowing loose in the wind off the ice.

"The samples," he said suddenly, wildly, turning toward the canvas shelter where the core barrels sat racked in their padded cases. "The cores are the claim. Physical evidence, in my possession, legally extracted—"

The glacier answered him.

It was not an explosion. It was a decision. A section of the serac wall the size of a chapel leaned forward, ponderously, almost courteously, and let go. The sound arrived a heartbeat after the sight of it, a boom that Xanthe felt in her teeth and her ribs and the soles of her feet, and the wave that came off the impact was not a wave anymore but a wall, gray-white and roaring, sweeping across the pool and up the gravel shelf.

"RUN!" Bram's roar, and then everyone was running, all of them, up the scree, Vane stumbling among them with a core barrel clutched to his chest like a child, the drill rig vanishing behind them in white chaos, the canvas shelter lifting and tearing, the samples — the cores, the claim, the whole legal architecture of ownership — going into the pool that had kept her father's secrets for ten years and would keep these too.

Xanthe reached the high rocks and turned, gasping, in time to see the water settle. The wave passed. The pool shivered, shuddered, and began, incredibly, to still itself — the blue returning, the mirror rebuilding itself, as though the glacier had merely cleared its throat. Where the rig had been there was nothing. Where the shelter had been there was nothing. Somewhere down in that blinding blue, wrapped in cold that would outlast every nation on earth, lay the last physical proof that the source had ever been threatened at all.

Only the testimony remained. Only the people.

She counted them the way you count crew after a capsize. Bram, on his knees, laughing silently at the sky. Ferro crossing himself with a river-stone. Odile, soaked to the waist, looking at Xanthe with an expression that contained, among other things, a promise about later. Four others. Safe. All safe.

All but one.

"Cassius." Xanthe's stomach dropped. She scanned the shoreline, the scree, the water. "Cassius!"

There. At the water's edge, downstream of the surge, on his knees in the shallows with the floodwater dragging at him — he'd gone in after a core barrel, or been knocked in by the wave, it hardly mattered. What mattered was the current. Even here, even at the source, the water moved once disturbed, and it had him by the boots, and it was pulling him toward the outflow channel where the Meridian proper began — the young river, newborn and already strong, already hungry, already keeping what it took.

His hands slapped at the water. His voice had stopped working. One arm came up, and the fingers spread, and closed, and opened again.

*Xanthe.* Ten years of hatred said *let go.* Ten years of the empty grave said *let go.* The river itself, her oldest teacher, murmured the lesson it had always taught: *the river forgives nothing, forgets less, and owes you nothing either.*

But her father's letter had said something else. She could hear it, read aloud by lamplight in the gorge, in his voice in her memory: *A riverman's last duty is the man in the water. Whoever he is. Especially whoever he is.*

Xanthe was already moving. She ripped the coil of rope from Ferro's shoulder, tied the bowline around her own waist because there was no time to argue about who went, and looked at Odile — one look, everything in it — and Odile nodded and took the belay, planting her heels in the scree, and Bram's huge hands closed over the rope behind hers.

"Hold my belt!" Xanthe shouted into the wind, and stepped off the rocks into the newborn river, reaching for the hand of the man who had killed her father, while above them both, with a groan that shook the mountain, the glacier leaned further out over the pool — and began, at last, to calve in earnest.
