## 19. Icefall

The mountain spoke first.

Xanthe heard it from half a mile below the glacier's face — a sound like a cathedral door opening somewhere under the world, deep and grinding and patient. She had grown up on a river that talked all day and all night, that muttered over gravel bars and groaned in flood and sang its thin winter song through the ice. But this was different. This was the voice of something enormous deciding to move.

She climbed the last of the scree with her palms bleeding, Bram one step behind her breathing like a broken bellows, Ferro behind him muttering to every saint whose name he could remember. The meltwater lake spread out below the ice wall, gray-green and opaque with rock flour, and floating on it — absurd, obscene — was Vane's drill rig, lashed to two rafts of timber, its derrick leaning against the glacier like a drunk against a church.

And there at the base of the rig stood Odile, small and straight-backed, her hands moving over machinery while Vane's remaining hired man stood over her with a rifle.

"Odile." Xanthe said it aloud though there was no chance the wind would carry it. The name came out anyway. Some things you say because your body insists.

The glacier answered.

It was not an explosion. It was slower than that, more terrible — a crack that began high on the serac field and traveled down through the blue heart of the ice like lightning frozen mid-strike, and then a section of the face the size of the pilothouse simply *leaned*, the way a tired man leans before he falls, and came off the mountain in silence.

The silence lasted three seconds. Then the lake stood up.

Xanthe had seen water do impossible things on the Meridian — seen flood waves climb banks, seen rapids swallow trees whole — but she had never seen a wall of displaced lake rise forty feet and walk across the surface toward the rig, carrying icebergs the way a river carries leaves. The wave hit the rafts and the rafts vanished. The derrick went over sideways with a shriek of tearing timber. Figures scattered — she could see them, tiny as ants: Vane, the hired man with the rifle, Odile —

Odile was in the water.

* * *

Odile had known, in the way engineers know things, that the rig was doomed. She had known it for an hour, maybe longer, since the first hairline cracks had begun singing through the ice above them, since the meltwater in the pool beneath the drill site had started pulsing like a heartbeat gone wrong. She had told Vane this. She had told him in plain words, standing hip-deep in freezing slush with his drill bit jammed in stone: *the glacier is calving early, we have minutes, pull the rig back.*

He had put the rifle in her ribs and said, "Start it again."

So she had started it again. Not because she feared the bullet — though she did, she was honest enough with herself to admit she did — but because a stopped engine was a dead crew, and somewhere downstream or upscree were people she loved who might still need an engineer alive. She had bought time with obedience. It was the oldest bargain on any river.

Then the mountain called her bluff.

The wave took the raft she was standing on and folded it like paper. Cold hit her the way a hull hits a rock — total, instant, everything at once. The lake was barely above freezing; it closed around her chest and squeezed the air out of her in one involuntary shout of bubbles. She went under into green murk, into a world of churning silt and tumbling ice, and some cold clear part of her mind noted, distantly, professionally: *buoyancy vest soaked through, boots filling, hypothermia clock starting now.*

She surfaced gasping beside a slab of calved ice the size of a dinner table and got both arms over it. Her hands wouldn't close properly. The cold had already taken them. Across the water she could see the wreckage of the rig drifting in two pieces, the derrick trailing cable like a drowned man's hair, and she could see Vane.

Vane was swimming. Not away from the wreckage — *toward* it. Toward the floating strongbox where he kept his core samples, his sealed tubes of glittering ore, the physical proof of the claim he'd sold his soul in installments to buy. He had shed his coat, his rifle, everything, and he was stroking through water full of drifting ice with the desperate rhythm of a man drowning by choice.

"You fool," Odile whispered. Her voice came out as steam. "You absolute fool."

A second crack ran up the glacier, higher this time, and the sound of it went through her chest like a piston stroke. Above the serac field the whole upper face had begun to shudder, shedding smaller blocks in a steady rain, and Odile understood with terrible clarity what the next act of this drama would be. The mountain was not finished talking. It had only cleared its throat.

She looked at the shore. And saw Xanthe.

Small figure on the scree, dark against the gray stone, running along the shoreline with something coiled over her shoulder — rope, Odile realized, a coil of their good line, the one Bram spliced himself. Running not away from the water but along its edge, reading it, the way she read every river, looking for the seam in the chaos where a swimmer might be reached.

Xanthe was coming for her. Of course she was. Odile felt something in her chest that had nothing to do with the cold — a warmth so fierce it was almost pain — and then the ice floe under her arms rocked as a swell passed beneath it, and she looked up at the glacier's face, and saw the whole western wall begin to go.

* * *

There is a moment in every pilot's life — Xanthe had believed until now it came once, maybe twice, if you were lucky or cursed — when the river offers you a choice you cannot take back. A channel forks. A snag looms. You commit or you die, and sometimes committing is dying too, and the only skill left to you is choosing which death has the better odds.

The lake was offering her that moment now, twice over.

Vane was closest, thirty yards off the wrecked rig, going under and coming up, going under and coming up, his strokes shortening. His hands had found the strongbox and he was trying to hold it and swim at once, which meant he was doing neither. Every few seconds his head went down and stayed down a beat too long.

Odile was farther out, clinging to her floe, being carried by the slow rotation of the lake's churn toward the base of the glacier itself — toward the zone where the falling ice came down, where the water was white with debris and anything that floated there would be crushed or swamped within minutes.

Bram skidded down the scree beside her, Ferro behind him. "Captain," Bram rasped, "tell me you're not thinking the water."

"I'm thinking the water."

"The water's killing men right now."

"The water's always killing men, Bram. That's the job description." She was paying out the coil in her hands, measuring, watching the drift patterns the way she'd watch eddies below a dam. There was a current in the lake — the inflow stream pushed a tongue of moving water along the eastern shore, and the churn of the calving had set up a slow counterclockwise gyre, and where those two forces met there was a seam, a lane of relative calm, exactly the kind of thing she'd spent ten years learning to see. The seam ran past Odile's floe. It did not run past Vane.

Ferro grabbed her arm. His face was gray, his burned hands shaking. "Cap'n — the old woman at the village, she said — the river keeps what it takes—"

"The river keeps what it takes," Xanthe agreed. "But I decide what I give it."

Above the glacier, the western wall leaned.

She tied the line off around her own waist. Bram saw it and made a sound that wasn't quite a word. "Two anchors," she said. "You and Ferro. Dig in. If I go in after someone, you're the only reason I come back out."

"Captain—"

"That's an order, Mr. Bram."

She looked at the water one last time. Vane went under and did not come up. Three seconds. Four. Then his hand broke the surface, clawing, still gripping the corner of the strongbox, dragging it down with him like an anchor of his own choosing.

The choice lay open in front of her, clean as a forked channel. Let him drown. Let the river keep what it takes. He had sabotaged her boat, held a gun on the woman she loved, desecrated her father's memory, and sold a nation's greed a map to the last wild water on earth. No jury on the Meridian would ever hear about it. No one would ever know she had watched and done nothing.

Her father would have thrown the line.

That was the whole of it, she thought, and the thought arrived with no anger in it at all, which surprised her. Elias Sandoval had died protecting a source he refused to own, murdered by men who wanted what he wouldn't sell, and if he had stood on this shore with a rope in his hands and Cassius Vane drowning in front of him — he would have thrown it. Not because Vane deserved it. Because the throwing was who *he* was, and a man's mercy is the one cargo no one can seize, sink, or buy.

*Xanthe,* her father's voice said in her memory, in the tone he used when explaining knots, *a line isn't for the person at the other end. It's for the person holding it.*

The western wall fell.

It came down in three great steps, each block bigger than the Provision, and the sound was the end of the world played backward. The wave it threw across the lake was a moving hill. Xanthe waited — one heartbeat, two, the timing pure instinct, the same instinct that threaded the Staircase at flood stage — and threw.

The line uncoiled over the churning water like a living thing. It dropped across Vane's reaching hand just as the wave hit.

* * *

The world became noise and then became nothing, and in the nothing Odile held onto her floe and prayed to no god in particular, just to arithmetic: *the line holds, the anchors hold, the knot holds.* She had taught Xanthe that knot herself, a bowline-on-a-bight, doubled for shock load, and she had never imagined teaching it for this.

When the water settled enough to see, she saw them: Xanthe in the shallows to her waist, hauling hand over hand, and Vane beyond her, coughing, half-drowned, the strongbox gone from his grip — torn loose by the wave, sinking, she could see it going down through the green water, settling into the silt with all its sealed and glittering proof. The rope had torn free of his fist in the impact and Xanthe had thrown it again, without hesitation, a second cast, and this time he'd caught it.

They came ashore together. Xanthe dragged him up the gravel like a landed fish, and Bram and Ferro fell on the line, and for a long moment the four of them were just a tangle of bodies and rope and heaving breath on the stones, and the glacier rained its smaller ice down into the lake with a sound like applause.

Odile waded out of the water on legs that barely answered, and Xanthe turned, and saw her, and the look on her face — Odile would keep that look for the rest of her life. Not relief exactly. Something older than relief. Recognition, maybe. The face of a woman finding the one thing she'd been afraid to hope was still afloat.

"You're supposed to be dead," Xanthe said, her voice cracking on it.

"You're supposed to hate me for voting upstream."

"I'm working on it." And then they were holding each other, soaked and shaking, and Odile pressed her face into Xanthe's salt-and-river-smelling neck and let herself shake, because the cold was in her bones now and the fear was in her bones now and there was no more pretending to be steel.

Behind them, Vane knelt in the gravel, staring at the lake.

"It's down there," he said. His voice was hollow, scraped out. "Ten years of surveys. The assays. The letters. Everything. It's—" He turned to Xanthe, and his face was doing something complicated, grief and fury and disbelief fighting for the same ground. "You could have let me drown."

"Yes," Xanthe said.

"Why didn't you?"

She crouched in front of him, dripping, and Odile watched her husband-to-be-if-such-things-were-ever-formalized-on-a-river look at the man who had ruined her father's name and nearly killed them all, and what she saw in Xanthe's eyes was not forgiveness. It was something harder and quieter than forgiveness.

"Because my father would have," Xanthe said. "And I spent ten years thinking his mercy made him weak. It didn't. It made him the only man on this river Vane's money couldn't buy." She stood. "The evidence is gone, Cassius. The samples are gone. All you get to keep is what my father kept — the story of what you did. And stories are heavier than ore. You'll carry yours the rest of your life."

Vane opened his mouth. Whatever he meant to say, the mountain interrupted.

The final section of the serac field — the crown of the glacier, hanging above the source pool itself — let go all at once, and the sound was not a crash but a chord, vast and resonant, and the spray lifted off the lake and drifted over them like snow. When it cleared, the face of the ice had changed shape entirely. The shelf where the drill rig had stood was simply not there anymore. The source pool beyond it flashed blinding blue between walls of fresh-fallen ice, impossibly still, impossibly bright, keeping its own counsel.

Ferro crossed himself with his bandaged hands. Bram sat down heavily on a boulder and laughed, once, the laugh of a man who has decided not to think about how close it all was.

Xanthe looked at the water where the strongbox had sunk, then at the source, then at Odile. And Odile saw the decision settle over her the way evening settles over a valley — gradually, and then completely.

"We're done here," Xanthe said. "All of us. Get him up. We've got a long walk to the boat, and the river's not getting any younger."

She shouldered the coiled line — the line that had saved the man she had every right to hate — and started down the shoreline toward home, and the glacier groaned behind them like something finally, at last, going back to sleep.
