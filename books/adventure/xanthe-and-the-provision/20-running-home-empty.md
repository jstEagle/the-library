## 20. Running Home Empty

Bram had steered through fog, through flood, through the black hour before dawn when the river gave nothing back to a man's eyes. He had never steered through silence.

It came off the crew like a smell. Four days down from the glacier and nobody spoke unless the boat required it — *hard a-starboard*, *ease her*, *watch the snag* — and even then the words came out flat as coins rubbed past spending. Ferro cooked and nobody ate much. Odile kept the engine turning with a rag in one hand and a wrench in the other, and if she looked up at Xanthe more than she needed to, Bram pretended not to see it, because some things a man was not meant to witness even when they were happening three feet from his elbow.

And Vane lashed to the mast base like cargo that had gone bad.

They'd trussed him with the same line they'd used for the drill rig's tarpaulin, because it was what they had, and there was something fitting in that, Bram thought. A man who'd crossed half a continent to own the river's roof, bound now in canvas rope and sitting in his own filth by the third day because nobody could be bothered to untie him long enough for dignity. His polished boots had gone gray. His fine coat hung off him like a shed skin. He talked, at first — threats, offers, recitations of names that were supposed to mean something — and then he stopped talking, and that was worse somehow, because a silent Cassius Vane was a man doing arithmetic, and Bram did not want to know what numbers.

Xanthe stood the wheel most hours. She wouldn't give it up. Bram tried twice to spell her and got the same answer both times, which was no answer at all, just her hand tightening on the spokes until the knuckles went white as the ice they'd left behind.

"Captain," he'd said, the second time, keeping his voice low. "You're asleep standing up."

"I'm steering."

"You're steering asleep." He'd looked out at the water ahead, brown and slow and indifferent, carrying them home the way it carried everything — without opinion. "The gorge is behind us. The hard part's done."

She'd turned and looked at him then, and Bram had been running rivers for thirty years and had never seen a face do what hers did: grief and relief arriving together, neither willing to yield the deck to the other.

"The hard part," she said, "is going to be getting off this boat and looking people in the eye."

* * *

They ran empty. That was the thing that sat wrong in Bram's gut every time he walked the freight deck. The Meridian was a working river; boats went up heavy and came down heavy, ore or timber or salt or survey gear, and a boat riding high on the return leg was a boat that had failed at something. Their wake told the story to anyone who knew how to read it — too little draft, too much slap against the hull, the Provision II (and Bram still wasn't used to calling her that, though the old bell answered to it now) wallowing light as a leaf.

No cargo. No payment. Vane's contract had burned with everything else, or drowned, depending on which pieces you believed about the icefall, and either way there was no paper left in the world that said these people were owed so much as a copper bit for what they'd survived.

"We'll eat," Odile had said, when Ferro raised it, hunched over his galley stove on the second night, stirring a pot thin enough to read through. "I can keep her boiler fed on scrap and driftwood if I have to. We're not rich but we're not dead, and dead men don't eat at all."

Ferro had looked into his pot a long time. "My grandmother said the river counts what you carry," he said. "Going up and coming down. It balances the books."

"Then we're owed," Odile said.

"No." Ferro shook his head, and his voice had that quality it got sometimes, the one that made even Bram go quiet, the superstition burning off into something older and plainer underneath. "We're not owed. We were paid already. We just don't know yet in what coin."

Nobody had an answer for that, so they ate their thin soup and listened to the engine breathe.

* * *

On the fifth day the river narrowed toward Gray Landing, and Bram took the wheel for the approach because Xanthe finally slept — truly slept, folded into the corner of the pilothouse bench with her boots still on, and Odile had draped a coat over her without waking her, moving through the small space like a woman defusing something.

The cliffs came first. They always did. Gray Landing sat halfway up the cliffside in terraces, stone houses stacked like nesting boxes, and the village had no harbor worth the name — just a shelf of rock where boats could lie against the current and take on ropes and bread and news. But the villagers saw everything. They'd seen the Provision go up ten years ago. They'd watched young Xanthe Sandoval stand on this same deck with her jaw set like her father's, swearing at the water.

Bram brought her in slow, and the sound reached him before anything else did.

A bell. Not theirs — theirs was lashed below, cracked along one seam, mute since the icefall. This was the landing bell, the old bronze thing bolted to a post above the shelf, and somebody was ringing it, ringing it hard, the way you rang it for fire or flood or boats in trouble.

Then the fires.

They lit along the terraces one after another, dusk coming down the cliff face like a curtain and the fires chasing it upward — cookfires first, then signal fires, then, God help him, Bram realized what he was looking at, welcome fires, the kind the villages lit only for boats coming back from water they weren't expected to come back from. He'd seen it once before in his life, as a boy, when a timber raft had come down the Sorrow Fork after nine days missing. Half the crew hadn't been on it. The fires had burned anyway.

He rang down to the deck. "Xanthe. You need to see this."

She came up the ladder fast, hair loose, eyes still swollen with sleep, and stopped beside him at the glass, and Bram watched his captain — who had stared down the Staircase, who had held a broken rudder linkage with her bare hands over white water, who had thrown a line to the man who'd ruined her life twice over — watch a cliffside catch fire for her, and begin, very quietly, to cry.

Not sobbing. Nothing so generous. Just tears coming down her face while her hands stayed easy on nothing, while the boat slid toward the shelf of rock, while the bell above them hammered and hammered like a heart that wouldn't be reasoned with.

"They think we died," she said.

"They know better now."

"They think—" She stopped. Started again. "Ten years ago nobody lit fires. Ten years ago my father went up and the village went dark and stayed dark, like they were ashamed. Like they'd failed him by letting him pass." Her voice caught. "And now they're lighting fires for us."

"Maybe they figured out whose fault it wasn't," Bram said.

Below them, figures were gathering on the rock shelf, dozens of them, torches in hand, and Bram could pick out the elders by their stillness — the ones who'd refused them passage upstream, who'd told Xanthe the river keeps what it takes. The old woman was there, the one whose name Bram had never learned, standing at the very edge of the shelf with her arms folded, watching the boat come in with an expression he couldn't read at all.

Vane lifted his head at the noise. He'd been dozing against the mast, and now his eyes found the fires, the crowd, the bell, and something moved across his face that Bram would spend a long time deciding whether it was fear or calculation. With Vane it was always both.

"There will be witnesses here," Vane said. His voice was wrecked, sanded down to gravel, but the shape of the man was still in it, the architecture of persuasion. "Sandoval. Whatever you intend—"

"I intend to tie off," Xanthe said, without looking at him. "That's all I intend right now."

"The commission will want testimony. My backers will send lawyers. If you hand me to these people—"

"These people aren't handing you anywhere." She turned then, and looked down at him, and Bram saw her take inventory of him the way she took inventory of a damaged hull — coldly, completely, deciding what could be saved and what couldn't. "You're going to sit there until morning. Then we're all going to talk, in front of everyone, and the river gets a vote too. It always does."

* * *

They tied off at full dark.

The shelf of rock took the hull with a groan and a scrape, and lines went ashore, and hands Bram didn't know pulled them tight — weathered hands, work hands, the whole village apparently come down the cliff paths with lanterns. Nobody cheered. That was the thing Bram noticed, standing at the rail while the crowd pressed close: nobody cheered, nobody shouted questions, nobody surged forward to touch the famous survivors. They just looked. Hundreds of faces in torchlight, looking at the boat, at the crew, at the empty freight deck, reading the whole story off it the way Ferro said the river read its books.

An empty boat, coming home. They understood exactly what that meant.

The old woman pushed through last. She climbed the rail herself — eighty if she was a day, and she climbed it like a girl — and dropped onto the deck, and stood in front of Xanthe, and looked her up and down.

"You went up," she said.

"We went up."

"To the source."

"To the source." Xanthe's voice was steady. Bram marveled at it. "It's unmarked. It'll stay unmarked. What we found stays with us, and what we lost—" She stopped. Swallowed. "What we lost, the ice kept. All of it. Even the papers."

Something passed over the old woman's face then, and Bram realized with a start that it was satisfaction — deep, geological, the satisfaction of a woman who had waited ten years to hear one particular sentence and had just heard it spoken true.

"Your father stood where you stood," she said. "In this spot. The night before he went up. He asked us to light no fires if he didn't come back. He said a boat that comes home empty has told the truth, and a boat that comes home heavy has told a lie, and the river only forgives the truth." She reached out and took Xanthe's chin in one small hand, rough as tree bark, and turned her face to the torchlight. "You've come home empty, Xanthe Sandoval."

"Yes."

"Then you've told the truth." She let go. Turned to the crowd. And raised her voice, thin and clear as a whistle across water: "They've come home empty! The river has its answer!"

The sound that came off that cliffside Bram would remember the rest of his life. Not a cheer — a roar, yes, but underneath the roar something else, a low sustained note like the river itself answering, hundreds of voices and the water under the hull and the wind off the gorge all finding the same pitch, and the fires leaping, and the bell starting up again overhead, rung now by children climbing the post, and Ferro at Bram's shoulder saying softly, "There. That's the coin. Told you we were paid."

Odile had found Xanthe's hand somewhere in all of it. Bram saw that too. Saw their fingers lace together, saw Xanthe lean into her engineer the way a hull leans into a fender, taking the load, sharing it.

Vane alone made no sound. Lashed at the mast base, he watched the fires with his head tilted, and when the roar crested he closed his eyes, and Bram — watching him, because someone had to — saw his lips move. Counting, probably. Heads, torches, boats. Always the arithmetic.

But the arithmetic was finished and he didn't know it yet. Every soul on this cliff belonged to the river's side of the ledger now, and tomorrow they would all of them testify, and there wasn't a lawyer on the Meridian who could buy back a single word.

* * *

They anchored midstream that night rather than crowd the shelf — the village offered, Xanthe declined, saying the boat should stay on the water that owned her — and Bram took first watch because he couldn't have slept anyway.

Midnight, and the fires still burning on the terraces above, and the bell finally quiet, and the river sliding past black and patient. Xanthe came up around two, wrapped in a blanket, and stood beside him at the rail without a word for a long time.

"Bram," she said eventually. "Tomorrow, when they ask what happened up there. All of it. The oath, the mutiny vote, Pell, the icefall, Vane — all of it."

"What about it?"

"I'm going to tell them everything. Every mistake I made. Taking the contract. Breaking the oath. Throwing him the line." She was quiet a moment. "I need you to tell your parts too. Even the ugly ones. Especially mine."

Bram looked at her. Thirty years of rivers, and this was the hardest water he'd ever been asked to read.

"Captain," he said, "you threw a drowning man a rope that wanted you dead. There's nothing ugly in that worth hiding."

"That's not what I mean." She turned to look at him, and the torchlight from shore touched her face, and she looked — tired, yes, wrung out, but underneath it something new was setting like a keel. "Tomorrow isn't the end of it. Vane's backers are still out there. The commission will bury the claims, maybe, but men like that don't drown. They send more men. And when they come asking about the upper gorge — about routes, about depths, about what the ice keeps — this village, this crew, this boat..." She looked back at the water. "We're going to be the ones they come to. For the rest of our lives."

Bram considered that. Considered the fires on the cliff, the bell, the old woman's words. Considered the mortgage notice still pinned inside the pilothouse door, curling at the edges, and the salvage rights that might — might — cover it, and the long lean seasons between.

"Aye," he said at last. "Then we'd best get good at telling the truth. Sounds like we'll be doing it a lot."

Xanthe laughed — short, surprised, real — and it was the first sound any of them had made in five days that wasn't exhaustion, and it carried across the water, and somewhere above them a child on the bell post laughed back.

Down in the hold, unseen by either of them, the old bell shifted on its lashings in the night breeze and gave off a single soft note — the Provision's note, her father's note, low and round and unmistakable, as if the boat itself had cleared its throat to speak.

Morning, and the commission boats were already coming upriver. Two of them, flying gray pennants, paddles flashing in the dawn.

They'd heard the bell.
