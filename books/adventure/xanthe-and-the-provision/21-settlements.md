## 21. Settlements

The commission met in the customs house at Meridian Falls, in a room that smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular staleness of institutions that had never once been surprised. Xanthe had passed its windows a hundred times without looking in. Now she sat at a table scarred by decades of freight manifests and waited for the river to be discussed as though it were a ledger.

Three commissioners presided from a raised dais: an old surveyor named Halloway with ink-stained fingers and eyes like river fog; a woman from the Interior Office whose name was Corvane and whose manner suggested she had come to be bored; and a local magistrate, Dree, who had known Xanthe's father and could not look at her without his jaw tightening.

Behind her sat her crew. Bram filled his chair the way he filled a doorway — entirely, arms crossed over a chest still mapped with rope burns from the gorge. Ferro had put on his funeral coat, the good one, because he believed official rooms were a kind of grave and one should dress accordingly. Odile sat closest to her, close enough that their shoulders touched when either shifted, and that small point of contact was doing more work than any argument Xanthe had prepared.

Vane sat opposite, under guard, in clothes that had been cleaned but not replaced. The gorge had taken his fine coat, his hat, and most of his certainty. What remained was a man who kept glancing at the window as if the river might climb the stairs to finish him.

The hearing had run three days already. Surveyors testified about sediment cores. A hydrologist read figures on seasonal flow until the room's attention dissolved like sugar. Villagers from the cliff settlement came down in twos and threes to speak of the Provision, of the old woman's grandmother's warnings, of water that remembered. Their testimony carried no documents and all the weight of bedrock.

On the fourth morning, they brought out the letters.

Xanthe had surrendered them willingly — copies, that is. The originals stayed wrapped in oilcloth inside her cabin, against her skin some nights, though she'd never have admitted it. The commission's clerk read aloud the passages that mattered: her father's account of the survey party that had come before him, armed and paid, and what he'd found of them; his decision to sink his own charts rather than let the route be owned; the name of Vane's backer written plainly in a hand gone shaky with cold.

*Let the river keep what it keeps,* the letter said. *A man can be rich or he can be honest. On this water there is not room for both.*

The clerk's voice cracked on the last line. He was young. He hadn't expected the paper to feel like that.

Corvane leaned forward for the first time in three days. "Captain Sandoval. You recovered these documents from the wreck of the *Provision*."

"I did."

"And you also recovered — " she consulted her papers — "the vessel's bell, fittings, hull iron of salvageable quantity. Yet you did not recover the charts themselves. Your testimony states they were destroyed prior to the sinking."

"By my father's hand. His crew confirmed it before they died." She kept her voice level. It cost something. "I've seen what's left of them. They died protecting that choice. I'm not going to dig it up and sell it now."

"You understand," Corvane said, "that the claim filed by Vane's principals references surveys never completed. Without those charts, their filing rests on — "

"Nothing. That's correct." Xanthe let the silence do a moment's work. "They sent men upriver with guns and drills to take a thing that doesn't belong to anyone, and the river took the measure of them. I'm just here to read out the arithmetic."

Halloway made a dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Arithmetic. Go on, Captain."

"The source is unclaimable. Not undeveloped — unclaimable. There's a difference. Declare it watershed, all of it, headwater to calving line. Not open for filing, staking, lease, or purchase. Put it in the same category as weather." She spread her hands. "You can't own the rain. Stop letting men file paperwork pretending you can."

Dree spoke for the first time, his voice rough. "And the men who financed it? Names in that letter are names with lawyers, Captain. Powerful ones."

"They had guns too," Bram said from behind her, and the room went quiet.

* * *

The deliberation took two days, during which Xanthe learned that justice moves at the speed of committees and rivers move at the speed of gravity, and only one of them can be trusted.

She spent the waiting aboard the boat, which lay at the winter dock below the falls, patched and scarred and listing slightly to port where the gorge had bruised her. Odile lived in the engine room those days, coaxing the old girl back to even temper, and Xanthe would bring her coffee twice a day and stay for the third cup without either of them naming why.

It was on the second evening that Odile surfaced, wiping her hands on a rag, and said, "The mortgage notice came again."

"I saw."

"Third time since we got back. They're getting impatient."

Xanthe looked down the gray length of the river, running low and clear in the autumn light. "There's money coming. Salvage rights."

"To the *Provision*?"

"What's left of her. Hull iron, fittings, the bell if I wanted to sell it, which I don't. The commission appraised it while we were testifying. Enough to clear the debt." She turned the mug in her hands. "Exactly enough. Nothing more."

Odile studied her. She had a way of looking at Xanthe that felt like reading depth soundings — patient, precise, finding the bottom. "You could ask for more. Wreckage-iron testimony, the letters, everything we gave them. They'd pay it."

"That's not how it works." Xanthe said it quietly. "That's how Vane thinks. Everything's a price. Everything's leverage." She shook her head. "My father didn't die so I could invoice the river for his grave. I'll take what the law says is mine and not a coin past it. The rest stays where it lies."

"Even the bell?"

"Especially the bell." She almost smiled. "She'll ring again. Just not for sale."

Odile set down her rag and crossed the engine room and kissed her, tasting of coal smoke and machine oil, and neither of them said anything else, because nothing needed saying. Some settlements aren't signed. They're just lived into.

* * *

The ruling came down on a morning of hard frost, read aloud on the customs house steps to a crowd that included half the lower river's boatmen, the cliff village elders in their traveling wool, and Ferro, who wept openly and claimed afterward it was the wind.

Commissioner Halloway read it in a dry institutional voice that somehow made it more momentous, not less: the upper gorge and source basin of the Meridian, together with all tributary waters above the Provision Rapids, were hereby designated permanent protected watershed. No claims valid. No filings accepted. No exceptions "in perpetuity" — a word Halloway pronounced with visible satisfaction, as if he'd been waiting his whole career to use it in earnest.

The backers' claims were struck. Their lawyers, present in expensive coats, absorbed the blow the way large structures absorb earthquakes — swaying, groaning, remaining upright, but visibly compromised. One of them turned to leave and found himself facing the cliff village elders, who did not move aside, and who watched him the entire way down the steps, and who said nothing at all, which was worse than anything they could have said.

Vane's own disposition was handled separately. Fraud, conspiracy, the deaths attributable to his expedition — these went forward to criminal session in the spring. He would wait in the territorial jail through the winter, which everyone agreed was fitting; a man who had tried to buy the roof of the river would spend the frozen months learning what the river thought of being owned.

As they led him past, he stopped in front of Xanthe. Up close, stripped of polish, he looked older than his years and smaller than his reputation.

"You could have had everything," he said. "The route. The deposits. A dynasty."

"I had a father," Xanthe said. "Turns out that was the fortune."

They took him away. The crowd dispersed slowly, reluctantly, the way crowds do when a story ends and nobody wants to be the first one back in the ordinary world.

Bram appeared at her elbow. "Boat's wanting fuel and the mortgage man's office opens at ten."

"Then let's go do our arithmetic."

* * *

The bank was warm and smelled of paper and floor wax, and the clerk who handled the mortgage was a narrow man named Quill who had spent eleven years sending Xanthe notices and had clearly rehearsed disappointment for today.

But the figure was right. Salvage appraisal, certified by the commission, transferred against the outstanding balance — and the balance went to zero. Quill stamped the paper twice, checked it three times, and looked up with something that on a narrower face might have been awe.

"Cleared," he said. "Fully. Captain Sandoval, the *Marianne's* — your vessel's — free and clear."

"Not the *Marianne* anymore." Xanthe signed where he indicated. "She's the *Provision II*. Has been since we rebuilt around my father's bell."

Quill wrote that down too, carefully, as if the name itself required accurate record.

Outside, the frost was burning off the dock boards in thin white smoke. Odile was waiting, hands in her pockets, breath steaming. She didn't ask. She just watched Xanthe's face, and whatever she found there made her nod once, slow, like a gauge settling to true.

"Well?" Bram rumbled behind them.

"Paid," Xanthe said. "Every coin of it. And not a coin more."

Ferro, who had followed them uninvited and unregretted, spat ceremonially off the dock edge into the river. "Good," he declared. "Debts to banks are clean. Debts to rivers are forever, and those we keep."

Downstream, the Meridian ran broad and unhurried toward the sea, carrying ice at its edges now, the first thin lace of the season. In spring it would rise. In spring the flood would open every channel and drown every sandbar and make the whole lower river new again.

Xanthe stood at the rail of her debtless boat and felt the current through the hull, faint and constant, like a pulse.

Spring, she thought. High water. Time enough yet to fit the new planking, ring the bell once for the dead, and then —

Odile's hand found hers on the rail.

"— and then we see what the river gives back," Xanthe finished aloud, and the *Provision II* rocked gently at her moorings, as if in answer.
