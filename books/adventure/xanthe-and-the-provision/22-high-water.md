## 22. High Water

The river came up in the night the way it always did in spring — not gradually but all at once, as if somewhere far upstream a door had been opened and everything behind it let go.

Xanthe heard it before she saw it. She was awake in the pilothouse bunk of the *Provision II*, listening to the water change its voice against the hull. In low season the Meridian spoke in murmurs, a long slow syllable repeated against the planking. Tonight it had found its full throat. It roared the way a crowd roars, the way weather roars, carrying ice-shards and drowned timber and the brown memory of ten thousand hillsides all at once. She lay in the dark with Odile's breath warm against her shoulder and listened to the river say her name in every language it knew.

"Go back to sleep," Odile murmured, without opening her eyes. "It's just water."

"It's never just water."

"No," Odile admitted, and pulled her closer. "But it's ours now. Or we're its. Whichever way you want to count it."

Xanthe counted it neither way. She counted instead the things that were true: the mortgage paid in full, the paper burned in the galley stove by Bram's own hand while Ferro salted the ashes for luck; the bell hanging new-polished in the pilothouse, her father's bell, salvaged from the cold shallows where the old *Provision* had lain for ten years keeping its dead; the crew signed on freely this time, every name written in their own hand, no triple rates, no false pretenses, no rot under any crate. Just wages, honest ones, and a spring run down an open river.

She slept, finally, to the sound of high water.

* * *

They cast off from Gray Landing at first light, and the whole village came down to see them go.

That was new. Ten years ago Gray Landing had watched Xanthe Sandoval leave with her father's boat and her father's silence sitting on her like a coat too heavy for her frame, and they had said nothing, because grief is a private country and they knew the borders. But the village that forgets nothing had also learned, over one hard winter, not to forget kindly. The old woman who had told her the truth about her father — that he died protecting the source, not seeking it — stood at the end of the dock with a loaf of black bread wrapped in cloth, and when Xanthe took the wheel lines she pressed the bread into her hands and held on a moment longer than necessary.

"The river gave him back to you," she said. "In its own fashion. Don't waste the fashion."

"I won't."

"And the girl—" the old woman's eyes went past her, to where Odile stood at the engine-room hatch with grease to her elbows and the morning light catching the iron-gray in her hair, "—the girl keeps your engine and your heart both running. I can hear it from here."

Xanthe laughed, which surprised her; she hadn't expected the laugh to still be in her after everything. "You can hear an engine from the dock?"

"I can hear a woman being loved from further than that." The old woman released the bread, released her hand, and stepped back. "Go on. High water waits for nobody, and it forgets even less than we do."

Bram cast off the stern line. Ferro rang the bell once — her father's bell, its voice deeper than any bell had a right to be, as if ten years underwater had taught it patience — and the *Provision II* swung out into the current and the village fell away behind them in a haze of woodsmoke and waving arms and one dog barking itself hoarse on the bank.

The river was running eight feet above summer mark. Debris rode the surface in dark flotillas: whole trees traveling roots-first like drowned men swimming home, fence rails, a roof, a canoe paddling itself in circles. The current pulled at the hull with a hand that wanted to be obeyed. Xanthe stood at the wheel and let it.

"You're smiling," Bram said beside her. He'd taken the mate's watch without being asked, the way he did everything, gruffly and on time. His knees were better since the winter — the mountain walk had nearly finished them, and the doctor in Meridian City had said he'd be lucky to pilot again, and Bram had called the doctor a pessimist and walked out and been on deck within the month, limping and unrepentant.

"I'm allowed."

"Didn't say you weren't. Just noting it. Captain smiles, river rises. Ferro will want to build a shrine."

"Ferro builds a shrine to everything. He built one to my coffee pot last month."

"That coffee pot deserves it." Bram squinted downstream, reading the water the way she'd taught him a decade ago, back when he'd been the one at the wheel and she'd been a raw-keeled girl learning which boils meant rock and which meant nothing but the river showing off. "Sandbar off Miller's Point will have moved. High water rearranges the furniture."

"Then we'll visit the furniture as we find it." She eased the wheel two spokes to starboard. The *Provision II* answered sweetly, the way a well-loved boat answers, the way the old *Provision* had answered her father, and for a moment — only a moment — the two boats overlaid each other in her mind like double-exposed photographs, and she let them, because grief reframed was inheritance, and inheritance carried well.

Below Miller's Point the valley opened wide and golden-green, and the river ran free.

* * *

Odile found her in the engine room at midday, though technically Odile owned the engine room and Xanthe was the trespasser.

"You're dripping on my deck plates," Odile said.

"It's my boat."

"It's my engine room. Those are different sovereignties. Ask any married couple." She said it lightly, and then went slightly pink, because they hadn't said it aloud yet, the word, though they had been living inside its meaning since the glacier — since Xanthe had chosen, with the ice coming down like the end of the world, to reach for Odile's hand instead of Vane's throat.

Xanthe set down the oil can she'd been pretending to inspect. "Ask any married couple what?"

"Nothing. Forget it. Go steer your boat."

"Odile Marchetti." She crossed the engine room, ducking under the steam line, and took her partner's greasy hands in her own clean ones. "Ten years I've run this river alone in every way that mattered. Then you came aboard with your midnight inspections and your terrible singing and your refusal to be impressed by me, and you saved my rudder linkage, and my life, and probably my soul, though you'd deny the last one on technical grounds. If you're asking what I think you're asking—"

"I'm not asking anything. I'm working."

"—then the answer is yes, and it was yes on the gravel bar, and yes in the gorge, and yes the first night watch we ever shared when you told me the stars were just distant engines and I believed you, which should have told me something."

Odile looked at her hands in Xanthe's hands. Grease and rope callus and the faint tremor she still got sometimes, in cold weather, remembering seracs falling. "I was going to wait until we docked," she said. "There was going to be a ring. Ferro has it. He's been insufferable about hiding it."

"He hid it in the flour tin. He hides everything in the flour tin. He thinks it's sacred ground."

"It *is* sacred ground. That's why I put it there." Odile's mouth twitched. "Fine. No ring, no dock, no ceremony. You've ruined the choreography entirely."

"I'm a pilot. Choreography is for people whose rivers hold still." Xanthe kissed her, there among the pistons and the heat, with the engine humming beneath them like a second heartbeat, and above them through the deck overhead came the muffled sound of Ferro dropping an entire stack of pans, followed by his voice, delighted and appalled: "*I KNEW IT.*"

* * *

They ran the Staircase at two in the afternoon with the flood standing the rapids up taller than any chart had ever drawn them.

This was the stretch that had scarred their hull the first time, running upstream against the contract, against the oath, against sense. Downstream with high water it was a different animal — faster, louder, the holes drowned deep and the waves stacked like a congregation rising. Bram stood at Xanthe's shoulder and didn't speak, because there was nothing to say that the wheel didn't already know. Ferro lashed himself to the galley stove and prayed loudly to three separate saints and the coffee pot. And Xanthe steered.

She steered the way her father's letters had taught her — not the route, never the route, he had sunk his own charts rather than sell the river, but the *refusal*, the way of moving through water that treated it as a living thing to be negotiated with rather than conquered. Read the boil. Respect the hole. Give the river its due and it would, more often than not, give you yours. Her hands moved and the boat moved and the Staircase roared around them, and when they shot out the bottom into the long green calm below, the crew made a sound none of them had planned — a whoop, ragged and collective, four voices and a bell.

Ferro rang it twice more for the saints. Nobody stopped him.

* * *

They passed the turnoff to the upper gorge at dusk, and Xanthe looked at it, and kept steering.

That was all. No hand on the wheel now but her own. The gorge mouth opened dark between its cliffs, and somewhere up beyond it the source pool waited, blinding blue and impossibly still, declared unclaimable watershed by commission decree, sealed forever behind calving ice and law and the bones of men who had refused to mark it. The evidence had gone down with the rig. Vane sat in a commission cell in Meridian City writing testimony nobody read, because everyone already knew. Her father's last letter lived in a oilcloth packet under her bunk, and she read it on quiet nights not for information anymore but the way you visit a grave — to say thank you, and goodbye, and again, thank you.

"Thinking about going up?" Bram asked quietly.

"No."

"Could. Water's high enough. Nobody could stop us."

"That's exactly why I won't." She watched the gorge fall astern. "Some water you swear off so you can keep everything else. He taught me that. Took me ten years and a wreck to hear it."

Bram nodded slowly. "Your father would've—"

"Don't." She smiled, so he'd know it wasn't anger. "Everyone keeps telling me what he would've done. I'd rather decide what I do."

"What are you doing, then?"

"Running home empty," she said, "with everything."

* * *

Night watch, shared, the way it had begun.

The river ran silver under a half moon, wide and fat with snowmelt, and the banks slid past dark and breathing. Odile stood beside her at the wheel, not steering, just keeping company, a mug of Ferro's chicory steaming between them. Somewhere below, Ferro was teaching Bram to play cards and cheating, badly, and the sound of their argument came up through the deck boards like the heartbeat of the boat itself.

"Do you ever miss it?" Odile asked. "The anger. It carried you a long way."

"It carried me up a mountain. Carrying and living are different things." Xanthe turned the wheel a spoke, correcting for a drifting log, easy as breathing. "I spent ten years thinking my inheritance was a debt. Something owed. Something to collect. Turns out it was a gift. Something to keep safe and pass on." She glanced sideways. "Speaking of which. Flour tin. Tonight. Before Ferro eats the ring."

Odile laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound the river couldn't touch — and leaned into her shoulder, and the *Provision II* ran on down the open water with her father's bell bright in the moonlight and her love at her side and her family below decks, signed on freely, every one.

Ahead the river bent toward morning, toward the delta, toward the sea it had been traveling to since before there were names for any of it. It forgave nothing. It forgot less. It owed them nothing either.

And that, Xanthe thought, taking the bend smooth and true, was exactly how home should carry you.
