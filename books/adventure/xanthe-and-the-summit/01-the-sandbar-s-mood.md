## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The Vell was lying to her again.

Ansel Dravenmoor could tell by the way the water moved over the sandbar at Crooked Elbow — too smooth, too green, like a table set for guests who weren't coming. A river in a dying season didn't run; it *crept*, and it lied while it crept. What looked like a foot of clear passage over that bar was six inches of silt and a drowned fence post some farmer had lost three floods ago. She'd hit it once herself, seven years back, and she'd never forgiven either of them — the river or herself.

"Six inches," she said aloud, to no one. The wheelhouse of the *Marguerite* was thirty feet long and held one woman, a brass compass, a chipped mug of chicory going cold on the sill, and the particular silence of a person who had stopped expecting company.

She eased the throttle back until the paddlewheel's rhythm dropped from a heartbeat to a murmur. Below her feet she felt the hull whisper against the bar's shoulder — not a strike, just a greeting, the way you'd brush past an old acquaintance you owed money to. Then they were through, sliding into the deeper channel on the far side where the current ran cold off the high country.

"Still got it," she told the river.

The river said nothing. The river had stopped talking to her years ago, or maybe she'd stopped listening. Either way, the drought had taken its voice down to a rasp. Twenty-two days without rain. The gauge at Harrow Landing read four feet two inches and falling, and every boatman between the Falls of Ket and the delta was doing arithmetic with his keel. Most of them had already tied up for the season. The *Marguerite* kept running because Ansel had nothing else to do with her hands, and idle hands, in her experience, started digging graves.

Not literal ones. Not anymore.

She worked the boat downriver through the morning, past willow stands gone yellow at the edges, past a heron standing in six inches of water pretending there were fish. There weren't. Even the fish had the sense to go deep this time of year. At Tenmile Bend she passed the wreck of the *Cordelia Rose*, half-buried in new sand, only her sternwheel showing like a hand reaching up out of a grave asking for help nobody intended to give. Ansel tipped two fingers off the wheel toward her as she went by. Respect among the dead.

By noon she'd made Harrow Landing, which was less a town than an argument between a warehouse, a telegraph office, and a saloon about which of them was the settlement. She took on freight — nails, lamp oil, a crate of books for the mission school downstream that would arrive water-swollen and late, as everything did on the Vell — and she ate a plate of catfish at the Delta Belle that tasted mostly of cornmeal and patience.

It was while she was paying for the catfish that the boy from the telegraph office found her.

"You're Captain Dravenmoor." He said it the way people say *you're the one they warn us about*.

"I'm the one they warn you about," she agreed.

He handed over the envelope like it might bite. It was heavy paper, company stock, the kind that cost more than the message inside. She broke the seal with her thumbnail and read it standing up, because messages like this one were easier to take standing.

CORVUS SURVEY EXPEDITION SILENT 19 DAYS STOP LAST POSITION HEADWATERS ABOVE FALLS OF KET STOP COMPANY REQUIRES BOAT AND CAPTAIN WILLING TO ASCEND STOP REPLY BY WIRE STOP COMPENSATION GENEROUS

She read it twice. The first time for the words. The second time for what wasn't in them — no names, no manifest, no mention of how many souls had gone up that river in April with their instruments and their optimism. Nineteen days silent. Above the Falls of Ket. In August, when the water was dropping by the hour and the smuggler's channel — if it still existed, if it hadn't silted shut the way channels do when God decides to close a door — was the only way past the falls for anything bigger than a canoe.

Her father had known that channel. Had mapped it in his head, never on paper, because paper could be stolen and heads could only be drowned. He'd died twenty years ago trying to chart the source of the Vell, and when no source was ever found — when the expedition he'd sworn existed came home without him and without proof — they'd called him a liar at the inquiry. A drunk. A dreamer who'd spent other men's money chasing a fairy spring. Her mother had moved inland after that. Ansel had stayed with the river, because somebody had to keep the family's side of the argument going, even if the argument was only with herself.

And now the Corvus Survey had gone silent above the falls, and the company wanted her.

She looked at the boy. "Reply by wire, you said."

"Yes'm. They're at Fort Andressen. They'll pay your fare up—"

"They'll pay a lot more than my fare." She folded the telegram into her breast pocket, where it sat over her heart like something swallowed wrong. "Tell them the *Marguerite* doesn't run above the narrows in drought season. Tell them no captain alive knows the old channel well enough to try it."

The boy wrote it down faithfully, word for word, including the parts that were lies. Because here was the truth under the truth: Ansel Dravenmoor knew the smuggler's channel better than any living soul, because her father had taught it to her stroke by stroke before she was old enough to be trusted with it, and she had run it twice since his death, alone, at night, for reasons she'd never told anyone — once to prove he hadn't been lying about the river, and once because grief makes people do stupid things in fast water.

She walked back to the boat along the landing planks, which complained under her boots the way old men complain about weather. The *Marguerite* sat low in the water, patient, her paint gone the gray of driftwood, her name barely legible on the stern. Ninety feet of stubbornness. Her father had built her. Or rather, he'd paid for her, and Boaz Kettering had built her, and between the two of them they'd made something that refused to die out of spite.

Boaz was aboard, of course. Boaz was always aboard, the way barnacles are always aboard. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a stove, and currently elbow-deep in the engine's guts with a wrench in each fist and profanity in reserve.

"We got a telegram," Ansel said.

"I heard. Boy shouted it across the water like we're deaf." He didn't look up. "Corvus Survey. That's the map outfit."

"It is."

"Heard they went quiet."

"Nineteen days."

Boaz grunted, which from Boaz was a paragraph. "Above Ket?"

"Above Ket."

He straightened up slowly, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime in the previous administration. He looked at her with those pale eyes that had watched her grow up on this deck and had never once let her get away with anything. "You told them no."

"I told them no."

"And now you're telling me no, so I know you mean yes."

Ansel leaned against the rail. Downstream, the light was starting to go gold and long, the way it did in the dying season, making even the mud look expensive. "They offered generous compensation, Boaz."

"They offer that first. Then they offer more. Then they offer the thing you actually want." He tossed the rag down. "Company men are fishermen, girl. They don't bait the hook with what you want. They bait it with what you can't forgive yourself for wanting."

"That's very wise. Have you considered a career in fortune-telling?"

"I considered it. The pay's worse than engines." He picked up his wrench again, then paused. "Your daddy ran that channel."

"My daddy died running that river."

"Died living, more like. There's a difference, though I grant it's a fine one." He bent back over the engine. "I'll get her ready regardless. No point arguing with you before supper. You only get reasonable after supper, and only sometimes."

She left him to it. She climbed to the wheelhouse and stood at the window watching the water go by, gold-shot and slow, carrying leaves south like small boats full of passengers who'd paid their fares in color. Somewhere upstream, past the falls, past the narrows, past the last place any honest boat had ever turned around, nineteen people had stopped answering the wire. Maybe dead. Maybe stranded. Maybe sitting in the dark listening to the river say the things rivers say when nobody's left to hear them.

And somewhere in that silence, possibly, was a name she hadn't spoken aloud in eleven years.

No. She put that thought down carefully, the way you put down a loaded gun. The manifest, if they sent one, would be a list of professionals. Surveyors, geologists, packers. Men and women who chose maps over people, duty over —

Over her. Over a woman who'd asked him to stay and been answered with a train ticket and a handshake and the words *the work matters, Ansel, the work outlasts us*. Eleven years, and she could still feel the shape of that sentence in her chest like a splinter the body refuses to heal around.

The knock came at dusk. Not on the wheelhouse door — on the hull, from below, a boat hook rapping the planks. She went down and found a company launch alongside, all varnish and brass, with a man in a linen suit standing in it holding a leather case, looking deeply inconvenienced by the humidity.

"Captain Dravenmoor." He had a voice like a filing cabinet. "Marcus Pell, Corvus Geographic Company. May I come aboard?"

"You may ask again from there."
