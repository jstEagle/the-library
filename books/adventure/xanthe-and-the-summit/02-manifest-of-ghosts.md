## 2. Manifest of Ghosts

The Corvus Survey Company kept its offices in the old customs house on Vell's Landing, a building that smelled of mildew and official importance in roughly equal measure. Ansel sat across a desk from a Mr. Aldous Fenwick, who had the kind of face that looked like it had been ironed and was afraid of creasing again. Between them lay a folder tied with red string, and Ansel had learned long ago that red string meant trouble. Black string meant paperwork. Red string meant someone wanted something.

"You're the only captain," Fenwick said for the third time, "willing to run the Vell above Graywater."

"I'm the only captain *stupid* enough," Ansel said. "There's a difference, though I understand it's a fine one from where you're sitting."

Fenwick smiled the way men smile when they've been told to smile by someone more important. He untied the string.

Ansel had come intending to refuse. She'd rehearsed it on the walk over — *no thank you, no interest, my boat is laid up for the season* — three clean refusals, each politer than the last, all of them lies. The Marguerite was sound as a church bell. The truth was that she'd been restless for a month, pacing her deck at night like a cat in a room too small, listening to the river drop inch by inch beneath her hull and feeling, without any good reason, that something upstream was waiting for her.

She just hadn't expected it to arrive as a folder.

"Manifest of the expedition," Fenwick said. "Eleven souls. You'll want to familiarize yourself."

He slid the paper across. Ansel took it because not taking it would have been rude, and rudeness was the one luxury she couldn't afford in a town this small.

She read the names the way she read everything — fast, competent, already filing them away. Surveyor-in-chief: Edda Kroll. Geologist: Marcus Oyelaran. Botanist, cook, two students from the university, a photographer whose equipment list alone weighed more than Tilly — no, she didn't have a Tilly yet, that came later, but the point stood —

Her eye stopped.

*Ilya Marchetti, cartographer.*

The paper did not change. That was the strange thing. The world went on being an office in August, with a fan turning its slow head overhead and the river muttering outside the window, and the paper stayed exactly what it was: ink on linen stock, a name in a stranger's handwriting. It should have changed. It should have caught fire, or dissolved, or done *something*, because for four full seconds Ansel Dravenmoor could not breathe, and papers that do that to a person ought to have the decency to misbehave.

"Captain?" said Fenwick.

"I knew him," she said. Her voice came out level, which she counted as a small miracle. "Marchetti. We ran the upper trade together, years back."

"Ah." Fenwick brightened, sensing leverage the way sharks sense blood, but with better manners. "Then you'll appreciate the urgency."

She appreciated nothing of the sort. What she appreciated was the ceiling fan, and the way you could watch it turn if you didn't look at anything else.

* * *

They had met on the water, which was fitting, because Ilya Marchetti had never once in his life been comfortable on land. He got seasick on bridges. He drew maps the way other men prayed — bent over, murmuring, oblivious — and he'd come aboard the Marguerite one green spring to chart the middle reaches for the trading consortium, and within a week he'd rearranged her whole galley "for efficiency," and within two weeks she'd let him, and within a month she'd stopped pretending to herself that it was about the galley.

He used to stand at the bow at dawn with his coffee going cold, watching the mist burn off the water, and say things like, *"Do you know what's up there? Past the falls? Nobody knows. There are valleys on this earth that no human eye has ever — "*

*"Ilya."*

*"...Yes, Ansel."*

*"Drink your coffee before it gets cold."*

And he would grin at her — that crooked, sunburnt, entirely unrepentant grin — and drink the coffee, and be quiet for almost a full hour, which for Ilya was practically monastic silence.

Her father had liked him. That was the thing nobody warned you about loving somebody: they get into the people you love too, like dye through water. Old Tomas Dravenmoor had taught Ilya knots on the aft deck and told him stories about the source — the cathedral cavern, the water rising out of living rock — stories Tomas had never told Ansel, or maybe had, when she was small enough to still believe them. And then the survey offer came, the big one, the once-in-a-lifetime one, the chance to chart the headwaters proper, and Ilya stood on her deck with the letter in his hands looking like a man holding a live coal.

"They want me upstream," he said. "Past Ket. All the way."

"For how long?"

"A season. Maybe two."

"And after?"

"After—" He laughed, and it wasn't quite a laugh. "After, Ansel, there might not *be* an after. There might be a map. Do you understand what a map does? A map makes a place real. Real places can't be quietly destroyed."

She'd understood him perfectly. That was the hell of it. She'd understood exactly what the map meant to him, and she'd understood exactly what it meant for her, and she'd stood there in the galley he'd rearranged and said, "Then go," because she was proud, and because she loved him, and because those two things were, in her experience, the same disease.

He'd kissed her forehead at the Graywater dock like a brother. Like a *brother*. Twenty years later she could still have killed him for it.

* * *

"Captain Dravenmoor." Fenwick had produced a second document while she wasn't looking, which was either very skillful of him or very damning of her. "Before you decide, there is a further matter."

"There always is."

"The company is prepared to make a formal statement regarding your father's expedition of twenty years ago."

The fan turned. The river muttered. Somewhere out on the landing a deckhand swore at a crate with real poetry.

Tomas Dravenmoor had come down off the mountain alone, half-starved, his boats gone, his crew scattered, insisting he'd found it — the source, the true source, a cavern springing from the rock itself, water rising clean and cold out of the earth's own bones. And the world had looked at him — at the empty hands, the no map, the no proof — and pronounced him a liar. Not mistaken. *Liars* were worse than mistaken; liars were a moral category. They'd taken his master pilot's license over it, and he'd died two years later of a bad heart and a worse bitterness, and Ansel had inherited the boat and the name and the shame, in that order.

"What statement," she said carefully.

"A retraction of the findings of the inquiry board. Restoration of his license posthumously. His name entered in the company registry as a certified discoverer of record." Fenwick folded his ironed hands. "Contingent, naturally, on your accepting the commission."

"That's not an offer," Ansel said. "That's a hook with bait on it."

"Yes," Fenwick agreed pleasantly. "We find bait works better than hooks alone."

She should have walked out. She knew every beat of how walking out went — the door, the street, the walk back to the Marguerite, the rest of her life — and she stood up halfway, chair scraping, hand flat on the desk, and then she looked down at the manifest again. Eleven names. One of them written in ink that hadn't faded because it had never had time to.

*If he's alive,* said a voice in her that sounded disgracefully like hope, *he's upstream. And if he's dead, he's upstream. Either way—*

Either way.

She sat back down. Fenwick, to his credit, pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her all day.

"One condition," she said. "The statement about my father goes in writing. Signed. Before I cast off, not after."

"Done."

"And I pick my crew."

"Also done. Within reason."

"And if I find survivors, I bring them out. If I find bodies, I bring those too. Nobody gets left up there to make the books tidier."

Fenwick paused for just long enough to confirm everything she suspected about the books. "Agreed."

She untied the red string herself, took the manifest, and folded it into her coat pocket, over the heart, which was sentimental and stupid and she did it anyway.

* * *

That evening she sat in the Marguerite's wheelhouse with a lamp lit and the manifest spread on the chart table, and she made herself read every name properly, because they weren't names anymore, they were people, and people deserved at least that much. Kroll, the surveyor-in-chief — she remembered the woman from a conference in Port Adelheid, sharp as a fishhook, laughed like a landslide. Oyelaran, the geologist. The students, barely more than children. She read their trades and their next-of-kin and tried not to imagine the letters that would need writing.

Then she read the marginal notes, the little administrative annotations in Fenwick's tidy hand, and found the one beside Marchetti's name:

*M. — volunteered to push ahead from camp 6. Last contact 19 days ago.*

Nineteen days. Alone. Upstream of an ambush the company wouldn't call an ambush, toward a source the company officially hoped didn't exist, carrying whatever he'd managed to draw so far.

Ansel put down the paper and looked out the wheelhouse window at the river, low and brown and patient in the dusk, dropping another inch toward the sandbars that would close it like a fist.

"He went ahead," she said to the empty wheelhouse, to the ghost sitting in the second chair where ghosts sit in wheelhouses. "Of course he went ahead. You never could wait for a chart to finish before you needed the next one."

The ghost, being a memory, grinned crookedly and said nothing.

She pulled her father's old charts from under the bunk — the ones the inquiry board had called fabrications, the ones she'd kept anyway, wrapped in oilcloth like relics, because a liar's daughter learns early that the difference between a lie and a secret is mostly who's asking — and unrolled the smuggler's channel past the Falls of Ket across the chart table, pinning its corners with the coffee tin and the lamp base and Ilya's manifest, weighted down by his own name.

"Fine," she told the river, the ghost, the folder, the dead man's license, the living man nineteen days silent. "Fine. But you're coming home this time. Both of you. I'm not making this trip twice."

Outside, the first dry wind of the dying season came down the valley, smelling of dust and distant smoke, and the Vell dropped another inch, and somewhere above the narrows a lantern might or might not be burning in a window no map had ever marked.

Ansel blew out the lamp and went to find an engineer fool enough to run the Falls by night.
