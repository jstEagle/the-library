## 3. Upstream Against the Clock

The Marguerite had not carried a crew in six years, and she made her opinions known about it.

Ansel felt them in the wheelhouse — a sulkiness in the old boat's steering, a reluctance in the engines that Boaz swore was nothing and Ansel knew was grief for the quiet. A riverboat is like a widow's house: it settles into its emptiness, and it resents company.

She had hired exactly two someones, and only because the ascent to the Falls of Ket could not be done alone. Not honestly done, anyway. She could have lied to herself about that the way she'd lied about so many things — that she was fine alone, that solitude was a choice and not a wound — but the Vell did not forgive liars, and neither, these days, did her knees.

Boaz Ferren came aboard at Grey Landing with a toolbag in one hand and a jug in the other, and looked over the Marguerite the way a man looks at a horse he intends to buy cheap.

"She leaks," he said.

"She weeps," Ansel corrected. "There's a difference. Leaking is a fault. Weeping is character."

"Character's what you call it when you can't afford the repair."

"That," said Ansel, "is the wisest thing anyone has ever said on this dock, and I include the sermon the bishop gave here in '09."

Boaz was sixty if he was a day, built like something the river had tried to drown twice and given up on. He'd fired boilers on the Vell back when there were nine boats running her instead of one, and he had the particular deafness of men who have spent forty years beside an engine — not deafness of the ear but of the temper. He asked no questions about why a woman would run the forbidden channel above the narrows in a dying season. He simply named a wage that was insulting, accepted the one Ansel offered, which was worse, and went below to introduce himself to the boiler with the tenderness other men reserved for grandchildren.

Tilly came aboard the way smoke comes under a door.

Ansel found her at dawn on the second morning, curled in the coil of rope by the bow gangway, small as a stowaway and twice as determined to be mistaken for cargo. Thirteen, maybe fourteen. Hair cropped short with what looked like sheep shears and a steady hand, which told Ansel something. Boots two sizes too big, which told her more.

"You're stealing my rope," Ansel said from the rail.

"I'm borrowing your rope," the girl said, without opening her eyes, "and your boat, technically, since we're being honest."

"And where are we going?"

"Wherever it doesn't have my uncle in it."

Ansel considered this. Downriver lay Tilly's uncle, presumably; upriver lay gunboats, rapids, and whatever had eaten the Corvus Survey whole. It said something about the girl's circumstances that upstream sounded like the mercy.

"Rule one," Ansel said. "When I say jump, you ask how high *after* you've jumped. Rule two: nobody touches the wheel. Rule three—" she paused, because rule three had been her father's, and saying it aloud always cost her something— "rule three is that the river gets the last word. Always. You just try to make sure it's a word you can live with."

Tilly opened one eye. "That's a strange rule."

"It's the only rule that's ever kept anyone alive on the Vell." Ansel turned for the wheelhouse. "Galley's aft. There's bread if Boaz hasn't found it. He will find it. That's another law of nature."

They cast off at noon, and the Marguerite turned her nose upstream into country that grew lonelier by the mile.

* * *

The drought had the river by the throat.

Ansel had known dry seasons all her life — knew their moods the way farmers know weather, the way her father had known them better than any man living or dead. But this one had a meanness to it. The Vell ran a third of her summer width, bony through the shallows, her sandbars exposed like the ribs of something starving. Where in June the water had shouldered through the middle passage at Kessel Bend in one brown muscle, now it split into three thin threads around gravel shoals that hadn't seen sun since before the war.

"Reading water's easy in flood," Ansel told Tilly, who had appointed herself second mate, lookout, and self-appointed critic of the captain's swearing. "Flood water tells you everything. It shouts. Drought water lies. It goes quiet and lets you think the deep channel is where it looks deep, and then it eats your hull."

"How do you read it, then?"

"You don't read it. You remember it." Ansel eased the wheel a hand's width starboard, feeling the current shift against the rudder like a cat changing its mind. "Every bend of this river, I've run since I was smaller than you. My father had me sounding with a pole before I could reach the wheel. The Vell changes every season, but she changes *from* somewhere. You hold both maps in your head — what she was and what she is — and the truth lives between them."

"Your father sounds like he was quite a man."

"He was quite a liar, according to the Company." She heard her own voice go flat and hated it. "He claimed he'd found the source of the Vell. No proof. No map that survived. They struck his name from the pilots' rolls for it, and twenty years of captains have repeated the story at every dockside table from here to the sea." She nodded at the water ahead. "So mind the shoal off the willow point, and don't believe everything drowned men's daughters tell you."

Tilly was quiet a moment. Then: "I believe you found the shoal."

It was such a small kindness, offered so plainly, that Ansel had to look hard at the willows for a while.

* * *

They made Kessel Bend by the third day and the telegraph relay at Harrow Flats by the fifth, and it was at Harrow Flats that the river stopped feeling like home.

The relay station sat on the cutbank where the line crossed the Vell — a little plank building with a mast, a glass insulator winking in the sun, and forty miles of wire strung on poplar poles marching away north toward the headwater country. Ansel had passed it a hundred times. The operator, a fat cheerful man named Prewitt, had once taught her to send her own name in Morse, and she had never once come by without him waving his hat from the porch like the river itself was greeting her.

The porch was empty. The door stood open. And the wire was down.

Not fallen — cut. She saw it from a quarter mile and knew it before they tied off, because a storm drops wire in tangles and loops, but a knife takes it clean, pole after pole after pole, a neat black seam unpicked across the whole visible length of the line. Someone had walked the bank with a blade and a purpose, and the silence that came down the wire was not the silence of neglect. It was the silence of a throat held shut.

"Prewitt?" Ansel called, before she could stop herself, though the open door had already answered.

Inside, the station was stripped of anything worth taking and left tidy otherwise — cups washed, chair pushed in, the operator's logbook gone from its shelf. Only the spare fuses remained, lined up on the desk in a neat row, and beside them a single playing card, face down.

Boaz picked it up and turned it over. The ace of spades. He set it back down without comment, which from Boaz was a paragraph.

"They wanted the line dark," Ansel said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "North of here, there's nothing on the wire worth cutting except news of the survey. Somebody didn't want the Corvus Expedition talking to anybody."

"Somebody," said Boaz, "with a lot of walking ahead of him and time to be neat about it."

Tilly stood in the doorway, and for once she didn't have a remark. She was looking at the card. "My uncle plays cards," she said quietly. "He leaves them places. When he wants people to know he's been by." She swallowed. "He works for men who do things quietly."

Ansel looked at the girl — really looked, at the sheared hair and the stolen boots and the fear worn smooth as river stone — and understood that she had taken aboard one more ghost than she'd bargained for.

"We'll talk about that later," she said gently. "Right now we're burning daylight."

* * *

The waystation at Cold Larder was next on the chart — a fueling stop, wood stacked, water cistern, a bunkhouse for pilots waiting out high or low water. It was eight miles above Harrow Flats, and Ansel covered those miles with the engine at full steam and both maps turning in her head, telling herself stories the whole way: wind took the roof in, squatters burned it for warmth, lightning, accident, accident, accident.

Cold Larder was still smoking.

Not much. Just a gray thread rising off the ruin of the bunkhouse, soft as incense, which meant the fire was hours old — days, maybe, banked deep in the wet timber, breathing slow. The fuel shed was untouched. The cistern was untouched. Whoever burned this place had burned exactly one building: the one with beds in it.

Ansel put the Marguerite in against the bank and went ashore with Boaz behind her and Tilly forbidden, therefore following at a distance of twelve feet.

The bunkhouse had been set alight from the inside, deliberately, with lamp oil — she could smell it still, sweet and wrong under the char. And it had been torched while occupied, or nearly. In the ashes by the doorway, half-fused by heat, lay a boot heel, a brass button stamped with the Corvus Survey's crow, and — Boaz found it, turning it over with a poker — a pocket watch, stopped at 11:40, its case sprung like a mouth mid-scream.

"Abandoned stations don't burn like this," Ansel said. Her hands were very steady, which was how she knew she was afraid. "Torched stations do. This wasn't neglect. This was a message written in fire, and the message is: *stay out.*"

"Or else," Boaz said.

"Or else what? What's the else? Who burns a survey crew and cuts a telegraph line over a *map*?" She kicked a charred beam, and it collapsed into sparks, and she stood looking at the little red eyes of the fire waking in the wreckage. "A map can't hurt anyone. It's ink. It's paper. It's—"

It's a claim, said a voice in her head that sounded like her father. A map is a fence. And fences make poor men of rich ones.

She thought of the telegram in her coat pocket, the Company's seal, the offer that included clearing her father's name. She thought of the ace of spades lying face-down on Prewitt's desk like a bet already placed.

Behind her, Tilly spoke from the edge of the clearing, her voice small and certain.

"Captain. The woodshed. Look at the woodpile."

Ansel turned. Stacked against the surviving wall of the fuel shed, arranged with the same terrible neatness as the fuses on Prewitt's desk, were seven sticks of dynamite, sweating in the shade, fused and ready, with a paper tag wired to the first of them.

She crossed the yard and read it. The handwriting was fine, clerkish, unhurried:

*For the boat that comes next.*

Boaz spat. Tilly went pale. And far upstream, faint on the wind off the headwaters, Ansel heard — or imagined she heard, and would spend the rest of the night deciding which — the long, rolling boom of blasting powder at work in the narrows of Ket.

Somebody was already changing the river.

"Hold onto something," Ansel said, and turned the Marguerite's nose toward the sound.
