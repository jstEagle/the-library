## 4. What the Rapids Keep

The Falls of Ket did not roar so much as *argue* — a long, low argument between the river and the mountain that had been going on since before either of them had names, and which, like all the best arguments, neither intended to win outright.

Ansel heard it from three miles off, the way you hear a neighbor's dog before you see the fence. She stood at the wheel of the *Marguerite* with her boot hooked on the brass rail and her teeth set in something that was not quite a smile.

"You hear that?" she asked Tilly, who was swabbing the deck with the enthusiasm of a girl who had discovered that work was preferable to whatever she'd run away from.

"Sounds like the mountain's losing," Tilly said.

"The mountain always loses. That's not the point." Ansel tapped the wheel spokes one by one, an old habit, counting her blessings the way other people counted money. "The point is what the mountain does on its way down."

Boaz emerged from the engine hatch wearing his usual expression — that of a man perpetually disappointed by machinery and mildly surprised it hadn't betrayed him yet. "She'll hold," he said, meaning the engine, meaning the boat, meaning possibly himself. "Barely."

"Barely is a speed," Ansel said. "We'll run it at barely."

They had been three days above the burned waystation, and the river had gone strange. The drought had dropped the Vell until sandbars showed their spines like sleeping cattle, and the cut telegraph line swung from its poles in the wind, humming a note that made Tilly's hackles rise every time they passed under it. Somebody had cut it clean, with pliers, not weather. Weather tears. Pliers *decide*.

Ansel hadn't said any of this aloud. She'd said it to the river instead, quietly, in the language of hands on the wheel, and the river had kept her secrets as it always did.

Now the light was going. She could have waited for morning — any sensible captain would wait for morning — but the Falls of Ket were only passable at high water, and the drought was stealing the river an inch a day. Wait for dawn and they might find the smuggler's channel too thin to float a coffin. Run it now, in the last grey light, with the channel memorized from a dead man's charts.

Her father's charts.

"Well," she said, to no one in particular, "he never ran it at night either."

"That's comforting," Boaz said.

"It wasn't meant to be."

* * *

The smuggler's channel ran hard against the far bank, a ribbon of deep water threading between rocks that the maps politely called "shallows" and her father's charts called, in his cramped handwriting, *"teeth."* You entered it on a falling starboard list, held the boat's nose within a boat's length of a shelf of black granite, and then — for ninety seconds that felt like a career — you trusted everything your father had ever taught you.

Ansel took her down into the throat of it.

The *Marguerite* leaned. The lamp at the bow threw a shaking coin of light across water that moved like something alive and annoyed. Tilly whooped once, high and involuntary, and then remembered herself and went silent, both fists white on the rail.

"Starboard rock!" Boaz shouted from the engine well.

"I see it. I've always seen it. I dreamed about it for twenty years." Ansel fed the wheel through her fingers, left, left, hold — *hold* — and the black granite slid past close enough to read the lichen on it, and then the river dropped out from under them.

The falls themselves were not a waterfall in the picture-book sense. They were a staircase the mountain had built in a bad mood: three ledges, each one a lie about how much air a boat needed beneath it. Her father had run them forty times. On the forty-first, the ledger said, the third ledge had changed its mind.

The first ledge hit like a fist under the keel. The second like a hand closing. And on the third, the drought-thinned water gave them less cushion than the charts promised, and the *Marguerite* struck, spun half around, and began shipping water over the starboard gunwale in great green armsfuls.

"Tilly!" Ansel shouted, because the girl had been standing exactly where her father used to stand when the water came aboard — forward, where the rail dipped lowest, where a man could lean out and feel useful right up until the moment the river reached up and took him.

That was how it had happened. That was the sentence she had carried for twenty years, worn smooth as river stone: *the water came over the rail, and he leaned out to fend it off, and the river reached up.*

Tilly leaned out.

The river reached up.

Later, Ansel would not remember deciding. She would remember the wheel leaving her hands — the *Marguerite* groaning free in the churning pool below the falls, momentarily masterless — and she would remember cold, and rope, and the specific terrible weight of another human being in moving water, which is heavier than cargo and lighter than grief.

Tilly went under once. Ansel got a fist in the girl's collar and hauled, and the current argued, and Ansel argued back with the particular foul-tempered eloquence of a woman who has already buried one person in this exact stretch of river and refuses to file the paperwork twice.

They came ashore on the gravel bar below the falls, coughing, tangled in the same rope, Tilly laughing the wild broken laugh of someone whose body has outrun her sense.

"You *leaned out*," Ansel gasped, furious, holding the girl by both shoulders. "Never lean out. Never. The river is faster than you are brave."

"Yes, Captain," Tilly wheezed, grinning.

"I mean it. I have lost—" Ansel stopped. The sentence sat there between them, enormous, and she stepped around it the way you step around a sleeping animal. "Never lean out."

Boaz brought the *Marguerite* down through the pool alone, listing, alive, her portside planking groaning a complaint she would voice for the rest of her days. He grounded her on the bar beside them and stood at the rail looking down at the two of them, soaked and rope-burned and breathing, and said nothing at all, which from Boaz was a eulogy of relief.

It was Tilly, wringing out her shirt, who saw it come down the chute.

"Captain. Look there."

* * *

Flotsam rode the tailrace below the falls: a shattered oar, a green glass float, a tangle of canvas. And a case — brass-cornered, leather-strapped, riding the eddy in slow circles like a man pacing outside a door.

Ansel waded out and took it. It was heavy with paper. The clasp was sprung; the river had done its best. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had mostly worked, lay a surveyor's field journal, and on the flyleaf, in a hand she knew better than her own —

*I.M., Corvus Survey, dry season, year nineteen.*

Ilya Marchetti.

She sat down on the gravel without meaning to. Boaz looked away with the tact of a man pretending to inspect the hull. Tilly did not look away, because Tilly was seventeen and had not yet learned that some doors are nailed shut for reasons.

The early pages were survey business — soundings, bearings, sketches of the valley walls drawn with a confidence that made her chest ache, because nobody drew the Vell like that except someone who loved it. Then the entries shortened. Then they changed.

She read the last pages by lamplight on the *Marguerite*'s deck, while Boaz hammered at the worst of the planking below and Tilly pretended very loudly to sleep.

*Day 31.* We are being followed. Not closely — they keep a day behind, sometimes two, and they make no fires. Voss says it's poachers. Poachers don't burn waystations. The station at Red Clay was torched, not abandoned — I walked the ash myself. Someone is erasing us as we go.

*Day 33.* Found a fresh-cut telegraph line today. Cut clean, with pliers. Marchetti, you're writing this like a paranoid man. Maybe paranoia is just pattern recognition with bad manners.

*Day 35.* They want the river blind. That's what Halvard wants — I found one of their men's tobacco tins at our old camp, the kind the Consolidated issues its concession crews. Our map is the only thing standing between the Vell and a company that holds concessions contingent on the source never being found. If we finish the chart, the valley becomes protected watershed. If we don't, or if we die trying, the source stays a rumor, and rumors can't stop a dredge.

*Day 36.* I've decided to go ahead alone from San Vered. Faster without the full party, and if they follow anyone, let them follow me. The others will hold at the mission. Tell whoever finds this — tell *her*, if it's her — that I finished it. That I finally chose.

Ansel closed the journal.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstream, the falls went on arguing with the mountain, patient as geology.

"He went ahead alone," she said, to the deck, to the river, to the nineteen-year-old ghost of herself waiting on a dock with her heart in a carpetbag. "Of course he did. Duty's call. He never could hang up on it."

Tilly's eyes were open after all. "Is he alive?"

"He's alive." Ansel turned the journal over in her wet hands. "Men who write 'tell her' are planning to say it themselves. Dead men write letters. Living men leave instructions." She stood, and the exhaustion in her legs rearranged itself into something with a direction. "Boaz! How's my hull?"

"She'll float," Boaz called back, "in the sense that things float when they refuse to sink."

"Good enough. We make the mission by tomorrow night." She looked upstream, where the dark had swallowed the narrows whole, and somewhere past the dark, a cathedral cavern and a man who had finally chosen. Behind them, somewhere in that same dark, men with pliers and kerosene and a company's patience were following the river up, erasing as they came.

They wanted the river blind.

Ansel tucked the journal inside her coat, against her heart, where her father's charts used to ride.

"They picked the wrong river," she said, and rang the engine bell twice — *ahead, slowly* — into the night.
