## 5. The Syndicate's Silence

The trading post at Corben's Landing had once been a fort, back when the Vell was a border and not a highway to nowhere. Now it was a long porch of sagging timber, a warehouse that smelled of salt pork and gunpowder, and a saloon with exactly one honest mirror and no honest patrons. Ansel tied the *Marguerite* to the pilings with the particular knots her father had taught her — the ones that could be cast off in a heartbeat or hold through a flood — and told Boaz and Tilly to stay aboard.

"Stay aboard," Tilly repeated, already halfway down the gangplank.

"Tilly."

"I heard you."

"You didn't listen. There's a difference. It's the difference between a river and a ditch." Ansel pointed at the deck. "Boaz keeps the boiler hot. You keep your hands off everything else. If anyone comes asking questions, we're a freight run hauling pickles."

"Pickles," said Tilly, delighted. "I've never been a pickle boat before."

"Nobody has. That's what makes it believable."

Ansel left them arguing about brine and walked up the plank into Corben's Landing, where the dust hung in the air like it had signed a lease. She needed rope, lamp oil, and news, though she suspected the news would cost more than the other two combined.

The man from Halvard Consolidated found her before she found the rope.

He was standing at the end of the dry-goods counter like he'd been set there by a stagehand — a tall, neat figure in a gray suit that had no business within two hundred miles of a river, his boots too clean, his hat too new. He had the kind of face that made you think of ledgers: balanced, ruled, and quietly adding you up.

"Captain Villalobos," he said. Not a question. He knew the boat; he'd known the name.

"That depends on who's asking and what for."

"Hollis Vane. I represent the Halvard Consolidated Mining Company." He offered a card. Ansel looked at it without taking it. It was heavy stock, cream-colored, absurd this far upriver. Somewhere downstream, trees had died to make that card, and none of them had been Vell cottonwood, which was the only tree that mattered here.

"We haven't met," she said.

"No. But I've read your file."

"My file."

"The company keeps files on everyone who matters on this river. Yours is thin but interesting. Captain's license at nineteen. Salvage work. Two rescues the government never thanked you for. And—" he smiled, gently, like a man laying down a winning card he hoped wouldn't embarrass her, "—a father who drowned chasing a fairy tale."

The saloon noise went on around them — dice, a bad piano, someone laughing too hard at nothing — but for Ansel the room went quiet as deep water.

"Careful," she said.

"I don't mean any insult. Quite the opposite. May I buy you a drink? The whiskey here is terrible, which is why I brought my own." He produced a flask and two tin cups from nowhere, the way such men do, and poured without waiting for permission. Ansel took the cup anyway. It seemed rude to refuse good whiskey on principle.

They sat at a corner table beneath a mounted fish that some optimist had labeled TROUT, 40 LBS, though it was plainly a carp that had lost a fight with its own ambitions.

"To the river," Vane said, raising his cup. "May she stay exactly as she is."

"Now that," said Ansel, "is an odd toast from a mining man."

"Is it?" He drank. "You've come upstream looking for the Corvus Survey. Nine souls, silent since midsummer. The company that hired you wants them found. Halvard Consolidated wants something simpler." He set the cup down and turned it slowly, a small precise orbit, like a man demonstrating how planets behave when money governs them. "We want them left alone."

"They're dying up there."

"Some of them may be. That's regrettable. But consider what they were doing. Charting the headwaters. Fixing the source of the Vell on paper. Once that happens, Captain, the concessions we hold become worthless overnight. Our charter requires the source to remain officially undiscovered. Do you understand? Not unexplored. Undiscovered. There are men in this territory whose entire fortune rests on a blank space on the map, and they will defend that blank space the way other men defend their children."

"And so you burned the waystation at Miller's Bend," Ansel said. "And cut the telegraph line."

Vane's expression did not change, which was itself an answer. "The waystation caught fire. Telegraph lines fall. Rivers are hard on infrastructure." He leaned forward slightly. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I respect you enough not to haggle. Turn back. Report the survey lost to the rapids — which will likely prove true regardless. Halvard will pay you triple your contract, plus salvage rights to the *Marguerite*'s route monopoly for ten years. No one else runs the upper Vell but you. You'd never have to fight the drought season again. You could name your own water."

It was, Ansel admitted to herself, a beautiful offer. She thought of the *Marguerite* with her patched hull and her tired boiler, of Boaz grumbling over parts he couldn't afford, of winters counting nails because nails cost money. Triple pay. Ten years of quiet.

Then she thought of the journal in her cabin, wrapped in oilcloth, Ilya's handwriting marching across the pages like a man running. *Men follow us. They want the river blind.*

"And if I refuse?"

Vane sighed, genuinely regretful, which was somehow worse than menace. "Then I'm instructed to tell you a story. An old one." He turned the cup again. "Twenty-two years ago, a captain came to this same landing. A big man, loud, half the river loved him and the other half owed him money. He was running charts of the headwaters for the Crown Survey. And a representative of our company — a predecessor of mine, in this very chair — offered him the same terms I'm offering you. Triple pay. Route monopoly. Go home, Captain, and grow rich."

Ansel's hands had gone still on the table. She hadn't told them to.

"He refused," Vane went on softly. "Said the river belonged to everybody who floated her, and maps were how you kept rivers free. Brave words. Beautiful words. Three weeks later he drowned in the Falls of Ket, alone, in a boat no wreck was ever found from. The company paid for the search. We're generous that way." He looked up, and his eyes were not cruel — that was the terrible thing, they were simply clear, like water over a drop-off. "Your father took the same meeting you're taking now, Captain. Same chair. Same whiskey. He chose the map. And the river kept him."

The piano player hit a wrong note and laughed at himself. Someone ordered eggs at midnight. The world went on being ordinary while Ansel sat in it with her heart going like a paddlewheel in shallow water.

Because she had grown up on the official story: her father, reckless, chart-mad, pushing past sense into the falls, killed by his own stubbornness. She had carried that story like a stone in her chest for twenty years — not grief exactly, but shame's ugly cousin, the belief that he had chosen the river over coming home to her. That he had loved the blank space more than the girl waiting at the dock.

And it had been a lie. A purchased lie. He had refused their money, and they had killed him for it, and then let the whole valley believe he'd simply been a fool.

She understood, sitting there with the taste of good whiskey going bitter, exactly what had killed him. Not the falls. The falls were just where they'd put the body.

"Captain," Vane said, and there was something almost kind in it, "your father was not a fool. He was an honest man, and honest men drown at a remarkable rate on this river. Don't join him. Take the money. Grow old. Some secrets are worth more than the truth."

Ansel stood. Her legs worked, which surprised her.

"Mr. Vane," she said, "you should know something about me. My father taught me every smuggler's channel between here and the source. Every one. He drew them out for me when I was nine years old, on the back of a flour sack, because he said someday somebody would try to make the river blind and his girl would need to see." She picked up the cream-colored card from the counter, weighed it a moment, and dropped it into the spittoon. "So no. Keep your triple pay. And tell whoever holds your leash that the Villalobos family does its own arithmetic."

Vane rose too, unhurried, and straightened his too-new hat. For a moment something moved behind the ledger of his face — not anger. Pity, maybe. Or fear wearing pity's coat.

"I truly hope you find them," he said. "All of them. Alive." And then, at the door, without turning: "The narrows above Ket have been strange this season, Captain. High water where there should be low. Men report lights. Gunboats have no business up there, of course. But then, neither do you."

He stepped out into the dust and was gone, leaving the flask behind. Ansel stared at it. Even his generosity felt like surveillance.

* * *

Back aboard, Tilly was sitting cross-legged on the hatch cover, and Boaz stood at the rail with his arms folded, and both of them were looking at her with the expressions of people who had watched a conversation through a window.

"Well?" said Boaz. "Do we haul pickles or don't we?"

"We run upstream," Ansel said. "Tonight, if the boiler holds."

Boaz nodded slowly, unsurprised, and spat over the side with ceremony. "Figured. Man in the gray suit came aboard this morning while you were ashore. Looked at the engine room. Asked a lot of friendly questions." He patted the boiler housing the way another man might pat a dog. "Tilly chased him off."

"I threw a wrench," Tilly said proudly. "Near him. Not at him. Near. Very intimidating."

Ansel looked upriver, where the Vell ran silver and narrow toward the darkening hills, toward the falls, toward whatever waited above them in the strangeness. Her father had sat in that chair. Her father had said no. And the river had kept him — not as punishment, but as evidence.

"Light the lamps," she said. "We go through Ket in the dark. And Tilly—"

"Aye, Captain?"

"Clip yourself to something. Tonight I won't have time to catch you twice."

Above the landing, far up the black shoulder of the gorge, a light blinked once — steady, deliberate, nothing like a star — and went out.
