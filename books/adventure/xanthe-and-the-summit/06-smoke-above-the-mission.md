## 6. Smoke Above the Mission

The journal said *follow the bell*, and for two days there was no bell, only the river talking to itself in the shallows and Boaz swearing at the starboard boiler in a language he claimed was engineering.

"Third time she's coughed like that," he said, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last year. "She's telling us something."

"She's telling us she's twenty years old and running water no boat should run," Ansel said. "I know the feeling."

Tilly sat cross-legged on the cargo hatch with Ilya's field journal open on her knees. The girl had appointed herself keeper of it somewhere above the burned waystation, and Ansel had let her, because reading it aloud meant Ansel didn't have to. It was a cowardice she'd examined honestly and decided to keep.

"Read it again," Boaz said. "The mission part."

Tilly cleared her throat. She read the way she did everything now — carefully, like someone who'd learned that words could be supplies you rationed. "'The mission at San Vered shows on none of the company charts. Father Esteban's maps were confiscated when the order dissolved, which suits Halvard fine. A building that isn't on paper can't shelter anyone worth shooting.'"

Ansel felt that one settle into her chest like river damp.

"'We make for it tomorrow,'" Tilly continued. "'Marchetti says the bell tower is visible from three bends down if the mist lifts. He says—'" Tilly stopped.

"He says what?" Ansel asked.

"He says the bell still rings. On its own, some evenings. Wind through the crack in it, probably." She looked up. "Do you believe that?"

"I believe wind," Ansel said. "Wind I've met."

But that evening, rounding the third bend with the light going copper and the mist lifting off the water exactly as promised, Ansel heard it. One note, cracked and hollow, rolling down the valley like a rumor. Boaz stopped mid-swear. Even the boiler seemed to listen.

"There's your wind," Tilly whispered.

* * *

They tied off below a shelf of rock where the Marguerite couldn't follow — the channel narrowed to a throat of white stone, and beyond it the river climbed in steps too steep for any hull. Ansel took the journal, a rifle, and two days of hardtack, and told Boaz to keep steam up and keep Tilly aboard.

Tilly had opinions about that. Tilly always had opinions about that. But even she went quiet looking up at the ridge, where — thin as a signature — a thread of smoke stood against the evening sky.

Smoke meant people. Smoke also meant somebody who hadn't bothered to hide their fire, which meant either fools or desperate ones, and after four days of cut telegraph lines and burned waystations, Ansel had learned the difference mattered less than she'd like.

The climb took an hour through ferns wet enough to drown in. The mission appeared all at once, the way ruins do — one moment forest, the next a courtyard of pale stone gone soft with moss, a roof half-collapsed over the nave, the bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle at a wedding. And in the doorway of what had been a refectory, a man stood holding a broken oar the way a soldier holds a pike.

"Don't come closer," he said. His voice was a ruin itself. "I'm armed."

"You're holding half an oar," Ansel said.

"I'm holding *half an oar with intent.*"

Something in her chest unclenched for the first time since the Falls. Only survey men talked like that with a week of starvation behind them. "Corvus Survey?" she said. "I'm looking for the Corvus Survey."

The oar lowered an inch. Behind him, in the shadowed refectory, two shapes stirred — a woman, and a boy so young his beard was more rumor than fact.

"Who sent you?"

"The company. Nobody else would come." She held up the journal. "And this did. Ilya Marchetti's hand. Do you know him?"

The man stared at the journal a long time. Then he sat down right where he stood, on the threshold, like a puppet with its strings cut, and began to laugh — a terrible sound, dry as the drought.

"We left him at the fork," he said. "God forgive us. We left him at the fork and he waved us goodbye like we were going to market."

* * *

His name was Aldous Wren, assistant surveyor, and the woman was Dr. Pramila Osei, expedition botanist; the boy was Fen, a chainman of seventeen who had lied about his age to see the source of the Vell and had seen considerably more than he'd bargained for. They fed Ansel bitter tea made from mission-grown herbs while they told it, and she ate nothing, because watching them eat made appetite feel obscene.

"It was the third week out," Wren said. "Past the narrows. The canyon pinches there — single file along the towpath, cliffs on both sides. Captain Reyes went first with the mules." He turned his cup in his hands. "There was a slide. Rockfall, we thought. Half the path went into the river with the captain and two of the porters and most of our food."

"Rockfall," Ansel repeated.

Dr. Osei looked at her. She had a surgeon's eyes, flat and unhurried. "The slope above the path had been drilled," she said. "I found the charge holes afterward, when we climbed back up to bury what we could. Plugged with clay to look natural. Someone wanted the Corvus Survey to have an accident."

Fen spoke from the corner, very quietly. "It wasn't an accident."

"No," Osei said. "It wasn't."

"How many?" Ansel asked.

"Eleven dead," Wren said. "Of fourteen. We ran. That's the whole of my report and I'll sign it in my own blood if the company wants it pretty. We ran upstream because downstream was where they'd be watching, and we found this place, and we've been eating ferns and praying to a God whose jurisdiction here is unclear."

"And Marchetti?"

Wren and Osei exchanged a look that Ansel recognized — the look people share when the truth belongs to someone not present.

"He went back down," Osei said finally. "To the slide. Said he needed to survey it properly, document the drill holes, take measurements. We told him he was mad. The men who set the charges would come back to check their work."

"What did he say?"

Osei almost smiled. "He said whoever they were, they couldn't shoot straighter than he could draw. Then—" she hesitated, "—then he said something I've thought about every day since. He said, 'Tell her the map matters more than the man. She'll understand.' We didn't know who *her* was."

Ansel set down her tea. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "Did he finish it? The map."

"He never came back to tell us. But Ilya Marchetti doesn't leave work unfinished. If he went on toward the source alone—" Osei shrugged, a small, exhausted gesture. "Then the map exists, or he died making it. With him, those are the same thing."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the cracked bell, and the tower gave out its one broken note, and Fen flinched at it and then pretended he hadn't.

"They'll come back," Ansel said. "For you. Loose ends. You've seen the drill holes; you know it was murder. How long has your smoke been visible?"

"Since we stopped caring," Wren said simply. "About a week ago. We figured if anyone friendly saw it, better for us. If anyone unfriendly saw it—" he gestured at his half-oar, "—intent."

Ansel stood and walked to the ruined window. Below, far down, the Marguerite's smoke answered the mission's, two threads rising from the same wounded valley. Twenty years ago her father had stood in country like this and drawn a source nobody believed, and men had paid to make sure nobody ever confirmed it, and when he wouldn't stop saying it was real they had taken his charts and his reputation and eventually, by way of a river, his life. She had nailed that door shut. She had built a whole quiet life against it.

And now here was Ilya's voice coming through the boards anyway: *the map matters more than the man.*

"You're leaving," Fen said. Not accusing. Just knowing.

"I'm going upstream," Ansel said. "There's a fork past the slide. If he finished the survey, that's where he went — toward the headwaters, alone, carrying the only document in the world that proves what your eleven died for and what my father drowned for." She turned back to them. "You can't stay here. Your smoke's a beacon and your oar won't argue with rifles. Come down to the boat."

Boaz would complain. Boaz complained the way rivers ran — constantly, and yet somehow essential. Three starving surveyors would strain the Marguerite's stores to breaking, and the dry season was closing the upper river like a fist, and somewhere behind them Halvard's men were checking their work.

"Captain Villalobos," Dr. Osei said, rising with the careful economy of the starving, "why are you doing this? The company pays you to find us, not to feed us. And the company, I suspect, would rather we stayed lost."

Ansel looked at her a long moment.

"Because twenty years ago a man drew a map of this river's heart," she said, "and they called him a liar until the river killed him. Because the man who wrote this journal once chose a map over me, and I intend to ask him to his face whether it was worth it. And because—" she picked up the journal, tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, where maps apparently lived now, "—because somebody has to carry the truth out of these headwaters, and I am extremely stubborn."

Wren started laughing again, that dry terrible laugh, but this time it sounded almost like hope.

They struck camp in the grey of dawn. Fen carried Dr. Osei's satchel; Wren carried the broken oar, out of what he called principle and Ansel suspected was grief. At the edge of the courtyard Ansel paused and looked back once — at the leaning tower, the moss-eaten stones, the bell that rang for no congregation.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, drifting up the valley from downstream:

A motor. Not one engine. Several, running low and steady, the way gunboats idle when they think they haven't been heard yet.

Boaz was going to have opinions about this too.
