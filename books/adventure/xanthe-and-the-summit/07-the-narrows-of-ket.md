## 7. The Narrows of Ket

The gunboats came at dusk, the way bad news always does.

Ansel saw them first from the wheelhouse — two black shapes sliding down the long reach below the narrows, their lanterns hooded, their wakes too straight for fishermen. Nobody fished the Vell at dusk in a drought year. Nobody poled against the current with that patient, deliberate rhythm unless they were being paid to.

"Boaz," she called down the engine hatch. "Company."

Boaz's head appeared, gray-stubbled and unimpressed, a wrench still in his fist like an extension of his arm. "Halvard?"

"Halvard."

"How many?"

"Two. Maybe three behind the point." She watched the shapes a moment longer. "They're not hurrying. They think we're already theirs."

Tilly scrambled up onto the deck from where she'd been mending the jib — a job she did badly but enthusiastically — and squinted downstream. "Those don't look friendly."

"They're not paid to be friendly, girl. They're paid to be thorough." Boaz spat over the rail. "There's a difference, and it's mostly in the invoice."

Ansel let herself feel the fear for exactly as long as it took to walk from the window to the chart table. Then she folded it up small, the way her father had taught her to fold charts, and put it away.

Because under the chart table's false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were her father's secret charts. The smuggler's maps. The ones the Corvus Company had never seen, the ones Halvard Consolidated would burn a valley to keep out of official hands.

She spread the narrows sheet across the table and weighted its corners with the brass lamp, the coffee pot, and Tilly's slingshot, which the girl surrendered without comment.

* * *

The Narrows of Ket were not on any honest map. The river there pinched between walls of black limestone no wider than a wagon road, and for three miles it ran fast, blind, and mean through a country of overhanging rock that ate echoes and gave back nothing useful. The Corvus Survey had marked the whole stretch with a single dismissive line: *impassable.*

Her father had thought that was funny. *Impassable means nobody's looked,* he used to say, tapping his temple. *And nobody looking is the finest kind of door.*

Now she traced the channel with one finger: enter on the left bank past the split boulder shaped like a kneeling monk; hard to starboard at the first echo; then a sequence of turns her father had annotated in pencil so old it had gone brown — *listen here*, *trust the smell of water*, *pray if you must but steer while you do it.*

"You're smiling," Tilly said suspiciously. "People don't smile at charts like that."

"My father drew this when I was nine years old. He used to hold me up on his shoulders in the wheelhouse and make me call out the turns from memory until I could do it asleep." Ansel smoothed the paper flat. "I hated him for it half my life. Turns out he was just leaving me a door."

Boaz leaned over the table, reading upside down as engineers do. "Three miles of that in the dark, at drought water, with gunboats behind us." He scratched his jaw. "I've run boilers through worse. But not much worse, and none of it on purpose."

"We're not going through in the dark," Ansel said. "We're going through at moonrise. There'll be enough light to read the water, and dark enough that they can't see our wake." She looked up. "But hear me plain, both of you. The Marguerite will not come out of there whole. Those walls will take her hull like a whetstone takes an edge. We'll lose supplies. We may lose more than supplies."

Tilly set her chin at the stubborn angle Ansel had come to dread and secretly admire. "You're not leaving anybody behind."

"I'm not planning to. Planning and promising are different animals, and only fools confuse them."

"Well then plan harder," Tilly said.

Boaz laughed — a short bark, quickly strangled, because laughing hurt these days; his ribs had never quite forgiven the Falls of Ket. "Captain," he said, "I've been paid off by better companies than this one and abandoned by worse. Where you steer, I'll stoke. Just tell me one thing."

"What's that?"

"When we get through — what's left to eat? Because I saw you eyeing the salt pork, and I want it noted now, before the rationing starts, that I have seniority."

* * *

They entered the narrows an hour after moonrise, with the Marguerite stripped to her bones — deck cargo lashed low, the stove banked, every loose thing tied down twice. The moon stood high and white over the eastern rim, and the river below ran black as poured ink, except where it broke white over stone.

Ansel took the wheel herself. Some things you didn't delegate, and some doors you opened yourself or not at all.

Behind them, far back down the reach, a lantern kindled on the lead gunboat. Then another. A voice carried faintly across the water — a hail, formal and absurd, ordering the steamboat *Marguerite* to heave to in the name of the Halvard Consolidated Mining Company.

"Heave to," Boaz repeated, feeding coal with a tenderness most men saved for children. "That's sweet. Like asking a river to stand still."

"Answer him," Ansel said.

Tilly blinked. "What?"

"Answer him. Politely. Shout something back."

Tilly considered, cupped her hands, and bellowed downriver with the full power of a fourteen-year-old runaway who'd spent a season learning to swear among boatmen: *"WE'D LOVE TO BUT WE'RE BUSY!"*

Somewhere behind them, faintly, a man repeated *busy?* in a tone of pure bafflement, and Ansel laughed despite everything, and the sound of it echoed off the black walls and was eaten.

Then the walls closed in, and there was no more time for anything but the river.

* * *

The kneeling monk boulder rose out of the moon-shadow on the left, exactly where her father's pencil said it would be, and grief hit her sideways — sudden, physical, like striking a wave wrong. Twenty years dead, and the man was still steering her boat. She put the wheel hard over and felt the current take them, and whispered, *all right, Papa, I'm listening,* and the Marguerite swung into the first turn with six inches to spare on either side.

It got worse from there.

The channel twisted like a snake in a sack. Rock walls slid past close enough to touch — close enough that once, horribly, they did touch, a grinding shudder along the starboard side that made Boaz howl something unrepeatable from the engine room and made the whole hull ring like a struck bell. Water came in over the rails in cold sheets. Tilly clung to the cargo lashings and called out the turns from the chart, her voice cracking and steadying, cracking and steadying, and Ansel found she loved the girl with a fierceness that surprised her each time she noticed it.

At the second echo — *listen here* — she cut the engine to half and let the boat drift, and in the hush they heard it: the deep hollow boom of water falling into a hidden chamber somewhere ahead, the sound her father had written beside the penciled arrow: *the river swallows a creek here. Do not follow the swallow.*

"Starboard," she breathed. "Hard starboard."

The Marguerite groaned around the turn, and the hidden creek's mouth yawned past on the left, hungry and black, and Tilly made a small involuntary sound and gripped the rail.

Then the third mile, the worst of it, where the channel braided through drowned rock and the moon kept slipping behind cloud like a coward. Twice they grounded — soft, merciful groundings on gravel teeth that scraped free again with the engine backing full. Once a deadfall swept down out of the dark and Boaz caught it on the bow with a boathook and held it, cursing steadily and creatively, until the current peeled it away.

And through all of it, growing behind them, the sound of the gunboats' engines — patient, powerful, closing.

* * *

They came out of the narrows near midnight into a wide, quiet valley where the moon lay on the water like spilled milk, and Ansel allowed herself ten seconds of pure relief before she took inventory.

The inventory was ugly. The Marguerite's starboard hull had been opened along four feet of planking above the waterline and bruised badly below it; she was taking water that Boaz's pumps fought in shifts. Half their deck cargo was gone — torn loose in the third-mile rapids, including, Boaz reported with the gravity of a war correspondent, "most of the flour, all of the coffee, and the good rope."

"The good rope," Tilly mourned.

"We'll live," Ansel said. "Badly, but we'll live."

But the gunboats hadn't stopped at the narrows' mouth. Of course they hadn't. Halvard's men knew these waters too — or knew them well enough — and the lead boat's lantern was already visible far astern, working up the last bend, maybe forty minutes back.

Forty minutes. The Marguerite, holed and heavy, couldn't outrun a rowboat tonight.

"There," Tilly said suddenly, pointing upstream ahead of them, where the valley pinched toward the headwater gorges. "Smoke."

A thin thread of it, rising from a shelf of rock above the left bank. A campfire, banked low. And beside it, barely visible, a figure standing watch — and the figure raised a lantern, swung it twice, deliberately. Signal. Not Halvard's men; Halvard's men wouldn't signal a leaking steamboat.

"That'll be the survivors' picket," Ansel said slowly. "From the mission. Someone watching the river." Her heart did something complicated. If survivors were moving upstream toward Ilya — if they knew the paths —

The gunboat's lantern grew brighter astern.

"Captain," Boaz said quietly. "Decision time."

Ansel looked at her wounded boat, her empty cargo deck, her two ragged crew. Then at the shelving bank, and the smoke, and the swinging lantern.

"Bring her alongside that shelf," she said. "Gentle. We're putting ashore."

"And the gunboats?"

"They'll find an empty boat on a gravel bar and a cold trail into country their boats can't follow." She was already pulling the false bottom off the chart table, wrapping her father's maps in oilcloth, strapping them tight. "Let them keep the river. We're done needing it."

They came alongside rough and fast, and Tilly leapt to the rocks with a line, and they began to unload what little remained — and that was when the shot cracked off the valley wall above them, flat and echoing, and a voice shouted from the dark upstream: *Down! Get down!*

Boaz shoved Tilly behind the boiler housing and turned to shout a warning to Ansel — and the second shot took him high in the shoulder, spinning him off the gangway into the shallows, where Ansel reached him in three strides and dragged him up the bank with her arms burning and her heart gone cold and clear as ice water.

Above them, on the ridge, muzzle flashes stuttered. Below, the lead gunboat rounded the bend, lantern blazing, searchlight sweeping the water.

No going back now. There was nothing left to go back *to* — and ahead, up that smoking trail, someone was shooting at Halvard's men, which meant someone ahead was on their side.

"Tilly!" Ansel shouted over the gunfire, hauling Boaz's arm across her shoulders. "Get his other arm. We climb."
