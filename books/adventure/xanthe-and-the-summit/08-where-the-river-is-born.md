## 8. Where the River Is Born

The trail ended where all trails above the narrows ended: at a wall of stone that had no business standing there. Ansel had climbed since dawn — Tilly's small boots scuffing behind her, Boaz limping and swearing in a low liturgical rhythm he seemed to find comforting — and now the gorge simply stopped, as if the river had walked into a church and shut the door.

"Is that it?" Tilly whispered.

Ansel didn't answer. She was listening. Beneath the wind through the pines there was another sound, older, steadier — water moving under stone, vast and unhurried, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Her father had drawn this place from memory once, on the back of a supply invoice, drunk on fever and certainty: *a door in the rock, Ansie, green as bottle glass.* The company surgeons had called it delirium. The inquiry had called it fraud. She had called it grief, for twenty years, and built her life on top of the grief like a house on a filled-in well.

She put her hand against the rock face. It was cold. And wet. And faintly, unmistakably, humming.

"There," she said. "Behind there."

* * *

Ilya Marchetti had given up counting days somewhere around the point where counting days stopped being a discipline and started being a cruelty. He had instead taken up conversation with the cavern, which was patient, and with the map, which was not.

"You're finished," he told it. It lay rolled in oilcloth across his knees, and it did not dignify him with an answer. "You're the finest thing I'll ever make and you're going to get someone killed. We have that in common."

He had eaten the last of the survey rations eleven days ago, or perhaps nine; hunger made arithmetic soft. He had eaten worse things since, things the cavern offered without judgment. His beard had opinions of its own now. When he caught his reflection in the spring pool — a gaunt man in a ruined coat, eyes too bright — he tipped an imaginary hat to himself, because if he didn't laugh he would do the other thing, and the other thing was a luxury the map could not afford.

Then he heard footsteps, and voices, and one voice in particular, and every clever thing he had ever said left him at once.

* * *

The crack in the rock was barely wider than a person. Ansel went through sideways, Tilly after her, into darkness that opened — opened is the only word — into a space so large her lantern couldn't find its ceiling. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some drowned organ. And at the heart of it, rising out of living rock itself, the spring: a pool of impossible green, welling up silent and ceaseless, feeding a river that ran a thousand miles and had never once been seen being born.

"Oh," said Tilly, very quietly. "Oh, missus Captain."

Ansel's father had seen this. Had stood exactly here. Had gone home and told the truth and been destroyed for it, and she felt the disgrace fall off him like a coat dropping, twenty years late, and had to press her knuckles to her mouth to keep from making a sound.

Then the sound came anyway, because a figure rose from behind a shelf of rock with a rusted survey pistol held in both shaking hands, and the figure was Ilya Marchetti, alive, filthy, half-starved, and pointing a gun at her.

"Don't—" he began, and then his voice broke clean in half. "*Ansel.*"

"Put that down before you hurt yourself," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because nothing else about her was.

He lowered the pistol as though his arms had forgotten how. He looked at her the way a drowning man looks at a rope — and then, because he was still Ilya, he looked past her shoulder. "Boaz? You brought *Boaz*?"

"Somebody had to complain the whole way up."

"And that's—" He squinted at Tilly. "That's a child."

"I'm a deckhand," Tilly said with dignity.

"She's a stowaway we've decided to keep." Ansel crossed the distance between them before either of them could make it ceremonial. She meant to hit him. That had been the plan, more or less, for two hundred miles: reach the source, find him alive, hit him. Instead her hands closed on the lapels of his ruined coat and just held on, and he folded into her like something long dried-out finally given water, and they stood there in the cathedral of the river's birth while Tilly studied the stalactites with tremendous tact.

"You're real," Ilya said into her hair. "I'd started talking to the map. It doesn't answer but it listens better than Corvus did."

"The map." She pulled back and looked at him properly — the hollows of his cheeks, the fever-brightness. "Ilya. The others. The mission survivors said—"

"Ambush at the second bend. Halvard men dressed as river pirates." His voice went flat and careful, a man walking on ice over deep water. "Nine of them. I ran. I ran *up*, because the map wasn't finished, because if I died unfinished then they'd died for paper." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "So I sat in a hole in the earth drawing contour lines while my colleagues were being buried in shallow water. Tell me again how steady your hands are, Captain. Ask me what I'm worth."

Ansel took his face in both hands. "You're worth the truth getting out. That's all any of us are worth, and it happens to be everything."

* * *

Later — after Tilly had coaxed a fire from driftwood the spring had coughed up over centuries, after Boaz had produced from his pack a tin of coffee with the reverence of a priest producing relics — Ilya unrolled the map by firelight, and Ansel understood at last what her father had died trying to give the world.

It was beautiful. It was complete. Every channel, every fall, every depth sounding of the upper Vell, rendered in Ilya's precise, loving hand — and at its head, drawn with a cartographer's restraint and a lover's tenderness, the cavern itself. The source. Undiscovered no longer.

"Halvard's concessions expire the day this is filed," Ilya said. "Every claim they hold is contingent on the source staying a rumor. So they killed nine people to keep it a rumor, and they will kill whoever carries this out." He pushed the roll toward her across the stones. "Which is why it has to be you."

"No."

"Ansel—"

"*No.*" The word cracked off the cavern walls. "You don't get to hand me your death wrapped in flattery. I carried enough of you downriver once already, Marchetti. I'm not doing it twice."

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair! Your map, my boat, my father's name — none of it!" She was on her feet now, and the fire threw her shadow huge against the organ-pipes of stone. "You want to know what I learned on this river? Everything costs. The only question is whether you choose the price or it chooses you."

Boaz cleared his throat into the silence. "Begging pardon, Captain. But if nobody carries the map out, them nine folks died for paper after all. Seems to me somebody's got to be brave, and I'd volunteer, only I'm old and slow and my leg's shot, so really I'd just be slowing the brave one down."

Tilly raised a hand. "I'm fast."

"You're twelve," said Ansel and Boaz, together.

Ilya watched Ansel across the fire, and something in his face shifted — the madness draining out of it, leaving the man she had loved, the man who had chosen duty upstream and hated himself for it ever since. "Then we carry it together," he said quietly. "All four of us, or none. I won't spend anyone else's life buying mine meaning. I've done the arithmetic, Ansel. It doesn't come out."

She sat back down beside him. Shoulder to shoulder, the way they used to stand at the Marguerite's rail, a lifetime ago, when the future was a country neither of them had charted.

"Together," she agreed. "But not tonight. Nobody travels that gorge in the dark."

"Captain's right," Boaz said, already lying back with a groan of luxurious surrender. "Coffee first. Heroism after."

* * *

Ilya woke in the small hours to Ansel's breathing beside him and the spring's endless hush, and for one whole minute he let himself be happy — actually happy, a sensation so foreign he examined it like a specimen.

Then he heard it.

Engines. Far off, down the gorge, muffled by stone but unmistakable — the flat, greedy growl of Halvard gunboats running the narrows at night, which no sane pilot did, which meant they had found themselves a guide who knew the river and feared the company more than the water.

He reached over and shook Ansel's shoulder.

"Wake up," he whispered. "They're coming. And Ansel — they're bringing blasting stores' worth of trouble. Whatever we're going to do with the truth, we have about a morning to do it."
