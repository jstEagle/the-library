## 9. The Drowning Men

They came at dawn, the way men come when they mean to leave nothing behind but paperwork.

Ansel heard them before she saw them — a low, wrong sound in the gorge below the source cavern, an engine note that had no business being there. The Vell above the narrows was supposed to be hers alone. She knew every voice the river made: the chuckle of shallow gravel, the groan of deep pools turning over in their sleep, the hiss of the spring itself breathing in the dark of the cathedral rock. This was none of those. This was iron, coming upstream.

She stood at the mouth of the cavern with Ilya's map pressed against her ribs and counted the sound's meaning the way her father had taught her to count water: two gunboats, shallow draft, armored pilothouses, and behind them a barge riding low with something heavy aboard. Something that would be used to bring down the roof of the world.

"They're early," Ilya said behind her. He was thin as a winter branch, three months of cave-dark and guilt worn into him, but his eyes were clear this morning — clearer than she'd seen them since she'd pulled him out of his own madness two days ago. Clear, and afraid in the way that meant he'd already done the arithmetic.

"Rivers run early when they're paid to," Ansel said. "Halvard dredged the approach channel. That's how they got boats up here in drought season."

"Then we run."

"Where?" She turned to look at him. Behind them, Tilly was sleeping against Boaz's shoulder inside the cavern, both of them bandaged, both of them half-dead from the narrows, and the spring sang its ancient song over all of it like it didn't know it was about to be famous or dead. "There's no downstream anymore. They closed it behind us. There's no up. There's just this room."

Ilya came to stand beside her at the lip of the rock. Morning light came down the gorge in a long gold blade, and far below, small and certain as beetles, the gunboats nosed into view around the last bend.

"I have blasting stores," he said quietly. "The expedition's. We carried them for survey work — cutting sightlines, sounding bedrock. Forty cases. Maybe fifty. I hid them when the ambush came. I've been sitting on them for three months like a man hoarding dynamite instead of food." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Which is what I was doing."

Ansel looked at the gorge. It was a throat, that was the truth of it — a narrow stone throat the river poured through to reach the source basin, with walls sixty feet high and one road along the rim, and the gunboats would have to pass through it single file to reach the cavern pool. Her father had called it the Kettle Spout, back when he'd been alive and lying about maps. *Water goes in angry, comes out furious.*

"How much rock is above that channel?" she asked.

"Enough," Ilya said. "If you put the charges right."

"And if I put them wrong?"

"Then we die tired instead of wet."

* * *

They worked through the morning while the engines grew louder, and it was strange, Ansel thought, how familiar it felt — the two of them moving together again after twenty years of separate lives, handing each other fuses and drilling augers, falling back into the old rhythm of a survey camp where they'd been young and stupid and certain the world would make room for them. He'd hold the lamp. She'd set the charge. Neither of them said anything about it, because saying it would break it.

Boaz, propped against the cavern wall with his arm in a sling, watched them and offered exactly one piece of engineering advice, which was: "Don't blow yourselves up, Captain. I've grown accustomed to your face."

Tilly wouldn't stay inside. The girl scrambled along the rim rocks hauling fuse coil like she'd been born to it, quick and bright and refusing, absolutely refusing, to be cargo. "You'll want the east ledge cleared first," she said, breathless, "or the fall of it blocks the pool outlet and drowns the cavern from inside. My da worked a slate quarry. I know how rock falls."

"She's right," Ilya said, startled.

"Of course she's right," Ansel said. "Runaways always are. It's why they ran."

By noon the charges were set — forty cases of the expedition's dynamite tucked into the seams of the gorge walls on both sides, timed fuses running back to a firing point on the high shelf above the pool. And by then the first gunboat had entered the throat.

It came slow, wallowing, its armored bow pushing a bow-wave that slapped the stone like applause. Men lined its rail. Through the field glass Ansel could see the Halvard man from the trading post standing at the pilothouse rail, and beside him a gun crew cranking something long and black toward the cavern mouth. Not rifles. Something meant for rock.

"They're going to shell the spring," Tilly whispered. "The actual spring."

"The actual spring," Ansel said. "A river can't testify if it doesn't exist."

She thought of her father then, suddenly and completely — not the disgraced old man the company had made of him, but the one who'd stood on the Marguerite's wheelhouse roof and told a nine-year-old girl that the Vell wasn't water, it was a promise the mountains kept, and their family's job was to keep it too. *Keep it,* Papa. She spat on her palms and took the plunger grip.

"Now?" Ilya asked.

"Not yet." She watched the lead boat slide deeper into the throat, then the second behind it, then the barge, all three committed, all three inside the stone jaws. "Now they're drowning men. They just don't know it yet."

The first shell hit the cliff above the cavern and the whole world rang like a struck bell. Dust avalanched. Inside, Boaz swore with tremendous professionalism. Tilly grabbed Ansel's sleeve.

"Captain—"

"I know." Another shell. Closer. A crack opened in the cavern roof and a thread of daylight fell across the pool, and the spring seemed to flinch. "Ilya. Get them back. Deep as you can."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, looking at her hand on the plunger, looking at her face — and whatever passed between them passed without words, the way it always had. Then he was gone, pulling Tilly, shouting at Boaz, and Ansel stood alone on the high shelf with forty cases of borrowed thunder under her boots and the whole Vell holding its breath.

She waited until the third shell fell. She waited until the gunboats were abreast of the deepest seam, until the barge's deckhands were braced against their guns, until the Halvard man raised his arm to signal the next volley —

Then she threw the plunger home, and the mountain answered.

* * *

It did not sound like an explosion. It sounded like a decision.

The gorge walls shrugged. Sixty feet of limestone let go of itself on both sides at once, and the Kettle Spout closed like a fist. Rock went down into the channel in slabs the size of chapel roofs, and the river — denied its throat — stood up. Ansel had seen flood waves on the lower Vell, spring melt, dam breaks, once a cloudburst in the Redwall country. She had never seen water get *angry*. The pool behind the collapse swelled in three heartbeats, found the new dam imperfect, and began tearing at it with a patience that was worse than violence, and the displaced surge went the other way — down-channel, back the way the boats had come, a white rolling hill of river meeting the gunboats head-on.

The lead boat simply ceased to exist as a boat. It went sideways, then vertical, then under. The second broached against the new rockfall and broke its back with a sound like the end of the world clearing its throat. The barge swung broadside and the river rolled it twice and took everything off its deck, crates and guns and men, and the Halvard man's voice — she heard it even through the roar, thin and furious and human — shouting orders to no one, into no water left to carry them.

And Tilly was on the rim rocks.

Ansel saw her in the same instant the wave saw her — the girl had run out along the east ledge to watch, to see it done, and the backwash of the surge came up over the low rocks like a hand. Tilly went down. The water took her the way the Falls of Ket had tried to, the way it had taken Ansel's father, spinning her toward the gap in the broken dam where the river was already learning its new path, fast and black and final.

Ilya moved first. Of course he did. He'd spent three months learning what guilt cost and paying it daily, and he went off that ledge in a flat dive, no hesitation, no arithmetic at all, and caught the girl's wrist in the foam. For one moment Ansel saw them both — the cartographer and the runaway, two small dark things in a mile of white fury — and then the current peeled them apart, and Tilly fetched up against a boulder and held, screaming, and Ilya went under and did not come up.

Ansel did not remember deciding. Later she would search her memory for the decision and find only motion — the ledge, the drop, the cold hitting her like a door slamming, the river closing over her head. All her life the Vell had spoken to her, and now it shouted, and she understood every word: *you are nothing, you are driftwood, you are mine.*

She found him by feel, the way you find anything in white water — hands before eyes. His coat snagged her fist. She hauled, and the river fought her for him, genuinely fought, jealous, and somewhere in the churn she lost which way was up and which of them was saving which. Her shoulder struck rock. Her lungs burned. And the thought that rose through it all, absurd and warm, was of the Marguerite's galley, coffee, mornings, the life below that she had already spent — *this is the price, this is what it cost, pay it* — and she kicked off the bottom of the world and brought them both up into air and light.

The eddy behind the great boulder took them. Tilly was there, sobbing and reaching, and between the three of them — girl, woman, ghost of a man coughing the river out of his lungs — they clawed onto the shingle bar as the flood settled into its new course and the gorge above fell silent except for water doing its endless work.

Downstream, where the gunboats had been, there was only debris, drifting slowly, going away.

* * *

Ansel lay on the gravel with the sun on her face and the river talking over her, gentler now, almost apologetic. Ilya was on his side beside her, breathing, soaked, alive, his hand finding hers without either of them looking.

"You dove in after me," he rasped.

"You dove in after her."

"Somebody has to keep the tradition going."

She laughed — a cracked, drowned, unstoppable laugh — and Tilly flopped down on her other side, and above them the source cavern breathed its cool breath into the afternoon, sealed now behind sixty feet of fresh-fallen mountain, unmapped, unfindable, safe.

But far down the shingle, among the wreckage the river had politely returned, something glinted. Ansel sat up. A strongbox, sprung open, Halvard-stamped, half-buried in sand — and spilling from it, curling in the shallows like living things, were documents. Concessions. Deeds. The syndicate's claim to the entire valley, signed and sealed and very nearly drowned.

The river had given them back.

Tilly picked one up, read it, and looked at Ansel with her eyes gone wide and bright. "Captain," she said. "Is this worth anything?"

Ansel took the paper, felt the water-warp of it, and began, despite everything, to smile.
