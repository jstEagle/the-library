## 10. Downriver

The river gave him back to her at the second bend below the gorge, where the flood had spent its fury and spread itself thin across a gravel shoal.

Ansel had been swimming for what felt like a season. Her arms were rope and fire. She caught his collar on the third try, hauled his face above the water, and kicked for the shallows with a prayer she didn't believe in and a curse she did.

He was breathing. That was the first thing — before she registered the blood matting his hair, the arm bent wrong below the elbow, the way his eyes rolled white when she turned him over. He was breathing, ragged and wet and stubborn as ever, and Ansel Dravenmoor knelt in the gravel of the Vell's headwaters and laughed until it turned into something else entirely.

"Captain," Tilly said behind her, small-voiced. The girl stood dripping on the bank, alive because Ilya Marchetti had shoved her onto a ledge of rock half a heartbeat before the flood took him instead. "Is he—"

"He's breathing. Get Boaz. Tell him we need splints, the canvas, everything." She pressed her ear to Ilya's chest, listening to the water gurgle out of him with each cough. "And Tilly — tell him thank you. Later. When he can hear it."

The girl ran. Ansel stayed kneeling in the shallows, holding the hand that wasn't broken, while the river they had just sealed away forever rushed past her knees, cold and clean and keeping its secret.

* * *

They built the raft from mission timber Boaz had lashed to the Marguerite's deck weeks ago, back when they'd all still believed there'd be a boat to carry it home. There wasn't. The Marguerite lay somewhere upstream, holed and drowned in the narrows, and Ansel found she couldn't mourn her properly yet. Grief, she was learning, came in a queue, and Ilya's breathing was at the front of it.

Boaz set the arm with river whiskey and river curses, which he claimed were medically equivalent. "Lucky," he grunted, cinching the last knot. "Man dives into a flood to save a child and comes out with one broken arm and a head like a dropped melon. In my experience the river usually charges more."

"The river and I have an arrangement," Ansel said.

"Yeah?" Boaz spat over the side. "What's it charging you?"

She didn't answer. Behind them, upstream, the new lake sat where the approach gorge used to be — a mile of brown water over collapsed stone, deep enough to drown any gunboat Halvard Consolidated might send, wide enough that no road would be cut around it in this generation. Somewhere beneath it, past the wall of flood, the source went on springing from living rock in its cathedral dark, unmapped, unclaimed, and now unfindable by anyone who hadn't already been there.

Ilya drifted in and out of fever for two days. When he surfaced, he surfaced talking about contour lines. When he surfaced further, he looked at her with an expression she remembered from twenty years ago and one bad night on the Sandbar docks, and he said, "You let me keep it."

"Keep what?"

"The map. You could've taken it. It would've cleared your father's name. It would've—" He stopped, because she'd put her fingers on his mouth.

"I burned it," she said.

His eyes went wide. "Ansel."

"In the cavern. Before the blasting stores went up. Every sheet." She watched him absorb it — the cartographer watching his life's work go into flame, and understanding, because he was Ilya, exactly why she'd done it. "A map that exists is a map someone will kill for. A map that lives in three heads and a fever dream is just a story. Let them call us liars. Liars live longer than cartographers with famous maps."

He was quiet a long time. Then: "Your father would have hated that."

"My father," Ansel said, "would have thrown a party."

* * *

The raft took nine days downriver, and they were, absurdly, almost good days.

Ilya mended by increments, enough to sit propped against the cargo and argue about stars. Tilly appointed herself his nurse and tyrant, dosing him with willow bark tea so bitter it could strip paint, and discovered she liked authority. Boaz fished. Ansel steered, because steering was the one thing her hands still knew how to do without asking her heart's permission.

On the fourth evening, drifting slow through water so calm it held the whole sky, Ilya said, "Tell me about him. Your father. All I ever got were the official versions — the disgrace, the drunk who claimed a source nobody could find."

"He wasn't drunk. He was careful. That was the problem — he was so careful about the river that he was careless with people." Ansel eased the sweep oar through a lazy eddy. "He used to say the Vell kept its own ledger. That every chart was a debt and every dam a theft, and someday the river would collect. Everyone thought it was poetry. It was bookkeeping."

"And now it's collected."

"Now it's collected." She smiled despite herself. "He'd have hated how. He always said if you're going to sink a boat, do it on purpose."

Tilly, sprawled on the cargo with her hat over her face, snorted. "You did do it on purpose."

"That's why he'd have approved."

* * *

Halvard Consolidated's man met them at Corven's Landing, because of course he did. Mr. Ashe — the same smooth voice from the trading post, though now it had frayed edges — waited on the dock with two company men and a document case, and he looked at the four of them coming off a raft made of wreckage, and Ansel watched him do arithmetic and arrive at a number he didn't like.

"Captain Villalobos." A smile like a locked drawer. "We're relieved you survived. There's been some confusion regarding your account of events upstream. The company's own survey party reports no evidence of any source, any ambush, or any — forgive me — cathedral cavern. They report flood damage consistent with seasonal rockfall. Naturally, claims of sabotage and armed gunboats would be..." He paused delicately. "Difficult to substantiate."

"You're calling us liars," Tilly said.

"I'm saying the company regrets the loss of the *Marguerite* and hopes Captain Villalobos will accept fair compensation for her hull." He opened the document case. "Generous compensation. Along with a stipend for your crew's discretion going forward."

Ansel looked at the paper. She thought of her father's name, twenty years disgraced, and how close she'd come to buying it back with a map, and how the price had turned out to be a boat, a home, a life built plank by plank on the Sandbar — and how none of it, not one plank of it, weighed as much as the sound of the river running free behind that new lake.

"No," she said.

Ashe blinked. "No?"

"No, thank you. Keep your money. Keep your version of events — it's a fine version, very tidy. The river doesn't need my testimony. It has a mile of flooded gorge standing guard, and it doesn't take bribes." She stepped closer, and let him see, just for a moment, what twenty years of the Vell had put behind her eyes. "But hear me, Mr. Ashe. My father claimed there was a source. He was right. I know it, you know I know it, and now nobody can prove either way — not you, not me, not the next fool with a concession paper. That's not a disgrace. That's a door the river closed itself. And if your company ever comes asking me again — triple pay, triple anything — I'll spend the rest of my days telling every soul on this water exactly what happened up there, true or not, and I'm very good at being believed."

The dock was quiet. One of the company men shifted his weight.

"Captain," Ashe said finally, closing the case, "you understand this makes you unemployable on these waters."

Ansel grinned, and it felt like the first honest thing she'd done in a week. "Mr. Ashe, I just ran the Falls of Ket at night and blew up a mountain. I think I'll manage."

* * *

They settled, in the end, forty miles downstream of the Sandbar, where the Vell widened and slowed and forgot its temper, at a bend the old charts marked only as Good Holding.

It was Tilly who found it — a derelict ferry landing with a lean-to and an acre of bottomland, going cheap because the ferry had moved on and the soil was stubborn. Boaz fixed the lean-to's roof in an afternoon and pronounced it structurally optimistic. Ilya, arm in a sling, drew plans for a proper house on the back of a supply manifest, and then, catching himself, laughed until he had to sit down.

"What?" Ansel said.

"I was drafting. Automatically. Floor plans, load-bearing walls." He shook his head. "Twenty years I drew other people's territories. Lines on paper that meant someone could claim, tax, fence, flood. And here I am doing it again in my sleep." He looked at the river, gold in the late light. "I don't want lines anymore, Ansel. I want the thing itself."

So they built without plans, which drove Boaz quietly mad and delighted everyone else. The house leaned a little to the east, like a listener. Tilly claimed the loft and hung a sign on it that read SURVEY OFFICE, CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE RIVER, which she had painted herself and defended fiercely whenever anyone suggested it was silly.

In the spring, a government man came up from the capital with questions about the Corvus Survey. Ansel fed him catfish and told him a long, rambling, mostly invented story involving fog, mutiny, and a bear. He left confused and slightly charmed, and wrote a report that satisfied no one, which was exactly as intended.

Some nights, when the water ran high and loud under the bank, Ansel would walk down alone and stand where the current combed past her boots. She'd think of her father, whose name stayed dirty in every office from Corven's Landing to the capital, and who had been righter than any of them would ever admit on paper. She'd think of the Marguerite, sleeping in the cold dark of the narrows. She'd think of a cathedral of stone and water that existed now only in four memories and would never exist anywhere else.

Then she'd go back up to the house, where Ilya would have left the lamp lit and a plate covered, and Tilly would be arguing with Boaz about whether a catfish could outpull a pike, and the river would run on below, unmapped and unbothered, keeping its own ledger.

One evening in their second autumn, Ilya found her on the bank and stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, watching the water.

"Do you regret it?" he asked. "Any of it? The boat, the name, the—"

"The boat I regret," Ansel said. "She was a good hull. The rest—" She turned her hand over and took his, the one that had healed crooked and gripped just fine. "Ily. I spent twenty years building a life so solid nothing could knock it loose. Then the river knocked it loose, and do you know what I found underneath?"

"What?"

"Room." She laughed, and pulled him toward the house, toward the lamp and the noise and the stubborn, unplanned, uncharted life of it. "Come on. Boaz claims he's figured out dumplings and Tilly's promised to be merciless about it, and I refuse to miss that."

Behind them the Vell ran on in the dark, rich and secret and free, owing nothing to anyone — and neither, at last, did they.
