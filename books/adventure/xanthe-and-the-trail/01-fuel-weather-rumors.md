## 1. Fuel, Weather, Rumors

The rain had been falling on Meridian Strip since before dawn, and by the time Kestrel Hartwell finished her preflight it had settled into that fine, patient drizzle that didn't so much fall as hang in the air, waiting to be breathed in. She worked with her hood down because the hood always got in the way of the fuel check, and by the time she'd sumped the tanks and wiped the sample cups dry against her thigh, her hair was plastered flat and cold water had found the seam of her collar and made itself at home there.

She didn't mind. Rain was honest weather. It told you exactly what it was going to do.

The Cessna 208 sat on the gravel apron like a patient animal, gray on gray, its cargo pod already loaded with the week's medical freight: insulin in a foam cooler strapped where it couldn't shift, a crate of antibiotics, two boxes of vaccine vials packed in absorbent padding, a bag of sutures for the clinic at Two Rivers Landing, and — because Doc Amara never trusted anyone else's handwriting — a single envelope of prescriptions Kestrel had promised to carry unopened and undelayed. Four hundred pounds of other people's survival. She'd flown heavier loads through worse skies, but she still checked the tie-downs twice, then a third time, because the day you stopped checking twice was the day the sky decided to remind you why you used to.

"Kestrel."

She knew the voice before she turned. Rosa Vane, who ran the fuel pump and half the town's information economy from the same corrugated shack, came out under an umbrella she held like a shield she'd long since stopped believing in. Rosa was sixty-something and built like the counter she leaned on, and she had never once in eleven years asked Kestrel a question without already knowing three possible answers to it.

"Morning, Rosa."

"Fuel's topped. You want coffee? I got real cream today, not that powder." The offer was a hook, and they both knew it. Coffee meant standing in the shack. Standing in the shack meant conversation. Conversation meant questions dressed as kindness.

"Powder's fine," Kestrel said, which wasn't an answer, and Rosa sighed the sigh of a woman who'd been losing this particular chess game for a decade and kept playing anyway.

"You're loaded heavy," Rosa said, squinting at the struts. "Weather window's ugly. Radar shows a band sitting over the Kettle Range like a fat man on a park bench. You could wait till afternoon."

"Afternoon it'll be worse. Front's moving east; I'll be ahead of it if I leave now." Kestrel pulled the fuel cap snug and gave it a quarter-turn past snug, out of habit, then caught herself and loosened it back. Overtightening stripped threads. Her father's voice, saying it. She put the thought away the way she put away all thoughts of him: quickly, and somewhere deep, and without looking directly at it.

Rosa watched her do all of this with the frank appraisal of someone who considered watching a civic duty. "You hear about Alder Creek?"

Kestrel's hands did not pause, which took practice. "What about them?"

"Expedition outfit. Geology survey, upriver past the confluence. Backed by that company — Corvane Resources, or whatever the name is this year. Big money, fancy gear, satellite phones, the works." Rosa lowered her umbrella slightly, which for Rosa was the equivalent of leaning across a bar. "They missed their scheduled check-in yesterday. Whole town's talking about it."

"Whole town talks about everything. That's what makes it a town."

"They missed it, Kestrel. Forty-eight hours is the protocol, and they're coming up on it. And you know who flies that river better than anybody?" Rosa let the question sit there, fat and obvious, between them.

"I fly medicine runs. I don't do search-and-rescue drama." Kestrel zipped her flight jacket to the chin. "If they're late, they're late. Half these expeditions miss check-ins because somebody dropped a radio in a creek and they're waiting on a clear sky to get a bounce off a repeater."

"And the other half?"

"The other half you read about later." She said it flatly, and hated how much it cost her to say it flatly, and climbed the wing strut before Rosa could see anything in her face worth reporting.

* * *

The engine caught on the second turn, coughed, and settled into that steady drone that Kestrel had loved since she was small enough that the sound came to her through her father's chest, his hand over her ear, both of them laughing at the vibration in their teeth. She taxied out past the windsock — limp, useless, decorative — and paused at the hold-short line while a pair of ravens argued over something dead in the grass beyond the fence.

Meridian Strip slid away beneath her in the rain: the fuel shack, the row of houses with their woodsmoke standing straight up in the wet air, the school with its sagging basketball hoop, the graveyard on the hill where the spruce grew thick because nobody wanted to cut grass up there. Eleven years she'd been flying out of this strip, and she could count on one hand the number of times she'd stayed longer than a night. Fuel, freight, weather briefing, gone. People respected that about her, or resented it, depending on the person, but either way they'd stopped trying to change it around year four. There was a version of her life where she'd stayed somewhere — fixed a porch, joined something, learned the names of children — and she thought about that version sometimes the way you think about a road you didn't take: briefly, at altitude, with no intention of turning back.

The river appeared below her ten minutes out, brown and swollen with the early melt, braiding itself around gravel bars that wouldn't exist by July. She followed it north. The river was the only road up here that mattered; everything else was suggestion.

The cloud deck sat at twenty-five hundred feet, ragged, leaking. She threaded beneath it at eighteen hundred, close enough to the ridgelines to feel their presence rather than see it — a darkening on her left, a wall of green-black timber climbing toward rock. The rain streaked the windshield sideways. Her hands moved on the yoke in small constant corrections, the kind of flying that looked like nothing from outside and was everything from inside, a hundred tiny decisions per minute, each one meaningless alone and collectively the difference between arriving and becoming a story Rosa would tell for years.

This was the part people didn't understand when they envied her. They saw the freedom — no boss, no walls, the whole territory spread out like a table — and missed the arithmetic underneath. Every flight was a negotiation with weather that did not care whether you lived. Every landing was a bet placed on gravel, water, or ice, and the house always had the edge; you survived by being marginally luckier than the odds demanded, flight after flight after flight, until one day you weren't. She'd known pilots who talked about that edge the way gamblers talk about it, hungry for it. She just tried to keep her bets small and her checks thorough and her heart, such as it was, locked in a box behind the cargo net.

Two Rivers Landing came up out of the murk at noon: a gravel bar airstrip, a cluster of tin roofs, the clinic with its blue-painted door that faded a little more every summer. She landed long to avoid the soft spot mid-field that everyone knew about and no one ever fixed, rolled out, and shut down into a silence broken only by dripping rain and a dog barking somewhere with great conviction and no target.

Doc Amara met her at the plane, sixty years old and moving like thirty, her rubber boots leaving prints in the standing water. "Insulin first," she said, by way of greeting.

"Insulin first." Kestrel handed down the cooler. "Cold chain's intact, logger says it never went above four degrees."

Amara checked the logger anyway, because she checked everything, and nodded once, which from Amara was a parade. Then she lingered, which from Amara was unprecedented, and Kestrel felt the weather change before the woman said a word.

"You heard about Alder Creek?"

"So I'm told."

"Corvane called the regional office this morning. They're asking about charter availability. Nobody wants the trip." Amara shifted the cooler to her hip. "Monsoon band's parked over the upper river. Visibility's garbage, the gorges are fogged in, and everyone with a plane and sense is staying on the ground until it breaks."

"But?"

"But you don't have sense. Never have." Amara looked at her with those clinical eyes that had seen too many people die of things that were preventable if someone had simply acted sooner. "I'm not asking you to fly into that. I'm telling you that when the call comes, they're going to call you, and I want you to think about your answer before it rings instead of after."

"There's nothing to think about. I'm a freight pilot."

"Mm." Amara hefted the cooler. "Your father flew freight too, right up until he didn't."

Kestrel stood very still in the rain. Somewhere behind the clinic a generator kicked on, coughed, steadied. "That's not fair."

"No," Amara agreed, without apology. "It isn't. Most true things aren't." She turned and walked back toward the blue door, boots splashing, and threw one last line over her shoulder like a woman tossing a lit match onto a dry roof: "Check-in was due yesterday. Today makes forty-eight hours. Think fast, girl."

* * *

She took off again into the thickening afternoon and told herself the detour was operational. That was the trick she'd perfected over eleven years: every decision could be dressed in procedure. The direct route home ran along the lower river; the upper river route was longer but higher, above the worst of the turbulence, and she needed to log the terrain anyway for a charter quote she might give someday, hypothetically, to some hypothetical client. The fact that the hypothetical client was Corvane Resources and the hypothetical cargo was hope — that part she didn't write in any logbook.

The monsoon band showed itself twenty miles south of the confluence, exactly where Rosa's radar gossip had said it would be: a gray wall from treetop to heaven, rain falling out of it in visible sheets, lightning walking inside it like something pacing a cage. Below the wall, the river valley still held open air, a corridor of wet light between the cloud base and the water. She flew it low and slow, one hand on the throttle, eyes moving in the old rhythm — horizon, instruments, river, ridge, repeat — and the Cessna bucked and swayed in the gusts spilling off the hillsides.

Alder Creek camp should have been visible from here. On a clear day you could pick out the orange tarps from five miles, a little constellation of human stubbornness pinned to the bank where the creek met the river. Today she saw nothing but rain-haze and the brown flood, but she looked anyway, longer than the flying required, and the looking felt like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt.

It still hurt.

Her father had run supply flights into camps like that one for thirty years. He'd taught her to land on gravel bars when she was fifteen, to read water color for depth, to know that a river in flood was a liar and a river in freeze was a worse one. He'd died six years ago doing the thing he loved in the place he loved, and the territory had closed over him the way rivers close over stones, and Kestrel had responded by becoming very good at her job and very good at leaving, and she had told herself for six years that these were unrelated facts.

The radio crackled. Static, mostly — the front ate signals whole — but beneath the static, faint as a fish rising, a voice fragmenting across a repeater: *"—any station, any station, Alder Creek exp—"*

Then nothing. Just the hiss of the band, the drum of rain, the drone of the engine holding steady because she was holding it steady, because that was the job, because someone had to.

Kestrel stared at the radio for three full seconds. Then she keyed the transmit switch, and her voice came out level and professional and completely unlike the thing hammering behind her ribs.

"Station calling Alder Creek expedition, this is Charlie-Alpha-Kilo, Meridian Strip. Say again your last. Over."

Static answered her, vast and indifferent, the sound of a territory keeping its secrets.

She flew on into the gray, and behind her, far to the south, a phone was ringing in a fuel shack, and Rosa was answering it, and the answer was going to change everything.
