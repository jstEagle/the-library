## 2. The Silence on Alder Creek

The check-in was due at noon.

Kestrel Hartwell knew this the way she knew the weight of her own hands on the yoke — not as a fact she'd memorized but as a rhythm she'd absorbed, the way a river guide absorbs the pulse of water against the hull. Every Tuesday and Friday, twelve o'clock sharp, the Alder Creek expedition radioed in to the relay station at Fort Meridian, and Fort Meridian passed it along to Halloway & Voss Expeditions Ltd., and Halloway & Voss's logistics coordinator — a man named Dreele whose voice sounded like wet gravel poured through a speaker — logged it in a spreadsheet that Kestrel had never seen and did not want to see.

She'd flown the supply runs for the Alder Creek survey for six weeks. Six Tuesdays, six Fridays. Twelve check-ins, all of them dull as dishwater: *Alder Creek to relay, all stations nominal, weather holding, next window Thursday.* The voice belonged to a field leader named Osgood, a big slow man who pronounced his R's like he was chewing them, and behind him, faintly, always, there were other voices — someone laughing, someone arguing about contour intervals, someone whistling three notes of a song Kestrel half-recognized and could never place.

It had been, she would think later, a good sound to fly away from.

Now it was Tuesday, and the clock over the fuel shed at Corben's Strip said 12:47, and the radio on the shelf above the workbench said nothing at all.

* * *

Corben's Strip wasn't much. A gravel runway eight hundred meters long with a hump in the middle that every pilot in the valley swore they'd have the municipality fix and none of them ever had. A fuel shed with a hand-cranked pump and a cat named Diesel who slept on the oil drums because they held the day's heat. A Quonset hut that served as office, radio room, and — when old Corben's granddaughter ran the place — a café that sold coffee strong enough to strip varnish and cinnamon buns that people drove two hours for, which said more about the region than about the buns.

Kestrel had been there since six, prepping for a Wednesday run to the fish camps upriver — medical supplies, outboard parts, mail. Routine. She liked routine. Routine was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing and unglamorous, and she maintained it the way other people maintained their marriages: daily, deliberately, without expecting applause.

The radio sat where it always sat, its dial glow the color of weak tea. She'd turned it up twice without deciding to. At 12:47 she caught herself standing in front of it with a rag in her hand, wiping down a workbench that was already clean, and she made herself stop.

"He'll call," said Marisol Corben from the doorway, a cinnamon bun in one hand and Diesel draped over her shoulder like a stole. "Osgood's late all the time. Dreele told me last month they lost a whole day because somebody dropped the handset in the creek."

"Handsets float," Kestrel said.

"Then somebody dropped the *other* end in the creek." Marisol set the bun down on the workbench, an offering, and Kestrel looked at it and felt something in her chest do a small complicated thing she refused to name. Kindness, offered casually, was harder to deflect than kindness offered with ceremony. She picked up half the bun so Marisol wouldn't stand there waiting.

"You've got flour on your face," Kestrel said.

"I've got flour everywhere. It's a lifestyle." But Marisol lingered, and Kestrel understood why. Small towns ran on two currencies: gossip and worry, and they traded at nearly the same rate. The Alder Creek expedition was the most interesting thing to happen to the valley in years — eleven people, a science budget, a satellite uplink, sponsors with logos — and everyone from the fuel-shed cat upward had been following it the way people follow a serial in a magazine.

"They're saying at the lodge the river came up two meters overnight at the forks," Marisol said. "Rafting outfit pulled their boats."

"It's monsoon season. The river does what rivers do."

"Yeah, but—" Marisol shifted the cat higher on her shoulder. "Eleven people. On a gravel bar. In that."

Kestrel looked out the open door at the sky, which she had been professionally not-looking-at since dawn. It was the color of gunmetal rubbed with grease, low and soft-edged, no hard ceiling you could put a number on. The kind of sky that didn't threaten anything specific. The kind that just settled in over a drainage basin and stayed for days, raining with the patience of geology.

"I'm sure they're fine," Kestrel said, and heard how flat her own voice came out, and hated it a little.

* * *

Dreele called at 1:30.

By then Kestrel had given up pretending to work on the Cessna's left main tire and was sitting in the Quonset hut with the logbook open to a page she hadn't written in. When the phone rang — the landline, crackling, the only reliable link out of Corben's — she let it ring twice before answering, because answering too fast told people things.

"Hartwell."

"Miss Hartwell. Dreele, Halloway and Voss." Wet gravel, right on schedule. "I take it you've been monitoring."

"No traffic from Alder Creek," she said. "Nothing since Friday's check-in."

A pause on the line, long enough that she could hear an office behind him — keyboards, a printer, the ambient hum of a company that thought in quarters. "Friday's transmission was routine?"

"Routine. All stations nominal. They mentioned the river rising, said they'd moved the food cache upslope." She had the transcript in front of her; she'd typed it out herself from the relay rebroadcast, because that was what careful people did. "They signed off at 12:04."

"And nothing today."

"Nothing today."

Another pause. She pictured him: gray office, gray sweater, a man who processed risk as a column of numbers and found the column acceptable. "The forecast shows the system holding through Thursday," Dreele said. "Our assessment is that this is a communications issue. The satellite uplink may be down. Weather-related equipment failures are common at that latitude. We'd advise against any flight until conditions improve."

"Your assessment," Kestrel said.

"Our assessment, yes. We have eleven personnel on site and no indication of distress. A distress beacon would have triggered automatically. None has. The prudent course—"

"The prudent course is to wait," she said. "I know. I've heard it."

"Have you?" Something in his tone sharpened by a degree, the gravel gaining an edge. "Because I'll be honest with you, Miss Hartwell, we've had pilots get twitchy on us before. Fly into weather they shouldn't, burn our fuel, bend our aircraft, and for what? To feel useful. We contract you for scheduled runs. Scheduled runs happen on schedule."

Kestrel looked at the transcript. *All stations nominal.* Osgood's chewed-up R's. The whistling, three notes, off somewhere behind him.

"Understood," she said. "You'll call if anything changes."

"We'll call if anything changes."

He hung up first. She sat with the receiver in her hand a moment longer than necessary, listening to the dead line, and realized she was doing the thing she did — the thing she'd done her whole adult life — which was to agree with people while already planning to ignore them.

* * *

The pull didn't have a name. That was the trouble with it.

If it had been simple worry, she could have filed it under professional instinct and been done: pilot hears silence on a frequency, pilot gets restless, pilot wants to go look. That was a clean feeling, a mechanical one, and she knew how to work with clean feelings. You serviced them like engines. You acknowledged them, inspected them, and either acted or didn't, on the merits.

This wasn't that.

This was older and less orderly. This was the sensation of a door standing open somewhere in her chest with wind coming through it, and she couldn't tell if the wind was coming in or going out. Alder Creek was forty minutes' flying time north-northeast, up a river valley she'd threaded a dozen times, into a country of gravel bars and greywing pines and glacier melt running milky-green. And somewhere in that country — she had known this since the first contract, had read it in the fine print of the expedition manifest and felt it land in her stomach like a dropped wrench — was Elias Hartwell.

Her father. Field director. The name she hadn't spoken aloud in nine years, sitting there in black and white between *personnel* and *insurance*, as ordinary as a bolt specification.

She had taken the contract anyway. She'd told herself it was just money — good money, better than the fish camp runs — and that was even mostly true. But she'd also told herself that forty minutes of flying time was a large enough distance to be safe and small enough distance to be, what? Watchable. Controllable. She'd told herself she wanted to know he was out there, breathing, checking in on Tuesdays and Fridays, whistling three notes behind a radio handset, and that knowing cost her nothing because she'd never have to see him.

Twelve check-ins. He'd never once come on the radio himself. Osgood handled comms; that was protocol, and Elias Hartwell loved protocol the way other men loved whiskey. But she'd listened for him anyway, every time, some part of her tuned to a frequency below the broadcast band, waiting for a voice she'd know anywhere — dry, precise, the voice that had taught her to read weather off cloud bases before she could read words off a page.

And now the frequency was silent, and the silence had teeth.

* * *

By four o'clock she'd stopped pretending.

The hangar smelled of cold metal and avgas and the particular dust that lives in the joints of old buildings, and she worked in it with the door rolled halfway up so the grey light came in sideways across the concrete. The plane was the Cessna 185 she'd leased-to-own over five lean years, tail number ending in -KE because she'd gotten to choose and had chosen the only two letters that mattered to her and no one else's business. Skywagon. Taildragger. Ugly, honest, overbuilt in exactly the ways that saved your life. She'd landed it on gravel bars shorter than some people's driveways, in crosswinds that made grown guides sit down in the brush and wait for a better day.

She checked it the way she always checked it, top to bottom, but faster than usual, and she noticed herself going faster and made herself slow down, because rushing a preflight was how pilots died, and she had no intention of dying, not here, not now, not for this.

Tires. Struts. Fuel caps seated and wired. Oil, six and a half quarts. Control surfaces free and correct, cables intact, no bird nests in the cowl — though there was a spider in the elevator hinge gap, fat and indignant, and she evicted it with a screwdriver and an apology. Baggage compartment empty, tie-downs good. Survival kit current: sleeping bags rated to minus thirty, tarp, stove, pot, axe, flares, first aid, fishing kit, chocolate that tasted like sweetened chalk and might save your life. Radio, both of them. ELT, armed. Headlamp with fresh batteries plus two spares, because batteries failed in the cold and the dark was where mistakes lived.

Weather, then. She spread the charts on the workbench and weighted the corners with sockets and stood over them a long time.

The system sat over the upper drainage like a lid. Satellite pass showed a swirl of cloud rotating slowly counterclockwise, feeding moisture up the valley from the coast, and embedded in it — she traced them with a fingertip, reading the texture the way her father had taught her to read it, before, when she was small enough to stand on a chair beside the kitchen radio — embedded convective cells, scattered, mean, building in the afternoons and collapsing after midnight. Between the cells there would be holes. There were always holes. Not safe holes, not legal holes, not holes any dispatcher in the world would sign off on. But holes.

VFR into the valley was impossible. IFR was a joke; there was no approach, no navaid, nothing but the river itself for a nav line, and the river was in flood. Which meant the flight, if she flew it, would be low, slow, visual, seat-of-the-pants, in terrain that ate careless pilots and digested them quietly over decades. The kind of flying they wrote accident reports about afterward, with phrases like *continued VFR into instrument meteorological conditions* and *self-induced pressure* and *the pilot had been advised against the flight.*

Advised against, she thought. Well. She'd been advised against plenty of things.

Marisol found her there at dusk, rolling the last of the tie-down straps. She stood in the doorway with Diesel back on her shoulder — the cat commuted between them like a diplomat — and looked at the charts, the survival kit staged by the door, the fuel cans lined up, and her face did the arithmetic out loud.

"You're going."

"I'm going at first light. Weather's usually lowest early morning, but the convective stuff won't have built yet. If there's a hole, it'll be then."

"Dreele will fire you."

"Dreele can bill me."

"He'll bill you. He bills everybody. Last year he billed the search-and-rescue society for a cancelled meeting." Marisol came in and set a paper bag on the workbench, and Kestrel didn't have to look inside to know it was cinnamon buns, plural, wrapped in wax paper, still warm. Her throat did the complicated thing again. "Take the thermos too. It's the good one."

"Marisol."

"Don't 'Marisol' me." The girl — woman, really, twenty-three and tired-eyed and running her grandfather's strip because nobody else would — folded her arms. "My granddad flew that valley for forty years. He used to say the river doesn't kill people. People kill people, and the river just files the paperwork." She nodded at the charts. "You file a flight plan with me. You call me on the relay when you're down. And if you're not down by—" she glanced at the grey light failing outside, calculating, "—ten o'clock tomorrow, I raise the alarm whether Dreele likes it or not."

Kestrel looked at her for a moment. "You don't have to do that."

"I know." Marisol shrugged, and the cat adjusted, unbothered. "But eleven people is eleven people, and one of them is your dad, and you've got flour on your soul, Kestrel Hartwell, same as I've got it on my face. Somebody has to hold the other end of the rope."

Kestrel didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't crack something open, and she couldn't afford cracks tonight. She took the thermos. She filed the flight plan. She shook Marisol's hand because a hug would have been worse, and walked out into the rain, which had started sometime without her noticing, fine and cold and smelling of the river.

* * *

She slept three hours in the hut's back room, in her clothes, boots by the bed, and woke at 4:40 to an alarm she'd already turned off in her head before it rang. The dark outside was total and dripping. She drank coffee that could have stripped varnish, ate half a cinnamon bun, and stood at the window watching the runway lights — two kerosene lamps Marisol had lit and set out, because Corben's Strip didn't believe in electricity it couldn't crank by hand — glowing orange through the murk like the eyes of something patient.

At 5:15 she walked the strip with her headlamp, alone, kicking the gravel at the hump, checking for washouts and standing water. The rain fell straight down, soft as static. Somewhere north, forty minutes and a mountain range of weather away, a radio sat silent on a gravel bar, and eleven people were either fine or not fine, and one of them was a man who had taught her everything she knew about the sky and almost nothing about how to be a family.

She climbed in. Pumped the throttle three times, cleared the engine, key on, mixture rich, and the 185 coughed, caught, and settled into its rough idle, shaking the whole airframe the way it always did, alive and complaining.

"Alder Creek to relay," she said aloud, to no one, to the windshield, to the rain. "*All stations nominal.*"

She keyed the mic and called her departure to Marisol, released the brakes, and rolled.

The lamps slid past. The hump bumped under the wheels. And the grey ahead of her opened — just barely, just for now — like a door she'd have to fly through before it closed.
