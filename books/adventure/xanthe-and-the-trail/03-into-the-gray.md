## 3. Into the Gray

The weather closed in behind me like a door.

I'd been flying the interior for eleven years, and I had a private taxonomy for cloud. There was working cloud — the ragged stuff you could thread between, that gave you glimpses of the river and let you feel clever about it. And there was this: a solid gray ceiling pressing down from the ridgelines to the water, no floor, no ceiling, just an endless wet room with the river somewhere below it and the mountains hiding inside the walls like something waiting.

This was monsoon cloud. This was the kind of sky that ate planes.

I flew anyway.

* * *

Alder Creek lay ninety miles upriver from Meridian Flats, and for the first thirty of those I could still see the water. The Alder ran high and brown and furious beneath me, carrying whole trees in its current like splinters, the floodplain drowned so completely that I couldn't tell where the banks ended and the forest began. Every tributary mouth was a brown wound bleeding into the main channel. Somewhere under all that water were the gravel bars where the expedition had staged their gear, the low bench where they'd pitched their tents. Or where they'd pitched them. Nine days was a long time for a river in spate.

I climbed when I had to, dropped when I could, flying by memory as much as by eye. The Otter handled heavy — she always did with a load of fuel and my stubbornness aboard — but she was honest. She told me what she felt through the yoke, every gust a sentence, every downdraft a paragraph. I read her the way other people read faces.

At forty miles the rain arrived in earnest. Not falling so much as arriving, horizontal sheets of it that turned the windshield into a moving blur and made the wipers useless ornaments. I slowed to seventy knots and let her buck. The river disappeared. I flew on instruments and instinct and the shape of the valley in my head — I'd flown this stretch enough times that I could have drawn it blind, the way the canyon pinched at Caribou Bend, the way the ridge on the east side stepped up in three shoulders before the pass.

My father had drawn that first survey map. Forty years ago, before I was born, before any of it. His handwriting was on the original, filed away in some government archive: *E. Hartwell, field assistant.* I used to think about that sometimes, at altitude, crossing terrain he'd walked. Today I thought about it more than I wanted to.

The radio stayed dead. I called every ten minutes anyway — Alder Creek base, Alder Creek base, Kestrel Hartwell inbound from the flats, anyone on frequency — and each time the answer was the same wet static hiss, the sound of nobody. Static has a texture. After nine days of everyone else hearing it too, the backers in their glass office, the coordinator in Fairbanks, the satellite company with their little blinking dots, I knew exactly what that texture meant. It meant either the radio was broken or the people near it were.

I tried not to decide which one I was flying toward.

* * *

At sixty miles the valley narrowed and the weather got serious about killing me.

The cloud came down into the canyon itself, filling it like smoke fills a chimney, and I had to make a choice I'd been dreading since Meridian Flats: climb over or turn around. Climbing meant ice. The freezing level sat somewhere around four thousand feet and the pass was six, and my wings would collect rime the whole way up, white and creeping, until she stopped flying and started falling. Turning around meant going home to a strip full of people who would nod sympathetically and say, well, you tried, and mean it, and I would have to live with meaning it too.

There was a third option. There usually is, if you're willing to be stupid.

The canyon bent north at Caribou Bend, and on the outside of the bend the wall rose sheer for two thousand feet while the south side terraced down toward the water. In summer, in clear air, there was a shelf of kinder ground along the south terrace — benchland, old burn, scattered timber — where a pilot with more nerve than sense could stay low under the murk and pick her way upriver below the cloud deck. My father had called it the Low Road. He'd walked it once, hauling a damaged canoe, and written about it in a letter I no longer had. I remembered it anyway. Some things you keep even when you throw them away.

I dropped to eight hundred feet above the trees and went in.

The world became a corridor. Green wall on the left, gray wall ahead, the river a rumor below. Rain hammered the airframe like thrown gravel. My hands were steady on the yoke — they always are; it's the rest of me that shakes — and I flew the airplane in small corrections, small thoughts, nothing bigger than the next quarter mile. That's the trick, if there is one. Never look at the whole storm. Look at the piece of it you're passing through, and then the next piece, and let the pieces add up to having survived.

Twice the cloud reached down and touched me, and both times I turned hard back the way I'd come, found my hole again, and went on. Once a gust drove her down so fast the altimeter spun like a frightened thing and the treetops swelled up green and enormous, close enough that I could see individual spruce crowns streaming water, and then the gust relented and gave her back to me, and I climbed two hundred feet out of pure spite and kept going.

Somewhere in that gray I passed the point where turning around stopped being simple. The fuel math changed. The weather behind me changed. The decision made itself the way these decisions do — quietly, without my signature — and after that I wasn't choosing anymore. I was committed. Committed pilots live longer than hesitant ones, right up until they don't.

The pass appeared all at once, the way things do in murk: a lightening of the gray, a suggestion of open sky above, and then I was through, breaking out under a lower, softer ceiling into the wide bowl where Alder Creek met the river.

And there was the strip.

* * *

It shouldn't have been visible. That was my first thought, and my second thought was worse. The strip sat on a gravel bench forty feet above the confluence — I knew because I'd helped grade it, three seasons ago, dragging a harrow behind a borrowed ATV while my father stood at the edge with his hands in his pockets not helping, not talking, just being there in the way that managed to be both presence and absence at once. Forty feet of elevation. The floodwater should have been nowhere near it.

But the strip was underwater. Not all of it — the upstream third still showed its gravel spine, dark and glistening, ribbed with tire tracks that hadn't been driven in nine days. The downstream two-thirds had become a brown lake, calm now, deceptively gentle-looking, with fence posts standing in it like drowned men. Debris hung in the alders along the high bank: plastic sheeting, a fuel drum, a camp chair, snagged at the same height, marking the flood's high-water line like a tide mark.

The high-water line was above the tent platforms.

I brought the Otter around in a slow circuit, low, flaps down, looking. The main camp sat half a mile from the strip on the second bench — kitchen tent, sleeping tents, the big supply tent, the science tent with its folding tables. From the air it looked like a place that had been picked up and shaken. Two sleeping tents were gone entirely, their platforms bare, guy-lines trailing into the grass. The kitchen tent had collapsed sideways, its frame bent, its contents strewn across fifty yards of mud in a brown-smeared wake. The supply tent still stood, mostly, though its rainfly hung in ribbons and something dark was piled against its windward wall.

No people. No movement. No smoke, no signal fire, no laundry strung up, no bright parkas moving between the wreckage doing the thousand small tasks of survival.

Nine days. If anyone had been here nine days ago, alive, able, they'd have done what people do: they'd have signaled. They'd have stamped a message in the gravel strip where a plane might see it, or built a smudge fire, or laid out panels, or done anything, anything at all, because that's what the training says and panic follows training. The strip was empty. The mud was empty. The only writing in the whole drowned valley was the water's own.

I made two more circuits, closer, banking until my wingtip pointed at the wreckage, and on the third pass I saw the thing that changed the shape of the day.

The supply tent's door flap moved.

Not wind. Wind moves fabric in gusts, in rhythm with the trees, and the alders along the bench were hanging dead still in a lull between squalls. But the flap lifted, held, and settled — deliberate, controlled, the motion of something being pulled aside a few inches and released. A gap. An eye, maybe, looking out at the sound of an engine.

Someone was in there. Someone who heard a plane and didn't come running out into the open waving their arms, which is what rescued people do, what desperate people do, what I would do. Someone who heard a plane and pulled the door shut.

I set the Otter down on the surviving third of the strip — a shorter landing than I liked, wet gravel, tail low, brakes gentle because wet gravel will take your landing gear as a souvenir if you ask it to stop too fast — and rolled out with the engine idling loud in the empty valley. The sound went out ahead of me like a statement. Here I am. I'm real. I'm fuel and metal and a person.

Nobody came out to meet me.

* * *

I sat in the cockpit for a full minute with my hands on the controls, doing the thing I do instead of feeling things, which is making lists. Fuel: four hours' worth, enough to leave tomorrow if tomorrow allowed leaving. Weather: closing, not opening; whatever window had let me in was sealing behind me like scar tissue. Radio: dead, and mine wouldn't reach Meridian Flats from inside the bowl. People: zero visible, one probable, barricaded.

Every sensible voice I owned said the same thing: this is a recovery site, not a rescue site. Whatever happened here finished happening nine days ago. The river took what it took. You came too late, Hartwell, and the only thing left to do is confirm it and carry the confirmation back to people who will use it to write checks and hold meetings.

But the tent flap had moved. And my father's camp — his last camp, the far end of a career I'd watched from a distance of a thousand miles and twenty years of silence — was lying wrecked in front of me, and I discovered that sensible voices are cheap when your blood is up. I killed the engine. The silence rang.

I geared up standing on the strut: hip waders over my boots, because the ground between here and the bench was swamp now, bear spray on my right hip, hatchet on my left, the handheld radio in my chest pocket where it couldn't do any good but could pretend. I left the plane unlocked. Superstition. A locked plane means you're planning to fly away from something fast.

The walk took ten minutes and cost me dry socks. The flood debris got deeper as I climbed the bench — silt like wet cement, branches driven into the mud point-first, a scientific instrument I recognized as a sediment corer wrapped in a snarl of rope and alder brush. Everything wore the same brown coat. The flood hadn't destroyed this place so much as edited it, keeping the heavy things and erasing everything else, including, apparently, everyone.

The supply tent loomed up out of the murk, sagging, its nylon skin streaked black with silt. Up close I could see the pile against its windward wall wasn't debris. It was gear — duffels, crates, a folded table — stacked deliberately, packed tight, a barricade built from the inside. Somebody had spent hours, maybe days, making sure nothing could get in.

Or making sure nothing could get out without them knowing.

I stopped ten feet from the door. "Hello the tent," I called, in the flat carrying voice you use around bears and strangers. "Kestrel Hartwell. I'm the pilot. I'm alone."

Nothing. The rain ticked on the nylon. Somewhere upslope, meltwater chuckled through the rocks.

"I came up from Meridian Flats. Your check-in was missed nine days." I let that sit. "If you're hurt, I've got a kit on the plane. If you need out, I've got seats. I can't promise the weather, but I can promise I'll try."

Still nothing. But the silence had changed quality — I've spent enough years listening to radios and engines and empty places to hear the difference between an absence and a held breath. Something in there was deciding about me.

Then, close to the ground, at the seam of the door flap, a hand appeared. Small, mud-brown, fingers spread flat against the fabric — not reaching out, just showing itself. Showing me it was human. A woman's hand, or a young man's; from here I couldn't tell. It trembled, very slightly, the way hands tremble when the body has been afraid for a long time and has gotten good at hiding it everywhere except the extremities.

"Are you really alone?" The voice was hoarse, cracked from disuse, and careful — each word placed like a foot on ice. A woman, young. "Say it again. Slowly."

"I'm alone." I said it slowly. I said it twice. "One pilot, one plane, no passengers. Nobody knows I found this camp except the people I told I was coming."

A pause. Then, from inside, with the weariness of someone reciting a lesson learned the worst possible way: "That's what Marcus said. On day two. That he was alone." The hand withdrew. Through the nylon, muffled, almost too quiet to catch: "He wasn't."

The rain thickened. Behind me, upriver, thunder muttered in the high country like something clearing its throat, and the light began to fail in the middle of the afternoon, the gray deepening toward the color of the water. I stood in the ruins of my father's last camp, soaked through, my plane a quarter mile away on the only dry ground in a drowned valley, and understood that I was not leaving tonight — maybe not for days — and that the person behind the nylon knew something about why the river had taken seven people and left one.

The flap moved. Six inches of darkness, and within it, the pale shape of a face, watching me with eyes that had been counting days.

"You'd better come in," she said. "Before something else does."
