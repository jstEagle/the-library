## 4. Xanthe

Nine days is long enough to learn the grammar of a place.

Xanthe had learned the Alder Creek camp the way she learned everything — by its lines. The way the river's voice changed pitch when it rose at night, a half-tone deeper, a threat spoken in the dark. The way the supply tent's canvas snapped in a rhythm she could set her pulse against. The way silence itself had texture here: not empty, but dense, like wet wool pressed over the ears.

She had learned, too, the sound of an engine that did not belong to the river or the rain.

It came thin through the gray on the ninth morning — a drone, wavering, searching. She was out of the tent before her boots were laced, bare hands fumbling the flare gun from where she'd kept it dry inside her jacket for nine days, wrapped in a plastic map sleeve because some habits of cartographers die last.

The plane came down the valley low, following the river the way a hound follows scent. A taildragger, paint scoured to matte, flying with the particular economy of someone who knew exactly how much sky they could trust. Xanthe stood in the mud and fired the flare straight up, and watched the red star climb into the monsoon ceiling, and thought: *please see it, please see it, please* —

The plane banked. Wings waggled once — acknowledgment, or greeting, or just a pilot stretching her fingers — and began to lose altitude toward the gravel bar downstream that had once been the camp's airstrip.

Xanthe's legs nearly went out from under her. Nine days of rationing had made her body a rumor of itself. She steadied herself against the tent pole and made herself breathe, made herself think, because the relief arriving this fast was its own kind of danger. Relief made people honest. Honesty, right now, might be the most expensive thing she owned.

* * *

The woman who climbed out of the plane was not what Xanthe had expected, though she couldn't have said what she had expected instead. Tall, weathered past her years, moving with the deliberate care of someone who never trusted footing. Her eyes did a thing Xanthe recognized from her own mirror: they swept the camp once, cataloguing, and then stopped on Xanthe and stayed there, reading her the way you'd read a contour map — looking for the steep places, the places where the ground fell away.

"You're not expedition staff," the woman said. Not unkindly. Just as fact.

"Xanthe Reyes. Cartographer." She kept her hands visible, which felt absurd and necessary at once. "You must be the pilot. Hartwell."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes at her own name — a shutter testing its hinges. "Kestrel," she said, as if confirming it to herself rather than to Xanthe. "Where are the others?"

And there it was. The question Xanthe had rehearsed answering for nine days, alone, to the river, to the dark, to no one — rehearsed so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones. But rehearsal was no preparation for saying them aloud to a living face.

"The flood took them." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Downstream. All of them. I'm the only one who made it back up the bank."

Kestrel Hartwell didn't move. Didn't soften. That was the first strange thing, Xanthe thought later — most people flinched at news like that, reached for sympathy like a reflex. This woman just looked at her, longer, harder, as if the sentence were a coordinate and she was checking it against her own charts.

"Nine days?" Kestrel said finally.

"Nine days yesterday, too. And eight before that." Xanthe heard the edge creep into her own voice and didn't stop it. "I've been signaling every daylight hour the cloud let me. Rationing two-thirds of nothing. So if you're wondering whether I'm lying about being here, the blisters on my feet and the state of my hands are available for inspection."

That landed somewhere. Kestrel's jaw worked, and something in her stance shifted — not warmth, exactly. Recalculation.

"I wasn't sent," Kestrel said. "For the record. The backers told me to wait out the weather. I came anyway."

"Why?"

The question hung between them. Xanthe watched the pilot decide, visibly, how much truth to spend. It was a gesture Xanthe knew intimately; she was making the same calculation behind her own ribs.

"Because waiting is what people do when they've already decided someone's dead," Kestrel said at last. "I wasn't ready to decide that."

*Not ready,* Xanthe noted. Not *unwilling.* Everything this woman said had a door left open in it, a seam of escape. Xanthe filed that away with the rest.

* * *

They walked the camp together, and it was worse than walking it alone, because now there were two sets of eyes and each saw different wounds.

The flood had come at night on the fourth day of the expedition — Xanthe told it plainly, keeping her voice level the way she'd keep a hand level over a drafting table. A wall of water out of the side drainage, brown and roaring, no warning except the river's voice changing. Three of the six had been sleeping in the lower tents. Two more had tried to reach the boats. The boats were gone; the men were gone; the river had taken the whole geography of their camp and redrawn it in ten minutes, the way rivers do, indifferent to anything drawn on paper.

"And you?" Kestrel asked, standing at the torn edge of the bank where the lower tents had been. Below, the river ran high and milk-brown, carrying its cargo of trees.

"I was upstream, shooting control points on the ridge spur. Higher ground. I got down here at dawn and there was—" Xanthe stopped. Started again. "There was the camp, rearranged. And then there wasn't."

"You didn't go after them."

"I walked the banks for two days." Now the edge came back, sharper. "Every eddy, every snag. I know what a drowned person looks like after a day in cold water, Ms. Hartwell. Do you want me to describe it?"

"No," Kestrel said quietly. And then, almost against her will: "I'm sorry."

The apology sat strangely on her, like a coat cut for someone else. Xanthe studied her sidelong. There was something underneath this woman's competence, some ballast she carried low and deep, and Xanthe — who had spent three weeks in this man's expedition listening to the others talk about the legendary pilot who never came to dinner, who never drank with the crew, who flew in and out like a rumor — wondered if she was looking at it now.

Because yes. She'd known who Kestrel Hartwell was before she ever saw her face. Everyone on the Alder Creek expedition had known. They'd talked about her around the stove at night, the way people talk about a ghost in the house: *Eastwick's daughter won't fly for anyone but us, and barely for us. Eastwick's daughter hasn't spoken to him in eleven years.* Xanthe had listened to all of it with her face carefully blank, because her interest in Elias Eastwick was supposed to be professional, cartographic, clean.

And now his daughter was standing in front of her, asking questions with a surveyor's precision, and Xanthe had to decide — again, for the hundredth time since the flood — how much of what she knew she was willing to show.

* * *

They salvaged through the afternoon, because grief and work wear the same gloves.

Kestrel was methodical in a way that unsettled Xanthe slightly — she touched things as if inventorying them, as if the camp were a manifest she was reconciling. Radio, ruined. Food stores, half gone to the river, the rest soaked but edible. The theodolite, miraculously, survived in its case. Xanthe's own field books had dried crooked, the ink feathered, but legible — nine days of readings taken from the ridge while she waited for a rescue that the radio, dead since the second day, could not call in.

"Your radio died?" Kestrel asked, turning the cracked handset over. "Or was turned off?"

"Died. Water in the battery housing. I opened it, tried to dry it—" Xanthe gestured at the parts laid out on a rock, her failed surgery. "Why? What would 'turned off' even mean?"

"Nothing," Kestrel said. Too quickly. Then, slower: "In my experience, equipment fails in one of two ways. Like an accident, or like a decision. I like to know which one I'm looking at."

It was such an odd thing to say that Xanthe laughed — a short, rusty sound, the first in nine days, and it hurt her throat. "You always talk like a field report?"

"My father talked like poetry," Kestrel said, and immediately shut her mouth as if the words had escaped custody. She turned away and became very busy with the food tin.

Xanthe watched her back and understood, suddenly and completely, who she was talking to. Not just the estranged daughter. The daughter who had come anyway. Who had flown into monsoon weather against instructions, against sense, against whatever wall she'd built between herself and Elias Eastwick — and was now standing in his wrecked camp touching his wrecked things.

*She doesn't know yet,* Xanthe thought. *About the journal. About what he found.*

The knowledge settled into her like a stone dropped into still water, and she watched the ripples spread and made herself very careful.

* * *

Evening came down gray and dripping. Kestrel rigged the plane's survival tarp into a lean-to against the supply tent, and they ate heated rations in a silence that was not comfortable but was at least mutually armed. Truce conditions. Neither had disarmed.

It was Kestrel who broke it, staring into the little flame of the stove.

"Elias hired you himself?"

"Yes. Through the institute, officially. But he interviewed me personally. In Fairbanks." Xanthe turned her ration spoon slowly. "He tested me. Gave me a quadrant of old survey data with errors planted in it and told me to find them. I found four of five. He said the fifth one didn't matter because nobody would have caught it either, and that what mattered was that I argued with him about the third one."

"That sounds like him." Kestrel's voice was flat, but her eyes weren't. "He loved being argued with. As long as he won."

"He liked being *nearly* beaten," Xanthe said. "It's different."

Something flickered across Kestrel's face — pain, or its close cousin amusement. "You knew him well. For a hire."

"I knew him the way you know a teacher. Or a lighthouse." Xanthe hesitated. Here was a seam in the ice; she could feel the thinness under her boot. "He talked about you. Once. When we were waiting out weather at the forward cache. He said his daughter flew like the terrain was telling her secrets. He said—"

"Don't." The word came out quiet and absolute, a contour line closing. Kestrel set down her spoon. "Whatever he said, I've spent eleven years not hearing it. I'd like to keep the streak."

"All right." Xanthe held up a hand, palms out. But she didn't look away, and neither did Kestrel, and for a long moment the two of them simply regarded each other across the stove flame — the pilot with her sealed doors, the cartographer with her buried cargo, each recognizing in the other the same craft: the art of the withheld thing.

"You don't trust me," Kestrel said at last. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like approval.

"Do you trust me?"

"No."

"Good," Xanthe said. "Then we understand each other. Trust is a thing you earn with time, and time is the one thing this valley isn't giving us. So let's be precise instead. You need to know if I'm telling the truth about the flood. I need to know why a pilot who hates Elias Eastwick flew into a monsoon for him. Neither of us gets the full answer tonight. But we can trade installments."

Kestrel almost smiled. It transformed her face for a quarter-second and then was gone, like a bird crossing a gap in the clouds. "Installments," she repeated. "You really are a surveyor. Everything in payments."

"Everything in *measurements*," Xanthe corrected. "Payments imply I expect to collect."

Outside, the rain thickened against the canvas, and the river spoke its low night note, and somewhere upstream in the black the mountains held whatever they held. Xanthe lay awake in her bedroll listening to the pilot's breathing slow across the tent, and let herself think about the journal — the waterproof case she'd pulled from the wreckage on the second day, Elias Eastwick's cramped handwriting describing a valley that appeared on no map made by any government, a valley he'd written about in a voice she'd never heard him use, urgent and afraid.

*If anything happens to me,* the last entry read, *the only person alive I'd trust to finish this is my daughter. She'll hate that. She'll do it anyway.*

Xanthe stared at the tent roof and did not sleep, and did not decide, and understood that the decision was already making itself, the way rivers make themselves — one small surrender of sediment at a time.

Tomorrow, maybe. Tomorrow she would tell the pilot about the journal.

Tonight, the river rose another half-tone, and both women lay listening to it, each privately measuring how much higher it would have to come before it took the airstrip too.
