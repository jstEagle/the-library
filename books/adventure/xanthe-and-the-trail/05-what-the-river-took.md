## 5. What the River Took

*Kestrel*

Morning came up the color of dishwater, the kind of light that made everything look like it had already been given up on. Kestrel stood at the edge of what had been the Alder Creek camp and let it look back at her.

The river had done most of the work. It had peeled the main tent off its frame like a bandage from a wound and dragged it forty yards downstream, where it hung in the alders, bloated and gray, breathing slowly in the current. The cook shelter was gone entirely — not collapsed, not scattered, *gone*, as if the water had decided it had never existed. What remained was a scatter field: crates split open, their contents strewn along the high-water line like offerings left by something enormous and careless. A sleeping bag in a tree, ten feet up. A boot. A typewriter of all things, its keys furred with silt, sitting upright on a gravel bar as though waiting for someone to come back and finish a sentence.

Kestrel had landed on the strip at first light because there was nothing else to do. The plane couldn't fly until the weather broke anyway, and Xanthe needed food that wasn't freeze-dried despair, and Kestrel needed — she didn't know what she needed. To move. To put her hands on things.

"You'll want gloves," Xanthe said behind her. "The aluminum edges are sharp as razors when they're torn."

Kestrel took the work gloves the woman held out without looking at her. She'd slept four hours in the supply tent with a stranger six feet away, listening to the rain ease off and start again, and she still hadn't decided what to make of Xanthe Vale. Nine days alone. Rationing. Signaling. That part checked out — the signal mirror's aim point was scratched into a rock exactly where a pilot would look, and the smoke-blackened tarp had been rigged with an actual understanding of how thermals moved through this valley. But nine days alone did things to people, and Kestrel had learned long ago that the people who survived were not always the people you could trust.

"Work the downstream line first," Xanthe said. "Water drops about an inch overnight this time of year. Everything it took yesterday, it gives back today, a little further down."

"You've done this before."

"Three expeditions." Xanthe pulled her own gloves tight. Her hands were wrecked — cracked knuckles, a healing cut across two fingers bound with electrical tape. "You learn that the river isn't stealing from you. It's sorting. You just have to walk far enough to see where it puts things."

They worked the line together in a silence that wasn't comfortable but wasn't hostile either — the silence of two people who both preferred tasks to talk. Kestrel catalogued as they went, because cataloguing was a language she spoke fluently: two cases of medical supplies, mostly ruined but the penicillin vials intact in their foam. A satellite modem, dead. Rope, coiled and salvageable. A dented kettle. Someone's reading glasses, one lens starred.

It was strange, handling the dead expedition's belongings. Every object was a small biography. The glasses meant someone read at night. The kettle meant someone believed mornings mattered even out here. She set each thing on the tarp Xanthe had spread, arranging them in rows, and the rows became a kind of census, and the census became a question she kept not asking: *how many*.

"Four," Xanthe said, quietly, without being asked. She'd been watching Kestrel count. "Four others. Dr. Hartwell, Okonkwo, Reyes, and the twins — Brandt and Brandt, survey assistants, brothers. The flood came at night. We'd moved camp up onto the bench because the barometer had been falling for two days, but the river didn't rise so much as it *changed its mind*. Took the lower tents while we were scrambling. Dr. Hartwell got Okonkwo to the high ground and went back for the data case." Xanthe's voice stayed level, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who had told this to herself many times looking for the version that hurt less. "He didn't come back. Neither did the brothers. Reyes we found downstream two days later."

Kestrel's hands stopped moving over the crate she was emptying.

She had known, of course. Had known since the moment she'd seen the camp, since before that, maybe — since the backers' lawyer had said *your father is listed among the missing* in a tone usually reserved for delayed shipments. Knowing was a map. This was the territory.

"He went back for the data," Kestrel said. Not a question. Her father, who had once driven three hours back to a trailhead because he'd left a notebook in the truck. Who had taught her that instruments could be replaced and records couldn't, and had never once, in her whole childhood, applied that principle to anything as replaceable as himself.

"The valley survey," Xanthe said. "Eleven weeks of work. He carried it everywhere. Slept with the case buckled to his wrist, which I thought was theatrical until I watched him do it." She paused. "I'm sorry. About him. I know you two weren't—"

"We weren't anything," Kestrel said. "That was the arrangement."

Xanthe didn't answer, and Kestrel was grateful, and hated that she was grateful, and went back to the crate.

*

*Xanthe*

The pilot was a locked room, and Xanthe had spent enough of her life around locked rooms to recognize the difference between a door that was bolted and a door that was merely stuck. Kestrel Hartwell was bolted. But grief didn't care about bolts. Grief got in through the windows.

Xanthe saw it happen mid-morning, at the downstream bend where the alders gave way to a gravel fan. Kestrel had been efficient all day — too efficient, moving with the mechanical speed of someone outrunning their own thoughts — and then she waded out to a snag where a canvas pack had wrapped itself, and she hauled it free, and she opened it, and everything in her changed.

It was a small thing. A tin cup, dented, with a name scratched into the bottom in careful block letters: *E.H.* Elias Hartwell's cup. The kind of object a man used every morning for eleven weeks without thinking about it, the kind of object that outlived its owner by accident and then became unbearable on purpose.

Kestrel didn't cry. Xanthe half-expected her to and understood immediately why she didn't; some people's grief ran so deep underground that if you tapped it, you wouldn't get tears, you'd get an artesian well that would drown everything. Instead Kestrel sat down right there in the shallows, boots and all, holding the cup in both hands like something warm, and the river went past her, and her face did absolutely nothing at all, which was worse than crying.

Xanthe looked away. Some doors you pretended not to see even when they were open.

She busied herself with the snag line instead, working her way down the fan, and it was twenty minutes later, near the rootball of a fallen spruce, that she found the case.

She knew it instantly. Aluminum, olive-drab, corner-bumped, with a stenciled designation she'd helped carry across eleven weeks of country: *A.C. SURVEY / VALLEY / DO NOT SEPARATE*. It was wedged under the rootball where the flood had shoved it, half-full of gravel, and when she hauled it loose the latch fell open and water poured out of it like it was bleeding.

Empty.

No — not quite. She dug the silt out with her fingers and felt paper. One item, wedged into the hinge seam where the case's gasket had failed last and least: a book the size of a hymnal, bound in yellow rubber, its cover beaded with dried mud. Waterproof journal. Field-issue. She'd seen a dozen like it.

She should have called out. That was the protocol — finders report, especially with a survivor's kin standing thirty feet away. But her hands closed around the journal and her mind did a fast, cold calculation she wasn't proud of, the same calculation someone had paid her to make three months ago in a hotel bar in Meridian: *who benefits from what happens next.*

She hadn't taken the money then. She stood now in the wreckage of the man's expedition, holding his last written words, and made the choice again, for different reasons, or maybe the same ones.

"Kestrel." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Over here."

*

*Kestrel*

The case was gutted. Kestrel stood over it while Xanthe explained — the data drive gone, washed out or taken by the water, the film canisters scattered, the mylar maps shredded into ribbons that hung in the willows like strange fruit. Eleven weeks of her father's work, reduced to confetti.

But the journal had survived. Of course it had. He'd bought that journal in Fairbanks twelve years ago, the summer he still tried to call her, and he'd told her about it on the phone in one of their last real conversations — *they make these things so a plane crash won't kill your notes, kiddo, only bad handwriting can do that* — and she had laughed, and he had sounded pleased, and then the calls had gotten shorter and then rarer and then there had been a decade of silence with a Christmas card in it, once, unsigned except *E.*

Now she held it in her hands and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything.

"I found it wedged in the hinge," Xanthe said. "I didn't read it. I want that on the record."

"On the record," Kestrel repeated. Something about the phrase, the bureaucratic caution of it, almost made her laugh. Almost.

She turned the journal over. The spine was soft with water damage but the pages inside, when she carefully thumbed them, were legible — the waterproof stock doing exactly what the salesman had promised. Her father's handwriting filled the first page, dense and slanted, the handwriting she'd known before she'd known letters, because it had been on every note in the house, every lunch bag, every margin of every chart on the kitchen table: *Day 1. Alder Creek headwaters. Barometer 29.4 and falling. Crew good. Valley confirmed on the infrared — more on that when I believe it myself.*

More on that when I believe it myself.

Her thumb rested on the words. Around her the river sorted and sorted, patient as arithmetic, giving back what it chose to give back. Somewhere upstream a piece of ice went through the rapids with a sound like a door closing.

"Kestrel?" Xanthe said.

She hadn't heard the woman approach. She looked up and found Xanthe watching her with an expression Kestrel recognized from mirrors — the careful blankness of someone managing their own weather.

"We should get the rest before the light goes," Xanthe said gently. "And you should decide what you want to do with that. Because if anyone else comes up this river, that journal is worth more than everything else in this camp combined. Maybe more than everything else in the territory."

Kestrel looked at the journal. At the woman. At the wrecked silver case that had cost four lives and however many years of estrangement, all for whatever was written in these pages.

*Do not separate,* the stencil said. Too late for that.

She unzipped her flight jacket, slid the journal into the inner pocket against her ribs, and zipped it shut. It sat there like a second heartbeat.

"It stays with me," she said. "That's the decision."

Xanthe held her gaze a moment longer than politeness required — assessing, recalculating, filing something away — and then nodded once and turned back to the salvage line.

They worked until the light went pewter and then blue. By dusk they had a respectable pile under the tarp: rope, tools, the penicillin, a camp stove with a crushed fuel line Xanthe swore she could fix, three sleeping bags that would dry, and the typewriter, which Kestrel carried back for no reason she could defend.

That night, in the supply tent, with Xanthe asleep across the aisle and the stove ticking as it cooled, Kestrel lay with the journal under her jacket and did not open it.

Not yet. She told herself it was respect, or exhaustion, or the need for better light. But the truth, which she examined briefly and then put away like contraband, was simpler: as long as the journal stayed shut, her father was still somewhere ahead of her, still writing, still walking toward her through those pages. The moment she read the last entry, he would stop.

Outside, the river kept sorting. And in the dark, pressed against her ribs, the waterproof pages waited — and beneath the smell of mud and rubber, faint but unmistakable, Kestrel caught the scent of the last page's ink, fresh enough to suggest her father had been writing in this thing very close to the end.
