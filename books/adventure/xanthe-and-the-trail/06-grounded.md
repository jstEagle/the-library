## 6. Grounded

The river came for the airstrip in the night.

I woke to a sound I knew from childhood — not rain on canvas, which is a whisper, but water moving where it shouldn't, a low muscular hush beneath everything else, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. For a moment I lay in the supply tent with my boots half off and my father's journal zipped inside my jacket against my ribs, and I listened to that sound the way you listen to a stranger's footsteps outside your door at three in the morning.

Then I was up, headlamp bouncing, out into the gray drizzle of what passed for dawn here.

The strip was gone.

Not all of it — the gravel bar where I'd set down two days ago still rose above the flood at its upstream end, a dark tongue of land maybe sixty meters long, but the rest had become a brown lake flecked with foam and spinning debris. The Cessna sat at the high end, tied down to two boulders and a dead spruce, her wheels dry by perhaps four meters. Four meters of grace. Beyond her, where the runway used to run flat and pale toward the tree line, the Alder Creek had simply annexed it, wide and slow and patient, carrying whole trees past us like matchsticks.

"Kestrel." Xanthe stood behind me in the tent flap, hair plastered flat, her voice careful. "I heard it too."

"It rose overnight." My voice came out flat, professional, the voice I used on the radio when things were going wrong. "A meter, maybe more. The gauge rock's underwater — see, it should be there." I pointed at nothing. Water. Just water.

She came down beside me into the mud and we stood together looking at my airplane, my one way out of this valley, sitting at the edge of a lake that hadn't existed yesterday.

"How long?" she asked.

I didn't answer right away, because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and I'd learned a long time ago that *I don't know* is the most expensive sentence in the bush. You say it and people start making their own plans, and their plans are usually worse than yours.

"Monsoon peaks are seven to ten days," I said finally. "If this is the crest, we're flying out inside two weeks. If it's not—" I shrugged, and hated myself for shrugging. "Glacier route opens late season. Weeks."

"Weeks," she repeated. Not a question. A weight being tested.

* * *

We spent the morning doing what grounded pilots do: work. There is always work. I re-tied the plane with new knots and doubled lines, then moved her another meter upslope using the jack and rollers from the survival kit, sweating through my shirt while the rain fell soft and indifferent around me. Xanthe dug a drainage trench around the supply tent with a broken shovel, methodical as a surveyor, which of course she was. We didn't talk much. Talking felt like spending money we might need later.

By noon the water had come up another half meter and licked the plane's tires, and I stopped pretending it was going to stop.

That was when Xanthe said it. She said it casually, kneeling over the trench, not even looking at me, which is how people say the things they've been rehearsing.

"There's a game trail along the north bank. I walked part of it before the flood, checking sightlines for signals. It climbs away from the river after six or seven kilometers. From the ridge you could see down-valley. Maybe reach the ranger cabin at Two Moose if the bridges held."

I kept rolling the fuel drum upright. "There are no bridges. There's a gorge."

"The maps show a footbridge at the confluence."

"The maps are forty years old and drawn by a man who thought a goat track counted as infrastructure." I set the drum down harder than necessary. "Xanthe. Look at the river. Nobody walks out of here in monsoon. People who try end up as a story rangers tell each other in winter."

"People who stay put also die sometimes," she said quietly. "Nine days alone in that tent, I did the math on both options every morning. Staying isn't safe. It just feels safe because you can see the thing that kills you."

I straightened up and looked at her properly for the first time since dawn. Rain ran off the brim of her hood. She was twenty-something and hollow-cheeked from rationing and she had survived nine days alone in a flooded camp, and everything in me wanted to respect that, and something older and uglier in me wanted to win the argument instead.

"You want to walk out," I said. "Through a flooded gorge, on a trail you saw once, in the wettest season this valley has."

"I want to *not be trapped*," she said, and her voice cracked open a little, and I heard under it the nine days, the barricade, the sound of the river taking people she'd known. "You don't know what it's like waiting for a plane that doesn't come. I waited nine days. I'm not doing it again. If the water's rising and the plane's stuck, then walking isn't crazy, it's arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic. It's panic wearing arithmetic's clothes."

Her jaw tightened. "You don't get to say that. You flew *in*. You can leave whenever the sky gives you a window. I don't have a window. I have feet."

"And those feet will take you into the gorge, and the gorge will take you into the river, and the river already took enough from this expedition." I heard my own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "I am not losing anyone else on this creek. Do you understand me? I came up here for—"

I stopped. Too late. Her eyes sharpened, quick as a heron's.

"For who?" she asked. "For whom, actually. Because you've been carrying that man's journal around like it's a live grenade, and you haven't read it, and you haven't told me why you came, and you look at me some mornings like I'm a ghost wearing his protégé's face. So either tell me what we're staying for, Kestrel, or admit we're staying for nothing but your stubbornness, and let's do the arithmetic honestly."

* * *

The argument burned itself out the way arguments do in small spaces — nowhere to go, so it went inward, smoldering. We ate cold beans and hardtack sitting on opposite sides of the tent with the rain hammering the fly, and neither of us spoke, and the silence between us got so loud I could hear my own pulse in it.

Claustrophobia is a strange thing in open country. You'd think a valley couldn't trap you, that there'd always be sky. But the walls were cloud today, the whole upper valley sealed in gray wool, and the river had eaten our runway, and the plane sat with water kissing her tires like a dog chained at the edge of a yard, and I felt it closing in — the same feeling I'd been running from for fifteen years, the feeling of a place holding you whether you consent or not. Small towns did it. Families did it. Now a flood was doing it, and the only difference was that this time I couldn't taxi away from the feeling.

I caught Xanthe watching me across the tent.

"Your turn," she said. Not hostile anymore. Tired. "Tell me something true. Anything. You know my whole story — the hire, the map, the nine days. I know exactly nothing about you except you fly like the weather owes you money and you flinch when anyone says his name."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the river kept up its low conversation with itself.

"My father left when I was eleven," I said, because apparently exhaustion writes checks that privacy has to cash. "Not died. Left. Walked out of our house in Whitehorse with a duffel bag and a grant check and I didn't see him for years at a stretch after that. Postcards. A phone call on birthdays, some years. He chose the map over us, over and over, and everyone always told me what a remarkable man he was, like that was supposed to feed me." I turned the tin cup in my hands. "He sent me a letter last spring. First letter in six years. I never answered it. Then the backers called and said Alder Creek had gone silent, and I told myself I was flying up because I was the only pilot stupid enough to go. But I knew."

Xanthe said nothing. She had the good sense to say nothing.

"So no," I said. "I'm not walking into a gorge in a monsoon to prove I'm not afraid. And I'm not letting you drown proving you are brave. We wait for the water. That's the whole plan. Wait, salvage, survive. In that order."

"And if the water doesn't drop?"

"Then we get very good at fishing."

Almost — almost — she smiled. It faded fast, but it had been there, and I clung to it like the last dry gravel of the runway.

* * *

Evening brought the kind of light that makes you feel like the last person awake in the world: a thin copper seam splitting the clouds far down-valley, gilding the flood for about ninety seconds before the gray closed over it again. I walked the perimeter out of habit, checked the tie-downs, checked the trench Xanthe had cut, which was working better than mine would have. She was good. That was the problem with her. Every day she was a little more competent, a little less a stranger, a little more someone whose drowning I would actually have to grieve.

At the upstream end of the bar, near where the water now chewed at the old landing, I noticed something I'd missed all day.

Debris line. Normal — driftwood, sedge, a tangle of orange rope from the camp's stores. But among it, snagged on a willow whip, a strip of aluminum. Bright. Unweathered. I crouched and worked it free.

It was a section of aircraft skin, maybe thirty centimeters long, riveted edge intact, painted white with a fragment of blue registration showing. Not my plane. Not any plane I knew based at the regional strip. The break was clean, sheared, recent — the metal still sharp-edged, uncorroded, the paint barely scuffed.

Wreckage doesn't float upstream. Which meant it had come down somewhere above us, close, within these last days of flood — or been carried down from higher on the creek by the first surge, nine days ago, when the expedition died.

I stood there a long time with the rain coming down and the piece of somebody's airplane dripping in my hand, and I thought about the radio call I'd gotten in Whitehorse before I took off — the backers' dispatcher, smooth and hurried, telling me the site was confirmed empty, telling me not to risk the flight, telling me everything was handled.

Everything was handled.

Behind me, up at the tent, Xanthe's silhouette moved against the lamplight, and I realized I was going to have to decide tonight whether to show it to her — whether we were two people arguing about arithmetic, or something else, something that shared what it found on the shoreline.

I pocketed the aluminum and started back up the bar, and the river followed me with its patient, hungry hush.
