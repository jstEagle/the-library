## 7. The Leaking Boat

The water kept rising the way bad news does — slowly at first, then all at once, and then it was everywhere and there was no pretending otherwise.

Xanthe woke to it on the third morning after the airstrip drowned. Not the sound of it; she'd stopped hearing that days ago, the river's voice had become as constant as her own pulse. It was the smell. Wet canvas, wet wool, the green rot-smell of the supply tent's floor where the ground had finally given up being ground and become something else entirely. She lay in her sleeping bag and watched a bead of water travel down the tent pole above her head, gather, hang, fall onto the tarp beside her with a sound like a small clock ticking.

*Tick,* she thought. *Time's up here too.*

Kestrel was already gone from her spot by the radio crate. Xanthe could hear her out along the tree line, boots sucking in the mud, doing whatever it was Kestrel did every dawn — walking the perimeter of their small drowned kingdom, checking the plane's tie-downs, staring upriver as if she could glare the weather into submission. Xanthe had learned the pilot's rhythms in three days of forced proximity: the dawn walk, the silent breakfast, the way she cleaned the rifle that never got fired, the way she touched the journal-shaped lump in her jacket pocket when she thought no one was looking.

That last one interested Xanthe most. The pilot thought she was subtle. She was not.

Xanthe dressed in layers that had never quite dried, pulled on her ruined boots — the left one still held its shape only through stubbornness and duct tape — and crawled out into the gray.

The camp sat on what had been a gravel bench above Alder Creek and was now an island maybe forty meters across, shrinking daily. The tents stood in ankle-deep water. The supply crates they'd lashed together floated in a lazy raft formation, bumping against each other like cattle. And beyond the tree line, where the creek had been, the river ran wide and brown and fast, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks, going somewhere with terrible purpose.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the trees, hands jammed in her pockets, watching the current.

"Morning," Xanthe said.

"You slept."

"Barely." She came to stand beside her. Up close, Kestrel smelled of engine oil and woodsmoke and something underneath that might have been exhaustion worn down to its skeleton. "Any change?"

"Water's up another hand-span since yesterday. The strip's gone. I told you it would go." Kestrel's voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone reporting instrument readings. "What didn't go is my patience for standing here."

"So we're doing it."

"We're doing it." Kestrel turned and looked back toward the far side of the island, where the treeline thickened into old growth, and beyond that, upslope, the dark notch of a ravine that climbed toward higher country. "There's a trapper's cabin up that draw. Your father's notes mentioned—" She stopped herself. Something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "*The expedition's* notes mentioned it. Waypoint marker, fresh water, solid walls. Two hours' walk if the ground were dry. It isn't, so call it four. But between here and there—" she nodded at the flooded slough that separated the island from the slope, a quarter mile of drowned alder and deadfall and brown moving water, "—we need a boat."

"And you found one."

"I found one." Almost, despite everything, the corner of Kestrel's mouth moved. "Come on. You're going to hate this."

* * *

She hated it.

The skiff lay overturned in the alder thicket at the island's far end, half-buried in flood debris, and it was less a boat than an argument that boats should exist. Fourteen feet of ancient cedar planking, its paint gone to bare gray wood, one gunwale split, the transom checked with cracks, and a smell of ancient fish and creosote that made Xanthe's eyes water. Someone had dragged it up here decades ago and abandoned it, and the forest had been patiently eating it ever since.

"This," Xanthe said, "is not a boat."

"It's a hull with opinions." Kestrel heaved it over with a grunt, revealing a bottom furred with moss and a long gouge along one plank. "It floated once. Everything floats once."

"That's not how buoyancy works."

"It's exactly how buoyancy works when you're motivated." Kestrel was already pulling gear from the salvage pile they'd hauled together yesterday — the epoxy kit from the expedition stores, fiberglass cloth, a roll of copper wire, the canvas tarp. She worked the way she flew, Xanthe was realizing. Fast, economical, no motion wasted, no question asked that she couldn't answer herself. "You patch, I'll reseat the planks. We've got daylight until about six, and I want it in the water tomorrow morning before the wind comes up."

"Why before the wind comes up?"

"Because after the wind comes up, that slough turns into chop, and chop finds every mistake we make." She glanced up, and for a moment her eyes were almost warm, or at least amused in a dry, wintry way. "So don't make mistakes, cartographer."

Xanthe crouched opposite her with the epoxy kit and began mixing hardener into resin, the smell sharp enough to cut through the rot. "My name isn't actually cartographer."

"No?"

"It's a job description. You keep using it like it's a personality."

"What should I use instead?"

"Xanthe."

"I know your name, Xanthe." Kestrel said it carefully, like testing a rope knot. Then she turned back to the hull, and the moment closed again, the way it always did.

They worked through the morning. The rain came and went in bands, sometimes a fine drifting mist, sometimes a real downpour that drove them under the tarp strung between two alders while the epoxy cured. Kestrel talked in fragments while she worked — not conversation, exactly, but narration, the running commentary of a person who had spent years alone with machines. *Hold that there. Wire's no good, see the corrosion? That plank's sound underneath, feel it. Don't breathe the fumes directly, unless you want to see God.*

By midday the skiff had begun, improbably, to resemble a vessel. The split gunwale was wired and epoxied. The gouged plank wore a fiberglass bandage. Kestrel had reseated two loose planks and driven new cedar shims — whittled from a piece of flood-killed alder — into the seams, and now she was running a bead of sealant along the transom cracks, her tongue between her teeth, her hands steady despite the cold.

Xanthe found herself watching those hands. Capable hands, scarred at the knuckles, a faded burn mark across the back of the right one. Hands that had done things alone, for a long time.

"You're staring," Kestrel said, without looking up.

"You're good at this."

"I'm good at fixing things that are broken." A beat. "It's a whole theme."

Xanthe laughed before she could stop herself — a short, rusty sound, the first in nine days, and she felt Kestrel register it, some small shift in the set of her shoulders, though the pilot said nothing.

They broke the last seam open with a screwdriver and a mallet around two o'clock, and that was when Xanthe saw it.

She'd been inside the hull, holding a shim while Kestrel drove it home, her cheek nearly against the bottom boards, and the light through the alders fell just so across the wood — and there, low on the starboard side near the garboard seam, was a cut.

Not a crack. Cracks wander. This was straight. Clean-edged, deliberate, a slot perhaps eight inches long driven clean through the bottom plank, the wood around it pale and raw where the blade had gone through, and — this was the part that stopped her breath — the edges had been *sanded*. Smoothed. Someone had wanted the wound to look like age, like rot, like failure.

"Kestrel."

"Hold the shim."

"*Kestrel.* Look at this."

Kestrel leaned in. Xanthe watched her face do something complicated — the flat competence draining out of it, replaced first by confusion, then by a stillness that was worse than any expression. She put her fingers on the cut. Traced it. Ran her thumbnail along the sanded edges.

"That's a knife cut," she said quietly.

"It's a knife cut that somebody tried to hide."

"Boats rot. Boats crack. Boats get gouged on rocks." Kestrel's thumb moved along the slot again, slow, reading it like braille. "Nobody sands a crack smooth. That takes time. That takes *intent*." She looked up, and her eyes had gone the color of the river. "This boat was disabled on purpose. Somebody wanted whoever used it next to end up in the water."

Xanthe sat back on her heels. The rain ticked on the tarp overhead. Somewhere out in the flood, a log bumped against a tree with a hollow knock, like a door.

"The expedition," she said slowly. "Before the flood. Someone was preparing to leave. Or preparing for someone else to leave."

"Or making sure nobody could follow." Kestrel sat back too, wiping her hands on her thighs, and Xanthe watched her go somewhere else behind her eyes — running numbers, running scenarios, the pilot's mind treating this like weather, mapping the front. "Think about it. This skiff has been sitting here for years, right? Nobody touched it. Then sometime recently — weeks, months — someone cuts the bottom and sands it neat, and puts it back, and walks away, and waits."

"Waits for what?"

"For the river to rise." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "For exactly what happened. For people trapped on high ground with one boat between them and safety, who find it, patch it, launch it, and sink in the middle of a quarter mile of moving water in monsoon season." She exhaled through her nose. "Except whoever did it expected the expedition crew. Six people, heavy packs, probably at night, probably panicked. Not two half-starved women with an epoxy kit."

The full weight of it settled over Xanthe like cold water down her collar. Nine days alone in the supply tent, rationing, signaling, telling herself the flood was an act of God, that the river had simply taken the others, that survival was luck and luck was random. And now here was evidence, carved in cedar, that luck had help.

"This wasn't only weather," she said.

"No." Kestrel was already moving, pulling the skiff fully upright, checking the other planks with new and harder attention, tapping each one, listening. "No, it wasn't."

* * *

They finished the repairs anyway. That was the strange part, the part Xanthe turned over that night as she lay listening to the rain — that neither of them suggested stopping. Kestrel simply widened the patch over the sabotage cut, doubling the fiberglass, driving in a backing plate of aluminum scavenged from the wrecked camp's stove, and Xanthe mixed epoxy until her fingers went numb, and neither of them spoke about knives or intent or the word *someone*, because saying it aloud would have made the forest full of it.

Instead they talked about the crossing. Kestrel drew a map in the mud with a stick — actual lines, actual contours, and Xanthe caught herself leaning in despite everything, the cartographer in her sitting up like a dog hearing its name. The slough's current set downstream and east. The deadfall would create eddies along the north bank. They'd ferry-glide across, angle upstream of the landing, let the river do the work.

"Paddle on my count," Kestrel said. "If we ship water, you bail and I steer. If we capsize—" she tapped a point in the mud, "—we stay upstream of the boat. Always. The boat is armor. The water is not."

"And if the patch fails?"

"It won't fail."

"You can't know that."

"I built it. So yes, I can." But her voice had a hairline fracture in it, and Xanthe heard it, and understood that Kestrel Hartwell was afraid too, and simply refused to let fear drive, the way she refused to let anyone else drive, apparently, ever.

They launched at first light.

The world at dawn was all gray water and gray sky, the boundary between them dissolved, the alders standing in the flood like drowned pilgrims. Kestrel took the stern. Xanthe took the bow with a bail bucket and the pack with the maps sealed in plastic against her chest. The skiff slid off the mud and into the current, and for one suspended moment everything was silence and drift —

— and then the river took hold.

It was nothing like flying, Xanthe thought wildly, gripping the gunwale. Flying was mathematics made graceful. This was chaos with a current. The skiff swung, corrected, swung again, and the water came through the seams in thin cold ropes that found her knees, her wrists, the small of her back. She bailed. Bailed again. The aluminum patch held — she could see it under her feet, Kestrel's ugly beautiful handiwork, holding — but along the starboard seam, forward, where they hadn't doubled up—

"Water's coming faster," she called. "Forward starboard. Faster than seepage."

"How much faster?"

"A cup a minute. Maybe more."

Kestrel's jaw worked. "Bail. Keep bailing. We're committed — bank's soft on the left, we'd swamp turning back. Thirty meters. Paddle."

Xanthe paddled. The river shoved. The skiff groaned like a living thing. And through the gray roar Xanthe became aware of a sound she couldn't place at first — a rhythmic knocking, coming from beneath her feet, from the hull itself, from the forward starboard seam where the water ran cold and constant over her ankles.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Not debris. Debris doesn't keep time.

She looked down through the bilge water, through the brown churn, and pressed her palm flat against the wet cedar, and felt it: a vibration, a working, a flexing — the seam opening and closing with each wave like something breathing. Like something that had been *loosened*. Because when she angled her head, squinting through the murk, she could see it — forward, low, half-hidden under the forward thwart's shadow, where no one repairing a rotted abandoned skiff would ever think to look.

A second cut. Narrower. Neater. Sanded smooth, same as the first, but plugged — plugged with a dowel of soft white pine, wrapped in waxed thread, seated flush and painted over with the same gray as the hull. A plug designed to hold. Designed to hold for days, for weeks, for exactly as long as it took for the boat to be found, patched everywhere else, trusted, launched into deep water — and then to work loose, slowly, in the cold, in the swell, one knock at a time.

"Kestrel!" Her voice cracked. "Second one! There's a second cut — plugged — it's letting go!"

Kestrel didn't waste breath on a curse. "Thwart! Pull the thwart pin — the seat brace — give me the pry bar!"

Xanthe wrenched loose the aluminum brace, passed it back, and Kestrel wedged it against the plug, levering, her arms shaking, the river carrying them sideways toward a snag of drowned alder whose black branches combed the surface like fingers. Xanthe bailed with one hand and paddled with the other and the plug gave with a pop and Kestrel slammed the brace into the gap, bending it, jamming it into the hole as a makeshift patch, water screaming around the metal—

—and then the keel kissed gravel, and the skiff lurched, and slewed, and grounded, hard, tilting, and the current pushed them up onto the shoal at the base of the slope like a hand setting down something fragile.

Silence, except the rain, and the river, and Xanthe's own blood in her ears.

They got out into knee-deep water and hauled the skiff up the gravel until it grounded for good. Xanthe sat down right there in the shallows, shaking, the bail bucket still in her hand. Kestrel stood over the hull, chest heaving, staring down at the bent aluminum brace jutting from the seam like a splinter of bone.

"Two cuts," Xanthe said. Her teeth were chattering. "One hidden, one disguised. That's not vandalism. That's engineering."

Kestrel crouched. Worked the brace free. Turned the pine plug over in her fingers, once, twice. Waxed thread. Fresh-cut wood, pale as a tooth. Paint matched to a hull that hadn't seen paint in twenty years — which meant whoever did it had brought the paint *with them*, had studied the boat, had planned.

"Somebody spent real time on this," Kestrel said softly. "Real skill." She looked up, and beyond her the ravine climbed into the fog toward the trapper's cabin, and beyond that, invisible, the whole drowned watershed, and somewhere in it, possibly, the person who had built this trap and walked away and waited for the river to do the rest.

"They're not done," Xanthe said. It wasn't a guess. It landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. "Whoever it is. The boat was step one. They're still out there."

Kestrel slipped the plug into her jacket pocket, next to the journal-shaped lump, and picked up her pack.

"Then we move," she said. "And we don't sleep at the same time ever again."
