## 8. Journal Entries

The trapper's cabin held the day's heat like a cupped hand, and Kestrel sat alone in it while Xanthe slept.

She had waited for this. That was the honest shape of it — she'd carried the journal against her ribs since the riverbank, through the salvage and the argument and the long wet walk to higher ground, and she had told herself she was waiting for privacy when really she was waiting for courage. Nine days of rain between her and her father's handwriting. She'd needed all of them.

Now the lamp was turned low, a stub of candle guttering in a tin dish because she didn't trust herself with more light. The journal lay on the table between her hands, its waterproof casing beaded with river water that refused to dry, as if the thing were still trying to breathe.

Outside, the monsoon worked the valley. Rain came off the glacier face in sheets, hissing across the scree, filling the gullies with a sound like static. Somewhere downriver the flood kept chewing at what was left of Alder Creek camp. Kestrel listened to it the way you listen to a dog circling — knowing it would settle eventually, not knowing when.

She opened the cover.

The first page was dated fourteen months ago. Her father's handwriting hadn't changed: small, slanted forward, always in a hurry, as if the words might outrun him if he didn't press hard enough. *Field Journal 7*, the header said, in block capitals. Below it: *Alder Creek Survey — Meridian Geoscience Contract.*

Meridian. The backers' name sat there on the first line like a splinter she'd been carrying without knowing where it entered.

*Day 1. River high but passable. Crew of six plus pilot support (K.H., contract flights). Terrain better than the aerials suggested. There is something here the satellite imagery can't see — the drainage doesn't match. I've flagged it for follow-up.*

K.H. Kestrel Hartwell. She read her own initials in her dead father's hand and had to set the journal down flat on the table, palms pressed to the wood, until the cabin stopped tilting.

*Contract flights.* So he'd known she was flying for Meridian. He'd known and never said a word, never picked up the radio on any of those gray mornings when their paths crossed over the interior and she'd keyed the mic twice, three times, hoping. He'd watched her plane come up the valley and written about it in a journal instead of saying hello.

*Day 4,* the next entry began. *Flew the east wall today. There's a notch in the ridge line that isn't on any chart I own. Cloud came in before we could get above it. Tomorrow.*

*Tomorrow* was crossed out. Beneath it, in fresher ink: *Weather held us four days. X. used the time to re-draw the lower survey grid from memory. Remarkable head for terrain. Hired her off a recommendation and would hire her twice more.*

Xanthe.

Kestrel looked toward the bunk. The cartographer slept curled on her side, facing the wall, one arm under the rolled jacket she used for a pillow. In the lamplight her face looked younger than it did awake, younger than it had any right to look given nine days alone in a wrecked camp rationing a crate of protein bars and boiling creek water in a dented pot. Kestrel had watched her all day — watched her mend netting, patch the skiff canvas, do small necessary work with steady hands — and had told herself the steadiness was competence. Now she wondered how much of it was performance. How much of anything in that camp had been.

She turned pages. The entries thickened through the summer, dense with bearings and elevations, sketches of strata, water temperatures, lichen samples. Her father had been a thorough man; the science was real. But threaded through the fieldwork, growing more frequent as the weeks passed, ran another kind of entry. Shorter. Written later at night, the ink different, the handwriting tighter.

*June 19. Meridian sent a second liaison. Name of Corrigan. Friendly man. Too friendly. Asked twice today whether the "interior drainage anomaly" could be "resolved" from existing data. Resolved. As if the valley were an error to be corrected rather than a place.*

Kestrel's mouth went dry. She knew Corrigan. Everyone who flew contract for Meridian knew Corrigan — the liaison who showed up at regional strips with a briefcase and a smile, who remembered your daughter's name and your fuel supplier's golf handicap, who made you feel that refusing him would be a small discourtesy with large consequences. She'd taken his charter bookings for two years. She'd laughed at his jokes.

*June 26. Found the notch today. Flew into cloud to get it and came out the other side into air I have no name for. There is a valley behind the east wall. Unmapped. Not poorly mapped — unmapped. The river enters it through a limestone gorge and does not come out where the charts say rivers go. It comes out forty miles south, warm, which means it runs deep and slow through country nobody has ever surveyed. Glacial till on both rims. Old forest in the floor. I stood on the rim and felt like the first man on the moon, except lonelier, because the moon has no one to lie to you about it.*

Kestrel read that last sentence three times. *Except lonelier, because the moon has no one to lie to you about it.*

He had been alone up here. All those months, surrounded by a crew, funded by a company, flying with his daughter overhead — and he had been alone in exactly the way she had been alone in every cockpit of her adult life. They had built the same walls in different countries. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

*July 3. Told Corrigan about the gorge approach only. Held back the valley itself. His reaction was wrong. A scientist hears "unrecorded valley" and asks what lives there. Corrigan asked who else knows. Then he asked me to sign an addendum to the contract — "data exclusivity provisions." I read it twice. It isn't exclusivity. It's ownership. They don't want the survey published. They want the land quiet until they own it.*

*Mineral rights, water rights, carbon credits — pick your century, they'll take all three. A valley that isn't on a map belongs to whoever keeps it off the map. I understand now why my predecessor on this contract retired so suddenly to a place with no postal address.*

Kestrel turned the page slowly, as if the paper might bruise.

*July 11. X. finished the contour work on the upper basin. She is the best field cartographer I have worked with in thirty years, and I am going to have to lie to her, and I hate it. Meridian has asked for a full data package by end of month. I will give them the gorge, the rim geology, everything except the floor. If they want the heart of it they will have to walk in themselves, and the gorge in flood season will kill anyone who tries. Let the river keep my secret a little longer.*

So he'd hidden it from Xanthe too. Kestrel glanced at the bunk again and felt something complicated move through her — not quite sympathy, not quite suspicion. The girl had been hired anonymously, she'd said. Verified nothing except what she was handed. Maybe she genuinely didn't know what her employer wanted. Or maybe she knew exactly, and the protein bars and the barricaded tent and the nine days of signaling were a story built to be found by the one person guaranteed to come looking.

Kestrel hated herself a little for thinking it, and thought it anyway.

*July 20. Corrigan flew in unannounced. Brought a lawyer. They walked the camp smiling at everyone. Afterward Corrigan took me aside and said, "Your daughter does good work." Just that. No context. He said it the way a man says a thing when he wants you to know he's checked.*

*I have spent my whole life being careful with maps and careless with people. I taught myself to read terrain before I taught myself to talk to my own child. If Corrigan thinks he has found a lever, he should know the lever works both ways. I will finish this survey. I will put that valley on the record where no company can bury it, and then I will fly home and knock on a door I have no right to knock on.*

Kestrel closed her eyes. The rain filled the silence where his voice should have been.

He had been coming home. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it — not that he died out here, but that he died *en route*, that somewhere in these pages was a man packing up thirty years of cowardice to carry back downriver to her. And the river had taken him before the river could take the map. She thought of the wrecked camp, the gear scattered like dropped letters, the supply tent with its slashed guy-lines, and grief hit her mid-breath, sudden and total, the way it always did — not a wave but a door opening onto a room she'd sealed years ago.

She let it. She sat in the lamplight and let it hurt, because he deserved to be missed loudly at least once, and because there was no one awake to see it and no one ever would be.

When it passed, she wiped her face on her sleeve and went back to the journal, because he had left her instructions and she was, after all, her father's daughter.

*August 2. The crew is uneasy. Two of them got an offer — same pay, half the season, "reassignment to a southern project." Meridian is hollowing us out. Anyone loyal to the science goes, anyone loyal to the paycheck stays. I counted this morning: four of us left who care where the river actually goes.*

*August 9. Moved the master map. Not telling anyone where. If something happens to me, the valley exists in two places: my head, and a copy I've entrusted to the only person I trust to finish what I started. She flies like the weather owes her money and she reads country like her mother used to read people. I have never told her I watch her land in crosswinds just to see if she'll make it look easy. She always makes it look easy. I don't know where she learned it, because it wasn't from me.*

*Kestrel. If you are reading this, then the river won, or worse did, and I am sorry for every year I chose the work over the call. This is not an apology. Apologies are cheap and I've been expensive. This is a job. Finish the survey. Photograph the floor, log the drainage, file it with the registry and the press on the same day so no one can buy it, lose it, or burn it. The valley survives by being known. Do not let them make it a rumor.*

*And take care of yourself. You were always better at taking care of machines than of yourself. Learn to need someone. It won't kill you. I checked.*

Kestrel laughed — a broken, wet sound — at *I checked*, and then the laugh became the other thing again and she let the candle burn down a quarter inch before she could see the page.

The last entry was dated August 14. Three days before the check-in that never came.

*Corrigan returned today with a helicopter and two men who don't introduce themselves. He offered me a number with enough zeroes to buy a life I never wanted. I told him the valley wasn't mine to sell. He smiled and said, "Everything's someone's to sell. The question is whether it's sold before or after."*

*I've moved the original tonight. Tomorrow I tell the crew we're running the gorge to reach the south rim transects. The river is high but I know the eddy lines. If I'm wrong, the map is already safe. If I'm right, we finish the survey and I go home.*

*Either way, it ends well. I finally believe that.*

Three days later the camp was wreckage and the river had six of them, and the only soul left on Alder Creek had been a young woman barricaded in a supply tent, signaling with a mirror.

Kestrel sat very still.

The pieces wouldn't stay separate anymore. Corrigan's helicopter. Two men who didn't introduce themselves. A skiff that leaked from a cut that was too clean. Boot prints upstream, fresh, days after the flood supposedly swept everything away. A garbled radio transmission claiming survivors had already been evacuated — a lie designed, she understood now, to stop anyone from looking, or to flush out whoever was still hiding with the map.

They hadn't come for the crew. The crew had been an inconvenience. They had come for a folder of contours and a valley that wasn't on any chart, and her father had drowned knowing exactly why.

And Xanthe — anonymous hire, perfect survivor, sole witness — had been placed on that expedition by someone. The question Kestrel had been politely not asking for five days stood up in her chest and demanded asking:

Hired by whom?

Behind her, the bunk creaked. Kestrel slid the journal shut and slid it beneath the table's loose board in one motion, smooth as stowing a chart, and by the time Xanthe's voice came sleep-rough out of the dark — *"You should sleep too. Big water tomorrow if we're testing the skiff"* — Kestrel's face was composed, her hands quiet, her voice level as a horizon.

"I know," she said. "I was just reading."

It was even true. What she didn't say was that she'd finished, and that the last page of her father's handwriting had named her, and that somewhere between the cabin and the dawn she was going to have to decide whether the girl in the bunk was the protégé he'd trusted —

— or the second pair of eyes Corrigan had sent to find what the river couldn't.
