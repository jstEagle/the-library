## 9. Monsoon Days

### Kestrel

The rain found them before the cabin did.

It came down the valley like something with intent, a gray wall that erased the ridgelines one by one and then erased the river, and then erased everything but the sound of it — a sound like static, like applause, like the sky emptying itself of every secret it had ever kept. Kestrel flew the last ten minutes on instruments alone, hands light on the yoke, Xanthe silent in the right seat with the journal's oilcloth wrapping pressed to her chest like a passenger she'd been told not to drop.

The trapper's cabin sat on a bench of gravel above the flood line, exactly where her father's journal said it would be. *Hollis's old place,* he'd written, years ago, in handwriting she was only now learning to read again. *Roof leaks over the bunk but the stove draws well. Leave wood for the next fool.*

She left the plane tied down with three anchors instead of two and doubled the tie-downs because the wind came off the glacier in gusts that shoved the Cessna sideways like a drunk at closing time. By the time they had the gear hauled up the slope, both of them soaked past the point where wet mattered anymore, the world had shrunk to forty feet of visibility and the smell of spruce and cold iron.

Inside, the cabin was one room, low-beamed, dark as the inside of a pocket. Xanthe found the lantern while Kestrel got the stove lit, and when the flame caught — first the wick, then the wood — the room assembled itself around them out of the dark: a bunk with a bare frame, a table scarred by decades of knives, a shelf of tin cans with labels faded to ghosts, and a window that showed nothing at all except rain.

"Well," Xanthe said, wringing out her braid over the drain bucket. "Cozy."

"It's dry."

"Mostly dry."

"It's what we have."

Xanthe looked at her then — that long, level look she did, the cartographer's look, the one that measured things without pretending not to — and something in it made Kestrel turn back to the stove. She fed it another split of spruce and told herself the heat crawling up her neck was from the fire.

Three days, the forecast said. Maybe four. The monsoon had settled over the interior like a hen over eggs, and nothing was going anywhere: not the plane, not the river, not the truth sitting between them in an oilcloth bundle on the table.

Kestrel put water on to boil and tried not to think about how small the room was. How small it was going to feel by tomorrow. How small it already felt, with this stranger — this *her father's protégé*, this woman who knew his handwriting better than his own daughter did — sitting six feet away, watching the steam rise off the pot like it was the most interesting thing in the world.

Outside, the rain went on applauding itself.

* * *

### Xanthe

The third night, the storm stopped being weather and became a country they lived in.

Xanthe had learned its geography the way she learned all geography: by attention. The drip over the bunk, timed like a metronome gone slightly mad. The way the stove pipe whistled on north gusts, a thin high note that meant the wind had swung. The particular quality of dark beyond the window — total now, since the lantern fuel had gone to half and Kestrel had started rationing it without discussion, the way she rationed everything, silently, preemptively, as if generosity were a resource that could run out.

But tonight Kestrel had turned the wick up.

Xanthe noticed it the way you notice a door left open in winter — not as comfort, exactly, but as invitation. The pilot sat across the table with her father's journal closed under her forearm, guarding it even now, even asleep-adjacent, her eyes half-lidded and her coffee untouched. Three days of rain, three days of shared chores and careful silence, and the silence had begun to change texture. It wasn't hostile anymore. It was just full.

"You're staring," Kestrel said, without opening her eyes.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous hobby."

"My only one." Xanthe turned her tin cup slowly on the table, watching the lamplight swim in the dregs. "I've been trying to work out how to say something for about two days."

"That long? You? I thought talking was your whole skill set." But there was no edge in it. That was new too. Somewhere between splitting firewood and teaching Kestrel the difference between a spur and a ridge on the topographic sheets, the edges had worn down to something almost gentle.

"It is my skill set. Which is why I know how hard this one is." Xanthe set the cup down. "You're going to want to hear all of it before you react. Can you do that?"

Kestrel opened her eyes. In the lamplight they were the color of the river in flood — brown-green, opaque, moving fast underneath. "That depends on what it is."

"Fair." Xanthe took a breath. Outside, the rain hammered the roof like it wanted in, like it had been listening at the eaves for three days waiting for exactly this confession. "I wasn't hired by the expedition. Not really. The expedition was the cover story. I was hired — anonymously, through a broker in Whitehorse, paid in advance, no name, no company — to verify a map."

The stove ticked. The drip over the bunk kept its mad time.

"A map," Kestrel said.

"Your father's map. Or the survey data that would become his map. The valley." Xanthe held up a hand before Kestrel could speak. "You said you'd listen."

"I'm listening." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the cockpit voice, the voice that meant turbulence ahead.

"The job was simple on its face. Get close to Alden Hartwell, gain access to his field notes, confirm whether the valley existed as described — coordinates, hydrology, access routes. Report back through the broker. That was it. Verification. I told myself it was academic. Cartographic due diligence. Someone, somewhere, wanted to know if the place was real."

"And was it real?" Kestrel asked quietly. "The job?"

"No." Xanthe met her eyes and didn't flinch, because flinching would have been worse than the truth. "It was reconnaissance. I figured that out in the second week, when the broker started asking follow-up questions that weren't about geology. Who else had seen the notes. Whether Alden kept copies. Whether he trusted anyone enough to share them." She paused. "Whether he had family."

The word landed in the room like a dropped tool.

"He never mentioned a daughter," Xanthe said softly. "Not once. I thought he was alone in the world. It wasn't until we were on the river and he talked in his sleep — said a name, *Kes*, said *tell Kes the west wall lies* — that I understood there was someone. And by then I'd already decided I wasn't sending anything back through that broker. Whatever the job had been at the start, I'd quit it. Quietly. I just — didn't tell anyone I'd quit."

Kestrel hadn't moved. The lamplight stood very still in the stillness, and the rain filled the pause like a held breath.

"The west wall lies," she repeated.

"That's what he said."

"That's a survey instruction. That's—" Kestrel's hand moved to the journal, stopped, curled into a fist on the table instead. "He used to test me with stuff like that when I was a kid. Trick questions about maps. He'd draw a false feature and see if I caught it."

"Then he taught you well, because whoever reads that journal next will need it." Xanthe leaned forward. "Kestrel. I'm telling you this because you deserve the whole shape of it, not the version I've been rationing out like lantern fuel. I took money from people I now believe killed your father's expedition — or at least profited from its disappearance. I was their instrument. And then I spent nine days barricaded in a supply tent eating one ration bar a day rather than hand them what they wanted." She spread her hands, palms up, an offering or a surrender. "That's the whole map. Every contour. Do what you want with it."

The stove ticked. The drip counted. Somewhere outside, the wind found the pipe and the thin whistle rose and fell, rose and fell, like something breathing.

Kestrel looked at her for a long time. Then she reached across the table, lifted the journal from under her own arm, and set it down between them, dead center, equidistant.

"Show me the west wall," she said.

* * *

### Kestrel

They worked until the lantern burned low.

It shouldn't have felt like relief, spreading her father's final pages across a scarred table in a leaking cabin while a monsoon tried to take the roof apart. But it did. For the first time since Alder Creek — maybe for the first time in years — Kestrel's hands were busy with something that wasn't escape.

Xanthe read the field notes aloud in a low, even voice, translating her father's shorthand the way she'd clearly done a hundred times before, and Kestrel followed along on the sketch maps, catching the trick features one after another. A phantom lake drawn where the gradient said none could sit. A tributary bent fifteen degrees too far east. And there, on the western boundary of the unrecorded valley, a wall of cliffs rendered a full mile inside where the terrain logic demanded it must actually stand.

"He hid the real location inside the errors," Kestrel said, wonder making her voice strange to her own ears. "The map's only true if you know which lines are lies."

"He said once that a map should be honest to its friends and dishonest to its enemies." Xanthe tapped the false cliff line. "This isn't bad science. It's a lock. He built a map you could carry through hostile territory and it would lie for you."

Kestrel laughed — a short, cracked sound, startled out of her. "He used to hide my birthday presents behind fake contour lines. I'd spend a week triangulating. He called it *field training.*"

"What did you find, in the end?"

"Every year. Even the year I was seven." She shook her head. "God. He was doing this my whole life. Preparing me for — for *this.*" She gestured at the pages, the storm, the whole impossible inheritance of it.

"Not for this," Xanthe said. "For the valley. This is just the weather in the way."

The lamp guttered, steadied. Outside, the rain had softened without either of them noticing, from assault to murmur, the storm exhaling at last. Kestrel realized her shoulders had come down from around her ears sometime in the last hour. She realized she was warm. She realized, with a small internal shock, that she could not remember the last time she had spent an evening with another person where she hadn't been counting the minutes until she could leave.

"So the broker," she said. "Whitehorse. Anonymous client. You never got a name?"

"Never. But the payment trail was clean — too clean. Corporate money launders itself politely." Xanthe gathered the pages into their original order with a cartographer's precision, squaring the corners. "Whoever it is has resources. Patience. And they lost their instrument nine days ago when the river ate the camp. They'll want to know what happened to their investment."

"Meaning us."

"Meaning the map." Xanthe glanced up, and in the low lamplight her expression was hard to read — apology and warning braided together. "We should assume they'll come looking. Eventually. When the weather breaks."

Kestrel looked at the journal, at the false walls and lying lakes, at her father's handwriting marching across the years toward a valley no one was supposed to find. She thought about the buyout-shaped silences of the backers' phone calls, the pressure to wait, the way everyone in town suddenly knew her business the week the expedition went quiet.

"Then we'd better finish reading it before they do," she said.

Xanthe smiled — tired, crooked, real. "There's a chapter near the end I haven't been able to decode. Page forty-something. Shorthand I don't recognize. I assumed it was personal."

Kestrel reached for the journal. "Personal's usually the key to everything else. He taught me that too."

She opened to the flagged page — and stopped.

The shorthand was familiar. Not her father's. Older. She'd seen it before, years ago, in the margins of her mother's flight logs, in the letters that stopped coming, in the one box of belongings Kestrel had never been able to throw away.

Her mother's hand. On her father's final pages. Writing something neither of them had ever lived to explain.

"Xanthe," she said, and her voice came out wrong, thin, young. "I need to tell you something about my mother."
