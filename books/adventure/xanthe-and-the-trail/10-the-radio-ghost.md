## 10. The Radio Ghost

The storm broke sometime before dawn, and by the time the light came it was a thin, apologetic thing, gray as dishwater, leaking through the trapper's cabin's one small window. Kestrel had been awake for hours, lying in her sleeping bag on the plank bunk, listening to the cabin settle and drip and tick as the meltwater found new paths down the mountainside. Nine days of rain had ended. The world outside smelled like wet stone and spruce resin and something green and urgent, the way the interior always did after a monsoon passed — like everything alive was exhaling at once.

She got up quietly so as not to wake Xanthe, who was curled on the far bunk with the journal's waterproof case tucked against her chest like a child holds a stuffed animal. Kestrel had stopped pretending she didn't notice that. She'd also stopped pretending she minded.

The radio sat on the shelf above the cold stove, where it had lived since they'd hauled it up from the skiff along with the rest of what they'd salvaged. It was an old set — her father's, actually, one of the pieces of gear that had survived the flood because it had been lashed high in a tree with the food cache, which meant he'd expected trouble, which meant he'd known something. Kestrel had cleaned its contacts two nights ago while Xanthe slept, working by lamplight, and this morning, for the first time since Alder Creek went silent, it had power enough to try a transmission.

She fed it current from the solar panel propped in the window, watched the needle climb, and put on the headset. The band was quiet. That early-morning quiet that pilots know, when the night freight has landed and the day traffic hasn't lifted yet, and the whole sky feels held.

Kestrel keyed the mic. "Any station, any station, this is Hartwell, Cessna one-eight-zero, stranded upper Alder Creek, two souls, requesting relay to regional. Do you copy?"

Static. The long, oceanic static of empty frequencies.

She tried again, gave her position, gave the weather report — skies clearing, river dropping, strip still soft but flyable within days if nothing else came apart. She signed off with her call sign and sat back, and for a moment there was nothing but the hiss of the atmosphere and the drip of water off the eaves.

Then the static changed.

It was subtle at first — a texture shift, the way water sounds different over rock than over sand. Kestrel leaned forward without meaning to, one hand rising to the earcup. Underneath the white noise there was a voice. Or the ghost of one. Broken into syllables, scattered across the band like wreckage on a beach.

"...copy... Hartwell... one-eight-zero..."

She froze.

The voice was male, middle-aged by its grain, clipped and official in a way that made her think of boardrooms and charter manifests rather than bush strips. It came and went, swallowed and regurgitated by the mountains, but it was there, unmistakably there, transmitting on the emergency frequency at six in the morning from somewhere in the interior of a drainage where, as far as Kestrel knew, no radio existed except hers.

"...confirming... survivors of the Alder Creek expedition... recovered... four days ago..."

The words arrived in fragments, each one a separate act of decoding. *Recovered.* *Four days ago.* Kestrel's hands went still on the table. Four days ago she and Xanthe had been bailing out the skiff in the gorge, nearly dying, and the only other human being within fifty miles had been the man whose boot prints they'd found upstream, the man who'd cut their boat, the man with the satellite phone.

"...all personnel accounted for... expedition concluded... no further search required..."

*No further search required.*

Kestrel pulled off the headset slowly, the way you'd back away from a snare you'd just noticed at ankle height. The cabin was very quiet. Xanthe hadn't moved on her bunk, but her eyes were open now, fixed on Kestrel with the flat, total attention of someone who had been listening too.

"You heard it," Kestrel said. Not a question.

"Every word I could get." Xanthe sat up. Her hair was a wild dark halo, and there was a crease from the sleeping bag on her cheek, and none of it softened the sharpness in her face. "Recovered four days ago. All personnel accounted for. Expedition concluded." She said each phrase like she was weighing it. "That's a lie."

"That's a lie," Kestrel agreed.

"Because we're the survivors. And nobody recovered us."

"Because we're the survivors," Kestrel said, "and somebody wants the world to believe there's nothing left to find up here."

They looked at each other across the cold stove, and Kestrel felt the morning tilt under her, some load-bearing assumption giving way. She'd been operating on a simple model: storm, flood, bad luck, one saboteur on the river. A problem with edges. But a false all-clear broadcast on the emergency frequency was different. That wasn't sabotage. That was a story — a story being told to someone, carefully, officially, with the calm diction of a press release. Someone was managing the narrative of Alder Creek. And the only people whose rescue that story would erase were sitting in this cabin.

"They're not talking to us," Xanthe said slowly. "They can't know we have a receiver. They're talking to *rescuers*. To anyone monitoring the band. They're closing the file." She swung her legs off the bunk. "If a search plane comes through, if anyone checks, the answer is already waiting: everyone's been picked up, go home, nothing here."

"Except us," Kestrel said. "Two people the record says don't exist."

"Two people the record says don't exist," Xanthe echoed, "holding a map that certain people would very much like to stop existing too."

Outside, the light strengthened by degrees. Water dripped off the eaves in a steady rhythm, patient as a clock. Somewhere down the valley the river ran high and brown and loud, carrying the last of the monsoon toward the coast. It all sounded exactly like a normal morning, and that was the most frightening thing about it — that the world could sound so ordinary while a voice on the wind told it a lie about them.

Kestrel keyed the mic again. "Station calling, this is Hartwell one-eight-zero. Identify yourself. Say again your position. Over."

Static. Then, faintly, deliberately, the carrier dropped — someone releasing a transmit key. And then silence, clean and total, as though the frequency had never carried anything at all.

"They heard me," Kestrel said.

"Or they were done talking," Xanthe said. But her voice had gone careful, and Kestrel recognized the tone because she used it herself: the voice you use when you've just revised your estimate of how much danger you're in.

* * *

They spent the morning doing ordinary things with extraordinary attention. Xanthe boiled snowmelt for coffee on the camp stove; Kestrel checked the skiff's patch, checked the fuel cans, walked the perimeter of the clearing with the shotgun from the cache broken open over her arm more for the feel of it than any plan to use it. The trapper's cabin sat in a fold of ground above the river, screened by alder and deadfall, invisible from the air unless you knew where to look. Her father had known where to look. The thought arrived unbidden and stayed.

By midday the clouds had torn open enough to show blue, hard and high, and Kestrel climbed the rise behind the cabin with binoculars to read the sky. What she was looking for was anything. A contrail where no scheduled route ran. A drone's insect whine. The glint of wings in the passes.

What she saw, at 1:40 in the afternoon, was a plane.

It came up the valley from the south, low, running the river the way she herself ran rivers when she wanted to stay under terrain shadow — a technique you learned flying supply, not sightseeing. Twin-engine, dark-colored, moving with purpose. It passed below her vantage point close enough that she caught the register of its engines, a sound she knew the way she knew voices: a charter twin, the kind the backers' company kept on retainer out of the regional strip. She'd fueled beside that aircraft. She'd shared hangar coffee with its pilot.

It flew the length of the drainage without deviating, without circling, without ever once tipping a wing toward the trapper's cabin or the flooded airstrip or the wreck of the field camp. A plane with nothing to look for. A plane that had already been told there was nothing here.

Then, half a mile past the cabin, it waggled its wings once — a slow, deliberate rocking — and continued south out of the valley.

Kestrel stood on the rise with the binoculars pressed to her eyes long after the sound faded, her heart going hard and slow, like footsteps on stairs.

A wing-waggle was a signal. Every bush pilot knew it. *I see you. Message received.* But it had come half a mile *past* them, aimed at nothing, at trees, at empty air — unless it hadn't been aimed at nothing. Unless it had been aimed at whoever was watching from wherever they were watching, a confirmation passed hand to hand down a chain Kestrel couldn't see: *area clear, no survivors visible, proceed.*

Or unless it had been aimed at her. Unless somewhere behind those dark windows someone had seen the smoke from the stove pipe, or the shape of a person on a ridge, and was telling her, calmly, with the whole sky as his mouth: *we know exactly where you are.*

She went back down to the cabin and told Xanthe, and watched the color leave the other woman's face and return, controlled, deliberate, like a pilot trimming out turbulence.

"The transmission said four days ago," Xanthe said. "Which means the decision to lie was made at least four days ago. Before the flood even finished dropping. Before they could possibly have known whether anyone survived." Her fingers moved on the tabletop, tracing lines that weren't there — contour lines, Kestrel realized, the unconscious habit of a cartographer thinking in elevation. "You don't pre-write a rescue announcement unless you already know how many people are supposed to come out of the hills. They knew the number. The number was zero."

The word sat between them. Zero.

"So either they wrote us off in the flood," Kestrel said, "or they counted on the flood to do the work, and the announcement was just cleanup."

"And the man on the river," Xanthe said, "was the mop."

* * *

Night came early under the rebuilding cloud, and with it a decision neither of them wanted to say aloud, so they circled it instead, the way you circle a weather cell looking for the hole.

"We can't transmit again," Kestrel said. She'd spread the chart on the table and weighted its corners with stones, and now she stared down at it without seeing it. "Not on the emergency band, not on anything. Every word we send is a bearing. We've been shouting our position into a room where someone's standing in the dark taking notes."

"So we go silent."

"We go silent." She traced the river with one finger, down through the gorge they'd run, out toward the low country. "Which means no weather, no traffic, no help. If we go down, we go down unwitnessed."

Xanthe was quiet for a moment. "And the alternative is?"

"There isn't one. That's the point of the trick." Kestrel laughed, short and without humor. "Whoever's running this understood something basic: a missing-persons search is loud and expensive and full of witnesses. So they made sure there'd be no missing persons. Just a concluded expedition, a tidy announcement, and two ghosts in the hills that officially were never in danger because officially they were never there."

The lamp hissed softly between them. Outside, the river murmured its endless brown commentary, and higher up, faint as a held breath, came the sound Kestrel had been hearing for two days and trying not to name: a deep, tectonic groan from the direction of the glacier, followed by a crack like a rifle shot, rolling down the valley and fading.

Xanthe's head came up. "Was that—"

"Calving," Kestrel said. "Early. Way early." She felt something shift in her chest, hope and dread in the same instant, because the glacier calving early meant the ice corridor might open weeks ahead of schedule — might already be opening — which meant the way out of this valley was beginning to exist. And it meant the backers' tidy timeline, their concluded expedition, their zero survivors, was built on a calendar that the mountain had just torn up.

"They think the window is closed," Kestrel said slowly. "They announced it closed. Everyone who matters has been told the story is over. Which means—" she met Xanthe's eyes across the lamp, "—for the next little while, we're the only ones who know the door is opening."

Xanthe smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone doing arithmetic. "Then we should be ready to walk through it before whoever sent that transmission realizes the mountain disagrees with their paperwork."

The glacier groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere in the dark above the valley, tons of ancient ice let go and fell, and the sound rolled over the cabin like a verdict.

Kestrel reached over and switched off the radio. Its little dial-light died, and the frequency went quiet, and for the first time in her life the silence of the band felt less like solitude than like hiding.

Somewhere out there, a voice that claimed the survivors were safe was still echoing off the peaks, patient and official and false. And somewhere closer — downriver, or upriver, or folded into the next valley over — the man who'd cut their boat was listening to the same static, waiting to hear whether the ghosts answered.

They wouldn't answer. From tonight on, Kestrel Hartwell and her father's map and her father's protégé were going to be exactly what the transmission said they weren't: unheard, unaccounted for, and very much alive.
