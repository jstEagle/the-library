## 11. Upstream Evidence

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, and the silence it left behind was worse.

I woke in the trapper's cabin with my ears straining against that silence, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. For nine days on Alder Creek I had learned every voice of this valley — the river's low mutter, the glacier's distant groan, Kestrel's even breathing through the canvas wall we'd hung between our bunks. This morning all of them were present and accounted for, except one. The drip off the eaves had changed its rhythm. Slower. Colder air moving in behind the front, which meant the meltwater would be dropping, which meant —

Which meant the boat.

I was out from under the wool blanket before I'd finished the thought, boots half-laced, and found Kestrel already at the window with her binoculars pressed to the glass, her hair uncombed and her jaw set in the particular way I'd come to recognize as *she has already decided something and is waiting for the world to catch up*.

"Water's down maybe eight inches," she said without turning. "Skiff should be sitting on mud instead of floating loose."

"You want to check it."

"I want to check it." She lowered the binoculars. "You don't have to come."

That was an offer she made every morning, and every morning I understood it for what it was: not kindness but courtesy between two people who hadn't yet agreed to need each other. I pulled my jacket off the nail by the door. "If someone cut that hull," I said, "they did it while we were sleeping. I'd like to see their work up close."

Something flickered across her face — approval, or its close cousin — and she nodded once and started packing the kit.

* * *

The river had retreated into its banks overnight like an animal withdrawing into its den, leaving behind a skirt of gray silt and the debris of its tantrum: snapped willow whips plastered flat, a drowned muskeg smell, driftwood stacked in new and improbable architecture. The skiff sat where we'd dragged it above the high-water line, keel down, and from thirty meters away I could see what I'd been refusing to see since the day it nearly swallowed us in the gorge.

The cut was clean.

Kestrel crouched over it before I did, running her thumb along the gash in the planking near the stern. Not a split, not a stress fracture, nothing the river could have done. A straight-edged wound, the wood fibers pale and bright inside it where the blade had gone through in a single stroke. Around it, the older gray of weathered timber.

"Somebody opened this boat like a letter," she said quietly.

"And read it," I said. "And left it for us to drown in."

We worked in silence after that, tipping the skiff to look underneath, and it was there — in the shadow beneath the hull, pressed into the soft mud the flood had left — that I saw the prints.

Boot prints. Fresh ones, the edges still sharp, the tread pattern crisp as type in wet clay. Two sets, actually, though one was fainter, mostly heel-strikes, as if someone had stood here a long time and shifted their weight. Standing over the boat. Looking at the cut they'd made. Or waiting to see whether we'd take the bait.

Kestrel went very still, the way she did in the cockpit when the engine spoke a syllable she didn't like. Then she took out her phone — no signal, of course, there was never signal — and photographed the prints from four angles, methodical as a surveyor, because that was what she was under everything else: a woman who documented things so she wouldn't have to feel them yet.

"Size eleven," she said. "Vibram lug sole. Expensive." She glanced up at me. "Your expedition backers issue gear?"

"They issued stipends." My mouth had gone dry. "The field team wore whatever we brought ourselves. Half of us wore rubber boots from the co-op in Tern Harbor." I looked at the print again, at the deep, confident impression of someone who knew exactly how much weight to put where. "Nobody on Alder Creek owned boots like that."

"Then somebody came upstream after the flood." She said it flatly, factually, but I saw her hand close around the binocular strap until the knuckles whitened. "After. They waited for the water to drop enough to walk the bank."

The math of it settled over me like the cold coming down off the glacier. Someone had been here within the last — how long? The rain had masked everything for two days; before that, the flood. These prints predated the last rainfall, barely. Which meant that while Kestrel and I had been arguing about contour lines and rationing chocolate, while we'd been trading stories by lamplight in the cabin like castaways building a country out of conversation, someone had walked past us. Close enough to touch the boat. Close enough, if they'd wanted, to touch us.

"Why not just take the map?" I asked, and heard my own voice come out smaller than I intended. "If they were standing right here. Why cut the boat and leave?"

Kestrel stood, scanning the treeline with those binoculars, sweeping the alder thickets and the slide slopes with the patience of someone who had learned to look for things that didn't want to be seen. "Because a drowning looks like weather," she said. "A theft looks like a crime. They wanted the river to do the work." She lowered the glasses. "It almost did."

* * *

We followed the prints upstream.

I told myself it was reconnaissance, sound practice, the kind of thing any careful person would do, but the truth was uglier and simpler: I needed to know. Nine days alone in a supply tent had taught me that not-knowing was the thing that actually ate you — not the dark, not the hunger, but the imagination filling the silence with shapes. I had spent nine days inventing watchers. Now there might be a real one, and some stubborn cartographer's instinct in me demanded coordinates.

The prints ran along the high bank where the gravel firmed up, unhurried, evenly spaced. Whoever it was hadn't been sneaking. That frightened me more than running would have. Running means fear; walking means confidence, and confidence on this river meant either local knowledge or satellite imagery telling them exactly where we were and how long the water would keep us.

Kestrel moved ahead of me the way water moves — finding the low ground, reading the terrain three bends ahead. Twice she raised a fist and we stopped, breath held, listening to nothing. The valley gave us back our own sounds: our boots, the river, a raven somewhere overhead making its dry wooden comment on the proceedings.

At the third bend, where an old log jam had grounded itself against the cutbank, the prints left the gravel and climbed toward a flat shelf of moss under a leaning spruce. And there, half-sunk in the sphagnum like something the forest had tried to swallow and failed, lay a phone.

Not a phone like mine. A satellite handset, ruggedized, matte black, the kind that costs more than a season's wages and works anywhere on earth. Its little antenna stood up out of the moss like a periscope. One corner of the casing was scuffed bright, and beside it, pressed into the mud, the Vibram print — planted, weight forward, as if its owner had knelt here to make a call.

Kestrel didn't reach for it. She crouched, studied it from three sides, and looked at me. "Trap?"

"It's not wired to anything," I said. "No trip lines, no disturbance in the moss except his knee." I hesitated. "But it'll have a GPS chip in it. If it's still powered on, picking it up tells whoever owns it exactly where it is."

"So it's a beacon wearing a disguise." Her mouth twisted. "Or litter from a man who doesn't care who finds his secrets."

"We could leave it."

"We could." She was quiet a moment, watching the phone sit there in its bed of green, patient as a spider. Then she pulled a bandana from her pocket, wrapped her hand, and picked it up anyway — holding it by the antenna, like something venomous. "But if I'm going to be hunted," she said, "I want to know the caliber."

The screen lit under her wrapped fingers. No passcode — arrogance, or hurry, or both. She thumbed through it with the efficiency of a woman who had grown up fixing radios, and I watched her face change as she read.

"What?" My heart was going hard. "What is it?"

"Call log." She turned the screen toward me. "Last outgoing call, six days ago. Before the flood peaked." She scrolled. "Incoming yesterday, 0417. Duration eleven seconds. Missed."

Six days ago. The night the skiff was cut. Yesterday, before dawn — while we slept fifty meters from this moss shelf, someone had stood here trying to raise someone else, and failed, and walked away unhurried, leaving the phone behind like a man leaving a coat he expected to retrieve later.

"The contacts are empty," Kestrel said. "Wiped. But the browser history isn't." She scrolled once more and then went very still, and I saw the color drain out of her face in the gray light until she looked carved from it.

She handed me the phone without a word.

The page on the screen was a corporate portal — Meridian Terra Resources, the expedition's principal backer, the name that had appeared on every funding letter and every crate of dehydrated rations. But this wasn't the public site. It was a contractor login, a logistics dashboard, and even I could read the shape of what it showed: asset tracking, personnel manifests, a task list with one active item highlighted.

**RECOVERY — ALDER CREEK SITE. PRIORITY ONE. DO NOT ENGAGE SUBJECTS DIRECTLY.**

Subjects. Plural. Us.

Below it, a timestamped note, terse as a telegram: *River option compromised. Subjects alive. Awaiting window.*

"Awaiting window," I repeated. The words tasted like glacier melt, cold and mineral and old. "Waiting for what window?"

Kestrel took the phone back and looked north, up-valley, toward the white wall of the icefield hidden behind the ridgelines. I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing — the calving season, the meltwater, the weeks until the glacier route opened — and I watched her arrive at the answer before I said it aloud.

"The same window we're waiting for," she said. "They can't get to us by land or air until the ice opens. Neither can we leave." She laughed, one short note with no humor in it at all. "We're all three of us locked in this valley together, and they've got a schedule."

* * *

We didn't talk on the way back. There was a different quality to the silence now, the silence of two people listening past each other for a fourth set of footsteps. Kestrel kept the phone in her outer pocket, wrapped in the bandana, and twice I caught her touching it through the fabric like a talisman, or a wound.

At the cabin she swept the whole interior before she let herself relax — such as she relaxed — checking the floorboards, the chimney, the cache under the bunk where we kept the rifle and the flare gun. Nothing disturbed. Nothing touched. Which meant our watcher was disciplined, or distant, or both.

Kestrel sat down heavily on the bunk and turned the satellite phone over in her hands. Outside, the light was beginning to fail behind the clouds, the short northern afternoon folding itself away.

"'Do not engage directly,'" she quoted softly. "You know what that means?"

"That they're cowards."

"It means they think we're dangerous." She looked up at me, and there was something in her eyes I hadn't seen before — not fear, exactly. Recognition. "Xanthe, they cut a boat and walked away. They're playing a long game with rules. Which means there's a version of this where they stop following the rules." She set the phone on the table between us, screen dark, antenna pointing at the ceiling like a compass needle that had finally found its north. "And it means your anonymous employer — the one who hired you to verify the map —"

"Was never anonymous to them," I finished. The thought landed in my stomach like a stone. All those encrypted emails, the wire transfers from a shell consultancy, the careful instructions. I had told myself I was a freelancer with a discreet client. Sitting in this cabin, with a contractor's dashboard glowing on a dead man's phone, I understood that I had been a piece on a board, moved by the same hand that had sent the man in the expensive boots. Maybe hired to verify the map. Maybe hired to lead them to it.

Maybe hired to lead *Kestrel* to it.

"I need to tell you something," I started, "about the contract —"

The radio crackled.

Both of us froze. The set sat on its shelf by the window, the one Kestrel had repaired with solder and stubbornness, tuned to the expedition frequency out of habit, hissing static into the dim room. Static, and then — threading through it, faint and warped by distance and weather, a human voice.

Kestrel lunged for the dial, easing it, coaxing it, and the voice swam up out of the noise, clipped and professional:

*"— repeat, all Alder Creek personnel recovered, medevac complete, families notified. Expedition concluded. Anyone monitoring this frequency, please stand down—"*

Kestrel's hand hovered over the set. Slowly, deliberately, she reached back and switched it off.

The silence came back, total now, pressing in from all sides.

"That's a lie," I whispered.

"Yeah." Kestrel stared at the dead radio, and then at the black phone lying on the table, and I saw her make some decision, deep and quiet, the way she made all of them. "Somebody wants the world to believe we're already dead."

Outside, far downstream, so faint it might have been wind — a single, deliberate click. Like a radio transmitting.

Or a camera shutter.
