## 12. The Valley in the Pages

The rain had found a new rhythm by the third night in the trapper's cabin — not the monsoon's drumming assault but something slower, almost patient, like fingers tapping on a lid from underneath. Kestrel lay awake listening to it and thinking about lids.

Xanthe slept in the far bunk, curled toward the wall with the map tube clutched against her chest even now, even unconscious, as if the cardboard might roll away without her. The oil lamp had burned down to a coin of light. Somewhere under the floorboards a mouse was conducting its own small expedition, and outside, the river that had eaten their airstrip spoke in its sleep.

Kestrel had not opened the journal.

It had been six days since she'd pulled it from the wreck of the supply tent, six days since she'd felt its waterproof casing slick against her palm while Xanthe catalogued salvaged gear ten feet away, oblivious. Six days of carrying it the way you carry something hot — close to the body, never quite touching it with your whole hand. She'd told herself she was waiting for privacy. Then she'd told herself she was waiting for the right frame of mind. Then she'd stopped telling herself anything at all, because the truth was simpler and worse: she was afraid of what her father's handwriting would do to her.

She'd spent eleven years practicing not-thinking about Elias Hartwell. It was a discipline, like flying in cloud — you trusted the instruments, you kept your hands level, you did not look at the gray void pressing against the glass or think about how much of the world was hidden inside it. She could go months without his name surfacing. And then Alder Creek had happened, and now he was everywhere: in the bent tent poles, in the coffee tin Xanthe had rationed so carefully, in the way the man had clearly taught this stranger to read terrain the way he'd once tried to teach Kestrel to read weather, back when she was twelve and furious and wouldn't learn.

She sat up. The bunk frame complained. Across the cabin, Xanthe didn't stir.

Kestrel dressed in the dark out of habit, boots laced quietly, and took the journal and the lamp to the table by the window. The window itself was a sheet of river ice gone gray with trapped air; the rain wrote its slow code against the roof. She set the lamp down, adjusted the wick until the flame stood tall and steady, and unzipped the casing.

The journal smelled of river silt and wax and, faintly, of him — cedar shaving soap, the same bar he'd kept on the sink of every house they'd ever lived in, the one constant in a childhood of moving. Her throat tightened around it. She breathed through it. Instruments steady. Hands level.

The early pages were water-swollen at the edges but legible, written in the small, slanted, impatient hand she remembered from grocery lists and postcards that never came. Dates ran back fourteen months. There were supply inventories, radio schedules, sketches of river gauges. Mundane things. Expedition bookkeeping. She turned pages faster, looking for —

Looking for what? An apology? An explanation for eleven years of silence? A letter addressed to *Kestrel* that would make all of it make sense?

What she found instead, forty pages in, was a map.

He'd drawn it freehand across two facing pages, in pencil later inked over, and at first glance it looked like any survey chart — contour lines stacked like fingerprints, a river braiding through the middle, elevation marks climbing toward a ridge system on the east. But the coordinates were wrong. Not mistaken-wrong. *Deliberate*-wrong. Shifted, she realized after a long moment of staring, by exactly the kind of offset a man would use if he suspected someone else might read his notes and wanted them to find nothing. She knew because she'd done the same thing herself on flight plans, once, when a charter broker with too many questions had asked to see her route into restricted country. He'd taught her that without ever teaching her anything. Some inheritance skips the telling and goes straight to the blood.

Beneath the map, in tighter lettering than the rest:

*Coordinates true only in final section. If found, disregard everything above.*

Her heart was doing something arrhythmic. She turned pages with more care now, each one a held breath. The entries grew shorter, denser, the handwriting losing its impatience and gaining something else — urgency, yes, but also a strange steadiness, like a man who has stopped arguing with the storm and started working with it.

*Aug. 9.* The backers sent another "logistics liaison." Voss. He asks about land tenure, not science. Asks who holds mineral rights adjacent to the survey grid. I have started keeping a second set of books for him.

*Sept. 2.* Confirmed today from the high bench. The valley exists. Not a rumor, not a glacial melt channel — a full drainage, thirty-some kilometers, forested floor, its own microclimate. The ice dam on the north arm has held it sealed off from every map ever drawn. The river goes UNDER the moraine and comes up miles south. That's why nobody found it. You don't fly over it. You fly over a wall of ice and see nothing.

*Sept. 14.* Xanthe is better with a plane table than I was at thirty. She doesn't know why I keep the real coordinates separate and I won't tell her yet. Trust is a thing you spend carefully out here.

Kestrel looked over at the sleeping form in the far bunk. So he *hadn't* told her. Whatever Xanthe thought she knew about why she'd been hired, there were layers under it, and her father had been the one holding the deepest layer shut. Something about that loosened a knot Kestrel hadn't known she was tied in — not satisfaction, exactly, but a grim kinship. He'd kept secrets from everyone. At least she wasn't uniquely excluded anymore.

*Oct. 3.* Voss offered me a consulting fee that would buy a house in Whitehorse. For "assistance relocating the survey focus." Translation: stop mapping the interior of the valley, start mapping its edges, so the company can define a claim boundary before anyone else knows it's there. I declined. He smiled like a man revising a budget.

*Oct. 19.* Found a marker stake on the south bench that isn't ours. Orange flagging, machine-stamped tag. Somebody has been in here ahead of us. I burned it.

*Nov. 1.* Radio acting strange. Receiving fine, transmitting intermittent. Could be moisture. Could be moisture.

That last line made the skin on her arms prickle. *Could be moisture.* His dry humor, intact to the end. But also: someone may already have been inside their radios nine days before the flood. Before the check-in that never came. She thought of the garbled transmission they'd caught last week claiming survivors had been evacuated — the voice that didn't belong to anyone. Thought of the skiff taking water faster than a patched hull should. Thought of boot prints upstream, fresh, pointing toward camp.

The lamp flame leaned in a draft she couldn't feel. She turned the page.

And stopped.

The final section began here — a dozen pages, dense, written in the last weeks, and the first thing she saw was a second map. This one covered the whole spread, larger scale, and it was beautiful. Even half-blind with grief she could see that. He'd rendered the valley the way old cartographers rendered places they loved: every bend of the river given a name, every tributary labeled in his cramped capitals, contour lines flowing like something alive. A lake shaped like a lung. A hanging valley feeding a fall marked *Maren's Thread* — and Kestrel's breath caught, because Maren was her mother's name, dead twenty years, and she'd never once heard him speak it aloud.

He'd named the pass at the valley's southern gate *Maren's Pass*. He'd named the eastern ridge *The Kestrel's Wing*, and beside it, smaller: *for my daughter, who flies.*

She put her hand flat on the page, over the ink, as if she could press the warmth back into it. The rain ticked on the roof. The mouse had gone quiet. In the far bunk, Xanthe breathed slow and even, and Kestrel was suddenly, fiercely glad the girl was asleep, because whatever her face was doing right now, she didn't want witnesses.

She read on.

*Nov. 8.* If you are reading this and you are not Kestrel, put it down. I mean that. What follows is not for funders, not for companies, not for the curious. It is for one person.

*Nov. 8, continued.* Kestrel. If it's you — and I've thought about how to write this for three weeks, and every version sounds like a man making excuses, so I'll skip to the part that matters.

I know what I owe you an explanation for. Eleven years of it. I told myself I stayed away to protect you from the people who circled this project, and that was half true. The other half is that I didn't know how to come back as anything less than the man I was when I left, and I wasn't that man anymore. Pride is just fear wearing good boots. I should have called. I'm writing it here because I couldn't say it on a phone, which is cowardice, and you can be angry about it, and you should be.

But here is the part I need you to hear.

This valley cannot be unmapped. Once it's recorded, it belongs to whoever gets the record filed first — and the people backing this expedition do not intend to file it. They intend to hold it. A drainage this size, unclaimed, unrecorded, sitting on top of whatever the geology says it's sitting on — Voss's employers would rather it stay a ghost than become public knowledge on anyone's terms but theirs. I have watched them move pieces for a year. The flood, when it comes, will be convenient for them. Disasters erase witnesses.

So I am leaving you the only thing I have that's worth anything. Not money — God knows there's none. The survey. The true coordinates, the field data, the photographs, all of it, sealed in the aluminum case under the floor of the supply tent, third board from the door. The journal tells you where. The map tells you what. But neither of those is the inheritance.

The inheritance is this: finish it. Fly the corridor when the ice opens. Photograph the valley entire, log the positions, and get the record to people who will make it public before anyone can bury it. It has to be flown by someone who can read weather the way I taught you to hate reading it, and it has to be someone they can't buy, and I only ever knew one person who was both.

You'll be angry that I'm asking. Be angry. Anger flies well. It's grief that stalls engines.

I named the ridge for you because you were always the one who went over the top of things. Finish the survey, Kestrel. Then live your life however you want, and know that wherever you flew, I was proud of the shadow your wings made.

— E.H., Alder Creek, the ninth of November. Nine days before the river rose.

Kestrel sat very still for a long time.

The lamp had burned lower; she didn't trim it. The rain kept its patient rhythm. Inside her, everything was moving and nothing was — the way the air feels in the seconds after a stall warning stops and before the nose comes down, that suspended weightlessness that isn't peace at all, it's physics deciding what happens next.

*Third board from the door.*

She was on her feet before she'd decided to stand, boots silent, moving to the corner where the salvaged gear lay sorted under tarps. No — the aluminum case hadn't come back with them. They'd taken what floated: food, fuel, the radio, the skiff parts, the journal itself, which she'd found wedged in the collapsed tent wall where the floodwater had shoved it. The supply tent's floor had been buried under the slide scree when they searched. Buried, or moved, or already dug up by whoever left those boot prints.

She stood in the dark middle of the cabin with the journal open in her hands and let the arithmetic finish itself. If the case was still there, it was under tons of wet scree. If it wasn't there, then Voss — or whoever Voss answered to — already had the coordinates, and everything her father had died protecting was already in a briefcase headed somewhere with lawyers in it.

Unless. Unless the watcher was still looking. Unless that was why someone was still on the river, nine days after the flood, walking the banks in fresh boots. They didn't have it either. Everyone was hunting the same ghost, and the ghost was a floorboard under a mountain of mud.

"Kestrel."

She flinched hard enough to rattle the lamp. Xanthe was sitting up in the bunk, hair loose, eyes finding her across the dark with that unnerving cartographer's precision — the girl missed nothing, had missed nothing since the day Kestrel had found her barricaded in a supply tent with a signal mirror and a rifle she clearly didn't know how to use.

"You're reading it," Xanthe said. Not a question. Her voice was rough with sleep, and something else. "The journal. You've been carrying it for six days like it was going to bite you, and now you're reading it at three in the morning standing up." She swung her legs off the bunk. "What does it say?"

Kestrel looked down at the open pages, at her father's last words to her, at the ridge named for her wings. She thought about trust spent carefully. She thought about how many secrets this cabin could hold before its walls gave out.

She thought about the fact that whoever was out there in the rain had been hired by the same people who had hired Xanthe — anonymously, Xanthe had admitted, lamplight and shame between them — to verify a map. Or steal one.

"It says," Kestrel said slowly, "that my father hid a sealed case somewhere under the supply tent slide. Coordinates. Field data. The whole true survey." She closed the journal. "And it says the people funding this expedition want that valley to stay a ghost."

Xanthe had gone very pale in the lamplight. "Then we have to get back to the slide."

"The river's up four feet since yesterday. The gorge is a whitewater chute, and the skiff barely held together on flat water." Kestrel heard her own voice go flat and pilot-cold, the voice she used for go/no-go decisions. "And if the case is still there, the man with the fresh boots is camped between us and it."

Outside, beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, carried up the valley on the wind that funneled through the trapper's pass — a sound neither of them could pretend they hadn't heard.

An engine. Small. Aircraft. Circling low, once, twice, and then gone south into the murk.

Xanthe whispered what they were both thinking: "They're looking for it too."

* * *

They didn't sleep again.

By lamplight they built the plan the way you build anything that has to hold weight — honestly, and with no spare parts. Kestrel spread the salvage inventory on the table: twelve liters of usable fuel, the patched skiff, two paddles, the rifle with nine rounds, rope, the tarp, three days of food if they stretched it. Xanthe drew the river from memory — she'd been sketching it daily since the flood, contour by contour, and her memory for water was better than Kestrel's, which was a sentence Kestrel had never expected to think.

"The slide is eight kilometers downstream," Xanthe said, tapping the page. "Downstream is fast but it's runnable if we line the boat past the first chute. Coming back is the problem. Eight kilometers upriver against current that's running at—" she glanced at Kestrel, "—what, ten knots in the narrows?"

"More. Twelve, maybe, in the gorge." Kestrel stared at the little penciled river. "We can't paddle back up. We run down, dig, and then we walk out along the north bank to the glacier toe and hope the corridor opens before we starve."

"That's four days' walk in monsoon."

"That's the option that exists."

Xanthe was quiet for a moment, studying the map, and then she said, without looking up: "Your father trusted you with this. Out of everyone. He wrote that?"

Kestrel's jaw worked. "He wrote that."

"Then we dig." Xanthe rolled up her map with the crisp economy Kestrel was beginning to recognize as the girl's version of resolve. "At first light. Before the rain thickens — it always thins around dawn, you've noticed, there's a drainage wind that pushes the front back up-valley for maybe two hours."

Kestrel blinked at her. "You've been reading the weather."

"I've been watching it for nine days with nothing else to do." The ghost of a smile crossed Xanthe's face and vanished. "Someone had to."

Dawn came gray and thin, and the rain did ease — the promised two-hour window opening in the east like a seam. They loaded the skiff in silence that was not unfriendly, that had stopped being unfriendly somewhere during the cold days and hadn't fully recovered its warmth but had at least stopped bleeding. Kestrel stowed the journal inside her jacket, against her ribs, zipped in the inner pocket where she kept her license and her mother's photograph. Where it belonged. Where it had apparently always been headed.

She pushed the skiff into the brown, roaring water and climbed in aft, and Xanthe took the bow paddle, and for a moment they both just looked at each other across the twelve feet of patched rubber and borrowed courage between them.

"For the record," Kestrel said over the river's noise, "if we capsize in the gorge, the journal floats. Grab the journal."

"And you?"

"I float too. I'm mostly ego."

Xanthe laughed — short, surprised, real — and dug her paddle in, and the current took them.

Eight kilometers downstream, under the thinning rain, the wreck of the Alder Creek camp waited in its shroud of scree. And somewhere above the river, unseen, the sound of that engine still hung in the wet air like a question neither of them wanted to answer.
