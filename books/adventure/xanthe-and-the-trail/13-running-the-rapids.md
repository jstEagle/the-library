## 13. Running the Rapids

### Kestrel

The boot prints went upstream. The satellite phone, dead and salt-crusted with river silt, had gone into Kestrel's jacket pocket like a stone she couldn't put down.

"He'll know we found it," Xanthe said behind her, voice low, as if the trees were listening. "The phone. He'll come back for it."

"Then we're not here when he does." Kestrel was already moving, already doing the arithmetic she hated: the skiff was moored two bends down, the gorge began three bends after that, and the watcher — whoever he was, whatever name the backers had pinned to him — would be back at his own camp within the hour to find his phone missing and his tracks read like a confession. They had maybe ninety minutes of grace, and grace wasn't a word this river had ever used.

They ran. Not the panicked sprint of prey — Kestrel refused that — but the fast, low, deliberate movement of people who had learned, separately and painfully, what it cost to be caught unprepared. Xanthe carried the map tube strapped across her back like a rifle. Kestrel carried the journal inside her coat, against her sternum, where she could feel it rise and fall with her breathing like a second heartbeat.

The skiff sat where they'd left it, upside down on the gravel bar, hull patched twice over with resin and canvas and one strip of Xanthe's emergency blanket, which had been her idea and which Kestrel had privately mocked until it held. The river had climbed another foot overnight. It moved now with the sound of freight — a long, rolling thunder that came up through the soles of their boots before it reached their ears.

Kestrel stood at the water's edge and looked downstream at the gorge and felt her stomach do something cold and professional.

"That's not a river anymore," Xanthe said. "That's a landslide with a current."

"It's a corridor." Kestrel flipped the skiff. The hull was sound. The patches were holding. The thwarts were lashed, the gear was bagged and tied down, and the single oar-lock on the starboard side still bore the crack they'd wedged shut with a tent stake. Everything about the boat said *maybe*. "It runs straight through to the lower flats. Walking around takes four days through country where that man has already proven he can move faster than us. Running it takes forty minutes."

"Running it takes dying," Xanthe said. But she was already climbing in.

Kestrel paused with one foot on the gunwale. "You don't have to—"

"I'm coming." Xanthe's jaw was set in a way Kestrel was beginning to recognize as the cartographer's version of her own stubbornness — the same stone, different quarry. "You don't run rapids alone. That's not bravery, that's just drowning with an audience of none."

Something in Kestrel's chest loosened half a degree. She pushed off.

* * *

### Xanthe

The first mile was survivable, which Xanthe understood immediately to be the river's way of lying to them.

They rode the flood channel down the center of the valley, the banks blurring past in sheets of green and gray, rain needling sideways out of a sky the color of wet slate. Kestrel handled the single oar with a economy that fascinated Xanthe even while her hands were clamped white-knuckled to the gunwale — no wasted stroke, no correction made twice, the blade entering and leaving the water like punctuation. She'd watched the woman fly the same way, once, from the ground: total attention, zero drama.

"Rock!" Kestrel's voice cut flat across the roar. "Left side — lean right!"

Xanthe threw her weight starboard and the skiff swung, kissed past a boulder the size of a truck cab with six inches to spare, and the wake slapped them both full in the face with water cold enough to stop thought.

"You okay?"

"Ask me when my heart starts again!"

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. Then the valley walls began to close.

Xanthe had mapped this gorge. On paper. In ink, at a scale of one to fifty thousand, from aerial photographs taken in July by someone in a comfortable plane who had never once imagined water could look like this. The paper version showed a tidy slot canyon, two hundred meters wide, gentle gradient, class two at summer flow. What the paper version did not show — what no map could show — was what two weeks of monsoon did to a river confined between granite walls. The water had risen into the canyon's throat and turned it into a pipe. Standing waves marched downstream in ranks. The roar came off the walls and doubled and redoubled until Xanthe could feel it in her teeth, in the bones behind her eyes, in the small terrified animal of her chest.

"Kestrel." She had to shout directly into the other woman's ear. "There's a sieve at the bend! My map shows a rock garden below the confluence — if the water's up, it'll be a strainer—"

"I know." Kestrel's eyes never left the current. Rain ran off her chin in ropes. "I've flown it."

"Flying it and running it are not—"

"They're the same skill," Kestrel said, and there was something in her voice then, something that sounded less like confidence than like inheritance, like a sentence handed down, "you read the terrain ahead and you commit before you have all the information. You just do it faster in a plane because you die faster when you're wrong."

And then the gorge took them.

* * *

### Kestrel

Later, Kestrel would try to reconstruct those twenty minutes and fail, because memory under adrenaline doesn't record — it fragments. What remained were shards:

The bend arriving faster than any bend should arrive, the whole river leaning into it like a runner into a turn, and her hauling the oar across her body with her shoulders screaming, feeling the hull heel until the river was looking up at her through the starboard gunwale—

The sieve. Just as Xanthe had promised: a chaos of flood-swallowed boulders at the outside of the curve, water pouring *through* them instead of around, the gaps between the rocks sucking down in black inverted whirlpools that would hold a boat the way a fist holds a moth. She'd seen a raft pinned in a sieve once, years ago, on a river she didn't name anymore. She remembered how long it took to get the bodies out.

"Hard left! LEFT—" Xanthe's voice, cracking, and Xanthe's weight already throwing itself portside without waiting for the order, and the skiff slewing, sliding, the current grabbing the stern and swinging them like a gate—

A gap. There was a gap. One gap, boat-wide, maybe boat-and-a-prayer wide, a lane of green water threading between two house-sized stones, and Kestrel stopped steering and started *choosing*, the way she chose in cloud, the way her father must have chosen in country like this with a map tube on his back — you pick your line and you give yourself to it completely, because hesitation is just a slower way of crashing.

"Hold — everything — still—"

The rocks rose on either side. The noise became physical, a pressure. Spray exploded off the upstream faces and hung in the air like weather. And for three seconds — three seconds that contained most of what Kestrel Hartwell had ever believed about herself — the skiff ran a corridor of moving water between two walls of granite older than anything human, and she was flying again, low and slow and committed, except this time the mountain was trying to eat her.

Then the gap spat them out and the river dropped away beneath them.

Not gently. A ledge — a pour-over, the kind of thing that on paper would have been a charming little waterfall and in flood was a two-meter step of brown violence — and the bow lifted, hung, and slammed down into the trough beyond with a impact that went through the hull, through the thwarts, through Kestrel's spine and teeth. The patched section aft cracked like a shot.

Water came in.

* * *

### Xanthe

"We're taking water!" Xanthe had the bail bucket — a cut-down fuel jug — going before Kestrel finished shouting, scooping in a rhythm that was mostly panic wearing discipline's clothes. Cold water swirled around her shins, her knees. The patch on the aft quarter had split along its seam and the river was coming through in a steady, muscular pulse, like the boat had grown a heart and it was beating backwards.

"How bad?" Kestrel called.

"If I stop bailing, very!"

"Then don't stop!"

The gorge was opening now, the walls falling back, the worst of the drops behind them — but the river didn't care about their relief. It ran fast and heavy through the widening valley, and every minute in the water was a minute the watcher might be watching, and every minute bailing was a minute the boat grew heavier and sat lower and shipped more.

Xanthe bailed. Her arms burned. Her hands had gone past pain into a strange buzzing numbness. And somewhere in the middle of it — mid-scoop, mid-roar, soaked to the bone and more frightened than she had been in nine days alone at Alder Creek — she caught herself laughing.

"What?" Kestrel shouted, hearing it.

"Nothing! Nothing, keep rowing!"

But it wasn't nothing. It was this: nine days ago she had been alone behind a barricaded tent flap, rationing crackers, signaling a sky that never answered, and she had told herself that if she ever got out, she would never again trust anyone with her life, because everyone she'd trusted had floated downstream or sold her out or both. And now here she was in a leaking boat in flooded rapids, trusting a stranger with her father's temper and her mother's name stitched somewhere in the geography, and the trust felt — not safe, nothing about this was safe — but *correct*. Like a contour line finally closing on itself.

"Rock ahead, center channel!" Kestrel's voice again, and Xanthe leaned when she was told to lean, bailed when she could, and together — clumsily, loudly, with more water aboard than either would admit — they ran the last of it out onto the long, slow, braided shallows of the lower flats, where the river spread out and forgot its anger and the rain softened to a gray whisper.

The skiff grounded on a gravel bar with a grinding sigh. Xanthe kept bailing until Kestrel's hand closed over hers and stilled it.

"We're aground," Kestrel said. "You can stop."

Xanthe looked down at the water sloshing around her calves and discovered her legs wouldn't entirely obey. Kestrel, without comment, took her elbow and hauled her upright, and the two of them stood swaying in the shallows like a pair of drunks, streaming, gasping, alive.

* * *

### Kestrel

The inventory, conducted on the gravel bar in fading light, was short and ugly.

The skiff was holed but repairable — the same seam, worse now; it would take the rest of the resin and probably the last of the canvas, and after that they had no patches left. The oar had survived. The food bag had survived, barely, though the matches were a lost cause and half the flour had become paste. The radio was dead — drowned sometime during the sieve, its casing full of river, its silence suddenly enormous. And the journal—

Kestrel's hands went to her coat and stopped working.

It wasn't there.

She went through the pockets once, twice, a third time with a speed that tipped over into tearing, and the world narrowed to a gray tunnel edged in static. *No.* Not this. The river could take the boat, the radio, the flour, her hands, her —

"Kestrel." Xanthe's voice, very calm, the calm of someone talking a person back from a ledge. "Think. When did you last feel it?"

"The — the drop. Before the drop. I checked it at the bend, I felt it through my coat—"

"Then it's in the boat or it's in the water between the bend and here." Xanthe was already wading back toward the skiff, turning it, tipping it, and Kestrel dropped to her knees in the shallows beside her and together they went through the hull like women searching a wound. Gear bags emptied. Thwarts lifted. The broken patch flapped under Kestrel's fingers, mocking her.

And then Xanthe made a sound — small, sharp — and pulled, from underneath the forward seat where it had slid during the drop, wedged into the chine between hull plank and frame, sodden but whole, the oilskin-wrapped rectangle that weighed less than a bird and more than everything else in the world combined.

Kestrel took it in both hands and couldn't speak.

"It's fine," Xanthe said quietly. "Oilskin did its job. See? He built things to last, your father."

He. *Your father.* Xanthe had never said it so plainly before — had always skirted the word, the relationship, the whole drowned continent between them. Kestrel looked up, and the rain was coming down soft now, and the light was going blue and silver over the flats, and this stranger with river water in her boots was kneeling in a gravel bar holding her family's ghost out to her with both hands.

"Thank you," Kestrel managed.

"Don't thank me." Xanthe's mouth twisted — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "Just next time, strap it to your body. Some things you tie down. Some things you wear."

They made camp above the high-water line, a fireless camp, cold rations, the repaired patch curing under a tarp. Kestrel sat with the journal dry and warm against her knees and did not open it, because she knew what waited in the final pages and she wanted one more night of not knowing.

It was Xanthe, wrapped in the emergency blanket, staring downstream into the dark, who broke the silence.

"The radio's dead," she said. "So we can't call anyone. And the boat needs a day to cure. So we can't move." A pause. "Which means if he's following the river — and he is, it's the only way — he knows exactly where we are. We just can't hear him coming."

Kestrel followed her gaze downstream, to where the river vanished into the dark, carrying its freight of silt and secrets toward the coast. Somewhere down there, or up there, or out there in the wet black country between, a man with missing satellite phone was making his own calculations.

"Then tomorrow," Kestrel said, "we build a signal fire big enough to see from the air — and a hiding place big enough to disappear from the ground. Both. Because whatever comes down that river tomorrow—"

She stopped. Because she heard it first: faint, upstream, threaded thin through the rain and the dark.

An engine. A boat engine, running quiet, running slow, the way a man runs an engine when he doesn't want to be heard.

Xanthe had gone rigid. "Tell me that's the wind."

Kestrel put her hand on the journal, and killed the last ember of their fire with her boot heel, and watched the darkness upstream where the sound was growing.

"No," she said. "Get down."
