## 14. The Watcher

The fog came off the river at dawn like something breathing.

Kestrel stood at the mouth of the trapper's cabin with a mug of coffee gone cold in her hands, watching the water slide past in that heavy, muscular way it had since the rains began. Somewhere upstream, hidden in the gray, was the man who owned the satellite phone. The man who had cut their boat. The man who had been on this river for days while they huddled and rationed and told each other stories by lamplight like children playing at survival.

She'd slept maybe two hours. Xanthe had slept less. They hadn't spoken about it directly — they never spoke about anything directly anymore, not since the burning of the buyout letter, not since the confession that sat between them like a stone neither would step around — but Kestrel had seen her checking the rifle's action by candlelight, and she'd said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the gesture didn't already say better.

"We should go today," Xanthe said from inside. Her voice was rough with sleep. "Before he comes to us."

Kestrel turned. Xanthe stood in the doorway of the inner room, hair braided tight for travel, the journal wrapped in oilcloth and strapped against her ribs where she'd taken to carrying it. She looked thinner than when Kestrel had pulled her out of that supply tent nine days and a lifetime ago. They both did. Monsoon rations did that.

"He knows where we are," Kestrel said. "The cabin. He's known since the boat."

"Then we choose the ground." Xanthe came to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, not quite. "Not him."

* * *

They went down to the river through mist so thick it beaded on their eyelashes. The skiff — patched twice now, once after the sabotage and once after the gorge, its hull scarred with epoxy and stubbornness — sat where they'd hauled it above the flood line. Kestrel checked the seams out of habit, though she'd checked them twice last night. Some rituals you perform because they keep your hands busy while your mind does harder work.

The plan was simple, which is to say it was desperate. The boot prints they'd found yesterday ran north along the bank toward the old survey hut at the bend below the moraine — a structure marked on exactly one map in the world, the one sewn into the lining of her father's journal. If the watcher was holed up there waiting out the weather, then he was waiting alone, and he was waiting between them and the only route to the glacier overlook where the plane sat buried under canvas and gravel.

So they went to him. Better the devil you can see.

The river carried them the two miles in silence broken only by the oar-locks' small complaint. Kestrel watched the banks slide past — alder and black cottonwood, the water-stained trunks showing high-water marks like old bruises — and thought about her father's hands. She'd been thinking about his hands a lot lately. In the journal he wrote about them constantly: *the knuckles ache before rain, an old man's barometer.* She'd inherited those hands. Hers ached now too, and the rain was coming back.

The survey hut materialized out of the fog all at once, close enough to touch — a low rectangle of weathered planks beneath a sagging tin roof, door hanging open on darkness.

"Door was closed," Xanthe murmured. "On the aerial photo from Dad's—" She stopped herself. Old habits. "On the reference photo. It was closed."

Kestrel grounded the skiff on the gravel shelf and stepped out with the rifle slung across her back, not raised. A message and a warning both.

Inside, the hut smelled of kerosene and wet wool. A camp stove ticked as it cooled. A sleeping bag lay rolled but not packed. And sitting on an overturned supply crate, hands folded, patient as a banker, was a man of about fifty with gray at his temples and a face designed to be forgotten — average height, average build, a jaw you wouldn't remember five minutes after looking away.

"Miss Hartwell," he said. His voice matched his face. Pleasant. Unremarkable. "I was hoping we'd meet before you reached the glacier. Roads here being what they are."

Kestrel felt Xanthe come up beside her, felt the girl's breath go shallow and controlled. "You've been following us," Kestrel said.

"I've been *waiting* for you. Following implies effort." He gestured to the crate opposite him, an invitation. When neither of them moved, he continued as if they'd sat. "My name is Voss. I represent Meridian Land & Resource, though I suspect you've already deduced that. Your father was thorough, Miss Hartwell. You appear to be too."

"My father is dead."

"Yes." For one unguarded moment something moved behind the forgettable face — not grief, but recognition, the look of a man who has filed many deaths and remembers filing this one. "I'm sorry for it. Genuinely. Elias Hartwell was respected in my profession, which is a rare thing to say about a cartographer."

"You cut our boat," Xanthe said. Flat. Cold. The voice she used for coordinates.

Voss turned to her with mild interest. "And you must be the anonymous contractor. The one hired to verify a map that didn't officially exist." He smiled faintly. "You were paid well to make sure it stayed hypothetical, if I recall the terms correctly. Yet here you are, carrying it against your ribs like scripture. Curious career trajectory."

Xanthe's hand twitched toward the oilcloth bundle. Kestrel shifted half a step, putting herself fractionally between them — not consciously, just the way water finds level.

"The boat," Kestrel repeated. "Answer her."

"The boat was a miscalculation by a subcontractor who believed you'd turn back at the first setback. I apologized to no one, because there was no one left to apologize to." He spread his hands. "I don't work in casualties, Miss Hartwell. I work in acquisitions. Casualties are expensive and draw attention. What I want is very cheap, actually. It costs me nothing but paper."

He reached into his jacket — Kestrel's hand found the rifle sling — and produced instead a slim document folder, which he set on the crate between them with the care of a man laying down a winning card.

"Meridian has been acquiring mineral leases along this drainage for six years. Quietly. Through eleven shell companies in three countries. What we lacked was the interior valley — the one your father found and recorded nowhere except in his own head and his own notebook. Unrecorded means unprotected, Miss Hartwell. No registry entry, no claim, no standing. To the government it doesn't exist. To us, it's worth more than everything else in the portfolio combined."

"And the expedition?" Xanthe's voice cracked on it. "The people who died on that river?"

"The expedition was science, funded for appearance. Its tragic outcome was weather." Voss said it without flinching, which was somehow worse than if he'd flinched. "I want to be clear that I take no pleasure in any of it. But I am here now, the weather will break soon, and when it does, you'll want options. So." He tapped the folder. "A purchase agreement. The journal, the negatives or data files if any exist, and your signature on a discovery waiver affirming you found nothing. In exchange: two million dollars, flown out on the first plane through the corridor, plus a consulting retainer should you ever wish to fly contract work — legitimate work — anywhere in the territory. Your debts cleared. Your airstrip lease secured. Your future, purchased at fair market value."

The hut was very quiet. Water dripped somewhere behind the walls, counting seconds.

Kestrel looked at the folder and did not touch it.

Two million dollars. She thought about the airstrip lease she was three months behind on. About the engine overhaul the Cessna needed, the one she'd been putting off by flying on prayer and preflight checks. About the medicine run money that barely covered fuel, the winters when she ate rice and told the town gossip economy she was dieting. Two million dollars was every hard year made suddenly soft. Two million dollars was never having to leave a town before anyone got close again, because there'd be no towns, no need — just her and the sky and enough money that loneliness could afford to be comfortable.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the dripping water: her father's handwriting. *Knuckles ache before rain.* The final pages naming her. *If anyone finishes this, it has to be Kestrel. She'll hate me for asking. She'll do it anyway.*

He'd known her. That was the thing that broke her open every time she let herself think it — he had known her, across ten years of silence, well enough to predict her grief and her stubbornness both.

"You killed him," she said. Not loudly. "Maybe not with your hands. But you sent people into his valley after him, and the river took the expedition, and he died out here mapping land you wanted to strip-mine. And now you're offering me money for the only part of him I have left."

"I'm offering you money for a piece of paper," Voss corrected gently. "Your father isn't in the journal, Miss Hartwell. Your father is wherever you keep him. The paper is just paper. Sell the paper. Keep the memory. Everyone wins."

"That's what you tell yourself," Xanthe said quietly, "so you can sleep."

Something flickered across Voss's face then — the first real expression he'd shown, and it looked almost like respect. Or hunger. Hard to tell the difference in men like him.

"You're the daughter who flew into monsoon weather when every other pilot refused," he said to Kestrel. "I read your file. I know exactly how stubborn you are. Which is why the offer is generous rather than insulting." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and left the folder on the crate. "Keep it overnight. Consider it seriously. The glacier corridor opens within thirty-six hours — yes, we have our own forecasts — and after that, my window closes and so does yours. If the answer is no..." He paused at the door, fog curling past his shoulders. "Then Meridian proceeds through other channels, and those channels are less courteous than I am. There are ways to invalidate a survey, Miss Hartwell. Disputed provenance. Questioned methodology. A crashed aircraft in remote terrain with no surviving witnesses would raise very few questions at all."

"There are two of us," Xanthe said.

"Yes," Voss agreed pleasantly. "There are."

He stepped out into the fog and was gone — swallowed whole, as if the river had simply decided to take him back.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Xanthe crossed the hut, picked up the folder, and held it out to Kestrel like something dead.

"You should burn it," she said.

"I know what I should do." Kestrel didn't take it. Her hands had started aching, deep in the knuckles, and outside the fog was thickening, the light going the color of pewter. Rain coming. Her father's barometer, working fine in a dead man's daughter. "But I want to look at it first. I want to see what he thinks I am."

She opened the folder on the crate. The figures were laid out in clean type — escrow details, wire instructions, a confidentiality clause, a liability waiver absolving Meridian of all claims arising from "recreational aviation activity in the Alder Creek drainage." Recreational. As if her father had been fishing.

At the bottom, a signature line waited under her printed name. *KESTREL M. HARTWELL.* Someone had done their homework; even her middle name was right. Marek. After her mother. The pass her father named for her mother was on the maps as Pass 47-B, unnamed, because the backers had never approved the name. He'd named things for the people he loved and let the world call them numbers.

"Two million," Xanthe said. She wasn't looking at the folder. She was looking at Kestrel. "It would fix everything. The lease. The engine. You could stop eating rice and calling it a diet."

Kestrel laughed despite herself — a short, startled sound. "You knew about that?"

"I rationed for nine days next to you. I count everything." Xanthe's mouth almost smiled, then didn't. "Burn it, Kestrel."

"In a minute." Kestrel stared at the signature line a moment longer. Then she tore the pages in half, and in half again, methodically, feeling the paper resist and give, resist and give, until the two million dollars was confetti on a trapper's floor. She fed the pieces into the cold stove, struck a match, and watched the escrow details curl and blacken. The confidentiality clause burned last, blue-edged, like it didn't want to go.

When it was ash, she straightened and found Xanthe watching her with an expression she couldn't read — something between relief and fear.

"He gave us thirty-six hours," Kestrel said. "Same window as the glacier. Which means he doesn't plan to wait for our answer. He plans to make sure there's only one answer to give."

"Then we dig the plane out tonight," Xanthe said. "In the dark. Before his channels get less courteous."

Kestrel nodded, already moving, pulling the oilcloth-wrapped journal from Xanthe's satchel and strapping it against her own chest this time. Through the hut's open door, the fog was lifting off the river in long ragged sheets, and beyond it, far upstream, the glacier's shoulder showed white against a sky the color of gunmetal.

Thirty-six hours. A buried plane. A watcher on the river who had just learned his offer was ash.

Kestrel stepped out into the thinning mist and turned her aching knuckles toward the mountain.

"Let's go get my inheritance," she said.
