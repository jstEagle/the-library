## 15. Refusal

The man's name, he said, was Corliss. He said it the way a man lays down a card he believes is trump — flat, unhurried, expecting the table to fold around it. He stood at the edge of our camp in the gray drizzle of late afternoon, water beading on the shoulders of a rain shell too clean for this country, and he set a sealed envelope on the overturned crate we used as a table.

Xanthe Voss, he'd said, when he first stepped out of the alders. And Kestrel Hartwell. As if he'd been introduced to us by someone who knew us both. As if he'd been reading our mail.

He had a satellite phone holstered at his hip like a sidearm, and the particular stillness of a man who is not afraid of the wilderness because he does not intend to stay in it long.

"Ms. Hartwell," he said, "your father's work has significant commercial value. The consortium is prepared to compensate you fairly. Generously, even. There's no need for either of you to suffer through another week of this."

Kestrel didn't touch the envelope. She stood with her arms crossed, rain dripping off the brim of her cap, and looked at him the way she looked at weather she intended to fly through anyway.

"You were on the river," she said. "You cut our boat."

"I facilitated a controlled delay." Corliss's smile was thin and professional. "Nobody was meant to be hurt."

"You left a nineteen-year-old alone in a flooded camp for nine days."

"The flood," Corliss said, "was an act of God."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up the envelope, and I thought — with a lurch I didn't understand yet — that she was going to open it. That some part of her, the part that had grown up with nothing but a father who chose rivers over her, might want to see the number. Might want to know what her father's silence had finally been worth on paper.

She walked to the fire pit instead. The coals from breakfast were still breathing under a skin of ash, and she stirred them with a stick until they glowed orange, and she dropped the envelope in.

Corliss made a small sound, almost a laugh, the laugh of a man who assumes everything is negotiable. "That's a quarter of a million dollars, Ms. Hartwell."

The paper caught. The flame went up blue at the edges first, then yellow, and the envelope curled and blackened and let go of whatever it held inside it — the figures, the clauses, the careful lawyer's language that would have turned her father's last work into somebody's property line. Kestrel fed the whole thing in with the stick, piece by piece, until there was nothing but ash and a scrap of corner with a letterhead fragment that read *Meridian Terr*— and then not even that.

"It's not for sale," she said. "None of it. Not the map, not the data, not the valley. And if you come near this camp again, I'll report your position to every pilot, every ranger, and every journalist who'll take my call — and I know a lot of people with radios."

Corliss studied her. Then me. His gaze on me lasted a half-second longer, and something in it — a flicker, a recalculation — made my stomach drop before I understood why.

"Ms. Voss," he said pleasantly. "Your employer sends his regards."

Then he turned and walked back into the alders, unhurried, and the rain closed behind him like a door.

* * *

Kestrel stood at the fire pit a long time after he was gone, watching the last of the ash dissolve into the wet. I watched her too, from the doorway of the trapper's cabin, and the cold that came over me had nothing to do with the drizzle.

*Your employer sends his regards.*

He'd said it to me. Not at me. To me. The way you say a thing to someone who already knows the person you're quoting.

Kestrel turned. Her face was still hard from the burning, still lit along the jaw with anger, but her eyes had gone somewhere else — somewhere careful.

"Xanthe," she said. "What did he mean by that?"

The fire crackled. Somewhere up the valley the river kept up its low, permanent argument with the banks. I had rehearsed this confession a dozen times in the dark over the past two weeks — in the cabin, on watch, in the gray hour before dawn when the guilt got loudest. I had rehearsed it with gentleness, with context, with the whole architecture of explanation built around it like a levee. And now, standing in the rain with her looking at me like that, every word of it washed away and all that was left was the plain, ugly truth.

"I was hired," I said. "Before the expedition. By Meridian. By the people Corliss works for."

The rain ticked on the cabin's tin roof. Kestrel didn't move.

"Hired to do what?" she asked, and her voice was very even, the way it went even right before she said something that cut.

"To verify the map. That's what I told you, and that part was true — they had fragments of your father's early surveys, bought from a grad student who sold his access, and they wanted a cartographer to confirm the valley was real before they moved on it. But that wasn't all of it." I made myself keep looking at her. She deserved that much, at least. "The full contract — the part I signed and the part I was supposed to fulfill quietly — was to get the complete map out of the camp. Copy it, or take the original if I could. And if the expedition ended, if anything happened to it — I was to recover the materials and bring them out. Alone."

The silence went on so long I could hear my own pulse in my ears.

"Anything happened to it," Kestrel repeated.

"The flood. The — " I stopped, because saying *your father's death* out loud in that sentence was more than I could carry and I knew it. "They didn't plan the flood, Kestrel. I have to believe that. But they planned for it. The contract had a clause. *In the event of expedition loss.* They wrote it before we ever flew in."

Kestrel looked past me, at the river, at the gray wall of the valley where her father had walked into water. When she spoke again her voice had gone quiet in a way that was worse than shouting.

"Nine days," she said. "You sat in that tent for nine days. You watched me land. You watched me find his camp. And you didn't say any of this."

"I was going to."

"Were you?" She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of exhausted wonder. "Or were you going to wait until we flew out and then decide which version of you got off the plane?"

"Kestrel —"

"I pulled you out of that tent." Her voice cracked open a degree, and I wished it hadn't, because the anger I could have stood. "I gave you my dry socks. I gave you half of everything I had on that plane, and I sat up with you when you had the shakes, and the whole time — the whole *time*, Xanthe — you were their person. You were the thing they left inside my father's camp."

"I never sent them anything." I stepped toward her and she stepped back, and that small step back did more damage than anything else that day. "I never transmitted, I never copied the map, I never — Kestrel, I burned my copy of the contract the second night, before you even found me. I chose. The moment I understood what they were, I chose you. I chose the work. Your father's work."

"You chose," she said, "and you let me believe I'd chosen you back, without telling me the deck was stacked."

She picked up her cap from the crate, pulled it low, and walked past me into the cabin. At the door she stopped. She didn't turn around.

"I'm glad you burned it," she said. "I'm glad you chose. But you should have told me when I landed. You should have told me the first night. Every day you didn't was a day you chose for me too."

Then she went inside, and the door shut, and I stood in the rain for a long time, letting it do what it wanted to me.

* * *

The evening was the coldest we'd had, and not because of the temperature.

We shared the cabin because there was nowhere else, and that was its own kind of cruelty — the stove small, the room smaller, her bunk and mine six feet apart. She cooked without speaking. I cleaned up without speaking. The lamp between us made two small islands of light and a continent of shadow, and we each stayed on our own island.

I kept thinking about how it had been before. The monsoon nights in this same room, the two of us trading stories across the lamplight — her mother, who had named a pass; my grandmother, who had taught me to draw contours before I could write cursive. The night Kestrel had laughed at something I said, really laughed, head back, and then looked almost surprised at herself. I had thought: *this is what it's like to be trusted.* I had never had it before. I had grown up useful — hired, contracted, competent, paid. Trust was a country I'd only ever mapped from a distance.

And I had stood in it for two weeks and lied by omission the whole time, telling myself that silence was a kind of mercy, that the confession would come later, when the weather broke, when it was safe, when — always *when*. When was just the word cowards use for *never*, and I had known that, and I had stayed silent anyway, because the truth risked the only warmth I'd ever been given, and I wanted to keep the warmth a little longer first.

That was the thing I couldn't make her understand, and maybe shouldn't. That I hadn't betrayed her out of greed. I'd betrayed her out of hunger. It doesn't make it better. It might make it worse.

"Kestrel," I said, near midnight, because I couldn't stand it anymore. "Say something. Yell at me. Anything."

She was lying on her bunk facing the wall, boots on, jacket on, as if she might need to leave at any moment — as if the room itself had become somewhere she was only stopping through. For a long time I thought she wouldn't answer.

"You want absolution," she said finally, to the wall. "I can hear it. You want me to say it's okay, you made a hard call, you were young, they used you. And maybe all that's true. But here's what I keep coming back to." She turned over then, and looked at me across the lamplight, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "My father is dead because he trusted the wrong people. I learned that this week. And the whole time I was learning it, the wrong people had a person inside my camp, sleeping under my tarp, eating my rations. So no. I'm not going to yell. I'm not going to absolve you. I'm just going to lie here and be glad I burned that envelope before I signed anything else in my life without reading the fine print."

"Kestrel —"

"Good night, Xanthe."

She turned back to the wall. The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain thickened against the roof, and the river rose a little more in the dark, patient as everything else in this valley.

I lay awake for hours. Once, near the gray edge of morning, I heard her breathing change — the catch and slow release of someone who has been crying quietly and has decided to stop. I didn't go to her. I didn't have the right, and I knew it, and knowing it didn't make the not-going hurt any less.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. I was up first, out first, working — because work was the only language I had left that she might still accept. I checked the skiff we'd patched, found the seams holding, bailed the rainwater out of her. I restacked the fuel drums the flood had scattered. I dug the latrine trench deeper. I did everything a person does when she can't do the one thing that matters.

Kestrel emerged mid-morning, went straight to the plane, and started on the engine cowling with a wrench and a look that dared the weather to interrupt her. I brought her coffee. She took it without looking at me. She drank it. It was something. It was almost nothing. I couldn't tell the difference, and I was beginning to understand that the difference was going to be measured in weeks, not gestures.

I got out my field kit instead — the real one, the cartographer's kit, the one clean thing left in my life — and spread my working sheets on the crate where Corliss had left the envelope. The valley, from the fragments. Her father's contour lines in his small, sure hand, and mine layered over them in pencil, the gaps where no survey had ever been. The blank heart of the map that everyone wanted and no one had ever seen.

Kestrel came over at some point. I heard her boots stop behind me. I didn't turn around.

"The northeast drainage," she said at last. Her voice was rough with the night, but it was a working voice, a pilot's voice. "His journal says the river bends east there. Your contours have it going north."

I looked. She was right. Her father had seen it and I hadn't, and she'd read his journal — I still didn't know when, or how much — and she was standing over my shoulder correcting my map of the valley her father died for, and that was not forgiveness, not even close, but it was her, beside me, looking at the same page.

I picked up the pencil. My hand wasn't quite steady.

"Show me," I said.

She leaned in, and for a moment we were just two people bent over a map in the rain, and the valley waited north of us with its blank heart, and the river kept rising.

That was when we heard it — thin, warped, half-swallowed by weather, but unmistakable. An engine. Not ours. High and to the west, descending through the cloud deck toward the river, toward the corridor Corliss had walked out of yesterday.

Kestrel straightened, coffee forgotten, eyes tracking the gray sky like a woman reading a chart.

"That's not a floatplane," she said. "That's a turbine. And it's coming back."
