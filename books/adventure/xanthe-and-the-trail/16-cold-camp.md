## 16. Cold Camp

**Kestrel**

The trapper's cabin had a name scratched into the doorframe — *HALVORSEN, 1974* — and beneath it, in a different hand, *and gone*, and Kestrel thought that was about right for the interior. Everything out here belonged to somebody who'd left.

The first day after the burning of the buyout offer, neither of them spoke more than ten words. Kestrel counted them the way she counted fuel gallons: precisely, because running short meant dying. Xanthe split kindling by the woodpile without being asked. Kestrel checked the plane's tie-downs twice and drained water from the fuel sump a third time just to have something to do with her hands. The smoke from the stove went up straight, which meant high pressure somewhere past the ridgeline, which meant cold nights and no flying and nothing to do but wait for the glacier to decide something.

She'd burned Marrow & Voss's offer on the stove lid. The paper had curled like a living thing and gone up in a breath of orange, and she'd felt nothing at all while it happened, and then everything, all at once, an hour later, alone in the dark with her father's journal under her palm. She hadn't told Xanthe what she'd felt. Xanthe hadn't asked. That was the shape of it now — two people sharing four hundred square feet of log walls and a silence wide enough to land a plane in.

On the second morning she woke to find her boots dry by the stove, stuffed with newspaper, laces loosened. She stood over them for a long moment, one sock on, one off, listening to Xanthe breathe in her bedroll across the room. The girl slept like someone rationing it — flat on her back, hands folded, as if even unconsciousness were a task to be performed correctly.

Kestrel put the boots on. They were warm. She didn't say anything, and Xanthe didn't look up from the map she was drawing, and that was the whole conversation.

* * *

**Xanthe**

There was a discipline to guilt, Xanthe had learned. You did not confess it; you carried it in your posture. You arrived early to chores. You took the worst sleeping spot, nearest the drafty door. You made coffee before anyone asked and you never, ever let your hands be idle, because idle hands started thinking, and thinking led back to the envelope she'd signed in a hotel bar in Meridian six months ago — *verification services, map recovery, fee negotiable* — and the moment in the flooded camp when she'd looked at Alder Hartwell's last field notes and understood exactly what she'd been hired to steal, and chosen, for the first time in her mercenary little life, not to.

Kestrel hadn't forgiven her. Xanthe hadn't expected her to. Forgiveness was a currency and Kestrel Hartwell kept her accounts balanced; Xanthe respected that more than she could say. What she could do was work. So she worked.

The cabin sat on a bench above the river, half a mile from the flooded strip where the Cessna sat tied down in brown water to its wheel hubs. Every morning they hiked down together in silence, bailed what needed bailing, checked the tie-downs, hiked back. It was liturgy. Xanthe found she liked liturgy — the repetition of it, the way it required no words. On the fourth morning Kestrel handed her the fuel dipstick without looking and Xanthe read the level and called it back and that was the longest exchange they'd had since the fire.

It was on the fifth day that Xanthe broke first. Not with words — with paper.

She'd been redrawing the valley contour sheets from memory, working by lamplight after Kestrel turned in, because the originals were water-swollen and some of the detail lived only in her head now. She'd gotten good at contours the way some people got good at music: young, obsessively, alone. Her mother had been a surveyor in a country that no longer existed on current maps, and contour lines were the only inheritance that had survived the border redrawings.

She spread the sheets on the table the next morning, weighted with tin cups, and said, "If you're going to fly this valley, you should know how to read it."

Kestrel looked at the papers a long time. "I can read a chart."

"A chart tells you where things are." Xanthe tapped a closed loop near the sheet's center. "A contour tells you what the ground is going to do to you. See this? Lines close together — steep. This spacing here—" her finger moved west, where the lines fell apart like a dropped deck of cards— "that's a bench. Flat enough to turn a plane around, if you ever have to."

Kestrel sat down across from her. She didn't say thank you. She pulled the lamp closer and studied the lines the way she studied weather — suspiciously, thoroughly, until she'd found every lie in them.

"Show me again," she said. "The bench."

Xanthe showed her. And that was how the thaw began: not with an apology, but with a bench, drawn in pencil, in a cabin that smelled of spruce smoke and wet wool.

* * *

**Kestrel**

Her father had taught her the radio when she was nine, standing on a crate to reach the set in his old hangar at Fairbanks East. *Say what you mean, mean what you say, and always tell them where you are.* He'd ruffled her hair after her first solo call-up and she had spent thirty years trying to forget how that had felt, and failing, especially now, with his journal in the footlocker under her bunk and his handwriting describing a valley he'd named for her mother.

She caught herself teaching Xanthe the same way he'd taught her. She noticed it on the sixth evening and hated it and kept doing it anyway.

"Say it back," she said, hand on the volume knob of the salvaged handheld. "Who you are, where you are, what you want."

"Xanthe Reyes, trapper's cabin, upper Alder Creek drainage, requesting any station, over." The girl's accent rounded the words strangely, carefully, each syllable placed like a footing on scree.

"Again. Slower. The mountains eat fast talk."

"Xanthe Reyes. Trapper's cabin. Upper Alder Creek drainage. Requesting any station. Over."

Kestrel keyed the mic herself and sent it out into the static — the same three sentences, plus their situation, plus the date. Nothing came back but the long hiss of empty spectrum. She'd transmitted every evening since the radio ghost, and every evening the silence held, and she couldn't decide whether the silence meant nobody was listening or somebody very much was.

"Again," she said.

"You already heard me do it right."

"I heard you do it once right. Once is luck. Again."

Xanthe's jaw tightened — Kestrel watched her decide whether to fight — and then she said it again, slower, and the third time she added, unprompted, "Two souls aboard, fuel sufficient, over," and Kestrel felt something in her chest unclench a single degree.

"That's the whole religion," she said. "Everything else is decoration."

Outside, the temperature was falling through twenty and the stars came out hard and close. Inside, Xanthe copied the phonetic alphabet onto the back of a contour sheet — *alpha bravo charlie* in her small precise hand — and Kestrel drank her coffee and pretended not to watch, and thought: *Dad would have liked her.* The thought arrived uninvited and stayed like a guest with muddy boots.

* * *

**Xanthe**

The chores developed a grammar. Kestrel cooked breakfasts — terrible, burnt, generous — and Xanthe cooked suppers, rice and tinned fish doctored with spruce tips until it almost tasted intentional. Kestrel took the axe; Xanthe took the splitting maul. Neither offered to trade. On the seventh day Xanthe found the axe handle freshly wrapped in new cord whipping, and said nothing, and re-wrapped the maul's loose head the following night, and that was the closest either of them came to saying what needed saying.

On the eighth day the river dropped enough to expose the skiff where they'd dragged it above the flood line, and they spent the afternoon re-caulking seams in the cold, fingers stupid with chill, passing the putty knife back and forth without speaking. At one point Kestrel swore at a seam and Xanthe laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound like a bird flushing — and Kestrel glanced up, and for a moment neither of them looked away, and then both did, and the work continued, and something had changed anyway. Xanthe could feel it sitting between them like a third person warming itself at the stove.

That night Kestrel brought out the journal.

Not to show her — Xanthe understood that. To read aloud, or partly aloud, the pages about the survey methods, the sight lines, the benchmarks Alder Hartwell had cut into the granite at the valley mouth. "You'll need these if you're drawing it," Kestrel said, gruff, eyes on the page. "Coordinates don't mean anything unless you know how they were taken."

So Xanthe listened, and copied, and when Kestrel's voice caught on a passage — a small hitch, quickly mastered, like a wing dipping in turbulence — Xanthe looked very hard at the lamp and gave her the privacy of not having seen it.

Near midnight Kestrel closed the book and said, to the stove rather than to her: "He trusted you with his notes. Out of everyone on that expedition."

"He trusted the work," Xanthe said. "I was careful with it."

"No." Kestrel shook her head once. "He didn't trust people with the work. I know that better than anyone." She stood, banked the stove, and paused at the ladder to the loft. "Get the benchmarks right, Reyes. He'd check."

It wasn't forgiveness. Xanthe knew forgiveness when she heard it, and this wasn't it. But it was something adjacent — a door left unlatched — and she lay awake a long time afterward, listening to the wind test the chimney, holding the unlatched door carefully open in her mind so it wouldn't swing shut.

* * *

**Kestrel**

On the ninth day it snowed, early and dry, the first real snow, and the world went quiet in the particular way the interior went quiet — a hush with weight behind it. They walked down to the plane through falling white and Kestrel showed Xanthe how to read the sky for the window they needed: the glacier route east opened only when a ridge of high pressure slid down from the north and the katabatic winds off the ice ran light and steady, carving a clean corridor through the pass.

"How will you know?" Xanthe asked.

"The wind changes its mind. You'll hear it." Kestrel put Xanthe's bare hand flat against the Cessna's strut, cold enough to burn. "Feel that? North-northeast, steady, no gusting. When it smells like ice instead of rain, we go."

"And if it doesn't come?"

"It comes. It always comes. The question is whether it comes before we run out of reasons to stay sane." She said it lightly, but they both did the arithmetic silently: food for eleven days, maybe twelve, fuel for one flight out, and a watcher somewhere on the river who had stopped leaving boot prints, which was worse than boot prints.

They walked back through the snowfall side by side, not touching, not talking, and Kestrel realized she was measuring the distance between their shoulders and finding it smaller than it had been nine days ago. Smaller every day. She didn't examine that too closely. Some instruments you didn't want a reading from yet.

* * *

**Xanthe**

On the eleventh night, Xanthe finished the map.

Not the whole valley — that would take months, seasons, a lifetime maybe — but the mouth of it, the entrance corridor, drawn fair from her memory and Alder Hartwell's benchmark coordinates, every contour checked twice against the journal. She laid it on the table while Kestrel cleaned the stove, and stepped back, and waited.

Kestrel wiped her hands slowly on her thighs. Then she came to the table and bent over the sheet and was silent for so long that Xanthe began composing retreats.

"There," Kestrel said finally, touching a point at the corridor's narrow throat. Her voice had gone strange. "That's where he named the pass. After my mother." A pause. "Larkspur Pass. He wrote it in the journal like it was just another feature. It isn't. She died in April and he named a mountain pass after her and never told me."

The stove ticked. Snow slid off the roof in a soft sigh.

"He was going to tell you," Xanthe said quietly. "The last entry — I read it, I'm sorry, I read everything — he wrote *I'll show Kestrel when she stops being angry at me.*"

Kestrel straightened. For a moment her face did something complicated and unfinished, grief and anger and something warmer all arriving at once, and then she folded the map along its existing creases, precisely, and handed it back.

"Pack it with the journal," she said. "We fly it out together or not at all."

And then, because the moment had become unbearable in its tenderness, the radio on the shelf crackled.

Both of them went still.

It wasn't the ghost frequency. It was the emergency band, and the voice that came through was thin, distorted, repeating — but underneath the static, unmistakable, human:

*"—any station, any station, this is Alder Creek base camp, we have injured, repeat, we have injured—"*

Kestrel's eyes met Xanthe's across the lamplight, and Xanthe saw the same thing written there that she felt in her own chest: *everyone from that camp was supposed to be dead.*

The voice repeated, patient and broken, and outside the snow kept falling, and somewhere north the wind was beginning, very faintly, to change its mind.
