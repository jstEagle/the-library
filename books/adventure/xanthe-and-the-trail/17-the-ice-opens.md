## 17. The Ice Opens

The sound woke me before the light did.

It came up through the floor of the trapper's cabin and through the bones of my chest — a deep, grinding crack, like the earth clearing its throat after weeks of mumbling. Then a boom rolled down the valley, long and low, the kind of sound you feel in your teeth before you hear it with your ears. I was out of my sleeping bag and at the window before I'd decided to move.

Gray dawn. Rain had finally stopped sometime in the night, and the world outside was rinsed and sharp, every ridge etched in cold detail. And there, upvalley where the glacier shouldered out of the high country like a sleeping animal, a plume of white dust was still hanging in the air.

"Xanthe." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Get up."

She surfaced from her bunk in stages — one eye first, then a groan, then the blanket thrown back. "What time is it? What's—"

"That was the glacier." I was already pulling on boots, my hands moving faster than my thoughts. "It calved."

That got her upright. She crossed the cold floorboards in two strides and pressed her shoulder against mine at the window, both of us staring upvalley through the fogged glass like the ice might do something else just for our benefit.

"It's early," she said. "You said weeks."

"I said weeks based on last year's melt pattern." I grabbed the binoculars off the shelf between the stove and the stack of Xanthe's maps — she'd colonized half the cabin with them, contour lines creeping across every flat surface like ivy. The glass was cold enough to sting my hands. I found the glacier's snout, the blue-black wall of it above the braided outwash plain, and swept along its face until I saw what I was looking for: a fresh scar, pale as peeled fruit, where a section of the terminus had let go and slid into the meltwater lake below.

And behind it — this was the part that made my breath go shallow — behind it, the valley walls opened into a corridor of clear air. The storm system that had squatted on us for eleven days had finally been shoved east, and the pass between the two great ridgelines stood open, sky-deep, empty of cloud from wall to wall.

Thirty-six hours, maybe. That was the arithmetic of ice corridors. Once the glacier calved, the meltwater would surge, then refreeze as temperatures dropped overnight, and the katabatic winds would start pouring cold air down off the icefield and slam that door shut again. I'd watched it happen twice from the cockpit of other planes, in other years, in another life. The window didn't negotiate. It didn't extend. It closed.

"How far is the plane?" Xanthe asked. She already knew. She'd helped me inventory everything we'd cached at the airstrip strip when the flood took it — but she asked anyway, the way people ask questions they need answered out loud to believe.

"Four miles downriver. Buried under three feet of water and whatever silt the river decided to leave on top of it." I lowered the binoculars. "Assuming the water dropped."

We went outside to find out.

* * *

The river had fallen four feet in the night. You could read it on the banks — a fresh dark band of mud above the current line, willows bent downstream and dripping, a dead log perched absurdly on top of a boulder like a hat. The water that remained ran fast and brown and loud, but it was a river again instead of a rumor of an ocean.

The airstrip strip — Alder Creek's so-called runway, twelve hundred feet of gravel bar that Elias Hartwell had graded himself with a borrowed blade twenty years ago — emerged from the floodwater in sections, like something surfacing from a dream. The upstream third was clear. The middle was still a shallow lake, glittering in the early light. The downstream end, where we'd lashed the Cessna to two spruce trunks and a rock anchor before the water took the field, was a question mark.

"We dig today," I said. "We fly tomorrow morning. First light, coldest air, calmest wind."

"And if the window closes early?"

"Then we're here for the winter, and I teach you to play cribbage, and we eat a lot of beans." I said it lightly, but we both knew what overwintering meant — not just boredom, but the watcher somewhere on the river, the backers' man with his cut boat and his satellite phone, patient as weather. We hadn't seen him since the gorge. That was its own kind of dread. Men like him didn't leave. They waited for the ice to make their work easier.

Xanthe didn't joke back. She was already loading the pack frame: shovels, the pry bar, rope, the tarp, thermos, the emergency rations we'd stop pretending we weren't saving for exactly this. She'd gotten good at packing in the weeks since the flood — economical, balanced, nothing wasted. Watching her move, I felt that familiar double image again: the stranger barricaded in the supply tent, nine days rationed down to crumbs, and this person, this wiry, stubborn cartographer who'd learned my plane's tie-down knots by heart and could read a contour map faster than I could read weather.

"Kestrel." She'd stopped at the edge of the porch, looking at me with that directness she never quite unlearned, no matter how many times I deflected it. "If we get out — if we actually fly out — are we going straight to Copper Basin? Or are we doing what you said?"

What I'd said. Three nights ago, by lamplight, half a cup of tea into a conversation about her father-figure maps and my father's journal, I'd heard myself say it out loud for the first time: *we divert. We fly the valley first. We record everything.* Her father's valley. My father's final pages. The inheritance neither of us had asked for and both of us, apparently, intended to claim.

"We're doing what I said," I told her. "Straight line's for people with nothing to prove."

Something moved through her face — relief, maybe, or the beginning of fear, which looks similar from certain angles. Then she shouldered the pack frame and we started downriver.

* * *

The walk took two hours and cost us most of our dry socks. The outwash plain was a treachery of hidden channels — water running clear over gravel that looked solid and wasn't, sinkholes where the flood had scoured out the fines beneath cobbles that held until they didn't. Twice I went in to the knee. Once Xanthe went in to the hip and came up swearing with a fluency that would have impressed the bush pilots I'd grown up around, and I laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than I wanted to count — until she threw a handful of glacial mud at me and hit me square in the jacket.

But when we crested the last gravel berm and saw the plane, neither of us laughed at anything.

The Cessna stood in a lake.

Not deep — eighteen inches, maybe two feet where the flood had pooled against the downstream berm — but wide, a sheet of brown water lying flat and mirror-still over what should have been dry gravel, stretching from the wheels to the tree line. The plane itself looked intact. The lashings had held; the spruce trunks were still standing, though the river had stripped their bark and left them pale as bone. Silt had climbed the fuselage to the wing roots and dried in a tide line, gray-brown, like the plane had waded through history and come out the other side.

"She held," Xanthe said quietly.

"She held." I said it like a prayer, because it was one. Every pilot has a relationship with their aircraft that non-pilots find slightly embarrassing, and mine had just survived being anchored in a flood for eleven days. I waded out to her — the water was snowmelt-cold, the kind of cold that squeezes the breath out of your calves — and put my hand flat on the cowling like taking a pulse.

Then we dug.

* * *

I want to record what that day actually was, because memory smooths things and the truth was that it was brutal, tedious, holy work.

The flood hadn't buried the plane so much as built around her. Silt and gravel had deposited in the eddy behind the fuselage to a depth of nearly four feet, packed dense as wet concrete, and the meltwater sat on top of all of it, seeping into every hole we opened faster than we could bail. We started at the tail, because physics — you don't dig out the front of an airplane sitting in water unless you want the tail to settle and the prop to eat gravel — and because the tail was shallowest, and because starting shallow lets you believe the deep part will be easier, which is a lie but a useful one.

We shoveled. We bailed. We rigged the tarp as a cofferdam and it failed twice and held on the third try once Xanthe thought to weight its edges with rocks instead of stakes, because stakes wouldn't bite in saturated muck. We pried silt out of the elevator hinges with the pry bar and my multi-tool and, at one desperate point, a spoon. We checked the pitot tube and found it packed with mud, and cleared it with a length of safety wire, and cleared it again an hour later because I didn't trust it, and cleared it a third time because I still didn't.

By midday the sun came over the eastern ridge and the temperature climbed, and that became the enemy. Warm air meant active melt up on the icefield meant more water coming down the valley by evening. I kept checking the river gauge in my head — the dead log on the boulder, the mud line on the willows — and by two o'clock I didn't like what I was reading. The water in the wheel-well hollows had risen an inch since morning.

"It's refilling," Xanthe said. She was standing in the lake beside the left main gear, up to her thighs, hauling gravel out of the wheel arch by the double handful. Her hair had escaped its braid entirely and plastered across her face and she hadn't once stopped to fix it. "The pool. Look at the tarp line."

She was right. The cofferdam was losing. Not failing — losing, slowly, the way a siege is lost, water finding its way through seams we couldn't see.

"Then we dig faster," I said, which is not a plan, but sometimes it's the only plan there is, and she nodded and put her head down and dug.

Here is what I remember most clearly, later: not the cold or the ache in my lower back, but a moment around four in the afternoon when we'd cleared the right main gear completely and I crawled under the fuselage to check the fuel drains, and I looked out through the gap beneath the belly of my father's old plane — the plane he'd flown supply runs in before I did, before the silence grew between us wide enough to land a Cessna in — and I saw Xanthe on the far side, silhouetted against the water, wringing out her shirt and immediately putting it back on because the wind had teeth, and I thought: *he sent me her. Of everything he could have willed me, he sent me a witness.*

I crawled back out and kept digging.

* * *

By dusk we had her free.

All four tires on gravel — wet gravel, soft gravel, gravel that would never in a thousand years support a takeoff roll in these conditions, but that was tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow's problem was a problem I knew how to solve. I'd landed on worse. Elias Hartwell had taught me that a runway is a state of mind, and I'd spent thirty years proving him right in places that weren't runways at all.

We drained the sump cups and got clean fuel, no water, which surprised me so much I checked twice. We pulled the inspection covers and found the muck had stayed out of the guts of her, mostly. The engine had been pickled in preservative oil before the flood — my own paranoia, eleven days ago, done with a funnel and a prayer — and now that paranoia paid its dividend. Xanthe wiped down the leading edges while I ran the preflight, and when I tested the flaps they groaned, hesitated, and traveled full range, shedding a curtain of muddy water like a dog shaking off a swim.

"She flies," Xanthe said.

"She flies." I stood there with my hand on the flap switch and let that land. Around us the light was going gold and then copper, the way it does in the north when the sun gets low and honest, and the whole flooded strip turned to hammered metal, and the glacier upvalley caught the last of it and burned white.

Then I looked upvalley and stopped burning with satisfaction and started burning with something colder.

The meltwater in the channels between us and the cabin had risen. Visibly. Since noon. The little stream we'd hopped across on stones that morning was now a brown ribbon ten feet wide, and the main channel beyond it had climbed back over its morning bank, and away up at the glacier's face, catching the sunset, I could see the melt streams pouring off the ice in silver ropes — dozens of them, all draining toward us, all day, all night.

"The window," Xanthe said. She'd followed my eyes. She always did.

"Tomorrow morning," I said. "First light. We fly at first light or we don't fly."

* * *

We walked back in the dark because waiting for morning meant losing hours we didn't have, and we spent those hours instead doing the last of it by lantern light in the cabin: fueling the ferry tanks from the drums we'd cached, loading the survey camera, the film magazines, the notebooks, the journal — wrapped in oilcloth, riding in the map case behind my seat, where it had ridden since the gorge and would ride until I decided otherwise. Xanthe laid out her charts on the floor and drew the flight line in grease pencil, the diversion route into the valley, the photo runs, the altitudes, her handwriting small and exact as stitching.

"Wind's forecast to back to the northeast overnight," she said, tapping the notes she'd copied off the radio sked. "That gives you a quartering tail out of the strip and a headwind coming back over the pass."

"You've been studying."

"I've been living with a pilot for three weeks. Some of it soaks in." She glanced up. "Also I intend to be useful in that right-hand seat, so learn to tolerate it."

I almost said something then. Something about how I'd already stopped tolerating it and started depending on it, and what that meant, and whether either of us was going to survive saying it out loud. But the lantern was low and the hour was late and there was a glacier upvalley with a deadline, so I said instead:

"Sleep. Both of us. Real sleep. Tomorrow we earn it."

She rolled into her bunk. I lay in mine listening to the cabin tick and settle, and beneath that, fainter, from far upvalley, the glacier talking to itself — cracks and groans and the occasional distant boom, an animal turning over in its sleep, dreaming of movement.

Somewhere in the black hours I woke once, certain I'd heard a sound that didn't belong to ice — a thin electronic chirp, there and gone, out toward the river. I lay still and listened for a full minute and heard nothing but water.

Probably nothing. Probably the cold playing tricks.

At 4:40 the alarm went off, and outside the window the eastern sky had gone the color of a blade, and the air, when I cracked the door, was five degrees colder than the night before — cold enough to freeze, cold enough to kill the melt, cold enough to open the door.

Thirty-six hours. Maybe less.

"Xanthe," I said into the dark. "Up. We're flying."

Behind me, in the silence of the cabin, out by the woodpile where nobody should have been, something snapped a branch.
