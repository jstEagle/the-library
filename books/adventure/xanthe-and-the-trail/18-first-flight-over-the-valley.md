## 18. First Flight Over the Valley

**Kestrel**

The plane came out of the ice like a diver surfacing.

Thirty-six hours. That was what the calving had bought them — a corridor of open air between the glacier's shattered shoulder and the high pass, a window that would slam shut when the meltwater refroze or the next squall line rolled off the divide. They had spent eleven of those hours digging the Cessna out of three feet of slush with a shovel, a tarp, and Xanthe's stubbornness, and now the wheels were wet, the engine was warm, and Kestrel's hands were shaking on the yoke — not from cold, though there was plenty of that, but from the simple animal disbelief of being airborne again after nine days of mud and silence and fear.

The strip fell away beneath them, a brown scar in the green. The river ran wide and braided below, still swollen, carrying the monsoon's leavings seaward. Somewhere down that current were the men who had cut their boat, who had sent the ghost transmission, who had offered her a check with enough zeros to buy her father's silence twice over. She had burned the offer. The ash was probably still in the fire ring at the trapper's cabin.

"Course heading?" Xanthe asked from the right seat. She had the journal open on her knees, weighted with a chart case, her pencil hovering over the final pages like a translator approaching an unbroken code.

Kestrel looked at the heading painted on her kneeboard. Two-seven-zero, straight out through the pass, south to the regional strip, safety, upload, done. Every hour in this corridor was an hour borrowed against the weather.

She banked left instead.

"What are you doing?" Xanthe's voice went sharp. "Kestrel. The window—"

"I know the window." The river swung beneath the wing, then the tributary, then the narrow granite throat where the water went from brown to white. "We're not flying out yet."

"We have maybe twenty hours of clear air. You want to spend them sightseeing?"

"Not sightseeing." Kestrel climbed, feeling the load lighten as they gained the cooler air, watching the terrain rise to meet them — the country her father had described in his last entries, page after page of coordinates and sketches and one sentence underlined twice in his cramped hand: *No one has mapped this. No one has named it. That is worth more than what they're paying.* "Surveying. He willed it to me, Xanthe. Not money. Not the camp. This." She nodded at the windshield, at the wall of peaks ahead where the terrain lifted into country no chart in her aircraft showed anything but blank shading. "I'm not flying it out secondhand. I'm flying it first."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. When Kestrel glanced over, the cartographer had already turned to a fresh page, pencil moving, drawing the ridgeline freehand before the camera could even capture it.

"There," Xanthe said softly, pointing. "That notch. His sketch matches. God, it *matches*."

Kestrel pushed the throttle forward and flew toward the blank space on the map.

* * *

**Xanthe**

The valley opened beneath them the way a book opens — all at once, and nothing like what the cover promised.

One moment they were threading the granite throat, wings close enough to the walls that Xanthe could see individual lichen crusts on the stone, could see the old flood lines written in pale mineral bands ten meters above the current water. Then the walls parted, and there it was: a long trough of valley floor perhaps six kilometers across, holding a river so blue it looked dyed, fed by hanging glaciers that stepped down the headwalls like frozen staircases. Meadows the color of deep moss. A lake at the far end, gray-green with rock flour, calving its own small bergs off a tongue of ice that reached down to touch the water.

Nothing. That was what every map said. Nothing here but blank shading and a contour interval guessed at from fifty miles away. And here was all of *this*.

"It's a refugium," she breathed, half to herself, pencil racing. "Look at the aspect — north walls shaded, south walls open. During the last glaciation this whole basin would've been ice-free while everything around it was buried. There could be plant communities in there that exist nowhere else on the continent." She caught herself, flushed, aware she was lecturing. "Sorry. I know we're on a clock."

"Don't apologize." Kestrel's eyes stayed on the terrain, but her voice had gone strange — thick, careful. "He would've loved that. The science. He always said the backers thought he was mapping land, and he kept telling them he was mapping *time*."

Xanthe looked at her. In nine days she had learned to read Kestrel Hartwell the way she read contours — by what was implied between the lines. She knew about the father now, knew the whole shape of it: the estrangement, the expedition that was supposed to be a reconciliation and became a grave, the journal hidden under the pilot's seat for two nights before Kestrel could bear to finish it. She knew that every mile they flew right now was a eulogy Kestrel hadn't been able to say at any altitude lower than this.

"Talk me through what you need," Xanthe said. "For the record to hold up. Legally, scientifically, whatever."

Kestrel blinked, pulled back into the present. "Overlapping passes. North-south lines, sixty percent overlap between frames, so the photogrammetry software can build a model. GPS logging running the whole time." She tapped the mounted unit. "Timestamps tied to the track file. Nobody can claim we fabricated this later — the data proves its own chain of custody."

"And the naming?"

Kestrel was quiet for a moment. Below them the river bent around a gravel bar shaped like a crescent moon.

"He left instructions," she said finally. "Last page. He wanted the headwall glacier named for my mother. Elena Glacier." Her jaw worked. "And the valley itself — he wouldn't name it. Said naming was a promise, and promises should be made by someone who intended to keep them. So I guess that's mine now."

"You intend to keep it?"

"I intend to make sure nobody can sell it." Kestrel rolled into the first survey line, wings level, airspeed nailed to the number, and Xanthe watched her become something she'd only glimpsed before — not the guarded woman who slept in her boots, but a pilot at the absolute top of her craft, flying a perfect grid over country that had never been photographed. "Camera's yours. Shoot when I call the line."

"Copy." Xanthe thumbed the shutter release, and the first frame of the unrecorded valley became a fact instead of a secret.

* * *

**Kestrel**

They flew the grid for two hours and forty minutes.

It was the best flying of her life and she barely had time to feel it. Sixteen north-south lines, each one a knife-straight track down the valley and back up the other side, wind correcting, density altitude climbing as the afternoon sun worked the rock. Xanthe called features and shot frames; Kestrel held the lines true within a wingspan; the GPS ticked away its incorruptible testimony. Twice they climbed to photograph the hanging glaciers, and once Kestrel put the Cessna into a slow orbit over the lake while Xanthe documented the ice tongue, and in the engine noise and the turning light Kestrel felt her father beside her — not as a ghost, exactly, but as a standard. *Fly it straight. Record it honestly. Don't flinch.*

On the ninth line, Xanthe said, "There's a herd. Look — northwest meadow, by the oxbow."

Kestrel tipped a wing. Caribou, dozens of them, scattered across the green like dark coins spilled on a table. Unbothered. Uncounted. Animals that had never learned to fear an airplane because almost none had ever come.

"They'll put a road through here," Kestrel said. "If the backers get the claim. Or a mine. Whatever pays fastest." She leveled the wings. "That's why he underlined it twice."

"That's why you're flying it in circles instead of going home," Xanthe said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite teasing either. It was the tone she used when she'd finished a map and was checking it against the ground.

"Yeah," Kestrel said. "That's why."

Her fuel gauge said she had forty minutes of margin for the crossing, plus reserves. Her weather eye said the pass was still clean, cumulus building fat and white over the southern divide but nothing organized yet. Her gut said *one more thing*, and she'd learned long ago that her gut was a better instrument than anything Garmin made.

"The lake," she said. "One low pass along the shore. He sketched a cairn on the east point — said he built it on his last walk. I want to see it."

"Low pass over an unfamiliar lake with rock flour water — depth perception's garbage on silt," Xanthe said, but she was already bracing the camera. "Fifty feet minimum. Call your go-around early."

"Fifty feet. Copy."

She brought them down the length of the lake at fifty feet, the gray-green water sliding past close enough to count the wavelets, the bergs drifting like patient animals. And there on the east point, exactly where the journal's last sketch placed it: a cairn of flat stones, man-high, weathered but standing, with something pale catching the sun at its summit.

Antlers. A caribou rack, wired upright, the way prospectors marked claims in the old days.

Kestrel's throat closed. She pulled up gently, banked away over the water, and for a few seconds she couldn't speak at all. Xanthe didn't say anything either. She just reached over and rested her hand on the back of Kestrel's shoulder, once, briefly, and took it away, and that was somehow more than any speech.

"He kept his promise," Kestrel managed. "He came back. He just didn't come back to me."

"He came back to this," Xanthe said. "And now so did you."

* * *

**Xanthe**

They turned for the pass at 4:17 in the afternoon, and Xanthe spent the first ten minutes of the climb cataloguing what they carried.

In the camera: four hundred and twelve frames of the valley, overlapping, geotagged, timestamped. On the data card: the full GPS track, the elevation profiles, her own field notes transcribed during the fuel-crunched quiet of the climb. In the seat pocket behind Kestrel: the original journal, wrapped in the waterproof bag that had saved it once from the river and might have to save it again. In Xanthe's breast pocket, against her heart like a cliché she'd stopped being embarrassed by: a memory card duplicate of everything, sealed in tape.

Two copies. Two carriers. If anything happened to the plane, the valley would still survive them.

"Pass looks good," Kestrel said, scanning it. "Wind's quartering out of the south but it's light. Twenty minutes and we're through."

Xanthe looked back once at the valley, shrinking behind the granite throat, the blue river folding itself out of sight. She had been hired — anonymously, wire transfer, no name — to verify that Elias Hartwell's map existed and to acquire it by whatever means necessary. She had told herself a hundred stories about what that meant. Standing in the wrecked camp nine days ago, barricaded in the supply tent with a flare gun and nine days of ration bars, she had assumed the job was dead.

It wasn't dead. It had just changed employers. She worked for the valley now.

"Kestrel." She said it quietly, and Kestrel glanced over. "When we land — when the data goes out — there will be a day or two where it's chaos. Lawyers, journalists, the backers scrambling. You should know that whatever happens, I'm testifying. Under my real name. About who hired me and what they asked for. It'll burn me professionally for a while. Maybe permanently." She shrugged, surprised at how light it felt to say. "Worth it."

Kestrel was silent for several seconds. The pass walls slid closer, golden in the low sun.

"You know what my father wrote about you?" she said at last. "Third-to-last entry. 'The new cartographer is better than she lets on. Careful, thorough. Hiding something, but honest underneath it — you can tell because she corrects her own mistakes before anyone else can.' " Kestrel's mouth twitched. "He hired you knowing. I think he was betting on you."

Xanthe stared at her. The cabin suddenly seemed very small and very warm. "He never said—"

"He wouldn't. He wasn't a saying person." Kestrel faced forward, hands steady on the yoke, and the pass swallowed them, granite rising on both sides, the afternoon light pouring through the gap ahead like something spilled. "Neither am I. But for the record: I'm glad it was you in that tent. Out of everyone in the world it could have been."

Xanthe looked down at her notebook, at the fresh map taking shape under her pencil — the valley, the river, the lake, the cairn on the east point, and beside it, in letters small enough to be deniable and firm enough to be permanent, a name she had drafted during the survey lines and now traced over once more:

*Hartwell Valley.*

"Entering the pass," Kestrel said. "Next stop, the world."

Then her hand froze on the throttle, and Xanthe followed her gaze up through the windshield, to the far end of the corridor — where a white aircraft with no markings, too fast and too low, was coming through the pass toward them.
