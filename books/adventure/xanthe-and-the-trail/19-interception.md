## 19. Interception

The valley fell away beneath us like a held breath finally released.

I had the Cessna trimmed and climbing through the ice corridor's throat, the walls of the glacier rising on either side in blue-white serried ranks, calving thunder rolling behind us like applause we hadn't earned yet. Xanthe sat in the right seat with the camera braced against the window, shooting frame after frame of the survey grid below — the river braiding silver through the valley floor, the stands of cedar no logger had ever touched, the meadows still holding their spring snowmelt in a hundred small mirrors.

"Grid seven complete," she said, voice tight with something that wasn't fear. Joy, maybe. Or its close cousin, terror. "Kestrel — do you see it? It's *real*. It's not just his lines on paper. It's real."

"I see it." My hands were steady on the yoke. Inside my chest was another matter entirely.

Then the radio crackled, and the joy curdled.

"—November Four-Seven-Bravo, this is Meridian Air Charter Two-Two-One out of Fort Renner, squawking four-five-one-two. We have you on TCAS, four miles south-southeast, climbing through nine thousand. Request you state intentions."

Xanthe lowered the camera slowly, like a drawn breath. "That's them," she said. "That's Hollis's people. That has to be them."

Meridian Air Charter. I knew the name the way you know the name of a dog that's bitten you before. The backers' charter outfit — the same one that had ferried Hollis Vane's survey crews up and down the coast for three seasons, the same one whose pilot I'd shared hangar coffee with in Fort Renner two winters back, back when I'd still thought the expedition's money was clean.

"They're forty miles from here at most," I said, scanning the sky ahead. "If they launched when the corridor opened—"

"They knew it would open." Xanthe's voice had gone flat and hard, the cartographer's voice, the one that measured things. "They've been watching the glacier the whole time. They didn't need to be on the river anymore. They just needed to wait for us to come to them."

The pass was eleven miles ahead. My mother's pass.

My father had named it the year I was six, according to the journal — the year he'd carried me up the moraine on his shoulders and shown me the notch in the ridge where the wind came through singing, where the clouds poured over the crest like water over a weir. He'd written: *Named it for Elena, because she always said the way home is never the way you came.* I'd read that line by lamplight in the trapper's cabin with Xanthe asleep across the room, and I'd had to set the journal down and press both palms flat against the floorboards until the shaking stopped.

Now that pass was the only door out of the mountains that didn't lead straight into Meridian's arms.

"Two-Two-One," I keyed, keeping my voice bored, professional, the voice of a pilot with nothing to hide. "Four-Seven-Bravo. We're a survey flight departing the Alder Creek area, returning to Fort Renner via the eastern route. No assistance required."

A pause. Long enough to mean something.

"Four-Seven-Bravo, be advised there are active weather advisories along your filed route. Suggest you divert north to join our track. We can escort you through."

*Escort.* The word sat in the air between Xanthe and me like a snake in a tent.

"He wants us in front of him," she said quietly. "Where he can see us. Where he can follow us all the way down and know exactly which strip we land at, exactly who we hand the data to."

"Or he wants us to turn north so he can box us against the ridge." I rolled the plane into a gentle bank, checking behind us. At first I saw nothing but cloud — then the cloud *moved*, wrong, against the wind, and resolved into a twin-engine shape sliding out of a deck of stratus two thousand feet above and behind us. White fuselage. Corporate clean. It hung there without closing the distance, patient as a hawk over a field mouse.

"There," Xanthe said. "There he is."

"I see him."

"Can you outrun him?"

"A Baron will outrun a 206 every day of the week and twice on Sundays." I pushed the nose down slightly, trading a little altitude for airspeed, feeling the old machine gather herself beneath me. "But I'm not going to outrun him. I'm going to lose him."

"Where?"

I looked at the pass. The wind was pouring over the crest in a long white banner, streaming east, and beyond it the weather I'd been reading since dawn was doing exactly what I'd hoped it would do — building. A front out of the northwest, moisture-rich and unstable, stacking itself against the high country like a wave deciding whether to break. Any sane pilot would give that pass a wide berth today.

Any sane pilot also wouldn't have flown into Alder Creek during a monsoon.

"My mother's pass," I said.

Xanthe looked at me. Then at the pass. Then at the white Baron hanging off our tail like a debt collector. "Through *that*?"

"Through that."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about flying in mountains: the terrain doesn't care about you, and that's the whole secret. A flatland pilot fights the weather. A mountain pilot *reads* it — reads the lenticulars stacked over the peaks like coins, reads the rotor clouds tumbling downwind of the crests, reads the way snow smokes off a ridgeline to tell you exactly how hard the wind is blowing and from where. Every feature of the sky is a sentence, and if you've spent enough years alone in the cockpit, you learn the language.

My father taught me the first words of it. The rest I taught myself, mostly out of loneliness, which turns out to be an excellent instructor.

"Talk to me about the wind," I said.

Xanthe unclipped the camera and pulled the laminated chart onto her knees. She'd spent nine days learning this country from the ground; she knew its bones better than any pilot who'd only seen it from above. "West-northwest, strengthening with altitude — look at the banner off the summit, it's horizontal. Over the crest it'll spill down the lee side in waves. Rotors. Maybe moderate, maybe worse."

"And past the rotors?"

She traced the contour lines with one finger, and I watched her mind work — watched her see the terrain the way I saw the sky, as a living thing with habits and moods. "The front's filling the valley beyond. Low ceiling, rain, maybe embedded stuff. But—" Her finger stopped. "There's a gap. Here. Where the river cuts through before it drops toward the coast. If the ceiling holds at two thousand along the gorge—"

"It'll hold. Orographic lift keeps the deck off the water in the narrows. I've flown that gap in worse."

"You've *flown* that gap?"

"Once. In a hailstorm. Don't ask." I checked the fuel — ninety minutes, comfortable, no margin for heroics — and keyed the radio one more time, because the last thing you do before you vanish is leave a lie behind for your pursuer to chew on. "Two-Two-One, Four-Seven-Bravo. Copy the advisory. We'll take your suggestion and join your track north. Give us five minutes to climb to ten thousand."

"Four-Seven-Bravo, copy. We'll keep the light on for you." The voice was smooth, unhurried, certain. Somewhere behind that calm was a man being paid by the hour by people who thought my father's map was theirs to burn.

"Hold your course north," I told Xanthe, "and don't look back. Looking back is how they know."

"How long do we play along?"

"Until the pass is abeam. Then we stop existing."

* * *

We flew north into the teeth of the front, and the Baron flew with us, half a mile back and a thousand feet up, close enough now that I could see the registration on its tail. N-number ending in Victor-Kilo. I put it away in memory, the way my father had taught me to put things away — not forgotten, filed.

The mountains rose around us. The air went rough, then rougher, the Cessna bucking through the leading edge of the front like a boat entering surf. Rain began to stipple the windshield, then to streak it. Ahead, the pass appeared — or rather, the idea of it, a gray notch in a black wall of rock and cloud, the wind screaming over the crest and tearing the moisture into streamers that whipped away east like hair in a gale.

"One minute," I said.

Xanthe had gone very quiet. She'd cinched the camera case down, zipped the map case, done everything a good crew does before things go wrong. Now she just watched the pass grow, and I felt her fear and her faith sitting side by side in the right seat, and I found — strangely — that they steadied me. For years I had flown alone precisely so that no one would ever have to trust me with their life, because trusting meant being trusted, and being trusted meant being known. Now a woman I'd known for three weeks was betting everything on my hands, and instead of the old claustrophobia I felt something else. Something like the opposite of alone.

"Kestrel." Her voice under the engine noise. "Whatever happens — the data's in two places. Camera card and the backup drive. If we go down in there, the drive goes in the waterproof tube, and the tube goes in the river. The river goes to the coast. Someone finds it eventually."

"Nothing's going to happen."

"That's what my father used to say too."

"Did anything happen?"

"...He died in a flood, Kestrel."

I put the nose exactly where it needed to be. "Then I won't say it. But nothing's going to happen."

Thirty seconds. Twenty. The Baron behind us, matching our course, lulled. In his headset I could imagine the conversation — *she's taking the northern route like she said, she's cooperating, easy pickings* — and I let myself imagine it because imagining your opponent's comfort is the sweetest part of any ambush.

The pass drew abeam off our left wing.

"Now," I said, and dropped the left wing, and dove.

* * *

It was not flying so much as falling with intent. I rolled into a descending turn that would have made my old instructor apoplectic — sixty degrees of bank, full power, the stall horn chirping its warning like a nervous passenger — and the world spun from gray sky to black rock and back again as we corkscrewed down through eight thousand feet in under a minute. Grit pressed me into the harness. Beside me Xanthe made a sound that was half gasp and half laugh, wild and involuntary, and I understood it completely. There is a moment in every desperate maneuver where fear tips over into exaltation, and we went through it together.

Behind and above, the Baron's shape swung wide, confused, its pilot surely keying the radio — *Four-Seven-Bravo, what are you doing, say intentions* — and I didn't answer, because answering was a thread and threads get followed.

We came out of the dive two hundred feet above the valley floor with the rain hammering down and the river flashing beneath us, and I turned hard south, hugging the terrain the way my father's journal said he'd hugged it, low and slow and inside the ground clutter where radar eyes go blind. The gorge narrowed. The walls closed in, wet black granite streaming with waterfalls, and the ceiling came down to meet us until we were flying in a slot barely wider than the Cessna's wingspan, cloud above, rock on both sides, the river a brown ribbon below.

This was the part I couldn't teach and couldn't explain. This was the part that lived in my hands.

"Talk to me," I said, because two sets of eyes beat one, even now.

"Rock spire, left, fifty yards!" Xanthe's voice cracked out sharp and clear, no panic in it, pure function. "Bend right! Ceiling lifting ahead — maybe twelve hundred feet — bridge of rock, HIGH, it's high, we're under it —"

We went under the stone bridge with maybe eighty feet to spare, the roar of the river bouncing off the walls and filling the cabin, and then the gorge opened its fist and spat us out into the wider valley beyond, and the front was there waiting for us like a wall.

Rain in sheets. Visibility a quarter mile, then less. Lightning walked the ridgelines to the west, silent behind the engine's bellow, and the turbulence hit us in fists — up, down, sideways, the kind of air that breaks airplanes piloted by anyone less than certain. The Baron, I knew, was somewhere behind us, above us, its pilot facing the same choice I'd offered him: follow the crazy woman into the maelstrom, or climb above the weather and lose her in the clutter.

He hesitated. I felt the hesitation the way you feel a fish stop pulling — a change in the tension of the line. And in that hesitation I turned south along the front's edge, riding the seam between the storm and the clearing, the place where the radar returns smeared into noise, the place where a single low-flying Cessna becomes indistinguishable from the rain itself.

"Is he still there?" I asked.

Xanthe twisted around, pressing her face to the rear window. Ten seconds. Fifteen. "No," she said. "No — wait — there's a shadow, high, moving north — he's giving up. He's *giving up*, Kestrel, he's turning away!"

"Not giving up." I leveled off, wiped the sweat from my eyes with the back of my wrist. "Reporting in. By the time he tells them he lost us, we'll be on the ground at the regional strip with the data uploading. He knows it too. That's why he's not chasing. Chasing costs him the thing they actually want."

"Which is?"

"The story. If we land quiet, they can still spin it. If we land loud—" I glanced at her, and despite the adrenaline still singing in my blood, despite the rain still lashing the glass, I smiled. "We're going to land loud."

* * *

Twenty minutes later the front was behind us and the coast was opening below — green hills, gray sea, the regional strip's single runway lying across a headland like a ruler someone had dropped. I called inbound on the radio in the clear, plain voice, no codes: survey aircraft, two aboard, requesting parking near the terminal building, and I made sure to say it twice, and I made sure the frequency was one that got recorded.

Xanthe had the laptop open across her knees, the satellite modem's antenna wedged against the window, the upload queue loaded and ready: the photographs, the survey grids, the scanned pages of my father's journal, the coordinates of the valley, all of it addressed simultaneously to the registry office in the capital and to a journalist named Okafor who had spent three months answering Xanthe's careful, anonymous emails.

"Signal's strong," she said. "One bar. Two. As soon as we're parked—"

"As soon as we're parked," I agreed, "and not one second before. I want wheels stopped, doors open, and witnesses. Lots of witnesses."

The runway grew. The wind, mercifully, was straight down the tarmac at eight knots, the gentlest gift the sky had given us all day. I flared the Cessna onto the pavement with a touch soft enough to make my father proud, wherever he was — or wherever he wasn't, which amounted to the same thing now, because everything he'd built, everything he'd died protecting, was riding in a waterproof tube between Xanthe's feet and a camera card in her pocket.

We rolled out. We turned off. And there, parked on the apron beside the fuel farm, white and corporate-clean and unmistakable even from a mile out on final—

—the Baron.

N-number ending in Victor-Kilo. Empty. Its pilot standing beside it with a phone pressed to his ear, watching us taxi in, making no move at all to stop us.

Xanthe saw him the same moment I did. Her hand went to the camera bag.

"He's not here to fight," I said slowly, shutting down the engine, listening to the silence rush in. "He's here to watch us fail. Or—"

The phone in my own pocket buzzed. Then buzzed again. Three messages, back to back, from a number I didn't know.

I opened the first one, and the bottom dropped out of the morning.

*Kestrel,* it read. *Before you press send — you should know what really happened to the others at Alder Creek. Your father didn't die in a flood. Call me. — H.V.*

The second message was a photograph: my father's camp, intact, taken from the air. The third was a time stamp from nine days ago.

Nine days before the flood. Nine days before the silence. Nine days before we ever found that tent torn open and empty.

"Kestrel?" Xanthe's hand was on my arm. "What is it? What's wrong?"

I stared at the screen, at the man across the apron still watching us, phone to his ear, and the data sat unuploaded in Xanthe's lap, and the whole story I thought I'd survived shifted beneath me like ice going out.

"He's lying," I said. But my voice didn't believe me.
