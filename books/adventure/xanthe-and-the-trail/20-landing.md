## 20. Landing

The valley let us go the way a hand opens — slowly, finger by finger, as if reluctant to release what it had been holding.

I watched it fall away beneath us through the camera port, green and impossible in its fold of mountains, the river inside it running silver where no map had ever given it a name. Kestrel had flown the survey lines twice already, once for the wide shots and once low enough that I could see individual spruce crowns, the pale scar of a rockslide, the blue eye of a tarn her father had drawn from memory and never seen from above. The memory card sat in my shirt pocket like a second heartbeat. Every few minutes my hand drifted up to touch it, checking.

"Stop fidgeting," Kestrel said over the intercom. Her voice was hoarse from shouting into wind and radio static, but there was something underneath it I'd never heard before. Not quite joy. Something adjacent to it — the sound of a woman who has decided to land somewhere instead of merely arriving.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're touching your pocket every ninety seconds. I can see you in the reflection off the instrument glass."

I took my hand away. Put it back. She laughed — actually laughed, a short rough bark that the headset turned into something almost gentle — and banked us south toward the regional strip at Carrowmere, forty minutes out, the first place with a tower, a fuel pump, and a telephone line that reached past the mountains.

Forty minutes to decide everything.

* * *

We had rehearsed it on the ground, crouched over the tailgate of the plane while meltwater refroze around our boots, because Kestrel insisted that if we were going to do this we would do it properly, with contingencies, the way her father had taught her to plan a fuel stop.

"The moment the wheels stop," she'd said, "you go inside. Not me. You."

"Why you and not me?"

"Because they'll be watching for me. Hartwell's daughter, the pilot who went dark, the one who stole their charter's attention in the pass." She'd said it flatly, without drama, the way she said most true things. "You're nobody to them. A name on an invoice. That's our advantage, and we use it exactly once."

I hadn't liked being called nobody. But she was right, and we both knew it, and there wasn't time to argue about dignity when the ice corridor behind us was closing like a door.

So now, as Carrowmere rose out of the gray — a scratch of runway between black water and blacker forest, two hangars, a tower with fogged windows — I ran the sequence again in my head. Upload the photographs to Marisol Vance at the *Coastal Ledger*. Upload the coordinates and the journal scans to the provincial land registry's public comment portal before the five o'clock filing deadline. Both at once, both before anyone could take the memory card, the satellite phone, or me.

Kestrel brought us in against a crosswind that smelled of rain and diesel, and the tires touched tarmac with a sound like a held breath finally released.

She didn't shut down the engine.

"Go," she said.

* * *

The terminal at Carrowmere was one room: four plastic chairs, a vending machine humming like a hive, a corkboard furred with decades of pinned notices, and a payphone bolted to the wall beside a window streaked with old rain. The clerk — a heavyset man doing a crossword, who looked up with the mild interest of someone who saw one plane a day — pointed me toward the phone and the outlet behind the vending machine without being asked. Small towns, I was learning, ran on a different economy than cities. Here, the currency was simply letting people alone.

My hands shook so badly the first attempt at the jack missed entirely.

I thought about that later — how fear doesn't leave you all at once, how it drains out in stages, the way floodwater leaves a valley. My fingers remembered nine days of rationing, the cold arithmetic of half-biscuits. They remembered the gorge, the white water standing up like walls, the journal nearly going into the river and Kestrel's arm, impossibly long, impossibly sure, hauling it back over the gunwale while I bailed with a fuel jug. They remembered the man on the gravel bar, Voss, with his clean boots and his offer folded in oilcloth, and Kestrel striking a match.

But mostly, in that moment, my hands remembered that we had made it. That the data existed. That it was real, and it was mine to give away.

The modem in the terminal's ancient desktop crawled through its handshake like something waking from hibernation. While it whined, I fed the payphone coins and dialed the number Marisol Vance had left, three weeks ago, in a message Kestrel had almost deleted.

"*Ledger newsroom.*"

"It's Xanthe Reyes. From Alder Creek. We spoke through the relay station — I told you I'd call when I had proof."

A pause, then the sound of a chair rolling fast across a floor. "You're supposed to be dead. Or missing. There's a wire story — the backers put out a statement yesterday, Hartwell Expeditions lost with all hands, memorial service planned—"

"We're not dead. And the statement is about to become a problem for them." The progress bar crept. Forty percent. "I'm sending you photographs. An entire valley, unrecorded, inside the boundaries of the Meridian Resource Group's pending claim. Their own hired cartographer took them. Me. Along with scans of Elias Hartwell's field journal, which documents that Meridian knew the valley existed and moved to bury the survey rather than file it. It's all timestamped. It's all geotagged."

Silence on the line. Then, quietly, the way people speak when they understand they are holding something live: "How long until I can publish?"

"Ninety seconds after this upload finishes. I'm also filing the same package to the land registry's public portal. Once it's in the record, it's in the record."

"Miss Reyes." I heard her take a breath. "For what it's worth — I'm glad you're alive."

Seventy percent. Eighty. The vending machine hummed. Outside, through the rain-streaked glass, I could see Kestrel still sitting in the cockpit, arms crossed, watching the road that came in from the highway. Watching for a charcoal-gray truck.

One hundred percent. *Upload complete.*

"Marisol. Publish."

* * *

What happened next happened in overlapping waves, some of them visible and some of them not, and I have tried since to keep the order straight, but memory is a cartographer too and it draws what it needs.

The registry portal accepted the filing at 4:47 p.m., thirteen minutes before deadline, and generated the automated confirmation that would later be quoted in three separate inquiries: *Public submission received; document enters review queue; public comment period opens immediately.* Which meant, in plain language, that the valley's coordinates were now part of the permanent record, attached to Meridian's claim, visible to any journalist, any rival company, any government hydrologist who cared to look. You cannot un-file a mountain.

Marisol's story went live at 5:12 under a headline I did not choose but will never forget: **THE VALLEY MERIDIAN BURIED**. Beneath it, the lead photograph — my photograph — the river running silver through the unnamed green, taken from eight hundred feet, from the right-hand seat of a plane whose pilot had spent twenty years flying away from her father and had just flown straight back into his life's work.

By 5:30 the terminal's landline was ringing. By 6:00 it hadn't stopped.

The clerk unplugged it eventually, apologetic, and wrote numbers on a pad: the *Ledger*, a CBC producer, a lawyer whose firm's name meant nothing to me then and a great deal later, and — last, circled twice — a message from the regional police detachment requesting that Ms. Reyes and Ms. Hartwell remain available.

"They know we're here," I said, coming out onto the apron. The rain had thinned to mist. Kestrel stood by the wing with her jacket collar up, and in the failing light she looked carved rather than tired.

"They've known since we entered their airspace," she said. "Question is whether knowing does them any good anymore." She nodded at the terminal. "It's done?"

"It's done. Registry, press, timestamps. Marisol says the story's already being picked up." I hesitated. "There's a wire piece about a memorial service."

Something moved across her face — quick, weather-shadow, gone. "For us."

"For the expedition. All hands lost." I looked down at my boots. "They wrote your father's obituary twice, Kess. Once when he actually died, and once as cover."

She was quiet for a long moment. Mist beaded on the aluminum wing and ran off in threads. When she spoke again her voice was level, but I had learned by then to hear the effort that levelness cost her.

"Then we'll want a correction issued. Names spelled right this time." She shouldered her bag. "Come on. We need fuel, food, and a locked room, probably in that order."

That was when the charcoal-gray truck turned off the highway.

* * *

I felt Kestrel go still beside me, the way water goes still over something deep. The truck rolled down the access road unhurried, wipers ticking, and stopped thirty meters short of the plane. The driver's door opened.

Not Voss. Someone older, silver-haired, wearing a coat that cost more than the truck, and he walked toward us with his hands visible and open, which I understood was itself a kind of message.

"Ms. Hartwell." He stopped at a respectful distance. Rain misted on his shoulders and he didn't seem to notice or care. "My name is Alden Marsh. I'm chairman of the board at Meridian Resource Group." A pause, calibrated. "And I believe I owe your family an apology measured in decades."

Kestrel said nothing. Beside her, I kept my eyes on his hands.

"Voss acted beyond his authority," Marsh continued. "He has been terminated this afternoon, along with two others, effective as of the moment your photographs appeared on the front page of a newspaper I used to read over breakfast." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely weary. "You should know the board voted unanimously, forty minutes ago, to withdraw the claim. Publicly. Citing, if you'll forgive the language, 'irreconcilable discrepancies in the underlying survey data.'"

"You mean citing the fact that you got caught," I said.

His gaze shifted to me, and there was no weariness in it at all — just a quick, appraising sharpness, the look of a man re-pricing an asset. "You must be the cartographer. Elias spoke highly of you, in the end. In writing." He reached slowly into his coat, and Kestrel's weight shifted forward onto the balls of her feet, and he froze, palms out again. "A letter. That's all. He addressed it to you both. It arrived at our offices six weeks ago with instructions that it be delivered only if certain conditions were met." He held out a thick envelope, water-stained at one corner, sealed with wax gone dull with age. "I'm told the condition was that the valley survive."

Nobody moved. The mist fell. Somewhere beyond the hangars a raven said something sour to the evening.

"He mailed it before he died," Kestrel said. Not a question. Her voice had gone very quiet, the quiet of deep water.

"He mailed it the day your expedition's funding was finalized. He suspected, even then, what some of us intended. He wanted—" Marsh stopped, and for a moment the chairman's polish slipped and underneath was just an old tired man standing in the rain. "He wanted it known that not everyone at Meridian was Voss. That some of us read his journals and understood what we were being handed, and were ashamed. Whether that absolves anyone is not mine to say."

Kestrel looked at the envelope for a long time. Then she took it — carefully, by one corner, the way you take something that might still be burning — and Marsh stepped back, nodded once to each of us, and walked to his truck.

At the door he paused. "The withdrawal announcement runs tomorrow morning. Your valley, Ms. Hartwell, is about to become very famous. I'd suggest deciding soon what you want it to be called."

The truck pulled away, taillights dissolving into the mist, and the silence it left behind was enormous.

* * *

Later — much later, after fuel and canned stew and a motel room with two beds and a radiator that knocked like a polite ghost — Kestrel sat on the end of her bed with the envelope unopened in her lap. She had carried it all evening without opening it, through dinner, through the phone calls, through my pacing. Now she turned it over and over, reading her father's handwriting on the front the way I'd once watched her read cloud formations: looking for what it was telling her before she committed to trusting it.

*For my daughter, and for the one who finishes the map.*

"She finishes the map," Kestrel said softly. "He wrote that about you. Before he ever met you." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and shining all at once. "He knew. He knew he might not come back, and he knew who'd be out here, and he — he trusted a stranger with the ending of his life's work, because he couldn't ask his own daughter to—"

"Kess."

"Don't." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Don't be kind to me right now, I'll come apart, and I haven't got time to come apart, we fly the final documentation run at first light and the light only holds until ten—"

I crossed the small room and sat down beside her. I didn't say anything. I had learned that from her — that presence is sometimes the whole message — and after a while her breathing slowed and her hand found the envelope again.

"Do you want to open it tonight?" I asked.

"No." She set it on the nightstand, square with the lamp base, precise as everything she did. "Tomorrow. After we fly. He waited six weeks in a boardroom drawer; he can wait one more night." A breath that was almost a laugh. "Besides. If I open it now I'll cry all over the ink, and I've been told by a certain cartographer that archival materials require dry handling."

"That cartographer sounds insufferable."

"She's growing on me."

Outside, the rain had stopped altogether, and through the gap in the curtains I could see the sky clearing from the west — hard stars coming out over the coast range, the kind of sky that promised a cold, clean, perfect morning. The valley lay somewhere north of those stars, unnamed officially for perhaps another twelve hours, and already, in a thousand screens across the country, being renamed by strangers. *The Valley Meridian Buried*, the headline said. But I knew what we would file in the registry at dawn, the name from the last page of the journal, the one Elias Hartwell had saved for the end:

*Kestrel's Valley.*

I was almost asleep when Kestrel spoke again from her bed, so quietly I might have dreamed it.

"Xanthe. The letter says *for the one who finishes the map.* Plural address. Yours and mine." A pause long enough for the radiator to knock twice. "When this is over — when the protection goes through — I'm going to need a head cartographer. Small operation. Terrible coffee. Mostly bad weather."

I smiled into the dark. "I'll think about it."

"Liar."

"Complete liar," I agreed, and slept without dreaming of floods for the first time in weeks.
