## 21. What Remains

The river had dropped two feet since the monsoon broke.

You could see it in the mud lines on the alders — a dark tidemark running along their stems like a ledger someone had finally balanced — and you could hear it in the water itself, which no longer roared but talked, low and conversational, the way a man talks after he's said everything angry he had to say and is just tired now. Kestrel stood at the edge of what remained of the Alder Creek camp and let the sound wash over her. Three weeks ago this gravel bar had been a battlefield of tarps and toppled crates. Now it was just a place. The flood had taken the tents, most of the stores, the expedition's pretensions. It had left the fire ring, a bent aluminum table leg sticking out of the silt like a fossilized wishbone, and her father's footprints — or what she chose to believe were his footprints — pressed into the clay where the water couldn't reach them.

She had the copy of the journal in a dry bag under her arm. She'd made it herself, three nights ago at the regional strip, at a copy shop that smelled of toner and burnt coffee, feeding page after water-stained page through the machine while Xanthe waited outside with two coffees going cold. Forty-one pages. His handwriting started confident and slanted and ended cramped and small, as if the words themselves were running out of room to live in.

She hadn't told Xanthe why she wanted the copy. Xanthe hadn't asked. That was one of the things Kestrel had learned about her over the cold weeks of waiting: she knew when a silence was load-bearing.

* * *

She walked the camp's perimeter first, because her father would have. Elias Hartwell had been a man of perimeters — he checked the edges of things before he trusted the middles of them, camps and maps and arguments alike. She found the survey stakes he'd set on their first day, cedar laths with orange flagging gone gray in the weather, and she found the spot where the supply tent had stood, marked now by a rectangle of flattened grass and six tent pegs nobody had bothered to pull. One of them was bent into a hook shape. She remembered him teaching her to straighten pegs like that when she was nine, kneeling beside her at some lakeside campsite, his hands enormous around hers. *A bent peg is a lesson, Kess. A broken one is a choice.*

She knelt and pulled all six, wiped the mud off each one, and coiled them into her jacket pocket. It was a small, stupid act of salvage. She did it anyway.

The river slid past, brown-green and patient. Somewhere upstream, past the gorge they'd run in a leaking skiff with the journal zipped against Xanthe's chest and Kestrel's hands cramping on the oars, the valley waited under its new protection — surveyed, photographed, logged, its coordinates already sitting in a land registry office and a journalist's inbox, doing the quiet legal work that would keep it unroaded and unnamed by anyone but her father. He'd called it the Hollow of Bright Water in the journal, which was sentimental by his standards and therefore, she understood now, the truest thing he'd ever written.

She unsnapped the dry bag.

The copy felt lighter than the original, though she knew paper didn't work that way. Weight was a thing you assigned. The original sat in a fireproof box in the plane's baggage compartment, wrapped in a flight suit that still smelled faintly of avgas, and it would stay there, because some inheritances were meant to be carried forward and some were meant to be put down, and she had spent the whole flight in this morning deciding which was which, turning the question over the way you turn a stone to see what lives underneath.

Her father had written the last entry eleven days before the flood took him. She knew it by heart now, the way pilots know a frequency:

*If anyone reads this, I was wrong about the weather and right about the rest. The valley is real. The backers know it's real, and that is exactly the problem. I've sent my notes to the only person I trust to finish what I started. She won't come looking for me. That's my fault, not hers. But she'll come for the map. She always finishes what she starts.*

*Tell my daughter the river wasn't her fault.*

Kestrel read it again, standing there, and then she folded the copy closed and crouched at the river's edge.

She'd thought about burning it. There was a symmetry to fire; she'd burned the buyout offer in a coffee can while Xanthe watched with an expression Kestrel still couldn't fully read. But fire was erasure, and erasure was the backers' method, and she was done doing their work for them even by accident. Water was different. Water carried things downstream and gave them back changed. Her father had loved rivers the way other men loved churches.

She waded in to her ankles. The current tugged at her boots, insistent, familiar — the same current she'd fought in the gorge, the same one that had taken the skiff's stern and nearly taken Xanthe's hand and very nearly taken everything. She opened the copy and tore out the pages one at a time, forty-one of them, and fed them to the river.

They didn't sink right away. They rode the surface, ink bleeding, his sentences dissolving into gray blooms, and then the current took them under and they were gone, or they were somewhere else, which amounted to the same thing. She stood until the last white rectangle vanished around the bend, and she realized her face was wet and that it wasn't rain, and she let it be that for a while without grading herself on it.

*Tell my daughter the river wasn't her fault.*

"Okay, Dad," she said aloud, to no one, to the water. "Okay."

Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Grief, she was learning, didn't always shout. Sometimes it just moved in and paid rent and stopped being a guest.

* * *

Xanthe was at the plane when Kestrel got back, sitting on the wheel chock with her knees drawn up, drawing.

Of course she was drawing. Xanthe drew the way other people breathed — constantly, unconsciously, a stub of pencil traveling across whatever paper was within reach. Right now it was a corner of the fuel log, and she was sketching the airstrip from above, the way a bird or a god would see it: the gravel strip, the alder stand, the creek's new channel where the flood had cut it, all of it rendered in quick sure lines with little contour ticks climbing the hillsides like flocking birds. Even her shorthand looked like cartography. Kestrel had watched her annotate a grocery list once and come away feeling like she'd seen a topographic secret.

"Any luck?" Xanthe asked, not looking up.

"It's done."

Xanthe's pencil paused. Then she nodded, once, and went back to drawing, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was exactly enough. Kestrel had spent years around people who performed their feelings at volume, colleagues who processed out loud, dispatchers who needed their sympathies acknowledged in triplicate. Xanthe simply made room. She'd done it in the trapper's cabin during the monsoon, saying nothing when Kestrel surfaced at 3 a.m. from a dream about her father's voice on the radio, just sliding the lamp an inch closer and keeping her eyes on her maps until Kestrel was ready to speak or not speak. It was, Kestrel had decided somewhere around day four of the cold camp, the single most decent thing a person could do.

"Fuel's topped," Xanthe said. "I checked the tires for the third time, so if you want to check them a fourth time to feel useful, be my guest."

"I'll allow it." Kestrel stowed the empty dry bag and ran her preflight out of habit, though she'd done it an hour ago. Tires, struts, pitot cover off, control surfaces free. The Cessna sat on the gravel looking patient and slightly rumpled, the way it always did, its paint sun-faded to the color of weak tea. Nineteen years old and still the most reliable relationship in her life. Until recently, anyway.

She climbed up on the wing to check the fuel caps and found herself looking down at Xanthe, who had tilted her head back to watch her, pencil still moving, and Kestrel felt the thing she'd been circling for two days come around again and settle, heavy and warm, in her chest.

There was a seat open in her cockpit. There had been one for nineteen years. She'd filled it occasionally — ferry passengers, a wildlife biologist, once a very airsick senator's aide — but never permanently, never by choice, because a permanent right-hand seat meant a permanent witness, and Kestrel Hartwell had built her entire adult life on the principle that witnesses were liabilities. Leave before anyone gets close. Fuel up, file out, don't tell them your middle name. She'd flown that pattern so long it had worn grooves in her like a record.

And then a stranger had barricaded herself in a supply tent with nine days of rationed food and a signal mirror and a stubbornness that Kestrel recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting.

"Hey," Kestrel said. "Question."

"Mm."

"When we get back to town. What's your plan?"

Xanthe looked up fully now. "Plan?"

"You heard me. You can't go back to the backers — obviously. Your anonymous employer is either in jail, embarrassed, or both. Your gear's mostly at the bottom of the gorge. So. Plan."

"I assumed," Xanthe said slowly, "I'd find contract work. Mapping. There's a mining outfit up the Mackenzie that hires freelancers, and—" She stopped, because Kestrel was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name yet, and people who survived nine days alone in the wilderness were usually good at reading expressions, and something in Kestrel's face made her stop mid-sentence and wait.

"The mining outfit will want you to falsify contours eventually," Kestrel said. "That's how those places work. They hire the best and then ask them to lie with lines."

"I know how they work."

"Yeah. You do. That's kind of my point." Kestrel hopped down off the wing, landed in the gravel, and discovered that her heart was going faster than it did on short-field approaches. Ridiculous. She was offering a job, not proposing marriage. She shoved her hands in her pockets and found the six tent pegs there, cold and smooth, and held onto them like talismans.

"So here's mine," she said. "I'm filing the survey company papers next week. Small. Stubborn. One plane, two seats. We've got the valley data, we've got the journalist contact, we've got a reputation now — apparently word travels when you burn a half-million-dollar buyout in a coffee can. And I need somebody in the right-hand seat who can read terrain better than I can, which, before you argue, is demonstrably true, because I flew us into that pass behind the charter and *you're* the one who spotted the cut bank that saved our tails."

"That was luck."

"That was contour lines. Don't be modest, it doesn't suit you." She took a breath. The river talked behind her. "Permanent seat, Xanthe. Right-hand side. Radio, navigation, and veto power on any client who smells like the Alder Creek backers. Pay is bad, hours are worse, and I will check the tires more than you think is reasonable."

Xanthe was very still. Pencil down. In the weeks Kestrel had known her, she'd catalogued maybe five expressions on the woman's face with any confidence, and what crossed it now wasn't any of them — it was something younger, unguarded, the look of someone who has been hired anonymously and abandoned publicly and has stopped expecting anyone to say *stay.*

"You're asking me," Xanthe said, "to be your copilot."

"I'm asking you to be my family," Kestrel said, before she could stop herself, and then, because retreat was worse than exposure, she planted her feet and finished it. "The paperwork says copilot. The paperwork lies. My father willed me a map instead of money, and I have decided to continue the tradition of unconventional inheritance. So. Yes or no?"

The pause lasted three seconds, which on the ground feels like a full instrument approach. Then Xanthe picked up her pencil, turned the fuel log over, and on the blank back drew — fast, sure, no erasing — a small precise rendering of a Cessna 180 in profile, two figures visible through the windshield, one at each stick. She signed it bottom right, the way she signed her surveys, and held it out.

"Yes," she said. "But I get the good headset."

"They're both the good headset."

"Then I get the one that works."

Kestrel took the drawing. Her hands were not entirely steady, and she elected not to have feelings about that in front of witnesses, except that the witness was family now, so technically she was allowed. She folded the sketch with exaggerated care and put it in the breast pocket where she kept her license — documents that certified who she was, filed together at last.

"Deal," she said. "Preflight's done. Get in."

* * *

They lifted off Alder Creek at 10:40 in the morning, into air so clean it felt rinsed. The strip fell away beneath them, and then the creek, silver-brown and harmless-seeming, and then the whole wounded green country of it, scarred channels healing over, the trapper's cabin a gray tooth in the trees, the gorge a dark seam where Kestrel's hands remembered gripping oars instead of yokes.

She keyed the radio out of habit and then, instead of signing off with the regional strip, she did something she hadn't done in nineteen years of flying. She reached over and let Xanthe make the call.

"Alder Creek traffic, Cessna One-Eight-Zero-Bravo, departing to the northeast, climbing through two thousand," Xanthe said, her voice level and clear, and Kestrel listened to her own call sign in someone else's mouth and found that it sounded less like losing something and more like doubling it.

They climbed. The valley opened below them for one last look — her father's valley, the Hollow of Bright Water, green and guarded and officially, irrevocably real — and Kestrel tipped a wing toward it, a small private salute, and turned north.

In her breast pocket, over her heart, the drawing rode where her license used to sit alone. In the baggage compartment, the original journal rode in its fireproof box, going wherever she went, which was, she was beginning to understand, no longer a place she fled to but a direction she chose.

The horizon ahead was wide and cloudless, and for the first time in her life, Kestrel Hartwell wasn't measuring it in miles.

She was measuring it in years — and the first one was already asking for a destination.
