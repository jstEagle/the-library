## 22. Miles and Years

### Kestrel

The hangar smelled the way it always did in October — cold aluminum and engine oil and, underneath it now, woodsmoke drifting in from Xanthe's stove in the office she'd claimed as her own. Kestrel stood under the wing of the Otter with a torque wrench in her hand and let herself do nothing for a full minute.

That was new. A year ago she wouldn't have known how.

Outside, the first snow of the season was coming down soft and unhurried over Hartwell & Vale Survey Company — which was to say, over a gravel strip, two hangars, one windsock that Xanthe had repainted orange because "beige is a coward's color," and a sign Kestrel had welded herself from scrap pipe. The valley had a name on the maps now. *Eastwick Basin*, protected under a conservation order that had gone through in March, after the journalist ran the story and the registry filings made the backers' claim collapse like wet cardboard. Her father's handwriting was even in the citation — they'd quoted his journal, the line about *water remembering every shape it's ever been*. He'd have hated the attention and loved the permanence, probably in that order.

Kestrel wiped her hands and looked at the calendar tacked to the workbench. Not the flight log — the calendar. Another thing that was new. She'd spent her whole adult life measuring time in hours flown, in nautical miles, in fuel remaining. The logbook was still there, still honest: eleven hundred hours this year, most of them survey lines over the basin, grid after patient grid until the valley was mapped down to its seeps and slide paths. But the calendar had other things on it now. Xanthe's birthday, circled twice. The day the ice corridor opened last spring, marked with a small drawn airplane. A square in December labeled only *don't fly — soup*.

She heard boots on gravel before she heard the voice.

"You're under the plane again," Xanthe called through the hangar door. "It's snowing sideways and you're under the plane."

"It's pre-season inspection."

"It's October. Nothing flies until the passes clear anyway." Xanthe came around the wing, ducking under it without looking, the way she did — she knew this aircraft's geometry better than some of its rivets by now. She was carrying two enamel mugs and wearing the wool hat Kestrel's mother had knitted decades ago, salvaged from a box of Eastwick things Xanthe had quietly unpacked into their shared life one item at a time, like a cartographer extending a map edge by edge. "Tea. Sit down. I found something in your father's field notes you need to see, and you're going to look at it sitting near a heat source like a person."

Kestrel took the mug. It was too hot to drink, so she held it against her palms and followed Xanthe into the office, where the stove ticked and the walls were papered in maps — the basin, the gorge, the pass named for her mother, each one annotated in two hands now, her father's faded pencil and Xanthe's precise ink, and lately a third hand, hers, learning contour lines the way you learn a language: slowly, then all at once, then like you'd always known it.

Xanthe spread a page on the desk. "He sketched this in the last week of the journal. Look at the margin."

Kestrel looked. In the corner, in her father's cramped hand: *K. will want to run the east ridge line low. Tell her the updraft off the glacier is a liar in October.*

She laughed — a short, startled sound that still surprised her when it came out. "He never met you. How did he know you'd be the one telling me?"

"Cartographers know things," Xanthe said, deadpan, and then ruined it by grinning.

Kestrel sat down near the heat source, like a person. Outside the window the snow thickened, erasing the strip, the windsock, the world beyond the fence, and for once the whiteout didn't make her restless. There was nowhere she needed to be that wasn't here.

### Xanthe

Xanthe had learned, over twelve months, to read Kestrel the way she read terrain — not by staring directly at the summit but by watching the shadows move across it.

So she noticed, that evening while they ate stew at the little table wedged between map drawers, that Kestrel kept glancing at the shelf above the radio. On the shelf sat three objects: the original journal, wrapped in oilcloth; a photograph of a young woman in front of a de Havilland, squinting into sun; and a small brass compass that had belonged to Elias Eastwick and that Xanthe had bought back, at absurd expense, from a collector in Whitehorse who'd gotten it in a lot sale of expedition effects. Some inheritances you had to go retrieve by hand.

"You're doing the thing," Xanthe said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you almost say something and then file it under *later*, and later becomes a year, and a year becomes never. I've mapped that pattern. It has no outlet."

Kestrel put down her spoon. For a long moment she looked at the compass instead of at Xanthe, which Xanthe understood to be the same as looking at her. Some people could only face you by facing slightly away.

"A year ago tonight," Kestrel said, "I was sleeping in the Otter at Alder Creek with a shotgun across my knees, listening to the river eat the airstrip, thinking I'd just met someone who was either going to get me killed or save my life, and I genuinely couldn't tell which."

"And now?"

"Now I know which." Kestrel turned the mug in her hands. "Both. In that order, actually."

Xanthe felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the day she'd confessed what she'd originally been paid to do — the betrayal she'd carried out of the gorge like water in her boots. Forgiveness, when it finally came, hadn't arrived as a speech. It had arrived as a second key cut for the hangar door, left on her pillow without comment. That was Kestrel's language. Xanthe had learned to speak it fluently.

"I've been thinking about names," Xanthe said, to move past the moment before it got heavy enough to drown in. "For the company. Everyone keeps asking what *Hartwell & Vale* means, and I keep making things up. Last week I told a client it was Latin."

"It's not Latin?"

"It's you and me, Kestrel. Hartwell and Vale. I named us after ourselves because I couldn't think of anything grander than that, and I didn't want to."

Kestrel was quiet. Then she said, "Vale means valley, doesn't it. In old place-names."

Xanthe blinked. "Yes. Actually. It does."

"So we're named after the valley too." Kestrel almost smiled. "He'd say that was showing off."

"He'd say it was *thorough*," Xanthe corrected. "There's a difference, and he'd insist on it."

They washed the bowls together in the small sink, shoulders bumping, an economy of motion they'd developed like a flight crew — which, Xanthe supposed, they were. Two-person operation. Small and stubborn. The accountant had told them in June that they were structurally unprofitable and emotionally irrational, and Kestrel had thanked him sincerely and shown him the door.

Later, Xanthe sat at the drafting table working on the winter commission — a school atlas, of all things, the basin rendered in colors bright enough for children — while Kestrel tuned the radio, not for traffic but for the sound of it, the way some people leave a window open in winter just to hear the weather. Static rolled through the hangar like surf.

"Anything out there?" Xanthe asked.

"Same as always. Freight schedules. A guy in Fort Nelson complaining about his snowblower." Kestrel paused. "And once a year or so, somebody asks if we'll take a contract we shouldn't. And once a year or so, we don't."

"That's the whole business model?"

"That's the whole business model."

Xanthe put down her pen. Under the lamp, the basin map glowed, every contour a sentence her father-teacher had begun and she and Kestrel had finished. She thought about the nine days alone in the supply tent, rationing chocolate and talking to a river, certain she would die unmapped and unmissed. She thought about the skiff taking on water. The boot prints. The man with the buyout offer and the eyes of someone who'd stopped seeing land as land. All of it felt like it had happened to a different cartographer, one who believed that being hired meant being valued, that a signature on a contract was a kind of belonging.

She knew better now. Belonging was a second key on a pillow. Belonging was a hat that had been knitted before you were born, given to you anyway.

"Kestrel," she said. "Next summer. The east ridge line. Your father's note — the updraft liar. I want to run it with you. Low, if you'll let me call the terrain. High, if you won't."

Kestrel looked over her shoulder, and in her face Xanthe saw the whole year settle into place like a final contour closing on itself.

"Low," Kestrel said. "But we wait for morning air. We're not twenty anymore."

"We were never twenty."

"No," Kestrel agreed, and there was no grief in it, only the plain fact, worn smooth as river stone. "We were never twenty. Good. Twenty doesn't know what it's flying toward."

### Kestrel

Snow kept falling through November, and Kestrel kept not leaving.

That was the measure of it, she realized one grey afternoon while she re-caulked a window seam that didn't urgently need caulking. Every winter of her adult life she'd gone somewhere — south for contracts, west for fuel prices, anywhere the runway wasn't buried under her own history. This winter she'd booked nothing. The furthest south she intended to fly was the regional strip, forty minutes each way, to pick up a crate of chart paper and, if Xanthe's letter was right, a very large and deeply unnecessary telescope.

She caught herself counting, sometimes. Not miles. Winters. This was the second one. Her father had gotten thirty-one; she'd counted them from the journal, the way you count tree rings, and decided that whatever number she reached, she'd reach it mapped — knowing the names of her neighbors' dogs, knowing which stovepipe smoked in which wind, knowing that in February the light came through the hangar's east window at 9:40 and laid a gold bar across the Otter's propeller like a blessing nobody had ordered.

In December, on the square marked *soup*, they drove the truck into town for the winter fair, and Kestrel stood in a gymnasium smelling of cinnamon and wet wool while children pointed at the basin map Xanthe had donated to the school and asked questions neither of them had answers elegant enough for. *Why is it protected? Who found it? Are you famous?*

"My father found it," Kestrel told a girl with missing front teeth. "My friend finished finding it. And no. We're just thorough."

On the drive home the sky cleared hard and cold, and the stars came out the way they only do over country with no cities — ferociously, like the night was showing off. Xanthe navigated by them out of habit, calling turns before Kestrel made them, and somewhere past the second bridge Kestrel understood that this was what family was: not the people who shared your blood or your name, but the ones who knew your route well enough to call your turns.

She pulled the truck over, just for a minute, to look up.

"You okay?" Xanthe asked.

"Yeah." And for the first time in her life, checking, she found it was simply true. "Yeah. Just measuring."

"Measuring what?"

Kestrel looked at the stars, at the dark shapes of the ridgelines she could name in the dark, at the woman in the passenger seat wearing a dead woman's hat and holding a thermos like it was a shared heartbeat.

"Years," she said. "Turns out they're longer than miles."

Xanthe smiled and poured her a cup. "Told you. Cartographers know things."

The engine idled. Snow began again, fine and dry, ticking softly on the roof. Ahead of them the road ran home through country that was, at last and officially and permanently, itself — no claim staked on it, no secret folded inside it, nothing left to protect because it was protected, nothing left to hide because it was home.

Kestrel put the truck in gear.

They had a ridge line to fly in the morning.
