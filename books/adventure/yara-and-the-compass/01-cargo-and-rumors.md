## 1. Cargo and Rumors

The fog came off the river before dawn, the way it always did in the low country, thick as wool and smelling of silt and rotting reeds. Sable Winterbourne stood on the float of the plane and watched it swallow the settlement whole—first the drying racks, then the tin roofs, then the church steeple with its crooked cross, until there was nothing left of Miller's Reach but the sound of water lapping at the hulls of boats that hadn't moved in years.

Good. Fog was a friend. Fog meant the plane sat invisible on the river until the sun burned it off, and by then Sable would be gone.

The medicine run was the kind of work that kept a pilot fed without keeping a pilot noticed. Quinine and penicillin and insulin packed in waxed cardboard, a crate of sutures, two bottles of morphine for the mission nurse who signed for them with a hand that shook from something other than age. Nothing worth stealing. Nothing worth killing over. That was the rule, the first rule, the one that had kept Sable alive through ten years of flying the interior: *never carry anything worth stealing.*

The second rule was *never stay.* The third was *never ask.* Sable had learned all three the hard way, back when the rules had names attached to them—back when there had been a family, a farm in the valley called Ashgrove after the trees along the creek, and a magistrate named Aldous Renner whose signature had ended all of it. But that was a country Sable didn't fly over anymore, even in memory. Memory was just another kind of cargo, and it was worth far too much to steal.

Sable hauled the first crate up onto the dock planks, breath fogging, fingers stiff. The dock was old growth timber gone silver with weather, soft in places where the river had been eating at it for decades. A dog somewhere behind the fish sheds started barking, then thought better of it. Even the dogs in Miller's Reach knew not to make noise before they'd been fed.

"You're early." The voice came out of the fog, low and female and unsurprised. Nurse Odile Vasseur materialized the way she always did, as if she'd been standing on the dock since the night before, waiting. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a fence post, with hands that could set a bone or start an IV in the dark. She'd been the mission nurse for thirty years, which meant she'd outlasted three priests, two floods, and every attempt the territorial government had ever made to close her down.

"I like the light," Sable said, which wasn't true, but Odile knew that, and Odile knew why, and neither of them said so.

"Light's for people with nothing to hide." She took the crate from Sable's arms without being asked, hefted it, frowned at the weight. "You're short."

"Short?"

"Insulin. I ordered twelve vials. This is eight."

"The distributor shorted me. I'll make it up next run."

Odile's eyes were pale gray, the color of the river in November, and they missed nothing. "You paid for twelve."

"I know what I paid for."

"So you ate the difference rather than argue with a man in a warehouse four hundred miles away, because arguing means paperwork and paperwork means a name on a list." She said it without judgment, which was somehow worse. "Ten years, Winterbourne, and you still think nobody's noticed you."

"Nobody has."

"Mm." She set the crate down on a handcart already loaded with other crates—fish, salt, lamp oil, the ordinary commerce of a place that existed because the river allowed it. "Come inside. There's coffee. It's terrible."

"Coffee's against my religion."

"Your religion is paranoia. Coffee's a sacrament." She was already pushing the cart toward the mission, and Sable, because refusing would take more energy than complying, followed.

* * *

The mission house smelled of carbolic soap and woodsmoke and, underneath it, the sweetish smell of sickness that never quite left a building where people came to die or be mended. Odile poured coffee into enamel mugs—terrible, as promised, burnt and bitter enough to strip paint—and sat down across the table from Sable with the particular patience of a woman who had watched men die slowly and saw no reason to hurry a living one.

"Rumors," she said.

"I don't deal in rumors."

"No. You deal in medicine and silence. Which is why I'm telling you this, because you'll want to hear it from someone who isn't selling anything." She wrapped both hands around her mug. "There's a salvage fleet moving upriver. Three boats, maybe four. Big ones, steel-hulled, with cranes. They stopped at Fort Dessa two days ago, bought every drum of diesel the factor had, and asked questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The bad kind. Who flies the interior. Who runs cargo nobody asks about. Whether anyone's seen a prospector named Ede Voss come down out of the high country lately." Odile took a sip of her terrible coffee. "They're looking for someone, Winterbourne. And they're paying for answers."

Sable kept their face still. It was a skill learned young, in a household where a raised eyebrow could be reported, and it served now: nothing moved above the collar except a slow blink. Ede Voss. The name meant nothing—or meant nothing Sable was willing to look at directly. Prospectors came and went in the interior like weather. Half of them were chasing gold that wasn't there, and the other half were chasing something worse, and the ones who found what they were looking for usually ended up dead or rich or both.

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you fly alone, you land where there are no strips, and you don't ask questions—which makes you exactly the kind of person those questions get pointed at." Odile leaned forward. "Also because old Tomas Ferro came through last week from the upper settlements, and he says Voss passed through Kestrel Bay a month ago asking about tide tables. Extreme lows. The kind that come twice a year, if that."

"Tide tables." Sable turned the mug in their hands. "A prospector asking about tides."

"A prospector asking about tides is a prospector who found something under water." Odile shrugged. "Or something he thinks is under water. Either way, someone with money believes him, and money with cranes attached is coming up the river to collect."

Outside, the fog was thinning. Light came through the mission windows in long gray bars, and dust motes hung in them like things suspended between decisions. Somewhere down the street a door banged; a child shouted; the ordinary machinery of the morning resumed. Sable listened to it the way other people listened to music—for the false notes.

"How long have we known each other?" Odile asked suddenly.

"Eight years. Nine."

"In nine years you've never once told me your first name. You've never told me where you learned to fly, or why a person who handles a plane like an artist won't so much as look at the coastal towns. You pay cash, you land at dawn, you're gone before noon, and if I mention the word 'magistrate' your face does that thing it's doing right now, which is nothing at all, which is how I know." She set the mug down. "I'm not asking you to break your rules, Winterbourne. I'm telling you the rules might not matter anymore. Fleets don't care about quiet people. Fleets grind."

"Fleets need pilots."

"Some do." Her eyes narrowed. "Some are hiring."

"I'm not for hire."

"Everyone's for hire. The price is just different." She stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and began gathering the empty mugs. "Go on, then. Deliver whatever it is you deliver and vanish, like always. But Winterbourne—" she paused at the stove, back turned, "—if a dying man ever asks you for something, think hard before you say no. Some cargoes choose their pilots."

"That's superstition."

"That's experience." She glanced over her shoulder. "And here's another piece of free advice: the fleet's lead boat is called the *Renner's Fortune*. Ask yourself who names a ship that, and what they think they own."

The name went through Sable like cold water through a cracked hull. Renner. Aldous Renner. Magistrate of the coastal district, keeper of ledgers, signer of orders, the man whose face had been printed on warrants and proclamations and, once, on a notice nailed to the gate of a farm in a valley full of ash trees—the notice that said the land was seized for failure of title, that the family had thirty days, that appeal was futile. Thirty days. Sable had counted every one of them, and on the thirtieth day there had been fire, and after the fire there had been nothing but a name changed and a river chosen and a life spent learning how to be no one.

"Odile," Sable said carefully, "why would a salvage fleet name its flagship after a magistrate?"

"Because magistrates grant salvage rights. And salvage rights, upriver, are worth more than the salvage." She rinsed the mugs, unhurried. "Word is Renner himself holds the concession for the whole middle reach. Everything pulled out of the water between the falls and the delta belongs to him, legally. Which means everything anyone else pulls out belongs to him illegally, and illegal salvage gets people hanged." She dried her hands. "So. Routine run. Nothing worth stealing. Go home, wherever home is, and stay clear of the water."

* * *

By the time Sable walked back down to the dock, the fog had burned to rags and the sun was doing its blunt, honest work on the river. The settlement had woken around Sable while they drank Odile's terrible coffee: smoke from cook fires, the smell of frying fish, kids running barefoot along the plank walkways. A woman mended a net outside her house, fingers moving without her attention, eyes following the stranger in the flight jacket. Not hostile. Just noting. In a place like Miller's Reach, strangers were weather—you endured them, you didn't invite them in.

Sable checked the plane out of habit. Tie-downs, struts, the fuel line, the little brass drain cup held up to the light to check for water in the gas. Ten years of habit, ten years of the same small liturgy performed at dawn in a dozen river towns, and the ritual itself was the only home Sable had. The Cessna—old, patched, repainted so many times its original color was a matter of archaeological speculation—sat on its floats like a tired bird that had learned not to sing.

That was when Sable saw the man on the dock.

He was sitting on a mooring post with the ease of someone who'd been there a while, smoking a cigarette down to the filter. River clothes, but too clean—boots that had never known mud, a jacket with the creases still in it from a store shelf. His hair was oiled flat. He had the build of a man who lifted crates for a living and the posture of one who supervised.

"Winterbourne?" he said.

Every instinct Sable owned stood up at once. "Who's asking?"

"Name's Pell. I'm with the salvage operation coming upriver." He smiled with professional warmth. "Heard you're the best bush pilot on this stretch. We're hiring. Short-term contract, good money, hazard rates. All you'd do is ferry survey gear between our boats and the shore camps."

"Not interested."

"You haven't heard the number."

"Don't need to." Sable stepped past him onto the dock, putting the plane between them, hands busy with the tie-down ropes—something to hold, something to do that wasn't breaking his nose. "I fly medicine. That's my trade."

"Medicine doesn't pay." Pell flicked the cigarette into the water and stood, unhurried, big hands loose at his sides. He wasn't threatening, exactly. He was worse: patient. "Look, friend, everybody's got a history. Everybody's got a reason to keep their head down. But heads come up eventually. Might as well come up for money." He reached into his jacket, and Sable's hand moved—not quite to a hip, because Sable carried no gun, but to the space where a gun would be if the world were a fairer place—and Pell grinned and produced instead a card, heavy stock, embossed. He tucked it under a rope coil on the dock. "When you change your mind. Or when you want to sell information. We buy that too. Especially about prospectors."

"We've never met," Sable said. "You've got the wrong pilot."

"Sure." Pell walked away down the dock, easy as Sunday, and paused once to look back. "Oh—one thing. Our boss is very thorough. Buys up debts, deeds, old court records. You'd be surprised what turns up in old court records." He touched two fingers to his hat brim. "Fly safe, *friend*."

Then he was gone into the morning crowd, and Sable stood on the dock with the river slapping the floats and the card lying under the rope coil like something venomous.

Old court records. A farm called Ashgrove. A signature in green ink.

Sable picked up the card. Heavy cream stock, embossed crest—a crane lifting a chest from stylized waves—and beneath it, in engraved letters: *CORVANE SALVAGE & RECOVERY — BRANN CORVANE, MASTER.* On the back, handwritten in pencil, a single line:

*Ede Voss. Seen him? Worth 500 to the right answer.*

Sable looked at the name a long moment. Ede Voss. Tide tables. Extreme lows. A man asking questions about the sea's calendar in a country of rivers, which meant a man who'd found something the sea was keeping for him.

Burn the card. That was the sensible move. Drop it in the river, fire up the plane, climb to altitude, and put Miller's Reach and its rumors behind—rule one, rule two, rule three, the whole catechism of invisibility recited in order.

Sable folded the card instead and put it in the jacket pocket, over the heart, where important and stupid things were kept.

The engine caught on the second try, coughed, settled into its familiar ragged hum. Sable ran up the throttle, checked the mags, watched the river for traffic. Odile stood at the end of the dock with her handcart, watching, and as the plane swung out into the current she raised one hand—not waving, exactly. More like marking a debt.

Sable lifted off into the bright morning, wheels of mist peeling off the floats, and banked north up the river channel. Below, the water unspooled brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea: logs, leaves, secrets. Somewhere ahead, three steel ships with cranes were crawling upstream, buying diesel and answers, dragging a magistrate's name behind them like a net.

Never carry anything worth stealing. Never stay. Never ask.

Three rules, ten years, one fragile peace—and Sable flew north anyway, because the current below had shifted, because a name had surfaced that should have stayed drowned, and because some part of the pilot that had spent a decade practicing invisibility understood, with cold certainty, that the river had just changed its mind about letting Sable pass unnoticed.

Two hundred miles north, at a fogbound strip Sable had landed at a hundred times and never once remembered fondly, something was waiting. Sable didn't know what yet. Only that the morning felt like the held breath before a stall warning—and that the fuel gauge, the weather, and the rumors all pointed the same direction, whether Sable liked it or not.

North. Into the fog.
