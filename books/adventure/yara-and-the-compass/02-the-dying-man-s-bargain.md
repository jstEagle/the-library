## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The fog came off the river at dusk the way it always did in the low country—thick as wool, patient as debt—and by the time I set the Cub down on Voss's strip it had swallowed everything past fifty feet. I flew on instruments of habit: a memory of where the trees leaned away from the clearing, a count of seconds between the last bend of the river and the moment the wheels should kiss gravel. Old strips talk to you if you've landed on them enough. This one talked in a voice I half-remembered from three years back, when I'd hauled a crate of rifle cartridges and a sack of flour to a prospector who paid in gold dust and never once asked my name.

I liked him for that.

The tires found the ground with a soft double thump, and the fog closed over the wings like water over a stone. I rolled out slow, tail-low, and killed the engine because there was no sense announcing myself to a country that didn't need to know. Silence came rushing back in—the drip of condensation off the spruce boughs, the far complaint of a loon somewhere out on the black water, and underneath it all, faint and wrong, a sound I'd heard before and hoped never to hear again.

A man groaning. Low, rhythmic, the sound of someone trying very hard not to scream and losing the argument one breath at a time.

I sat with my hands on the yoke for a long moment. Every rule I had told me to turn the mags back on and lift into that fog and be gone before whatever was bleeding out there in the dark could become my problem. Rule one: never stay. Rule two: never ask. Rule three, the one that kept me alive while better pilots than me fed the river: never carry anything worth stealing.

But the medicine run was done. The quinine and penicillin were delivered to Miller's Landing that morning, and what remained in the cargo bay was a coil of rope, two tins of kerosene, and a canvas mail bag with nothing in it but newspapers three weeks old. Nothing worth stealing. Which meant, by the cold arithmetic of my life, that whoever was making that sound had nothing to take from me except time.

And time, God help me, was the one thing I'd never learned to ration properly.

I took the flashlight and the first-aid kit—sturdy, dented, more habit than hope—and walked up the strip toward the shape of Voss's cabin, which resolved out of the fog all at once, close enough to touch, the way things do in fog. A lantern burned behind the one window, turned so low it was barely a rumor of light. The door stood open an inch. In a country like this, an open door means one of two things: nobody home, or somebody who can't get up to close it.

It was the second kind.

He was on the floor beside his own bunk, propped against the frame like a man who'd tried twice to climb into bed and been defeated both times. Big frame, gone hollow. Grey beard shot through with old gold dust that never washed out, glittering faintly when the lantern flame breathed. His hands were pressed flat against his belly, and between his fingers, dark and glistening, was the whole story. Gut-shot. Somebody had known what they were doing—or at least where to aim.

"Voss," I said. "Ede Voss."

His eyes found me, and there was a lucidity in them that frightened me more than the blood did. Dying men are usually somewhere else, halfway down the long tunnel. He was right here, right now, taking inventory of me with the same flat attention he'd probably given ore samples for forty years.

"You're the pilot," he said. Voice like gravel poured slow. "The one who doesn't talk."

"I talk." I knelt, already opening the kit, already knowing what I'd find and what I wouldn't. No surgeon within two hundred miles. No radio on this strip—he'd let his own set rust to pieces years ago, another man practicing invisibility, though his reasons and mine were different. "Who did this?"

A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could bleed. "Does it matter? You going to fly back and arrest them?" He coughed, wet and deep, and I watched the effort cost him something he couldn't afford. "You going to testify?"

No. I wasn't going to do either of those things, and he knew it, and I knew he knew it, and there was a strange comfort in that honesty. The interior runs on transactions, not promises. I peeled his hands away from the wound—gently; he was still strong enough that it mattered—and looked at what was under them, and then I looked away, because there is a limit to what professional detachment can survive, and I'd found mine.

"How long ago?" I asked.

"Yesterday. Maybe longer. Fog's got no hours in it." His breath whistled on the intake. "You got morphine in that kit?"

"A little."

"Then we can do business."

* * *

Here is what I want to be clear about, because the harbor gossips later made it into a story about heroism, and heroism was never once in the room: I gave him the morphine because leaving a man to die in pain is a cruelty I couldn't stomach, and I stayed because the alternative was flying away and hearing that groan every night for the rest of my life. That's all it was. Compassion by subtraction. Whatever else grew out of that night grew out of me like a weed grows through a sidewalk—not intended, not welcomed, just inevitable given a crack and rain.

I made him comfortable against the bunk with a folded blanket under his head. I boiled water on his stove and cleaned the wound as best as cleaning could do anything, which was nothing, which we both understood. The bullet had gone in below the ribs and hadn't come out. Somewhere in the dark of him it was doing its slow work, and the morphine only bought silence from the pain, not pardon from the outcome.

While I worked, he talked. Not about the shooting—that door he kept shut, and I didn't knock—but about other things. About the river in spring, how it rises brown and insane and carries whole trees like matchsticks. About a dog he'd had, years ago, a yellow bitch named Sorrel who could smell gold-bearing quartz through six feet of gravel, or so he claimed, and I let him claim it. Talking was the morphine he gave himself between doses. I have seen men pray like that. I have seen men do worse.

Toward midnight the fog thickened until the lantern's glow seemed to end a foot from the glass, and the cabin felt like the last lit room in the world. He asked for water, and I gave it, holding the tin cup steady while he drank in small sips, and when he finished he lay back and studied the ceiling beams as if reading something written there.

"I have a daughter," he said.

I said nothing. Rule two. But rules are for men whose hands aren't still wet.

"Yara." He said the name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Kestrel Bay. Boatwright. Builds hulls fairer than any man on the coast, and she learned none of it from me, because I wasn't there to teach her." A pause. The loon called again, farther off, or maybe closer—fog plays tricks with sound, makes the world feel held inside a bell. "She'll tell you I was never there at all. She'd be right."

"Ede—"

"She thinks I chose the river over her." His eyes came off the ceiling and fixed on me, and they were terribly clear, terribly present, a dying man spending his last clarity like a miser's final coin. "And she's wrong, but being wrong has kept her safe, and safe is worth more than right. You understand that. Don't you. You're a man who understands the price of being known."

I didn't answer. My face answered for me, apparently, because something like satisfaction moved through his expression, brief as a fish turning under water.

"There's a package," he said. "Under the third floorboard from the stove. Left side. Bring it here, and mind you bring it whole, because half of what's in it won't survive being torn."

I should have refused. I want that on the record too, wherever records go. Every instinct I owned stood up and voted no. Taking cargo from a dying man binds you; taking *promised* cargo binds you twice, once to the dead and once to the living, and I had spent fifteen years ensuring that no one living or dead held a claim on me. Anonymity isn't cowardice. It's a craft. It takes discipline, the way flying in weather takes discipline—you commit to nothing visible, you file no plans anyone can read, and you live.

But he was watching me with those clear grey eyes, and under us the river moved in the dark toward a sea I couldn't see, and I thought: refusing means walking out that door and letting him die alone in a room that smells of blood and kerosene, with the fog pressing in and the loon calling, and no voice but his own to hear the last of what he has to say.

Some cargoes you don't choose. They choose the hour, and you're simply the thing standing nearest when it does.

I pried up the floorboard.

* * *

The oilskin wrapping was old, cracked at the folds, tied with waxed cord that had been knotted and reknotted so many times the knot had grown a history. Inside were three things: a chart, folded small, hand-inked on backing cloth; a journal bound in oiled leather, swollen with damp years; and a brass compass, heavy as a heart, its glass scratched nearly blind but its needle swinging true the moment I held it level. It swung, hesitated, and settled north with the confidence of a thing that had never once doubted itself.

I envied it.

"The chart's half a chart," Voss said, when I laid it on the blanket across his knees. "Don't waste your breath asking for the other half. I don't have it. Never did. It belongs to—" and there he stopped, and his jaw worked, and I watched a man weigh whether to spend his last words on hatred or on caution, and caution won, the way it usually does in this country. "It belongs to someone who'll come looking. Soon. Maybe before the fog lifts. That's why you leave tonight."

"I'm not leaving tonight. Fog like this will put me in the trees."

"Then dawn. First grey. Promise me."

"I haven't promised anything yet."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But you're still holding the compass."

I looked down at my hands. He was right. At some point during the unwrapping I had taken the compass without deciding to, the way you catch something falling before your mind gets involved. Its needle trembled against the glass, pointing at a north that contained Kestrel Bay, and a daughter, and whatever truth was sewn into these oilskins like shot in a duck.

"What am I carrying, Ede?"

"A map to a wreck that was never supposed to be found." His breathing had gone shallower; the morphine was losing its argument with the bullet. "The *Meridian*. Forty years down. And under her salvage, in the captain's strongroom, proof of what a certain man did to get his fleet, his magistrate's chair, and his clean white name." His hand crept over mine on the compass, and his grip still had iron in it, a prospector's grip, a man who'd pulled himself up cliffs by fingers alone. "Renner's people shot me for less than what's in this wrapping. They'll shoot you for carrying it. I want you to know that going in. I'm not buying your help with a lie."

"That's your sales pitch. *They'll shoot you.*"

"My pitch," he said, and the ghost of humor crossed his ruined face, "is that they'll shoot you anyway if they find out you saw me. You're already in it, pilot. The only question left is whether you're in it uselessly."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the fog lay on the strip so thick the plane might have been a dream I'd have trouble proving tomorrow. I thought about the salvage fleet rumored at Miller's Landing—three days ago, over bad coffee, the trader Wicks had mentioned it with that particular casualness liars use, *some outfit moving gear upriver, big money, none of our business*—and I felt the pieces begin, reluctantly, to touch each other. A fleet. A wreck. A magistrate's clean white name. A gut-shot prospector who'd let his radio rust on purpose.

"You knew they'd come for you," I said. "You stayed here anyway. Waiting for me."

"Waiting for the mail plane. You're the only one flies this stretch regular." His eyes were glazing now, the clarity ebbing like a tide going out. "Knew I had one delivery left in me. One promise left to make." His grip tightened. "Not the map. Anyone can carry a map. I need you to promise the rest. Yara. Kestrel Bay. You find her, you put this in her hands, and you say—" his breath caught, and he rode through it, jaw clenched, furious at his own body for failing him at the one moment that mattered— "you say her father sent it, and that he was thinking of her at the end. Not the river. Her. Tell her the river was just the road. Tell her—"

He stopped. Not because he wanted to. Because the sentence had used up more of him than he had left.

I sat there on the floor of a dying man's cabin with a compass in my fist and a hole in my quiet life exactly the size of a promise, and I understood that I was going to say yes, and I hated it, and I said it anyway.

"All right," I heard myself say. "Yara. Kestrel Bay. Your words, word for word as I can hold them. I promise."

Something went out of him then—not life, not yet, but fear, the particular fear of a man who dies with unfinished business. He sagged back against the bunk and his hand slid off mine and lay open on the blanket, palm up, empty at last.

"Third board," he murmured. "Left of the stove. You checked. Good man." A long breath. "Dawn, pilot. First grey. Fog burns off fast after sunrise on the river, and I'd take it kind if you were gone before I stop needing you."

Meaning: don't watch me die. Meaning: let me keep the last of my pride, the way I kept you from asking questions, the way I kept her safe by staying away. Everything this man had ever loved, he'd protected by absence. It was the saddest arithmetic I'd ever witnessed, and I recognized it completely, because it was mine.

I gave him the last of the morphine—not enough to stop the dying, just enough to soften it—and I sat in the corner with the oilskin bundle on my knees and the compass in my hand, and I kept him company in silence while the fog breathed against the windows and the candle burned down toward its plate. He drifted. Sometimes he spoke—a name, once, *Yara*, soft as snowfall; once, sharper, *not the strongroom, the space beneath it*—and sometimes he only breathed, each breath a little shorter than the last, a tide going out by inches.

Near four in the morning, by the cheap tin watch I keep because it doesn't matter if it's lost, the breathing stopped having gaps in it, and then stopped altogether, gently, between one drip of fog off the eaves and the next.

I closed his eyes. I covered his face with the blanket from the bunk, because a man shouldn't lie open to a room, even an empty one. Then I pried up the third floorboard one more time to make sure I'd taken everything, and found beneath it a scrap of paper in a crabbed hand, addressed in pencil: *For my daughter. She'll know the hand.*

As if I needed telling. As if the whole night hadn't been one long telling.

Outside, the east had gone the color of pewter, and the fog was thinning at the top the way it does before sunrise gets serious. I loaded the bundle into the cargo bay, lashed it down, and ran up the engine in the grey half-light. The Cub lifted off Voss's strip for the last time with nobody watching, which was how he would have wanted it, and I banked south toward the river and pointed the nose at a north I'd never flown before.

Kestrel Bay was six hours' flight, weather willing. Behind me, the fog was already burning off the strip, erasing the cabin, erasing the evidence, doing its work the way fog always does.

In my breast pocket, over my heartbeat, the compass ticked softly against my ribs with every bump of air, keeping its stubborn faith in north—and somewhere ahead of me, in a boatyard I couldn't picture, a woman was about to learn that her father had died thinking of her, and that he'd sent a stranger to say so, because strangers are the only couriers a secret trusts.

I just hoped she'd believe me. Dead men make poor witnesses, and I'd never once in my life been able to prove I was telling the truth.

The river unrolled below me, brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea.
