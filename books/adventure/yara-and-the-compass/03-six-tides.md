## 3. Six Tides

The tide went out of Kestrel Bay the way it went out of everything—slowly, and taking more than it promised to return.

Yara Voss knelt in the sawdust of her shed with a adze in her hands and a plank across her trestles, and she did not look up when the harbor bell rang the half hour, and she did not look up when the gulls argued over the fish guts behind the cannery, and she did not look up when the door of the shed scraped open on its swollen hinges and a stranger stood in the gray light with rain beading on an oilskin coat.

She had learned, in the eleven years since her father's boat had cleared the breakwater without him aboard it, that looking up was how you got hurt.

"You're Yara," the stranger said. A woman's voice, low and roughened, like a rope dragged through a block one too many times. "Yara Voss."

"Shed's closed," Yara said to the plank. The adze bit, curled a ribbon of cedar, spat it onto the floor. The plank was for a dory she was building on commission for a fisherman who paid late but paid, and the work was the only conversation she trusted anymore. Wood didn't leave. Wood just waited, if you were patient, if you kept your hands moving, for you to make it into what it needed to be.

"He's dead."

The adze stopped. Not because she meant it to. Her hands simply forgot, all at once, everything they knew.

The ribbon of cedar curled and broke and fell.

"Who's dead," she said, and her voice came out flat as a caulking iron, and she hated it, hated that even now, even hearing the word *dead*, she could not make her voice sound like a daughter's.

"Ede Voss. Shot through the belly at Miller's Landing, two nights back. He lived long enough to give me something and make me promise." The stranger crossed the shed, and her boots left wet prints on the sawdust, and Yara tracked them the way you track a snake in grass. "I'm Sable. I fly the river. He flagged me down on a fogbound strip with his guts leaking out of him, and he asked me to bring you this."

Something hit the trestles beside the plank with a soft, dense weight. Oilskin-wrapped. The size of a prayer book. Tied with waxed cord that had gone gray with age.

Yara looked at it the way you look at a spider on your own arm.

"I don't want it."

"I figured." Sable—just the one name, no family attached to it, the way river people named themselves when they had reasons—leaned against the doorframe and made no move to leave. Rain dripped off her collar. "He said you'd say that. He said, *she'll say she doesn't want it, tell her it isn't for her, it's for the bay.*" A pause. "I don't know what he meant. I didn't ask. I never ask."

"Well, don't start now." Yara picked up the adze. Her hands had come back to her, but they were shaking, and the first stroke went wide and gouged the plank deep, a wound in the wood she'd have to fair out later, and she stared at it and felt something in her chest crack along a seam she'd spent eleven years caulking. "Get out of my shed."

"He died at dawn," Sable said. "He talked about you. Not much. He didn't have much left to talk with. But he said—" and here the woman's voice did something Yara had not expected, something almost gentle, almost apologetic, "—he said your hands were better than his ever were. Said you could build a boat that would outlive the both of you."

The adze came down again and again and again, and the cedar flew, and Yara was not shaping a plank anymore, she was shaping her fury into something with edges, and when she finally stopped, gasping, the plank was ruined and the stranger was still there, patient as tide, and the oilskin bundle sat on the trestles like a coal that wouldn't cool.

"Eleven years," Yara said. "Eleven years, and not one letter. Not one coin sent home. My mother died waiting for him, did he tell you that? Died in this bay, in a winter he could have prevented with one crate of medicine, one sack of flour, one *letter*—and you stand there and tell me his last words were about my *hands*?"

"No," Sable said. "His last words were about a tide."

* * *

They sat across from each other at the workbench because Yara could not bring herself to offer the stranger a chair and could not bring herself to remain standing either, and between them the oilskin bundle lay unrolled, and Yara's whole body was a held breath.

Inside was a chart. Hand-drawn, on paper gone amber and brittle, the ink brown as old blood. It showed the coast north of Kestrel Bay—a coastline Yara knew the way she knew her own knuckles, every bight and bar and shingle—and it showed a sound that was not on any chart she'd ever seen, a deep notch in the land maybe forty miles north, unnamed, unmarked, with a single word inked across its mouth in her father's cramped, slanted hand:

*MERIDIAN.*

And around the edges of the chart, in the margins, in the corners, crammed between the soundings like secrets between floorboards, were numbers. Dozens of them. Rows of them. Each with a small symbol beside it—a crescent, a dot, a cross-hatched line.

"What is this," Yara said. Not a question. An accusation.

"That's what I was hoping you'd know."

"I haven't spoken to him in eleven years. I don't know what he had for breakfast in 1849, let alone what he—" She stopped. Her eyes had caught on something. Down in the lower corner of the chart, beneath the soundings, a line of writing in a steadier hand than the rest, as if he'd written it last, when he'd had time to think:

*She reads water. She'll know.*

Yara read it three times. The shed seemed to tilt around her. *She reads water.* He had left when she was fourteen, and she had spent the summers of her childhood in his shop, and he had taught her to read a tide table before she could read scripture, and she had thought—she had *told herself*—that none of it had stayed, that she had burned it all out of herself like rot out of a plank.

*She reads water. She'll know.*

"He knew me," she said quietly, and it came out like grief, because it was.

"Read it, then," Sable said. "I've stared at this thing for two days and it's just numbers."

Yara pulled the lamp closer. Outside, the rain had thickened, and the harbor was a smear of lights, and somewhere a dog was barking at the weather, and she made herself look at the numbers the way she'd look at a hull that had come in damaged—without flinching, without feeling, just reading what the water had written.

The numbers were heights. She saw it in a moment. Feet above chart datum, the way a tide table spoke. And the symbols—

The crescents were the moon. The dots were the days. And the cross-hatched lines—

Her breath went out of her slowly.

"These aren't random. He's marked tides. Specific tides." Her finger moved along the rows. "Look. Every number's a low-water height, and every one with the cross-hatch is lower than the one before. He's counting *down*. He's marked a series of low waters, each one lower than the last, and the last one—" her finger stopped at the bottom of the column, at a number so low it would strip the kelp off the rocks, "—the last one is an extreme. A spring low. The kind that comes maybe twice a year, when the moon and the sun pull together and the bay empties itself like a wound."

"Surfaces," Sable said.

Yara looked up. "What?"

"Before he died. He said, *it only surfaces at the bottom of the world's breath.* I thought he was delirious." Sable leaned over the chart, and Yara caught her scent—engine oil and woodsmoke and something under it, something like fear kept too long in a small room. "He was talking about the wreck. The Meridian. Whatever's down in that sound only breaks the surface at extreme low water. And this—" she tapped the column of numbers, "—this is the countdown to the next one."

Yara did the arithmetic without wanting to. It was in her blood, the moon's arithmetic; her father had put it there before she was old enough to refuse the inheritance. The cross-hatched lows, the sequence of them, the spacing of the dots for the days.

"Six," she said. "Six tides. The extreme low is six tides from now. A little over three days." She sat back, and the workbench was cold under her palms, and she heard her own voice as if from across the harbor: "He was counting. Right up until they shot him, he was counting."

"They?"

"You said shot. Prospector, upriver, alone, with a chart like that." Yara's finger came down on the word *MERIDIAN* and pressed until the nail went white. "Men don't get shot by accident in the interior, not when they're carrying something like this. Somebody wanted what he had and didn't want to buy it." She looked up, and found Sable watching her with an expression Yara recognized because she wore it herself in the mirror—wariness worn so long it had become the face. "Who else knows about this chart?"

"I don't know. He said—" Sable's jaw worked. "He said it was half of a pair. That the other half was already in other hands. He said I'd know what that meant when I met the fleet."

"What fleet?"

"Savage rumor in the last settlement. Salvage fleet moving upriver. Steam launch, barges, a lot of men with more guns than gear." Sable straightened. "I didn't think much of it then. I think a lot of it now."

Yara looked down at the chart, at her father's cramped hand, at *she reads water, she'll know*, and the grief that had been sitting in her chest like ballast for eleven years did something it had never done before.

It turned over. It caught. It became something with an engine in it.

He had left her. He had left her and let her mother die and never sent a word, and she had built a whole life out of that abandonment the way she built a hull—plank by plank, tight and sealed and seaworthy, and now one line in a dead man's ink had sprung every seam she owned. But underneath the grief, underneath the fury, cold and clear as the water under ice, was this: someone had shot her father for this chart. Someone was coming upriver for whatever the Meridian held. And the only two people in the world who knew what the chart said were sitting in a sawdust-floored shed in Kestrel Bay with three days and six tides in front of them.

"He wanted me to have it," she said. "All these years I told myself he forgot me. He didn't forget me. He was *keeping* me. Keeping me off this chart, away from this—" she gestured at the sound, at the word *MERIDIAN*, at everything it implied, "—like you'd keep a match away from powder. And then he ran out of time, and he sent it to me anyway, because there was no one else."

"You don't know that," Sable said. But her voice had no conviction in it. She was reading the chart again, and her finger had stopped on the sound, and her face had gone the color of the paper.

"What?"

"Nothing. A name. It's nothing." But it wasn't nothing; Yara had grown up reading faces the way she read water, and Sable's face had a ripsurface current under it, something pulling hard. "Six tides," Sable said instead. "That's not long. And if the fleet has the other half of this chart, they know the sound too. Maybe they know the tides. Maybe they're already north of here."

"Then we'd better not be here when they arrive."

"*We*?" Sable's eyebrows went up. "I made a promise to a dying man. The promise was *deliver the map*. It's delivered. I fly a plane, and my plane's at anchor in your harbor, and I was leaving on the morning tide before you ever—"

"Your plane's at anchor where?"

"Harbor moorings. North float."

Yara was already moving to the one small window of the shed, the one that looked out over the water, and the rain, and the gray smear of the harbor. She looked north, to the mooring buoys.

"Then you'd better hope it's still there," she said, "because a salvage fleet doesn't send its steam launch into a harbor town without somebody walking the docks first, and there were men on the north floats an hour ago. I saw them from here. I thought they were inspectors." She turned back. "I thought they were *Renner's inspectors*."

The name landed on Sable like a dropped anchor. Yara watched it happen—watched the woman go very still, the way water goes still over a thing that's sunk.

"Say that name again," Sable said.

"Renner. Magistrate Renner. His inspectors audit the harbor every few months. Paperwork, mooring fees, cargo manifests. Everyone hates it, everyone complies." Yara frowned. "Why? What does the magistrate of the coast have to do with—"

She stopped. Because Sable had pushed off from the workbench and was moving toward the door, and the look on her face was not the look of a courier finishing a delivery. It was the look of a woman who had just heard the name of an old debt called in.

"Get your things," Sable said. "Whatever boat you have, whatever you're building, whatever floats. Six tides, Yara. And I think we just found out who shot your father."

* * *

The rain came down harder as they crossed the shingle toward the north floats, and the harbor lights bent and smeared in it, and Yara ran with the chart pressed inside her coat against her heart like a coal she could not put down and could not let burn her.

She did not know yet what she was going to build out of this—what the next six tides would make of her, of the stranger beside her, of the half-finished hull sleeping under canvas in her shed that she had been shaping for two years without ever quite knowing for whom.

She only knew that her father had said *she reads water*, and she did, and the water was rising.

And somewhere out in the dark of the harbor, on the north floats, men were moving toward a floatplane with lanterns and kerosene, and the tide of everything was turning, and Yara Voss ran into the rain with grief in her chest and a heading she had not chosen but could not refuse.

Six tides. The bay was already emptying.
