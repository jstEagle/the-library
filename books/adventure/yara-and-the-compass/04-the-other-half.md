## 4. The Other Half

Kestrel Bay smelled of tar and herring and the particular rot of a harbor that had been worked too long by too few hands. Sable stood at the top of the ramp with the oilskin roll pressed flat against their ribs, feeling it there like a second heartbeat, and watched the fishing boats knock against each other in the swell. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings. Somewhere behind the fish sheds, a winch groaned through its gears.

Three days since the fogbound strip. Three days since Ede Voss had died with his hand still gripping Sable's sleeve, as if letting go would let go of everything else—the map, the daughter, the promise. Sable had buried him under stones at the tree line because the ground was too hard for anything deeper, and had said words over him that weren't prayers, because Sable didn't pray, but were at least true: *You kept your heading. I'll keep mine.*

Now the heading was a girl named Yara Voss who worked, according to the harbormaster's bored directions, at the boatyard past the third slip, and who was going to receive her father's last possession from a stranger's hands, and Sable already dreaded every version of how that might go.

But first—first there was the fleet.

Sable had seen it from the air on the approach, before the clouds closed in: four vessels riding at anchor in the outer roads, gray-hulled, working-ugly, with cranes folded against their decks like sleeping insects. Salvage ships didn't come this far up the coast without reason. The interior had no wrecks worth a fleet's fuel, unless you counted the ones men made deliberately. And one of those, Sable knew now, was supposed to stay lost.

The tavern was called the Drowned Lantern, which was either a joke or an apology, and it stood on pilings over the water with a lean to starboard that suggested it had made its peace with sinking. Inside it was dim and warm and loud with the midday crowd—deckhands, netmenders, two customs men playing cards in the corner with the studied idleness of men being paid to look like they weren't watching anyone.

Sable took a stool at the end of the bar, ordered stew and whatever passed for beer this far upriver, and did what Sable always did in new places: listened.

Listening was a craft, same as flying. You learned which conversations were private and which were performances, which silences meant secrets and which meant nothing at all. In the Drowned Lantern, most of the talk was ordinary—prices, weather, a marriage gone wrong in vivid detail three stools down—but underneath it ran a current, the way it always does when money has come to town. Money changes the sound of a harbor. The laughter gets a little too bright. The silences get a little too careful.

"—fourth time they've sounded the channel," someone was saying, low, near the window. "Fourth. You don't sound a channel four times unless you're bringing in something with a deep keel."

"Or taking something out," his companion said.

"Nobody takes anything out of here worth that trouble."

Sable ate the stew, which tasted mostly of barley and salt, and turned the oilskin roll's presence against their ribs into a kind of discipline. Don't ask. Don't linger. Find the boatyard, deliver the thing, be gone before the tide turns. That was the whole shape of the day.

Then the door opened and the room changed.

It wasn't dramatic. Nobody stopped talking. But the pitch of it shifted, the way a river shifts around a new rock, and Sable understood that the man coming in was known, and known in a way that made people arrange themselves. He was big without being soft about it—a working bulk, shoulders built by cranes and capstans—and he wore a captain's coat that had been good once and was now good in the way that only expensive things are good after they've been beaten up. His hair was iron-gray, cropped short, and he moved through the tavern like a man walking his own deck, nodding here, acknowledging there, touching nobody.

"That's Corvane," murmured the man beside Sable, to his companion, not quietly enough. "Brann Corvane. Fleet master."

"The salvage fleet?"

"You seen another?"

Corvane bellied up to the bar two stools down and ordered without looking at the tapman, who clearly already knew. And Sable—Sable should have looked away then. Should have finished the beer and left by the back stairs over the water, the way invisibility is done. But Corvane began talking to the tapman in a voice pitched just low enough to seem private and just high enough to carry, and what he said pinned Sable to the stool like a nail through the shoulder.

"Tell your harbor master I'll want the survey charts again tonight," Corvane said. "The old ones. Pre-war soundings."

"Pre-war?" The tapman laughed. "Half this coast's moved since pre-war."

"I know where it's moved." Corvane drank. "I'm not looking for what's there. I'm looking for what isn't."

Sable kept their eyes on the bar's scarred wood and felt the cold come up through the floorboards.

*What isn't.* A wreck that surfaces only at extreme low water. A chart drawn by a man who'd spent twenty years pretending he'd never seen it. Ede Voss's notes, tide-marked, half-legible, describing a hole in the world that only opened six tides from now. If Corvane was sounding for absences—

He had the other half. He had to have the other half. One chart is a rumor; two charts make a destination, and fleets don't move on rumors.

Sable paid, left coins enough to buy forgetfulness, and went out onto the porch over the water to think, and that was where the second blow landed, because the customs men from the corner came out after Corvane, and they weren't customs men.

They were too well fed, first of all. Customs work pays in boredom and thin soup. These two had the sheen of men whose salaries came from somewhere softer, and when they spoke to Corvane on the dock below, they spoke *up* to him—not the way subordinates speak up, but the way partners do when the partnership is deniable.

"Magistrate Renner sends his regards," the taller one said. "And his question. When."

"When I have it, he has it." Corvane's voice carried off the water, flattened by distance but not enough. "Tell him the Meridian's not a story. Voss confirmed the position before he went inland. We're inside six tides."

"And the prospector himself?"

There was a pause long enough that Sable's stomach dropped through the pilings and into the bay.

"Inland," Corvane said finally. "Old men wander. Renner wants him found before he talks? He should have thought of that years ago."

The tall man smiled at something. Sable couldn't see it from above, only the tilt of his head, and heard him say, softly, almost fondly: "The magistrate thinks of everything. That's why he asked me to mention—" and here the wind took the rest of it, or the gulls did, or Sable's own pulse did, because the name had already done its work.

*Aldous Renner.*

Sable stood very still on the tavern porch while the harbor went on knocking and slopping beneath them, and twenty-two years fell away like fog burning off.

Everyone from the valley country knew the name. It wasn't a name so much as a weather system. Aldous Renner had come up from the capital with law books and a surveyor's chain and a smile that never reached the part of his face where decisions got made, and within five years he owned the water rights to three valleys and the deeds to most of the farms along them, all of it legal, all of it stamped, all of it done with signatures instead of soldiers because signatures were cheaper and left fewer questions. The Ashgrove holding had been the last to fall. The best land on the river bend, and the stubbornest family on it.

Sable had been young. Young enough to believe that papers could be fought with other papers, that if you were right loudly enough and long enough, rightness counted for something. Then the eviction notice came, and the marshals came behind it, and there was a night involving fire that Sable did not think about, had trained themselves not to think about, in the way you train yourself not to look down from height—except that pilots look down constantly, and every valley Sable ever flew over had a shape, and some shapes were a bend in a river that Sable's hands remembered better than their mind permitted.

The Ashgroves had scattered. Some names changed. One grave changed. Sable had spent a lifetime becoming no one in particular—no surname offered, no history told, a bush pilot with a rented hangar and a reputation for silence—and had believed, had *needed* to believe, that the past was a country you could emigrate from permanently.

And now here was its weather system, rolling up the coast after the same wreck, backed by the same patient machinery, hunting the same paper that sat against Sable's ribs.

This was never a coincidence. Sable understood that with a cold clarity that felt almost like relief, because relief was easier than fear. Ede Voss hadn't stumbled into that fogbound strip by accident any more than the fleet had anchored in these roads by accident. A dying man had chosen his courier the way you choose a lockpick—someone with wings, someone with no attachments, someone with reasons of their own to hate the magistrate. Maybe he'd even known. Old men in the interior knew more than they said. Maybe the gut shot itself had a signature Sable hadn't wanted to read.

Below, the meeting broke up. Corvane shook the taller man's hand—two fingers and a palm, a merchant's handshake, nothing warm in it—and walked back toward the fleet's tender, and the two false customs men strolled toward the harbor office, unhurried, untouchable, men whose violence was always somebody else's signature.

Sable stayed on the porch until their hands stopped shaking, which took longer than it should have, and used the time to do arithmetic.

Six tides. That was what Ede's notes said, and Corvane had just said it aloud: *inside six tides.* Two parties, one prize, and the other party had a fleet, a magistrate, and a matching chart. Sable had a seaplane, a promise, and a girl in a boatyard who didn't yet know her father was dead.

The math was bad. The math had always been bad, from the moment Sable had knelt in the fog and taken an old man's hand.

But there was one thing the fleet didn't have, and Sable touched the oilskin roll through their jacket as they pushed open the tavern door and headed for the boatyard, and that was the reason. Corvane was sailing for salvage. Renner was sailing to bury evidence. Only Sable was carrying a dead man's word, and a dead man's word, Sable was learning, weighed more than lead and steered truer than any compass.

The boatyard smelled of cedar shavings and hot pitch. At the far slip, hull-up on trestles, a woman was driving fastenings into a half-planked keel with a rhythm like a heartbeat, and Sable recognized her before anyone said her name—Ede's jaw, Ede's stubborn set of shoulder—and understood that the hardest landing of the day was still ahead.

* * *

Yara Voss drove the fastening home and reached for another before she saw the stranger standing at the edge of her slip, hat in hand, wearing the particular posture of someone delivering news that will ruin your afternoon.

She set down the mallet slowly.

"You're looking for me," she said. Not a question. Strangers didn't stand at her slip looking at her like that for any other reason.

"I am." The pilot's voice was rough, wind-cured, genderless in the way of voices that had been shouted at by weather for years. "My name is Sable. I flew supply runs out of the interior strips. Your father was one of my—"

"My father," Yara said, "is a letter twice a year and a lie twice a year, and I've stopped opening either." She wiped her palms on her apron, leaving cedar dust in the creases. "So whatever he sold you, take it up with him."

"He can't hear it." Sable held her gaze. "He's dead, Yara. Three days now. Buried under stones at the tree line past Miller's strip, because the ground was frozen and I couldn't dig deeper, and I'm sorry for that, and I'm sorry for all of it."

The mallet was still in Yara's hand. She looked at it as though she'd forgotten what it was for, and then at the stranger, and then out at the harbor—at the gray fleet riding the outer roads, though she didn't yet know why her eyes went there—and something in her face closed like a hatch against weather.

"What happened to him," she said, and it wasn't a question either, and Sable, who had promised a dying man honesty above comfort, began to answer.

Behind them both, unnoticed, a tender put off from the salvage flagship, rowing a slow circuit of the harbor, and a man in its bow sketched the moored boats in a little book—one of which, hull-up on trestles at the third slip, he marked with a small, careful X.
