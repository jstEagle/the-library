## 5. Stolen Runway

The fire woke me before the sound did.

That's the thing about a harbor at night—you learn its breathing. The slap of water against hulls, the groan of mooring lines, the far-off cough of a generator on some trawler too cheap to shut down for the dark. I'd been sleeping in the Kestrel's half-built hull with my boots on and my jacket rolled under my head, and somewhere in that shallow, listening sleep, the light behind my eyelids turned orange.

Then came the smell: aviation fuel burning, sweet and acrid, a smell I knew the way you know your own blood.

I was up the ladder and out onto the dock before Yara could call my name behind me.

* * *

She was burning beautifully. That's the obscene truth of it—my plane burned like she'd been built for it, like every hour I'd spent keeping her alive had only been seasoning the wood for this. The *Wren* sat at her mooring forty feet off the quay, and flame stood up out of her cabin windows in long orange banners that leaned east with the wind, and the whole harbor of Kestrel Bay had come out to watch, because a fire is the one thing that gets people out of bed faster than free liquor.

Nobody was fighting it. There was nothing to fight it *with*—no pump wagon in a town this size, no pressure worth the name—and by the time I reached the end of the dock, close enough to feel the heat press against my face like an open hand, the floats were already black below the waterline and the fabric on the fuselage had gone from white to brown to nothing. The engine cowling glowed dull cherry along its seams. Somewhere inside, the aluminum of the fuel tanks was softening, sagging, letting go.

I stood there and let it happen, because there was nothing else to do, and because running toward a burning floatplane is how you die twice—once in the fire and once in the papers as a fool.

"Sable." Yara's voice, low, at my shoulder. She'd come up from the boatyard with a fire hook she'd clearly grabbed on instinct, some old tool of her father's, and now she just held it like she didn't know what hands were for. "Sable, who—"

"Look at the mooring line," I said.

She looked. The line that should have held the *Wren* to her buoy had been cut clean and retied with a slipknot—a knot that would let the plane drift free once she burned through, so the wreckage would wash out on the ebb and take the evidence with it. Except whoever tied it hadn't counted on the wind swinging her back against the quay pilings, where she'd catch and stay, burning her whole life out ten feet from shore, in front of God and the entire town.

"They wanted it gone," Yara said. "Quiet."

"No." My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "They wanted it *seen*. Gone is what happens when they don't want a message delivered. This—" I nodded at the banners of flame, at the crowd murmuring on the quay, at old Marta Voss's grandkids staring open-mouthed from the fish shed roof. "This is a letter. We're the envelope."

The *Wren*'s wing spar finally gave. The whole port wing folded down into the water with a hiss like a thousand snakes, and steam rolled up white and enormous, and for one moment the fire was hidden behind it entirely, and I felt something in my chest lurch toward hope before the flames punched back through the steam, higher than before.

Twelve years I'd kept that plane flying. Twelve years of scavenged gaskets and hand-mixed dope and fabric patches sewn by lamplight in whatever shack passed for a hangar that month. Twelve years of never parking anywhere longer than a tide, of always knowing which way the wind lay, of being nobody, going nowhere anyone could predict, carrying nothing worth stealing.

I had broken all three rules in four days. And here was the invoice.

* * *

The crowd thinned as the fire burned down to structure—people can watch a thing die but they get restless watching the corpse—and by two in the morning it was just me and Yara and the harbor master's boy, who took one look at my face and found urgent business elsewhere. The *Wren* sat low in the water, a black skeleton with a few red coals still breathing in her ribs. The smell hung over everything: fuel, char, and underneath it that particular scorched-metal sweetness of an engine that will never turn again.

Yara sat on the dock edge, legs dangling over the black water, and didn't say anything for a long time. When she finally spoke, it wasn't comfort. It was better than comfort.

"The radio," she said. "Is it gone?"

I looked at her.

"You said last night you'd been meaning to rewire the set anyway. That the antenna lead was chafing through. Which means you'd have pulled the panels." She was staring at the wreck, working it through the same way she worked timber—finding the grain, following it. "Radios don't burn first. They're buried in the middle of everything else."

She was right. Of course she was right; she was a boatwright, and a boatwright knows that a vessel dies from the outside in, skin first, bones last, and whatever you've stowed deepest has the best odds of anything.

"I need a skiff," I said.

"There's my tender. It leaks."

"Everything leaks. That's what bilge pumps are for."

* * *

We went out in the dark with a lantern hooded down to a coin of light and a bail bucket between us, and I'll say this for the cold water of Kestrel Bay at three in the morning: it makes a person very clear about their priorities. The *Wren* floated low and dead, awash to her door sills, and getting aboard meant wading hip-deep through the flooded cabin while she rolled her slow death-roll under my feet. The water inside was warm near the top—from the fire—and freezing at the knees, and every inch of the cabin walls was char that came off on my hands like black snow.

The radio bay was aft, under the second seat, behind a panel I'd been meaning to replace since before Yara was born, probably. The panel itself had warped and cracked, but the set inside—the old military-surplus transceiver I'd traded two crates of medicine for, upriver, from a man who'd looted it off a survey camp—was a brick of gray steel built by people who expected it to be shelled. The casing was scorched. The wiring harness was melted into modern art. But the guts, the actual guts of the thing, might live.

I got it out, and the charts, and the logbook—which was mostly blank, because I kept my real records in my head, a habit that had just paid for itself—and one more thing I didn't mention to Yara: the spare ammunition box, sealed in wax paper, that rode bolted under the floorboards where no casual thief would ever find it and no casual fire had reached. I'm not proud of carrying it. I'm not ashamed either. The interior teaches you that shame and prudence are different countries.

Yara bailed and held the skiff steady against the wreck's rolling, and neither of us talked, and the work was good, because work is the only prayer I've ever trusted.

By the time we rowed back, the sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of dirty pewter, and the *Wren* had settled until only the tips of her floats showed, like a drowned bird's beak breaking the surface.

* * *

We spread the salvage across the Kestrel's building stocks in the boatyard shed, and Yara lit the lamp, and we took inventory the way you take inventory after any death: what survived, what didn't, what it means.

The radio was the question mark. I pulled its cover plate there on the sawdust floor, and Yara held the lamp, and we both leaned in over the tangle of melted insulation and cooked solder like surgeons over a wound.

"Tell me honestly," she said.

"Honestly? The receiver section might be fine. The transmitter's final amplifier took heat—I can see it, the plates are discolored. But heat damage isn't always fatal. Sometimes it just needs cleaning and new wire." I started picking at the harness with a knife point, freeing conductors one at a time. "Problem is parts. Wire I can scavenge. A replacement valve, if the output valve's cracked—that's not something a boatyard keeps."

"Corvane's fleet does," Yara said quietly. "Salvage ships carry spares for everything. Radios, engines, men."

"And we're not going anywhere near that fleet."

"We're going to have to go near *something*. You can't fix that without parts, and even if you could, even if it sang again tomorrow—" She set the lamp down and straightened, and in the yellow light her face had the set look I was starting to recognize, the one that meant she'd stopped grieving and started planning. "Sable. Look at what they did. They didn't come for us in the night with knives. They burned the plane on a public quay in front of the whole town. That's not an assassination. That's an announcement."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've been thinking about it, and here's what it says: *we know where you are, we know what you're doing, and we can reach you whenever we want.* They let us keep our lives so we'd understand the terms." She picked up a scrap of charred fabric from the *Wren*'s fuselage, turned it over in her fingers. "My father ran from Renner for fifteen years and it bought him a gut full of lead in a fogbound field. Running isn't a strategy. It's just dying slowly with better scenery."

I should have argued. Some part of me, the part that had spent twelve years perfecting invisibility, wanted to argue—to say we'd take the Kestrel apart and bury the map, scatter north, start over, vanish. That was the doctrine. Never stay, never ask, never carry anything worth stealing. I'd written those rules in other people's blood and followed them faithfully ever since, and they had kept me alive when cleverer, braver people were in the ground.

But I looked at the map. Ede Voss's oilskin-wrapped map, sitting there on the stocks among the curled shavings, patient as a seed. Six tides. Five, now. And somewhere upriver a man named Aldous Renner was reading reports about a burned floatplane in Kestrel Bay, and every hour we spent deciding was an hour his fleet spent closing the distance.

And I looked at Yara, standing in her father's boatyard with her father's fire in her face, and I understood that the rules had a flaw I'd never let myself see. Invisibility works only if nobody ever needs you. The moment you promise something to somebody—really promise, the kind you can't walk away from—you become visible, and everything you've hidden becomes a target, and the only question left is what you do about it.

Ede Voss had needed me. So now I was a target. Fine. Then the target was going to move.

"The Kestrel," I said. "How fast can you finish her?"

Yara blinked. Then something kindled behind her eyes that I'd learn to know well in the days after—not quite a smile, more like a keel finding water. "She's planked to the gunwales. Frames are fair. She needs her deck, her rails, her fittings, caulk, paint, and an engine bed." She walked the hull as she spoke, touching it, counting. "With help? Three days. Alone? Five. Without sleep and with luck, maybe less."

"We have five tides. Four and a bit."

"Then I'd better not be alone." She was already pulling tools off the wall rack, laying them out in order, her hands moving with a certainty that made the shed feel suddenly like a place where the future was being manufactured instead of mourned. "There's a marine engine in the loft—Dad's, from a boat he never finished. If it turns over, we're in business. If it doesn't, I'll make it turn over."

I gathered up the radio's guts, wrapped them in the least-burned of the *Wren*'s canvas covers, and set them under the stocks where they'd be safe. My hands were steady. I noticed that with a kind of distant surprise. Underneath the steadiness, though, underneath the inventory and the planning, something had shifted its weight permanently, the way ballast shifts in a hull and changes forever how she sits in the water.

They'd taken the plane. They'd meant it as a message, and I'd read it correctly, and the message was: *be afraid, be small, stop.*

Here was my reply, unwritten as yet, but already taking shape in the sawdust and lamplight of a dead man's boatyard: *You've made me visible. So now you'd better wonder what I'll do with the view.*

Outside, the tide was turning. I could hear it in the harbor—the soft reversal of current around the pilings, the creak of moored boats swinging to meet the new flow. One tide gone. The river was already moving, and Corvane's fleet with it, and somewhere upstream a magistrate was waiting for his evidence like a spider at the center of a very large web.

Yara called from the loft: "Sable! Come look at this engine. Tell me if you hear what I hear."

I climbed the ladder toward the lamplight, leaving the ashes of the *Wren* behind me in the dark water—leaving them deliberately, the way you leave anything you can't afford to carry.

Four tides. One boat. No plane, no anonymity, no way back.

It was, I reflected, the freest I'd felt in twelve years.
