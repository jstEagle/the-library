## 6. The Kestrel

The boatyard smelled of cedar shavings and tar, and Yara had missed it the way you miss a tooth—only when your tongue finds the gap.

She stood in the doorway of her shed with the morning fog still clinging to her sleeves and watched Sable Winterbourne walk the length of the building like a woman casing a bank. The pilot moved quietly for someone who'd spent years in engines; her boots found the boards that didn't creak, which told Yara she'd already walked this floor once before, probably in the dark, probably while Yara slept.

"You're looking at my boat," Yara said.

"I'm looking at your boat," Sable agreed. She stopped at the stern where the Kestrel sat on her cradle, half-skinned, her ribs bare as a fish skeleton above the waterline. "How long has she been sitting?"

"Two years." Yara came down the aisle between the workbenches, arms crossed. "Give or take."

"Whose design?"

Yara hesitated. It was a simple question. It deserved a simple answer. But everything about the boat was tangled up in the man whose map now sat oilskin-wrapped on her workbench, and simple answers had been in short supply since Sable had walked into her life two days ago smelling of aviation fuel and other people's dying wishes.

"My father's," she said finally. "He drew her lines when I was fourteen. Said we'd build her together." Her laugh came out wrong, too sharp. "He drew them and then he left. I started her anyway. Got as far as the frames and the garboards and then—" She gestured at the dust sheeting the forward sections, the cobwebs strung between ribs like rigging on a ghost ship. "Then I ran out of reasons."

Sable said nothing for a long moment. She had a way of not saying things that filled a room more than most people's talking. Then she laid one hand flat against the hull, over a rib Yara had steamed and bent herself, at nineteen, alone, swearing at the wood in three languages.

"She's sound," Sable said. "Whoever built these frames knew what they were doing."

"I did."

"I know." And there it was—the smallest ghost of something that might have been respect, gone before Yara could decide whether to be flattered or insulted. "Question is whether she's sound enough to run six hundred miles of river against a salvage fleet, with a magistrate's inspectors crawling this harbor, in five days."

"She isn't finished."

"No," Sable agreed. "She isn't."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and Yara hated how good it felt.

That was the treacherous part. Every plank she fitted, every seam she caulked, her hands remembered things she'd spent two years trying to forget: Ede Voss standing behind her at this same bench, his big cracked hands guiding hers around the spokeshave, saying *let the tool do the arguing, girl, you just hold the grudge.* He'd taught her to read grain the way other fathers taught daughters to read scripture. He'd taught her to fair a curve by eye and trust the eye over the rule. And then he'd taught her nothing else, because he'd been gone before her seventeenth birthday, off upriver on some prospecting claim he never described and never invited her to share, sending money orders like apologies and letters like postcards from a country he'd emigrated to.

*Working hard, little kestrel. Wish you were here. Can't say where here is yet.*

She drove a screw until the slot stripped, swore, backed it out, and started again.

Sable worked beside her without being asked, which was its own kind of unsettling. The pilot turned out to know boats—not the way a sailor knows them but the way an engineer knows anything, as systems and stresses, weight and balance. She re-hung the rudder gudgeons after checking them twice. She rebuilt the raw-water intake on the auxiliary engine Yara had never gotten around to installing, working with small precise movements and an economy of words that made conversation feel like a luxury neither of them could afford.

It was near noon, fog burning off into a thin bright haze, when Yara straightened up, back aching, and heard herself say:

"We'll need to close the decks today if we want to launch tomorrow's tide."

Sable looked up from the engine bed. "Can it be done?"

"It can be done badly or it can be done right. Badly takes eight hours. Right takes twenty."

Sable glanced toward the harbor, where the fog was peeling back to reveal the masts of Kestrel Bay—and among them, unmistakably, the black funnels of the salvage fleet, riding at anchor like crows on a fence line. Three ships. A tender. And a gray patrol launch with a magistrate's pennant snapping at its masthead, fresh in from downriver sometime in the night.

"Right," Sable said. "But fast."

* * *

The inspectors came at two o'clock.

Yara saw them first from the loft window: four men in magistrate's gray walking the quay with clipboards and the unhurried confidence of men who have never once been told no. Behind them came a fifth, older, soft-bodied, with the pale hands of someone who'd never hauled a line in his life, and Yara knew him by reputation before she knew him by name—Inspector Crale, Renner's harbor auditor, the man who'd shuttered two boatyards on the south shore last spring over "irregular documentation" and sold their stock at auction to pay the fines.

"They'll come here," she said. "We're the only yard with an unfinished hull. Unfinished means undocumented. Undocumented means confiscatable."

"Then we finish the documentation," Sable said. She was already moving, pulling the dust sheets off the forward sections, sweeping shavings, doing violence to two years of accumulated neglect in ten minutes flat. "Where do you keep your registration papers?"

"In a strongbox under the floor. My father's old registration—for a different boat, a skiff he sold before I was born. I've been meaning to transfer—"

"Transfer it now. Name, tonnage, builder's mark. Make it the Kestrel. Ink dries faster than inspectors walk."

It was forgery. Yara knew it was forgery, and her hands shook so badly on the first attempt that she blotted the date. Sable took the pen from her, steadied the paper with a palm, and wrote in a hand so unremarkable it could have belonged to anyone—a clerk's hand, a customs officer's hand, a hand designed never to be remembered.

"You've done this before," Yara said.

"I've done a lot of things before." Sable blew on the ink. "Most of them I'm not proud of. This one doesn't even make the list."

Boots on the quay. Voices. The scrape of the shed door.

Crale came in like weather, his men fanning out along the benches, touching things. One of them lifted Yara's caulking mallet and set it down again with a faint smile, as if cataloguing it for later. Crale himself walked the length of the Kestrel, trailing one soft finger along the sheer, and made a show of consulting his clipboard.

"Vessel under construction," he read aloud. "Owner of record?"

"That would be me." Yara kept her voice level with an effort that cost her something physical. "Yara Voss. Boatwright. Registered berth seventeen."

"Construction commenced?" Crale's eyes were wet and gray and patient, the eyes of a man who enjoyed this part.

"Two years ago. Interrupted by"—she let the pause stretch just long enough—"family illness."

"Mm." He flipped a page. "And documentation of construction? Materials receipts, labor records, registration of the hull itself?"

Yara handed him the paper. Sable had moved to the far side of the shed and become furniture—just another hired hand, slouched against the bench, whittling a piece of scrap with a knife that had seen honest use. Crale barely glanced at her. People like Crale never looked at working people; they looked *through* them, at the property attached.

He read the registration. His finger traced the builder's mark—Ede Voss's mark, cut into the stemhead years ago, the only genuine thing on the document—and something flickered across his face.

"Voss," he said. "Any relation to the prospector Ede Voss? There was a notice circulated. Missing person, last seen upriver. The magistrate's office has an interest."

"He's my father," Yara said. "And he's not missing. He's dead. I buried what was left of him Tuesday." Let them chew on that. Let grief be armor; it was the only kind she owned.

Crale's expression performed sympathy the way a bad actor performs it—all the right movements, none of the feeling. "My condolences. Then you'll understand the office's position. An orphaned vessel, an incomplete build, a deceased owner's outstanding interests—it all rather complicates the estate. We may need to impound the hull pending probate."

"The hull is mine," Yara said. "Built with my money, my labor, on my leased ground. Probate applies to his estate. This was never his."

"Wasn't it?" Crale smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was kind. "His lines, though. His design. His mark on the stem. You'd be surprised how much of a dead man ends up belonging to the magistrate. Dead men are so convenient that way—they sign nothing, object to nothing, and their paperwork always has gaps." He tucked the registration into his clipboard, and Yara felt the whole day tilt toward disaster. "I'll need to retain this for verification. You'll hear from the office within the week. In the meantime, no launching. A vessel under dispute cannot legally take the water. Understood?"

He turned to go, and that was when Sable spoke, from her corner, in a voice like gravel settling.

"Verification against what?"

Crale paused. Turned. Looked at her properly for the first time, taking in the flight jacket, the wind-burned face, the total absence of deference.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Verification," Sable said. She pushed off the bench and ambled closer, still whittling, curls of cedar dropping from her knife. "Against the master registry at the customs house. Which keeps duplicate filings. Which means the original stays with the vessel—that's the law, Inspector, section eleven, you wrote half of it yourself last year when you were auditing the south shore yards." She held out her free hand, palm up. "So either give the lady back her paper, or ride down to the customs house and pull the duplicate, and we'll all wait together. Could take a while, though. Busy season. Lots of vessels. Lots of gaps in lots of dead men's paperwork." She smiled, and it was not a nice smile. "You'd be surprised how much of a live woman ends up belonging to herself."

The shed went very quiet. One of Crale's men shifted his weight. Crale looked at Sable for a long moment, and Yara watched him do the arithmetic—watched him weigh whatever he thought he knew against whatever he couldn't prove, watched the magistrate's machine encounter a part it didn't recognize.

He put the registration back on the bench.

"Audit continues tomorrow," he said. "All berths. All vessels. Have your papers in order." At the door he stopped, and did not turn around. "Kestrel Bay remembers its friends, Miss Voss. And its debts. Do think about which one you'd rather be."

The door banged shut behind them. Yara discovered she'd been holding a caulking iron hard enough to print its shape into her palm.

"What section eleven?" she demanded.

"There isn't one." Sable went back to her whittling. "There's a section nine and a section twelve. Nobody audits the auditors."

* * *

They provisioned after dark.

Not bought—bought left records, and records were how the magistrate's machine tracked things. Instead Sable produced a roll of banknotes and a list written in pencil, and they moved through the harbor's back channels like smugglers in their own town: flour and salt pork from a widow who owed Ede Voss a favor going back fifteen years and asked no questions beyond *is it true what they're saying about the fleet?*; lamp oil from a fisherman who traded it for Yara's promise to re-plank his dinghy come winter; a coil of secondhand warping line, a canvas tarpaulin, a medical kit assembled from three separate purchases at three separate hours.

Yara carried the heavy loads. Her shoulder burned by midnight, but it was a clean burn, an honest burn, the ache of work instead of the ache of waiting. Somewhere in the third hour, hauling a drum of engine oil down a ladder to the shed's water door, she caught herself humming—an old work song, Ede's song, the one he'd used to time his hammer blows when they'd steamed the frames—and stopped, furious at herself, and then, standing in the dark with the oil drum against her hip, let the rest of the verse come anyway.

By two in the morning the Kestrel was closed. Decks on, seams caulked, hatches seated, the auxiliary engine bolted to its bed and turning over by hand with a compression that made Sable grunt approval. She lay in her cradle no longer a skeleton but a ship—still unpainted, still raw, cedar pale as bone—but whole. Yara stood at her bow with tar on her hands and shavings in her hair and felt something move in her chest that she refused, absolutely refused, to name.

"She needs a coat of paint," she said. "Properly, she needs three."

"She needs to float," Sable said. "Paint is vanity. Float is survival."

They launched on the morning tide, before full light, sliding her down the ways with greased skids and catching her at the float with fenders and curses. She took the water like she'd been built for it—which, Yara thought, was the one true thing anyone had said about her in years. Ede's lines. Ede's mark on the stem. Her father's bones under someone else's muscle, carrying both of them upriver toward whatever he'd died trying to send them.

Yara took the wheel. Sable cast off the last line and stood beside her in the gray light, watching the harbor slide past—the salvage fleet at anchor, black-funneled and patient; the gray patrol launch with its pennant; Inspector Crale's silhouette in a quay window, watching them go, making notes.

"He'll report us," Yara said.

"He already has." Sable checked the chart, folded it against the binnacle. "Which means somewhere ahead, someone's reading our name and deciding what to do about it. So we'd best be somewhere else by the time they decide."

The Kestrel swung her head toward the river mouth. Ahead, the current waited, brown and broad and running hard, and past it the narrows where the tide fought the river like two dogs over a bone.

"First tide's at dusk," Sable said. "We run the narrows tonight."

Yara opened the throttle, and her father's boat answered.
