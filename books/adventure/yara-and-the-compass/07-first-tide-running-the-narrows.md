## 7. First Tide: Running the Narrows

**SABLE**

The narrows did not have a name on any chart Sable trusted. The rivermen called it the Throat, and they called it that the way you'd name a thing that had eaten people.

They anchored above it through the last of daylight, tucked into a fold of alder and shadow where the Kestrel's hull sat low against the bank like a moored bird pretending to be part of the landscape. Sable lay in the bow with the engine cover off, hands buried in the machinery, not because it needed work but because their hands needed something to do besides shake. Across the water, three miles downriver where the channel widened into the fleet's anchorage, lanterns burned in a loose constellation. Corvane's ships. The salvage fleet at rest, lit up like a town that didn't know it was a target.

"You're going to wear a hole in that gasket," Yara said from the stern. She was mending a seam in the spray hood by lamplight turned low, needle flashing, her injured pride from the harbor still sitting on her like wet wool.

"Better I wear it than the river does." Sable wiped their palms on a rag and looked upriver instead, where the sky was doing what it always did an hour after sundown—turning the color of a bruise healing. "We go when the tide turns. Not before, not after."

"And if we mistime it?"

"Then the Throat chews us up and spits out kindling, and Corvane finds the pieces floating past his anchorage tomorrow morning looking embarrassed."

Yara's needle stopped. In the lamplight her face was all angles and stubbornness, Ede Voss written over every line of it, though Sable doubted she'd thank anyone for saying so. "You have a gift for reassurance," she said.

"I have a gift for staying alive. Reassurance is extra."

But the truth was that Sable's stomach was doing slow, cold arithmetic, and the numbers kept coming out wrong. They had run bad water before—everyone who flew or boated this interior had a story about bad water, told in bars with too much gesture and not enough detail—but the Throat was different. It was a mile of channel barely wider than the Kestrel was long, where the tide poured through between two rock walls and the current ran at eight knots when it ran honest, and faster when it didn't. At slack water there was maybe forty minutes of grace. At any other time the channel became a millrace studded with rocks that showed themselves only as seams of white water, or worse, didn't show themselves at all.

And somewhere in the middle of it was the sweep rock. Every stretch of bad water had one—a rock that sat just under the surface off the true line, that swept boats sideways into the wall or into the standing waves beyond. The old pilots had marked it on their private charts with a little skull, or a cross, or nothing at all, depending on how much they liked the person asking. Sable's father had marked it with a small inked circle and the word *listen*.

Sable hadn't thought about that chart in years. Hadn't let themselves. But now, lying in the bow of a dead man's daughter's boat, waiting for the moon to clear the ridge, the memory came back whole: sitting in a floatplane cockpit at fourteen, legs too short to reach the rudder pedals properly, while their father tapped the map and said, *The river tells you where it doesn't want you. You just have to shut up long enough to hear it.*

"Hey." Yara's voice, quieter now. "You went somewhere."

"Just checking my arithmetic." Sable sat up. The moon was clearing the ridge to the east, laying a pale road down the center of the river, and the water below the anchorage had begun its first subtle lie—the surface smoothing, the chop dying, the whole river holding its breath. Slack water coming. Forty minutes, maybe less, and the moon would be their only light because running the Throat with a lamp showing was suicide twice over.

"Time," Yara said.

"Time." Sable dropped the engine cover, checked the fuel line one last time, and felt the fear settle into place—not gone, but filed, put where it could be carried. That was the trick nobody taught you. Fear didn't leave. You just learned to pack it. "Cast off when I say. And Yara—whatever happens in there, whatever I shout, you hold the wheel steady unless I tell you different. A scared correction kills more boats than a rock does."

"What will you be doing?"

"Listening."

* * *

**YARA**

The river went silent, and then it didn't.

That was the thing Yara would remember afterward, in the nights when sleep came hard: the moment the current died between tides and the Throat stopped roaring and for eleven seconds—she counted them, gripping the wheel with both hands—the world held perfectly still. Moonlight on black glass. Rock walls rising sheer on either side, close enough to touch, hung with moss that looked silver in the dark. Her own breath loud in her ears. Sable crouched in the bow like something carved there, head tilted, listening to water the way other people listened to music.

Then Sable said, "Now," and the Kestrel leaned forward into the gap, and the silence tore open behind them.

The current caught the hull like a hand closing. Not gradual—*instantaneous*, the river reaching up and taking them, and suddenly they were moving faster than the engine alone had ever pushed them, the rock walls streaming past, spray coming off the standing waves ahead in cold sheets that hit Yara's face and ran down inside her collar. The wheel bucked in her grip. She fought it level, teeth clenched, eyes on the pale seam of moonlit water that was the only road there was.

"Left!" Sable's voice, flat and absolute. "Two points left—*hold*—"

She held. The boat swung, the wall on the right came so close she could see individual stones in it, wet and gleaming like teeth, and then the channel bent and the wall fell away and there was open black water ahead full of white noise.

"Good," Sable said, which from them was practically a parade.

Yara discovered she was laughing. Short, involuntary bursts of it, half terror and half something else, something that felt disgracefully like joy. She'd built this boat with her own hands—or most of it; the last third had gone unfinished for two years because finishing it meant admitting whose design it was, whose drawings she'd worked from, whose handwriting was still on the lofting floor under the sawdust—and now the boat her father had drawn was carrying her through water that killed people, and it was *holding*, the hull biting the current like it had been born to exactly this, and some furious grieving part of her wanted to shout that at him across the distance of the grave he didn't even have yet.

"He built you well," she muttered to the wheel, because she couldn't say it about him. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

"Talk later," Sable called back. "There's a seam ahead—do you see it? Off the left, where the moonlight breaks?"

She saw it a heartbeat later: a line across the channel where the smooth water simply *stopped*, as if someone had drawn a border on the river. Beyond it the surface boiled low and ugly, no whitecaps, just a rolling muscular confusion that meant rock underneath, big rock, rock with opinions.

"That's not on any chart," she said.

"It's on mine." Sable was moving now, crawling back along the gunwale toward her, hand over hand, utterly unbothered by the motion—which was either courage or a lifetime of practice wearing courage's clothes. "My father called it the sweep. It sits just under the surface and it shoves everything sideways toward the right wall. We can't go over it and we can't stop, so we thread the gap between it and the wall, and the gap is—" Sable paused, doing some private calculation, "—about a boat and a half wide."

"A boat and a half. This boat?"

"This boat. Exactly this boat, actually, which is why I'm telling you and not the river." Sable knelt beside her, one hand closing over hers on the wheel—not taking it, just resting there, a point of contact in all that chaos. "I'll call the line. You steer it. Don't look at the rock. Look at the water you want, and put the nose there, and trust me about the timing."

"The last man who asked me to trust his timing was my father, and look how that went."

Something moved across Sable's face in the moonlight—not quite a flinch, more like a door opening and shutting. "Yeah," they said quietly. "Well. He's the one who taught me to listen to rivers. So maybe his timing wasn't the problem. Maybe it was everything else." Then, louder, all business again: "On my mark. Steady... steady..."

The sweep rock came at them.

* * *

**SABLE**

Later, Sable would try to reconstruct those six seconds and fail. Memory refused to file them in order. What remained were fragments: the boiling seam sliding closer, huge and patient. Yara's hands iron-steady under theirs, steadier than Sable's own heart. The sound of the current changing pitch as the channel narrowed around the hidden rock—the river *telling* them, exactly as their father had promised, singing the one note that meant *not here*. And Sable's own voice, calm as a flight instructor's, saying words they couldn't remember choosing:

"Now. Hard right. *Hard*—"

The Kestrel pivoted. The sweep rock passed so close beneath the hull that Sable felt it through the sole of their boot, a deep subsonic tremble, the boat's skin kissing the rock's shoulder with a sound like a drawn breath. The right wall loomed. For one impossible instant they were committed to a gap that existed only because both of them refused to believe in the alternative—

—and then the gap widened, and the wall fell back, and they were through, shot out the far end of the Throat into the broad lower reach where the tide was just beginning to build and the moon laid its road all the way to the sea.

Yara made a sound. Not a word. Something between a sob and a laugh, wrung out of her like water from a rag.

"Again," Sable heard themselves say, and realized they were grinning, grinning like an idiot, adrenaline singing in their blood like high voltage. "Say something, are you—"

"I'm fine." She wasn't. Her hands finally came off the wheel and were shaking so hard she had to press them flat against her thighs. "I'm absolutely fine. Did we—did the rock—"

"We kissed it. Politely. It'll write to us." Sable took the wheel, gave her room to come apart quietly, and looked back the way they'd come.

The Throat roared behind them, already awake, the tide pouring back in and turning the channel back into the millrace that had nearly killed them. And downstream, far off across the black water, the fleet's lanterns were still burning in their patient constellation. Nobody over there had moved. Nobody had seen them slip past in the dark, riding the one window the river allowed.

They'd gained a tide. Maybe a day and a half, once you counted Corvane's caution, his charts, his need to run the narrows in daylight with scouts ahead. It wasn't much. On this river, it was everything.

"Fuel," Yara said suddenly, straightening. "Check the spare drum—it was lashed portside, it should—"

Sable checked.

The lashing was intact. The deck ring was intact. The space where the drum had been was empty, wiped clean by the river, and Sable understood with sick clarity exactly when it had happened: the kiss with the sweep rock. The hull had flexed, the drum had jumped its chock, and the Throat had swallowed sixty liters of their future without breaking stride.

They had the tank in the engine—half full, maybe a bit more. Enough to run the lower reaches, enough to make the next fuel stop *if* the next fuel stop was friendly. Miller's Landing lay a day downriver. A trader named Osk Hale kept a dock there, and Hale owed Sable nothing but had never charged them full price either, and in this country that was as close to friendship as the arithmetic allowed.

Or the next fuel stop was Corvane's fleet, catching up tide by tide, patient as debt.

"How bad?" Yara asked. She'd read it off Sable's face before Sable said anything. She was going to keep doing that, Sable thought. It was going to be inconvenient.

"We lost the reserve. We're alive, we're ahead, and we're thirsty." Sable eased the throttle forward and the Kestrel answered, running clean and fast down the moonlit reach. "Miller's Landing by tomorrow night. We fuel there, and we don't stop longer than it takes to pour."

"And if Hale's dock has visitors?"

Sable thought about the tavern in Kestrel Bay, about the way conversations died when certain men walked in, about how many ears a salvage fleet could buy along a river where everyone was poor and information was the only crop that never failed. They thought about Magistrate Renner's name, spoken once in a harbor tavern, and how the air had changed around it.

"Then we buy our fuel and we lie beautifully," Sable said. "It's the one cargo I always carry."

Ahead, the river bent south into darkness, and somewhere down that darkness the first gray hint of dawn was waiting, and somewhere behind them the tide was rising into the Throat, filling the channel for the boats that would follow. Sable flew the throttle gently, listening—to the engine, to the water, to the small persistent voice that had kept them alive this long.

It was saying what it always said, in its wordless way: *you're not safe, you're just ahead.*

For now, ahead was enough.
