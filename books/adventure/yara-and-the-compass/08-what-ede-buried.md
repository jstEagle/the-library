## 8. What Ede Buried

The journal was bound in oiled canvas gone soft as chamois, and it smelled of my father—tobacco and spruce pitch and the faint iron tang of river water that never quite dries out of a man who lives on it. I had not expected the smell to undo me. I had braced for the words, for his cramped left-slanting hand, for the arithmetic of a stranger's life. But when I opened the cover in the grey light before dawn and that smell rose up off the page like breath, I had to set the thing down on the Kestrel's thwart and stand with both hands gripping the gunwale until my stomach agreed to behave.

Sable pretended to be asleep in the bow. They were kind that way, or disciplined—I hadn't yet learned which.

We were tied up in a backwater slough two hours below Miller's Landing, hidden under alder shadow, waiting out the last dark before we'd run the open stretch toward the dead village Ede's notes described. The river talked to itself all night, low and continuous, the way it does when the current is sorting through gravel. I'd read maybe forty pages by lamplight after we anchored, and then I couldn't stop, and now here I was at first light with the journal open across my knees and my father's voice assembling itself in my head out of ink.

"Read it aloud," Sable said from the bow, without opening their eyes. "Some things are too heavy to carry alone."

So I did.

* * *

The early entries were nothing. Fuel costs. A cracked prop shaft. A woman in Halfway Bend who paid her passage in dried salmon and gossip. My father's handwriting was small and exact, an engineer's hand, and he numbered everything—even jokes, even complaints, as though life were a manifest he intended to balance. I remembered that about him from before he left: the way he'd count things twice, the nails in a plank, the stairs down to the dock. I used to think it was impatience. Reading him now, I understood it was fear—the fear of a man who knows the world can be lost through any uncounted gap.

Then, three years back by the dates, the entries changed.

*"Day 214. Took a charter party up past the Narrows for a man named Corvane. Salvage survey. He has half a chart and says the other half belongs to a wreck called the Meridian, lost '09 with government bullion aboard. He talks like the money is already his."*

I stopped there. Sable's eyes were open now, fixed on me across the boat's small length.

"Keep going," they said quietly.

*"Day 216. Corvane's backer came upriver to inspect. Never seen his like this far from marble floors. Magistrate Aldous Renner. Grey coat, grey gloves, grey men. He walked the bank at Miller's Landing like he was measuring it for a coffin. Corvane fawns. Renner looks through people. When he looked at me I felt catalogued."*

My mouth had gone dry. "That's the magistrate," I said. "Your magistrate."

"The same," Sable said. Their voice had a flatness I'd learned to hear as weather coming. "Go on."

I went on. The next entries were ordinary again—a week of them, engine trouble, rain—and that ordinariness made what followed worse, because it meant my father had carried this next part around inside himself for days before writing it, turning it over, deciding how to say it, the way you decide how to lift something you know will burn your hands.

*"Day 229. I have seen a thing I cannot unsee.*

*Renner sent us up the Serrat branch to survey a village called Ash Landing. Forty souls, maybe fifty. They held salvage rights to the upper bars—filed legal, grandfathered, stamped. Renner wanted those bars. He offered to buy. The headman refused—not rude, just firm; it was their living. So Renner smiled and said he understood, and we pulled out downstream.*

*At midnight he woke me. Said we were going back up. Said there'd been 'a complaint of banditry' and he was obliged to respond. There was no complaint. I heard him dictate one to his clerk while we ran the dark water, inventing bandits out of whole cloth, and his voice never changed, pleasant as a man ordering dinner.*

*I told him I wouldn't run the boat. He put his grey glove on my shoulder and said, 'Mr. Voss, you are either crew or you are evidence. Choose.'*

*I chose crew. God forgive me. I ran him up to Ash Landing and I sat in the boat with my hands on the wheel and I watched the sky go orange over the trees, and I listened to the sound a village makes when it burns. It is not silence. I thought it would be silence. It is not.*"

I had to stop. Not because I was crying—though I was, hot and silent, hating myself for it—but because my hands were shaking too hard to hold the pages flat. Sable took the journal from me without a word, found the place, and read the rest of the entry themselves, their voice low and level as a river under ice.

*"They say eleven died. Eleven counted. The headman's family got out ahead of the fire because the headman trusted no grey coat, ever, and had drilled his children in running. The rest ran into the night with what they wore. Renner filed the salvage rights within the month. Legal. Stamped. Clean as a surgeon's table.*

*I keep asking myself why I am writing this instead of burning it. Here is why: because paper survives. Because someday someone will ask what happened to Ash Landing, and the answer must not be whatever Renner pays to have it be.*

*I copied the order. His own hand, his own seal—he wrote it standing on my deck, dictating the 'response to banditry,' and I kept the carbon he threw away. It is with the chart. If anything happens to me, whoever holds the chart holds the proof. Tell them: the wreck is only half of it. The other half is what he did to get there first."*

Sable closed the journal gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"That's why Corvane wants the map," they said. "Not the bullion. Or not only. Renner sent him for the paper. A signed order in a magistrate's hand, naming a village he burned to steal from the ashes—it would hang him. It could hang his whole network, every clerk and constable he's bought between here and the capital." They looked at me, and there was something raw under the calm. "Ede wasn't hiding treasure, Yara. He was carrying a live coal. For three years."

* * *

I should tell you what I did with that, sitting in the bottom of a borrowed boat in a slough full of morning fog. What I did first, shamefully, furiously, was think about myself.

Three years. Three years since the last letter, since the birthday card that arrived four months late with a note in his cramped hand—*Sorry, sweetheart. Work. Will explain someday.* Three years of me telling anyone who'd listen that Ede Voss was a coward, a drifter, a man who loved rivers more than daughters. Three years of building my resentment plank by plank the way I build a hull, tight and watertight and proud of the joinery. And the whole time—

The whole time he had been walking around with a magistrate's murder in his pocket, staying away from me for the same reason you stay away from a house with plague in it. Not because he didn't love me. Because loving me out loud would have put my name on Renner's list, and I had just read what happened to people on Renner's list.

"He stayed away to keep me off it," I said. My voice came out wrong, scraped. "The letters that said nothing. The excuses. The—" I laughed, and it wasn't a laugh. "I told everyone my father abandoned me. I built a whole self out of that. And he was protecting me the only way he knew how, and I—" 

I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes.

Sable let me. That was another thing I was learning about them: they didn't rush in to fill silence with comfort. They waited it out like weather.

When I could speak again I said, "He could have told me. One letter. Coded, careful, anything. He could have trusted me."

"Could he?" Sable asked, not unkindly. "You're reading his reasons right now, in his own hand. He trusted exactly one person with this, and he chose a stranger with a floatplane, because a stranger had nothing to lose." They paused. "And because he figured a stranger might actually deliver it. Family runs toward the fire. Strangers run away from it. He needed someone who'd run away from it long enough to survive."

"That's a terrible thing to say."

"It's a true thing. Those often are."

I looked at the journal in their lap—at the oilskin packet beside it, where the map lay folded with its tide-marks and depth-soundings in my father's hand, and somewhere inside it, folded smaller still, a carbon copy of a magistrate's order that smelled, I imagined, of smoke. My father had spent three years being a coward in every letter so he could be brave in exactly one place, and the brave place was this packet, and the packet was in my lap, and he was dead in the ground at a fogbound strip because Renner had finally caught up with the coal he'd been carrying.

Forgiveness came then. That's the truth of it, and I hate how easy it sounds. It didn't arrive like a decision. It arrived like the fog lifting—one moment the world was all white anger, and the next there were shapes in it, his shapes, the counting-twice father, the man who numbered his fears so they couldn't leak. I forgave him all at once and completely, sitting in the Kestrel's bilge with river damp soaking through my trousers.

And he wasn't there to hear it.

That's the part nobody warns you about. Everyone tells you forgiveness is a gift you give yourself, and fine, it is, but it is also a letter you write to someone who can't read it. I sat there holding mine, warm and finished and undeliverable, and I understood that I would carry it the way he'd carried his—for however long it took to find somewhere to put it down.

"I'm going to finish it," I said. I didn't mean the sentence. I meant all of it—the boat, the run, the wreck, the paper. "His work. All of it."

"I know," Sable said. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

We raised anchor as the fog began to thin, and ran the open water with the journal lashed under my jacket against my spine, because I could not bear to leave it in a locker. By midmorning the banks changed. The farms thinned out. The alders gave way to blackened snags standing in the shallows like burnt matches planted by a giant hand, and then the snags gave way to foundations—chimney stubs, a crumbled wellhead, fence posts with no fences.

Ash Landing.

We brought the Kestrel in slow, engine at idle, as if the village could be woken by noise. Nothing woke. The river had taken the near street; you could see rooflines under the clear water, green with weed, a whole drowned row of them. On the higher bank the footprints of houses remained in the earth—darker rectangles where floors had been, doorways opening onto nothing. Fireweed grew waist-high in every ruin, which is the cruel joke of that flower: it blooms best on burned ground, purple and gorgeous, feeding on the very thing that killed everything else.

I walked the high street. There was a church frame with no church in it. There was a schoolhouse foundation smaller than my workshop, and beside it, half-buried in fireweed roots, something pale.

I dug it out with my fingers.

It was a toy boat. Carved, hand-painted, red and yellow flaking, small enough to fit in a child's two hands. Someone had whittled it for someone, and painted it at a kitchen table that no longer existed, and some child had sailed it on this same river, and then one night the child had run barefoot into the dark with the toy clutched to their chest—or dropped it, or left it, or lost it in the running—and the toy had waited three years in the weeds for me.

I stood there in the ruins of a place I'd never known, holding a stranger's childhood, and I finally understood the scale of what my father had witnessed. Not eleven counted dead. Not rights and ledgers and carbons. This. A carved boat going unclaimed forever. A doorway opening onto nothing. Fireweed eating a town.

I put the toy in my jacket pocket, over my heart, next to the journal. It seemed right that they travel together—the evidence and the grief. Neither one means much without the other.

Sable found me there. They didn't ask what I held. They just looked at the chimney stubs and the fireweed and said, "Now you know what we're really racing for."

And that was when the sound came—thin, insectile, wrong for the morning—and we both turned our faces up to see the scout plane coming down the valley line at altitude, grey as Renner's gloves, flying the river slow, the way you fly when you're looking for something.

It hadn't seen us yet. But it was flying straight up the Serrat branch, straight toward Ash Landing, and between us and the treeline where the Kestrel lay hidden there was nothing but open fireweed and a quarter mile of ground with nowhere to hide.
