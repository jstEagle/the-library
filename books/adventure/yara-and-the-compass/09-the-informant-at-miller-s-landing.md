## 9. The Informant at Miller's Landing

Miller's Landing announced itself the way a liar announces himself: warmly, and a little too soon.

We smelled it before we saw it—woodsmoke and frying grease riding the current on a soft morning wind—and then the bend gave it up: a shelf of plank buildings stacked against the north bank like crates someone had abandoned mid-unloading. A fuel dock jutting into the brown water. A drying rack hung with fish that flashed silver when the light found them. And above it all, hand-painted on a board that had once been white, the words MILLER'S LANDING — PROVISIONS — FUEL — A GOOD NIGHT'S SLEEP.

That last line was what made my neck prickle. Nobody upriver sold sleep. Sleep was something you took in shifts with one eye open, in the reeds, with the engine cold so no one could hear you breathing. A man who advertised rest was a man who wanted you to stop moving.

"Fuel," Yara said from the helm, reading my silence the way she'd learned to read weather. "We're down to fumes and prayer."

"I know what we're down to."

"And he's got fuel."

"I know that too." I watched the dock come closer, counting things without meaning to—two skiffs tied off, both empty; a dog asleep on the planks, which meant no strangers had come through recently enough to keep it barking; smoke from two chimneys, not three, though there were three buildings that needed heat. Small arithmetic. The kind of sums I'd been doing since I was old enough to understand why we never lit lamps in windows facing the road.

Yara brought the Kestrel alongside with a touch I was starting to trust, which was its own kind of danger. Trusting things was how people ended up in ditches. Or burned villages. Or warrants with their names spelled out in a magistrate's careful hand.

I stepped onto the dock first. Habit. If the boards were going to give, better they give under me.

* * *

The trader came out wiping his hands on an apron that had seen more years than most of his customers, and his face did everything right. Surprise, pleasure, the small theatrical squint of a man trying to place a face he half-remembered. He was sixty-odd, maybe, gray in his beard, a gold tooth that caught the light when he smiled, and hands that were genuinely scarred from rope and fish hooks—the hands of a working man, or of a man who understood that working men's hands were part of the costume.

"Well now," he said. "Boat traffic this far up in one week's more than I've seen all season. You'll be wanting fuel."

"You'll be wanting coin," I said.

"That I will." He laughed like it was a good joke between friends. "Name's Odell. Odell Marsh. This is my landing, such as it is."

"Sable." Just the name. Never the surname. There wasn't one anymore, not one that belonged to me.

He didn't push for more, which I noted. Most traders pushed. Curiosity was currency out here as much as scrip, and a man who didn't spend his was either rich already or being paid in something else.

Inside, the store smelled of kerosene, salt pork, dried apples, and lamp oil—a hundred years of river commerce soaked into the walls. Shelves climbed to a ceiling lost in shadow. A pot-bellied stove ticked in the corner, and beside it, arranged just so, two chairs angled toward each other like they were used to holding conversation. Comfortable chairs. Chairs you sank into and stayed in.

Yara went straight to the shelves with her list, and I let her, because watching a trader watch a customer told you more than watching the customer. Odell Marsh watched Yara the way a man watches anything worth reporting: briefly, thoroughly, and then away, as if he hadn't looked at all.

"So," he said, dragging a ledger across the counter, thick as a Bible and swollen with damp. "Fuel. How much you needing?"

"Forty gallons."

His eyebrows went up honest as sunrise. "Forty. That's a serious boat."

"She has to be, where we're going."

"Where would that be?" Light. Easy. A man making conversation because the river was long and lonely.

"Up." I let him fill in the rest himself. People always filled in the rest themselves, and whatever they imagined was usually worse than the truth and more useful to me.

He wrote the sale in the ledger while his boy—I hadn't heard him come in, another mark against the place—went down to work the pump. And that was when I saw it.

Not the ledger itself. Ledgers were everywhere; every trader, every dockmaster, every ferryman kept one, and half of them were fiction anyway. It was the seal. Pressed into the bottom corner of the page in faded violet wax, half-hidden by the counter's lip but unmistakable to anyone who'd spent their childhood staring at it across a courtroom aisle: a stag's head crowned with a bar of iron, antlers spread like a fence meant to keep the world out.

Renner's seal. Aldous Renner, Magistrate of the Interior Districts, whose warrant was somewhere behind us on the river with our names on it, whose fleet was somewhere ahead of us, and whose wax sat on the ledger of a friendly trader at a friendly landing advertising a good night's sleep.

My hands didn't shake. I'd trained them out of that years ago, the way you train a dog not to beg at the table. But something behind my sternum went very still and very cold, the way it does when you realize the ice you've been walking on has been singing for a while now, and you just weren't listening.

* * *

Here is the thing about trust out here: you don't get to have it. Not whole. You get rations, measured out like water in a dry season, and every drop you spend you spend knowing you might need it later and it won't be there.

I trusted Yara. That had happened somewhere back in the narrows, between the sweep rock and the quarrel about the falls, and I hadn't noticed it happening any more than you notice a tide coming in until your boots are wet. That was one ration spent, and it scared me more than Renner's seal did, if I'm honest. Renner I knew how to survive. Trust was the thing that had killed my brother—trust in a magistrate's signature, trust in the law's promise that a family's land was a family's land.

But Odell Marsh? Odell got nothing.

"Rough country upriver," he said, leaning on the counter, gold tooth flashing. "Hear there's salvage boats working the sound. Big operation. Fellow named Corvane running it." He said the name casually, a stone tossed into still water, and waited for the rings.

"Salvage men are like gulls," I said. "Loud, greedy, gone by winter."

He chuckled. "And hear the magistrate's got his own interest in it. Renner." Another stone. Bigger.

"Magistrates have interests everywhere. That's what makes them magistrates."

He studied me a moment longer than was comfortable, and I gave him back a face like a closed door in an abandoned house—nothing to read, nothing worth breaking in for. Behind us, Yara counted out coins for rope and salt pork, and I loved her a little for not looking over, for playing her part without being asked, for understanding without discussion that the performance had started the moment our hull touched his dock.

"Your girl know much about boats?" Odell asked.

"She built that one," I said, and watched his eyes flicker—there, and gone. A boatwright and a pilot, traveling light, buying forty gallons, heading upriver into a salvage war. Every word I gave him was a word he'd sell, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so, because that was the etiquette of the river: everyone robs everyone politely, and calls it trade.

Which gave me an idea.

* * *

While his boy pumped fuel and Yara haggled over a coil of line she didn't need—she was good at haggling; it ran in her blood along with the boatbuilding, Ede Voss's stubbornness expressed in cents saved—I drifted toward the stove and the two comfortable chairs, and let myself look tired. That part took no acting. I was tired down to the marrow, tired in ways sleep didn't fix anymore.

"Bad business, whatever you're running from," Odell said, settling into the other chair like we'd planned it. "Man can see it on a person. You've got the look."

"What look is that?"

"The look of somebody the law's got a paper on." He said it kindly. That was the worst of it—he said it like sympathy, like an offer of shelter, and maybe in some buried honest version of himself it even was. "This is a free landing, friend. No questions asked here. Man wants to sit by my stove and rest his bones, that's his business. I don't sell information." He paused, precisely, the way a good liar pauses. "Don't need to."

"No," I agreed. "You just write it down."

He blinked. "What's that?"

"In your ledger." I nodded toward the counter, casual as commenting on the weather. "Names, boats, headings. Every trader keeps one. How else would supply runs make sense?"

Something moved behind his eyes—quick, controlled, but not quick enough. "Just bookkeeping," he said.

"Course it is." I smiled with the gold-tooth warmth he'd taught me five minutes ago. "Say, you wouldn't have a chart of the upper sound? Ours got soaked in the narrows. Ink ran clean off."

He hesitated exactly one beat too long, and then produced one from under the counter, rolled and tied, like he'd been waiting for someone to ask. "Old survey," he said. "Ten years stale, but the rocks don't move much."

The rocks don't move much. Neither do lies; they just change hands.

I unrolled it on the counter, weighted the corners with salt-pork tins, and studied it while Yara finished paying. The sound branched like a lung. Three channels in, one deep channel out, and the wreck—if Ede's tide-notes were true—lying in the throat of it where only extreme low water would ever show her ribs. Corvane's fleet would come up the main channel. Any reasonable person would assume we would too.

Reasonable people had been losing to me my whole life.

"Good chart," I said, loud enough for the boy at the pump to hear. "Yara! Look here—" I turned the chart so it faced the room, and tapped the eastern channel with one finger, slow and deliberate, the way a tired navigator confirms a plan. "We run the east passage past the burned village, cut through at the second tide. Saves us half a day against the main channel."

Yara's eyes met mine across the store, and I watched her take it in—the ledger, the seal she couldn't see from where she stood but the shape of my voice told her plainly enough—and watched her become an actress in the space of a breath. "East passage," she repeated. "Past the village." She frowned theatrically. "You sure the draft'll clear?"

"Draft'll clear at low water. We'll time it." I rolled the chart up and bought it without haggling, overpaying cheerfully, because a man who overpays is a man who believes he'll live to spend the difference.

Odell watched all of it with his pleasant face and his pleasant stove, and I shook his hand at the door—his palm dry and warm as paper—and thanked him for the hospitality of a free landing where no questions were asked.

"My pleasure," he said. "Travel safe. Water's high this season."

They always warned you about the water. It made them feel honest.

* * *

Out on the dock, the boy finished fueling and Yara cast off the lines herself, and we slid away from Miller's Landing into the brown morning current with forty gallons of fuel sloshing in our tank and a lie sitting fat and warm in a trader's ledger, waiting to be copied out and sealed in violet wax and sent downriver to a magistrate who thought he was herding us.

Neither of us spoke until the landing was a smudge of smoke behind the bend.

"The seal," Yara said finally. She was at the wheel, eyes on the channel, hands easy. "You saw it."

"Bottom corner of his ledger. Stag's head, iron bar. Same as on the warrant."

"How long's he been reporting?"

"Years, probably. Maybe since before we were born, in one form or another. Landings like that aren't businesses, they're instruments. Renner doesn't need soldiers everywhere. He needs ledgers." I looked back once at the emptying river. "Every boat that stops there gets written down. Where it came from, where it claimed it was going, who was aboard, what they bought. Forty gallons says long trip. Rope and salt pork say rough country. And tonight a rider or a launch carries it all to whoever's listening."

"Then they'll be waiting at the east passage."

"They'll be waiting at the east passage," I agreed, "with a great deal of patience and nobody there."

She was quiet a moment, working it through, and I could see her doing the same arithmetic I'd done inside—what it cost, what it bought. "How did you know? Before the seal. You went stiff the minute we tied off."

"The sign said a good night's sleep."

She almost laughed. Almost. "That's it?"

"Nobody sells sleep, Yara. Sleep is free if you're safe and impossible if you're not. A man advertising it is selling something else and calling it comfort." I settled onto the thwart beside her, close enough that our shoulders touched when the Kestrel rolled, and didn't move away, and noted that I didn't move away, and filed that under dangers I was choosing anyway. "Trust is like fuel out here. You ration it. You never run the tank dry, because the moment you need it and it's not there, you walk."

"And Odell?"

"Odell gets none. Odell gets a performance." I turned the stolen chart over in my hands, ten years stale, the rocks mostly where they'd been drawn. "It's a strange thing, though. Sitting in that chair by his stove, drinking his coffee, letting him be kind to me. Part of me wanted it to be real. Some old fool part that's still hungry for a friendly harbor."

Yara glanced at me sideways. "And the rest of you?"

"The rest of me knows the coffee was probably bought with Renner's coin, same as the wax and the boy and the good night's sleep." I looked upriver, where the channels braided and split and the mountains stood shoulder to shoulder keeping their own counsel. "That's the trick of a place like this. It makes you grateful for the hand that's measuring out your chains, because at least it's warm."

Ahead, the river narrowed toward the fork—the main channel swinging west, broad and confident, and the eastern passage threading away through country the charts barely bothered with, past a shoreline that had been green the last time anyone surveyed it and was ash now, if Ede's journal told the truth. Past the dead village. Past everything Renner had signed his name to.

"We'll anchor early tonight," I said. "Cold camp, no fire, in the reeds below the fork. If Odell's report moves fast, they'll sweep the east passage tomorrow and find it empty, and by the time they work out we doubled back to the main channel, we'll have a tide on them. Maybe two."

"And if they don't believe the ledger?"

"Then we're in the reeds with a cold engine and a quiet boat, and we'll hear them coming." I said it lightly. Neither of us believed it lightly. Being hunted by patient men in fog was not a thing you survived by believing in it lightly; it was a thing you survived by believing in it completely and going anyway.

The current took us. The fork opened ahead like a question, and I realized, somewhere in the cold arithmetic of headings and tides, that I'd answered it without noticing: not the safe channel, not the invisible life, not the rules that had kept me alive and alone for twenty years. Those rules had gotten my plane burned at anchor and my name on a warrant. Invisibility, it turned out, was just another kind of drowning—one you did slowly, in plain sight, and called it peace.

"West channel," I said. "Past the fork, hug the far bank. And Yara—when we pass the mouth of the east passage, don't look at it."

"Why not?"

"Because somewhere behind us there may be glass pointed this way, and a woman who looks where she shouldn't is a detail a ledger remembers." I handed her the stale chart to hold, which was as close to an apology for the whole rotten world as I knew how to make. "From here on, we're the most interesting thing on this river. Best thing we can do is be boring in the wrong direction."

She put the wheel over, and the Kestrel leaned into the turn, and the east passage slid by on our left like a door we'd locked behind us with someone else's key.

It was another hour before we saw the smoke—not Odell's chimney smoke, thin and domestic, but a heavy black smear rising off the water two bends downriver, in the direction of the main channel, where no settlement stood on my charts and no fire had any honest reason to be burning.

Yara saw it the same moment I did. Her hands went still on the wheel.

"That's between us and the sound," she said.

"Yes," I said, and reached for Ede's journal, and the tide clock in my head began to count.
