## 10. Second Tide: The Dead Village

The river told us before the map did.

We'd been running since first light, the Kestrel shouldering through a brown-green channel that narrowed and widened like something breathing, and for the last hour there had been no birds. I hadn't noticed it at first—Sable had. They'd cut the throttle without a word and let us drift, head cocked, listening to an absence the way other people listened to music.

"No herons," they said finally. "No swallows over the water. Nothing that nests in standing timber."

I looked at the banks. The forest came down to the river's edge thick and green and indifferent, willow and alder and the black spires of spruce, and it took me another mile of water to see what Sable had heard: that the trees were all the same age. Young. Even. A forest with no old men in it, growing up over ground that had been scoured clean inside one lifetime.

Ede's journal lay open on my knees, weighted with a coil of line against the wind. The page was soft as cloth from handling, his handwriting slanting downhill like a man hurrying away from what he was saying:

*Third bend past the twin snags, north bank. Ashby Landing. Or what's left. Don't stop unless you have to. If you do have to, stop anyway—it's the only deep-water anchorage for twenty miles, and Renner's men never go back to places they've already finished with.*

"Finished with," I read aloud, and the words sat wrong in my mouth, like a bone too big to swallow.

Sable said nothing. They'd been saying very little since Miller's Landing, since the trader's ledger with the magistrate's seal pressed into its cover like a brand. But their eyes flicked to me once, quick and gray, and then back to the channel, and I understood that they were letting me find this place at my own speed. It was the closest thing to kindness they knew how to give.

The twin snags came up on the starboard side exactly where Ede had drawn them, two drowned trunks leaning together like conspirators, and past them the river bent north and opened out, and there it was.

Ashby Landing.

I'd expected ruins. Ruins are honest—they announce themselves, they let you keep your distance, you can look at a burned house the way you look at a photograph. What I hadn't expected was how *unfinished* it looked. The fire had been years ago, but nothing had been cleared, nothing rebuilt, nothing even properly fallen down. Chimneys stood alone in the nettles like sentries abandoned mid-watch. A church bell tower leaned at an angle that defied physics and patience both, held up by its own charred beams. Fence posts ran off into the grass toward gates that no longer existed, marking boundaries for fields that had gone back to seed and then gone back to scrub, and along the one street—or what had been a street—the foundations of houses lay open to the sky like pulled teeth.

Nobody had come back. That was the thing I couldn't get past. In Kestrel Bay, when a boat burned at its moorings, half the harbor turned out before the paint stopped blistering, and by the next week someone had salvaged the fittings and told the story so many times the story was worth more than the boat. Here there was no story. There was just a hole in the world, and the world growing over it like skin over a wound that had never been allowed to drain.

"The journal says forty-one households," I said. My voice came out flat, which was better than the alternative.

"It says more than that." Sable brought us in slow, reading the water for deadheads, and I could hear the effort it cost them to keep their voice level. "Page after page. Names. He wrote down every name he could remember. He was here after, you understand. Came through maybe a year later, walking the bank, writing down who had lived where."

"Why?"

"Because nobody else did."

We tied off at a stone jetty that had survived the fire the way stone survives everything—sullenly, usefully—and Sable went forward to check the anchor lines while I stood on the jetty and made myself look.

The village faced the water the way river villages always do, as if the water were the road and everything else was the yard. Boathouses first, or their skeletons. Then the houses, set close, porch rails almost touching, the kind of closeness that means borrowing sugar and watching each other's children. Farther back, upslope, a larger building with a wide foundation—Ede's notes identified it as the cannery, the reason the village existed at all, the reason it died. Salvage rights. The word in the journal was underlined twice, the pen pressed so hard it had torn through in places. *He wanted the cannery's charter. The village owned it communally. So he burned the village and filed the loss.*

I walked up the street because I couldn't not walk up the street.

Grass had taken everything soft. Underfoot there was glass fused into the soil, and bits of iron too rusted to name, and here and there a square of melted bottle-glass gone opaque and milky, like frost that had decided to stay. A cookstove sat in the middle of what must have been a kitchen, door hanging open, still holding—God help me—a charred log where someone had set it for morning. The morning had come anyway. The morning always comes anyway. That was the thought that broke something loose in my chest, that somewhere behind me in time there had been a woman or a man who banked that stove against the cold and went to bed in a house full of sleeping people, and the last thing they did in this world was an ordinary kindness.

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that Sable came looking, and found me, and didn't say anything, which was right.

It was Sable who found the toy.

They crouched at the edge of a foundation near the upslope end of the street—a small house, small foundation, a child's house—and lifted something out of the ash with both hands, careful, the way you lift a bird with a broken wing.

A tin horse. Maybe six inches long, painted once in colors the fire had stripped to ghost-traces: a smear of red along the mane, a suggestion of blue on the saddle. One leg was buckled where heat had softened it, so it stood forever mid-stumble, forever about to fall and never falling. Someone had loved it. The paint was worn off its back in an oval the exact size and shape of a small hand carrying it everywhere, bed to table to riverbank and home again.

"He was four," I said. I don't know why I said it, or where the certainty came from, only that it arrived whole, the way grief sometimes does—not reasoned but simply *known*. "Whoever carried this. Four, maybe five. Old enough to love a horse. Not old enough to run fast enough."

Sable set it down again in the ash, gently, facing the way it had been facing. "You can't carry all of them," they said quietly. "There were villages before this one. There would have been more after, if he'd gotten the chance."

"I know."

"You don't. Nobody does, the first time. The first one goes into you like a splinter and you think you'll die of it, and then you learn the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is worse than the splinter." Their voice stayed flat, but their hands, I noticed, had closed into fists at their sides. "This is what he was protecting you from. Not the map. This."

And that was the moment the anger I'd been carrying since Kestrel Bay—that cold, well-fed anger at the father who'd missed my birthdays, who'd sent money instead of himself, who'd chosen rivers over his own daughter—finally turned over and showed me its other face.

Because I had spent years telling myself a story about Ede Voss. The story had a shape and it fit my hand nicely: he was a selfish man, a restless man, a man who loved the interior more than he loved me. Every unanswered letter confirmed it. Every Christmas parcel postmarked from some settlement I couldn't find on a map confirmed it. I had built him out of absences and I had built him mean, because mean was easier to live with than *gone*, and now I was standing in the reason he was gone, holding the proof in a child's toy horse, and the story collapsed under me all at once.

He hadn't chosen the river over me. He'd chosen the river *for* me. He'd put himself between me and Aldous Renner the way you throw yourself between a child and a snake—not heroically, not cleanly, just completely, for years, until it killed him. And he couldn't tell me, because telling me would have put me on the list, and so he'd let me hate him instead, because a daughter who hates her father doesn't ask questions about where he goes.

"You could have told me," I whispered—to Ede, to the ash, to nobody. "I'd have kept the secret. You didn't even let me try."

The wind moved through the young trees, all the same age, all planted by fire, and it sounded almost like surf, almost like breathing, almost like an answer.

* * *

We made camp in the cannery, because Ede had been right—it was the only deep-water anchorage, and because its stone walls felt less like trespass than the houses did. We ate hard bread and smoked fish in silence while the light went long and coppery across the water, and I read the journal aloud, because it seemed suddenly important that somebody's voice be in this place besides the wind.

The names took longest. Forty-one households, and Ede had written them in columns, family by family, trade by trade: *Olek Marsh, netmender. The Ferrow women, three sisters, ran the smokehouse. Danil Ashby, whose grandfather founded the landing. His wife, Petra. Their children: Sorrel (9), Tam (6), Wren (2).*

Wren. Two years old. I looked at the tin horse in my pocket—I'd gone back for it, and I wasn't going to apologize to anyone, including myself—and tried to make a two-year-old out of the name and failed, and then succeeded, and wished I hadn't.

"What happened to the survivors?" I asked. "The journal doesn't say."

"It wouldn't. He didn't know yet when he wrote those pages." Sable fed a stick into our small cookfire and watched it catch. "But I can guess. Some scattered upriver. Some took work on boats and never spoke of it. And some—" They paused, and I saw them deciding whether to spend the truth. "Some signed on with salvage fleets. Corvane's fleet takes anyone who'll work and asks no questions. Men from burned places always sign on. It's the only way to be near what you lost."

"That's monstrous."

"That's survival. It usually looks monstrous from the outside." They glanced at me, and for a moment something moved behind the gray eyes, something old and banked like the stove in the ruined kitchen. "Ask me someday how I know. Not today."

I let it go. We were rationing truths, the two of us, spending them carefully like the last of a water supply, and I was learning that this was not coldness. It was how people like us loved each other—one measured cup at a time.

I slept badly, and woke before dawn to the sound of an engine that had no business being there.

Not a boat engine. Higher. Thinner. An engine with wings.

"Sable." My hand found their shoulder in the dark and they were already awake, already moving, killing the cookfire's coals with sand in two efficient motions. "Scout plane?"

"Or patrol launch with a loud exhaust. Either way we're a lit match in a dark room." They were rolling the charts as they talked, moving through the cannery's black interior with the unerring economy of a person who has hidden from things before, often, professionally. "Kill the lantern. Take the journal, take the horse if you must, and get to the boat. Walk, don't run—running makes noise, noise makes witnesses."

The plane passed over while I was crossing the shingle.

It came out of the pre-dawn gray like a rumor given teeth—a high-wing monoplane, floats slung beneath it, running lights dark, and the only warning was the sound swelling out of the north. It passed directly over the village, low enough that I felt the wash of it in my chest, and I flattened myself against the cannery wall among the nettles and looked straight up into its blind black belly and understood, with total clarity, that there was a man in that machine with a map on his knees, and that our names were written on it.

It circled once over the anchorage. Once over the village. Its engine note changed—throttled back, the pilot looking, *seeing*—and hung there for three heartbeats, five, seven, long enough for me to count the ways this could end.

Then it climbed away south, toward the fleet, and the sound faded, and the river took the silence back.

Sable appeared beside me out of the dark so smoothly I flinched. They were looking at the southern sky where the plane had gone, and their face in the faint starlight was the face I imagined Ede had worn for twenty years: calm as a frozen lake, and underneath, currents.

"It marked us," I said.

"It marked the anchorage. Whether it marked *us* depends on how good the pilot is and how much Corvane trusts him." Sable turned for the boat, then stopped. "Either way, we're done sleeping here. We run on this tide, and we run quiet, and we don't stop again until the sound."

I got aboard. I cast off the lines with hands that only shook a little. And as the Kestrel slid out into the black water and the dead village fell away behind us—chimneys and nettles and the leaning bell tower and all forty-one names asleep in the grass—I took out the tin horse and set it on the chart table where the compass light caught its buckled leg.

"We're coming back," I told it. I meant the whole village. I meant the names. I meant that whatever else happened at the wreck of the Meridian, the ledger with Danil Ashby's family in it was going to be read aloud in front of witnesses, and Aldous Renner was going to hear every single one.

Ahead, far down the channel, a cluster of lights moved against the stars—too many lights, too steady, riding too high in the water.

The salvage fleet, running the same tide we were.

And between us and the sound, six hours of river that Corvane's scout plane now knew like the back of its pilot's hand.
