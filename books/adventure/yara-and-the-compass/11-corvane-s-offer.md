## 11. Corvane's Offer

The fog came off the water at dusk like a held breath finally released, and Sable hated it.

Fog was good for hiding in—Sable had built a life on that truth—but it was also good for other people to hide in, and the Kestrel was running low on open river, high on noise. Yara had the tiller, steering by the sound of current against rock, and Sable sat forward with the chart spread across their knees, marking their position by dead reckoning and memory. Somewhere behind them, Corvane's launch had been dogging their wake since morning, patient as a debt.

"He's not even trying to catch us," Yara said quietly. She'd said it an hour ago too. "Why isn't he trying to catch us?"

"Because he doesn't have to." Sable folded the chart along its worn creases. "A man who knows where you're going can afford to walk."

The river narrowed ahead into a reach called the Throat—a mile of water pinched between granite shoulders, deep enough for a launch to run fast, shallow enough at the edges to open a hull like a tin of sardines. There was no way around it. Ede's notes put the next anchorage beyond it, and the tide would not wait on their caution.

Sable checked the fuel gauge out of habit, though they already knew what it read. Half a drum. The drum they'd lost in the Narrows still ached like a missing tooth; every calculation since had been arithmetic done with one hand tied.

"Throttle down when we're through," Sable said. "I want to hear anything that's behind us before it hears us."

Yara nodded and didn't ask why. She'd stopped asking why somewhere around the second day, and Sable had noticed the silence the way you notice a splinter—not painful exactly, but always there, reminding you it was in you.

The Throat took them in.

Walls of wet stone rose on either side, black with lichen, streaked white where springs bled down the face. The air inside was colder, older, smelling of iron and drowned things. The current ran hard in the middle channel, and Yara rode it with her whole body, knees bent, wrists loose, reading the water the way Sable read sky. For a while there was nothing in the world but the sound of the river and the creak of the Kestrel's timbers and the small animal warmth of two people breathing in the same cold air.

Then, from the fog behind them: an engine. Not hurrying. Just there.

Yara's head came up. Sable held up one hand—*steady*—and listened. Twin screws, big displacement, throttled back to match their speed exactly. It stayed a half-mile back through the whole length of the Throat, close enough to be a threat, far enough to be a courtesy, and when the walls fell away and the river opened into the long dark lake below, the engine changed its note.

It began to close.

* * *

It caught them at full dark, on the lake, under a sky scoured clean of cloud so that the stars came down to the waterline and made it impossible to tell where the world ended. The launch ran up alongside without lights, which told Sable everything about the confidence of the man aboard—you didn't run blacked-out on strange water unless you'd already bought the map of it—and a voice carried across the gap, unhurried, almost pleasant.

"Miz Ashgrove. Or should I say—" a pause, savored, "—the name your mother gave you?"

Sable's hands went still on the rail.

Beside them, Yara turned, and in the starlight her face was a question. *Ashgrove?* she mouthed. Not *who is he*. Not *what does he want*. Just the name, turning over, finding no purchase.

"Keep your heading," Sable called back, and their own voice came out level, which surprised them. They'd spent thirty years making sure it would. "You'll hole yourself on the shoal off the point if you crowd us."

"I know about the shoal." A lantern kindled on the launch's deck, and by its light Sable saw Brann Corvane for the first time at less than a hundred yards. He was broader than his reputation, gray at the temples, with the settled calm of a man who had stopped needing to prove anything years ago. He stood at his own rail with a mug in one hand, steam rising off it, for all the world like a neighbor come to borrow tools. "I know this water better than you do, I think. I've been running it since spring. So let's not waste each other's fuel."

"We don't have any to waste," Sable said. "That's rather the point of running from you."

Corvane smiled. It was a real smile, which was worse. "May I come alongside properly? I give you my word no one touches anything on your boat. You have my word I won't board without asking twice."

"You'll forgive me if I price your word at market rate."

"At market rate it's worth more than you'd think." He raised the mug slightly, a toast across the water. "I'm offering you a partnership, Miz Ashgrove. One-third of the salvage. Cash value, no questions, paid wherever you like—or not paid at all, if you'd rather never see me again after. All I need is the map."

"And the daughter?"

The smile thinned by a degree. Only a degree. "The daughter keeps what her father left her. Sentimental items. Letters. I'm not a monster."

"No," Sable said. "You're a professional. It's more efficient."

Behind them, Yara had gone very still at the tiller. Sable could feel her listening the way you feel weather change.

"Come alongside, then," Sable heard themselves say, and wondered, distantly, whose voice that was. "Since you asked twice."

* * *

The launches kissed fenders in the middle of the lake, engines idling, drifting together like two animals deciding whether to fight or graze. Two of Corvane's crew stood well back on his deck, hands visible, faces blank in the way of men paid to be furniture. Corvane stepped across the gap alone, light on his feet for a big man, and stood in the Kestrel's cockpit with his mug steaming and his eyes moving once, quickly, professionally over everything—the chart case, the oilskin roll's outline under the thwart canvas, the axe, the spare line, Yara's hands.

He catalogued all of it in three seconds. Sable respected the craft and despised the man, and saw no contradiction.

"You're burning daylight sitting here," Yara said. Her voice was flat and hard, and Sable heard Ede Voss in it, which must have been like swallowing glass for her. "Say what you came to say."

"I did. One-third." Corvane drank, watching her over the rim. "You look like him, you know. Around the eyes."

"Don't."

"All right." He set the mug down on the coaming, deliberately, a guest settling in. "Then let me talk to the pilot instead, since the pilot is the one who decides things. That's how it works on boats, isn't it? One hand on the wheel."

"The wheel's hers tonight," Sable said. "But go on."

Corvane turned to them fully then, and the pleasantness dropped away—not into menace, but into something more unsettling: candor. "Here's what I actually know, so we can stop pretending. In 1909 a magistrate named Aldous Renner foreclosed on a farmstead in the Alder Valley. Four hundred acres, timber rights, water rights. The family contested it. The family lost. There was a fire in the winter that followed, and a boy didn't come out of it, and the parents were gone by spring—one dead, one vanished. The vanished one turned up eight years later flying medicine into river towns under a name nobody could trace, because whoever taught them to fly also taught them to disappear." He paused. "That's you. That's the history. And here's the part that matters: Renner is the money behind my fleet. Has been for six years. Which means the man who burned your family out is the man paying me to find this wreck—and you've spent a week leading me straight to it."

The lake made its small sounds against the hulls. A loon called somewhere north, long and lonely, and got no answer.

"So," Corvane went on, softer now, almost gentle, which was the cruelest register he had. "One-third of whatever's in the Meridian's hold, and my personal guarantee that Renner never learns your name is attached to any of it. You walk away rich and invisible. Or you keep refusing, and I follow you to the wreck anyway—I will, you understand, I've never needed your map, only your skill at reading it—and Renner learns that the ghost of the Alder Valley has come home. He keeps lists, Miz Ashgrove. I've seen them. Your name is already on one. The only question is which column."

Sable looked at him for a long moment.

Thirty years. Thirty years of rented hangars and cash payments and names invented and discarded like gloves, of never sleeping two nights in the same bed, of learning the interior's fog and current and rock so thoroughly that the wilderness itself became the only witness who knew them—and knew them as nobody, as a shape, as a rumor with a floatplane. All of it built on the single certainty that Aldous Renner had forgotten them, that a magistrate's attention span was shorter than a survivor's discipline.

And now a stranger stood in the cockpit holding that certainty up to the lantern light, showing the rot in it.

"You said the fire," Sable said. Their voice sounded far away to themselves. "What do you know about the fire?"

"What everyone who's looked knows. It started in the barn. The inquest called it a lamp." Corvane's eyes didn't move. "Renner signed the inquest findings himself. That's the kind of man he is. He doesn't just take things—he writes the paper that says he never took them."

Something moved through Sable then, slow and seismic, thirty years of sediment shifting. Not rage—rage would have been simpler, cleaner, a thing you could aim. This was older and colder: recognition. The bill arriving. Every fugitive knows, in some sealed room of the heart, that the past is not dead but merely unpaid, and that someday a man will step out of the fog holding the invoice.

They had simply always assumed it would be Renner himself.

"No," Sable said.

Corvane waited.

"One-third of a dead ship's cargo, bought with thirty years of somebody else's justice." Sable shook their head slowly. "You're offering me hush money for a crime I didn't commit, against a man who committed worse ones than yours, and you think the price is money. Take your mug and get off my deck."

"It's not your deck."

"It's the only thing that's ever been mine," Sable said, "so it will do."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered behind Corvane's composure. Not anger. Something almost like envy, or its cousin, respect. He picked up the mug. He looked at Yara, standing at the wheel with her jaw set and her father's stubbornness written all over her, and something passed across his face that might have been regret, filed away for later examination.

"You could have been rich," he said.

"I've been rich," Sable said. "Once. Before I understood the exchange rate."

Corvane laughed then, short and genuine, and stepped back across to his own launch. At the rail he paused, one hand on the wire, and looked back.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I'd have kept my word. About the name." He shrugged, a big man's shrug, carrying weight. "Doesn't matter now. Renner reads my reports. He'll know you refused. He'll know you're still coming." He turned forward. "Get some sleep, both of you. You'll want it. The sound's another two tides, and the last stretch is all fog and islands, and I'll be behind you the whole way."

The engines of the launch came up out of idle with a growl, and the gap between the boats widened, and the lantern shrank, and then there was only the lake again, enormous and indifferent, and the stars, and the sound of Yara breathing beside them.

She didn't speak until the launch's wake had died entirely.

"Ashgrove," she said finally. "That's not your name."

"No."

"The boy in the fire."

"My brother." Sable looked north, toward the sound, toward the wreck, toward the man who had written the inquest findings in his own hand. "His name was Tam. He was nine."

Yara was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—harder at the edges, but warmer underneath, the way steel warms. "My father ran from that man for twenty years to keep me off his lists. And I hated him for it."

"I know."

"He died gut-shot in the fog because he wouldn't stop carrying the proof." She gripped the wheel. "And now that man is two miles behind us, following us to the place where the proof is buried."

"Yes."

"Good," Yara said. "Let him follow."

Ahead, far off across the black water, the first islands of the sound stood up against the stars like the ribs of something vast and waiting—and Sable, who had spent a lifetime believing that invisibility was safety, felt the last of that belief go out of them, quiet as a lamp in a barn, and found that what remained felt strangely like relief.

Two tides. Then the fog, and the islands, and everything owed coming due at once.
