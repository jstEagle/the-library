## 12. Third Tide: The Falls Portage

*Yara*

The falls announced themselves an hour before we saw them—a low, continuous thunder rolling up the river like weather that had decided to live in one place. By the time we nosed the Kestrel into the eddy below the first white water, the sound had gotten into my teeth. I could feel it in the fillings Ede never paid for.

"Portage trail's on the left," Sable said, and there was something in their voice I'd learned to distrust over three tides of traveling together: certainty delivered too quietly, the way a person says a thing they're hoping you won't examine too closely.

I examined it. "How far?"

"Two miles over the ridge. Then a mile of shallows before it's deep enough to float again." Sable killed the engine and let us drift, hands resting easy on the tiller in that way they had, as if the boat were a bird that might startle. "The fleet can't run the falls either. Corvane will have to portage same as us."

"Then we still have our lead."

"We have what's left of it." They looked at me across the cluttered cockpit—the coiled line, the jerry cans we'd lashed down after losing the spare drum in the narrows, Ede's journal wrapped in oilskin and tucked under my arm like a sleeping animal. "Yara. Two miles of portage means carrying everything twice. The boat comes apart into sections, but she's still eight hundred pounds of cedar and stubbornness. We'll be making trips. Four, maybe five each."

"Then we'd better start."

Sable didn't move. Fog was burning off the water behind them, and the light came through it in long gray shafts, catching the gray at their temples, the scar along their jaw I'd never asked about. "There's a path around," they said finally. "Downriver. Adds a day, maybe more, but nobody carries anything."

"And Corvane takes the wreck while we're walking the scenic route." I stood, and the boat rocked, and my shoulder—the right one, the one that had been complaining since the narrows—sent a bright wire of pain down my arm. I ignored it. I was getting good at ignoring things. My father had taught me that without ever meaning to; he'd ignored me for nineteen years, and look how much he'd accomplished. "Break her down. Show me how."

For a moment I thought Sable would argue. They had that look, the pilot's look, weighing load against weather against fuel and finding the sum always short. But then they nodded once, and started unbuckling the keel bolts, and that was that.

We worked in silence for a while, because work has its own language and we spoke it well by now. That was the thing I hadn't expected, in all those nights I'd rehearsed hating this stranger who brought me my father's map and my father's dying words: how quickly the hatred would wear smooth into something else. Not trust, exactly. Trust was a word for people who hadn't been burned. It was more like—interlocking. Like the scarf joints in a planked hull, where no single piece is strong enough alone but overlapped in the right pattern they'll hold against open water.

Sable carried the bow section up the first stretch of trail to show me the route, moving with a sureness that made the ground look flatter than it was. I followed with the midsection, and the weight settled onto my shoulder exactly wrong, and I heard something in me go *twang* like a harp string failing.

I didn't put it down. That's the part I keep coming back to, later. I could have put it down. Instead I breathed through my teeth and took the next step, and the next, and told myself the pain was just information, and information could wait its turn.

By the third trip the waiting was over. The shoulder had stopped complaining and started screaming, a hot white flare every time I lifted, and I was carrying the stern section—the lightest piece, Sable had seen to that without saying anything, which was its own kind of insult—and halfway up the ridge trail my knees simply informed me they were done negotiating.

I sat down hard on a root. The boat section slid off my shoulder and into the moss, and I heard myself make a sound I didn't recognize, something between a laugh and a sob.

Sable appeared out of the green above me so fast I hadn't even heard them coming back down. They crouched, set down their own burden, and reached for my arm—and when their fingers found the joint, gently, testing, I made the sound again, louder.

"Dislocated?" they asked. All business. All calm. I wanted to hit them with my good arm.

"It's fine."

"It's not fine. It's out, or close enough." They were already shrugging off their jacket, folding it into a pad. "Hold still."

"Don't—" But their hands were already moving, one bracing my elbow, one cupping the joint, and they said, "Breathe out. Now," and pushed, and the world went white and then, abruptly, mercifully, ordinary. The pain dropped from screaming to shouting. I sat there shaking, sweating through my shirt, while Sable bound the shoulder with a strip of canvas and a length of line, quick and practiced, like they'd done it in worse places than a mossy ridge above a river full of thunder.

"Who taught you that?" I managed.

"A man I owed money to." They tied off the knot. "He accepted payment in kind."

I laughed. It hurt, and I laughed anyway, and then the laugh turned into something else, the way things do when your body has been holding more than your body should hold, and suddenly I was crying on a portage trail with my father's boat in pieces around me and my arm strapped to my chest like a broken wing.

Sable didn't say anything. They just sat down beside me in the moss, close enough that our shoulders almost touched, and waited out the storm the way they waited out weather—which is to say completely, patiently, as if storms were simply another kind of terrain.

"I'm sorry," I said finally. "I'm slowing us down."

"You're not."

"One arm. You'll be hauling double."

"Yes."

"So I *am* slowing us down."

Sable looked at me then, and there was something in their face I hadn't seen before—not the careful blankness, not the pilot's arithmetic. Something rawer. "Yara," they said. "Four days ago I was a rumor with a fuel bill. Now I'm half of a crew. You don't slow a crew down. You *are* the crew." They stood, and offered me their hand, and pulled me up with a care that contradicted everything else about them. "Now sit on that rock and guard the boat sections. Guarding is a one-armed job."

"That's not fair—"

"No," Sable agreed, gathering up the stern section and then, after a moment's visible calculation, the midsection too, staggering slightly under the combined weight before finding the balance. "It's not. Most true things aren't."

They climbed. I watched them go, this person I'd known four days and would apparently trust with my life, swaying up the trail under eight hundred pounds of my father's legacy, and I understood—with a clarity that felt almost like grief—that if they stumbled, if they went down into that ravine with the boat, I would follow them without hesitating. Not for the map. Not for the wreck. For them.

That scared me more than the falls did.

* * *

*Sable*

Three trips. I counted them the way I counted everything now—in loads, in minutes, in the shrinking coin of the tide. Three trips up and I'd moved five of the seven sections plus the engine, and my back had filed its formal complaint somewhere around the top of the ridge, and Yara sat below on her rock with her strapped arm and her furious eyes, guarding nothing, guarding everything.

On the fourth trip I stopped counting and started bargaining. With my spine. With the river. With whatever god kept books on bush pilots, and I suspected that god kept Renner's ledgers too.

The trail was bad. It had been cut decades ago, probably by prospectors, probably by men like Ede Voss, and it had been eroding ever since, switchbacking up through alder and deadfall with the ravine dropping away on the left side into a green chaos of spray and noise. On the second switchback I found an old iron ring bolted into the rock, rusted to lace, and understood that someone had once lowered boat sections down this cliff face on ropes, and decided not to think about it.

Halfway up, the thought arrived, uninvited, the way the worst thoughts do when your hands are full and your footing is doubtful: *You could drop it here. Let the river take the pieces. Tell her the current got them.*

Nobody would know. The wreck would stay buried until the next extreme tide, years from now, and by then Renner would be dead or promoted, Corvane dispersed, and Sable Winterbourne—or whoever Sable Winterbourne was this decade—would be a rumor again, anonymous, alive. The evidence in Ede's journal would rot in an oilskin wrap instead of getting us both killed.

I stood on the switchback with eight hundred pounds of cedar dragging my shoulders toward the ravine and considered it honestly, because dishonesty with yourself is how pilots die.

And then I thought of Yara's face when she'd read the entry about the burned village aloud, her voice going flat and careful the way voices do when feeling would otherwise spill. I thought of the child's toy she'd found in the ashes, and how she'd carried it since, wrapped in a rag, refusing to explain it. I thought of Ede Voss bleeding out on a fogbound strip, trading his last possession for a promise, and how I'd said yes, and how a yes given to a dying man isn't a contract—it's a heading. And you don't abandon a heading halfway just because the weather turns. Every pilot knows that. The ones who forget it are the ones the mountains keep.

I climbed.

At the top of the ridge the trail crested through a saddle of wind-bent pines, and beyond it I could see the downstream water—wide, brown-green, braiding through gravel shoals, calm as a held breath. A mile of shallows, then depth. I set the sections down, went back, and made the last trip with the engine, which weighed as much as sin and forgave as little.

When I came down the final time, Yara was standing. Of course she was standing. She'd rigged a sling from her jacket and was loading the smallest section onto her good hip, jaw set at an angle I recognized from mirrors in other lives.

"Put it down," I said.

"You put yours down first."

"Mine are down. All of them. We're reassembling at the waterline." I took the section off her hip before she could protest, and she swayed, and for a moment she leaned into me—just a moment, just her weight against mine, brief as a touch of wings—and neither of us mentioned it, because some things mention themselves loudly enough.

We reassembled the Kestrel in the shallows, working around each other in the old wordless grammar. She could hold, brace, guide—one-handed but precise, her good arm doing the work of two because her mind refused to inform it of the shortage. I watched her seat a plank joint blind, by feel, and thought: *Ede built this into her without ever building anything with her.* Boatwrights' daughters learn the way rivers learn their beds.

The last bolt seated as the sun came down through the pines in long copper bars. The Kestrel floated in the shallows, whole again, scratched and patched and uglier than she'd ever been, and I felt something absurdly like pride in her, which was ridiculous, because she wasn't mine.

"She's not his either anymore," Yara said quietly, reading my mind or her own. "Not after today. After today she's ours."

* * *

*Yara*

We quarreled at dusk, in the lee of a gravel bar, over cold beans and the question of whether any of this was worth dying for.

It started small, the way the bad ones do. Sable said we should anchor and rest until morning. I said the tide tables didn't care about our backs. Sable said tired people died on rivers. I said—too loud, too sharp, the pain talking, the grief talking, everything talking at once—"So does everyone eventually, so what difference does it make what kills us?"

The silence after that had texture. Sable set down their tin plate with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you might throw.

"Do you want to know what I think?" they said. "Or do you want to win?"

"I want to *know*," I said, "whether we're doing this for him or for us or for a village full of ghosts, because I've been carrying all three and I can't tell them apart anymore, and my shoulder is screaming, and Corvane is a day behind us, and I am so *tired*, Sable."

Something in them loosened. I watched it happen—the armor unseaming, just a little, just enough.

"I'll tell you what I know," they said. "I know that six days ago I would have taken a job like this for pay and never learned your name. I know that somewhere between the narrows and Miller's Landing, that stopped being possible. I know that when your shoulder went out on that trail, my first thought wasn't the schedule." They looked at the fire instead of at me, which was how they looked at the biggest things. "That's new. That's expensive. And I don't know yet if it's worth what it costs—but I know I'm not willing to find out by quitting."

The fire popped. Downriver, faint under the falls' eternal thunder, the water ran dark and patient toward the sound, toward the wreck, toward whatever Ede had buried there that men were willing to burn villages for.

"My father left to protect me," I said. "Nineteen years, and I thought he just—didn't want me. And now he's dead and the only conversation we'll ever have is a map." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "So no, I don't know if it's worth dying for. But I know it's worth *this*. Worth the shoulder. Worth the tired. Worth being afraid together instead of alone."

Sable was quiet a long time. Then they picked their plate back up and said, "Beans are getting colder," which was, I understood, the closest thing they had to agreement, and possibly to affection.

We ate. We doused the fire. We anchored in the lee and took watches, and somewhere past midnight, on my watch, I heard Sable talking in their sleep—a name, a place, half a sentence about land—and then stop, the way sleepers do when the dream turns to the page with the bad news on it.

Dawn came up gray and smelling of rain. And with it, from downriver, thin over the water, came a sound that emptied the night out of me:

Engines. More than one. Running hard against the current, hours earlier than Corvane should have been able to manage.

Someone had told them where we were.
